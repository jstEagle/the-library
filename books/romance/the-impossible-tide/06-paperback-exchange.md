## 6. Paperback Exchange

**WREN**

The bell over the door of Tidewrack Books made the same sound it had made eleven years ago, and Wren Okafor stood in the doorway like a woman who had forgotten what doors were for.

She'd rehearsed this. Not consciously—she didn't let herself do anything consciously anymore, not where Jonah Thackeray was concerned—but all week her mind had been running drills without her permission. *You'll buy a paperback. You'll pay. You'll say thank you. You'll leave.* Four steps. Pilots liked checklists; it was how they stayed alive.

The shop smelled the same. Salt-damp and old glue and that particular vanilla scent of aging paper, and underneath it something else she couldn't name until she could: him. Coffee gone cold in a mug somewhere. The cedar shaving he must still use on the counter because there was a pale scar of it in the air. Eleven years, and the man had become a smell, and her body knew it before her brain caught up.

He was behind the register with his back to her, shelving a stack of hardcovers, and for one long moment she just looked at him. He'd gone grey at the temples early, which was unfair of him, because it suited him. His shoulders were broader or maybe she'd shrunk them in memory. He moved the way he'd always moved, careful and a little too quiet, like a man practicing for invisibility.

*Four steps,* she told herself. *Buy. Pay. Thank. Leave.*

She picked up a paperback at random from the front table—a thriller with a lighthouse on the cover, which felt like the island mocking her personally—and carried it to the counter, and set it down, and waited.

Jonah turned around.

Whatever she had expected—and she hadn't let herself expect anything—it wasn't the way his face did three things at once: recognition, panic, and then a kind of terrible politeness sliding over both like weather coming in off the water.

"Wren." Her name came out steady. She hated him a little for how steady it was.

"Jonah."

"You're back." As if she were a tourist. As if she hadn't been flying that ferry past his window twice a day for three weeks.

"Interim pilot," she said. "Until the route closes."

"Right. The route." He looked down at the paperback in her hands as though grateful for it. "Good choice. Everyone says the ending's a cheat, but the middle holds up."

"I'll take my chances."

He rang it up. His fingers on the till keys hadn't changed either, and that was somehow the worst part—not his face, not his voice, but his hands, doing their small familiar arithmetic while neither of them said one true thing.

"That's eight fifty," he said.

She paid in exact change, coins counted out one by one because it made the transaction last longer, and she couldn't have told you why she wanted it to last longer except that some animal part of her was not ready to walk back out into the rain.

"Bag?" he asked.

"No."

"Receipt?"

"No."

A pause. Somewhere in the back of the shop a radiator ticked, keeping its own time.

"How's the boat?" he said, at the same moment she said, "Business looks good."

They stopped. He gestured for her to go first. She refused with a small shake of her head, and he almost smiled—not quite, but the muscles around his mouth remembered trying.

"The boat's fine," he said. "Old girl. Like everything else here."

"And business is business," she said. "Seasonal."

"Yes," he agreed. "Everything's seasonal now."

It was nothing. It was two strangers making conversation about a book and a boat, and it was also the most exhausting thing Wren had done since the gale crossing, because every sentence required navigation. There were shoals everywhere. *Eleven years* sat under the floorboards. *Why did you go* sat behind the biography section. *I wrote to you* — no. That one she didn't know yet. That one was still sealed in a basement she didn't know existed.

"Well," she said.

"Well," he said.

Neither of them moved toward the door.

**JONAH**

She bought a thriller with a lighthouse on the cover and Jonah Thackeray performed the role of bookseller so well that he wanted to applaud himself.

That was the thing nobody warned you about cowardice: it got better with practice. Eleven years ago he had stood at Marisol's counter with an envelope in his jacket and his mouth full of stones, and he had walked away, and it had nearly killed him. Now he could stand three feet from Wren Okafor, ring up her change, comment on a thriller's ending, and his heart only tried to exit through his ribs twice.

Small mercies.

He watched her take the paperback without a bag, watched her hold it against her chest like a shield, and thought: *she used to read with her thumb holding her place, and she bit her nails in winter, and she laughed from her belly when something actually got her, and I know none of these things are mine to know anymore.*

"Do you still fly in weather?" he heard himself ask, and immediately wished he hadn't, because it was a question with a history. Because the answer to whether she flew in weather was the whole reason—

"When they make me," she said. Her eyes came up and held his, and for a second the civility thinned like ice in March. "You'd know that if you'd been watching."

"I watch the ferry," he said. "Everyone watches the ferry. It's the only thing that moves."

Something crossed her face—disappointment, maybe, or relief, hard to tell which was worse. "Right," she said. "Of course. The only thing that moves."

The bell jingled. A customer, thank God—an actual customer, old Tam Broadbent wanting his westerns—and Jonah turned to him with the gratitude of a drowning man thrown a rope, and by the time he'd found Tam his Louis L'Amour, Wren was already at the door.

"Thanks for the book," she said.

"Anytime," he said, which was true, which was the truest thing either of them had said, and then she was gone into the rain and the bell settled and Jonah stood with his hand flat on the counter like a man steadying himself on a deck.

Tam brought his westerns up. "That the Okafor girl?" Tam said. "Back flying the boat?"

"That's her."

"Huh," said Tam, who had known Jonah for thirty years and missed nothing, and left with his paperbacks and his knowing look, and Jonah went to straighten a shelf that didn't need straightening.

**NELL**

Nell had been in the poetry section, which was code for hiding, because the poetry section was directly across from the front table and had sightlines to everything.

She'd seen the woman come in out of the rain—the pilot, the new one, the one the whole island talked about in the lowered voices people used for weather and other unavoidable things. And Nell had watched her father turn around, and something had happened to his face that Nell had never seen happen before, and she was twelve and she read constantly and she knew exactly what it was, even if she couldn't have written it down.

It was the face of someone seeing a place they'd been before.

Not a person. A *place*. Like when you smell bread and you're suddenly six years old in a kitchen you can't quite picture. Dad had looked at the pilot the way he looked at certain pages in certain books—the ones he kept spine-in so nobody saw the titles.

And the weirdest part, the part Nell kept turning over like a stone, was that the pilot had done it too. Looked at Dad like that. They'd stood there talking about absolutely nothing—you could hear the nothing from across the shop, it had that sound, like radio static shaped into words—and the whole time the air between them had been doing something. Pulling. The way the tide pulls at your ankles even when it looks calm.

Nell knew about gravity. They'd done it in school. Mass attracts mass; the bigger the object, the harder the pull. Nobody had ever told her what to call it when it happened between two people in a bookshop, but she recognized the physics anyway.

The woman left. Dad put his hand flat on the counter and stood there like the counter was the only thing holding up the building.

Nell slid out of the poetry section, casual as anything, and drifted toward the front table, and picked up another copy of the thriller with the lighthouse on the cover, and said, "Is that any good?"

Dad started slightly. "What? Oh. Everyone says the ending's a cheat."

"You said that already. To her."

The look he gave her then was quick and cornered, and Nell filed it away with the others—the pinned ferry schedule with the old date, the way he never said the word *mother*, the way he went quiet on dock days.

"She seemed nice," Nell said, testing.

"She's fine," Dad said. "She's a good pilot."

"That's not what I asked."

"Nell."

"What?" she said, all innocence, flipping the thriller over. "I'm just asking about a book."

But she wasn't, and they both knew she wasn't, and her father took the paperback gently out of her hands and reshelved it, and said, "Go start your homework," in the voice that ended conversations, and Nell went, because she was twelve and outnumbered.

But on the stairs she paused, and looked back down through the railing at him standing at the counter with his hand still flat on the wood, staring at the door like it might open again.

*Mass attracts mass,* she thought. *The question is what's in orbit around what.*

Upstairs, she opened her notebook and wrote one line at the top of a fresh page, the way she did when a story was starting:

*The pilot knew where the till was.*

Because that was the thing. That was the detail that wouldn't leave her alone. The woman had come in out of the rain after eleven years—or however long, Nell didn't know the number yet, only that there *was* a number, she could feel it, big and locked—and she had set her paperback on the counter without looking, without hesitating, without asking where.

Like she'd done it a thousand times before.

Like she'd once worked here.

Nell clicked her pen closed and looked at the ceiling of her room, which was also the floor of her father's, and listened to him moving around up there, restless as weather.

Somebody in this house, she decided, was going to tell her the truth eventually.

She just had to figure out which questions were load-bearing.
