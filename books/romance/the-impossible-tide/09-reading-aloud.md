## 9. Reading Aloud

The Thursday story hour had been dying a slow death for three years. When I started it, back when Nell was small enough to fall asleep against my ribs mid-chapter, we'd get twenty kids and their parents crammed between the shelves like sardines with juice boxes. Now I was lucky to pull six, and two of those were usually the Delgado twins, who came less for the stories than for the ritual of heckling me.

I almost cancelled it that week. The basement still smelled of brine no matter how many times I scrubbed it, and the letter sat where I'd hidden it—slipped inside the dust jacket of my first edition of *The Shipping News*, which felt like a joke only I would get—and every time I climbed the stairs from the stockroom I could feel it down there, patient as water. But cancelling meant thinking about why I wanted to cancel, so instead I made cocoa, dragged out the good chairs, and pinned the week's pick to the corkboard: *The Mousehole Cat*. A story about a fisherman who goes out into a storm he shouldn't survive, and the cat who goes with him.

Nell set up the chairs without being asked. She'd been doing that lately—doing things without being asked, watching me while she did them, in a way that made the back of my neck prickle.

"Big crowd tonight," she said, deadpan, surveying the four chairs she'd arranged.

"It's quality over quantity."

"Mm." She straightened one chair a degree that didn't need straightening. "Dad. Why do you have an old ferry schedule above your desk?"

My hands went still on the cocoa tin. "It's not old."

"It's dated 2013."

"Tides repeat," I said. "Every pattern comes back around eventually. It's practically new information."

She looked at me the way she looked at a math problem she'd already solved but was letting me finish anyway. "That's not how calendars work."

"Story hour," I said. "Go tell the Delgados they can't sit on the table this time."

She went, but she went looking back at me, and I stood there holding a tin of cocoa like it was the last solid thing on Merrow's Reach.

* * *

They came in ones and twos, the way island people do. Marisol arrived first, because Marisol arrived first everywhere, and took her usual seat in the front row—the postmistress's privilege, she claimed, though she'd retired from the counter years ago and simply refused to surrender the title's perks. Then the Delgado twins, then a toddler named Birdie whose mother sold bread at the Saturday market and who spent the entire hour trying to eat the corner of a picture book.

I was halfway through reading—Mowzer on the boat, the Great Storm-Cat prowling outside the harbor—when the bell over the door rang.

I didn't look up. That was deliberate. Some animal part of me knew before my eyes did, the way you know a change in barometric pressure, and I kept my voice level and read Mowzer singing her lullaby to the storm, and only then, at the end of the paragraph, did I lift my head.

Wren Okafor stood at the back of the shop.

She'd changed out of her ferry uniform into a sweater the color of wet slate, hair pulled back, and she held herself the way she always had—one shoulder slightly higher, weight on her left foot, like she was perpetually about to step onto a deck. She scanned the room, found the empty folding chair in the last row, and sat down in it with the finality of someone dropping anchor.

Nell saw her. Of course Nell saw her. My daughter turned in her seat, studied the newcomer with open twelve-year-old interest, then turned back to me with an expression I couldn't read and didn't want to.

"Keep going," Marisol whispered, not whispering at all. "You've lost your page, Jonah."

I hadn't lost my page. I'd lost eleven years. But I found the line again—*the Great Storm-Cat came leaping down the wind*—and I read on, and my voice only cracked once, on the word *home*, and I covered it with a cough that fooled nobody except possibly Birdie, who was asleep.

* * *

Here is what I will say about the next twenty minutes: they were the longest and shortest of my recent life. Every sentence I read aloud, I heard twice—once as the words on the page, once as the thing underneath them. A cat who chooses to ride out the storm beside the man she loves rather than wait safe on shore. I'd chosen the book on Tuesday. On Tuesday it had been a charming classic. On Thursday night it was a confession I was broadcasting to a room containing my daughter, the town's most dedicated gossip, and the woman I'd written a letter to and never mailed.

When I closed the book, the applause was modest and genuine. Birdie woke up and applauded too, clapping at nothing, which got a laugh. Parents gathered children, coats were located, and the room drained the way rooms do—slowly, then all at once—until it was just me stacking chairs, Nell shelving books, Marisol pretending to browse the local history section she'd memorized decades ago, and Wren, still in the back row, not leaving.

"Dad," Nell said, too loudly, "I'm going to walk home with Priya's mom. She's outside."

"We live upstairs."

"I'm going to walk home with Priya's mom," she repeated, with the emphasis of a girl depositing a plot point, "and then come upstairs." And she was gone before I could construct an objection, which I suspected was the point.

Marisol materialized at the counter with a paperback she had no intention of buying. "Good turnout," she said.

"Four children and a sleeping infant."

"And Wren Okafor." She said it the way you'd announce a weather system. "First time anyone's seen her at story hour since—" She paused, savoring the arithmetic. "Well. Ever, actually. You started this after she left, didn't you? Funny, that."

"Funny," I agreed.

"She stayed till the end," Marisol said, and patted the counter twice, and left her unbought paperback on the shelf upside down, which I chose to interpret as a blessing.

The bell rang behind her. And then it was just the two of us in a bookshop at eight o'clock on a Thursday, with the lamps making small warm islands in the dark, and Wren Okafor walking toward me down the center aisle like she was crossing a gangway in rough water.

* * *

"You changed the ending," she said.

I blinked. "What?"

"The Mousehole Cat. When you used to read it—you read it differently. You used to do the storm-cat's voice." She stopped an arm's length away, close enough that I could see she was tired in a way that went deeper than the season. "You read it straight tonight. Like you were afraid of it."

"I read it exactly the same."

"You didn't."

"Wren." Her name came out of me like something I'd been holding under water. "Why are you here?"

"That's a question with layers, Jonah." She glanced at the chairs, the scattered picture books, the corkboard with its weekly picks. "I came for the story hour. It's public. It's on the flyer."

"There's no flyer. There hasn't been a flyer in two years."

"Then I came because I heard you'd be reading." She lifted her chin. "And now I'm here, and you're going to look at me, or are we going to keep doing this thing where we orbit each other until the season ends and one of us leaves again?"

The word *again* landed like a hook set deep. I gripped the edge of the counter. "You're the one who left."

Something moved through her face—not surprise; more like the moment a wave meets a seawall and decides what shape to break into. "Say that again."

"You heard me."

"No, I want to hear you say it slowly. Because I've spent eleven years turning that night over, Jonah, and I've never once managed to make it land on me leaving. So walk me through it. Slowly."

"The job was in Bristol," I said. "The ferry company. A full pilot's certification, a salary, a flat. You had the offer in your hand for three weeks. And then one morning you were on the boat."

"I had the offer in my hand," she said, "and I asked you one question. One. Do you remember what it was?"

I remembered. God help me, I remembered it in her exact voice, standing at the sink in the flat above this very shop, dish towel in my hand, the whole future balanced on a single syllable.

"Come with me," I said quietly. "Or stay. Either one. Just say which."

"And what did you say?"

"I said I couldn't leave the shop. I said the island needed—"

"You said *it's complicated*." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about arguing with Wren; she never raised it, she just took the floor out. "You said it's complicated, and you looked at the dish towel, and you wouldn't look at me. And I stood at that dock for forty minutes, Jonah. Forty minutes. The engine running. Watching the door of the shop. Waiting for you to come down the hill. And you know what I thought? I thought, if he doesn't come, that's the answer. If he doesn't come, he's telling me to go, and at least it'll be clear."

"I wasn't telling you to go."

"Then what were you telling me?"

And there it was—the question I'd rehearsed answers to for a decade, in the shower, on the stairs, in the dead hours after Nell fell asleep. I opened my mouth and the honest answer was right there, sitting on my tongue like a coin: *I was telling you I loved you and I was too much of a coward to say it, because saying it meant either following you or begging you to stay, and I couldn't afford either.*

What came out was: "I was telling you I had responsibilities."

Wren laughed. It was a short, hard sound with no humor in it, and it hurt worse than shouting would have. "Responsibilities. Right. The shop. Which, by the way, was doing fine—I checked the numbers, Jonah, I helped you with the accounts, we both knew you could have taken a year. Or come back. Ferries run both ways. They ran both ways then, too."

"I had a life here."

"We had a life here," she said, and her voice finally cracked, just once, on *we*, and I saw eleven years of it cross her face—the Bristol flat, the mainland routes, whatever she'd built and whatever it had cost. "That's the part you keep editing out. There was a *we*. And you dissolved it without a vote."

"I didn't dissolve anything. You boarded a boat."

"Because you handed me a reason to!"

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked, the building settling the way old buildings do. We stood there breathing, two people fluent in each other, and I realized with a strange, floating clarity that this was the most alive I'd felt in years. That anger, real anger, aimed at me by Wren Okafor, was more intimate than anything I'd shared with another human being since she left. That was its own kind of damning.

"You could have come back," I said. Quieter now. "Any summer. Any Christmas. The route ran until this year. Eleven years of crossings, Wren. You piloted past this island hundreds of times."

"I piloted past this island," she said, "because the man I loved told me it was complicated, and I was twenty-six, and I believed him. And every time I came back I'd have to look at him and find out whether it was still complicated, and I couldn't—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her sternum, an old gesture, one I knew. "Do you know what I did in Bristol, the first winter? I wrote you letters. Four of them. Never sent them. Isn't that funny. Two cowards, mailing nothing."

The word *letter* went through me like cold water through a hull.

"You wrote—"

"Forget it." She shook her head fast, retreating from her own admission, and I watched the drawbridge go up. "It doesn't matter. It was a long time ago."

"It matters."

"It doesn't." She picked up her bag from the folding chair. "I came here tonight to hear you read a story, and instead I got a decade-old argument, and I'd like to say we cleared the air, but we didn't, we just proved the air was never cleared at all. It's been sitting in this shop going stale for eleven years."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and it wasn't unkind, which was worse. "I'm on the 7:40 tomorrow. I'll be back Friday afternoon. We'll do this again sometime, apparently, because neither of us knows how to stop."

She walked down the aisle toward the door, and I called her name—just her name, nothing else, no sentence attached, because I still couldn't build one—and she paused with her hand on the bell.

"Nell," she said, not turning around. "She has your way of watching people. Did you know that?"

And she was gone, the bell settling into silence behind her, and I stood alone among the stacked chairs with the storm-cat's lullaby still caught in my throat.

Upstairs, Nell was already home, sitting at the kitchen table with her homework spread out and a look on her face that I recognized, with a lurch, as my own.

"How was story hour?" she asked, not looking up.

"Fine. Small crowd."

"Mm." She turned a page. "Dad, for my history project—Marisol said I should ask you about 2013. She says you'll know the story." She looked up then, and her eyes were steady and brown and terribly patient. "She says you were there when it happened."

I reached for the kettle because my hands needed a job, and the kettle was empty, and I filled it anyway, and the water ran and ran while I tried to remember how to lie to my daughter, and found—to my horror—that I'd forgotten how.
