## 11. Storm Warning

The gale came in off the Atlantic on a Tuesday, the way the worst ones always did—unannounced by anyone who mattered and over-announced by everyone who didn't. By noon the harbor office radio was a chorus of voices stepping on each other: the fishery patrol, the mainland coastguard relay, old Tam Yeo on the moorings channel insisting his boat was fine when everyone knew it wasn't.

Wren stood at the wheelhouse window of the *Merrow Maid* and watched the water go from gray to the color of wet slate. Thirty knots out of the southwest and building. The barometer had been falling all morning like something with somewhere to be.

"Route's cancelled," said Dev Ainsley from the mainland operations desk, crackling through the handset. "Effective immediately. Nothing crosses tonight."

"Copy," Wren said, and hung up, and then stood there looking at the phone number taped to the console—the surgery's direct line, which she'd asked for on her first week back without explaining why, and which Dev had given her without asking either, because that was the kind of unspoken arrangement this route ran on.

At 12:40 the call came. Mrs. Adeyemi at the island clinic, voice level in the way that meant she'd already done all her panicking privately: a patient, sepsis indicators, antibiotics needed from the mainland pharmacy, tonight or the window closed. Evacuation by helicopter wasn't possible in this wind. The ferry was the only way across.

She could have refused. Protocol gave her every reason to. The *Merrow Maid* was rated for weather worse than this on paper, but paper didn't account for the crosscurrent at the Reach narrows, didn't account for a single-handed pilot running a supply run in a gale because there was no one else to ask.

She called the clinic back. "I'll take it across. I need someone on deck for the lines."

There was a pause, and then Mrs. Adeyemi said, "Jonah Thackeray volunteered an hour ago. He's been down at the dock since eleven."

* * *

He was waiting under the shelter roof at the end of the pier, a hard case of medical supplies at his feet and a yellow rain jacket that did nothing against the horizontal spray. He looked, Wren thought, like a man who had been standing in weather for a long time and intended to keep standing in it as long as the situation required.

"You shouldn't be here," she said through the wheelhouse door, once he'd hauled the case up the gangway.

"Neither should you."

"That's different. It's my job."

"It's my island." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said everything, and then he clipped the case into the cargo cradle and took up position at the stern lines without waiting to be told. Eleven years ago he'd have waited to be told. He'd have waited so long she'd have had to tell him twice. Something about that—about the man he'd become while she wasn't watching—sat in her chest like a stone dropped in still water.

"Life jacket," she said. "Aft locker."

"I know where they are."

Of course he did. He'd ridden this boat before it was hers, back when the pilot had been old Callum Reeve and Wren had been seventeen and seasick and Jonah had held her hair back over the rail with the solemn tenderness of a boy who hadn't yet learned that tenderness was a thing you could fail at.

She started the engines.

* * *

The first mile was manageable. The second was not.

The swell came at them quartering from the southwest, long and steep, and the *Merrow Maid* climbed each one and fell down the far side with a shudder Wren felt in her teeth. Rain moved across the water in solid gray walls. She flew the boat by instruments and instinct both, hands light on the throttles, reading the sea the way you read a sentence you half-remembered from childhood.

Jonah braced himself against the chart table behind her. She could hear him breathing—not panicked, just present, a steady rhythm that was somehow worse than panic because it made the wheelhouse feel inhabited, made it feel like the small warm room it had been once, in another life, with two people in it instead of one.

"Barometer?" he said.

"972 and falling."

"And the narrows?"

"Ugly. We'll hit them at slack water if we're lucky."

"Are we lucky?"

"We're alive," she said. "That's most of the budget."

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, quickly swallowed—and she hated how much she'd missed it, how the sound of it went through her like current through wire. She fixed her eyes on the compass card. North-northwest. Twenty-two degrees off the swell. Hold the angle, hold the angle.

Halfway across, the radio woke up.

"*Merrow Maid, Merrow Maid, this is Milford Coastguard.*"

She keyed the set one-handed. "*Merrow Maid, go ahead.*"

"*We show your track heading offshore in deteriorating conditions. Confirm nature of passage.*"

"Medical supply run. One passenger, essential personnel. Clinic request, island side."

A pause on the line, the particular pause of a watch officer deciding whether to argue with the sea or with her. "*Understood, Merrow Maid. Advise you reassess at the halfway point. We'll keep a listening watch.*"

"*Reassessing now,*" she said, and did not reassess anything, and put the handset down.

Jonah had gone quiet behind her. When she glanced back, he was watching her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere—something between admiration and grief, as if the sight of her doing her job competently in bad weather was a wound he'd earned the right to look at.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing. You're—" He stopped. Started again. "Callum used to say you were the best helm he ever trained. He undersold it."

"Don't flatter the pilot, Mr. Thackeray. It makes her nervous."

"Noted," he said, and then, softer, almost lost under the engine noise: "It makes me nervous too."

She turned back to the window before he could see what that did to her face.

* * *

The narrows were exactly as ugly as promised.

The tide was turning early, fighting the wind, and where the two forces met the water stood up in short, vicious peaks that broke white even in the rain-dark. Wren brought the boat around to take them head-on, throttles back, letting the bow rise and fall through each one rather than trying to power through. Spray came over the top of the wheelhouse in cold slaps. Somewhere aft, Jonah had wedged himself against the cargo cradle with one arm locked through the rail stanchion, holding the medical case steady with his body.

"Case secure?" she shouted.

"Secure!"

"Fifteen minutes to the lee of the point!"

He gave her a thumbs-up, and then, absurdly, in the middle of the worst water she'd steered in three years, he smiled at her. Not a big smile. Just the corner of his mouth lifting, the specific crooked lift she would have known blindfolded, underwater, dead. And the terrible thing—the thing she would think about later, turning it over like a stone in her pocket—was that her first feeling wasn't anger or grief or any of the feelings she'd rehearsed for eleven years. Her first feeling was homecoming.

That frightened her more than the sea did.

They came through the last of the standing water and the point took the wind off them all at once, and the *Merrow Maid* settled into the relative calm of the lee like a animal lying down. Wren realized her hands ached. She realized she'd been holding her breath in increments, rationing it. Behind her, Jonah straightened up slowly, rolling his shoulder where he'd wrenched it against the rail.

The dock lights of Merrow's Reach swam out of the rain, blurred gold smears. Two figures with a stretcher trolley waited under the shelter. Mrs. Adeyemi's people, fast.

"Lines," Wren said, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to.

* * *

The delivery took nine minutes. Jonah carried the case down the gangway through driving rain, handed it to the waiting orderlies, exchanged words she couldn't hear, and came back aboard soaked through, breathless, alive in a way that showed all over him. He stepped into the wheelhouse dripping, grinning, and the grin was the boy from the ferry deck twenty years ago, undimmed, impossible.

"We actually did that," he said.

"We do it most weeks."

"Not in that."

"No," she admitted. "Not in that."

And then the wheelhouse was too small. That was the only word for it. The space contracted around the two of them until there was nowhere to stand that wasn't close, nowhere to look that wasn't at each other. The engines idled below their feet, a low animal warmth. Rain hammered the windows and the world outside dissolved to gray, and inside there was just the amber glow of the instrument panel and the smell of wet wool and diesel and, underneath it, faintly, the bookshop—paper and glue, the smell he carried on him the way she carried salt.

"Wren." His voice had changed. Lower. Careful, the way you're careful with something that might go off.

"Don't," she said.

"I'm not going to say anything. I just—"

"You're saying it right now by saying my name like that."

He shut his mouth. Good. But he didn't move away, and neither did she, and the distance between them was maybe two feet of wet deck plate and eleven years of unsaid things, and the storm outside made the whole world small enough that two feet felt like nothing at all.

Her heart was going. She told herself it was adrenaline, the standard lie pilots tell themselves, and then she stopped telling herself anything at all because he raised one hand—slowly, telegraphing it, giving her the whole length of the approach to refuse—and touched a strand of wet hair back from her face.

His fingers were freezing. She leaned into them anyway. Just slightly. Just enough to be undeniable.

"Wren." Barely a whisper now.

If he kissed her, she decided, she would let him. That was the thought, arriving whole and shameless: *I will let him, and I will deal with the wreckage after, and God help me it will be worth the wreckage.* Eleven years of discipline, of radio courtesies and paperback transactions and keeping her eyes on the horizon whenever he was on the dock—all of it folding at once, like a chart in the wind, because he was looking at her mouth and she was looking at his and the storm had cracked open some sealed compartment in both of them—

The radio shrieked.

"*Merrow Maid, Merrow Maid, Milford Coastguard.*"

They broke apart like teenagers caught in a barn. Wren got the handset up on the second grab, her pulse slamming, her voice coming out one octave too high before she dragged it down.

"*Merrow Maid, send it.*"

"*Confirm you are underway for return passage. Weather window closing. Recommend departure within the hour or you hold overnight at the Reach.*"

She looked at the barometer. Still falling. Looked at the black wall of the harbor entrance, where the sea was getting up again. Looked, briefly, at Jonah, who was studying the chart table with the concentration of a man reading scripture.

"*Holding overnight,*" she transmitted. "*Advise next update at oh-six-hundred.*"

"*Copy, Merrow Maid. Coastguard out.*"

Silence filled the wheelhouse, total except for the rain and the idle. The moment lay between them, broken, its pieces visible—she could see in his face that he knew exactly what had almost happened, and that he was as terrified of it as she was, and that knowing that made it worse, not better.

"The harbormaster's office has a bunk room," Jonah said, in the tone of a man reciting tide tables. "For stranded crew. There's a kettle. I can bring you something from the shop, dry clothes, whatever you—"

"Just the kettle," Wren said.

He nodded. He went down the gangway into the rain. She watched him go, one hand pressed flat against the cold glass of the wheelhouse window, and understood with perfect clarity that she had just agreed to spend a night thirty feet from him on an island with no way off, and that whatever happened before dawn would happen because they had both run out of storms to hide behind.

Out past the point, the gale kept coming, wave after wave after wave, like something that knew exactly what it wanted and had all night to get it.
