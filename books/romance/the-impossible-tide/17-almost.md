## 17. Almost

I woke before the alarm, which never happens, and lay in the grey light listening to the flat above the shop settle around me like a man clearing his throat before a speech. The ferry office smelled of instant coffee and damp wool and the ghost of everything we'd said to each other in the dark. Wren was already awake. I knew it by the silence—she slept like a sailor, in short decisive shifts, and when she was awake the room changed pressure.

She was standing at the window with a mug, watching the harbour do its slow grey arithmetic. The cancelled sailing had left the *Merrow Maid* tied fast, gulls arguing on her rails. Low tide had stranded us both, and the night had done what nights do when there is nowhere to run: it had taken us apart and laid us out on the table, piece by piece. Eleven years of grief. The pregnancy test neither of us had held together. The letter she didn't yet know existed. I had told her everything except the one thing, and she had listened with her whole body, and somewhere around four in the morning she had fallen asleep in the office chair with her hand an inch from mine, and I had sat there like a man guarding a fire he was afraid to warm himself at.

"Jonah." She didn't turn around. "The tide comes back at nine. First sailing at ten."

"I know."

"I'm going to say something, and I need you to let me finish it."

My chest went tight in the specific way it does before a customer asks for a book you don't have. "Okay."

She turned. Morning light off the water moved across her face and made her look both older and exactly twenty-six, the age she'd been when she left, as if the years were a costume she'd only half put on. "I'm offering you a clean slate. Not a reset—we can't do that, we both know it. A slate you write on fresh. Today. On the dock, before I take her out, you say the thing. The actual thing. Not the history, not the analysis, not the tide metaphor. The sentence."

"What sentence?"

"You know the sentence." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Wren; she never rose, she just got quieter and more exact, like water finding the crack. "The one you've been rehearsing for eleven years. Don't tell me you haven't. I watched you not say it for three years before I left, and I've watched you not say it for eleven years since. You're the only person on this island who doesn't know you've been rehearsing it."

The gulls argued. The harbour slapped the pilings. Somewhere behind me the office kettle ticked as it cooled.

"And if I say it," I said, "then what? You fly out of here on a Tuesday and I'm back to—"

"Stop." She set the mug down with a click that ended the sentence for me. "That's the old script, Jonah. That's the 2013 draft. I'm not asking you to predict what happens after. I'm asking you to say it while I'm standing in front of you. That's the whole offer. Say it plainly, and we start from there. Say it plainly, and I'll believe there's something to start from."

"And if I can't?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and what was in her face wasn't anger. It was something worse. It was the look of a pilot reading weather she's seen a hundred times and knows exactly how it ends.

"Then I'll know," she said, "and I'll stop waiting, and it will be a kindness to both of us. I can't do another eleven years of almost, Jonah. I'd rather have the truth in one piece than the almost forever in instalments."

She picked up her bag. She'd slept in her clothes; we both had. At the door she paused with her hand on the frame, and I thought—this is it, this is the moment, the sentence is right there in your chest, it has been right there for a decade, say it now, say it while her hand is on the door, it doesn't have to be on a dock, it doesn't have to be cinematic, just say it—

"Nine o'clock," she said. "The dock."

And she was gone, and the room was just a room, and I stood there in the cold coffee smell understanding that I had just watched the cleanest, widest-open door of my life swing shut on its own weight, and that it would reopen once more at nine, and that I had until then to become a different man.

* * *

I didn't go home. I walked the long way around the harbour with my hands in my pockets, doing what I always do, which is narrating. That's the sickness, if I'm honest. I narrate instead of live. I'd been doing it since I was a kid—turning everything into a story with a safe distance built in, past tense, third person, *the boy walked the long way around the harbour because he could not face the short way through himself.* Even my bedtime stories for Nell, when she was small, were all about people who meant to say things and found the words later, and I used to think I was teaching her patience, and now I wonder if I was teaching her my disease.

The shop was cold. I put the kettle on and stood in front of the romance section like a man visiting a country he'd been exiled from. Eleven years of shelving other people's love stories, other people's confessions, other people's grand gestures on docks and in rain, and I'd catalogued every one of them, and I'd believed—this is the humiliating truth—that knowing the genre meant I'd be ready when my chapter came. That familiarity was a kind of rehearsal.

It isn't. Rehearsal is what you do instead. That's the thing nobody tells you. The sentence I'd been polishing for a decade was a stone in my pocket, and I'd mistaken the weight for progress.

I thought about the letter in the first edition of *The Shipping News*, third shelf, behind the glass case, where I'd hidden it from myself. I'd read it now, in the small hours, after Wren fell asleep. All of it. The whole ridiculous, bleeding, eleven-year-old thing. *I loved you. I was afraid. I didn't know how to be what you needed and so I made myself need nothing, and I called that strength.* It was all in there. Every word I'd need at nine o'clock, written in my own hand, in ink that had bled a little at the edges from the flood, as if the letter itself had been trying to drown before I could read it.

I could have brought it. I could have handed it to her on the dock like an exhibit. But she'd said *say it*, and she was right, and I knew she was right, and knowing she was right was somehow the heaviest thing of all. Because a letter is a text, and a text can be edited, and I have spent my whole life editing.

At twenty past eight Nell came down the outside stairs and let herself in with her own key, hair still wet from a shower, school bag over one shoulder. She took one look at me and put the kettle on again, because it had gone cold, because I'd forgotten to drink anything, because I am a man who can run a bookshop and a household and a child alone for eleven years and cannot, in the presence of the woman he loves, operate a kettle.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you."

"Did you talk to her?"

"A lot."

"Did you *say* anything?"

I looked at my daughter—twelve years old, ferocious, patient in the way of people who have learned to wait out adults—and she looked back at me with Wren's exact eyes, and the sentence rose in my throat like a tide, and I opened my mouth, and what came out was:

"It's complicated, Nell."

She didn't sigh. She did something worse. She nodded slowly, like a doctor confirming a diagnosis, and zipped her bag, and said, "Dad. The ferry leaves at ten. Marisol told me. Everyone on this island knows, because Marisol knows, which means everyone knows, which means if you don't say it today I'm going to have to watch you be like this forever, and I'm twelve, and I deserve better material."

She kissed the top of my head—she'd started doing that this year, reversing our heights in spirit if not yet in fact—and left for school, and the bell over the door rang behind her like a small verdict.

* * *

At nine I was on the dock. I want that on the record, wherever the record of my failures is kept. I was there. The tide had come back in the night and the harbour was full and bright and indifferent, and the *Merrow Maid* sat at her berth with her lines fresh, and Wren was on the deck checking the moorings with the economy of a woman who has done ten thousand of these departures and will do ten thousand more.

She saw me and came down the gangway. She'd changed into her uniform jacket. That detail went through me like a splinter: she had dressed for the crossing, for the job, for the version of the day in which I failed. She had prepared for my silence the way you prepare for weather.

"Okay," she said. "I'm listening."

And here is what I want to explain, and I will fail to explain it, because the whole problem is that I explain instead of say. In the night office, in the dark, with no audience and no stakes, I had told her everything. Grief, fear, the pregnancy, the years. It had poured out of me like water finding a hole, because at four a.m. there is no past tense, there is no narrator, there is only the raw material. But the dock at nine a.m. is a stage, and the harbour has witnesses, and Mrs. Calloway was already at the rail of the chandlery with the transparent interest of the professionally informed, and the sentence—the sentence, the actual one, the four-word one, the one that costs nothing and everything—would not come.

What came instead was this.

"Wren. I've been thinking about logistics."

I watched her face do it. I watched the light go out of it in stages, like a house going dark room by room.

"The route's decommissioned after this season," I said, and my voice had the bright, brisk, unbearable tone of a man giving a presentation, "but the winter maintenance posting—they'll need someone for the layup, the engine work, the mooring refit. It's not much, but it's something. And there's the mainland offer, obviously, the captaincy, which you should take, you should absolutely take it, you'd be brilliant, but if you wanted a reason to— I mean, Nell's in school here, and the shop's here, and there are ferries to the mainland twice a week even after decommission, so it's not as if—"

"Jonah."

"—and I know it's not a solution, but it's practical, it's a practical arrangement, we could build something practical, people do, people build whole lives on less than—"

"*Jonah.*"

I stopped. The harbour slapped the pilings. A gull screamed somewhere with what felt like editorial intent.

She looked at me the way you look at a book you loved that has fallen apart in your hands—not with blame, exactly, but with the grief of a thing breaking along its old crack. "I asked you for one sentence," she said quietly. "One. I stayed up all night listening to you. I gave you the whole night. I came back to this island, I took this job, I flew through a storm with you in that wheelhouse, and all I asked for was the one sentence you've had eleven years to write." Her voice didn't break. I almost wish it had. "And you gave me a ferry schedule."

"Wren—"

"I know what you're doing," she said. "I've finally figured it out, after all these years. You think if you never say it, it can never be refused. You think if you keep everything practical, everything deniable, you can't lose it. But Jonah, you *did* lose it. Eleven years ago. You lost it at the counter of the post office, you lost it on this dock, you lose it every single morning, and the practical arrangement is just the losing with better bookkeeping."

The words went into me like cold water into a boot. Because she was right. She was so precisely, devastatingly right that some part of me, the narrator part, the coward part, was already composing the paragraph.

"I have to go," she said. "Pre-departure checks. The sailing's at ten."

"Wren, wait—"

She was already walking up the gangway, and I stood on the dock with the sentence in my chest like a swallowed stone, whole and entire and unsayable, *I love you, I have always loved you, I was afraid, I am afraid, I am sorry*—right there, right there, one breath away, and my mouth, my traitor mouth, my eleven-year mouth, called after her the only thing it could manage:

"I'll see you at the crossing."

She didn't turn around. She lifted one hand—not a wave, exactly; more the way you acknowledge a radio transmission you've received and do not intend to answer—and went up onto the deck of the *Merrow Maid*, and the gulls lifted off the rails in a white scatter, and I stood on the dock of Merrow's Reach in the bright indifferent morning, holding nothing, having said nothing, having failed the exam I'd spent a decade studying for.

The rehearsal was over. The performance had come, and I had frozen in the spotlight, and the house lights were coming up, and somewhere in my body I understood—with the terrible clarity that arrives exactly one sentence too late—that she had offered me a clean slate, and I had handed her back the old one, still blank after all these years.

I looked at my watch. 9:41. The sailing was at ten.

Nineteen minutes. And the letter—the letter that said everything, that had said everything since 2013—was sitting behind the glass case in my shop, forty yards away, and I was already turning toward it, already running, because I am Jonah Thackeray, and when I cannot say a thing, I go and get the thing written down.

The bell over the shop door rang as I burst through it, and the first edition was on its shelf where I'd hidden it, and I pulled it out with shaking hands, and that was when I saw, through the front window, Nell standing on the dock—skipped school, bag still on her shoulder—watching the *Merrow Maid* with an expression I recognized instantly, because I see it in the mirror every day.

She had the letter's twin of a plan on her face. And she was looking at the ferry.
