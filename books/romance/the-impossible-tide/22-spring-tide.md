## 22. Spring Tide

The tide came in on time, the way it always did, and for the first time in eleven years Jonah Thackeray was not narrating his life in past tense.

He noticed it on a Tuesday in April, standing behind the counter of Tidewrack Books with a box of new stock open at his feet, when he caught himself thinking *I am happy* instead of *I was happy once, before*. The thought arrived so quietly he almost missed it. That was the thing about the present tense. It didn't announce itself. It just moved in, the way Wren had moved in—first a duffel bag in the hallway of the flat above the shop, then a toothbrush, then her laugh coming through the floorboards while he closed up downstairs, until one morning he woke and the silence he'd lived inside for a decade was simply gone, replaced by the small domestic weather of another person's existence.

The bell over the door rang. Nell came in from school, backpack slung over one shoulder, hair still carrying the salt wind off the harbour road.

"Marisol says the chairs came," she announced. "Forty of them. She counted twice because she didn't believe it."

"Forty chairs," Jonah said. "For a party that isn't a party."

"For a gathering," Nell corrected, with the gravity of a twelve-year-old who had learned, over the past six months, exactly how much words matter. "Wren said if anyone calls it a wedding she's docking their ferry privileges."

"There's no ferry to dock privileges from."

"Then she'll invent one." Nell dropped her backpack behind the counter and went straight to the shelf where they kept the new arrivals, running her finger along the spines the way she'd done since she was small enough to need a stool. "She's been practicing saying it, you know."

"Saying what?"

"'My partner runs a bookshop.'" Nell said it herself, testing the shape of it. "In the wheelhouse. To the radio. The Coast Guard station thinks she's lost her mind."

Jonah laughed, and the laugh felt unguarded, which was still new enough to surprise him. Six months ago he had stood on a dock and shouted a sentence across open water—the sentence, the one he'd rehearsed for eleven years—and the world had not ended. It had, in fact, begun. That was the part nobody warned you about. You spent years believing that the truth was a detonation, and then you said it, and instead of rubble there was just Tuesday. There was stock to unpack and chairs to count and a daughter who corrected your vocabulary.

* * *

The gathering—*not a wedding*, though Marisol had already ordered a cake with two figures on it and dared anyone to comment—was scheduled for the first Saturday of May, when the spring tide runs highest and the whole island comes out to watch the water climb the sea wall like it's showing off. Wren had chosen the date deliberately. "If we're going to do this," she'd said, "we do it on the day the water comes all the way in. No more low tide for us."

Jonah had wanted to argue with the poetry of it and found, to his ongoing astonishment, that he no longer wanted to argue with anything she said. It was disorienting. It was like discovering a room in your own house you'd never opened.

The shop had been transformed by Thursday. Marisol commandeered the front window for a display of every book the island's book club had read in the past decade, arranged in a lopsided heart that she defended as "rustic." Someone strung lights along the poetry shelf. Mrs. Calloway from the bakery brought samples that disappeared before noon. And Nell—Nell had claimed the story hour corner, dragging the big armchair into position and arranging a semicircle of small chairs with the precision of a stage manager.

"You're reading Saturday," she told him, not asking.

"Am I?"

"Two parents at story hour. That's the whole point." She said it plainly, the way she said everything now, and Jonah felt the old flinch—the instinct to deflect, to tell a story about tides instead of answering the question—and watched it pass through him and out the other side like weather. "Wren does the voices. You do the reading. That's the system."

"The system," he agreed.

She hesitated then, in the doorway to the stacks, and for a moment he saw the ghost of the girl who had found a letter hidden in a first edition and read one line before stopping—who had spent months leaving ferry timetables on his desk like breadcrumbs toward a life he was too afraid to walk into. That girl had done more than any of the adults around her. She had finished what he couldn't start.

"Dad," she said. "I'm glad you got on the boat."

He had to set down the book he was holding. "So am I," he managed.

"Obviously." And she was gone into the stacks, twelve years old again, leaving him to stand behind his own counter with his heart in pieces and reassembled, which was, he was learning, what love did. It didn't keep you whole. It kept you *held*.

* * *

Saturday came in gold. The tide climbed the sea wall at midmorning, right on schedule, and by noon the shop was full in a way it had never been—fishermen in their good jumpers, the schoolteacher, half the crew of the mainland ferry service who'd come over on Wren's last official crossing of the decommissioned route and were now, technically, her passengers on a boat she still piloted for supply runs. The route was gone. The *route* was gone, but Wren Okafor had found a way to keep a boat on this water, because that was what Wren did: she looked at an ending and found the crossing hidden inside it.

Jonah stood at the counter watching her across the room—she was deep in argument with Marisol about the cake, gesturing with a slice of it—and felt the full strange weight of the present tense settle over him.

There was a letter on the counter.

He'd written it in March. It had taken him four attempts and a great deal of muttering, and Nell had proofread it over his shoulder with the merciless eye of a girl who'd read the first line of its ancestor and never forgotten it. It was addressed to Wren Okafor, care of the harbour office, Merrow's Reach—a delivery distance of four hundred metres, which Marisol had found so funny she'd had to sit down. It was stamped. It had been *mailed*, properly, through the post, because some things needed to travel the whole way to be real.

*Wren. I loved you in 2013 and I love you now, in the present tense, which is the tense I intend to stay in. I was afraid for eleven years and I am not afraid today. Nell knows your name and your laugh and the sound of the engine, and so do I. Stay.*

She'd read it at the harbour office, he'd been told, twice, and then carried it in her breast pocket for a week like a talisman, and now it lived in the drawer of the wheelhouse, which she called "the filing system."

But this letter on the counter was different. This one was addressed to Nell Thackeray, and Jonah hadn't written it. He picked it up. Wren's handwriting, sure and slanted. Stamped. Postmarked that morning, because Wren had insisted it go through the post, because—Jonah was beginning to understand—Wren understood something he'd spent a lifetime getting wrong: that the words you say are a beginning, but the words you *send* are a promise. A letter is proof against the day you lose your nerve. A letter says: I meant this even when no one was watching.

He set it back down exactly where Nell would find it, and said nothing, and went to join his family in the middle of the room.

* * *

Story hour happened at four, because some rituals hold. The children arranged themselves in their semicircle; the adults pretended not to watch. Wren took the armchair's arm, not the whole chair—leaving him the seat, an act of quiet choreography that made Marisol dab her eyes with a napkin she claimed was for cake crumbs—and Nell sat on the floor at their feet, too old for story hour and present anyway, because she had earned her place in this story more than anyone.

Jonah read from a picture book about a lighthouse keeper's daughter, because of course he did, because the island had a sense of humour and so did he, finally. Wren did the voices. The gull was Scottish. Nobody knew why. It destroyed the children every time.

And when the last page turned and the small audience dispersed to the cake, Nell stayed where she was and looked up at the two of them in the big chair and said, "Read the dedication."

Jonah frowned. He'd read this book a hundred times. "There's no dedication."

"There is now." She nodded at the counter. "The letter. It's for me. You're allowed."

He looked at Wren. Wren looked at the ceiling with the studied innocence of a woman who had absolutely planned this. He crossed the shop, picked up the envelope, and brought it back, and Nell opened it with the care of someone who understood, better than any child should, what it costs to put words in the mail.

She read it silently. Her face did something complicated—wobbled, steadied, wobbled again—and then she read it aloud, because Nell Thackeray had never in her life let a good sentence go to waste:

*"Dear Nell. I wasn't there for the beginning of your story, and I'm sorry, and I will spend the rest of it making up the distance, one page at a time. You found the letter when no one else would. You built the bridge your father was too scared to cross. This is me, crossing it. Love, Wren. Your other parent. Present tense."*

The shop had gone very quiet. Marisol was openly weeping. Mrs. Calloway had produced a handkerchief from somewhere.

Nell folded the letter along its creases, precise as a librarian, and looked up. "Okay," she said, voice only slightly unsteady, which for a twelve-year-old was heroic. "But the gull still has to be Scottish."

"Forever," Wren promised.

* * *

That evening, after the chairs were stacked and Marisol had gone home with half a cake and a full heart, Jonah stood at the shop window looking out at the harbour. The tide had turned and was running out, but that was all right. That was just the water doing its work. It would come back. It always came back. He'd spent eleven years thinking the tide was a metaphor for loss—everything the sea takes, the leaving, the low water of his life—and he'd had it exactly backwards. The tide was a metaphor for return. The tide was proof that nothing true stays away forever.

Wren came to stand beside him, shoulder against his shoulder, and they watched the lights come on along the quay.

"I got a letter today," she said.

"I know. I posted it."

"You posted it." She turned the words over like stones. "Four hundred metres. Through the Royal Mail."

"Some things need to travel the whole way."

She laughed—that laugh, the one that had come through the floorboards and then through the walls and then into the rooms themselves—and took his hand, and out in the dark the ferry lights blinked at their mooring, patient, going nowhere, staying.

Above them, faintly, Nell was reading aloud in her room—her own book, her own voice, steady in the present tense—and Jonah Thackeray stood in his shop with the woman he loved and the daughter they were raising and thought: *this is the sentence. Not the one I rehearsed. This one. The one I'm living in.*

The bell over the door stirred in a draft that smelled of salt and spring, and the tide, far out in the channel, was already on its way back in.
