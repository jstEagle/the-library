## 4. The Deadline

Marcus Hale had a theory about geniuses, and it was this: they were all hiding from something, and the something was always worth money.

He'd built a career on the theory. Twenty-two years in publishing, first as an editorial assistant who fetched coffee for people who mistook cruelty for candor, then as an editor, then as publisher of his own imprint, Hale & Vane, which occupied the eleventh floor of a building on West 21st Street that smelled permanently of other people's ambition. Marcus had signed three National Book Award finalists, two Oprah picks back when that still meant something, and one poet who later threw a chair at him at a Frankfurt book fair — an incident Marcus now described, at dinner parties, as "the most honest review I ever received."

But nothing — nothing — in his stable performed like Vesper Lane.

Four novels in nine years. Each one debuted higher than the last. The latest, *The Salt Almanac*, had spent thirty-one weeks on the list and was currently being optioned for film by a studio that kept sending Marcus gift baskets containing things like truffle honey and a candle called "Ember & Oud," as if Marcus could be bought with condiments. (He could. He'd lit the candle just that morning.)

And in all those years, Vesper Lane had never appeared. Not at a launch party. Not on a panel. Not for a photo, a podcast, a bookstore signing, not even a phone call — all communication routed through an agent named Delia Marsh, who was either the most loyal woman in publishing or the most frightened, and Marcus had learned early that those were often the same person.

"No one is that private," Marcus said to his assistant, Priya, on the morning everything changed. "People who are that private are usually dead. Or famous under another name. And Vesper Lane isn't famous under another name, because I would have smelled it."

"You say 'smelled it' like that's a professional skill," Priya said, not looking up from her screen.

"It is a professional skill. I once identified a ghostwriter by her semicolons." He straightened his cuffs. He wore cuffs worth straightening; Marcus believed in dressing like the meeting mattered, because then it did. "Book me lunch with Delia. Somewhere quiet. Somewhere with tablecloths, so she understands this is serious, but not somewhere so expensive she gets defensive about the markup on the wine."

"Which is your real motive."

"Priya. Both things can be true. That's what makes me good at my job."

* * *

Delia Marsh arrived at Le Coucou-adjacent bistro — the kind of place where the menus had no prices because if you had to ask, you were eating elsewhere — ten minutes late and radiating the specific wariness of a woman who had represented difficult authors for two decades and recognized a trap when it ordered her a glass of the Sancerre.

"Marcus."

"Delia. You look wonderful. Is that new?"

"It's four years old."

"It's aging beautifully." He smiled at her over the menu he didn't need; he'd chosen his order from the taxi. "How is our favorite recluse?"

"Vesper is fine. Working. You know how the process goes."

"I do know how the process goes. What I don't know is what the process looks like. Nine years, Delia. Four books. I've published authors who've shown me their kitchen renovations. I've had a novelist describe his divorce to me in such detail I had to bill it as research. And Vesper Lane sends manuscripts through you like messages smuggled out of a prison camp. Beautiful messages! Bestselling messages! But I'm starting to feel less like a publisher and more like a dead-drop."

Delia sipped her wine. "There's no mystery to solve, Marcus. Some writers just write."

"Some writers just write," Marcus agreed pleasantly. "And some writers write four novels in which every single book features a protagonist who has disappeared, changed their name, or buried something so deep it comes up only in the last eighty pages, usually near water." He let that sit between them like a third guest. "Have you noticed the water? There's always water. A lake in book one. The coast in book two. A flooded quarry in book three. And now a lighthouse, Delia. Our mysterious friend has moved us to a lighthouse."

"People like lighthouses."

"People love lighthouses. Which is my point." He leaned forward, and here it came, the charm turned up to working temperature — warm, reasonable, the voice of a man offering to help you carry something heavy. "I'm not asking you to betray a confidence. I would never ask that. I'm asking you to consider whether the confidence is serving anyone anymore. Because here's what I see: a readership starving for connection. A market that has gone absolutely feral for authenticity — memoirs, essays, author photos taken in bad lighting on purpose. And sitting on top of all of it, the most beloved voice in literary fiction, completely invisible. Do you know what that's worth? Do you have any idea what the story of Vesper Lane is worth?"

"I know what it's worth to Vesper," Delia said quietly, "and it isn't for sale."

The waiter arrived with their food. Marcus had the duck; Delia had ordered the fish with the resigned air of someone who knew the meal was going to be wasted on her.

They ate. Marcus told a story about the chair-throwing poet. Delia laughed despite herself, because Marcus was, infuriatingly, funny, and because twenty years of lunches had built a real affection underneath the chess.

It was over coffee that he played his best card, the one he'd been keeping warm since the appetizers.

"The memoir," he said. "That's the offer. Not a demand — an invitation. Vesper doesn't even have to write it alone. We pair them with someone. A collaborator. Gentle process, total editorial control, final approval on every word. All they have to do is say yes to being known."

"They'll say no."

"Probably." He stirred his coffee though it needed nothing. "But here's the thing about no, Delia. It's a very clear signal. It tells me exactly where the walls are. And I've been in this business long enough to know that when someone builds walls that thick—" he held his hands apart, measuring an imaginary fortress, "—there's always a reason. And reasons, in my experience, come in two varieties. The boring kind, and the kind that sell two million copies in hardcover."

Delia set down her cup with a small, precise click. "You're threatening my author."

"Heaven forbid. I'm *courting* your author. Aggressively. With tablecloths." He smiled, and for a moment the smile was entirely genuine, which somehow made it worse. "One year, Delia. Give me one year of gentle encouragement. If Vesper still says no after a year of my very best manners, I'll drop it forever and we'll never speak of it again."

"And if you get impatient?"

Marcus paid the check before she could reach for it — a courtesy, a message, a leash made of gratitude. "Then I suppose," he said pleasantly, "we'll both find out what I do when I get impatient. But let's hope it doesn't come to that. I hate being impatient. It's terrible for my skin."

* * *

Back at the office, the afternoon light came slanting through the windows overlooking the rooftops of Chelsea, and Marcus stood at the glass with his jacket off and his sleeves rolled, doing his best thinking, which had always looked, from the outside, like a man doing nothing at all.

Priya found him there. "Sales meeting notes," she said. "Also, legal sent the contract redlines for the film option. Also, your three o'clock cancelled, so you have a hole."

"A hole," Marcus said, "is an opportunity wearing a bathrobe." He took the notes without reading them. "Priya. Humor me. If you wanted to find out who Vesper Lane really was — legally, ethically, all the fine print observed — how would you go about it?"

Priya finally looked up from her laptop. She'd worked for him three years and had learned that the question behind the question was always the real question. "You're not asking hypothetically."

"I'm asking professionally."

"Then professionally? I'd start small. Shipping records. Royalty statements route through the agency, but payments eventually touch a bank, and banks touch names. Tax filings, if you can get near them. Copyright registrations — those are public, actually. Pen name or not, the copyright claimant has to be a real entity." She paused. "You already did this, didn't you."

"I did a little of this," Marcus admitted, unashamed. "Months ago. The copyright filings list a holding company. Marsh Literary Services Trust. Dead end dressed as a dead end. But the tax angle—" he tapped the window, where far below, a delivery truck idled in traffic, carrying someone's books to someone's hands, "—money leaves fingerprints. And I've got a researcher who owes me a favor from the old days. Very good at following paper trails without leaving any of his own."

"This is going to blow up," Priya said. "You know that. When it blows up, it's going to blow up on you."

"Everything blows up eventually. The trick is to be standing next to the thing you want when it does." He turned from the window. "Get me the researcher. And move my Friday calls — I may need to travel."

"Travel where?"

Marcus smiled at the skyline, at the whole glittering, gossiping city that had never once managed to tell him who Vesper Lane was.

"That depends," he said, "on where the fingerprints point."

* * *

The researcher's report arrived eleven days later, in a manila envelope hand-delivered to the office, because Marcus's researcher was the sort of man who didn't trust email and charged extra for paranoia.

Marcus read it standing up, twice.

The trail was thin but it ran in one direction. A royalty disbursement, years ago, routed through a regional bank branch. An address history attached to the holding company's registered agent — a law firm, naturally, but law firms have clerks, and clerks have memories, and one clerk remembered a client who'd signed paperwork with a return address "somewhere up the coast, Maine way, a lighthouse town — Merrow-something. Pretty spot. Sad story, maybe. They didn't talk much."

Merrow Point.

Marcus sat down slowly at his desk, the report flat under his palm, and felt the particular pleasure he imagined astronomers must feel — the cold vast dark, and then one star where there hadn't been one, and knowing, knowing, that where there was one star there was a whole sky.

A lighthouse. Of course a lighthouse. Book four had practically mailed him the answer, and he'd been too busy admiring the prose to read the postmark.

He buzzed Priya. "Cancel my Thursday. And find out everything about Merrow Point, Maine — population, economy, property values, local newspapers going back fifteen years. Oh — and whether anything notable happened there around eleven years ago. Anything at all. Fires, scandals, court cases, disappearances. Small towns keep their secrets badly; they just bury them shallow."

"You're going there yourself?"

"I'm sending eyes first. Mine go later, if the eyes like what they see." He was already reaching for the phone, dialing the number he kept for favors of the delicate kind — freelancers, mostly. Journalists between staff jobs. People who were good at looking at a town and seeing what it preferred not to show.

When the man answered, Marcus leaned back in his chair, put his feet briefly — irreverently — on the corner of the desk, and said, in his warmest register:

"Do you know Maine in the fall? I have a job for you. Small town. One lighthouse. One very interesting question." He smiled at the ceiling. "Find me the keeper."

* * *

Two days later, Priya knocked on his open door with the odd expression of someone delivering news she wasn't sure she approved of.

"The freelancer confirmed," she said. "Wren Calloway. He's taking the assignment. Says he knows the coast, grew up not far from there, actually." She hesitated. "Marcus — he asked one question before accepting. He asked who the subject was. I told him it was confidential, and he said—" she glanced at the note in her hand, "—he said, 'Then I'll guess it's someone from home. Those are the only stories worth the bus fare.'"

Marcus turned that over, and found he liked the shape of it. A local boy. Local boys saw things strangers couldn't; local boys had keys to doors that didn't advertise their locks.

"Perfect," he said. "Book him. Expense account, generous deadline, and Priya —"

"Yes?"

"Tell him to start with the lighthouse." Marcus opened the drawer where he kept the good pens and the better secrets, and took out the researcher's report, reading again the single line that had made his heart beat like a young man's:

*Subject believed to reside at or near Merrow Point Light, keeper's house, year-round occupancy, sole resident.*

Eleven years of silence. Four perfect novels. One lighthouse at the edge of the map, occupied by someone who did not want to be found.

"Well," Marcus said softly, to the report, to the city, to the whole delicious machinery of a plan coming together. "Let's see what you've been hiding."

He capped his pen, and began, happily, to draft the memo that would tighten the noose — politely, warmly, with excellent tablecloths — around the most valuable secret in publishing.
