## 5. A Car in the Fog

The fog came off the water the way bad news does—slowly, then all at once, and always when you'd left your coat in the car.

Wren Calloway had been driving the coast road for two hours, following a GPS that kept apologizing to him in a voice too polite for the terrain, and now the road had simply given up. It narrowed to a single lane of cracked asphalt with grass growing up through the centerline like the land was trying to heal itself over the wound of pavement. On his left, the sea made sounds he couldn't see. On his right, a wall of blackberry bramble reached out and dragged its thorns along the side of the rental car as if checking his pockets.

"Turn left in four hundred feet," said the GPS.

There was no left. There was fog, and there was more fog, and somewhere inside the fog, allegedly, Merrow Point.

He pulled over, put his forehead against the steering wheel, and laughed at himself—a short, humorless sound that the car absorbed without comment. Eleven years a professional journalist. He'd interviewed war correspondents in hotel bars and a reclusive composer who answered questions only in writing. He had once gotten a sitting senator to admit, on tape, that he didn't read the bills he voted on. And here he was, defeated by a coastal road and a weather system.

Hale's people had found him in March. That was how he still thought of them—*Hale's people*, though by now he'd met Marcus Hale himself twice, been bought three lunches, and signed a contract that paid better than anything he'd done in years. *Find Vesper Lane,* they'd said. *Nobody knows who they are. Nobody has ever known. You're good at finding people.*

Wren had said yes for the money, which was the true reason, and for the story, which was the respectable reason, and because some private, unexamined part of him wanted to know what kind of person could write those books—those books that had gotten him through the worst winter of his life—without leaving so much as a fingerprint. All three reasons were true. None of them was the whole truth, and he knew it, and he drove anyway.

Because the whole truth was this: the trail to Vesper Lane led through Merrow Point, and Merrow Point was where Cyrus Ishikawa had grown up, and Cyrus Ishikawa was the person Wren had loved at nineteen with the entire reckless volume of a nineteen-year-old heart, and Cyrus Ishikawa was also the person who had vanished one August morning eleven years ago without a note, without a phone call, without so much as a returned text, leaving Wren to spend an entire autumn walking past the Ishikawa house and telling himself there was a reasonable explanation, and then another year telling himself he'd stopped telling himself that.

So yes. He'd taken the assignment. He told himself it was professional detachment. He was very good at telling himself things. He'd had eleven years of practice.

The fog thinned for exactly long enough to show him a shape: a white tower rising out of the gray like a held breath. The lighthouse. Below it, a keeper's house with a slate roof gone soft with moss, and beside the house, a gravel lot with one car in it—an old blue hatchback with a bike rack and a bumper sticker that read I'D RATHER BE READING, which struck Wren as either charming or ominous depending on how the next ten minutes went.

He parked. Sat. Watched his own breath fog the windshield and thought, not for the first time, that he should have called ahead. Hale's people had advised against it. *Don't let them know you're coming,* the researcher named Priya had said, tapping her pen against her teeth. *People lie less when they're surprised.* Priya talked about human beings the way entomologists talk about beetles, and Wren had nodded along, and only now, sitting in the fog with his hands still on the wheel, did he understand that he had agreed to ambush someone. Possibly the last person on earth he had any right to ambush.

"Okay," he said aloud, to no one. "Okay."

He got out. The air hit him like a wet towel—salt and cold and something green underneath, kelp and cut grass and woodsmoke from a chimney he could barely see. His shoes crunched on gravel. Somewhere above him, invisible in the cloud, a gull made a sound like a rusty hinge being opened by someone with opinions.

The front door of the keeper's house was painted the deep blue-green of bottle glass. There was a brass knocker shaped like a fish, worn smooth by decades of hands. Wren stood in front of it for a moment longer than dignity allowed, then knocked.

Footsteps. Unhurried. A voice through the door—not raised, just carrying, the way voices carry in houses built to be heard over storms:

"It's open. If you're from the historical society, the answer is still no, and if you're selling gutters, I will pretend to be a much less patient person than I am."

Wren's stomach performed a slow, complicated maneuver. He knew that voice. Of course he knew that voice. He'd spent eleven years trying to forget it and had instead memorized it so thoroughly that hearing it now felt like reading his own handwriting.

He opened the door.

The kitchen was warm and yellow-lit and cluttered in a way that was clearly deliberate: books stacked in towers, a kettle just going off the boil, a cat—a large gray animal with the expression of a middle manager—asleep in a bread basket. And standing at the counter with a mug halfway to their mouth, wearing a flannel shirt and reading glasses pushed up into dark hair that was longer than Wren remembered, was Cyrus Ishikawa.

Cyrus looked at him.

Wren watched it happen—the recognition arriving, the mug stopping midair, the exact moment when surprise curdled into something older and heavier. Cyrus set the mug down with exaggerated care, the way you do when your hands are not to be trusted.

"Wren," Cyrus said.

"Cyrus." His voice came out steady, which felt like a small miracle, or possibly a betrayal of everything he was feeling. "Hello."

A silence. The kettle ticked as it cooled. The cat, sensing drama and finding it beneath its dignity, left the room.

"You look good," Cyrus said, and then immediately winced, as if the words had escaped custody. "That's—I mean. You look. Tall."

"I was tall when you left."

"You were tall-adjacent. You've since committed."

Wren laughed—one startled syllable of it—before he caught himself and shut the door on it. No. Absolutely not. He was not going to stand in this kitchen doing banter. He had rehearsed this moment for eleven years, in showers and on trains and once, memorably, during his sister's wedding vows, and in none of those rehearsals had the scene opened with *you've since committed.*

"I'm here on assignment," he said. Flat. Professional. He put his bag down on the floor between them like a piece of luggage in both senses. "I'm a journalist. I've been hired to find Vesper Lane."

And there it was. He watched Cyrus take the sentence the way you'd take a punch you'd been expecting for a long time—not staggering, but going very still, very careful, the way people do when moving wrong might cost them everything.

"The novelist," Cyrus said.

"The novelist," Wren agreed. "Four bestsellers. No public appearances. No photo ever published. An agent who communicates exclusively by email and claims never to have met them face-to-face. Every royalty check routed through a law firm in Portland. And every thread—" he took a breath, because this was the part he'd turned over a thousand times on the drive, the part that had felt like a coincidence right up until the door opened, "—every thread leads back here. To Merrow Point. To the lighthouse."

"Fascinating," said Cyrus, in the tone of someone discussing drainage.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That. The thing where you talk like a press release. You used to do it whenever your grandmother asked about school. I used to think it was funny. Right now I want to put my fist through this lovely butcher-block counter."

Cyrus glanced at the counter, apparently assessing its structural integrity, then back at Wren. Something moved behind their eyes—something that wasn't the press release. Something tired and frightened and, absurdly, almost fond.

"You want tea," Cyrus said. Not a question. They were already reaching for a second mug, and Wren hated—hated—that they remembered he took it with honey, that the honey jar was already coming down from the shelf before he could object, that his body relaxed by degrees he hadn't authorized.

"I don't want tea. I want answers."

"You can have both. The tea is non-negotiable; it's how my grandmother raised me. Grief, fury, romantic catastrophe—all of it goes better with tea." Cyrus poured, spooned honey, stirred, and slid the mug across the counter toward him. Their hands, Wren noticed, were not entirely steady. Good, he thought, and then immediately, *God, don't let me be glad about that.*

He picked up the mug anyway. Warmth bloomed through his palms. Traitor palms.

"So," Cyrus said. "Vesper Lane."

"You've heard of them."

"A little. My grandmother liked the third one. She said whoever wrote it understood lighthouses." A pause, delicate as a hairline crack. "She died in January."

Wren lowered the mug. "Cyrus. I'm sorry."

"Are you?" It came out fast, and Cyrus closed their eyes briefly, as if embarrassed by it. "Sorry. That was—no. Yes. Thank you. She was eighty-nine and she won every argument she ever entered, including several with the ocean. It was a good death, if such a thing exists. She'd hate all this fuss." Cyrus wrapped both hands around their own mug and looked at him over the rim, and for one unguarded second they were nineteen again, sitting on the breakwater, sharing contraband coffee, and Wren's chest did something structural.

Then the second arrived, and the wall went back up, and Wren remembered why he was here.

"Eleven years," he said quietly.

"Don't."

"Eleven years, Cyrus. You want to talk about tea?"

"No," Cyrus said. "I really, genuinely don't."

"Because I have done the math. I've done it so many times the numbers have grooves worn in them. We saw each other on a Tuesday. You kissed me on the breakwater—you kissed *me*, I want that on the record—and Wednesday morning your house was empty. Your sister wouldn't return my calls. Your grandmother told me you'd gone to visit family, and then she stopped answering the door, and then the house was sold, and I stood in your driveway holding a mixtape like an idiot while a U-Haul I wasn't supposed to see backed out of the road." His voice had risen without permission. He brought it back down. "You don't get to hand me honey and talk about your grandmother like we're catching up at a reunion. I spent six years angry and five years trying not to be, and neither one worked, and now you're standing there making tea like none of it happened."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

"You're right," Cyrus said finally. "It was unforgivable."

The ease of it knocked Wren sideways. He'd imagined this conversation a hundred ways—in most of them Cyrus argued, deflected, gave him something to push against. He had not prepared for surrender. Surrender left him swinging at air.

"That's it? That's your—eleven years, and that's it?"

"What would you like me to say?" Cyrus's voice stayed level, but their knuckles had gone white around the mug. "That I had reasons? I did. They were terrible ones. That I'm sorry? I am, in a way that has rearranged my entire life, but I know how 'sorry' sounds after this long. It sounds like a receipt." They set the mug down. "You came here for Vesper Lane. So ask me about Vesper Lane. That part I can do."

"Why?" Wren said. "Why that part? Why is the pseudonym discussable and nothing else is?"

"Because the pseudonym doesn't hurt anyone."

The words landed strangely—too heavy for their size—and Wren, who had spent twenty years learning to hear the weight under sentences, heard it. But before he could press, Cyrus turned away, busying themselves with the kettle, the stove, the small domestic machinery of avoidance.

"Here's what happens now," Cyrus said, back still turned. "You drive back down the coast, you tell your client that Merrow Point is a dead end, and you invoice them for the trip. Everyone goes home."

"I can't do that."

"Sure you can. It's a very easy lie. I'd offer pointers, but you seem to have a natural gift."

"There it is," Wren said. "There's the person I remember."

Cyrus turned around. For a moment they just looked at each other across the yellow kitchen, across eleven years and one cooling mug of tea, and Wren saw the exact instant Cyrus's composure slipped—not into guilt, exactly, but into something rawer. Fear, maybe. Or the memory of fear, worn smooth as sea glass.

"You don't understand what you're asking," Cyrus said softly.

"Then explain it to me."

"I can't."

"You *can*. You just won't."

"Yes," Cyrus admitted. "That's more accurate."

Wren set down the mug. He walked to the window, mostly to put something between himself and the sight of Cyrus's face, and looked out at the fog-bound tower. Six weeks, Priya had said. Hale wants a name in six weeks or the memoir angle dies and the contract dies with it. Six weeks of this kitchen. Six weeks of this voice.

He turned back. "Here's what I'm offering. I'm staying at the inn in Crosshaven until I have my story. I have a contract that says I find Vesper Lane, and I intend to honor it—but I'd rather hear it from you than dig it out of tax records. So: interviews. On the record, about the lighthouse, about this place, about whatever you're willing to give me. You control what I print. In exchange, when I find what I find—and Cyrus, I *will* find it—you don't lie to me again. Not about this. Not anymore."

Cyrus stared at him. "You're proposing we work together."

"I'm proposing a hostage exchange. You give me the truth in installments; I give you time."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I start with the county archive tomorrow morning, and you get to watch me do it the hard way."

The silence stretched. The cat returned, assessed the geopolitical situation, and settled on the windowsill between them like a neutral observer. Somewhere far off, the foghorn sounded—one low note, patient, ancient, the sound of a thing that had been saying the same warning for a hundred years and intended to keep saying it.

"All right," Cyrus said at last, and the word seemed to cost them something physical. "Interviews. About the lighthouse."

"About the lighthouse," Wren agreed. "For now."

"For now," Cyrus repeated, and there was something in the echo—something that might have been dread, or might have been relief, or might have been the ghost of a girl and a boy on a breakwater who had once believed they had all the time in the world.

Wren picked up his bag. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and made the mistake of looking back. Cyrus was standing in the middle of their yellow kitchen with their arms crossed, watching him go, and the expression on their face was one Wren recognized instantly, because he'd seen it in the mirror every morning for eleven years.

It was the look of someone watching the past walk out the door and knowing, with total certainty, that it would be back.

"Drive carefully," Cyrus said. "The fog gets worse past the point. People miss the turn."

"People miss the turn," Wren repeated. "Right."

He stepped out into the gray. Behind him the door closed, soft as a held breath released. And it was only halfway down the gravel path, with the fog swallowing the tower inch by inch, that he remembered the detail he'd missed—the one that would keep him awake that night at the inn, staring at the ceiling while the foghorn counted the hours:

Cyrus had never asked him how he'd found the place. Had never asked what evidence he had, what threads he'd pulled, how close he already was.

As if Cyrus already knew exactly what he'd find.

As if Cyrus had been waiting eleven years for someone to come asking.

* * *

Back in the kitchen, Cyrus stood motionless for a long moment. Then they walked to the counter, picked up Wren's untouched second cup of tea—poured, sweetened, abandoned—and held it in both hands, staring into it like a mirror.

"Well," they said to the cat. "That went fine."

The cat did not dignify this.

Cyrus carried the cup to the window, where the fog had fully reclaimed the world, and stood there until the tea went cold, thinking about county archives, and tax filings, and a locked drawer upstairs containing a letter written eleven years ago in their own handwriting.

Thinking: *he'll find it. He'll find all of it.*

Thinking: *I have maybe six weeks to decide which version of the truth he finds first.*
