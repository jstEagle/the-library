## 6. One Honest Evening

**WREN**

The storm came in off the ridge like something with a grudge, and by four o'clock the kitchen windows had gone the color of pewter. Wren stood at the counter with her sleeves pushed up, watching the first fat raindrops hit the glass sideways, and made the calculation every cook in Maple Hollow had learned to make: if the power went tonight, two hundred pounds of apples would rot before morning.

"Jonah," she called across the room. He was at the long table with the ledger open, pencil moving in that careful, controlled way of his—everything about him careful and controlled, as if he'd built himself out of scaffolding and forgotten to take it down. "We're canning tonight. All of it."

He looked up. Outside, thunder rolled down the valley like a barrel of stones.

"All of it?"

"Every apple we picked this week. If the lights go, the stove still works on gas, but I'm not losing the harvest to a blackout." She was already pulling jars from the shelf, lining them up like soldiers. "You wanted to keep the books. Consider this an entry: labor owed."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the kind she'd spent three days learning to hate herself for noticing. "Labor owed," he repeated. "I'll note it under *liabilities*."

"You'd better."

They worked while the sky darkened. She cored; he peeled. His hands were steady at the work, she noticed—steady at everything except her, except the past, except whatever lived behind his eyes when he thought she wasn't looking. The paring knife moved through apple skin in long unbroken ribbons, and she hated how beautiful it was, how much it reminded her of watching him play, ten years ago, in a practice room with the afternoon light coming through the blinds in stripes.

She put that memory down like something hot.

By seven the storm had arrived in earnest. Rain hammered the roof, the wind found the chimney and moaned through it, and just as the last jar went into the water bath, the lights died with a soft electronic sigh. The gas range burned blue in the sudden dark, and the whole kitchen contracted to its warmest circle: the stove, the steam, the two of them.

"Well," Jonah said into the dark. "That's that."

"That's that," Wren agreed, and neither of them suggested leaving, because they both knew what the road to the village would be by now—a river running through the orchard—and neither of them said it either, because saying it would make the night real.

* * *

**JONAH**

The kitchen at night was a different country.

Jonah had worked in it for three years and never once been alone in it after dark—it belonged to suppers and mornings, to other people's laughter, to the safe noise of community. Now it was just the two of them and forty jars cooling on every flat surface, the storm pressing against the windows like a hand, and a single oil lamp Wren had found in the pantry throwing their shadows enormous against the wall.

She sat on the table's far end, feet tucked under her, an apple turning slowly in her fingers. He sat at the near end with the ledger closed in front of him, pretending there was anything left to record.

He should have walked to the village anyway. He should have stood in the rain for two hours rather than sit here in the lamplight with the woman he'd destroyed, breathing the same cinnamon-and-steam air. But his legs hadn't asked his opinion.

"The apples from the north rows," she said, conversationally, dangerously casual. "They're your grandmother's grafts. Did you know that?"

"I didn't."

"She wrote to my grandmother about them. About all of it." Wren turned the apple over. "I've been reading letters out of my grandmother's attic. They planned this, you know. The deed. The kitchen. Us." Her voice stayed level. "Two old women playing matchmakers with property law."

"I know," he said quietly. "I found the letters too. In her desk."

"And?" The word cracked like the apple skin under her thumb. "What did you think, reading them? That they were sweet? That they were right?"

The lamp flame leaned in the draft. Jonah watched it steadying itself and wished he could do the same.

"I thought they were kind," he said. "And wrong. And kind."

Wren laughed once, without any humor in it. "That's the most honest thing you've said since I got here."

"It's the only honest thing I have."

She set the apple down. For a long moment the only sound was the storm and the tick of cooling jars, and then she slid off the table and crossed the kitchen—not toward him, never toward him, but to the window, where she stood looking out at nothing, at the black lashing dark.

"One question," she said to the glass. "I get one, and you don't get to hide behind *it's complicated* or *you wouldn't understand*. One question, and you answer it true, or I walk out into that flood and take my chances."

His heart was suddenly very loud. "Ask it."

She turned. In the lamplight her face was stripped bare of everything she usually wore—the competence, the wry calm, the armor of being needed. What was underneath was twenty-two years old and standing in a train station holding a ticket she'd bought for a concert he never played.

"Did I matter?"

Three words. Ten years. The question he'd rehearsed answers to in a hundred sleepless kitchens, and every answer he'd ever drafted dissolved now, because none of them were big enough and all of them were lies of one size or another.

"Yes," he said.

The word came out wrecked. He heard it wrecked and kept going, because she deserved more than one word even if more was impossible.

"You mattered. You matter. There hasn't been a day—I baked bread through it, Wren. Three years of bread, because kneading dough is the only thing that shuts my head up, and my head has said your name in it every single morning." His hands had started, the fine tremor he hid so well, and he pressed them flat against the ledger to still them. "You mattered so much that losing you is the architecture of my entire life. Everything I built, I built around the hole."

The storm filled the silence after. Wren hadn't moved. Her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Then why—" she began, and stopped herself, jaw tightening. "No. That's not the one question. That's the thousand questions. I said one." She dragged a breath in through her nose. "You answered it true?"

"Every word."

"Then I'll take it." Her voice shook on the last word, and she hated that too; he could see her hating it. "For tonight, I'll take it. The rest—" She shook her head slowly. "The rest is another night. Maybe another year. But it's coming, Jonah. Whatever you're not saying, whatever sent you away without a word—you're going to tell me. Not because I demand it. Because keeping it is eating you alive, and I've watched you do the math on that for three weeks now."

He couldn't speak. He nodded.

She crossed back to the table—not the near end, not yet, but closer than before—and pulled the quilt from the bench where the supper circle kept it for cold evenings. She spread it over her shoulders and settled back onto her end of the table, and the message was clear as a signature: *here, but not there. Close, but not close.*

"Sleep," she ordered. "We start the second batch at dawn."

* * *

**WREN**

She didn't expect to sleep. The storm saw to that part, rattling the panes, and her own heart saw to the rest, replaying *yes, you mattered, you matter* until the words wore grooves in her.

But exhaustion is its own mercy, and somewhere past midnight the adrenaline burned down to embers. She watched him across the flour-dusted table—watched him finally stop pretending to read the ledger, watched him fold his arms on its surface and lower his head onto them like a boy falling asleep over homework. Within minutes his breathing went slow and deep.

Even asleep, his hands weren't still. They twitched against the wood, fingers moving through some silent music no one else could hear, and Wren lay with her cheek on her arm and studied them in the dying lamplight and felt the ground shift under everything she'd decided about him.

People who abandon you out of cruelty don't flinch when you say their name. People who abandon you out of cowardice don't look like they're carrying a cathedral's worth of guilt into every quiet room. She had spent ten years building a man out of her anger, and the man asleep across the table kept refusing to match the blueprint.

*Whatever sent you away,* she'd told him. *It's coming.*

She closed her eyes. The storm was easing, the rain gentling to a whisper, and the last thing she heard before sleep took her was the small, broken music of his hands moving on the wood—practicing, even now, even dreaming, some piece he'd never finished.

Outside, dawn was still hours away. Inside, forty jars of apples cooled in the dark, sealed tight, full of sweetness that wouldn't keep forever—and on the table between them, half-buried in flour dust, Jonah's phone lit once, silently, with a message he wouldn't see until morning:

*Mr. Sandoval — we need to talk about the orchard. Before someone else does.*
