## 2. The Man Who Moved Into the Cottage

The cottage next door had been empty for three years.

Silas knew this the way she knew most things about Port Alder: through careful observation and a notebook she kept in the glovebox of her rental car, labeled *Local Intel* in handwriting so neat it looked typeset. The cottage—sea-gray shingles, a porch with one sagging rail, a garden gone feral with rosemary—belonged to a widow named Mrs. Tavares who had moved inland to be near her daughter and had been letting the place sit like an unopened letter ever since.

So when Silas pulled up to her own rental on Tuesday morning—the first day of what Margot Vance's assistant had already termed "the countdown"—and saw a man on the neighboring porch carrying a single duffel bag, she did what any professional would do.

She updated her notebook.

*New tenant. Male, 30s. Arrived by foot from the ferry dock (no car). One bag only — either minimalist or broke. Will affect noise levels during wedding prep weeks 2–4.*

She was still writing when he noticed her, which meant she was still writing when he started walking over, which meant she had exactly four seconds to decide whether to close the notebook like a guilty teenager or hold her ground like a grown woman who took notes for legitimate reasons.

She closed it like a guilty teenager.

"Morning," the man said. He had the kind of voice that sounded like it had spent years being used sparingly—low, a little rough at the edges, the verbal equivalent of driftwood. Up close he was taller than he'd seemed on the porch, lean in the way of someone whose body had gotten used to doing without, with dark hair going silver early at the temples and eyes the flat gray-green of the harbor in October.

"Morning," Silas said, and deployed the smile she reserved for strangers who might become problems: warm enough to disarm, vague enough to end the conversation. "You must be Mrs. Tavares's new tenant."

"That's me." He didn't offer a name. Interesting. Most people offered their names the way they offered handshakes—automatically, defensively, before you could even ask. This man held his back like a card he wasn't ready to play.

"I'm in the blue cottage for the season," she said. "Work."

"What kind of work?"

"A wedding." She said it lightly, the way you'd say *a root canal* if you'd learned to make root canals sound charming.

Something happened to his face then. It was small—it was the kind of thing nobody would have caught unless they'd spent ten years studying faces through the filter of handwritten words—but Silas caught it. A flicker. Not distaste, exactly. Not pity. Something closer to *recognition*, as if she'd said *I collect sea glass* and he'd thought, *so do I.*

"A wedding," he repeated.

"In October. Here in town. You may have noticed the town is small enough that everyone will have noticed by Thursday."

He almost smiled. It was a good smile, or would have been, if he'd let it finish. Instead it got about seventy percent of the way there and then retreated, like a tide that had learned not to trust the shore. "I'll keep my curtains closed, then."

"That won't be necessary. I'm very quiet."

"You don't look quiet."

Silas blinked. "Excuse me?"

"You look like someone who makes lists." He nodded at the notebook still clutched in her hand, its cover now facing outward, the label *Local Intel* visible in all its damning neatness. "People who make lists are never quiet. They're just quiet out loud."

It was such a strange, specific thing to say—and such a familiar *way* to say it, sentences that doubled back on themselves like paths through dunes—that Silas felt something tilt slightly under her ribs, the way a floor tilts when a ship moves and your body hasn't caught up yet.

"Well," she said, because wit had temporarily abandoned her and professionalism was right there, reliable as ever. "Welcome to Port Alder. The water pressure in your cottage is temperamental. Turn the tap slowly and insult it gently and it behaves."

"Noted." And then, unexpectedly: "Thank you."

She was halfway to her own door when he added, "What's the bride like?"

Silas turned. He was still standing in the strip of gravel between the cottages, hands in the pockets of a canvas jacket that had seen more weather than most jackets see in a lifetime. It was an odd question. Neighbors asked *when's the wedding* or *big party?* They did not ask *what's the bride like* with the gravity of a man asking about a medical diagnosis.

"Demanding," Silas said. "Rich. In love, allegedly."

"Allegedly?"

"All love is alleged until proven in court. I've planned sixty-one weddings. I believe maybe nine of them."

His eyebrows went up. "And you keep doing it?"

"It pays well." She smiled the smile again, the one with nothing behind it. "And I'm very good at pretending."

This time he did smile—all the way, briefly, and it rearranged his whole face into someone younger, someone who might once have laughed easily on a boat deck somewhere—and said, "Aren't we all," and went inside, and shut the door, and left Silas standing in her own gravel holding a notebook full of notes about a man whose voice she couldn't stop hearing.

*Aren't we all.*

She stood there for a long moment, wind coming off the water, gulls conducting their usual committee meeting overhead, and told herself the tilt under her ribs was low blood sugar.

* * *

By Wednesday, the wedding supplies began arriving, and Silas's front yard became what the neighbors would later describe as "an event."

There were eleven crates of glassware from a rental house in Astoria. There were two hundred yards of silk ribbon in a color the vendor called *blush dawn* and Silas called, privately, *expensive beige.* There were forty-two hurricane lanterns, a folding screen painted with peonies, a cake stand the size of a bicycle wheel, and one crate marked FRAGILE–LIVE MATERIALS containing three hundred stems of ranunculus that needed water within the hour or would begin their public death.

Silas unloaded all of it herself. This was not necessary—she could have hired the boy from the hardware store, could have asked Margot's assistant to drive down—but there was a part of her that trusted no one else's hands with the objects of a perfect day, and a deeper part, which she did not examine, that liked the work itself: the weight of things, the order of them, the way chaos became inventory became plan became, eventually, beauty. Other people's perfect days were the only kind she believed in anymore, and she built them the way other people built cathedrals—one stone at a time, on her knees, alone.

She was on her knees, in fact, wedging the ranunculus crate open with a box cutter, when the neighbor appeared at the edge of her yard.

He didn't come in. He stood at the property line with two mugs of coffee in his hands, watching her wrestle the crate with the expression of a man watching a nature documentary in which the narrator has clearly made poor choices.

"Do you need—" he started.

"No," said Silas, at the exact moment the crate gave way, the box cutter skidded, and three hundred dollars' worth of flowers began sliding toward the gravel.

He crossed the yard in about four strides. He did not ask again. He simply put both mugs of coffee down on the porch rail—her porch rail, an act of territorial presumption she intended to object to later—and caught the crate's far end, steadying it while she rescued the runaway stems.

For a moment they knelt there together over the flowers, close enough that she could smell salt and coffee and, beneath it, something like engine oil, the ghost of machinery, boats.

"They're beautiful," he said, and his voice had changed—gone quieter, almost careful. "Ranunculus."

"You know flowers?"

"My mother grew them." He said it looking at the blooms, not at her. "On a terrace. In pots, because we didn't have ground. She said they were stubborn flowers. They bloom from the bottom up, all these layers, and each layer looks finished but there's always another underneath."

Silas's hands went still around a stem of coral-pink.

That was not the kind of thing men said. That was not even the kind of thing *people* said, not casually, not to a stranger kneeling in gravel. That was the kind of thing that got written down. That was—

*He describes things in layers. He notices how things are built. Last month's letter said: I think people are like tides, they seem simple until you realize the moon is involved.*

She straightened up too fast and heard her own spine pop.

"Thank you for the assist," she said briskly. "Coffee too. Although I should warn you, taking liberties with my porch rail is a slippery slope. Next you'll be borrowing my ladder. Then my hedge trimmers. By November we'll be common-law neighbors."

"Is that how it works?" He handed her a mug without being asked—presumptuous, presumptuous—and she took it because her hands wanted something to do. "Common-law neighbors?"

"There are statutes. Look it up."

He laughed. It was short and surprised, like the laugh had escaped custody, and Silas felt that tilt under her ribs again, stronger this time, and firmly told her ribs to get a grip.

They drank coffee standing among the crates. He didn't fill silence the way most people did—not with weather, not with small complaints about ferries or parking. He let it sit, and Silas, who could talk to anyone about anything for as long as a contract required, found that she didn't want to fill it either. The silence between them felt less like absence and more like a room neither of them had furnished yet.

"So," he said eventually. "Sixty-one weddings. What's the best one?"

"The best one?"

"The one you'd do again."

No one had ever asked her that. Clients asked about *their* wedding, endlessly, exclusively; colleagues asked about budgets and vendors; her mother asked when Silas would plan her own, in the tone of a woman asking when she'd stop disappointing the family. Nobody had ever asked which one *she'd* choose.

"A small one," she heard herself say, before the professional filter could catch it. "Fourteen guests. A fisherman's daughter married a marine biologist on a beach at six in the morning because that was the only hour the tide gave them. Nobody wore shoes. The mother of the groom cried so hard the vows had to pause twice, and nobody minded, and afterward they served chowder out of the back of a pickup truck." She stopped. "That's not a useful answer for you. Forget it."

"Why is it not useful?"

"Because it doesn't scale. You can't sell chowder out of a pickup truck to a Margot Vance."

"Margot Vance," he repeated, and this time the flicker across his face was different—harder, a door closing. "The bride. That's her name? Margot Vance."

"You know her?"

"No." Too fast. Definitely too fast. "Should I?"

"She's marrying a Reyes," Silas said, watching him the way she watched clients during toast rehearsals—for the tell, the flinch, the truth leaking out around the edges. "Of the Reyes Ferry Line. Old Port Alder money. Half the town will be at this wedding whether they're invited or not, just to be seen near it."

Something moved behind his eyes then, something large and slow, like a shape passing under the surface of dark water. When he spoke, his voice was level, but level the way a rope is level right before it snaps taut.

"And which half will you be in?"

"Neither," Silas said. "I'm staff. Staff is furniture that talks."

"That's a terrible way to think of yourself."

"It's an excellent way to think of myself. Furniture gets to go home at the end of the night. People have to stay and find out what the marriage actually turns into." She set down the mug. "Anyway. Thank you for the coffee and the flower rescue. I have a seating chart that hates me."

He stepped back, hands returning to his pockets, and for a second he looked like he wanted to say something else—something that was climbing toward his mouth with real effort—and then he swallowed it, whatever it was, and nodded.

"Good luck with the chart," he said. "Seat the difficult relatives near the exits."

"That's amateur thinking. You seat them near the bar. Exits are for running away. Bars are for staying put."

He paused at the property line and glanced back at her, and there it was again—that almost-recognition, that sense of a word on the tip of the tongue.

"You know," he said slowly, "someone told me once that the trick to any gathering is controlling where the light falls. Put the trouble in shadow and the hope in the spotlight, and nobody remembers what went wrong."

Silas's breath did something unauthorized.

Because she *had* written that. Three years ago. Page forty-something of a letter she'd signed with nothing at all, sent to a P.O. box, to a person identified only as *A*, in reply to his question about why his family's dinner parties always felt like funerals. *Control where the light falls,* she'd written. *Put the trouble in shadow and the hope in the spotlight. It works on weddings and it works on everything.*

Coincidence. It had to be coincidence. Phrases traveled; good advice metastasized; people repeated things they'd read somewhere and forgot where. The world was full of orphaned sentences wearing other people's coats.

But her heart was doing something unprofessional, hammering against the inside of her competence like a bird against glass, and the man on the property line was watching her with those gray-green harbor eyes as if he were waiting—patiently, hopefully, terribly—for her to say something.

"Sounds like a smart person," she managed.

"He had his moments," the man said quietly. And then he turned and walked back to his cottage, and his door closed behind him with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

That night Silas sat at her kitchen table with the seating chart, the lanterns, and a glass of wine she wasn't drinking, and did not think about him.

She thought about him extensively.

She thought about the way he'd said *ranunculus* like a word he'd handled before. The way he'd known about layers blooming from the bottom up. The way he'd flinched at Margot Vance's name and gone still at the name Reyes, stillness rippling off him like heat off summer asphalt. The way he'd quoted her own sentence back to her without knowing—or knowing?—and watched her face while he did it.

She got up, retrieved the archive box from the closet—the shoebox, actually, a battered old thing that had survived four apartments and one flood—and set it on the table. Inside: ten years of letters. His to her, hers to him, filed by month, tied in bundles with the same expensive beige ribbon she used on wedding programs because she'd run out of string once and never switched back.

She didn't reread them. She just rested her hand on top of the bundle, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal, and looked out the window at the neighboring cottage, where a single lamp burned behind drawn curtains.

*New tenant,* her notebook said. *Male, 30s. Minimalist or broke.*

Underneath, in handwriting even neater than usual, because precision was how she held fear at bay, she added:

*Possibly impossible. Investigate nothing. Ask nothing. You have a wedding to build and a life to exit and you did not move to the edge of the map to start wondering about strangers.*

She underlined *nothing* twice.

Then she turned off the lamp, and lay awake listening to the water, and somewhere around two in the morning admitted to the dark ceiling of a rented cottage in Port Alder that the stranger next door said *layers* the way her letter-writer said *layers*, and that she had recognized his voice the moment he'd said *aren't we all*—not the sound of it, but the shape of it, the architecture underneath.

Which was absurd. Which was impossible. Which meant nothing.

Across the strip of gravel, behind the drawn curtains, the lamp in the neighboring cottage stayed lit until nearly dawn—as if someone over there were also sitting up with a box of words, also telling himself it meant nothing, also failing completely to sleep.

But Silas didn't know that yet.

All she knew was that tomorrow she had to call Margot Vance about napkin folds, and the final guest list had arrived that evening by courier, and she hadn't opened it yet, and something in her gut—the same gut that had planned sixty-one flawless days—was telling her, quietly and with great authority, to brace.
