## 5. Coffee, No Names

### Silas

The trouble with a shared porch was that it invited conversation, and Silas Okonkwo had built her entire adult life on the principle that conversation was optional.

The cottage next door—her cottage, technically, since she'd rented both from Mrs. Ferreira for the season—had been empty for three years. She'd chosen it precisely because of that emptiness. The two places sat shoulder to shoulder above the harbor like old sisters, sharing one long wraparound porch divided by nothing more than a line of potted rosemary and an unspoken agreement about personal space. For three years, Silas had had all of it to herself: the left side, where the morning light came in gold and forgiving, and the right side too, when she felt greedy.

Then August Reyes had arrived with two duffel bags and a face like weather coming in off the water, and now there was a man on *her* porch every morning at seven-fifteen, drinking coffee he'd brought out in a chipped enamel mug, saying things like "Morning" and "Looks like rain later" as though they were people who did this.

They were not people who did this. Silas did not do this.

And yet on the fourth morning, she heard herself say, "You're brewing it wrong."

He looked up. He had the kind of face that took its time deciding whether to be amused. "Wrong how?"

"You're scalding it. You pour the water the second it boils." She nodded at the camp stove he'd set up by the railing, because apparently the cottage kitchen offended him somehow. "Let it come off the heat for thirty seconds first. Thirty-one if you're feeling generous."

"That's very specific."

"I'm a very specific person."

"So I'm learning." He poured the water anyway—after a pause that was either compliance or theater—and handed her a mug without asking if she wanted one, which should have annoyed her and instead felt like being caught.

She drank it. It was better. She hated that it was better.

### August

Here was the thing August Reyes knew about Silas Okonkwo, wedding planner to the wealthy and professionally invisible: she crossed her sevens.

Not all of them. Only sometimes—the way some people only bit their nails under stress. He'd watched her write in her planner on the porch that first week, her pen moving in quick decisive strokes, and there it was: a seven with a little crossbar, struck through like it was defending itself. His own sevens did the same thing. He'd learned it at nineteen, sitting on a cold dock with his hands too numb to hold the pen properly, copying the handwriting of the woman beside him because hers looked like what certainty must feel like.

He'd told himself it was coincidence. Lots of people crossed their sevens. But then Tuesday morning she'd written him a note—*borrowed your corkscrew, will return, S.O.*—and left it weighted under a stone on the porch rail, and he'd stood there holding it for a full minute like an idiot, because the *S.O.* had a period after the O, small and precise, exactly the way A.'s letters ended. Exactly the way her replies began.

*Dear A.* Period. Every month for ten years. *Dear A.*

He could not have explained why he didn't say anything. Cowardice, probably. Or something worse than cowardice: hope. Because if he said *I know you*, and he was wrong, he lost the porch mornings. And if he said *I know you*, and he was right, he might lose them anyway, because the whole architecture of their ten years was built on not knowing, and knowing was a wrecking ball dressed as a gift.

So he drank his coffee and said, "Margot Vance's fiancé is my cousin, you know."

Her mug paused halfway to her mouth. "I'd heard."

"Small town."

"Small peninsula," she corrected. "Smaller ferry schedule."

"You've met Theo?"

"At the venue walkthrough. He seems—" She searched for a diplomatic word. "Energetic."

"He's a golden retriever with a trust fund," August said, and was rewarded with something he suspected she rarely gave clients: a real laugh, short and startled out of her, quickly repossessed.

"He adores Margot," she said, recovering.

"He adores the idea of being adored. Margot's the ambitious one." He watched her file that away behind her eyes, cataloguing it somewhere between client intelligence and threat assessment. "You're good at your job."

"I'm aware."

"No, I mean—" He gestured vaguely at her, at the planner, at the general air of a woman who could organize a hurricane into labeled boxes. "Most people do their jobs. You *are* yours."

Something flickered across her face—not pleasure. Something closer to grief wearing pleasure's coat. "Thank you," she said, in the tone of someone accepting a compliment and an insult in the same envelope.

### Silas

By the second week, she had developed a system, because Silas had a system for everything, including the things that were supposed to be spontaneous. Porch coffee happened between 7:15 and 8:00. Topics were limited to: weather, the wedding, the town, the ferry schedule (a rich vein; he had opinions), and Mrs. Ferreira's ongoing war with the raccoons. Topics explicitly excluded: his family beyond the cousin, her past beyond the résumé, and anything that required the word *actually*.

It was working beautifully until Thursday, when he said, "Do you ever write letters? Actual ones. On paper."

Silas's pen stopped mid-stroke.

This was the danger of systems: they never accounted for ambush. "Why do you ask?"

"No reason. The post office here still has the pneumatic tube thing from the fifties. There's a plaque. I found it fascinating and had no one to tell."

"There's a plaque for a pneumatic tube."

"There's a plaque for everything in Port Alder. There's a plaque on the fish market commemorating a cod of unusual size."

"A cod of unusual size."

"Ninety-four pounds. 1971. The town has never recovered."

She laughed again—that same startled sound, like a bird getting loose indoors—and covered it by writing something in her planner that she would later have no memory of writing. "To answer your question: yes. I write letters."

"Anyone interesting?"

"Someone interesting," she said, and immediately wished she'd said *no one*. "A friend. We've never met."

"Never?" He looked genuinely astonished, which was either excellent acting or genuine astonishment. "Ten minutes ago you were telling me the correct thirty-second window for coffee water, and you have a friend you've never met?"

"Some friendships work better at a distance."

"Like pen pals?"

"Like—" She considered. "Like lighthouses. Two lights that signal each other across the water. They don't need to be in the same room to know the other one's there."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed registers, dropped down into something careful. "That's a beautiful way to put it."

"It's a practical way to put it."

"It's both. That's the trick of you, I think." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't feel light at all, and Silas had the sudden vertiginous sense of standing on a dock at night, nineteen years old, while a stranger asked her what she was running from and she'd answered *nothing* and he'd said *that's the saddest nothing I've ever heard.*

She had never told anyone that story. She had told A. the entire story, in her third letter, back when the letters were new.

"Your coffee's getting cold," she said.

* * *

### August

He was going to tell her. He decided this approximately nine times over the following week, usually at night, staring at the ceiling of the cottage that smelled faintly of her lavender sachets through the shared wall, composing the sentence. *Funny story. Do you remember a dock, ten years ago? Do you remember a boy who couldn't go home?*

Nine times he composed it. Ten mornings in a row he failed to deliver it, because delivering it meant explaining Edmund, and the company, and the name Reyes, which functioned in this town less like a name than a stain. And it meant explaining the letters, and the letters meant explaining that he'd known her for a decade while she'd known a signature, and there was no version of that confession that didn't sound like theft.

Instead he collected evidence, the way he'd once collected shipping manifests looking for discrepancies. Her handwriting: confirmed, the crossed sevens, the period after initials. Her laugh: confirmed, identical to the one he could hear in her replies, the one that came through even in ink. Her habits: she read with her index finger tracking the lines, just slightly, like a child—which matched the single detail she'd ever given him about herself, early on, before the letters got careful. *I still read with my finger,* she'd written, *don't tell anyone.* He had told no one for ten years. It was the easiest secret he'd ever kept, because keeping it felt like holding something of hers in trust.

Friday morning she came out with two mugs instead of one and handed him one without ceremony, and he understood that he had just been promoted to some category that Silas Okonkwo did not officially recognize.

"Fair warning," she said. "Today I'm in a mood. Margot wants swans."

"Swans."

"Live swans. At the rehearsal dinner. On the harbor."

"The harbor where the ferry comes in."

"She feels the swans say 'timeless romance.'"

"The swans will say 'hazard to navigation.'"

"Thank you." She pointed her pen at him. "That's exactly what I needed someone to say. My staff would have said 'what a vision.'"

"Where are your staff?"

"Mainland. I work alone up here." She said it lightly, but he heard the seam in it. Alone was a word she used like armor—worn so long it had molded to her shape.

"You always work alone?"

"I prefer it. Clients can't disappoint each other if I stand between them." She caught herself, frowned slightly, as if surprised by her own honesty. "That sounded cynical."

"It sounded experienced."

"Cynicism is just experience with the romance filed off."

"And what's underneath the filing?"

She looked at him then—a long look, assessing, the look she probably gave venues and vendors and brides who were lying about their guest counts. Whatever she saw made her decide to answer honestly, or honestly-adjacent. "Underneath? Someone who's very tired and very good at pretending otherwise. Your turn. What's underneath the man who moved to a cottage at the end of the world with two duffel bags?"

August held his coffee and watched a gull harass the fish market and thought: *now*. Say it now.

"I'm hiding," he said.

The honesty landed between them like a dropped plate. She didn't flinch, didn't fill the silence with comfort, which he appreciated more than he could say. She just waited.

"From what?"

"My family. Sort of. From what my family *is*." He turned the mug in his hands. "There's a company. Ferry line. It's been in the family three generations, and there's a question about who it belongs to, and I came here to figure out if I want to fight for it or let it go." He looked up. "That's the honest version. The detailed version requires more coffee and fewer mornings."

"I respect a teaser trailer," Silas said, and he laughed, and something in his chest unclenched that had been clenched since the ferry docked.

But she didn't ask for the detailed version. Not that morning, not the next. And he noticed—he noticed everything about her now, the way you notice weather—that she never offered her own detailed version either. They were two people trading coins with the faces worn smooth, spending currency that had passed through so many hands neither could prove whose portrait it originally bore.

### Silas

On Sunday she wrote her monthly letter.

She wrote it at the kitchen table with the window open to the porch, and she was aware—acutely, uncomfortably aware—of the man twenty feet away reading a book with his finger tracking the lines, and she was aware that her letter was different this month, and she could not have said how until she reread it:

*Dear A. — This month I met someone who brews coffee wrong and fixes it when told. He has opinions about swans. I keep almost telling you things and realizing I already have, in these letters, over years, and now I'm telling them twice, to two audiences, and I don't know which one of you is the real friend and which one is becoming one. Don't answer that. —S.*

She read it back. She did not cross out the last line, though her pen hovered over it for a long time.

Because here was the thing she would not admit, not to A., not to the planner in her head who ran risk assessments on everything including sunsets: the letters had started to feel like a rehearsal. Ten years of practicing intimacy at a safe distance, and now here was a live audience member, sitting on her porch, drinking her coffee, laughing at her jokes—or making her laugh, she was losing track—and every morning the distance got smaller and her hands shook more.

She sealed the envelope. Walked it to the post office with the pneumatic tube plaque. Dropped it in.

When she came back, August was on the porch holding an envelope of his own, stamped and addressed, turning it over in his hands.

"Mail day?" she asked, too casually.

"Monthly ritual," he said. "Yours too, I saw. Same day, actually."

"What a coincidence."

"Mm." He looked at the envelope, then at her, and there was something in his expression she couldn't parse—something warm and stricken at once, like a man watching his own secret walk around in daylight wearing a disguise. "Silas. Can I ask you something strange?"

"You can ask. I reserve the right to deflect."

"When you write to your friend—the lighthouse one." He hesitated. "Do you sign your name?"

The question went through her like a needle finding a nerve she'd forgotten she had. "No," she said. "Do you?"

He smiled, and it was the saddest smile she'd ever seen on a man holding good news. "No," he said. "Guess we're both careful."

"Careful," she agreed, and went inside, and stood with her back against the door, heart hammering, telling herself the shaking in her hands was caffeine.

It was not caffeine. It was the specific terror of a woman who has spent ten years building a room with no doors, hearing a knock.

* * *

### August

Monday morning, Silas didn't come out at seven-fifteen. Or eight. At nine-thirty she emerged with her planner clutched like a shield and announced that Margot had requested a tasting menu revision and she'd be at the venue all day, and she said it all in one breath, and she did not meet his eyes.

August sat on the porch after she left and turned his own envelope over and over.

He had written his October letter the night before, and it had been different too, and he knew exactly how: he had nearly signed it. His pen had touched the paper after the final dash and hovered there, and he'd written *A* and then stopped, because signing it meant the letters could end, and ten years of Tuesdays—of her replies arriving like clockwork, funny and fierce and quietly devastating—was not a thing he was prepared to gamble on a porch acquaintance, however much the porch acquaintance felt like coming home.

Except it wasn't an acquaintance. That was the joke neither of them was telling. He'd known her longer than anyone alive except his mother. He knew she cried at other people's weddings and pretended it was allergies. He knew she'd once wanted to study marine biology and chose logistics instead because logistics couldn't break your heart. He knew her favorite word was *nevertheless* and she used it like a life raft.

And she knew him. Whoever A. was, she knew him—knew the shape of his failures, the ledger of his shames, the year his father died and the letters got shorter and hers got gentler, as if she could tell through the paper.

The problem, he thought, watching her car disappear down the road toward the venue, was geometry. Two truths, ten years apart, occupying the same space. If he pulled one thread—*I am A*—the whole fabric came undone, and she might see the pattern at last, or she might see only the hole.

His phone buzzed. Edmund.

*August. Lunch this week. There are matters to discuss regarding your stake, and regarding your living arrangements. I hear you've taken a cottage in Port Alder. Charming town. Small. People talk.*

August stared at the screen for a long moment, the sea wind moving through the rosemary, the empty porch suddenly feeling less like a refuge and more like a stage.

He typed back: *What kind of matters?*

Edmund's reply came fast, the way bad news does: *The kind that concern your pretty neighbor. I'll bring the details. Bring your pen.*

August looked up at Silas's door, closed and quiet, her crossed sevens locked inside along with ten years of his own words, and felt the first cold thread of the season come in off the water.
