## 7. The Rehearsal Dinner Disaster

The call came at 4:17 in the morning, which Silas would later decide was the universe's way of telling her it had opinions about her career choices.

"Silas Lockridge." She answered on the second ring, already sitting up, already reaching for the notebook she kept on the nightstand like other people kept water glasses.

"Ms. Lockridge, this is Dev from Tide & Thyme Catering." His voice had the particular wet gravel of a man who had been crying or about to be. "I'm so sorry. I'm so—my walk-in died. The compressor. Everything's gone. The oysters, the beef tenderloin, the—the lemon possets, Ms. Lockridge. Two hundred of them."

Silas closed her eyes and did the math. Rehearsal dinner: tomorrow night. Guest count: one hundred and forty. Venue: the Harbor House, whose kitchen she had inspected personally and found adequate but not generous. Margot Vance's expectations: stratospheric, and climbing.

"I understand," she said, because the first rule of crisis management was to let people believe you were calm even when you were composing your own obituary. "Dev. Listen to me. Do you have any product left at all?"

"Some of the cold stuff. The crudo survived."

"Then you're not dead. You're wounded." She was already out of bed, bare feet on cold floorboards, flipping to a fresh page. "Here's what happens now. You call every kitchen within forty miles that has refrigeration and capacity. I'll call the ones you're too proud to call. We rebuild the menu around whatever we can source by noon. And Dev—you will call me back with an inventory list before seven, or I will come to your kitchen myself and read your freezer contents aloud at the wedding."

A watery laugh. "Yes, ma'am."

She hung up and stood in the dark of her rented room above the chandlery, notebook open, pen moving. By 4:40 she had three columns going: *Have / Can Get / Impossible*. By 5:15 she had dressed, made coffee in a pot older than most of her clients' marriages, and stepped out onto the porch into a morning so gray and soft it looked like the town had been wrapped in wool.

August Reyes was already on his porch next door, mug in hand, watching the fog eat the dock.

"You look," he said, "like a woman who's received bad news before sunrise."

"My caterer's walk-in died overnight. Two hundred lemon possets, gone to the great refrigerator in the sky." She sat down on her step with her mug. "One hundred and forty guests. Tomorrow."

"That is bad news." He took a sip. "What's the menu?"

She told him. He listened the way he listened to everything—with a stillness that made her feel heard in a way she found slightly dangerous—and then he said, "Tide & Thyme?"

"You know them?"

"Everyone knows Dev. He trained at the Anchorage Hotel before it closed. Half the kitchens in this town have somebody who came up under him or under Chef Ibarra at the Net Shed." He turned the mug in his hands. "What do you actually need? Not the dream menu. The real one."

Silas studied him. This was the thing about August that kept snagging her attention like a loose thread: he asked operational questions. He thought in contingencies. Most men who looked like him—heir-shaped, handsome in a weathered way, with hands that suggested they'd done actual work lately—asked nothing at all and expected the world to arrange itself.

"Protein for a hundred and forty," she said. "Dessert for a hundred and forty. Something hot, because Margot Vance believes a rehearsal dinner without a hot course is a suggestion, not a meal."

"The Net Shed can do fish. Rosa Ibarra owes me a favor or two." He said it lightly, but something flickered behind it—a shadow of obligation, of history. "And Marta at the bakery does a cardamom cake that would make your possets weep with envy. If there are possets left to weep."

"You'd do that?" She caught herself. "I mean—I could pay. Whatever they need. My client has a budget that could fund a small navy."

"I know Rosa. It's not a favor I'd be spending, exactly. More like... calling in a debt that's been sitting around gathering interest." He smiled, and it transformed his whole face, and Silas looked away first, down at her notebook, where she wrote *Net Shed — fish — A knows Rosa??* and then, after a moment, crossed out the last part.

"Why do you know everyone's debts in this town?" she asked. "You've been here three weeks."

"I told you. I grew up on the ferries." He said it the way he always said it—true, incomplete, a door opened exactly one inch. "You spend enough summers riding the same route, you learn who feeds people and who doesn't."

* * *

By nine that morning, Silas understood that she was witnessing something close to a miracle, if miracles wore aprons and smelled like woodsmoke and garlic.

Rosa Ibarra of the Net Shed was sixty-ish, built like a bollard, and had greeted August at her kitchen door with a shout of *"¡El muchacho del ferry!"* and a hug that lifted him half off the ground. Then she'd fed them both grilled sardines at a prep table while negotiating terms with Silas in a manner Silas could only describe as naval.

"One hundred and forty covers, tomorrow night, at the Harbor House. I don't have staff for that."

"I'll give you my crew," Silas said. "Two servers, plus me. I've worked a line."

Rosa raised one eyebrow at August. "Does she cook?"

"She organized a benefit dinner for three hundred once," August said. "In Seattle. Raised—" He stopped. Blinked. "How did I know that?"

Silas's pen froze over her notebook.

It was on the tip of her tongue—*because I told you, because you asked me once, in a letter dated March four years ago, whether I'd ever pulled off anything impossible, and I told you about the benefit dinner for the maritime museum, and you wrote back that you'd read the whole letter twice.* It rose in her throat like a tide. She swallowed it.

"Lucky guess," she said. "It was in a profile somewhere. Nobody reads those."

"Huh," said August, and looked at her a moment too long, and Rosa looked between them with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she intended to bring up later.

The deal was struck: black cod, roasted over alder wood; a potato dish Rosa called *papas con historia* ("potatoes with history," August translated, "which means she puts bacon in them"); the surviving crudo stretched with local oysters from a farm August knew up-channel. For dessert, Marta's cardamom cake, three tiers, delivered warm.

It cost Silas eighty percent of the contingency budget and all of her pride, and it would work. It would more than work. It would be, she admitted as she walked back along the waterfront with August beside her, better than what Dev had planned.

"You saved this wedding," she said. "You know that."

"I made some phone calls."

"You called in favors across an entire town's worth of kitchens in four hours. On a Tuesday." She shook her head. "Who *are* you?"

The question came out lighter than she meant it—or heavier. She couldn't tell which. August slowed his steps, and for a moment the fog seemed to gather around him like it was waiting for his answer too.

"Someone who used to love this place," he said finally. "Before it got complicated."

There it was again—that door, open one inch. And there it was again, in Silas's chest: the almost-confession, pressing upward. *I know why it got complicated. You told me. In letters. Eleven years of them, if you count the first one you sent to a PO box you found in the back of a wedding magazine.*

Because here was the truth she carried everywhere like a stone in her pocket: ten years ago, a nineteen-year-old boy had met a girl on this dock—she'd never confirmed it was this dock, but the details in his first letter matched Port Alder so precisely she'd stopped doubting it—and he'd written to her afterward, unsigned, and she had written back, also unsigned, because signing felt like stepping off a cliff, and somehow neither of them had ever stepped off, and the correspondence had become the truest relationship of her life, which was either romantic or pathetic depending on the hour, and the boy had grown into a man who now lived thirty feet away from her and knew her coffee order and her handwriting slant and apparently her résumé, and she could not say a word about any of it.

"A," she almost said. Just the letter. Just to see his face.

Instead she said, "Complicated how?"

He was quiet for three porch-lengths. "Family business," he said. "Literally."

* * *

The rehearsal dinner itself went off, against all odds and several acts of God, beautifully.

The Harbor House glowed. Rosa's black cod arrived golden-crusted and fragrant, and the potatoes with history caused a minor religious experience at table six. Marta's cardamom cake disappeared in eleven minutes flat. Silas ran the floor in a black dress with her sleeves rolled, refilling wine, redirecting traffic, smoothing a seating dispute between Margot's aunts with the diplomatic skill of a UN negotiator, and only checking the door every ninety seconds or so, which was not suspicious at all.

August had come as a guest—Margot's fiancé Theo was his cousin, and the Reyes name sat at the head table like a held breath—but he spent most of the evening near the kitchen pass, handing plates, refilling water, quietly fixing things before anyone noticed they were broken. Twice their hands touched over a tray. Twice Silas felt it in her sternum like a struck bell.

Margot noticed everything. That was the trouble with Margot. She was not stupid; she was worse than stupid, she was perceptive, and she watched Silas and August orbit each other all evening with the patient interest of a cat watching a drawer it intends to open later.

At one point she drifted over to the pass, champagne in hand, and said, sweetly, "You two move well together. Like you've done this a hundred times."

"We haven't," Silas said.

"Mm." Margot smiled with perfect white teeth. "Funny. You finish each other's sentences anyway."

Silas laughed the professional laugh, the one that lived entirely in her mouth, and moved away to rescue the cake knife from a toddler.

Later—much later, past midnight, after the last guest had gone and Rosa's crew had loaded the van and the Harbor House smelled of alder smoke and victory—Silas found August on the venue's back deck, looking out at the black water. The fog had returned. Somewhere out in it, a buoy bell rang, patient as a heartbeat.

"Hey," he said, without turning. "We did it."

"*You* did it. I just wrote the checks."

"You wrote the lists." He turned, and in the low light from the deck door his face was open in a way she'd rarely seen—tired, pleased, unguarded. "My mother used to say the person with the list is the one really running things. Everyone else is just improvising with confidence."

Silas's heart did something complicated. Because she knew that saying. She knew it the way you know a scar—she had *written* it, four years ago, in reply to a letter where he'd described his mother, and she'd offered the line about lists, and he had written back: *I think about that every time someone hands me a clipboard.*

"August," she said.

"Yeah?"

Tell him. Tell him now, on this deck, with the buoy bell keeping time. Say, *I'm the one who writes back. I'm the S. at the end of every letter you've ever gotten. I have been for ten years, and I didn't tell you because telling you meant you could leave, and I couldn't survive you leaving twice.*

"Your cousin's wedding," she said instead. "Tomorrow. The timeline's tight. Ceremony at four, boats need to be staged by three, and if Edmund Reyes gives another speech I want a hard stop ready."

Something in his face dimmed—one candle among many going out, barely noticeable unless you were watching, and she was watching, and she saw it, and she hated herself with a thoroughness that would have impressed Margot.

"Right," he said. "The timeline."

"August—"

"No, it's good. You're good at this." He smiled the polite smile, the one that lived entirely in his mouth, and she recognized it because it was hers. "Get some sleep, Silas. Big day tomorrow."

He went inside. The deck door clicked shut. The buoy bell rang out in the fog, and Silas stood alone on the deck with ten years of unsent truths stacked in her chest like cordwood, and understood that she had just done the thing she was best at in the whole world:

She had managed the event perfectly and let the person go.

She pressed her palm flat against the railing, cold and salt-rough, and whispered to the water—to the dock down the shore where a nineteen-year-old boy had once watched a girl load wedding crates and worked up the courage to write to a stranger—"Soon. I'll tell him soon."

The water said nothing. Water never did. But far out in the fog, the last ferry of the season sounded its horn, long and low, like something counting down.

And in the harbor office window behind her, reflected in the glass, she caught the shape of Margot Vance, still awake, still watching, phone glowing in her hand.

Silas told herself she hadn't seen it.

That was getting harder to do.
