## 12. What Silas Won't Plan

The tasting menu for the Vance-Reyes rehearsal dinner arrived at my cottage on a Thursday morning, printed on heavy cream cardstock, with a note in Margot's looping hand: *Final decisions by Friday, darling. Don't disappoint me.*

There were eleven exclamation points in that sentence if you counted the ones implied by the pressure of the cardstock. I counted. Counting was what I did. I had built a career on the assumption that any problem, properly itemized, could be flattened into a checklist and solved before lunch.

I made coffee, sat down at my kitchen table with the menu and a red pen, and got exactly four lines in before I found the landmine.

*Seating adjustment, per Ms. Vance:* the note read, tucked between the entrée selections and the wine pairings like a snake coiled in a bread basket. *August Reyes will be seated at Table Three, with the Harrowgate party. Ms. Vance requests that he be introduced to the room as "the family's prodigal cousin — we're so pleased he could tear himself away."*

I read it twice. Then a third time, the way you reread a sentence hoping it will rearrange itself into something kinder. It didn't.

Table Three was the Harrowgate table. The Harrowgates had financed half the ferry company's expansion in the nineties and had spent the two decades since behaving like the money had come with a deed to the town. Margot wanted August seated among them — his uncle's investors, his uncle's allies — and introduced with a joke that would land like a slap delivered in a warm voice. *Prodigal cousin.* *Tear himself away.* It would get a laugh. It would get exactly the laugh Margot wanted, and August would have to sit there and wear it, at his own family's table, in the town he'd come home to fight for.

I put down the pen.

This is the part where I should tell you what I did next, and the honest answer is: for a long time, nothing. I refilled my coffee. I watered the rosemary on the windowsill, which did not need watering. I alphabetized my ribbon samples, which is a thing I do when my hands need to be busy so my brain can pretend it isn't thinking.

Because here was the arithmetic, and I have never in my life been able to stop doing arithmetic. The Vance wedding was the fee. The fee was the exit. One flawless event, one final invoice, and I could close the business, pay off the last of my mother's medical debts, and become a person who answered letters on a porch instead of crises on a phone. Ten years of orchestrating other people's perfect days, and this was the door out. All I had to do was seat one man at the wrong table and let a room full of strangers laugh at him.

I thought about August on his porch, the morning after the storm, hands wrapped around a mug he'd brought over because mine had chipped and he'd noticed. I thought about the way he said *anyway* at the ends of sentences, like the word was a lifeboat. I thought about a decade of Tuesdays, though I would not have admitted that to anyone, least of all myself.

Then I thought about the laugh.

* * *

Margot received me in the great room of the Vance house at eleven sharp, which meant I stood in the marble foyer for eleven minutes while someone named Petra informed me, twice, that Ms. Vance would be right down. The house smelled like money trying to smell like the sea — candles called something like *Driftwood Reverie* burning in every room, fighting a losing war against the fact that the actual sea was four hundred yards away and free.

"Silas!" Margot swept in wearing cashmere the color of a storm warning. "Tell me you love the menu. I know the seating note is *bold*, but honestly, someone has to make that man feel welcome, and a joke is the fastest way. People relax when they laugh."

"People relax," I said, "when they're not the punchline."

She waved this away with a hand heavy enough with rings to constitute a weather event. "August has no sense of humor about himself. It's his worst quality. This will be *good* for him. Edmund thinks so too."

There it was. Edmund thinks so too. I filed that where I filed everything I learned about the Reyes family, in a drawer that had gotten uncomfortably full lately.

"Margot," I said, and I heard my own voice do the thing it does — the calm, reasonable, I'm-on-your-side register I'd spent a decade perfecting, the voice that made clients feel handled. "Let's talk about what this seating does to the room."

"It puts the *fun* people together."

"It puts August Reyes at a table of his uncle's investors, introduced with a joke at his expense, in front of the town where he grew up. He'll smile. He'll be gracious. He'll make himself the punchline before anyone else can, because that's what people do when they've been taught that their family name is a debt they're always paying down." I heard myself and stopped, briefly startled by how much I knew about it. "And then every person in that room will spend the rest of the evening performing sympathy at him, and sympathy is the worst possible thing to serve at a dinner. It curdles."

Margot's smile stayed on but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond. "Silas. I hired you to plan a wedding, not to psychoanalyze my guest list."

"You hired me to make your wedding flawless. That's what I'm doing. A room that laughs at one guest is not a flawless room. It's a room with a crack in it, and cracks propagate. By the toasts, half the table will be uncomfortable, a quarter will be performing delight, and your grandmother — who I've met, and who misses nothing — will have decided the whole evening was cruel, and she will be right."

"Edmund specifically requested—"

"I don't work for Edmund."

The words were out of my mouth before I'd fully decided to say them, and they came out quieter and harder than anything else I'd said. Margot blinked. In ten years, I had never once said *I don't work for* anyone. I worked for whoever was paying, that was the entire architecture of my life. I was a fixer, a smoother, a woman who made other people's visions real and left no fingerprints of her own. My name appeared in thank-you notes as *our wonderful planner, Silas — she thought of everything!* and never in a single memory of any wedding I'd made.

"Excuse me?" Margot said.

"I work for you," I said. "And I'm telling you, as the person you're paying to protect this evening: this seating chart will hurt it. And it will hurt a man who has done nothing to you."

"He's *difficult*," Margot said, and now the cashmere warmth dropped away entirely. "He's been back three months and he's already — people say he's asking questions. About the company. At the *harbormaster's office*, Silas. My fiancé's family is in a delicate position, and August is making waves, and Edmund says the kindest thing we can do is fold him back in gently. Make him feel like family. This *is* gentle."

I thought about deferred maintenance. I didn't know about it yet, not concretely — that would come later, in a boathouse, in a folder — but I knew the shape of it the way you know the shape of a thing behind a curtain. Edmund Reyes wanted August seated at a table of investors with a joke hung around his neck, and Edmund Reyes did not do anything without a reason, and none of his reasons had ever been kindness.

"Margot," I said. "I've planned two hundred and eleven weddings."

"Two hundred and— you *count*?"

"I count everything. It's the job. And in two hundred and eleven weddings, I have learned exactly one law: whatever is true between people before the wedding gets louder at the wedding. If this family is at war, the wedding won't paper over it. It will hand everyone ammunition and a captive audience." I held her eyes. "I won't seat him at Table Three with that introduction. I'll seat him at the family table, where he belongs by blood, and I'll give him an introduction so warm it would be strange to laugh at him. That's the version of this evening that works. That's the version I'll build."

Margot was quiet for a moment, which with Margot was more dangerous than shouting.

"You understand," she said finally, sweetly, "that the fee is contingent on my satisfaction."

"I understand the fee is contingent on my satisfaction," I said. "You wrote it into the contract yourself. Clause fourteen. *Planner's deliverables subject to client approval of event design.* I'm telling you now, formally, so there's no surprise later: I do not approve of Table Three, and I will not execute it."

"You'd walk away? From the biggest event of your career? Over a *seating chart*?"

"It's never about the seating chart," I said. "It's about what the seating chart is *for*."

* * *

I walked back along the bluff road because I couldn't stand the idea of being in a car with myself yet. The wind was up, the kind of bright cold wind that makes Port Alder look like a watercolor someone left out in the sun. The ferry was at the dock, loading crates of off-season wine, and I stood at the rail of the overlook and watched it and did the arithmetic again, because I couldn't help it.

Without the Vance fee, there was no exit. There was another year of this — of being the woman who thought of everything and stood for nothing. Of answering letters on Tuesdays and never saying who I was, because who I was had gotten so small it seemed rude to mention.

And yet my hands weren't shaking. That was the strange thing. I had just, by any reasonable estimate, set fire to the only door out of my own life, and my hands were steady as a surgeon's. There was a person in me — there had apparently always been a person in me — who, once she finally said a true thing out loud, found that true things were easier to carry than the other kind. Lighter. Like the difference between hauling a trunk and carrying a letter.

"You're doing the face," said a voice behind me, and there was August, hands in his jacket pockets, windblown, a paper bag from the bakery dangling from one wrist. "The one where you've won an argument and feel terrible about it."

"I haven't won anything. I may have just lost my entire year."

"Margot Vance is not a year-loser. She's a grudge-holder. Those are different animals." He held out the bag. "Cinnamon knot. The bakery woman said to tell you the cardamom ones are back and she's holding two under the counter like contraband."

I took the bag because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. "How do you know I argued with anyone?"

"Because you came down the bluff road instead of the coast road, and the bluff road is for people who need the walk to finish becoming angry." He leaned on the rail beside me, and for a while we watched the ferry pull out, running lights blinking against the gray water, and I felt the specific comfort of standing next to someone who reads you correctly and doesn't make a meal of it.

"I told a client no," I said. "A real no. A no with consequences."

"How'd it feel?"

I started to say *terrible*, because that was the correct answer, the professional answer, the answer of a woman who understood that her entire livelihood depended on never making anyone uncomfortable. But the wind was cold and the ferry was gone and August was looking at the water, not at me, which made honesty cheap in the best way.

"Like taking off a coat I forgot I was wearing," I said.

He turned then, and there was something in his face — recognition, or the ghost of it, the thing I kept almost-seeing and refusing to name. "Yeah," he said quietly. "It does feel like that."

We walked back toward the cottages, and I told myself the warmth in my chest was the cinnamon knot. I told myself a great many things. And when I got home, there was an envelope on my table that hadn't been there that morning — cream cardstock, Margot's looping hand, one line only:

*Friday. My house. Edmund would like a word with you privately.*

I stood in my kitchen holding the card, and somewhere out on the water the last ferry of the season sounded its horn, long and low, like a warning arriving early.
