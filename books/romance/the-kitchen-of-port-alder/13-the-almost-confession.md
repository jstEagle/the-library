## 13. The Almost Confession

### Silas

The dock at dusk was the only place in Port Alder that Silas had never tried to organize.

She noticed this on Thursday evening, standing at its far end with a clipboard she'd forgotten she was holding. Everything else in her orbit had a list attached to it—the wedding had forty-one line items in a spreadsheet color-coded by panic level, the cottage had a chore rotation she'd invented for a household of one, even her mornings had a sequence (kettle, porch, two pages of the novel she wasn't writing, coffee gone cold while she reread the same paragraph). But the dock resisted her. The planks were uneven. The gulls had no schedule. The water did what it wanted.

She'd come down to check the tide tables for the ferry schedule—Margot's seating chart depended on which guests would arrive by boat—and had instead found herself standing still for eleven unbroken minutes, which was, for Silas Okonkwo, roughly equivalent to a religious experience.

"You're going to fall in doing that."

August's voice came from behind her, easy and unhurried. She turned. He was carrying two mugs, which was becoming a habit she refused to examine too closely. His hair was doing the thing it did in salt air, and he had sawdust on one forearm from whatever mysterious project he'd been sanding all afternoon on his porch.

"I wasn't doing anything," she said.

"Exactly. You were doing nothing so hard I thought you might hurt yourself."

She accepted the mug. It was coffee, black, no sugar—correct, she realized, and then hated that she noticed. She'd mentioned exactly once, weeks ago, in some throwaway sentence about how her mother drank hers. He'd remembered. People didn't remember things like that about her. People remembered that she was efficient. People remembered that she could get a florist in Ketchikan on the phone at midnight.

They stood shoulder to shoulder at the rail. The sun was going down in the theatrical way it did here, all amber and long shadows, as if the town had hired a lighting designer. The ferry was gone for the night; the water lay flat and pewter-colored, holding the last of the light.

"Margot wants lanterns on the dock," Silas said, because silence had a way of making her say true things, and true things were dangerous. "Two hundred of them. I told her the fire marshal would have opinions."

"What do you want?"

The question landed strangely. She looked at him. He wasn't smiling the way he usually did when he asked things like that—half a joke, an escape hatch built in. He was just looking at her, waiting, as if the answer mattered.

"I want the fire marshal to have opinions," she said. "I want there to be a rule I can follow."

"You don't, though." He said it gently, not as an accusation. "I've watched you. You follow rules like they're a disguise."

Silas laughed, because it was either that or drop her mug into the harbor. "That's absurd."

"Is it?"

The gulls argued about something. She took a sip of coffee and bought herself three seconds.

"I've been thinking," August said, and something in his voice shifted—lowered, the way a person's voice does when they're stepping off a ledge and trying to sound casual about it. "About how long it's been since I could talk to someone. Really talk."

"Margot's cousin seems chatty enough."

"He's a bore and you know it." He turned to face the water, and she watched his profile go quiet. "There's someone I've been writing to. For a long time."

The world did not stop. The gulls did not fall silent. The tide kept doing its patient work against the pilings. But inside Silas's chest, something went very still and very bright, the way a room goes when lightning fills it.

"Writing," she said. Her voice came out level. Ten years of practice, that voice.

"Letters. Since I was nineteen." He was looking at the water, not at her, and she was grateful, because her face was doing something she couldn't control. "I met someone once. Here, actually. On this dock. And I never told them who I was, and then I couldn't, because the longer it went the worse the truth got. So I wrote. And they wrote back. Every month. For ten years." He huffed a laugh, embarrassed. "I know how it sounds."

"How does it sound?" she asked, because she needed to hear him say it.

"Like the only honest thing I've ever done."

### August

He had rehearsed this. Not the words—August Reyes had never once in his life successfully rehearsed words—but the shape of it. He'd stood in front of his bathroom mirror that morning, toothbrush in hand, and thought: *today*. The storm had cracked something open between them last week; she'd fallen asleep on her own couch mid-sentence, and he'd covered her with the good blanket and stood there like an idiot watching her breathe, and he'd understood that he could not carry this one more season. The letters were going to come up. Either he told her, or they surfaced some other way, and he'd learned from his family that things surfacing some other way was how empires ended.

So: the dock. Dusk. Coffee. The truth, in stages.

"There's someone I've been writing to," he said, and watched her knuckles whiten on the mug.

He kept going. He told it the way he'd lived it—a nineteen-year-old with his family's name like a weight around his neck, standing on this same dock after his mother's funeral, and a woman who'd asked him if he was okay and then, when he couldn't answer, had simply stood next to him and let the silence be okay too. He'd never gotten her name. She'd left on the morning ferry. He'd written to the only address he had—the wedding planning business, the return stamp on a flyer he'd pocketed like a thief—and signed it *A*, because *August Reyes* was a name that made people calculate what he was worth to them.

"And she wrote back," he said. "First reply came in November. I've still got it. I've got all of them." He laughed at himself. "Ten years. I used to plan my whole life around the second Tuesday of the month. That's when her letters came. Tuesdays."

He turned to look at her then, and that was his mistake, or his salvation, he still wasn't sure which.

Because Silas was looking at him with an expression he had never seen on her face—not the competent mask, not the dry amusement, not the warmth she let slip out sideways when she thought he wasn't watching. She looked, he thought, like a person standing in a burning building deciding whether to jump.

"Silas?"

"I should go." It came out fast. "The lantern order. Margot moved the tasting to Saturday, which means the tasting moved to Saturday, and I haven't—"

"It's Thursday."

"Time is a construct, August."

"Silas." He said her name and watched her flinch, and something cold moved through him, some instinct older than sense. "That's all I was going to say. That there's someone. I wasn't— I don't even know why I'm telling you this, except that you're the only person in this town who feels like—"

"Don't."

"—like the letters," he finished. "Like her. Like the person I've been writing to for ten years, which is insane, I know, you're not her, obviously, you're—"

"Stop." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Just—stop."

"Okay." He set his mug down on the piling. Slowly. The way you move around a frightened animal. "Okay. Talk to me."

And here is what August Reyes did not know, standing in the last of the light with his heart held out in both hands: he had already said everything. He had said *Tuesdays*. He had said *ten years*. He had said *this dock*. He had handed her the whole truth wrapped in a bow, and she had heard every word of it, and the thing she was afraid of was not that he didn't know. The thing she was afraid of was that he did.

### Silas

Later, Silas would try to reconstruct the exact mechanics of her own collapse, and she would fail, the way you can never quite reconstruct a sneeze or a fall.

Here is what she knew in the moment: he had said *Tuesdays*. He had said *ten years*. He had said *this dock*, and *nineteen*, and *her letters came on the second Tuesday of the month*, and every single fact had landed in her chest like a key finding its lock, and the door they opened was the door she had kept locked for a decade, and behind it was the truth, which was: *it's me, it was always me, I knew it was you by the second letter, I knew it was you when you moved in next door and I said nothing, I let you stand on my porch and tell me about your mysterious correspondent and I drank my coffee and kept my mouth shut like a coward, like a professional, like a woman who has made a career out of other people's happiness while hiding her own in a shoebox.*

And underneath that truth was the fear, which was bigger: *when he finds out I knew—when he finds out I watched him say her name and never claimed it—he will understand that everything I am is a performance, that the competence is a costume, that the woman who plans perfect days cannot survive an imperfect one of her own.*

He was looking at her. He had said her name twice. The sun was gone and the dock light had buzzed on above them, moths already throwing themselves against it.

"Talk to me," he said.

And Silas Okonkwo, who had spent ten years finding exactly the right words for other people's vows, who could defuse a mother-of-the-groom meltdown with a sentence, who had once talked a drunk best man off a hotel balcony using nothing but the dessert menu—Silas opened her mouth, and out came the cruelest thing she had ever said to anyone.

"People like you collect people like me as hobbies."

The words hit the air and just—stayed there. Hanging between them like smoke.

August went very still. "What?"

"Rich boys with collapsing empires." Her voice was doing something awful, something bright and brittle, and she couldn't make it stop. She could hear herself the way you hear yourself in a nightmare, shouting from underwater. "You get bored, or scared, or your uncle takes something from you, and you need someone to tell you you're still a good person. So you find someone with nothing—someone invisible, someone useful—and you write to her. For years. It costs you a stamp. And it makes you feel like the hero of your own sad story." She was shaking. She kept going. God help her, she kept going. "I've planned two hundred weddings, August. I know exactly how men like you use women like me. We're set dressing. We're the proof of your character. And when the story gets hard, you fold us up and put us back in the drawer."

The dock light buzzed. A gull screamed somewhere out on the water, outraged, as if it alone understood what had just happened.

August picked up his mug. His hands were steady, which she would think about later, how steady they were, how much that steadiness must have cost him.

"Okay," he said quietly.

"August—"

"No, it's— it's a fair thing to worry about." He wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking at the water, at the place where the ferry's wake would be if there were a ferry. "I should go."

"You don't have to—"

"Margot moved the tasting," he said, and there was something in his voice that wasn't anger, exactly. It was worse. It was the sound of a man putting something precious back in a drawer of his own. "I should check on the kitchens."

He walked the length of the dock without hurrying, which somehow made it worse, and she watched him go the way you watch a ferry pull away from a harbor—knowing you could still call out, knowing you won't.

### August

He made it four steps past the dock house before he stopped and stood in the dark with his hands in his pockets, breathing salt air like it was medicine.

*People like you collect people like me as hobbies.*

Here was the thing that kept him standing there, that kept him from walking back: it had almost made sense. For about eight seconds, standing in the mothy yellow light, he had thought—maybe she's right. Maybe I am a tourist in other people's loyalty. His uncle would have agreed. Edmund had a whole speech about it, about how the Reyes men loved things that couldn't cost them anything.

But then the rest of it assembled itself, the way facts do when you're lying awake at two in the morning. *People like me.* She'd said it like she knew the category from the inside. And she'd flinched at *Tuesdays*. She'd flinched at *ten years*. She'd looked at him on this dock, in this light, and she hadn't asked a single question—not *who was she*, not *what did she write*, not one of the hundred things a person asks when a stranger confesses a decade of secret correspondence.

She hadn't asked, because she hadn't needed to.

August stood in the dark and felt the truth arrive in his chest, not as a shock but as a slow, terrible warmth, like a stove you'd been leaning against without noticing the burn.

She knew. She had always known.

And she would rather wound him—would rather take the one soft thing between them and break it on the dock planks where they both had to see it—than let him find out gently. Which meant she was terrified. Which meant it mattered. Which meant, somewhere under the armor and the checklists and the cruel, well-aimed sentence, the woman who wrote him letters every second Tuesday of the month had been standing three feet away from him all along, holding a mug of black coffee, too scared to say *it's me*.

He could have gone back. He thought about it seriously. He stood there for the length of a tide change and thought about walking back down those planks and saying, *I know it's you, Silas. I've known since the storm. Say it and I'll say it back.*

But he had watched his family destroy itself for a decade with people saying things at each other in the dark, things meant to wound, things that were really fear wearing a costume. He was not going to corner her. Whatever she had to give him, she was going to have to hand over herself.

So he walked home. He climbed his porch steps. He went inside, and he took down the tin box from the top of his closet—the letters, all ten years of them, her careful handwriting on a hundred envelopes—and he set it on the kitchen table and looked at it for a long time.

Then he noticed the storage closet door, the one they shared, the wall between the two cottages being thinner than either of them pretended. It stood slightly ajar, the way it never did. And inside, on the shelf where he kept his winter gear, where he had left a cardboard box of his own just that morning—

The box was gone.

### Silas

Silas did not go home. She walked the beach road in the dark for an hour, replaying the sentence, hearing it in her own voice, *people like you collect people like me as hobbies*, and understanding with each repetition that she had done the one thing she'd sworn never to do, which was to use the truth as a weapon instead of a gift.

By the time she got back to the cottage, her feet hurt and her eyes were dry in that scraped-out way that meant crying was still available later, on credit. The porch light was on. August's kitchen light was on. His silhouette moved behind the curtain—standing at a table, not moving, for a long, long time.

She went inside and did not cry. She opened the closet where she kept the archive—her archive, her replies, ten years of second drafts and final drafts, the only honest writing she had ever done—and she counted the folders the way she counted everything, because counting was how she prayed.

Everything was there. Her copies. Her side of the decade, intact.

Which meant his side was somewhere else. Which meant—

Her phone buzzed on the counter. A text from Margot Vance, three words and a time, the kind of message that had never once in Silas's career preceded anything good:

**Come by tomorrow. 9 AM. Bring coffee. We need to talk about August.**
