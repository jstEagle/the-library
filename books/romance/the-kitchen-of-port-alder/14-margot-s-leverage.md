## 14. Margot's Leverage

Margot Vance drank her tea the way she did everything else: precisely, and as though someone were grading her.

She had summoned me to the Vance house on the bluff for what she called a "final walkthrough of the seating chart," which was a lie so transparent I almost admired it. The Vance house was all white clapboard and cold glass, perched above the harbor like a woman leaning away from a conversation. Inside it smelled of lemon polish and old money's particular loneliness—the smell of rooms no one had ever been allowed to relax in.

"Sit," Margot said, though I was already sitting.

I sat anyway. There is a difference between obedience and courtesy, and after twenty years in this business I have learned that clients cannot tell them apart, so you may as well choose whichever one keeps your hands steady.

The seating chart lay between us on the low table, annotated in Margot's narrow hand. She'd moved the harbormaster three times. She'd put Edmund Reyes at the head table, naturally—Edmund always ended up at the head of things, like water finding a drain.

"It's beautiful work," Margot said, not looking at the chart. "That's the problem."

"I'm sorry?"

"You're very good, Silas. That's what everyone says. *Silas Okonkwo can plan a wedding in a hurricane.*" She set down her cup with a small ceramic click. "And yet here we are, two weeks out, and my fiancé's uncle is telling me stories about you."

Outside, the harbor did its slow gray breathing. A gull said something rude. I kept my face arranged into the pleasant, faintly apologetic expression I wear when a client says something I need time to survive.

"Stories," I repeated.

"About letters." Margot turned then, and looked at me properly, and I understood that whatever this was, it had been rehearsed. Margot rehearsed everything. Her proposal had been rehearsed; I'd seen the binder. "Pages of them. Old ones. In a woman's handwriting, answering letters signed only 'A.'"

The room went very quiet, or perhaps I went very quiet inside it. It amounts to the same thing from the outside.

"That sounds private," I said, which was the stupidest possible thing to say, and I knew it even as I said it, the way you know you've stepped off a dock before the water closes over your head.

"They are," Margot agreed. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Let me tell you how blackmail actually works, because the movies get it wrong. In the movies there's a villain with a folder and a sneer, and the moment is operatic. In real life, blackmail arrives wearing a nice cardigan, offering you tea, and it speaks in the tone of a woman who believes—genuinely believes—that she is being reasonable. That is what makes it terrible. Not the threat. The sincerity.

Margot leaned forward. "Edmund showed me pages last week. He has more. He says they came from August's cottage." She paused delicately, giving me room to deny it, and I took none, because denial would have required my voice to work. "Ten years, Silas. Monthly. You wrote back to a stranger every month for ten years and never once asked his name."

"He never offered it," I said. My voice came out level. Small miracle. "Neither did I."

"Why?"

It was such a simple question, and it deserved an answer I didn't have, so I gave it the one I did. "Because some things are only true if nobody's watching."

Something flickered across Margot's face—not cruelty. Recognition, maybe. It passed quickly, the way weather passes over the water, but I noted it, because noting things is my whole profession.

"Here's my situation," Margot said, and now she picked up the seating chart and smoothed it flat, as if the paper needed comforting. "In two weeks I marry into the Reyes family. My father's money is in that ferry company—has been for thirty years. And August Reyes, who I have met exactly four times, who looked through me at his own engagement dinner like I was a window, is running around telling people the books are rotten. Telling people my father invested in rot." She looked up. "Do you understand what that does to a family? To a wedding?"

"August isn't lying," I said quietly. "If he says the books are rotten, the books are rotten."

"Oh, I believe you." She said it lightly, terribly. "But belief isn't useful. Proof is useful. And Edmund says August has been gathering proof—with help. With a friend on the inside who knows every guest list, every investor, every seat at every table." She let that sit between us like a dropped glass. "You."

"I'm a wedding planner."

"You're a woman who spent ten years writing love letters to a man whose name you didn't know, and then moved next door to him without either of you saying so. Don't insult us both by being small."

I want to be fair to Margot here, because history will not be. She was twenty-nine years old and had been told her entire life that her value was her composure, and she was holding both together with visible effort. Her hands, I noticed, were not steady on that seating chart. Whatever Edmund had given her, he'd given it wrapped in flattery and fear, the way men like Edmund package everything: *you're special enough to know, and special enough to be blamed.*

"What do you want?" I asked.

"Two things." She held up one finger. "First: the pages stay where they are. Unread. Unmentioned. If anyone asks, you've never received a letter in your life." A second finger. "Second: whatever August is planning for the wedding—you find out, and you tell me. Or tell Edmund. I don't care which. I just need to know before it happens, so it doesn't happen at my wedding."

"And if I say no?"

Margot smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever been on the wrong side of. "Then on the twenty-sixth, during the toasts, in front of two hundred guests including my father and every investor Edmund needs to keep happy, someone stands up and reads aloud from ten years of correspondence between a wedding planner and a man who signs himself 'A.' The parts about the dock. The parts about—" she glanced down at the page in front of her, and I realized with a lurch that she'd read them, all of them, Edmund had made sure of that, "—the part where you write that you'd like to know what his hands look like."

The gull outside said something else rude. I sympathized.

"You'd humiliate yourself too," I said. "Your wedding would become a spectacle."

"My wedding is already a spectacle," Margot said. "It's a merger with cake. At least if it explodes, it explodes on purpose." She stood, smoothed her skirt, and became again the bride from the binder, flawless and exhausted. "You don't have to answer now. But Edmund wants an answer by Friday, and Edmund's patience is shorter than his ethics, which is saying something."

At the door, she stopped with her hand on the frame, and for one unguarded second she was just a tired girl in a too-big life.

"He wrote to you for ten years," she said, not turning around. "Do you know what I'd give—"

She didn't finish it. She closed the door instead, gently, the way you close a door on a room you've decided not to cry in.

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the bluff road, because the short way went past the ferry office and I could not currently look at the ferry office without wanting to commit arson.

October had gotten serious about itself overnight. The wind came off the water with an edge on it, and the town below was doing its seasonal shedding—shutters going up, planters coming in, the ice cream place already dark. Port Alder in October is a town bracing. Everyone here knows winter is coming; the only question is whether the ferry keeps running, and lately that question had developed teeth.

I thought about the pages in Edmund's possession. Stolen from August's cottage—I didn't doubt it; Edmund Reyes struck me as a man who considered locks a formality for other people. Which meant Edmund had read them. Edmund had sat in whatever chair men like Edmund sit in, reading a nineteen-year-old boy's careful, ashamed, hopeful sentences, and had thought: *leverage.*

And Margot had read them too. Every line. Including the ones about—

No. I wasn't going to inventory them. I refused to stand on a public road conducting an audit of my own heart to determine exactly how much of it was now evidence.

Here is the thing about those letters, though, the thing I kept circling like a gull over a fish head: they were the truest thing I owned. Ten years of Tuesdays, of envelopes with no return address, of writing to a person precisely because he couldn't see me. Nobody had ever read me that carefully except him, and now two strangers had read me by theft, and the violation wasn't embarrassment—it was that they'd read the words without the reason for them. They'd seen the fire and assumed it was for sale.

By the time I reached the lane, I had done what I always do, which is make a list. It went:

1. Give Edmund what he wants (unthinkable).
2. Refuse, and let Margot detonate the letters (catastrophic, and also cruel to August, who had guarded his name for a decade and deserved better than to have his nineteen-year-old self read aloud between the toast and the cake).
3. Steal the pages back (I am a planner, not a cat burglar, though honestly the skill sets overlap more than people think).
4. Tell August everything.

I stood at the end of the lane looking at the two cottages, mine and his, their porch lights both on against the early dusk, and I thought about number four the way you think about a door you're afraid of.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape: if I told August, I had to tell him all of it. Not just Edmund's blackmail—the whole architecture. That I'd known who he was since the first morning he carried boxes up his steps and said something in a phrasing I'd have recognized anywhere, because I'd read it forty times. That I'd let him circle me for weeks, coffee and omissions, while he was probably dying to say it and I was pretending not to notice. That the woman he'd trusted with his anonymous heart had stood in his kitchen and lied to his face by simply, smoothly, professionally, never bringing it up.

He might forgive the silence. People forgive a lot when they're in love, and I was fairly sure—he'd never said it, but the letters said it, the porch mornings said it, the way he'd saved the rehearsal dinner said it—that August Reyes was in love with me, God help him.

But he might not forgive that I'd chosen the lie daily, comfortably, the way you choose a comfortable chair. And I couldn't bear that. Isn't that absurd? Blackmail I could face. Public humiliation I could nearly face. But the possibility of watching his face do the specific thing faces do when they recalculate a person—all of that warmth rerouting into caution—that I could not bear.

So I did what I do best. I planned around it.

Friday. I had until Friday. Two days to find option three, or invent option five, or become the kind of person who could survive option four.

* * *

That evening August knocked on my door with a loaf of bread and a report.

"Harbormaster meeting went long," he said, coming in the way he'd started coming in lately, which is to say like someone testing whether he's welcome and trying not to show it. "Delgado's with me. On the ledgers. We're close, Silas." He set the bread on my counter and looked at me, and his face did the open, undefended thing it does, the thing that makes me understand exactly why I fell for a stranger's handwriting a decade ago. "The wedding. I know what you're going to say. I'm not going to ruin it. But if the moment comes—"

"The moment will come," I said. "Moments always come at weddings. It's practically in the job description."

He laughed, and something in my chest pulled tight, because I was standing there holding bread-warm domesticity in one hand and a blackmail ultimatum in the other, and he had no idea, and every hour I didn't tell him was another brick in the wall Margot would eventually walk through.

"Silas." He touched my elbow, light. "You okay? You've got your planning face on."

"This is just my face."

"No," he said, gentle. "Your planning face is calmer than this. This is your planning face with weather behind it."

And that was the worst part, really. Ten years of letters, and he could still read me better than I'd ever managed to read myself.

"Friday," I said, before I could stop myself, and then covered it with, "the florist confirms Friday. Final numbers."

"Right," he said, and believed me, because he trusted me, because that was the whole catastrophe.

After he left I sat at my kitchen table with my checklists and my neat columns, and beneath the day's tasks I wrote one line in pencil, small, where no client would ever see it:

*Find a way to save everyone, or finally learn which of them you can live without.*

Then I underlined it twice, because I am who I am, and I turned off the light.
