## 18. Read Them Aloud

There is a particular silence that falls over a room when something goes wrong at a wedding. I have heard it perhaps forty times in my career—the cake collapsing, the wrong name spoken at the altar, the uncle who drinks too much and says the true thing—and I have always been the one to break it. That was the job. That was the whole job: stand in the gap between what people hoped for and what actually happened, and fill it with something smooth enough to pass for grace.

Margot Vance stood at the podium with a stack of envelopes in her manicured hands, and the silence in the boathouse was the deepest I had ever heard.

"These," Margot said, and her voice had a strange bright brittleness to it, like ice about to crack underfoot, "are love letters. Ten years of them. Written by August Reyes to a woman he never once named."

August had come back. He stood near the doors at the far end of the boathouse, still in his travel coat, salt wind still in his hair, and when our eyes met across the room full of Port Alder's finest—Edmund Reyes front and center with his silver hair and his investor's smile—August looked at me the way a man looks at a lighthouse when his ship is already on the rocks. Not with hope. With apology.

I understood then that he thought I had done this. That I had sold him out, or failed to stop it, or both. And why wouldn't he? The last time we'd spoken on the dock I had told him that people like him collect people like her as hobbies. I had handed him every reason to believe the worst of me, gift-wrapped, with a bow.

Margot lifted the first envelope. "This one is dated March eleventh, nine years ago." She slid out the page. "'Dear A. You wrote that you watched the ferry leave without you again and felt—'"

"Stop."

The word came out of me before I had decided anything. It came out of some place older than decision, some cellar of myself I'd kept locked for ten years, and it came out loud enough that the string quartet stopped tuning their instruments and the caterers froze mid-pour and Edmund Reyes's smile finally, blessedly, slipped.

Every head turned toward me. I was standing by the dessert table in my good navy dress, holding a glass of sparkling cider I had no memory of picking up, and I want to be honest here, because honesty is the entire point of what happened next: every instinct I owned screamed at me to smile, to deflect, to say *oh, Margot, you've found my props for the toast* and turn the whole thing into a charming bit of theater. I could have done it. I could have done it beautifully. It would have taken four seconds and cost me nothing but the last scrap of whatever I actually was.

I set down the cider glass instead.

"Those are mine," I said. "The replies. If anyone is going to read them, it's going to be me."

* * *

Let me back up thirty seconds, because even now I remember the arithmetic of that moment, the way a planner's mind works even while her heart is detonating.

Margot had said *a woman he never named.* She didn't know it was me. She couldn't know—I had never signed a single reply, not one, in ten years. As far as Margot and Edmund knew, they were holding ammunition against an anonymous pen pal, a ghost, someone who might not even be in this room. They were expecting embarrassment at a distance. A scandal with no face attached.

And there was a version of the next five minutes where I stayed that ghost. Where I let Margot read August's words—the ones he'd written at nineteen, raw and ashamed and achingly hopeful—and let the room pity him, and let Edmund win, and walked out of Port Alder on the morning ferry with my fee and my anonymity and my hollow little exit plan intact.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about building your whole life around being unseen: eventually you look around and realize you built it alone, and the view from inside all that careful invisibility is just a wall.

Margot had found the second envelope. "April second, same year. 'You asked whether I ever get tired of pretending. Here is what I told you—'"

"Margot." I crossed the floor. My heels sounded enormous. Somewhere in the crowd, Edmund Reyes leaned over to whisper to a man with a yacht-club tie, and I saw the man's eyebrows go up, and I understood that Edmund believed this was all going according to plan. Good, I thought. Let him believe it. Belief is such a fragile thing to build a ledger on.

I reached the podium. Margot looked at me with genuine confusion, which almost made me laugh—she had planned this ambush down to the ribbon on the programs, and she had never once considered that the person she was aiming at might simply walk up and take the gun.

"May I?" I held out my hand.

She hesitated. Then, because Margot Vance had hired me to execute flawless events and some trained part of her brain still obeyed, she passed me the stack.

Ten years of Tuesdays. Every letter I had ever written to a stranger who signed himself only *A*, returned to me in a thief's neat stack, and heavier than any object I have ever held.

I looked out at the room. Bridesmaids in blush. Uncles in seersucker. The harbormaster near the back, arms crossed, watching August with the expression of a man who has already read certain ledgers. And August, still by the door, shaking his head at me in tiny increments—*don't, you don't have to, please don't*—and looking so much like the boy on the dock ten years ago that my chest hurt.

"I'm going to tell you a story," I said, "and then I'm going to read you some mail, and then this wedding can proceed, because despite everything, Margot's flowers are extraordinary and I will not have them wasted."

A ripple of uncertain laughter. Good. Nervous rooms need somewhere to put their hands.

"My name is Silas Okonkwo. I have planned two hundred and six weddings. I have never planned my own life, which is why, until about ninety seconds ago, I was planning to leave this town in three weeks and never explain myself to anyone." I looked down at the top letter. "And for ten years, I answered a letter every month from someone who signed only the letter A. He never told me who he was. I never asked. We were, I think, the only two people either of us was ever entirely honest with."

I heard Edmund shift in his seat. I heard Margot say, very quietly, "Silas—" and I heard something in her voice that wasn't triumph anymore. It was the sound of a woman realizing she'd been handed someone else's knife.

"This one," I said, "is dated April second, nine years ago. In response to a letter I am not going to read, because it isn't mine to give you. But he had written to ask whether I ever got tired of pretending." I unfolded the page. My own handwriting looked strange under the boathouse lights, rounder than I remembered, younger. And I read it aloud.

"'Dear A. Yes. Constantly. Today I spent eleven hours making a stranger's happiness look effortless, and then I went home and ate cereal over the sink, and if that isn't the definition of pretending, I don't know what is. You asked what I would do if I stopped. I don't know. I genuinely don't know, and that frightens me more than anything you've ever told me about your family. You write that your name poisons everything it touches. Mine doesn't touch anything. Sometimes I think we're running the same experiment from opposite ends—one of us trying to escape a name, one of us trying to grow one. Write back and tell me if the water's warmer on your side.'"

The silence had changed quality. It was no longer the silence of scandal. It was the silence of two hundred people recognizing something true when it walked into the room wearing a navy dress.

I took out the next letter. Then the next. I did not read all of them—God knows there were a hundred and twenty, and half of them contained jokes about the ferry snack bar that were nobody's business—but I read enough. The one from the winter I got pneumonia and he'd mailed soup recipes and instructions to *actually rest, you impossible woman.* The one where I confessed I'd started keeping his letters in a hatbox under my bed like a teenager. The one where I wrote, three years ago, in a moment of weakness I had regretted every day since:

"'Some days I think I know exactly who you are. Some days I think I've known since the dock. But if I'm wrong, I lose the only honest thing I have, so I'm going to keep not asking, and you keep not telling, and we'll call it a draw.' I never sent that one," I added, looking up. "It's in the box marked *unsent*, which yes, exists, and yes, Margot apparently has excellent taste in other people's private correspondence."

Margot flushed crimson. Edmund did not flush; Edmund's face did something worse, which was calculate. He was already rearranging the narrative—*the wedding planner, an obsessed fantasist, a stalker of the family*—I could see the press release forming behind his eyes like weather.

So I gave him nowhere to put it.

"The last thing I'll read," I said, "is dated eight months ago. After he moved into the cottage next door. After I knew—or believed I knew—who he was. And I still didn't say it. Because I was a coward, and because I had convinced myself that a person like me gets to observe love but not hold it, the way you get to plan weddings but not have one." My voice cracked on the next line and I let it. Let them hear it. What was the point of claiming your words if you sandblasted the feeling off them first?

"'Dear A. You fixed Mrs. Halloran's dock rail today without being asked, and you hummed while you did it, badly, and I stood at my window like a fool and thought: this is the man from the letters. This is the man who wrote that he wanted a small life in a town that smells like salt and diesel. And I thought: if I ever tell him, and I'm wrong, I lose everything. And if I'm right, I lose everything differently. So here is what I'll do instead. Nothing. I'll keep answering, and he'll keep writing, and we'll grow old as two people who met once and never again, which is the safest tragedy I know.'" I lowered the page. "That was the last one I sent. I was going to stop, after this season. I had it all planned out. I always have it all planned out."

I turned to August, and the whole room turned with me, a slow tide of taffeta and wool.

"You asked me once, in a letter, whether I ever got tired of pretending. Here is my answer, ten years late, in front of everyone, because apparently I only ever tell the truth at other people's weddings." I set the letters down on the podium, squared their edges out of pure habit, and left them there deliberately unsquared in spirit. "I'm tired. And I'm done. Your name doesn't poison anything, August Reyes. It never did. The only thing that's been poisoning anything in this town is sitting in the third row checking his watch."

Every head swiveled to Edmund Reyes with the beautiful, terrible unity of a flock changing direction.

* * *

What happened next belongs to August, and to ledgers, and to the harbormaster's steady voice, and I'll let those chapters be his. But I want to record what I saw in the seconds after I sat down—because I did sit down, eventually, legs gone entirely unreliable, into the nearest chair, which belonged to Mrs. Halloran, who patted my knee and said, "Took you long enough, dear," as though she'd known all along, as though the whole town had maybe known, the way towns do.

I saw Margot Vance stand at her podium with nothing left in her hands, and I saw the exact moment she understood that Edmund hadn't given her a weapon—he'd given her a lit fuse and pointed it at her own wedding. Her chin came up. Whatever Margot did next, it wouldn't be Edmund's idea.

I saw Edmund Reyes rise, smoothing his jacket, opening his mouth to pour smooth institutional oil on troubled water—

And I saw August Reyes walk down the aisle of his enemy's party with a folder under his arm, and stop directly in front of his uncle, and say, in a voice that carried to the back of the boathouse and out through the open doors to the harbor beyond:

"Before anyone makes a toast, I think we should look at the ferry company's books. All of them. Harbormaster?"

Mrs. Halloran squeezed my knee one more time and whispered, "Now *that's* a man who reads his mail."

I laughed—a wet, undignified, completely unprofessional laugh—and reached for another letter, because if the evening was going to be ruined anyway, I intended to ruin it thoroughly, and there were ninety-eight more years of my heart in that stack, and I had only just begun reading them aloud.
