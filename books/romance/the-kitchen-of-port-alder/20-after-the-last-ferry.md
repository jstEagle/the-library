## 20. After the Last Ferry

The morning of the last ferry, Silas Okonkwo woke at five forty-seven without an alarm, which was how she knew something had already changed. For fifteen years she had woken to alarms the way other people woke to weather—inevitable, external, someone else's schedule imposed on her body. Today her eyes simply opened, and the first thing she thought was not *checklist* but *the ferry leaves at nine*.

She lay in the narrow bed of the cottage she'd rented for the season and listened to the gulls arguing over the harbor. Somewhere down the road, the bakery ovens would already be going; she'd learned the town's rhythms the way she learned everything, by observation and note-taking, in the margins of a planner she no longer needed. The wedding had happened four days ago. The ledgers had been read. Edmund Reyes had been escorted out of the annual shareholders' meeting by two harbormaster's deputies and one very satisfied fisherman who had been waiting years for an excuse. Margot Vance had withdrawn from her engagement with the particular dignity of a woman who has discovered she was a chess piece and decided, on the spot, to become a player instead.

And Silas had stood up in front of two hundred people and read her own words aloud.

She still hadn't fully processed that. She suspected she wouldn't for years. There were moments—pouring coffee, folding a towel—when the memory surfaced whole and physical, the weight of the letters in her hands, the sound of her own voice saying *I think about the dock in October* in front of the entire population of Port Alder, and she had to sit down for a second. Not from shame. That was the strange part. From the sheer vertigo of having no floor of secrecy beneath her anymore, and discovering she could stand anyway.

She made coffee. She did not make a list.

Well. She made a small list. She was not a saint. But the list said things like *walk to the point* and *ask Mrs. Okada about the winter market* and *return the cake stand*, which was a list for a life rather than an event, and she suspected that distinction mattered more than she could currently articulate.

At eight thirty she walked down to the dock.

* * *

The *Marigold*—and she had finally, after a whole season, learned the ferry's name, because August had told her, laughing, that she'd been calling it "the boat" for three months and it had feelings—sat low in the water, taking on its last cargo of the season. Crates of cranberries from the bog farm. A pallet of mail, which made her chest do something complicated. Two tourists with matching rain jackets who had clearly been told the last-ferry crossing was a romantic tradition and were photographing each other with the grim determination of people completing a chore.

The town had turned out the way towns do for these small ceremonies—not to wave goodbye exactly, but to mark it. Mrs. Okada from the bakery, flour still on her apron. The harbormaster, Dee, in a coat that had survived more storms than some ships. A scatter of kids who should have been in school and were being educationally enriched by the harbor instead. And August Reyes, standing at the dock's edge with a duffel bag at his feet.

Silas stopped walking.

The bag was small. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing her mind did with it was the worst possible arithmetic. A man leaving for good packs a lot. A man leaving for a weekend packs a little. A man standing at the last ferry of the season with a duffel that size was a man who had not decided, and was letting the boat decide for him.

She hung back near the fish crates, out of the lamplight of her own intentions, and watched.

He didn't look like a man saying goodbye. That was the second thing. He looked like a man doing arithmetic of his own—she recognized the expression, because she'd spent ten years watching it form in her own mirror. The look of someone weighing a door: what it costs to open, what it costs to leave shut.

Dee called the all-aboard in the flat voice of a woman who had called it a thousand times and would call it a thousand more. The tourists scurried up the ramp. The line-handlers cast off the stern line, then the bow.

August picked up the duffel.

Silas's heart performed a maneuver she had not authorized.

He took three steps toward the ramp. Then he stopped, turned, and looked back down the dock—not searching, exactly, but the way you look at a room you're leaving, cataloguing it, deciding what you're allowed to keep.

He looked at her.

Of course he looked at her. The whole town was watching him look at her, because Port Alder had spent four days being politely, ferociously discreet about the fact that its newest residents had declared their love via public letter reading, and the town's restraint had the quality of a dam under pressure.

He set the duffel down.

The *Marigold* pulled away without him.

* * *

Later, everyone would have a version of this moment. Mrs. Okada would tell it with the duffel over the shoulder. Dee would insist the captain had held the boat a full ninety seconds out of respect. The schoolchildren would compress it into "he jumped off the ferry for love," which was wrong in every particular and right in every way that mattered. Silas would let all versions stand, because she had learned, at forty-one, that the truth of a moment and the story of a moment could live in the same harbor without sinking each other.

But the truth was smaller and quieter. The boat left. He didn't get on it. He walked down the dock toward her with the duffel still in his hand, and when he reached her he said, "I overslept."

"You did not."

"I did," he said, with the gravity of a man testifying under oath. "I slept terribly. I had a very late night. There was a woman at a shareholders' meeting who read ten years of my mail out loud and then had the audacity to be brilliant about it. I couldn't stop thinking about it. Slept maybe four hours."

"Tragic."

"Completely ruined my morning. And then I got down here late and flustered and I missed the boat." He looked at the duffel. "So now I'm stuck here, apparently. For the winter. With no ferry out until spring."

"What a catastrophe."

"It really is." He set the bag down between them like a man laying down a card he'd been hiding. "I could have made it, you know. I want to be clear about that. I was on time. I was early. I stood there and did the math and the math said get on the boat—mainland, lawyers, the company transition, all of it's easier from the city, everyone said so. And then I looked back at the dock and the math fell apart."

Silas watched the ferry shrink against the gray water, growing smaller and more theoretical by the minute, until it was just a smudge with a wake. In fifteen years of event planning she had stood at the end of a hundred ceremonies and watched people leave as married, as transformed, as launched. She had never once watched a boat leave with the person still standing on the dock, and found it was the better ending.

"I did the same math," she said.

He turned. "This morning?"

"For about ten years, actually. The math always said go. Somewhere else. Somewhere my name isn't attached to other people's happiest days, somewhere nobody knows what I do, somewhere I could just be—" She gestured vaguely at the air, at herself, at the whole unsolvable problem. "Invisible. Comfortable. The math was very persuasive. It had footnotes."

"And now?"

"Now the math has a flaw in it." She wrapped her hands around her coffee cup, mostly for something to do with them. "I spent the whole season thinking the question was whether I'd stay in Port Alder or leave. And it wasn't. The question was whether I'd stay *anywhere*. Whether I was capable of it. Whether I could be in a place, in a life, in a—" she stopped, restarted. "A tense. The future tense. I've lived my whole adult life in the conditional. *I could. I might. If someone asked.* And nobody asked, because I made sure nobody could. And then you—" she laughed, helplessly, "—you didn't even ask. You just wrote to me for ten years like I was already a person. Like I was already *here*."

"I didn't ask," August said, "because asking felt like collecting. Like I'd finally be one more Reyes taking something that wasn't offered." He looked out at the water where the ferry had been. "My family's whole disease is acquisition. My uncle acquired a company and hollowed it out. My grandfather acquired a name and used it as a weapon. I wasn't going to acquire you. Not even by asking nicely."

"So you just... waited."

"I just waited. Ten years of Tuesdays." He smiled at the phrase, their phrase now, worn smooth by the whole town's retelling. "And then you stood up at that wedding and read your own letters, and you weren't waiting anymore, and I thought—okay. Now it's a choice. Now she's choosing, out loud, in front of everyone. So I get to choose too."

"And you chose a duffel bag on a dock."

"I chose a duffel bag on a dock," he agreed solemnly. "It's a very romantic duffel bag. It's seen things."

Silas laughed—really laughed, the kind that startled a nearby gull into flight—and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton.

* * *

They walked the length of the dock while the town pretended, with tremendous effort, not to watch. Mrs. Okada found a sudden fascinating interest in her own storefront. Dee examined a cleat with the focus of a woman defusing a bomb.

"I should say," August said, as they reached the end where the fish crates were stacked, "that I'm not asking you anything. I want that on the record. Dee's the record. Dee, you're the record."

"Absolutely not," Dee said, not looking up from the cleat. "I'm off the clock."

"Then the gulls are the record." He turned back to Silas. "I'm not asking you to stay. I'm not asking you to be with me, or to stay in this cottage, or to plan anything ever again, or to not plan things, or—God, I'm asking you a lot of things by saying I'm not asking you things. What I mean is—" He took a breath, and she watched him do it, watched this man who had spent a decade being careful with words decide to spend one recklessly. "Whatever you do next, I'd like to witness it. From nearby. For a long time. That's the whole offer. No terms. No exit clause. Just—nearby."

Silas looked at him for a long moment.

Here was the thing she could not explain to anyone who hadn't lived it: being wanted was easy. She'd been wanted her whole career—wanted for her competence, her calm, her ability to make catastrophe look like charm. Being wanted was a job with excellent benefits and no future. What she had never once been was *counted on*. Expected. Assumed to be there tomorrow, and the day after, in the boring grammar of ordinary continuity.

"You understand," she said, "that I'm going to be terrible at this. At staying. I will try to leave at least four times. I'll get a job in Seattle and pack the car and get as far as the bridge."

"Probably five times."

"Five. And I'll make spreadsheets about leaving. Color-coded."

"I'll help," he said. "I'll hold the clipboard."

"And I'll be difficult in ways that don't even have names yet."

"I've read your letters, Silas. I've been briefed."

And that was it, really. That was the whole negotiation. No terms, no exit clause, no contract—she, who had spent her life drafting contracts for other people's love, was being offered love with no paper at all, and discovering that the absence of paper was not the absence of promise. It was the presence of trust. Someone had finally looked at her, all of her, ten years of her, and decided she was worth the risk of not getting anything in writing.

"I'm staying," she said.

Not *I'll stay*. Not *I might*. Present tense, which was already a small revolution, and then she corrected herself, because she was done with small revolutions.

"I'm staying. And in the spring I'm staying. And next fall, when the last ferry leaves and everyone does their math again—I'm staying then too. Future tense. All of it."

August didn't kiss her, which she noted with approval, because he was learning that she liked to arrive at things on her own schedule. Instead he picked up the ridiculous duffel bag and offered her his arm, formal as a wedding, and they walked up the dock together past the fish crates and the cleats and the one gull who had appointed itself the record.

At the top of the ramp, Dee finally looked up.

"Dock's closed for the season," she said. "You two want to clear the area?"

"We live here," August said, and the words came out of him like something he'd been holding underwater for a decade, finally allowed to surface.

"Yeah," Dee said, and there was the smallest crack of a smile under the mustache. "Heard that somewhere."

* * *

That evening, Silas sat at the little desk in her cottage—*their* cottages were forty feet apart and everyone including Mrs. Okada had opinions about the forty feet—and did something she hadn't done since the wedding.

She wrote a letter.

Not to a client. Not to a vendor. Not to an anonymous stranger she'd decided, years ago, deserved a better correspondent than she could safely be. To August Reyes, whose cottage was forty feet away, because some things still needed to be said in ink, at a desk, with the window open to the sound of the harbor, because that was how they had built the thing and they were not going to pretend they'd built it any other way.

*Dear August,* she wrote, and then stopped, and looked at the two words for a long time.

*Dear August.* His actual name. Her actual hand. No A., no signature withheld, no careful distance. She thought about the fifty years of Tuesdays stretching out ahead of them, the winter market and the spring ferry and the cottage and whatever she was going to become now that she'd stopped being a ghost with excellent organizational skills, and she thought: I should start as I mean to go on.

She picked up the pen again.

*Dear August. I've decided to write you letters now that you're in possession of my address, my name, and my entire emotional archive, which means the letters will have to be much better. I hope you're prepared to be disappointed and then, eventually, not.*

*Also, I have an idea about the ferry line's winter schedule, and a letter I think we should write together, and a question about the cottage with the good light on Harbor Street, and I want you to know that I'm not going to answer any of these things tonight, because for the first time in my life there is no deadline.*

*There's just Tuesday. And the Tuesday after that.*

*Signed, finally and on purpose,*

*Silas*

She sealed it, addressed it, and walked the forty feet through the cold blue dusk to his door, where she wedged the envelope into the crack beside the handle the way she had wedged ten years of herself into the space between silence and speech.

Then she stood on the path a moment longer, listening to the harbor settle into evening, and noticed, with a smile she didn't bother to hide from the empty street, that there was already an envelope waiting under her own door—his handwriting on the front, her name spelled out in full, and beneath it, in small letters, like a man signing a treaty he intended to keep:

*To be opened in fifty years. Or Tuesday. Whichever comes first.*
