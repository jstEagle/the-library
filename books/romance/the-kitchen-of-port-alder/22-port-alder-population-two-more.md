## 22. Port Alder, Population Two More

The first snow came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays had always been our day—the day the letters arrived, the day the answers went out—and when the flakes started falling soft and sideways over the harbor, I was at the library window with a stack of blank paper and a thermos of coffee, watching the *Marianne V* chug back in from the mainland with her running lights on against the gray.

She was running. That was the miracle nobody in Port Alder had quite stopped remarking on. The winter schedule—*the* winter schedule, the one that wasn't supposed to exist—had been posted on the ferry office door in August's blunt, square handwriting: *Tuesdays and Fridays, weather permitting, because this town doesn't hibernate.* Someone had added, in pencil, underneath: *about time.* The pencil was probably Mrs. Calloway's. The pencil was usually Mrs. Calloway's.

I had lived a lot of places and planned a lot of events, and I could tell you the exact moment an event stopped being an event and became a life. It was never the ceremony. It was the morning after, when someone still showed up to make coffee.

Port Alder kept showing up.

"Silas." Ruthie Okafor, the librarian, appeared at my elbow with the particular expression of a woman who has already decided the answer to whatever she's about to ask. "Six people signed up for your workshop. Six. Do you understand what that means in this town? The book club has four members and two of them are the same woman under different names."

"Which names?"

"Deborah and, allegedly, her cousin Diane." Ruthie set down a sign-up sheet with a flourish. "You've broken every attendance record we have. I hope you're proud."

I looked at the sheet. Six names, seven if you counted the smudged eighth entry that said *maybe* in handwriting I'd have known anywhere—loose, slanted, the *y* always trailing off like it had somewhere better to be. August had signed up for my letter-writing workshop. The man I had exchanged two hundred and forty-some letters with over ten years had signed up to learn how to write letters.

"I'm going to have to have a talk with him about auditing," I said.

"You're going to have to have a talk with him about a lot of things," Ruthie said serenely. "That's marriage."

"We're not—" I started, and then stopped, because arguing with Ruthie Okafor was like arguing with the tide, and because I'd caught myself doing the thing where I reached for the correction before I reached for the truth. Old habit. The invisible fixer's reflex: deflect, minimize, make yourself small enough to slip out of the sentence.

I was working on it. I had a whole list. Item one: stop keeping lists.

* * *

The workshop met Thursdays in the library's back room, under a poster of a lighthouse that had been there so long the lighthouse had faded to a ghost of itself. My six students ranged from twelve-year-old Priya Nair, who wanted to write to her grandmother in Chennai and thought email was "a coward's medium," to Mr. Abernathy, seventy-eight, who wanted to write to no one in particular and said so with the defiant pride of a man announcing a hobby he expected to be mocked for.

"Letters to no one," I said. "Tell me more."

"You write 'em anyway," he said. "My wife did it for forty years. Wrote to her sister in Juneau, mostly, but sometimes to nobody. I found a shoebox of 'em after she passed. *Dear Whoever Is Listening.*" He cleared his throat. "They're the best ones. The ones she wasn't performing for."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when someone says something true by accident.

"That's actually the whole curriculum," I said. "Congratulations, Mr. Abernathy, you've taught the class."

I had them do an exercise I'd invented for myself a decade ago, back when a stranger's letter arrived on my desk and I had to decide who to be in my answer. *Write the sentence you'd never say out loud. Don't sign it. Put it in the envelope anyway.* I'd built a whole life on that exercise. I'd built a whole life on the space between what you write and what you claim.

Priya wrote furiously. Mr. Abernathy wrote slowly, pressing so hard the paper dented. The eighth chair—the *maybe* chair—sat empty, and I found I wasn't nervous about it. That was new. For ten years I had been nervous about everything: about being found out, found wanting, found at all. Now the chair was just a chair. He'd come or he wouldn't. He always came.

He came in late, shaking snow off his shoulders, carrying two coffees and no apology, because August Reyes had never once in his life apologized for being exactly when he was.

"Traffic," he said.

"There's no traffic in Port Alder."

"The ferry was slow."

"You walked."

"The dock was slow." He set a coffee in front of me, kissed the top of my head in front of God and Ruthie Okafor and everybody, and took the eighth chair. "Carry on, teacher. Don't let me disrupt the curriculum."

"Your handwriting is on my sign-up sheet," I said.

"I know. I wanted to see if you'd notice."

* * *

Margot Vance came into the hardware store on a Friday in December, and the whole town pretended not to watch her, which in Port Alder meant that everyone watched her with tremendous politeness.

She was buying paint. That was the talk of it—she was *buying paint*, actual paint, for the actual rooms of the actual house on Larch Street that she'd bought with her own money after she withdrew from the engagement and, more remarkably, from her family's arrangement with Edmund Reyes. Her father had cut her off, or she'd cut him off; the story changed depending on who told it, but the paint was constant. Warm white for the bedroom, the clerk said. A blue for the kitchen that was almost the color of the harbor in March.

"Silas." She found me by the sandpaper, because Port Alder is four streets and you cannot avoid anyone. She looked—this was the thing nobody had a word for yet—*lighter*. Not happy, exactly. Stripped down. A woman who had been a showcase and was becoming a person, in stages, like a house being renovated. "I owe you an apology."

"You don't—"

"I do. I held those letters over you because Edmund handed them to me and I told myself it was self-defense." She turned a paint chip over in her fingers, not looking at me. "It wasn't. It was the first time in my life anyone had ever handed me power, and I grabbed it with both hands before I asked what it cost." She looked up. "You read your own words out loud at my wedding. My fake wedding. And the worst thing—the thing I think about—is that you made them beautiful. I was holding a weapon and you turned it into a reading."

"It was a good reading," I admitted. "I'd had ten years of drafts."

That startled a laugh out of her, quick and real, and then she was quiet again. "I'm starting something. A small thing. A guesthouse—six rooms, no telephones, no wedding packages. People come to Port Alder to disappear for a weekend and I'd like them to do it somewhere warm." She hesitated. "I wondered if you'd—advise me. Not plan. I know you're done planning. Just. Advise. I want to do it honestly and I don't entirely know what that looks like."

I looked at her: Margot Vance, who had wanted everything, asking for a little bit of help with no leverage attached. Somewhere in the last three months the town had decided to forgive her, the way towns do—slowly, unsentimentally, through casseroles and borrowed ladders—and it seemed she'd decided to forgive herself in the same currency.

"Thursdays are busy," I said. "But I drink coffee at eight. Come by."

She blinked. "That's it?"

"That's it. Bring the paint chip. If the kitchen's going to be harbor-blue, it should be the *right* harbor-blue. There are at least four."

* * *

Christmas came and went the way Christmas does in small towns: as a logistics problem that everyone secretly loves. The harbor committee strung lights on the *Marianne V* until she looked like a floating birthday cake. August ran the Christmas Eve crossing himself, free of charge, and came home smelling of diesel and pine, with harbor ice in his hair, and stood in our kitchen doorway—*our* kitchen, the cottage's kitchen, the one with the bad drawer and the good light—looking at me like a man checking his bearings.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing. Just—" He shook his head. "I spent ten years writing to you and I still can't believe the mail actually delivered."

"Twice a month, rain or shine."

"Twice a month," he agreed, "rain or shine," and I understood that we were not really talking about the mail.

Here is what nobody tells you about getting what you spent a decade wanting: it doesn't feel like arriving. It feels like Tuesday. It feels like the bad drawer and the good light and a man who signs up for your workshop to make you laugh. The grand gesture was the wedding; the ledger; the reading. The reward is this—ordinary, repeated, chosen again every morning. Future tense. I was still learning to live in it. Some mornings I woke up reaching for a checklist and had to remind myself that the wedding was over, the exit fund was spent—not on an exit at all, but on a new boiler for the cottage, because I was staying, and boilers are how staying sounds.

New Year's Eve, the whole town came down to the dock. There was no fireworks budget, so the fishermen set off their flare guns at midnight, red arcs over black water, and everyone cheered like it was Sydney Harbor. August held my hand in his coat pocket. Mrs. Calloway stood next to us and said, "About time," and this time she wasn't talking about the ferry.

* * *

The letter-writing workshop ran through January. By February we were eight—Ruthie joined, ostensibly to supervise, actually to write to her sister in Tacoma—and by March we were doing the exercise I had been avoiding all winter.

"Write to someone you've never thanked," I told them. "Or someone you've never forgiven. Or yourself. Sign it or don't. But this time—" I looked at August, who was watching me with the expression he wore when he already knew the end of my sentence, "—this time, put your real name on it. If you want. That's the whole exercise. That's the whole ten years, honestly. Deciding whether to sign."

Priya signed hers. Mr. Abernathy wrote *Dear Whoever Is Listening* and then, in a hand that shook only a little, added underneath: *But if it's you, Margaret: I kept the shoebox.* He didn't read it aloud. He didn't have to. The room did the thing rooms do.

After class, August stayed to stack chairs, which was his way of staying, and I stood at the window watching the ice go out of the harbor in long, groaning plates. Spring was coming the way it does on the coast—reluctantly, then all at once.

"I have an announcement," he said.

"You're resigning from the workshop."

"I'm graduating." He held up an envelope. Sealed. My name on the front—my whole name, Silas Lockridge, in his loose slanted hand—and my address, which was here, which was ten feet away from him, which was the point. "The last assignment. You have to mail it to me. No hand-delivering. The mail has to do its work."

"August. You live next door."

"Rules are rules, teacher."

I laughed and took the envelope, and it was warm from his pocket, and I held it like it was the first letter all over again—*Dear S,* from a boy of nineteen on this same dock, hiding behind a letter because his name was a weight and mine was a wall, both of us posting our true selves into the dark and hoping the dark was kind.

It had been. That was the whole story, if I ever wrote it down. The dark had been kind.

* * *

In April, the library got a proper outgoing-mail box—Ruthie's doing, a small brass slot by the front door, because she said a town with a ferry schedule ought to have a mail ritual—and on the first warm morning of the season, I walked down with two letters in my coat.

The first was mine to August. I'm not going to tell you what it said. Some things you write for an audience of one, and I had spent ten years learning the difference between writing to be read and writing to be known, and this was the latter. I signed it with my full name and then, underneath, because I could, because no one was grading: *Yours, in present tense.*

The second letter was his to me, and I didn't read it either. It was sealed, and it had my name on it, and that was enough. That had always been enough. That was, it turned out, the entire foundation everything else had been built on.

We stood at the brass slot together, two grown adults in a library doorway on a Tuesday, taking turns like children, and Ruthie pretended not to watch us, which in Port Alder meant she watched us with tremendous politeness and then wrote it all down for the historical society.

"Your turn," I said.

He posted his. I posted mine. The envelopes made a small, soft sound, two paper hearts dropping into the dark, and somewhere down the line a mail boat would take them out to the mainland and the whole slow machinery of the world would carry them around and back to us, because that's what the mail does, that's what Port Alder does, that's what love does when you finally stop being afraid of it: it goes out, and it comes home.

"Population two more," August said, reading the old sign by the dock as we walked out—*PORT ALDER, POP. 3,412*, the number someone had amended in pencil, years ago, and never corrected.

"You know that sign's wrong," I said.

"It's wrong in every direction," he agreed. "There's Margot's six rooms. There's Priya's grandmother coming to visit in June. There's whatever Ruthie's plotting." He laced his fingers through mine. "The town keeps growing. Nobody tells the sign."

"Signs are like that," I said. "They describe a place once, and then the place keeps happening."

We walked home along the water, past the ferry office with its winter schedule still posted, past the hardware store with its harbor-blue paint drying in Margot's kitchen windows, past the library with its brass slot and its faded lighthouse and its six—eight—students learning to sign their names.

The last ferry of the season had come and gone the previous fall, and the first ferry of this one was already scheduled, and in between, nobody had left.
