## 9. Dessert, For Real This Time

The last night of the Villalobos reunion smelled like my mother's flan and charcoal smoke, and I stood at the edge of the patio with my phone in my hand like a man holding a live grenade.

I'd typed the text four times.

*Come back tonight. Not as a favor. As you.*

Deleted. Too much. Too little. Too honest for a man who'd spent three weeks learning that honesty was a currency he couldn't afford to spend carelessly anymore.

*There's flan. My mother will interrogate you. You'll survive. Probably.*

That one had been sitting in the draft folder since noon, and it was still wrong, because it made it sound like a joke, and this wasn't a joke anymore. It hadn't been a joke since the council meeting, since Marisol stood up in front of half of Sandpiper Point and killed her father's deal with her own voice, knowing exactly what it would cost her. Since Lucia had climbed onto a folding chair to defend her before I'd said a single word.

Since I'd lain awake at 3 a.m. admitting that the anger had burned down to something else, something with a shape I recognized from the inside of my own chest: the specific ache of loving someone who is trying to become worth loving, and wanting to be there when she arrives.

Lucia appeared at my elbow, hair still damp from the pool, smelling of sunscreen and stolen sugar. "Did you send it yet?"

"I'm working on it."

"You've been working on it since breakfast." She plucked the phone from my hand with the confidence of a child who has never once doubted her own jurisdiction over my devices, read the draft, and grimaced. "Papá. *There's flan.* That's your opening line? No wonder you've been alone."

"Excuse me—"

"I'm just saying." She typed fast, tongue between her teeth, then handed it back. On the screen: *Marisol. Tonight's the last night. Everyone who matters to this family will be here. I'd like you to be one of them — not pretending this time. Just you. — C*

I stared at it. My ten-year-old had out-written me in eleven seconds.

"That's—" I cleared my throat. "That's good."

"I know." She took back the phone and hit send before I could stop her, then looked up at me with her mother's eyes and my stubbornness and said, softly, all the performance gone out of her: "She didn't do it to hurt us, Papá. I figured that out already. When are you going to?"

Then she ran off toward the cousins, leaving me standing there with a sent message and no way to take it back, which, I was beginning to understand, was how all the important things in my life got done.

* * *

She came at seven.

I saw her from the kitchen window—standing at the gate in a blue dress, not moving, working up courage the way you work a knot loose, slowly and with both hands. My mother beat me to the door, of course. Rosa Villalobos does not wait for doors to be answered; doors answer to her.

"Marisol Reyes," my mother said, loud enough for the whole patio to hear, which was intentional. "You have some nerve showing your face."

"Mama—"

"Hush, Cyrus. I'm not finished." She looked Marisol up and down with the full weight of forty years of judging potential daughters-in-law, and then she stepped aside and held the door open. "Good. You'll need an appetite. Sit next to me. I have questions."

And that was it. That was the whole gauntlet, and Marisol walked through it with her chin up and her hands shaking only slightly, and I understood that my mother had just done the most Villalobos thing possible: punished first, forgiven second, and never once let on where the line between them fell.

Dinner was carnitas and my tía's tortillas and twenty-three people talking over each other, and Marisol sat in the eye of it, next to Rosa, enduring the interrogation she'd been promised.

"So. The bakery block." My mother passed her the rice without breaking eye contact. "You stopped it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because it was wrong." Marisol set down her fork. "Because I told myself I was fixing my father's mistakes when really I was making bigger ones, faster, with better spreadsheets. Because a woman named Elena Vasquez makes pan dulce at five every morning and has for thirty-one years, and I was going to erase her so a parking structure could go in, and I called that *strategy*." She paused. "Because of your son. And your granddaughter. Because they showed me what staying looks like, and I'd forgotten."

My mother chewed. Considered. The whole table pretended not to listen, which meant everyone heard everything.

"Elena Vasquez catered my wedding," Rosa said finally. "Her mother catered my mother's. If you'd torn that building down, I'd have haunted you personally, and I am *very* persistent." She refilled Marisol's water glass—an honor, in our family, roughly equivalent to a knighthood. "But you stopped it yourself. Nobody made you. That counts. In this family, that counts more than never falling."

"It shouldn't count more than—"

"It counts more than nothing, which is what most people offer." My mother turned away, conversation concluded, territory ceded. "Cyrus, the flan isn't going to serve itself."

* * *

Later, when the little ones were sticky and half-asleep and the adults had migrated to lawn chairs and beer, I found Marisol at the fence line, looking out at the dark strip of beach beyond the yard, where the sandpipers would come at dawn like they always did, indifferent to human drama.

"Hey," I said.

"Hey." She didn't turn around. "Your mother is terrifying."

"She likes you."

"She threatened to haunt me."

"That's how she says it. With Tía Carmen she led with the haunting and *then* offered seconds." I leaned on the fence beside her. Our shoulders almost touched. Almost. We were both being careful about the almost. "Lucia wrote my text, by the way. I want you to know the good parts weren't mine."

"The good parts?" A small smile. "So the flan draft was yours."

"The flan draft was mine, yes."

She laughed—a real one, short and surprised out of her—and then it faded into something heavier. "Cyrus. I need to say this while it's dark and I can't see your face. What I did to you—the lying, the whole time—I keep waiting for it to feel paid off because I fixed the deal, but it doesn't. Fixing it doesn't unmake the weeks you trusted me while I was the thing you needed protecting from." Her voice caught. "You believed me when I lied badly. That's the part I can't get past. You gave me trust I hadn't earned, and I spent it."

"Yeah," I said quietly. "You did."

She flinched.

"And here's what I learned from grief," I went on, watching the dark water instead of her, because some things are easier said sideways. "When Camila died, everyone kept telling me time heals. Time doesn't heal anything. Time just gives you enough distance to decide what you're going to carry forward and what you're going to put down. I put down the anger at you about two nights ago. Not because you earned it back—you haven't, fully, maybe not for a long time—but because I decided I'd rather build something with you than be right about you." I finally turned. "Forgiveness isn't a verdict, Marisol. It's a door. I'm opening it. Whether you walk through is your work, not mine."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then, barely audible: "What if I don't know how?"

"Nobody does. You just show up. Repeatedly. Until showing up is the truest thing about you." I shrugged. "It's how I got through the worst year of my life. One showing-up at a time."

* * *

We were still at the fence when the headlights swept the driveway, and Marisol went rigid against me.

A car I didn't recognize idled at the gate. Emilio Reyes stepped out into the porch light—smaller than I'd imagined, grayer, a man carrying his own demolition inside him—and for a moment nobody moved.

"I called ahead," Marisol said, barely above a whisper. "He wanted to see me before I left town. I told him to come here. I hope—I should have asked—"

"You asked by telling me now," I said. "Go."

She hesitated, glancing back at me, at the lit patio full of my loud, ridiculous, forgiving family. "Will you—"

"We'll be right here." I nodded toward the yard, where Lucia was pretending very badly not to watch. "All of us."

She walked to the gate. Father and daughter met at the property line, and I deliberately did not listen—turned my attention to the flan table, to my mother's raised eyebrow, to anything else—because some reconciliations deserve walls around them even in an open yard.

But I saw the shapes of it. Emilio reaching out; Marisol not quite taking his hand but not stepping back either. His shoulders dropping, the posture of a man setting down something heavy. Her head shaking once—*no* to whatever he offered—and then, after a moment, nodding—*yes* to something smaller. He spoke for a long time. She listened. At the end she hugged him briefly, arms stiff, and he held on a few seconds longer than she did, and when they separated he looked toward the house, toward me, and gave a single nod. Not approval. Acknowledgment. From one person who'd failed someone, to another who was trying not to.

Then he was gone, and Marisol stood alone at the gate for a moment before Lucia broke ranks, sprinting across the grass, and hit her at waist height like a small determined wave.

"You came BACK," Lucia announced, to the entire neighborhood.

"I came back," Marisol said, over the girl's head, looking at me.

* * *

Much later, when the reunion had collapsed into dishes and sleeping bags and my mother pressing leftovers on anyone standing still, Marisol found me in the kitchen drying plates.

"Dessert," she said. "For real this time. You promised flan."

"I promised nothing. Lucia promised flan."

"Close enough." She picked up a towel, stood beside me at the sink, shoulder finally touching mine, no almost about it. "Cyrus. Tomorrow I fly to Meridian headquarters to resign in person. After that—I don't know what comes next. I have no plan. For the first time in my life I have absolutely no plan."

I handed her a wet plate. "Funny. Neither do I. But I hear the bakery's available."

She looked at me. "Elena's lease—"

"Runs out Friday. Which means Saturday morning, there's a storefront on the bakery block with ovens in it, across from a bookshop with more books than customers." I kept my eyes on the plate I was drying, heart hammering like a boy's. "I'm not asking you to move in with me, Marisol. I'm asking if you want to figure out what comes next with a view of the ocean and a shared espresso machine. Business partners. Co-conspirators. Whatever we end up calling it."

She was quiet long enough that I risked looking at her. She was crying a little and smiling a lot, which seemed to be her natural state lately.

"A bookshop-café," she said. "Conchas and paperbacks."

"Lucia's already designed the children's corner. There's a mural involved. We have no vote."

Marisol laughed, wiped her eyes with the dish towel, and took my hand—flour-dusted, flan-sticky, mine.

"Saturday morning, then," she said. "I'll bring the coffee."

Outside, the tide was coming in, and somewhere down the beach the sandpipers were settling in for the night, patient as promises, waiting for the light.
