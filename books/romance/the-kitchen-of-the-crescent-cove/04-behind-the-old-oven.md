## 4. Behind the Old Oven

The range had been in the kitchen longer than Edmund had been alive.

He knew this because Marisol had told him so, once, when he was nine and she'd let him stand on a milk crate to watch her knead dough. *That stove came with the building, mijo. Older than the Cove itself, maybe. You treat old things gently and they'll outlive everyone who ever doubted them.* He had nodded, nine years old and solemn, and gone back to stealing pinches of raw dough.

Now, twenty-nine years later, he stood in front of the thing with a pry bar and a flashlight, and he could not make himself touch it.

The repair list Wren had drafted sat clipped to the refrigerator: *roof flashing, back step, water heater, range.* Four items. Manageable. He'd already done three. The range was last because he had been avoiding it, and he knew he'd been avoiding it, and he told himself it was because parts for a stove that old would be a nightmare to source, which was even true. But it was also because the range was the heart of the kitchen, and hearts were exactly what he had come here to avoid.

He'd been in Crescent Cove nine days. The deed sat in a folder in his rented room, annotated in his own neat hand with comparable listings and a projected sale timeline. Fourteen days remained. He was a man who could reduce a cathedral to a spreadsheet if you gave him a week, and he had given himself two, and the spreadsheet said he would be on a train out of here with a cashier's check and a clean conscience.

The spreadsheet did not account for the flour.

He'd found it the first morning, in the bin beside the range—Marisol's flour, dated the week she died. She'd been baking until the end. She'd been *planning* to bake, which was worse. There were index cards on the counter with a festival menu sketched in her looping hand, quantities multiplied out for a crowd. She had died with her sleeves rolled up.

Edmund set the flashlight on the counter, took hold of the range's edge, and pulled.

It didn't move. Of course it didn't. Things in this kitchen didn't do anything the easy way. He braced, adjusted his grip, and pulled again, and the old range came away from the wall with a groan of reluctant iron and a shriek of something else—a scrape, a papery whisper, and then a soft, obscene little *flump* as something dropped to the floor behind where the stove had stood for half a century.

He stood very still.

Dust hung in the light from the window, turning slowly, like it was deciding what to become. The kitchen was quiet in the way only early-morning kitchens are quiet, before the day has committed to anything. Edmund crouched, and the flashlight beam found it: an envelope, cream-colored, gone soft and yellow at the edges, dust-furred, sealed.

His name for it arrived before he could stop it: *trouble.*

He picked it up. The paper was heavy stock, the kind Marisol used for letters she considered important—she'd had a whole hierarchy of stationery, a taxonomy he'd forgotten he remembered. The front bore her handwriting, and his chest did something complicated and unwelcome, because her handwriting was a voice, and the voice said:

*Mrs. Ilse Brandt*
*Fjordveien 14*
*Bergen, Norway*

The postmark, when he turned it over, read eleven years ago. Eleven years and one month. He did the arithmetic without meaning to, the way you can't stop your hand from touching a burn, and the arithmetic put the mailing date four days after the night he had stood in Marisol's kitchen—this kitchen, this exact square of floor—while Ilse waited on the other end of a phone call he had made with his jaw clenched and his future already decided for him.

The envelope was unsealed. No—wait. It was sealed. The flap was glued down, the wax—no wax, just the glue—intact. It had never been mailed. It was addressed, stamped, postmarked—no, the postmark was on the *stamp*, the stamp was there, eleven-year-old postage, never cancelled. She had written it, addressed it, stamped it, and then she had hidden it behind the stove instead of sending it.

Or she had meant to send it, and hadn't been able to.

Or she had meant to send it, and had lost her nerve, and had kept it the way you keep a splinter, because removing it hurts more than leaving it in.

Edmund sat down on the milk crate—Marisol's milk crate, still tucked by the counter after all these years—and held the envelope in both hands, and his heart was going hard and strange against his ribs.

He should burn it.

The thought arrived fully formed, calm as an auditor. It was addressed to Ilse. It was private correspondence between two people, one of whom was dead and one of whom was eleven years and an ocean gone. He was the estate's executor; his duty was to inventory assets, not to read a dead woman's mail. Burning it was clean. Burning it was *duty.* He could put it in the old wood-fired oven's ash box, strike a match, and in ninety seconds it would be a small gray ghost and he could go back to his spreadsheet and his fourteen days and his carefully contained heart.

He crossed to the sink, actually crossed the kitchen with it, and stood there with his hand on the tap, because the other clean option was water—soak it, pulp it, watch it dissolve into nothing, no ash smell, no evidence. His aunt's last unsent words dissolving like a sugar cube. That was the right thing. That was the *responsible* thing. He was here to settle an estate, and estates were how you made death tidy, and this envelope was the single untidy thing in the whole building—

*You treat old things gently.*

He closed his eyes.

The tap handle was cold under his hand. Behind him the kitchen waited, flour-dusted and patient. He thought about Ilse at twenty-four, sitting across from him in this very room, butter on her knuckle, laughing at something Dev had said, and how she had looked at him on the last night with a face that was asking a question, and how he had answered with a sentence Marisol had helped him build—*it's not the right time, it's the grant, it's two years, it's my future*—and how her face had closed like a door.

He had never answered her letter. Her letter had come in September, and he had read it standing in a university lab at two in the morning, and he had folded it away unread—no, read, read four times, and then folded it away unanswered, because answering meant choosing, and he had already chosen, or believed he had, and the letter had lived in a drawer through two moves and a decade and he had never once taken it out, and he had told himself that was discipline.

It had not been discipline. He understood that now, standing over a sink with his dead aunt's unsent apology in his hands. It had been the same thing Marisol had done: keeping the splinter in.

His hand came off the tap.

He didn't burn it. He didn't drown it. He stood there for a long moment, shaking slightly, furious at himself for shaking, and then he did the coward's thing, which was also, he told himself, the prudent thing: he folded the envelope once, slid it into the inside pocket of his jacket, against his chest, where he could feel it with every heartbeat like a second, smaller, more disapproving heart.

He would decide later. Later was a country he visited often. Later had excellent infrastructure and no consequences.

He pushed the range back against the wall. It went with less protest this time, or maybe he was simply stronger now, or more tired. He retrieved the pry bar. He picked up the clipboard. He wrote, in his neat hand: *Range — serviced, functional, cosmetic wear.* A lie of omission, small and administrative.

The bell over the café door rang.

He knew who it was before he turned around, because in nine days only one person had ever entered without knocking, hesitating, or apologizing to the door itself. Wren Okafor came in carrying a paper bag and a folder and the particular expression of a woman who had been awake since five for reasons she refused to explain.

"Good news," she said. "The title search came back clean. Better news—" she lifted the bag "—Ruth's experimenting with cardamom buns and I have been drafted as a taste-tester, which is a human rights violation, there were nine of them, Edmund, I ate three before I got here."

She stopped.

He watched her stop. He watched her eyes go from his face, to the clipboard, to the range, to the faint smear of dust on the front of his jacket where the envelope had been pressed, and back to his face. She was a lawyer; he had watched her read contracts with the patience of a fisherman mending nets, and he watched her read him now the same way, and he understood, with a lurch, that she was very good at her job.

"You moved the range," she said.

"It was on the list."

"You moved the range and you look like you've seen—" she set down the bag and the folder, slowly "—like you've seen the *sea floor*, Edmund. What did you find?"

"Nothing." The word came out too fast and too smooth, which is to say it came out exactly like the lie it was. "Dust. A dead mouse, possibly. The gap behind these old stoves is a museum of lost spoons." He heard himself continue, appalled and unable to stop, building the lie a little porch and a little garden: "I serviced the valves. It'll hold."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. The morning light came through the east window and lay across the counter between them like something neither of them wanted to pick up.

"Okay," she said at last, lightly, in the voice of a woman closing a folder she intended to reopen. "Dead mouse." She took a cardamom bun out of the bag and set it on a plate in front of him, an act of diplomacy or siege warfare, he couldn't tell which. "Eat something. You look like a man who's been having an argument with a kitchen."

"I have been," he said, and that, at least, was true.

Against his chest, with every heartbeat, the envelope pressed its small, patient weight against him—eleven years old, addressed to a woman in Bergen, carrying whatever Marisol had needed to say and never could. He ate the bun without tasting it, and laughed in the right places, and lied with his whole face.

And Wren watched him do it, and let him, and he could not decide which frightened him more: what was in the envelope, or the fact that she had known he was lying, and had chosen, for now, to let the tide keep its secret.

* * *

That night, in his rented room above the hardware store, Edmund took the envelope out and set it on the desk beneath the lamp.

He did not open it.

He told himself he was respecting the dead. He told himself he was respecting Ilse. He told himself a great many tidy things in his tidy handwriting, and then he turned off the lamp and lay in the dark with the envelope on the nightstand, three feet from his head, radiating its quiet, impossible weight like a stone pulled up from deep water—still cold, still dripping, still holding the shape of the place it had lain.

Fourteen days. Thirteen, now.

Outside, the harbor lights blinked on, one by one, the way they always did, faithful as anything. And in the dark, Edmund Hartwell made the discovery that would undo him more thoroughly than any letter: that a secret does not stay behind a stove. It comes with you. It sleeps on your nightstand. It waits, patient as rising dough, for the moment you are too tired, too lonely, or too honest to keep your hands off it.

That moment, he already knew, would come soon.
