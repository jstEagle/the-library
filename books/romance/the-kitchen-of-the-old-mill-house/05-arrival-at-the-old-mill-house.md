## 5. Arrival at the Old Mill House

### Marisol

The car smelled of Theo's cologne and rented leather, and Marisol watched the valley come up to meet them through a windshield furred with the first flakes of the season. Ten years. Ten years since she had ridden this road in the opposite direction, a duffel bag on her knees and a bus ticket to Lyon in her pocket, and she had promised herself she would never look at the mill house again except through the clean, impersonal lens of a contract.

"Turn here," she said, before she could stop herself. "The GPS will take you the long way."

Theo glanced at her, one eyebrow lifting, but he obeyed. Of course he obeyed. Theo always obeyed the letter of things while quietly rewriting their meaning, and she loved him for his precision and forgave him for it in the same breath, the way you forgive a knife for being sharp.

The long way was shorter. It always had been. It wound along the creek where the willows went bare in November like women pulling off jewelry, and then the road climbed, and then—there it was.

The Old Mill House sat in its shallow bowl of vineyard the way it had always sat, patient as a dog that has learned not to expect the door to open. The stone was the same gray-brown, the slate roof the same bruised dark, the mill wheel long since stilled and furred with moss on its downstream side. The vines marched up the slope behind it in disciplined rows, frost-bitten now, their canes pruned back to knuckled wood. Somebody was still keeping them. Somebody was still out there in the cold every February with shears in his hands.

She had known, intellectually, that Ansel would be here. The appraisal required the owner's presence; the paperwork said so. But knowing a thing and seeing it are different organs of the body, and when she saw the thin gray ribbon of smoke rising from the kitchen chimney—*the kitchen chimney*—her chest did something she had not authorized it to do.

"You're quiet," Theo said.

"I'm reading the site," she said. "Slope, drainage, exposure. It's what they're paying me for."

It was even true. The firm was paying her a frankly obscene consulting fee to assess whether the property's outbuildings could be converted to a tasting facility or would need to be razed. She had read the file on the flight over. She had read it twice. She had not let herself read past the second page, where the photographs began, because she knew what was on the third page and she knew what her face would do.

The car crunched to a stop on the gravel forecourt, and the sound of it was so exactly the sound of her father's truck, all those years ago, that her hands closed involuntarily on her knees.

"Marisol." Theo's hand covered hers, warm, ringed, certain. "Whatever's in there, it's just a house. Stone and timber. It can't hold anything you don't bring to it."

She almost laughed. Almost. Instead she said, "You're right," and got out of the car, and carried everything she had brought to it up the path with her, all ten years of it, in a small leather bag and a body that remembered too much.

The door opened before she knocked.

She had rehearsed this. On the plane, in the shower in Lyon, in a hundred small stolen minutes, she had rehearsed the scene the way she rehearsed a service before a health inspection: calmly, completely, with contingencies. She would be brisk. She would be professional. She would shake his hand—his hand, that was the contingency she had drilled hardest—and say *Ansel, it's been a long time* as if he were the husband of a college friend, as if he were a supplier, as if he were anyone.

But the door opened and it was his face, and the decade landed between them like dropped glassware.

He was thinner. That was the first thing, the stupid first thing, the thing no rehearsal survives. He was thinner and his hair was longer and there was gray in it now, at the temples, a frost she had no right to feel responsible for and felt responsible for anyway. He was wearing a flannel shirt with the sleeves pushed up and flour on one forearm, of all things, flour, as if he had been interrupted in the middle of baking bread in the kitchen where they had—

"Marisol," he said.

Just her name. Her whole name, both syllables, given back to her in a voice lower than she remembered, roughened at the edges like a well-used cutting board. And she felt her rehearsed sentence dissolve on her tongue like sugar in a hot pan, and what came out instead was:

"Ansel. Hi."

*Hi.* Ten years, and she said *hi*, and somewhere in Lyon a version of herself who had rehearsed this for a hundred hours put her head in her hands.

"Hi," he said back, and then, because he had always been the one who rescued conversations, who filled silences the way she filled plates: "You found the shortcut."

"The long way's longer. It was always longer."

"It was always longer," he agreed, and for a moment they stood there in the doorway, two adults performing the small talk of strangers while underneath it something enormous shifted its weight, like a load-bearing wall deciding whether to hold.

Theo came up beside her with the luggage, and she watched Ansel's gaze move to him, take in the coat, the watch, the ring, and file it all away behind a face that had gotten very good at showing nothing. She knew that face. She had grown up on the other side of it.

"Ansel Vasquez," Theo said, extending a hand. "Theo Marchetti. We've spoken on the phone."

"We have." Ansel took the hand. His grip, she noticed, was careful. Too careful, the grip of a man managing his own strength, or his own anger, or both. "Come in. It's cold out, and the forecast says it's about to get a lot colder."

### Theo

Theo Marchetti had prepared for this meeting the way he prepared for everything: exhaustively, and with a private ledger no one else was permitted to read.

The house, when he stepped into it, was worse than the photographs and better than the photographs, which is to say it was exactly what he needed it to be. The entry hall smelled of woodsmoke and cold stone and, faintly, of bread. The staircase to the left had a banister worn pale in the middle by a century of hands. There was a hairline crack in the plaster above the door to the kitchen, running diagonally like a fault line on a map, and Theo catalogued it with something close to satisfaction. Cracks like that were arguments. Cracks like that went into reports.

"You have a lovely place," he said, and watched Ansel's jaw tighten by a millimeter at the past tense he hadn't used and the future tense he had.

"It has its moments," Ansel said. "Kitchen's through there. I was just making lunch, actually. I wasn't expecting anyone until tomorrow—the appraiser."

"Change of plans," Theo said pleasantly. "The weather. We wanted to beat the storm."

It was even true. The storm was real; he had watched it build on the radar in the car with the particular gratitude of a man watching a door close behind him. But the storm was only half the reason, and the other half he kept folded away in the inner pocket of his coat, next to the folded-away thing he had known about Marisol and this house for longer than she would ever guess. He had seen the way she'd said *turn here*. He had seen her hands on her knees in the gravel. He was not a jealous man—he told himself this often enough that the telling had begun to sound like fact—but he was a thorough one, and thorough men count.

He counted now. He counted the way Ansel's eyes went to Marisol when she took off her coat, and the way they went away from her immediately after, like a man snatching his hand off a hot stove. He counted the flour on Ansel's forearm and the fact that there were two place settings at the kitchen table and that one of them, he would have bet the firm, was not usually used. He counted the way Marisol stood in the middle of the entry hall with her chin up and her shoulders set, the posture she used in the kitchen when a dish was failing and she intended to save it through sheer force of will.

"So," Ansel said, rubbing his hands together, brisk, hospitable, a man performing the role of host with the desperate energy of a man performing anything at all. "Can I get you coffee? Wine? I'd offer the tour but I suspect—" he gestured vaguely at Theo's briefcase, at the whole apparatus of appraisal that traveled with him, "—you've seen the file."

"We've seen the file," Theo said. "Files are why we're here. The site is why Marisol is here. She doesn't sign off on anything she hasn't walked herself."

"Of course not." Ansel looked at her. "She never did."

The sentence sat in the air between them, small and casual and loaded like a hair trigger. Theo watched his fiancée's face do something very quick and very controlled, and he filed that away too, in the ledger, under the heading he never wrote down but maintained anyway.

"Coffee would be great," Marisol said, too lightly. "If you're making it. I remember you made it too strong."

"I still make it too strong."

"I'll risk it."

And they smiled at each other, both of them, two small polished smiles, and Theo stood between them in the cold beautiful hall of a house he intended to see demolished, holding the luggage, holding the ledger, holding the storm at bay for exactly as long as he needed it, and thought: *this is going exactly as planned,* and did not examine, even for a moment, why the thought tasted like ash.

* * *

### Ansel

He burned the coffee, which was fitting, and he was grateful for the small catastrophe of it because it gave his hands something to do besides shake.

They were shaking. He had opened the door expecting the appraiser—a soft, apologetic man from the bank, someone he could perform dignity for—and instead the decade had walked up his front path in a camel coat, and every muscle in his body had remembered at once that it was a muscle, and that muscles exist to fail.

*The heart is a trainable muscle.* He had built his whole life since her bus left on that sentence. You trained it the way you trained the vines: pruning, restraint, cutting back everything that grew wild so that what remained could survive the frost. He had gotten very good at it. He could stand in a room with Marisol Reyes, he now discovered, and pour coffee, and say words like *how was your flight*, and only his hands would know.

"Your flight," he said, handing her the cup. Their fingers did not touch. He had arranged that with sniper's precision. "Long?"

"Long. The connection in New York was delayed." She wrapped both hands around the cup, and he watched her take the first sip, and watched her eyebrows rise. "Still too strong."

"You still drink it anyway."

"I still drink it anyway."

Across the kitchen, Theo had stationed himself by the window with his own cup, looking out at the vineyard with the appraising stillness of a man pricing a painting. Ansel let him look. Let him see the frost on the rows, the mill wheel, the slate. Let him calculate. Ansel had done his own calculating, at three in the morning, more times than he could count, and the numbers always came out the same: the land was worth more dead than alive, and the men holding the paper knew it, and the only reason they hadn't already taken it was the last thin thread of a restructuring negotiation he was losing gracefully, installment by installment.

He had not known, until this morning, that the thread led back to this man's family. He had read the letterhead on the appraisal notice and felt the floor tilt under a life he'd thought was already lying flat.

"So," Marisol said, into the quiet. "The site assessment. I'd like to start with the outbuildings, if that's all right. The press house, the cellar. Theo can do the exterior measurements while I'm inside."

"Divide and conquer," Theo said, smiling.

"Efficient," Ansel said, and heard how the word came out of him like a stone.

She looked at him then, really looked, for the first time since the doorway, and he saw the exact moment her professional armor caught on something underneath it—saw her eyes go to the kitchen doorway behind him, to the pans he had never taken down, ten copper-bottomed pans hanging on iron hooks in the same order she had hung them in when she was sixteen and he was seventeen and neither of them had known yet what leaving would cost. He saw her look at the pans the way you look at a photograph of someone you loved who is still, impossibly, alive.

"The cellar's cold this time of year," he said. "I'll get you a lantern. The bulb's been out since—" he stopped. Recalculated. "For a while."

"I remember the cellar," she said quietly.

Of course she did. And he stood there in his own kitchen, host to the two people who held, between them, the paper on his life, and understood that the storm coming down off the mountain was the least of what was going to land on this house before the week was out.

Outside, the first real snow began to fall, thick and silent and unhurried, as if it had all the time in the world.

It did. He didn't.
