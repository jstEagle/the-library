## 7. First Snow

The forecast changed at noon, the way weather does in this valley—quietly, then all at once. Ansel stood in the tasting room with his phone pressed to his ear, listening to a voice from the county road service recite closures like a litany: Route 9 closed at the pass, Route 12 closed at Miller's Bend, secondary roads unplowed until further notice.

"Until further notice," he repeated, and the words sat strangely in his mouth. He looked out the window at Marisol's rental car, parked where Theo had left it that morning, already wearing a thin white shawl of snow on its hood.

One night. They had come for one night. The appraiser was due Thursday; Theo and Marisol had planned to drive back to the city Wednesday morning, and Ansel had built his whole strategy around the arithmetic of their departure—the hours he would have to endure, counted and rationed like the last of a bad vintage. Now the storm had torn up his ledger and written *indefinite* across it.

He hung up and stood for a moment in the cold blue light of the tasting room, listening to the silence of the house around him. Ten years he had rehearsed a sentence for this woman, and now the weather itself seemed to be conspiring to give him time he didn't want and couldn't refuse.

*The heart is a muscle,* his grandfather used to say, back when Ansel was small enough to ride the tractor fender while the old man pruned. *You train it or you lose it.* Ansel had trained his the way you train any muscle under injury—carefully, repetitively, with weight it could bear and no more. He had gotten very good at lifting the ordinary things: the vines, the debts, the seasons. It was only when something extraordinary came near—when Marisol laughed in his kitchen, when Theo's handshake lingered half a second too long, knowing—that the old injury announced itself, a dull ache beneath the sternum, familiar as weather.

He went to find them.

* * *

They were in the front parlor, the room nobody used except when nobody could help it. Marisol stood at the window with her arms crossed, watching the snow thicken over the vineyard rows, and Theo sat in the wingback chair with his phone held high toward the ceiling, hunting signal the way a man hunts water in a desert.

"Roads are closed," Ansel said from the doorway. "Both routes. Plows won't run till it stops."

Marisol turned. In the gray light her face did something complicated and then settled into composure, the way water settles after a stone. "How long?"

"The storm's supposed to break tomorrow night. Maybe Thursday morning."

"Thursday." Theo lowered his phone. "That's when your appraiser comes."

"That's when my appraiser was supposed to come." Ansel heard his own voice go flat and careful, a man walking on ice he knew was thin. "I'll call when there's signal again. These things reschedule."

"Do they?" Theo said mildly. "In my experience, banks don't reschedule. They repossess."

"Theo," Marisol said, not sharply, but with a warning note underneath, the way she used to say his name when a sauce was about to break.

"What? I'm being practical. That's what I'm here for, isn't it? Practicality." Theo smiled his boardroom smile, all warmth on the surface and nothing beneath it but more surface. "Ansel, we're grateful. Truly. You've been a generous host under awkward circumstances. But let's not pretend this visit is anything other than what it is."

"And what is it?" Marisol asked quietly.

Theo looked at her, and for one unguarded second something moved behind his eyes—something Ansel couldn't name and didn't like. Then the smile returned, smooth as poured concrete. "A courtesy call. Before the paperwork."

Ansel's hands wanted to close into fists. He made them stay open at his sides, relaxed, trained. "Well," he said. "Since we're all stuck with each other, we might as well eat well. I've got stores. And wine."

"You'd offer us your wine," Theo said, "while your bank counts every bottle."

"I'd offer anyone wine in a snowstorm. It's not generosity. It's survival." He turned to Marisol because he couldn't help it, the way a compass can't help north. "There's venison in the freezer. If you're still willing to cook."

Something flickered across her face—surprise, maybe, that he remembered she cooked, as if ten years could erase twenty before them. "If you'll let me near your kitchen," she said.

"It was never mine alone," Ansel said, and immediately wished he hadn't, because the sentence landed in the room like a dropped glass, and all three of them stood very still among its pieces.

* * *

The kitchen of the Old Mill House had been built in 1911 and updated never, unless you counted the refrigerator Ansel's father bought in 1987 and the range Ansel himself had installed three winters ago, secondhand, with a cracked enamel door he'd learned to live with. Copper pans hung from a rack above the island where they had always hung—his grandmother's rack, his mother's rack, and for four formative years, effectively, Marisol's rack too, because she had been in this kitchen more evenings than not from the time she was thirteen, doing homework at the corner table while his mother reduced sauces and told stories, until eventually Marisol stopped doing homework and started cooking.

He watched her enter it now the way you watch someone return to a house they grew up in and left. She stopped just inside the door. Her eyes went up—to the rack—and Ansel saw her hand rise an inch off her side, involuntary, reaching for the eight-inch copper sauté that hung third from the left, before she caught herself and folded the motion into pushing back her sleeves.

"Still third from the left," she said.

"Where else would it be?"

She didn't answer. She crossed to the freezer and opened it, and the cold breath of it fogged her face, and for a moment she just stood there, reading the contents like a menu, and Ansel leaned against the counter and pretended not to memorize her.

Theo appeared in the doorway behind them with two bottles from the tasting room, chosen, Ansel noted, without asking—the '19 Pinot and a Chardonnay that was older than all three of them. "I hope you don't mind. I took the liberty."

"It's a snowstorm," Ansel said. "Liberties are permitted."

Theo set the bottles down and clapped his hands once, briskly, a man delegating. "What can I do?"

"Nothing," said Marisol and Ansel, simultaneously, and then the silence that followed was so precisely awful that Ansel almost laughed. Theo's smile tightened by a millimeter.

"I'll build a fire, then," he said, and withdrew.

Marisol began unpacking the venison, and the kitchen filled with the sounds Ansel had spent ten years learning to live without: the knock of her knife finding its rhythm on the board, the hiss of butter going brown in the copper pan, her low hum—she still hummed when she seared meat, some tuneless fragment of whatever was playing in her head at fifteen. He moved around her in the old choreography, fetching shallots before she asked, setting the cast iron where her left hand would look for it, and each small anticipation landed between them like a confession neither had authorized.

"You remember where everything is," she said at last, not looking up.

"You remember where everything is," he answered. "I just never rearranged it."

Her knife paused. Resumed. Outside, the wind had begun to speak in earnest, a long low syllable through the bare vines, and the first real snow came against the windows not as flakes but as a fine dry hiss, like static, like a radio searching for a station.

* * *

Dinner was, by any objective measure, extraordinary. Marisol had done things to the venison with juniper and the last of his frozen blackcurrants that made Ansel understand, viscerally, exactly how far she had traveled from the girl who burned garlic at sixteen, and how much of that girl remained anyway, in the way she tasted from the spoon and handed it to him without thinking, the way she had done a thousand times, so that they both froze mid-gesture, spoon suspended between them, until Theo cleared his throat gently and Marisol set the spoon down and laughed—a bright, controlled laugh, armor polished to a shine.

They ate at the long table in the dining room because the parlor felt too small and the kitchen felt too true. Theo opened the old Chardonnay and talked. That was the thing about Theo, Ansel was beginning to understand: silence frightened him, so he filled it, competently, endlessly—with market analysis, with anecdotes about Lyon restaurants whose names meant nothing to Ansel and everything to Marisol, with a running commentary on the wine that was, Ansel had to admit, accurate. Theo was not stupid. That was the worst of it. A stupid man would have been easier to hate.

"So the appraiser," Theo said, somewhere into the second bottle. "Walk me through it. What's the number?"

"Theo." Marisol again, that warning note.

"No, really. We're colleagues tonight, aren't we? Snowbound colleagues." He swirled his glass. "Ansel, what does the Old Mill House owe?"

Ansel set down his fork. He had spent the evening steering the conversation away from the shoals—away from Lyon, away from the vintage, away from anything with depth—and here was Theo, cheerfully, deliberately, sailing straight at the reef. "Enough," Ansel said.

"Enough to matter."

"Enough that a man learns to stop counting and start working."

"That's either very wise or very expensive," Theo said, smiling.

"Usually both." Ansel lifted his own glass and found his hand steady, which surprised him. "Your turn. What brings Vasseur Capital to a valley with no cell signal and one road?"

For a heartbeat—just one—Theo's smile stayed exactly where it was while everything behind it went still, the way a pond goes still before it freezes. Then he laughed. "Opportunity. My family buys distressed assets. Vineyards, mostly. Pretty ones." He glanced at Marisol. "It's how we met, actually. She was consulting on a property evaluation in Burgundy. I was the client's son sent to babysit the money." He reached over and covered her hand with his, a gesture so natural it looked rehearsed. "Best acquisition I ever made."

Marisol smiled at him. It reached her eyes about halfway. Ansel watched the distance between her smile and her eyes and thought: *she has been practicing too.*

"The roads," she said, standing, gathering plates, changing the subject with the efficiency of a surgeon. "I want to check them before it gets worse."

"They won't be better," Ansel said. "They'll be worse. That's what storms do."

"Then I want to watch them get worse," she said, and carried the plates out, and after a moment Theo followed with the glasses, leaving Ansel alone at the table with the candles guttering in their drafts and the sudden loud absence of conversation.

* * *

Later, the three of them ended up in the parlor because there was nowhere else, and the fire Theo had built had settled into a good deep burn, and the wind outside had risen to a register that made the windows flex. Ansel opened the '19 Pinot with more care than he had ever opened a bottle—corkscrew aligned, foil cut clean, the first pour made slowly, ceremonially, because this wine deserved it and because his hands needed a task that demanded precision or they might have shaken.

"This is the estate bottling?" Theo asked, tilting his glass to the firelight.

"It's the estate's only bottling. Nineteen-nineteen barrels' worth of nineteen-year-olds' ambition." Ansel sat in the chair opposite, the one his grandfather had worn smooth. "We made it the summer before—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "The summer Marisol left."

"It's good," Theo said, after a sip, and Ansel could tell he meant it, and hated that he could tell.

"It's flawed," Marisol said softly. She was sitting on the window side, firelight on one cheek, window-dark on the other. "The extraction's uneven. There's a volatility in the finish." She took another sip. "But the fruit. Nobody taught us enough to be afraid of the fruit."

"We weren't afraid of anything that summer," Ansel said, and the words were out before the guardrail caught them, and Marisol looked at him across the candlelight with an expression he had no training to read.

Theo, mercifully or mercilessly, filled the gap. "So tell me stories. You two clearly have a decade of material and I have nothing but time and this excellent hostage situation." He gestured at the window, where the snow had gone horizontal. "Was she always like this? Terrifyingly competent?"

"Worse," Ansel said. "At fourteen she corrected my grandmother's roux. My grandmother. A woman who once made a grown health inspector cry."

"And what happened?"

"She was right about the roux," Ansel said. "My grandmother made her do it again, properly, and then fed her until she couldn't move, and after that Marisol was family. There was no appeal process."

Marisol laughed—really laughed, briefly, before she caught it—and the sound went through Ansel like warm water through frost, painful in the thawing. Theo watched them both with his glass at his lips, and over its rim his eyes moved from Marisol to Ansel and back with a patience that did not match his voice at all.

"And you?" Theo said to Ansel, lightly, terribly. "Were you always like this? The strong silent type who lets people leave?"

"Theo." Marisol's voice had lost its softness entirely.

"I'm making conversation."

"You're conducting an audit."

"Darling, I'm snowed in with a man who owes my family's firm a great deal of money and a fiancée who grew up in his kitchen. Conversation is the only honest thing left to do." He said it smiling, and raised his glass slightly, a toast to his own frankness, and the fire popped, and outside the wind threw a handful of sleet against the glass like gravel.

Ansel looked into his wine. He could feel the rehearsed sentence rising in him, ten years of rehearsal pressing against his teeth—*the night before you left, I*—and he drank instead, and put the sentence back in its cellar, and said, "More wood for the fire," and got up to fetch it, because a man can train a muscle to hold almost anything, but not forever, and not tonight.

Behind him, in the quiet he left, he heard Marisol say, very low, "Don't," and Theo say, softer than Ansel had yet heard him, "I'm not doing anything. Yet."

* * *

They retired early, because there was nothing else to retire from. Ansel gave them the east bedroom, the good one, with the quilt his mother had sewn, and took the narrow room off the kitchen that had been his since boyhood, and lay in the dark listening to the house talk to itself in the storm—the timbers ticking, the chimney drawing, the wind finding the loose shutter on the south gable that he had been meaning to fix for six years.

Down the hall, faintly, he could hear Theo's voice, low and continuous, and Marisol's answers sparser, monosyllabic, a shoreline eroding. He couldn't make out words. He wasn't sure he wanted to. He lay with his arm over his eyes and performed the old exercise, the nightly discipline: *name the debt, release the debt.* The vineyard's debt, named. His father's mortgage, named. The appraisal Thursday—or not Thursday now, who knew, the storm had swallowed the calendar along with the roads—named.

And then the last one, the one that never released, the principal and interest compounded across a decade: *the night before she left, I—*

The wind rose. Somewhere below, a branch came down with a crack like a shot, and the house shuddered, and Ansel thought of the three of them sealed inside these walls like figures in a snow globe, shaken by a hand none of them could see, waiting to see where they would land when the shaking stopped.

In the hallway, floorboards creaked. Footsteps—light, unhurried, pausing outside his door. He held his breath. The pause lasted three heartbeats, four, five—long enough that he knew, with a certainty that bypassed all his training, who stood there. Long enough that he almost spoke through the door. Long enough that the sentence climbed his throat like a man climbing out of a well.

Then the footsteps moved on, toward the stairs, toward the kitchen, toward the dark, and Ansel lay staring at the ceiling and understood that the storm had only just begun, and that it was falling inside the house as heavily as out.
