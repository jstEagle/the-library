## 1. The Assignment

There are three ways to leave Marlow Bend, and I have tried all of them.

The bus, twice, in my twenties, with a duffel bag and a plan that dissolved somewhere around the county line. The marriage, which was supposed to be an exit and turned out to be a layover. And the slow, dignified retreat into routine, which is the only one that ever worked, if working is the right word for a life that fits you like a coat two sizes too small—wearable, warm enough, impossible to run in.

So when Pearl Okafor came into Ashgrove Books that October morning with the particular brightness of a woman carrying an agenda, I should have recognized the fourth way out of town: being volunteered.

She set a manila folder on my counter, directly on top of the stack of returns I was pretending to sort, which is my favorite form of hiding in plain sight. Behind a counter, holding books, I look busy and slightly stern, and people leave me alone. It's the closest thing to a cloak of invisibility this town offers a woman of forty-three.

"Pearl," I said, not looking up. "Whatever it is, no."

"You don't know what it is."

"I know that folder. I know that face. I've known you since you were eleven and stole a candy bar from the pharmacy and made me swear on my mother's grave I wouldn't tell." I looked up. "I told, by the way. Eventually. Statute of limitations."

"That was a different century." She flipped the folder open with the confidence of a woman who has never once lost a vote at a town meeting, which is true, and slightly sinister. "The library's starting a pen-pal program. Anonymous correspondence. It's in the grant application—'combating isolation and fostering community connection among Marlow Bend's residents.' I wrote that sentence myself. I'm very proud of it."

"Pearl, I own a bookshop. I talk to people all day."

"You sell books to people all day. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone." She pulled out a form and smoothed it flat on the counter with both hands, the way you'd smooth a tablecloth before setting something breakable on it. "You answer questions about books. You recommend. You ring up. You do not talk. Beatrix, honey, I have watched you deflect a conversation about the weather. The weather. You told Mrs. Pruitt that discussing rain was 'redundant, given its evident ubiquity.' She still doesn't know if you insulted her."

"It wasn't an insult. It was an observation."

"It was a fortress with a gift shop."

I want it on the record that I laughed. A short, traitorous laugh, but a laugh, and Pearl heard it, and I saw her file it away the way she files everything, alphabetically, for later use.

"I'm not doing the pen-pal thing," I said. "I write enough. Price tags, overdue notices, the occasional apology to the wholesaler. My correspondence quota is full."

"Everyone in town over forty is doing it. The doctor. The Hollis brothers. Even Deacon Marsh, and that man thinks letter-writing is a gateway to dancing."

"Then you're full. You don't need me."

"I don't *need* you," Pearl agreed, and here she did the thing that made her the most dangerous woman in three counties: she got quiet, and kind, and looked at me the way she used to look at me across the library table when we were seventeen and I was lying about why I hadn't done my homework. "But I think maybe you do."

The bell above the door jangled and saved me for exactly four seconds—a customer, a tourist, wanting to know if we had anything on local history. I sold her a book about the mill and a book about the flood of '61 and did not mention the chapter in the mill book that concerned the fire at the Ashgrove Bakery, because the mill book doesn't have one. I made sure of that, years ago, when the historical society was assembling it. I wrote a very generous check and made a very polite suggestion, and the fire became, in the official record of Marlow Bend, a brief and regrettable structural incident. Twenty years of careful editing. That's what a reputation is, if you're me: a manuscript you keep revising, praying nobody asks for the first draft.

When the tourist left, Pearl was still there, arms folded, patient as a stone wall.

"Everyone in this town thinks they know what happened," she said. "And everyone's wrong in a different way, and you've spent twenty years letting them, because the truth is worse than any of their wrong versions. I'm not asking you to tell me the truth, Beatrix. I'm asking you to write to a stranger. Somebody who doesn't know the story. Somebody who can't look at you across the counter and see the fire before they see you."

"That's the most depressing sales pitch I've ever heard."

"It's the only one that would work on you." She slid the form across the counter and uncapped a pen, holding it out. "One letter a month. You sign with an initial. The library matches people and keeps everything confidential—I don't even know who you get. That's the whole design. You can be anyone."

"I've spent twenty years being anyone. It's exhausting."

"Then be someone new." Her voice gentled. "Or, and hear me out, be exactly who you are, to one person, on paper, where it's safe."

I took the pen. I want to be clear that this was a tactical surrender, the kind of concession you make to end a siege, not the beginning of anything. Pearl has a way of making you think the door you're walking through is the one you chose.

"Fine," I said. "One letter. Singular. And if it's terrible, I'm out, and you'll never mention this again."

"Deal." She was already moving toward the door, because Pearl knows that the moment a deal is struck, the smart move is to leave before the other party regains their senses. She paused at the threshold. "First letter's due Friday. Mail slot in the library lobby. No names, no addresses, no identifying details. Initials only."

"And if I sign 'B'?"

"Then you're B." She smiled, and for a second she was seventeen again too, and I understood that she had known exactly how this would end before she walked in. "Welcome to the program, B."

The bell jangled. She was gone.

* * *

I didn't write the letter that night. Or the next. I dusted the poetry section, which didn't need it. I reorganized the cookbooks by cuisine and then, in a fit of something I refused to name, by decade, which is a stupid way to organize cookbooks and I changed them back within the hour. I told myself the letter didn't matter. It was a grant requirement wearing a costume. It was Pearl's project, not mine. I had a shop to run and a daughter to raise and a crumbling annex out back that the whole town had opinions about, and no time for correspondence with a stranger who couldn't possibly understand—

Well. That was the thought that did it, actually. Sitting at the kitchen table Thursday night with a blank sheet of paper, I caught myself thinking *they wouldn't understand*, and I realized with a small, cold shock that I had no idea what it was they wouldn't understand, because I had never once tried to say it. Twenty years of editing, and I'd edited so thoroughly that I'd lost the original text myself.

So I wrote the letter the way I do everything I'm afraid of: sideways, guarded, with a joke at the front like a sentry.

*Dear You,*

*Let's establish terms. I did not sign up for this. A woman with a clipboard and a lifetime of leverage extracted my participation under duress, and I am writing to you the way one writes to a jury: briefly, politely, and with no intention of revealing anything that could be used against me.*

*I'm told the point of this exercise is "connection." I'm told isolation is a problem to be solved. I would argue that some of us have achieved a very comfortable arrangement with our isolation—we've furnished it, we've hung pictures, we've stopped noticing the draft. Tearing all that down on the promise of "connection" seems, frankly, like a demolition project.*

*Which reminds me.*

*There is a man in this town—no names, you'll get none, I've been briefed—who has made it his life's work to condemn the building behind my shop. He arrives with his clipboard and his tape measure and his face like a closed ledger, and he measures things. Cracks. Settling. Load-bearing capacities. He writes it all down in that handwriting people use when they want you to know their opinion of you is professional and final. He is going to take the old annex down, and everyone will call it progress, and I will stand there and let it happen, because the arguments he'd accept—safety, cost, sense—are all true, and the argument that matters is one I can't say out loud.*

*You don't know what the annex is. Good. Keep it that way, and we'll get along fine.*

*I'm told I should end with a question, to keep the correspondence going. So here is mine, and answer carefully, because I'll be judging you: what's the most useless beautiful thing you've ever refused to throw away?*

*—B*

I read it three times. I told myself I'd soften it in the morning. I did not soften it in the morning. Friday, I walked to the library in the cold bright air, dropped the envelope into the slot, and stood there a moment listening to it land at the bottom of the box with a sound like a small door closing.

Pearl was at the desk, pretending to catalog. She didn't look up. "Due Monday," she said, "in case you were wondering about the next one."

"I wasn't."

"Mm." She stamped a card with unnecessary force. "Whoever gets you is in for something."

"Whoever gets me should be warned."

"Oh, B." Now she looked up, and her eyes were kind and merciless. "The whole point is that nobody gets warned."

I walked home past the annex, the way I always do, the long way that isn't long at all, just the route that keeps the old bakery at my left shoulder like a chaperone. The afternoon light was doing what October light does to that building—gilding the broken windows, warming the scorched brick to the color of bread—and for a moment I let myself remember the smell of it on a morning like this one, yeast and woodsmoke and my mother singing off-key through the kitchen door.

Then I saw, taped to the annex door, a fresh notice. White paper, official border, that closed-ledger handwriting I'd know anywhere.

*NOTICE OF INSPECTION. E. WREN, BUILDING INSPECTOR. DATE: OCTOBER 24.*

I stood in the cold and read it twice, and the strange thing—the thing I couldn't have explained to Pearl or anyone—was that my first feeling wasn't dread.

It was the itch of a letter waiting to be answered.
