## 2. The Flyer She Can't Throw Away

The flyer arrived on a Tuesday, which Orsen would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day she always felt most like a woman pretending to run a bakery rather than an actual baker.

It came the way everything important came into her life: quietly, under the door of her apartment above the shop, while she was doing something mundane. In this case, she was standing at the kitchen counter in her socks, eating cold rice out of the pot and reading the back of a flour bag for lack of better literature. The paper slid across the floorboards with a soft whisper, spun once, and came to rest against the leg of her chair like a cat that had decided it lived here now.

She knew what it was before she picked it up.

She knew because Delia had been *humming*. All week. Delia Marsh had come into the bakery every morning for eleven years—first as a customer, then as a fixture, then as something neither of them had ever bothered to name but both of them understood—and for the past week she had hummed while she watched Orsen work, with the particular tuneless patience of a retired schoolteacher who has decided a student needs time to arrive at the correct answer on her own.

"You've got flour on your cheek," Delia had said that morning, instead of whatever she was actually thinking.

"Where don't I have flour," Orsen said.

"True." Delia had stirred her tea, hummed four bars of something suspiciously close to a fanfare, and left a five-dollar tip in the jar even though she got free refills and everyone knew it.

Now there was a paper on her floor, and Orsen looked at it the way you look at a letter from the tax office: knowing it will not go away by ignoring it, and ignoring it anyway.

She ate another bite of cold rice. The flyer sat there. The radiator clanked its winter complaint. Outside, the first real snow of the season was starting to come down over Maple Row, fat and unhurried, dusting the awning of Amberdale's in white like the bakery was being gently wrapped for storage.

Orsen put down the pot. She picked up the flyer.

*The Winterhaven Festival Bake-Off*, it said, in cheerful burgundy lettering. *Open Division. Grand Prize: $15,000, plus featured vendor status for the spring season.* Below that, in smaller print, the details she'd been avoiding hearing about secondhand for two weeks: application deadline, dates, judging panel. And below *that*, in bold, the name of the head judge, because apparently the universe had a sense of humor after all:

*T. Voss.*

Orsen set the paper down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be rigged to explode.

Then she laughed. It came out of her sideways, one short bark with no humor in it, startling her cat, Biscuit, off the windowsill.

"Of course," she told the empty kitchen. "Of course it's him."

Biscuit gave her the flat golden stare of a creature who has never once cared about human drama, then left the room, which Orsen felt was fair.

* * *

Here is what Orsen did not do that night: she did not throw the flyer away.

This was not for lack of trying. She carried it to the trash can twice. The first time, she stood over the bin with the paper dangling from her fingers and thought about the $15,000, and about the notice currently taped inside the register downstairs—the third one this year, the one she'd folded small enough that she could pretend it was a receipt—and her hand simply refused to open. Her fingers had opinions. Her fingers wanted the prize money more than her pride wanted safety, and that frightened her, so she retreated to the counter and made herself a cup of chamomile she didn't want.

The second time, she got as far as holding the flyer over the sink, lighter in her other hand, because the stove's burner had a draft problem and she wasn't going to burn down her grandmother's building over a piece of paper no matter how much it deserved it. She thumbed the lighter. The little flame wobbled up, orange and honest.

And what she felt, looking at that flame, was not resolve. It was memory. It came up fast and hot as the fire itself, uninvited, the way the worst ones always do.

* * *

Culinary school, nine years ago. The final practical exam of second year, the one that counted double, the one held in the demonstration kitchen with the tall windows and the visiting judges from the city. Orsen had been twenty-one and hungry in every possible way—hungry for the credential, hungry for the escape, hungry to be good at the one thing that had ever made her feel like she belonged anywhere, which was standing at a bench with flour on her hands making something true.

Her grandmother had taught her that torte. The hazelnut one, the rustic one, the one with the burnt-honey buttercream that the Amberdale flagship patisserie in the city had deemed "too rustic to matter" when Marguerite Amberdale proposed it for the menu in 1987. Grandma Ruthie had written it in her notebook in fading pencil and taught it to Orsen at twelve years old, standing on a step stool in this very kitchen, saying, "Fancy is easy, sweetheart. Honest is hard."

So Orsen made the honest thing. She made it beautifully. She remembered the smell of it—hazelnut browning, honey catching just shy of bitter—and she remembered feeling, for one entire hour, like maybe she had a right to be in that room.

Then the accusation came.

One of the instructors pulled her aside afterward, kind in the way people are kind when they already believe the worst. A recipe dispute, he said. Another student claimed the torte was lifted directly from a published Amberdale family recipe, and given Orsen's surname—which was not Amberdale, which was *Hale*, the name she'd been using since she left home at eighteen, the name she'd chosen off a list of common surnames precisely so no one would ever look twice—it should be addressed immediately. There would be a review. The exam result would be withheld.

She never found out who reported her. She had her suspicions—a suspicion shaped like a tall boy with dark hair who had spent three years orbiting her bench, competing with her for every grade, watching her hands when she worked with an expression she'd never learned to read—but she had no proof, and the review cleared her on a technicality (the recipe was unpublished, family-held, hers by inheritance), and still the damage was done. The instructors looked at her differently. The students whispered. And the voice—

The voice was what stayed. Not the instructor's voice. Her own. Standing in that kitchen while her exam sat ungraded, she had heard, clear as a bell, the sentence that would follow her for a decade:

*You'll never be more than a name you don't deserve.*

Because that was the truth underneath everything, wasn't it? She was an Amberdale who couldn't say Amberdale. She had the dynasty's hands and the dynasty's palate and the dynasty's notebook full of recipes, and none of it belonged to her publicly, because claiming it meant claiming the people who had let her walk out of that house at eighteen without following her. Claiming it meant standing in front of a room and saying *I am one of them* when they had made it abundantly clear she was not.

So she'd taken the technicality, declined to appeal for the full mark, finished school a semester early, and come home to Maple Row to hide behind her grandmother's counter, where nobody asked questions because nobody knew there were answers.

The lighter flame had burned all the way down to her thumb before she noticed.

* * *

"Ow," she said mildly, and ran her thumb under the tap, and stood there in her little kitchen with the water running and the flyer lying singed at one corner on the counter, and admitted the obvious: she could not do it. She could not burn it, and she could not throw it away, and she could not stop reading the line *$15,000* over and over until the numbers blurred.

Three payments from gone. That's what the register held. Maybe four if the holiday rush was kind. The festival was in six weeks. The prize money would clear everything—the equipment loan, the back taxes, the terrifying envelope from the bank—with enough left over to fix the oven door that groaned like a haunted house.

All she had to do was stand in front of a judging panel headed by Theo Voss, the boy who had orbited her bench for three years and whose face she still couldn't read, and bake her dead grandmother's recipe under her almost-dead name, in public, where anyone could look up the entry list and see *Amberdale's* printed in burgundy letters and start asking questions.

Delia would say the questions were the point. Delia was always saying things like that, in her gentle schoolteacher way, things like *"A secret kept too long stops protecting you and starts owning you, dear."* Delia said it about other people's secrets, mostly, over cinnamon rolls, in the tone of a woman who suspected nothing and saw everything.

Orsen dried her thumb, picked up the flyer, and did the only thing she could think to do with a document she could neither destroy nor obey.

She opened the drawer under the phone—the junk drawer, the drawer where rubber bands went to die and takeout menus went to be forgotten—and she put the flyer in it, face down, under the stack of expired coupons, and shut the drawer, and stood there breathing.

"There," she told the kitchen. "Dealt with."

The kitchen, wisely, said nothing.

* * *

But that was not the end of it, because nothing is ever the end of it when Delia Marsh is involved, and at seven the next morning the bell over the bakery door rang its familiar jangle and Delia came in out of the snow wearing a coat that predated the moon landing and an expression of elaborate innocence.

"Cold out," she announced, stamping snow off her boots.

"It is December," Orsen agreed.

"Mm." Delia hung the coat on the hook she'd unofficially owned since 2014 and took her seat at the corner table, the one with the view of the whole counter, and accepted her cinnamon roll and her tea without being asked, because some orders transcend language. Then she unwound her scarf with the slow ceremony of a woman settling in for a long siege, and said, casually, to the window: "Funny thing. I slipped an application under a certain someone's door yesterday evening, and I haven't heard a word about it. Not a yes. Not a no. Not even a good old-fashioned *absolutely not, Delia, how dare you*." She sipped her tea. "Which, in my professional experience, means the answer is sitting in a drawer somewhere, being read at midnight."

Orsen, who had absolutely read it again at midnight, face down though it was, felt her ears go warm. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No, of course not."

"I haven't checked my mail."

"The flyer was under your interior door, dear. Your mail doesn't come through your own hallway."

"Then I definitely haven't seen it."

"Mm-hm." Delia broke off a corner of cinnamon roll and regarded it with the patience of forty years of parent-teacher conferences. "You know, I taught fourth grade for thirty-one years. You learn to tell the difference between a child who can't do the thing and a child who's afraid to try. They sit exactly the same way at their desks. Shoulders curled in, eyes down, very busy with their pencil." She looked up, and her eyes behind the bifocals were kind and merciless. "You're sitting like that right now, and you're holding a piping bag."

"I'm filling éclairs."

"You're hiding inside an éclair."

Orsen squeezed the bag harder than necessary and produced an éclair with somewhat aggressive proportions. "Even if I wanted to enter," she said, to the pastry case, "the deadline's Friday. And I'd need a new recipe. Something original. Something—" *that isn't hers,* she didn't say. "It's not practical."

"Practical." Delia tried the word out like a fruit she suspected was rotten. "Your grandmother left you a bakery and a notebook full of recipes, and you're telling me the practical move is to let the bank take both because entering a contest might make someone ask you a question you don't like."

The piping bag went quiet.

Delia softened, the way she always did, one degree, which from Delia was a great deal. "I'm not pushing, love. Well—I am pushing, but kindly. All I'm saying is, I've eaten in this shop for eleven years, and I've watched you give away pieces of yourself like they were day-old bread. And I happen to know—" she tapped the table once, "—that whatever you're afraid of finding out if you stand up in front of people, the rest of us found out about you ages ago. We're just waiting for you to catch up."

Orsen stared at her. For one vertiginous moment she wondered—not for the first time—if Delia knew. If somehow, impossibly, the old woman had pieced together the surname and the hands and the notebook and the way Orsen flinched whenever the Amberdale flagship's holiday catalogue came in the mail. But Delia only smiled her schoolteacher smile, drank her tea, and changed the subject to the weather, which was coming down heavier now, big soft flakes that meant business.

That night, Orsen took the flyer out of the drawer. She smoothed the singed corner flat with her thumb. She read it once more, top to bottom, and her eyes snagged—as they always did, as they were apparently always going to—on the judge's name.

*T. Voss.*

Nine years, and she still couldn't decide which memory of him was the true one: the rival who'd glared at her across every exam kitchen, or the boy who had once, without a word, left a bag of the good Belgian chocolate on her bench the week her grandmother died, and vanished before she could ask why.

She got out a pen. She got out the application form Delia had thoughtfully included beneath the flyer, because of course she had.

She wrote her name—*Orsen Hale*—in the blank at the top, and then she sat there, pen hovering, heart hammering like a beginner's dough, unable to write the next line.

Outside, the snow kept falling on Maple Row, and somewhere across town, in a hotel she didn't know about yet, a man was unpacking a suitcase and thinking about a bakery window.
