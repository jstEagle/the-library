## 4. Flour on His Cuffs

The bell above the door had a particular voice. Delia called it "the welcome," and for five years it had greeted the same small chorus of people: Mrs. Okonkwo wanting her rye sliced thin enough to read through, the twins from the hardware store splitting a single cardamom bun with the solemnity of a treaty signing, old Mr. Petrosian who ordered one almond croissant every Tuesday and always said, "For my wife," though his wife had been gone eleven years and everyone knew the croissant was for him.

Orsen knew all of their footsteps by heart. She could stand at the ovens with her back to the room and know exactly who had come in, what they'd order, and how much change to have ready. That was the architecture of her safety: a bakery built out of familiar sounds, a life assembled from small predictable steps, each one measured so carefully that nothing — no name, no face, no question she couldn't answer — ever got past the counter.

So when the bell rang at 7:42 on a Thursday morning and the footsteps that followed were ones she did not know, her hands went still in the dough.

She didn't look up right away. That was a rule too. Unfamiliar customers got thirty seconds of being served by a woman very busy with her work, because busy women could not be asked questions like *Have I seen you somewhere before?* or worse, *Aren't you—?*

"Good morning," she said to the case of morning buns, which was safer than saying it to a face.

"Good morning."

And then the floor dropped out from under her, because she knew that voice.

Ten years, and she knew it instantly — the low, precise timbre of it, the way he ended his vowels like he was closing a door gently behind each word. Theo Voss's voice had narrated some of the worst moments of her life. It had said *the committee has reviewed the allegations* and *I'm afraid the evidence is quite clear* and once, devastatingly, across a crowded final-semester kitchen, *some people just don't belong here, whatever name they're hiding behind.*

She looked up.

He was older. Of course he was; so was she. But where she had spent a decade learning to be unremarkable, Theo Voss had spent it becoming exactly what everyone at Calloway Culinary Institute had predicted: polished, prominent, and photographed. He stood in her doorway in a charcoal coat worth more than her oven, snow melting on his shoulders, and there was flour on his cuffs.

That detail undid her more than the rest of it combined. Flour on Theo Voss's cuffs, smudged white against expensive dark wool, as if somewhere between here and wherever men like him came from, he had touched dough with his own hands and forgotten to care about the coat.

"Orsen." He said her name carefully, like testing whether ice would hold.

"That's me." Her voice came out steady. A miracle. Somewhere inside her, a woman was screaming into a dish towel. "Welcome to Amberdale's. What can I get you?"

Something flickered across his face — disappointment? relief? — at the professional flatness of it. He crossed to the counter, and she made herself stay where she was, palms dusted, feet planted on the tile her grandmother had laid, in the building her grandmother had bought, under a sign her grandmother had painted. *This is mine,* she reminded herself. *Whatever else is true, this is mine.*

"You're still baking," he said. It wasn't a question about croissants.

"I'm still open. Baking happens to be involved." She slid a tray of cardamom buns onto the shelf with a clack that was louder than strictly necessary. "Can I interest you in something, or are you sightseeing?"

"Sightseeing," he admitted. Then, softer: "Scouting, actually. For the festival. They want judges to visit local venues before the weekend, make sure we know the lay of the land." He glanced around the shop — at the mismatched chairs, the hand-lettered specials board, the jar of lollipops Delia kept stocked for children who cried when the good bread sold out. "It's smaller than I expected."

"People usually say that about things they don't understand."

The words escaped before she could catch them, sharp-tailed and alive, and she saw him flinch — actually flinch, shoulders drawing in a half-inch — and something ancient and hungry in her chest purred. Ten years ago she would have apologized. Five years ago she wouldn't have dared say it at all. Apparently there was a version of herself that lived somewhere between those two women, and she had just met her.

Theo recovered. He was always good at recovering; it had been one of the many things she'd hated about him. "Fair," he said. "That was rude. I've been told I lead with assessments instead of hellos."

"Only been told?" She pulled a second tray from the oven, mostly to give her hands a job. "You must not talk to many people."

"I talk to plenty of people. They're paid to agree with me." He smiled, and it was the smile from the brochures, the one from the magazine covers stacked in Delia's waiting room, and it made her furious how well she remembered its exact geometry. "Two of whatever smells best. And coffee, if you have anything better than the festival catering, which I am contractually obligated to praise next week."

"The bar is underground, then."

"Subterranean."

She boxed two cardamom buns — the ones she'd pulled apart and rebuilt eleven times until the swirl was right, the recipe her grandmother had written in pencil in a notebook Orsen still kept wrapped in cloth under the register — and poured his coffee without asking how he took it. Black. Some things didn't change. She'd watched him drink terrible cafeteria coffee black for two years, grimacing, refusing to admit weakness by adding cream.

He watched her do it and something moved through his expression too fast for her to name.

"How much do I owe you?"

"Six fifty."

He put a twenty on the counter. "Keep it."

"We don't do tips by overpayment. It confuses the register." She opened the drawer, counted out his change coin by coin, and set it down in a neat silver line between them. Thirteen coins and a fifty-cent piece. She took her time. It was petty. It was glorious. Each coin was a small safe step, and also a small declaration: *you don't get to rush me, you don't get to charm me, you don't get to be generous at me in my own shop.*

He looked at the coins. Then at her. "You haven't asked why I'm really here."

"You told me. Scouting."

"Orsen."

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended, and she let it stay hard. "Customers get baked goods and polite conversation. You've had both. That's the full menu."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the snow kept coming down in fat, unhurried flakes, already thickening toward the storm the radio had been threatening all week. The shop smelled of cardamom and woodsmoke and the yeast-warm smell of proofing dough, the smell she had built her whole life inside of, and she hated that he was standing in the middle of it, breathing it, as if he had any right to the warmth.

"I heard the name," he said finally. "On the venue list. Amberdale's. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought it might be a coincidence."

"It is."

"It isn't, though, is it." Not a question. He was looking at her the way he used to look at a soufflé ten seconds before the collapse — reading the structure, finding the fault line. "The handwriting on your specials board. The way you scored those loaves. I used to watch you work, Orsen. I'd know your hands anywhere."

Her heart performed something complicated and painful. *I used to watch you work.* As if he hadn't spent those same years making sure she had nowhere left to work at all.

"There's bread in the case," she said, "and a door behind you. Those are the options."

"Orsen—"

"Sir." She kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice she used with difficult customers and health inspectors. "It's early, I've got forty loaves proving, and you're standing in front of my only register. If there's nothing else—"

He reached into his coat and produced a card. Cream stock, heavy weight, his name embossed in letters that probably cost more per card than her weekly flour order. *THEO VOSS. Executive Pastry Director, Lumen Group. Festival Head Judge, Winterhaven.* He set it on the counter between them, on top of the neat line of change, and pushed it an inch toward her.

"If you ever want to talk," he said. "About anything. About the festival. About—" He gestured vaguely, helplessly, at the space between them, ten years wide. "I'm at the Hartwell Inn through Sunday. Room four."

"I know where the inn is. I live here."

"Right." He almost laughed, and for half a second it cracked the polish and showed something younger underneath, something that looked, horribly, like the boy who had once sat next to her in fermentation lecture and whispered that the professor's diagrams were wrong, and been right, and shared the correction with her first, before anyone else. She killed that memory where it stood.

She did not take the card.

She let it lie there while she boxed his buns, tied the string, and held the box out over the counter. He took it. Their fingers didn't touch; she had arranged that with surgical care.

"Thank you," he said. "For the buns."

"They're six fifty."

"I mean it."

"I know what you meant."

He stood there a moment longer, snowmelt darkening the shoulders of his beautiful coat, flour still ghosting his cuff — and she wanted, violently, absurdly, to ask where it had come from, whether he still baked at midnight when he couldn't sleep, whether the great Theo Voss ever stood alone in some gleaming kitchen with dough under his nails and nobody watching. She wanted to ask and she wanted never to speak to him again and both wants fit inside her at once, crowded and loud.

"Goodbye, Mr. Voss."

"Orsen—"

"Goodbye."

He went. The bell spoke its welcome to his back, which felt appropriate. Through the window she watched him pause on the sidewalk, look up at the sign — *AMBERDALE'S* in her grandmother's looping hand — for a long moment, then walk off down the street with his box of buns and his ruined cuffs, getting smaller until the snow took him.

* * *

She lasted nine minutes before she picked up the card.

She told herself it was to throw it away. That was the stated purpose. She carried it to the kitchen, dropped it in the compost bin beside the eggshells, and walked away with tremendous dignity.

At 8:15 she retrieved it, wiped off a fleck of orange peel, and stood holding it over the sink, where the disposal waited, humming with potential.

*Room four.*

She remembered the last time she'd seen him before yesterday — properly seen him, not in magazines or on Delia's television during some awards broadcast ("that insufferable man," Delia would say, eating popcorn, "turn it up"). It had been the hearing. The academic conduct panel, three professors and a fold-out table, and Orsen standing there with her grandmother's torte recipe in her portfolio and theft in her file, and Theo Voss across the room not looking at her, studying his own hands, which had been clean then too. Immaculate. Everyone had remarked on how composed he was.

She had rehearsed a defense for that hearing. She'd stayed up three nights preparing it, facts and timestamps and the notebook itself as evidence. And then she'd walked in, seen the panel, seen him, seen the word *Amberdale* hovering over everything like weather — because someone had leaked who her family was, and suddenly it wasn't a student accused of stealing a recipe, it was a dynasty's daughter caught cheating, a story everyone already believed — and she had said nothing. Nothing at all. She had signed the withdrawal forms that afternoon and taken the bus north with two suitcases and her grandmother's notebook, and she had not said her own surname aloud in ten years.

*I'm an Amberdale.*

The sentence lived in her like a swallowed stone. She'd been carrying it since she was nineteen, rehearsing it in showers and on buses and into the dark of the back room, the speech she would deliver someday to her father, to the industry, to the world: *I'm an Amberdale, and I built this myself, and you don't get to have it.* A decade of rehearsal. Zero performances.

And now the past had walked through her door with flour on its cuffs and ordered two cardamom buns, and the sentence sat in her throat like it was trying to get out.

"No," she told the disposal, firmly. She dropped the card in the trash instead — a compromise, less dramatic, deniable — and washed her hands, and went back to her loaves.

At 9:30, Delia arrived for her usual Thursday cinnamon roll and found Orsen aggressively scoring sourdough with the intensity of a woman defusing a bomb.

"You've got murder in your shoulders," Delia observed, hanging up her coat. "Who died?"

"Nobody."

"Somebody." Delia peered at her, then at the trash can, then — with the unerring instinct of a retired schoolteacher who had spent thirty years catching seventh-graders mid-lie — fished out the cream-colored card. She read it. Her eyebrows climbed with the slow majesty of a sunrise. "*Theo Voss*," she said. "Head judge Theo Voss. Magazine-cover Theo Voss. The one whose face makes you change the channel while calling him insufferable."

"Give me that."

"Oh, I will not." Delia tucked the card into her cardigan pocket, which was a violation of approximately nine laws. "You're going to tell me everything, over cinnamon rolls, and I'm going to listen the way I listened to Marcus Whitfield explain why he'd hidden a frog in his desk, which is to say with enormous patience and zero belief in your excuses."

"Delia—"

"And Orsen." Delia's voice gentled, and that was somehow worse than the teasing. "Sweetheart. Your hands are shaking."

Orsen looked down. They were. Flour drifted faintly off her knuckles, and she thought, absurdly, of his cuffs — of the one humanizing smudge on all that polish — and of the fact that she had thrown away his card and memorized the room number anyway.

*Room four. Through Sunday.*

Outside, the snow was coming down harder now, the sky the color of unbaked dough, and the radio, when it crackled on, was already using the word *storm*.
