## 6. Storm Warning

The forecast changed at 4:17 in the afternoon, which Theo Voss knew because he was watching the weather app on his phone instead of watching the road, which was exactly the kind of thing he'd have deducted points for if he were judging himself.

*Winter storm warning. Heavy snow. Travel not advised after 6 p.m.*

"Not advised," he said to the rental car, as though it might argue with him. "Not advised is not the same as forbidden."

The car did not answer, but its tires made a sound on the wet asphalt that he chose to interpret as skepticism.

He was somewhere between venues—this was the trouble with a festival spread across three towns, three kitchens, three committees who had each insisted their venue be inspected *personally*, by *him*, before anyone signed anything. He'd finished in Millbrook at two o'clock. Winterhaven proper wasn't until tomorrow morning. And in between lay forty minutes of country road that the sky had apparently decided to close like a shop pulling down its shutter early.

Theo pulled over at the next crossroads, mostly out of principle, and sat with his hands on the wheel while the first flakes came down sideways, fat and indifferent, already sticking.

He should have stayed in Millbrook. He'd been offered a room at the inn there; he'd declined because the inn smelled of potpourri and the proprietor had asked him four questions about his television appearances and none about the festival. He should have driven straight through to Winterhaven this morning and been safely installed at the Hartfield Inn by now, reviewing score sheets. Instead he had spent forty-five minutes of his life standing across the street from a bakery called Amberdale's, telling himself it was a venue inspection.

It had not been a venue inspection.

He turned the heater up and let himself think about it honestly, which was a new and uncomfortable exercise, rather like wearing shoes a half size too small all day and then finally taking them off only to discover the ache had been there so long he'd stopped noticing.

Amberdale's.

He'd seen the name on the festival entrant list six weeks ago—a small café, unranked, no competition history—and something in his chest had performed a maneuver he hadn't authorized. Then he'd seen the town name attached to it, and the maneuver had become a full-scale mutiny. Because he knew that town. He knew that stretch of road. He had, ten years ago, stood in a corridor at Fairholt Culinary Institute and said things into the quiet that could not be taken back, to a girl whose name he had since learned to avoid saying even inside his own head.

Orsen.

Not Amberdale. That was the thing that had kept him awake on the train from the city, that had made him get off two stops early and walk the last mile in the cold like a man doing penance without knowing for what. Orsen Wren. That was the name on her student file, the name she'd used, the name he'd shouted across an exam hall's worth of silence. But the bakery was called Amberdale's, and the woman behind the counter had her grandmother's hands—he'd recognized them through frosted glass, those quick precise movements, flour to the second knuckle, the way she tucked a strand of hair back with her wrist because her fingers were busy—and Theo Voss, who could blind-taste a roux and identify the farm a butter came from, had needed three visits and one very long conversation with a local florist to confirm what his own eyes had told him in four seconds.

She'd changed her name. She'd walked away from the most famous pastry dynasty on the eastern seaboard and buried herself in a small town under a name that fit her like a coat borrowed from someone kinder.

And he had spent five years of his own life becoming exactly the kind of man she'd have walked away from twice.

His phone buzzed. His mentor, Marguerite Chen, who communicated exclusively in messages that read like telegrams composed by a disappointed duchess.

*Where are you. The Hartfield has your reservation. Do not do anything dramatic.*

"I would never," Theo told the phone.

*That is what you said before the Geneva thing.*

"That was one time, and the soufflé was justified."

He put the phone away. Snow was coming down properly now, the fat committed kind, the kind that closed roads and rearranged weekends. The radio said the pass east of here would shut by seven. The radio said a lot of things in the tone of voice people use when they're enjoying themselves.

Theo looked at the map on his dashboard screen. Millbrook: twenty minutes behind him, potpourri, questions about television. Winterhaven: thirty-five minutes ahead, the Hartfield Inn, a confirmed reservation, and—his brain supplied helpfully, treacherously—a certain bakery, which would be closing around six, and which sat, according to the map, approximately ninety seconds' walk from the inn.

Ninety seconds. He could measure that distance in his sleep. He had, in fact, been measuring it all week.

"This is logistics," he informed the windshield. "The festival committee asked me to assess all participating establishments. Accessibility. Parking. Proximity to judge accommodation."

The committee had asked no such thing. The committee had asked him to show up, taste things, and hold a clipboard without dropping it. But the lie was warm and it fit well, and Theo had been wearing lies of that particular cut for ten years—they were tailored, they were expensive, and they had never once kept him warm.

He pulled back onto the road.

* * *

The drive took fifty minutes instead of thirty-five, because the world outside had gone soft and white and slow, and because Theo drove the way he did everything now: carefully, correctly, and without any real conviction that he was headed anywhere worth arriving.

He thought about the corridor at Fairholt. He thought about it more than he wanted to, which was how he knew it mattered.

He'd been twenty-two, brilliant, broke, and terrified—terrified in the specific way that gifted people are terrified, which is that everything they have is built on being the best, and the best is a title other people can take. Orsen Wren had been quiet and exact and strange, the girl who brought in a cardamom honey cake for the midterm that had made old Professor Ibarra close his eyes when he chewed, and Theo had looked at that cake and understood, with the cold clarity of the genuinely frightened, that she was better than him. Not more polished. Not better connected. *Better.* She baked like she was talking to someone she loved, and Theo baked like he was defending a thesis.

And then he'd found out who her family was—the Ambersdales, the Amberdales, the name on every magazine cover, the dynasty with the flagship patisserie and the cookbook empire—and something in him had curdled. Because it wasn't fair, was the thing. She had the talent *and* the name. She had the inheritance of craft *and* the genius. And Theo had nothing but talent, and talent, he'd been assured his whole life, was supposed to be enough.

So when the accusation came—someone claimed her final torte recipe wasn't hers—and Theo, who *knew* it was hers, who had watched her build it layer by layer in the shared kitchen at odd hours, said nothing, corrected nothing, let the suspicion stand and quietly added a log of his own to the fire—that was jealousy wearing the mask of integrity. He'd told himself it was about fairness. Standards. The purity of the competition.

He'd told himself that for ten years. He was, he reflected, steering through snow that was starting to feel personal, an extremely accomplished teller-of-that-story. If self-deception were a category at the festival, he'd have taken gold.

Because here was what he actually knew, in the place where he kept the things he didn't say: he hadn't done it because of the recipe. He'd done it because she'd smiled at him once, in November, over a shared bench, flour on her cheekbone, and he had wanted—so violently, so hopelessly—to matter to her that the prospect of not mattering had felt like a physical injury. And if he couldn't be first in her esteem, he could at least be first on the leaderboard. It was pathetic. He was thirty-two years old and he could admit it now, alone, in a rented car, in a snowstorm, where no one could hear him.

The Hartfield Inn appeared out of the white like a promise kept by accident. He checked in. The desk clerk, a cheerful woman named Bess who had clearly decided they were going to be friends, told him the roads were closing at seven, that the kitchen was serving until eight, and that he was lucky to make it in at all.

"Lucky," Theo repeated.

"Oh, absolutely. Last fellow who got caught out drove into a drift and had to be pulled out by the Hendricks boys. Took till midnight." She slid his key across. "Room 6. Radiator's temperamental but it works if you talk to it nice."

"Does it now."

"Everything in this town works if you talk to it nice." She said it lightly, but something in it landed on him like a hand on the shoulder. "Supper's stew tonight. Good stew. Don't be one of those guests who eats protein bars in his room."

"I would never."

"That's what you said about the radiator."

Theo went up to Room 6, talked nicely to the radiator, which clanked once in a manner suggesting it would consider his request, and stood at the window watching the storm erase the street.

From Room 6, if he leaned, he could see the corner where Amberdale's sat, its windows glowing warm gold against all that blue-white dark. A light was still on inside. Closing-time light. One figure moved behind the glass, small and steady, doing whatever closing involved—wiping counters, probably. Turning chairs onto tables. Building a small fortress of order against the dark, the way she always had. Even in the shared kitchen at school, her station had been the clean one, the calm one, the one you looked at when everything else had gone to chaos.

He should stay here. That was the correct decision. Stew, score sheets, an early night, a professional arrival tomorrow. Any competent judge would stay here.

But the light was on. And the roads were closing. And there was a version of tomorrow—he could see it with terrible clarity—in which the storm passed, the festival proceeded, and Orsen Wren, or whoever she was now, stood at her little table with her entry while he sat above her with a clipboard, and neither of them ever said the true thing, and he carried it another ten years, and it got heavier, the way stones do.

Marguerite texted again: *Are you settled?*

Theo typed: *Settled.* Then deleted it. Typed: *Define settled.* Deleted that too. Finally sent: *Yes. Reviewing notes.*

*Liar,* Marguerite replied, with the serenity of a woman who had known him for fifteen years and had once watched him eat an entire failed croquembouche alone in a stairwell rather than admit defeat.

He put on his coat.

* * *

The walk took four minutes instead of ninety seconds because the snow was knee-deep in places and because Theo stopped twice, once at the corner and once halfway down the block, to conduct negotiations with himself that he lost both times.

The bakery glowed. Through the glass he could see her at the counter, a ledger open, a pencil moving. She looked—there was no other word for it—*safe*. Contained. A woman who had built a life the size of exactly one room and kept it swept.

He raised his hand to knock on the window and then, appalled at himself, lowered it. What was the plan? *Hello, I'm the judge of the festival you may enter, also I destroyed your career prospects a decade ago, also it's snowing, also I've missed you in a way I refuse to examine*? There was no plan. There had never been a plan. Plans were for men who hadn't spent a week orbiting a bakery like an anxious moon.

He was turning to go—honestly, genuinely turning, one foot already committed to retreat—when the door opened.

She stood in it with the lights behind her, a dish towel over one shoulder, and the wind pushed snow into the doorway between them like a curtain neither of them had asked for.

"You've been out there," she said. Not a question. "Twice today. Once yesterday. Through the glass." Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone holding a door open exactly wide enough. "If you have festival business, business hours ended at six."

"It's—" Theo began, and discovered that ten years of polished eloquence had abandoned him entirely. "Logistics," he managed. "Accessibility assessment. For the—"

"In this?" She gestured at the storm with the dish towel, a small contemptuous flag. "You walked through a blizzard to assess my doorway?"

"The roads are closing," he said. Which was true. "I'm at the Hartfield. I saw your light. I thought—" He stopped. He made himself stop, because the next word out of his mouth was going to be *you*, and he wasn't ready, and she deserved better than a man weaponizing a snowstorm just to stand near her.

She waited. She had always been good at waiting; it was one of the many ways she'd been better than him.

"I thought I should check the building held heat properly," he said finally, weakly, "in case it's selected as a satellite venue."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a laugh, but the ghost of one, the memory of laughing, filed away but not discarded. "Satellite venue," she repeated.

"For the festival."

"You're lying," she said, with a terrible mildness. "Badly. You used to lie beautifully. In third year you told Professor Ibarra your oven 'failed' when you'd simply forgotten to turn it on, and he believed you, because you could make nonsense sound like weather reports."

The wind gusted. Somewhere down the street, a branch cracked and fell with a muffled whump.

"My car," Theo said, abandoning logistics entirely, "is at the inn. My reservation is at the inn. I am fully aware that I have no reason to be standing on your step, and I want you to know that I know it."

Her eyes narrowed. Behind her, the bakery breathed warmth out past her shoulders—butter and cinnamon and yeast, the smell of the only church Theo had ever believed in.

"So why are you?" she asked.

And there it was. The question, plain as a cleared counter, and every rehearsed answer he owned suddenly worthless. The snow came down between them. The light behind her made a halo of the flyaway hairs she hadn't bothered to pin, and Theo Voss, who had built a whole glittering career on never being caught off guard, stood in a drift with his cuffs soaking and told the truth, or the nearest piece of it he could carry:

"Because the forecast says the storm won't break until morning," he said, "and I couldn't bear the idea of leaving things the way they are between us for one more night than I have to."

She didn't move. The towel twisted slowly in her hands.

"That's not logistics," she said.

"No," Theo admitted. "It isn't."

For a moment—one long, suspended, snow-filled moment—he thought she was going to close the door. He could see her deciding, see the whole architecture of her caution assembling itself brick by brick, and he deserved every brick.

Then, behind her, the lights flickered. Once, twice. The hum of the ovens dipped and recovered.

"Storm's picking up," she said at last, not looking at him, looking past him at the white chaos of the street, where the world was disappearing inch by inch. "Power'll go before midnight if it keeps this up. It always does."

"Is that—" He hesitated. "Is that an invitation?"

"It's a weather report," said Orsen, and stepped back from the doorway, leaving it open behind her—an opening exactly wide enough for one man, one apology ten years late, and whatever else the storm intended to deliver.
