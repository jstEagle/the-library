## 10. Ten Years of Rehearsal

The confession sat between us like a third person at the table.

Theo had told me what he did—told me in the flat, careful voice of a man handing over something breakable—and now the kitchen was so quiet I could hear the storm pressing against the windows like a large animal leaning its weight on the house. The bread we'd baked at one in the morning had gone cold between us. Flour still dusted his forearms. There was a smear of it on his jaw that he didn't know about, and for some reason that detail kept snagging me, kept pulling me back from the edge of the thing I had to say next.

Because he'd given me his worst. It seemed only fair—only *honest*—to give him mine.

"You reported me," I said slowly, testing the words, "because you were afraid of my name."

"Yes."

"That's funny," I said, and I laughed, a small cracked sound with no humor in it at all. "Because I spent ten years trying to make sure nobody ever knew it."

Theo went very still. He had the particular stillness of a man who has just realized he's standing on ice and can hear it talking.

"Orsen—"

"Not Amberdale." I held up a hand, flour-white, trembling only a little. "Let me do this in order. I've rehearsed it, you see. Ten years of rehearsal. I know all my lines." I smiled, and I could feel how wrong the smile looked on my face, like a hat worn backwards. "I've just never had an audience."

He didn't move. He didn't fill the silence with comfort or questions or any of the things people do to make hard moments easier, which would have made them impossible. He just waited. And I understood, in some distant, clinical way, that this was the same discipline that had made him a champion—the ability to hold still while something delicate rose—but turned toward gentleness instead of winning.

I looked down at my hands. At the counter my grandmother had leaned on for forty years. At the final notice taped to the register, which I'd flipped face-down before we started baking, out of shame or superstition or both.

"My name isn't Orsen Hartwell," I said. "Well—it is, legally. Has been since I was nineteen. But I was born Orsen *Amberdale*. Of the Amberdales. The pastry dynasty. The ones with the flagship store on Fifth Avenue and the cookbook that won awards and the Christmas catalog that people actually wait for." My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "My father is Edmund Amberdale."

I watched the recognition land on Theo's face in stages: first blankness, then the flicker of a name remembered from magazine covers and competition programs, then—worst of all—understanding. The kind of understanding that rearranges everything behind it.

"The Hartwell thing—" he started.

"Hartwell was my grandmother's first name. Marguerite Hartwell Amberdale. When I left, I took the part of her name that belonged to her, not to them." I traced a line through the flour on the counter, an absent spiral, the shape of proofing dough. "She's why I'm here. She's why there's a bakery here at all."

* * *

Here is what happened, though I didn't say all of it in order, because grief doesn't come in order; it comes in whatever sequence will hurt least at the time, which usually means it hurts most overall.

I grew up in that house on Fifth Avenue the way hothouse flowers grow: carefully, expensively, and under glass. My father had plans for me before I could walk. Pastry school at sixteen, apprenticeship in Lyon at eighteen, executive position in the family empire by twenty-five. There was a photograph of me in a tiny chef's jacket that they used in press materials when I was seven. *The Next Generation of Amberdale Excellence.* I used to look at that photograph and feel like a product on a shelf, priced and shelved and waiting for someone to buy me.

And then there was Grandmother Marguerite, who had married into the dynasty and never quite been absorbed by it. She kept a notebook—this battered, grease-spotted, flour-dusted notebook full of recipes she'd learned from her own mother in a kitchen not much bigger than mine. Rustic things. Humble things. A torte made with brown butter and burnt honey that she called *ugly-delicious*, because it looked like a mistake and tasted like a revelation.

When I was seventeen, I entered one of her recipes in a school showcase. Just a small thing—a plum galette, folded crooked, dusted with too much sugar because I'd been nervous. It won. Not a ribbon; the whole thing. Best in show. And my father stood in the audience afterward with an expression I will remember until I die, and he said, "We'll have the test kitchen refine it. That presentation was embarrassing. We don't serve rustic."

*We don't serve rustic.*

As if the galette were a waiter who'd spilled wine. As if my grandmother's hands were an embarrassment.

"I ran away at nineteen," I told Theo. His eyes hadn't left my face. "That sounds dramatic. It wasn't dramatic. It was a Tuesday. I packed two bags, took the train north until the money ran out, and got off here because the station had a nice bench and I was tired. Grandmother found me within the week—of course she did; she always knew where I was, mothers and grandmothers have radar—and instead of dragging me home, she bought this building. Signed it over to me. Told my father I'd gone abroad to study." I swallowed. "She visited every month until she died. Five years ago. She taught me everything in that notebook. And she made me promise one thing."

"What thing?"

"That I'd never let the name be used against me again. So I buried it. Changed it legally. Told everyone I was from nowhere, related to no one, that Hartwell was just a name I liked." I laughed again, that broken little sound. "Delia thinks I'm an orphan. Half the town thinks I trained in France. I let them think it. It was easier than the truth, and the truth felt radioactive anyway. Say 'Amberdale' in certain rooms and people either bow or reach for knives."

"And culinary school?" Theo asked quietly. "You said—I always assumed—"

"I went. For two years. Under Hartwell, obviously. Nobody knew. I made sure nobody knew." I finally lifted my eyes to his. "Until the final exam. Until someone reported me for stealing a recipe I'd invented myself, and the committee pulled me apart like a loaf to check the crumb, and I thought—if this is what happens when nobody knows my name, imagine what happens when they find out whose daughter I am. They'd say the fix was in. They'd say Daddy bought the verdict. So I withdrew. Before they could finish deciding. I walked out mid-hearing and I never went back."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Theo's face had gone through several colors, none of them good.

"That was you," he said. "You're the student who withdrew. They never told us who—there was a gap in the roster, and Vasseur talked about it for years, the one who got away, the galette girl—"

"The galette girl," I repeated. Something hot pressed behind my eyes. "Ten years, and that's my legacy. A gap in a roster."

"No." His voice came out rough. "Orsen. No. Listen to me—"

"No, you listen." And here it was. Here was the sentence. Ten years of rehearsal, and my heart was going like a bird trapped in a window, throwing itself at the glass over and over. "You want to know the worst part? The truly pathetic part? I've been rehearsing a sentence for a decade. Every morning when I unlock this door. Every time someone asks about my family and I lie. Every time I flip that final notice face-down so nobody sees it. I stand in front of the mirror—or the register, or the ovens, anywhere—and I say it in my head. *I'm an Amberdale, and this bakery is mine.* Over and over. Ten thousand times. Like a prayer, except prayers are supposed to give you courage and this one just... sat there. In my chest. Unspoken." My breath shuddered out of me. "Because saying it means my father might hear it. Saying it means the dynasty might come here, to my grandmother's kitchen, and look at my ugly-delicious tortes and call them rustic. Saying it means being visible, and visible is where I got destroyed last time."

The storm threw a gust against the house hard enough to rattle the pans hanging above us. Neither of us flinched. We were both past flinching.

"You're the first person I've ever told," I said. "Not Delia. Not anyone. Ten years, and you're the first. Isn't that insane? You, of all people. The man who—" I gestured vaguely, a gesture that meant *reported me, ruined me, broke me at nineteen* and couldn't quite land on any of it. "If my life were a novel, an editor would cut you. Too cruel to be believable."

Something moved through Theo's expression—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, sad and self-aware. "Editors love a redemption arc," he said softly. "They just make you earn it."

* * *

We sat with it for a while. The bread, the flour, the storm. I poured us more of the tea that had gone lukewarm hours ago and we drank it anyway, because neither of us wanted to be the one to move and break whatever fragile architecture we'd built out of honesty.

"Why tell me?" he asked eventually. "Why now?"

I considered lying, out of habit. Then I considered that I had apparently retired from lying as of approximately one hour ago.

"Because you gave me your worst thing first," I said. "You handed it over without polishing it. And I thought—if he can carry his guilt into this kitchen and set it on the counter, the least I can do is put my fear down next to it. They match, sort of. Guilt and fear. They've been keeping each other company for ten years, yours and mine. It seemed rude to separate them now."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Theo said, deadpan, "and I once dated a food stylist."

I laughed—really laughed this time, startled by it, the sound escaping before I could vet it. It echoed strangely in the dark kitchen, bright against the storm. When had I last laughed like that? I genuinely couldn't remember. That seemed like important data.

But then the laugh faded, and what was underneath it was raw and new and terrifying, and Theo must have seen it on my face, because he set down his cup and gave me his whole attention, the way he had all night, the way almost nobody ever had.

"Say it," he said.

"Say what?"

"The sentence. The one you've rehearsed ten thousand times." His voice was gentle and absolutely serious. "You said you've never had an audience. I'm right here. I'm not going anywhere—the roads are closed, I literally cannot leave." A beat. "Say it to me. Practice. I'll be the dress rehearsal."

My heart slammed. "It'll sound stupid out loud."

"It won't."

"It's been living inside me so long it's grown roots. Pulling it out might kill it."

"Orsen." Just my name. He said it like it was worth saying slowly.

I gripped the edge of the counter—the same counter my grandmother had gripped, probably, on her own hard nights—and I looked at him, this impossible man with flour on his jaw and his worst sin sitting openly on the table between us, and I understood that the rehearsal was over whether I wanted it to be or not. The storm had seen to that. He had seen to that. Ten years of small safe steps, and tonight the floor had simply run out.

"I'm an Amberdale," I said.

My voice shook. The words came out hoarse, like a door opening after a decade shut—stiff, squeaking, letting in cold air and light all at once.

"And this bakery is mine."

The kitchen didn't explode. The earth didn't open. The storm went on raging and the ovens ticked as they cooled and somewhere a pipe knocked twice, indifferent. The world received my sentence the way the world receives most true sentences: without ceremony.

But something in my chest unlocked. Some muscle I'd been clenching since I was nineteen went slack all at once, and I felt dizzy with the release of it, and to my horror my eyes filled.

Theo didn't reach for me. He just stayed exactly where he was, solid and present, and said, "There. Now it's real. Now it exists outside you." A pause. "For what it's worth—it suits you out loud. Better than in rehearsal."

"You have no idea what you've done," I managed, wiping my face with a floury wrist, which stung. "I can never unsay it. You've ruined my whole system. Ten years of careful invisibility, dismantled in one snowstorm by a man with dough on his face."

"There's dough on my face?"

"Jaw. Left side. It's been there for an hour."

He swiped at it and missed. I laughed wetly and pointed, and he tried again, and missed again, and something in the absurdity of it—the two of us, wrecked open at three in the morning, chasing flour ghosts—felt like the beginning of something I didn't have a word for yet.

Then I stopped laughing, because a thought arrived, cold and clear as the weather outside.

"The festival," I said.

Theo's head tilted. "What about it?"

"If I enter—if I *were* to enter, hypothetically, which I'm not saying I will—everyone would know. The application has a legal name field, Theo. And if the Amberdales hear that a bakery called Amberdale's showed up at Winterhaven with my grandmother's recipes..." I stared at the face-down final notice on the register. "They'll come here. He'll come here. And everything I just said out loud stops being a rehearsal and becomes a declaration of war."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind changed pitch, the way storms do when they're deciding what to do with you next.

"Then you'd better decide," he said quietly, "which sentence you're going to live by. The one you rehearsed—or the one you just survived saying."

And I looked at the taped notice, and at my grandmother's notebook on its shelf, and at the man who had carried his guilt across a decade to lay it at my feet, and I realized with a lurch that the decision had already begun making itself—that somewhere between the flour fight and the confession and the sentence spoken aloud, the ground had shifted under my small safe life, and there was no path back to invisible that didn't run straight through everything I'd just become.

The storm would end by morning. Roads would reopen. And I had a feeling that whatever came down that road next was going to demand an answer.
