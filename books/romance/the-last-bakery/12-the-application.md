## 12. The Application

The pen was still in my hand when Delia came through the back door.

I want it noted, for the record of my own conscience, that I did try to hide it. I tried the way a child tries to hide a cookie behind her back—as though the evidence of my guilt weren't smeared all over my face, and my hands, and, apparently, the entire counter of my own bakery. The application lay in front of me, face-up, every line filled out in my small, cramped, unmistakable handwriting. *Orsen Amberdale. Amberdale's Bakery. Category: Heritage Torte.* My name. My whole name. The one I hadn't written on anything official since I was nineteen years old, when I'd signed a lease with a trembling hand and told myself *Hartwell* was a fresh start and not a hiding place.

Delia stopped in the doorway with her canvas tote full of library books and looked at the paper. Then at me. Then at the paper again.

She didn't say a word.

That was the thing about Delia—thirty-four years of teaching seventh graders had burned all the wasted words out of her. She could convey an entire lecture with one raised eyebrow. What she conveyed now was something more like a benediction. She crossed the kitchen, lifted the application off the counter with two fingers, and pinned it to the refrigerator with the apple-shaped magnet she'd given me my first Christmas at the bakery. Right at eye level. Dead center.

Then she took off her coat, put the kettle on, and said, "I hope you're planning to make the lemon ones today. Mrs. Prewitt asked after them twice and she's not getting any younger."

"I—" My voice came out strange. Thin. "I hadn't decided."

"Well, decide. She'll be here at eleven and she'll sulk until Thursday if she misses them." Delia got down two mugs, the chipped blue one and the one painted with wrens, and set them on the counter with a small decisive clink. "Also, you'll want to test that torte at least six times before the festival. Seven, if you're smart. Competition ovens are liars."

I stared at her. "Aren't you going to say anything?"

"About what?" She was the picture of innocence, pouring water over the tea like a woman with no interest whatsoever in refrigerator décor. "I see you've done some paperwork. Very tidy handwriting. Your grandmother would be pleased. She always said you wrote like the words mattered."

The kettle's whistle filled the silence while I tried to remember how to breathe like a person who had signed her own name on purpose.

"Delia."

"Orsen." She handed me the wren mug. "Drink your tea. Courage keeps better when it's warm."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about doing a brave thing: the brave thing is the easy part. It takes thirty seconds to sign a form. It takes the other twenty-three hours and fifty-nine minutes of the day to live with having done it.

I got through the morning on muscle memory. Lemon bars—because Mrs. Prewitt had asked, and because there is a kind of mercy in work your hands can do without you. I rolled, I zested, I dusted, and all the while the application hung on the fridge like a small white sun, and I kept catching myself looking at it the way you look at a bruise. Pressing on it to see if it still hurts.

It did. But underneath the hurt was something else, something I didn't have a name for yet. It felt a little like the first hour of daylight after a long illness, when you notice the light and realize you'd forgotten what it was.

At half past two, when the last customer had gone and the light through the front windows had gone honey-colored, I did the thing I'd been circling all day. I took my grandmother's notebook down from the shelf above the flour bins.

It's not much to look at. A school exercise book, the old-fashioned kind with a marbled cover gone soft at the edges, held together with a rubber band that Delia had replaced twice. My grandmother had written in pencil—she said pen was for people who never changed their minds—and her handwriting slanted hard to the right, as if she were always in a hurry to get to the next idea. I'd read it a hundred times. I'd baked from it a hundred times. But I had never baked from *these* pages, the ones near the middle, the ones I'd folded the corners down on and then unfolded and then folded down again, the way you dog-ear a letter you can't decide whether to answer.

The rustic recipes. The ones she'd made before the Amberdale name got grand, before the flagship shop in the city with its marble counters and its pastry chefs in pressed whites. The chestnut torte with the cracked top. The honey cake she'd baked in a dented tin. The plum thing with no proper name, just *for September, when the tree forgives us.* The ones my father had looked at—once, at a family dinner I wasn't supposed to remember being present for—and dismissed with a word I'd carried in my chest for twenty years.

*Rustic.* Said the way you'd say *shabby.* Said about the recipes my grandmother loved most, the ones from before the dynasty, the ones that had built the dynasty in the first place, before everyone agreed to forget that.

"Too rustic to matter," I said out loud, to the empty kitchen, and was surprised to find that my voice didn't shake. It sounded, in fact, almost angry. Well. That was new. That was possibly useful.

I tied on my apron. I took down the chestnut torte page, unfolded the corner for the last time, and got to work.

* * *

The first attempt was a disaster, and I mean that in the most technical sense. My grandmother's notes assumed a cook who already knew things—*bake until it looks right, you'll know*—which is charming in a dead woman and maddening in a recipe. The chestnut batter split. The top cracked in the wrong way, the way that means structure failure and not rustic charm. At six o'clock I stood over a pan of something that looked like a small brown landslide and felt the old familiar voice rise in me, the one that sounded like a lecture hall full of students and one boy in the front row who never had to try: *You were never going to be more than this.*

I put the pan in the sink. I did not cry. I want that on the record too.

The second attempt, I slowed down. I read her notes the way I'd learned to read her letters—between the lines, in the margins, in the little pencil asterisks that meant *this matters, pay attention.* *Cool the chestnuts fully,* she'd written, and underlined it twice. *Patience, pet. The batter knows when you're rushing.* Which is nonsense, obviously. Batter doesn't know anything. But I cooled the chestnuts fully, and I did not rush, and when the torte came out of the oven it had cracked down the middle in a long, honest seam, and it looked—there's no better word for it—it looked *true.* Like something a person made because she loved it, not because a magazine was watching.

I cut a slice. I ate it standing at the counter with the notebook open beside me, and it tasted like my grandmother's kitchen, which is to say it tasted like being loved by someone who never once asked me to be smaller.

That was when I understood, standing there with crumbs on my apron and my eyes burning, what the application on the fridge actually was. Not a form. A sentence. The same sentence I'd been rehearsing for ten years, written down in my own hand where I'd have to look at it every morning. *I'm an Amberdale.* Not the dynasty's version. Hers. The dented-tin version. The *too rustic to matter* version, which had mattered all along, which had mattered enough to build everything my father was so busy guarding.

* * *

Delia came back the next morning with the paper and a look of profound satisfaction, because the festival entry list had been published in the local section and Amberdale's was on it.

"Heritage Torte," she read aloud, with relish. "Entered by O. Amberdale. Well, well."

"Say it any louder and the whole street will know."

"The whole street *should* know." She set the paper down and studied me over her reading glasses. "Have you slept?"

"Four hours. The second torte was better than the first."

"And the third?"

"Is in the oven now."

She smiled then, the slow smile she saved for report cards and other small miracles, and reached up and touched the application on the fridge—just touched it, one finger, the way you'd touch something you were proud of. "You know," she said, "I taught a lot of children who were afraid of their own handwriting. Afraid of what it would say about them if they wrote the truth down." She tapped the paper once. "It always turned out the handwriting wasn't the dangerous part. It was the people who'd told them it was."

The oven timer went off.

The third torte came out golden, cracked, and unrepentant, and I was carrying it to the cooling rack when the phone on the wall rang—the old landline, the one almost nobody called anymore.

I almost didn't answer it. But the torte was warm in my hands and my name was on the fridge and something in me that morning was tired of flinching, so I set the torte down, wiped my hands, and picked up the receiver.

"Amberdale's Bakery," I said. "Good morning."

There was a pause on the other end. A polished, city pause. Then a voice I didn't know, smooth and careful as a pressed napkin:

"Good morning. Am I speaking with the proprietor of Amberdale's? The—entrant in the Winterhaven Festival?"

My stomach dropped the way it does in the first second of a fall. "This is she."

"Wonderful. My name is Corinne Ashe; I'm calling on behalf of Mr. Edmund Amberdale." The name landed in the kitchen like a dropped glass. "He's asked me to convey a message regarding your festival entry."

I looked at the fridge. At my name in my own handwriting. At the apple magnet holding it up.

"Go on," I said, and my voice was steady, and somewhere in the back of my mind a small astonished voice noted that fact for later.
