## 18. The Bake

The Winterhaven Festival hall smelled like every dream I'd ever been afraid to have.

Sugar and browning butter and the faint industrial hum of a hundred ovens working at once, all of it layered over the sharper scent of nerves—sweat under cologne, coffee gone cold on folding tables, the particular tang of people trying very hard not to look terrified. Forty competitors. Forty stations in neat rows under strings of white lights, each one a small kingdom of mise en place and hope.

My station was number twenty-three, which felt like an omen I couldn't decode. Third row from the back, near the loading door that kept sighing open and letting in knives of February air. I had chosen to think of it as the quiet corner. Delia would have called it what it was: the seat closest to the exit.

"Stop rearranging your piping tips," Delia said. She'd claimed a spot in the front row of spectator seating with the authority of a woman who had supervised forty years of schoolchildren and was not about to be intimidated by a bake-off. She wore her good wool coat and a scarf she'd knitted herself and an expression of ferocious confidence on my behalf, as if confidence were something she could lend me by the hour.

"They're not arranged," I said. "They're *staged*."

"They're being fussed with. There's a difference." She reached through the rope barrier and squeezed my wrist once, hard enough to mean it. "Orsen. Look at me."

I looked at her. Behind her, the hall stretched out in its glittering chaos—a banner reading WINTERHAVEN FESTIVAL BAKE-OFF, GRAND PRIZE $25,000; a judges' table draped in white linen, empty now but ominous; a crowd already thickening, locals and food bloggers and families with sticky-fingered children who kept drifting toward the sample tables. Somewhere in that crowd, possibly, was a man named Edmund Amberdale. I hadn't seen him yet. My body had decided this meant he wasn't coming. My body was a liar and we both knew it.

"You've made this torte forty-one times," Delia said. "I have eaten eleven of them. I am still alive, still delighted, and still asking for seconds. Whatever happens today, you walk out of here having made something true. That's more than half these people can say."

"That's a terrible thing to say about strangers."

"It's a wonderful thing to say about you. Now stop fussing."

The horn sounded—actually a horn, someone's brass instrument honking from the stage—and the hall settled into the particular hush of competition beginning. Around me, forty aprons were tied in unison, a sound like a held breath.

I untied mine. Retied it. Told my hands to stop being dramatic.

Then I opened my grandmother's notebook to the page I'd marked with a ribbon, and began.

* * *

The Amberdale Rustic Torte is not a beautiful process. That's rather the point of it.

Where the polished entries around me built architecture—spun sugar, mirror glazes, chocolate work so precise it looked machined—I browned butter until it smelled like hazelnuts and toast. Where they tempered couverture at exacting temperatures, I folded together flour and almond meal and the last of the honey from the jar Delia's neighbor kept bees for. My grandmother's handwriting marched down the page beside me, patient and slightly bossy: *Don't rush the browning. The butter will tell you when. It always tells you.*

For the first hour, I forgot to be afraid.

This is the thing nobody tells you about doing the thing you love in public: sometimes the love simply outweighs the audience. My hands remembered themselves. The batter came together glossy and fragrant, and the first layers went into the oven, and I stood there watching through the glass as they rose, uneven and golden and honest, and felt something in my chest unclench that had been clenched since I was nineteen years old.

Around me, the hall roared softly on. A man two stations down was building a showpiece castle out of gingerbread and ambition. A teenager near the front piped rosettes with the serene precision of a machine. Cameras drifted past on lanyards. And I browned butter, and whipped cream steeped with vanilla bean and a whisper of cardamom—the note the Amberdales' head pastry chef had once dismissed as "grandmother nonsense"—and let the smell of it roll out over my station like a flag.

Delia caught my eye from the front row and gave me a thumbs up so enthusiastic it nearly dislodged her hat.

I was assembling the second layer, hands steady, actually humming—humming, God help me—when the temperature of the room changed.

It wasn't a sound. It was a pressure, the way air moves before weather. The hair on my arms lifted. I looked up from the torte, and across the aisle, maybe fifteen feet away, stood a tall man in a charcoal coat too fine for a flour-dusted festival hall, flanked by a woman with a clipboard and the rigid posture of someone paid to anticipate needs.

Edmund Amberdale had aged well, the way money ages well. Silver at the temples now, jaw still carved, posture still broadcast-ready. He looked exactly like he did in the trade magazine profiles and exactly like he did in the one memory I couldn't seem to throw away: standing in the doorway of his office when I was seventeen, telling me that the family name required standards, and that I had shown him I didn't have them.

He was looking directly at me.

The clipboard woman said something low and urgent. He didn't appear to hear her. He crossed the aisle—not quickly, nothing so undignified—with the unhurried certainty of a man who had never once been told he couldn't go somewhere, and stopped at the rope barrier in front of station twenty-three.

Up close, he smelled like cedar and cold air. Up close, I could see that time had done something to his eyes after all. Not softened them. Just... tired them.

"Orsen," he said.

Not *Miss*. Not *the entrant at station twenty-three*. My name, in his voice, which I hadn't heard in ten years and which fit me like a coat I'd outgrown.

"Mr. Amberdale," I said, because if he got to be formal then so did I, and because my voice only shook a little.

His gaze moved over my station with the efficiency of an inspector. The rustic layers, cooling on racks. The notebook, open, ribbon-marked. The cardamon cream. His mouth did something complicated.

"This is a mistake," he said quietly. "Withdraw. Now, before the judging. I'll have my assistant handle any embarrassment—the entry fee, whatever you're owed. You can leave through the side and no one needs to know you were ever here."

And there it was. Ten years of rehearsal, and the moment arrived wearing exactly the face I'd expected.

Here is what I had imagined saying, in all those rehearsed versions: something devastating. Something eloquent. Something that would make him understand everything he'd thrown away standing in a doorway telling a child she wasn't enough.

What actually came out of my mouth was: "No."

His eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. In Edmund Amberdale's vocabulary, I suspected, *no* was a word that happened to other people.

"Orsen." He lowered his voice further, and here was the terrible thing—he sounded almost gentle, the way he'd sounded gentle when he explained why I couldn't come to the family Christmas party anymore, why it would be "easier for everyone." "You're making a spectacle of yourself. This—" a small, precise gesture that took in my crooked layers, my grandmother's notebook, the whole floury, imperfect truth of me, "—is not our standard. If the press connects you to the family, if you fail publicly under the Amberdale name—"

"Then it's a good thing," I said, and my heart was slamming against my ribs like it wanted out, like even it had opinions now, "that I'm not baking under your name."

Something flickered across his face. Fast, but not fast enough.

"Your grandmother filled your head with—"

"My grandmother," I said, and my voice cracked clean down the middle, and I let it, "taught me that butter tells you when it's ready. That uneven layers still taste like love. That a recipe written in a kitchen that smells like cinnamon is worth more than one written in a boardroom." I picked up the spatula. My hand had stopped shaking. I honestly don't know when. "She taught me everything you apparently paid other people to teach me badly. So no. I'm not withdrawing. I'm mid-bake, and the cream won't hold if I stop, and frankly, neither will I."

Behind him, the clipboard woman made a small strangled noise. From the front row, I heard Delia rise to her feet with the creak of righteous wool.

Edmund looked at me for a long moment. The hall went on around us—ovens ticking, a timer shrilling three rows over, someone laughing—but between us there was a silence with ten years inside it.

"You'll embarrass yourself," he said finally. Quieter. Almost, horribly, like a warning from a father instead of a statement from a brand.

"I've been embarrassed since I was nineteen," I said. "I survived it. Watch me."

He held my eyes one second longer. Then he straightened, adjusted his coat with a motion I recognized from a thousand magazine photos—the motion of a man reassembling himself—and turned away. The clipboard woman hurried after him. At the edge of the aisle he paused, just briefly, back still turned, and I thought I saw his hand flex once at his side, like a man reaching for something that wasn't there anymore.

Then he was gone into the crowd, and the hall swallowed him, and I stood at station twenty-three with a spatula in my hand and my whole body ringing like a struck bell.

Delia appeared at the rope barrier before I'd fully exhaled. Her face was fierce and wet-eyed at once.

"That," she said, "was the most rehearsed sentence you have ever spoken."

"It was four sentences."

"And every one of them took a decade." She gripped the barrier. "Now sit down before your legs do it for you, and finish your torte. You've got forty minutes and your cream is waiting."

She was right. Of course she was right. I sat, and I picked up the bowl, and the cardamon cream was perfect—softly peaked, fragrant, holding—and I began to build the final layer while the crowd murmured and the lights glittered and somewhere in my chest, a sentence I'd carried for ten years shifted its weight, testing the door.

*I'm an Amberdale,* it whispered. *And this bakery is mine.*

Not yet. But soon. First, the torte.

First, the truth, in butter and honey and cardamom, rising uneven and golden and honest under the white lights of Winterhaven.

The timer read thirty-eight minutes. My hands were steady.

And then, over the noise of the hall, I heard the announcer's voice lift with surprise: *"Ladies and gentlemen, please welcome to the judges' table—"*
