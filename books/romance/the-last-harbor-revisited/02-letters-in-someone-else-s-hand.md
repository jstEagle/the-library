## 2. Letters in Someone Else's Hand

The first letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Hazel would later remember as the day the tide changed without her noticing.

She found it wedged between a royalty statement and a rejection letter she'd written on behalf of a client whose novel she had privately loved. The envelope was heavy cream stock, the kind that cost more per sheet than her mother's entire stationery drawer once held, and across the front, in ink the color of deep water, someone had written *Ms. Wren* — the name the literary society had assigned her for the duration of the pen-pal program. Anonymous correspondents, anonymous hearts. That was the promise. That was the whole point.

Hazel turned the envelope over twice before opening it, because the handwriting did something strange to her pulse. It slanted left, against all convention, and the capital letters were tall and narrow like masts. She knew that slant. She had spent four months transcribing it from dictation tapes, shaping its arrogance into publishable prose, sanding down its edges until Julian Marsh's voice sounded almost humble.

But of course it couldn't be him. The society's matching algorithm paired strangers — strangers, explicitly, with no professional entanglement, no shared publishers, no history. And Julian Marsh did not write letters by hand. He dictated everything; he'd told her so in their first meeting, waving his recorder like a man surrendering to superior technology. *I think out loud, Ms. Eastwick. Ink slows me down.*

So it wasn't him. It only felt like him, the way a harbor at dusk can feel like every harbor you've ever grieved.

*Dear Ms. Wren,* the letter began. *They tell me I'm supposed to introduce myself to a stranger, but I've never trusted introductions. They're biographies written too early. So instead: this morning I watched a fishing boat come in with nothing, and the captain smiled anyway, and I've been thinking about that smile for six hours. What do you do with a day that gives you nothing and asks you to be grateful?*

Hazel read it three times. Then she sat very still in her kitchen while the kettle screamed itself hoarse, because she knew that question. Not its words — its wound. She had heard it asked once, years ago, on a pier in Saltmere Bay, by a man who had just watched his father's boat come home empty for the last time.

She wrote back that night under a name that wasn't hers, in a hand she kept deliberately plain. *Dear Mr. Lark,* she began — his assigned alias, absurd and birdlike. *I don't know what you do with such days. But I know what my mother did: she set an extra place at the table. She said hunger isn't only for food.* She hesitated over the next line, then let it through, because anonymity is a strange kind of courage. *I am a ghostwriter. I spend my life saying true things in other people's voices. Sometimes I wonder if I've said anything true in my own.*

She sealed it before she could regret it.

* * *

Julian read the letter standing in the doorway of his study, coat still on, salt wind still in his collar.

He hadn't wanted the pen-pal program. His editor had enrolled him as publicity — *the reclusive memoirist corresponds with a mystery reader!* — and Julian had agreed only because refusing meant explaining why he no longer trusted strangers with his interior life. He had been burned there once, thoroughly, by a woman with gray eyes who walked out of Saltmere Bay one October and never sent so much as a postcard to explain the shape of the hole she left.

But this correspondent — this Wren — wrote like someone confessing in a dark church. No performance. No flattery about his book, which everyone else led with. Just questions, sharp and small and human.

*I wonder,* she had written, *if you resent being known. You wrote a memoir, after all. You sold your interior life at market price. And yet your letters feel like a man locking doors behind him.*

He laughed out loud, alone, which he hadn't done in months. Then he sat down and answered honestly, which he hadn't done in years.

*You're right that I lock doors. Here's one I'll leave open a crack: I was engaged once. She left without a word, and I built a whole philosophy around not asking why. Duty, I called it. Pride, probably. Some nights I draft the letter I'd send her if I knew where she was, and every version sounds like a legal brief. How does a man ask a question twelve years late without sounding like an accusation?*

In the morning he would meet Hazel Eastwick in his publisher's conference room and fight her over chapter structure — she wanted the father's death moved earlier, he refused to lead with grief, and they would go at each other with the particular venom of two people who are always right. He would call her prose "competent and bloodless." She would call his manuscript "a fortress with no windows." Neither of them would notice that his hands were steady only when he described the letters, or that Hazel went quiet, suddenly, terribly quiet, when he mentioned his anonymous correspondent.

*My advice,* Wren wrote back, *is to stop drafting the letter and start answering it. You're not writing to her anymore. You're writing to the man who lost her. He's the one who needs to hear from you.*

Julian read that sentence eleven times. Somewhere below his ribs, a door he'd welded shut rattled on its hinges.

* * *

By the third exchange, Hazel had developed a system, which was to say a delusion with rules.

She answered his letters at night, as Wren, in the small hours when honesty came cheap. By day she was Hazel Eastwick again, ghost-for-hire, sitting across from Julian Marsh in cafés and conference rooms while he tore apart her revisions of his memoir. Their arguments had become legendary among the editorial staff. He wanted myth; she insisted on fact. He called her ruthless; she called him evasive. They fought like people who had once been intimate and were pretending otherwise — though neither of them examined why the pretense felt so practiced.

"You cut the lighthouse chapter," Julian said one afternoon, dropping her marked-up pages on the table between them like a dead bird.

"I condensed it. You spend nine pages describing a building."

"That building is where—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "It matters structurally."

"Where it *what*, Mr. Marsh?" Hazel leaned forward. "You keep doing this. Every time we reach the year you won't discuss, you develop a sudden reverence for architecture."

His jaw tightened. Outside the café window, rain was beginning, soft and gray as unwritten pages. "Not everything belongs in a book," he said.

"No," Hazel agreed quietly. "That's rather the problem with books. Someone has to decide what gets left out." She gathered her pages. Her hands were not entirely steady, and she hated them for it. "I'll restore the lighthouse. Give me something true to put inside it and I'll make it sing."

He gave her nothing. But that night, Wren received a letter that began: *There's a place I can't write about. My ghostwriter keeps circling it like a gull over a fish. Funny — I'll tell you things I won't tell her, and I pay her to know me. What does that make you?*

Hazel read it with the letter propped against the teapot and her own reflection ghosted faintly in the dark window behind it, and she thought: *it makes you a coward with excellent penmanship.* Because she knew exactly what the lighthouse held. She had stood inside it. She had been the thing he couldn't write about, the omission at the center of his architecture, and now she was being paid to excavate herself and didn't know how to hold the shovel.

She wrote back: *It makes you lucky. Some of us aren't paid to know anyone. We just know them, and have to live with it.*

Then, because the hour was late and the tea was cold and anonymity made her brave, she added a postscript she would spend the next week trying not to think about:

*P.S. — If your ghostwriter is any good, she already knows what the lighthouse means. Perhaps she's waiting for you to say it first. Some truths can only be given, never taken.*

* * *

Winter came down on the city like a withheld verdict. The letters grew longer, then shorter, then dense as driftwood — everything essential packed hard. Julian told Wren about his mother's silence, his father's empty nets, the engagement ring he still kept in a desk drawer he pretended was for stamps. Wren told him about a girl who gave up a man for a dying mother and then discovered that duty, unlike grief, never ends; it just changes landlords.

"I think I love my correspondent," Julian admitted aloud, to no one, in his study, and was appalled to find he meant it — appalled, and lit up like a harbor at night.

And Hazel, rereading his latest letter for the fourth time, caught herself smiling at the leftward slant of his capitals and thought, with a clarity that arrived like cold water: *you know this hand. You have always known this hand.*

She told herself the program guaranteed strangers. She told herself coincidence was a country she didn't live in. She told herself a great many things, in the fluent voice of a woman who had made a career of believing other people's versions of the truth.

Then, on a Thursday in February, her editor called with news that stopped her breath mid-chest: a page of Julian Marsh's draft memoir had leaked online — uncorrected, unattributed, and unmistakably hers. The message boards were already asking who the ghostwriter was.

And in the third comment down, posted forty minutes ago, someone had quoted a single line from the leaked page beside a single line from a published excerpt of *Wren's* letters, and asked the internet a question Hazel had spent two months refusing to ask herself:

*Does anyone else think these are written by the same person?*
