## 1. Salt, Flour, and Weather Warnings

The last loaf of the season came out of the oven at 4:47 in the afternoon, and Mara Eastwick stood in the doorway of her grandmother's harbor bakery and listened to the sea decide whether it would let her close.

Wind had been worrying the windows all day, working its fingers into every gap in the old frames, and the gulls had gone inland two hours ago—the surest weather warning the coast gave, and the one she'd spent all morning pretending not to read. The sky over the harbor had gone the color of pewter left out too long. The water beneath it was the green of bottle glass, choppy and strange, and the fishing boats had all come in except one stubborn trawler making for the breakwater with its lights already on.

"Closing early," she told the loaves, as though they might argue. "Closing for the season."

The bakery didn't answer, but the building itself had opinions. The Eastwick bakery had stood at the elbow of Harbor Lane since before anyone living could remember, and it had a way of settling around a person, of holding warmth in its brick like a held breath. Her grandmother had run it for forty years. Mara had run it for six, and for six years she had told herself the same lie every November: that closing for winter was practical, that the tourists were gone, that she'd reopen in spring the way the tide came back, inevitable and unbothered.

She had never once felt inevitable. She felt like a tide that kept going out.

She flipped the sign to CLOSED, and the little brass bell over the door gave its tired jingle, and that was when she saw the flour tin.

It sat on the shelf above the proofing bench, blue and white, dented on one side from a fall her grandmother had taken with it in 1987 and never bothered to explain. Mara had hidden the letter there three days ago, folded into eighths and pushed down beneath the flour like something buried alive. She'd told herself she'd deal with it Monday. Then Tuesday. Then she'd told herself the season's end was a reasonable time to deal with legal matters, and the season's end, conveniently, was today.

She took the tin down. The lid came off with its familiar complaint.

The letter was where she'd left it, on Reyes Development letterhead, and she read it again standing in her own doorway with the wind rising behind her, because apparently she was a person who needed to be hurt more than once by the same paragraph.

*...pursuant to the terms of the lease agreement executed between the Eastwick estate and Reyes Family Holdings, the lessee is hereby notified that the property at 4 Harbor Lane will be subject to inspection and assessment...*

Inspection and assessment. The words were soft as sifted flour and just as suffocating. Everyone in Port Merrow knew what inspection and assessment meant when the Reyes name was on the paper. It meant the bakery would be valued, then it would be vacated, and then it would become the ground floor of something with a render on a brochure—glass and pale wood and a name like *The Merrow House* or *Salt & Stone*, with a café inside that sold flat whites to people who would never know what had been there before.

The Reyes family. She said the name in her head the way you'd press on a bruise, just to check it still ached.

Ten years ago, the bruise had been a boy. Jonah Reyes, nineteen and flour to the elbows, who had spent one perfect summer working the ovens beside her, learning her grandmother's sourdough starter like it was a language, laughing at her terrible puns, kissing her once in the doorway with salt air in both their hair—and then, in September, without a word, without a letter, without so much as a returned phone call, vanishing. Three weeks later the Reyes family had filed the first papers against the bakery. Mara had spent a decade telling herself those two facts were the same fact.

She folded the letter back into eighths. Her hands were steady. She was almost proud of them.

"Season's over," she said to the tin, and put it back.

* * *

The knock came at six, when the light had gone entirely and the wind had stopped worrying the windows and started demanding entrance.

Mara was in the back, counting the day's till by lamplight because the storm had already flickered the power twice, and the knock was so firm, so *official*, that she knew before she opened the door who it would be. Some part of her had known all day. The storm had been gathering in her chest the same way it was gathering over the harbor.

Jonah Reyes stood on her step with snow already silvering his shoulders, older by a decade and no easier to look at. He'd filled out through the chest and shoulders the way men did when they'd spent years doing something physical, and there was gray starting at his temples that should have made him look distinguished and instead made her furious, because it meant time had passed for him too, and she had wanted him frozen at nineteen, frozen in a doorway, frozen at the moment before he left.

"Mara." Her name in his mouth was careful, like a dish he was afraid of breaking.

"You're trespassing on a closed bakery."

"I'm not. I'm—" He held up a folder, and she saw the letterhead and felt the bruise press itself. "I'm here for the inspection. I was supposed to have the surveyor with me, but he turned back at the county line. The roads are going to close."

"Then turn back too."

"I can't." He said it simply, and that simplicity was worse than any argument. "The bridge is already drifting over. I tried. There's no way back to town until this passes, and the radio says it's not passing tonight."

The wind chose that moment to throw a fistful of sleet against the door between them, as if to settle the matter. Mara looked past his shoulder at the white chaos of Harbor Lane, at the lighthouse at the end of it, its beam sweeping around and around through the murk like a slow searchlight looking for something lost.

The cottage. Of course. The keeper's cottage, hers now, a quarter mile up the point, with its wood stove and its stores laid in for winter and its two bedrooms she had never once had occasion to feel strange about until this exact moment.

"You can wait out the storm in the bakery," she said. "There's a couch."

"The bakery's the property I'm here to assess, Mara. I'm not sitting in it all night filling out a form in my head." He paused, and something moved through his face—something that looked, absurdly, like apology, or its distant cousin. "I'd rather wait in the cottage. If you're there. I'd rather not be alone in it."

*In it.* As though the storm were the thing he couldn't be alone with, and not the past.

She should have said no. She knew every reason to say no, and she catalogued them quickly, the way she catalogued ingredients: pride, anger, ten years, one letterhead, one vanished boy. But the wind screamed down the lane and the lights flickered again, and the truth—the plain, unglamorous truth—was that she could not in good conscience send any human being back out into a blizzard, not even this one. Her grandmother had fed strangers off these ovens through three hurricanes. Hospitality was load-bearing in the Eastwick house.

"Fine," she said. "One night. You sleep in the keeper's room. And you carry the firewood."

* * *

The walk up the point was a whiteout with a handrail. Mara led with the lantern and Jonah followed with the firewood and the rope she'd made him clip to her belt, and the whole way she was aware of him behind her the way you're aware of a fire in a room—not the warmth of it, exactly, but the fact of it, the attention it demanded. Twice he shouted something against the wind and she didn't catch it, and both times she was grateful, because whatever it was, she wasn't ready to hear it.

The cottage took them in the way old cottages did: reluctantly, then completely. She got the stove lit while he wedged the door shut with a chair, and within twenty minutes the room had gone gold and close, lamplight and woodsmoke, snow ticking against the windows like a patient visitor. The power had died for good somewhere on the walk. The radio, battery-powered, muttered about a nor'easter, a historic one, coastal roads closed through at least tomorrow night.

Tomorrow night. Mara did the arithmetic and didn't like the sum.

She cooked because cooking was the only prayer she knew. There was salt cod soaking from the morning, potatoes, the heel of a loaf she'd brought up from the bakery, butter, the last of the summer's onions braided and hanging by the stove. She worked with her back to him and her ears wide open, and he moved around the small room carefully, like a man in a museum of someone else's life, touching nothing, looking at everything. She felt his eyes on the photograph of her grandmother on the mantel. She felt them on her back. She felt them the way you feel weather coming.

"Your grandmother taught me to score a boule," he said at last, into the silence. "The summer I was here. She said I cut like I was apologizing to the dough."

"She was right. You did."

He laughed—one short breath of it, surprised out of him—and the sound went through the small warm room like the first crack in ice. Mara kept her eyes on the pan. The cod and onions had gone soft and gold together, and the smell of it filled the cottage, and it was, infuriatingly, the exact smell of that summer. Salt, flour, woodsmoke, him.

"Why you?" she asked, because she couldn't not, and because the storm had sealed the doors and there was nowhere for the question to go but out. "Of everyone they could have sent. Why did it have to be you walking up my step with that folder?"

Jonah was quiet long enough that she turned. He was standing by the window with the snow ticking past behind him, and the lamplight caught his face, and he looked—she searched for the word and hated the one she found—*wrecked*. Quietly wrecked, the way a house is wrecked, still standing, still a house, but wrong in its bones.

"Because I asked to be the one," he said.

The stove ticked. The beam of the lighthouse swept past the window, once, twice, a slow white wheel through the dark.

"Mara, there are things I came here to tell you, and I don't know how—"

The radio cut him off. Not with news—with a sound, a long electronic shriek, and then the emergency tone, and then a voice reading coordinates for a coastal flood warning, and beneath it all, faint through the walls and the wind, the deep bell of the harbor buoy, tolling like something beginning.
