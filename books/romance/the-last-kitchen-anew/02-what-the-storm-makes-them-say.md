## 2. What the Storm Makes Them Say

The power died at 6:47, and Mara knew it to the minute because she'd been watching the clock, counting the hours until the storm released them—or didn't.

The lights guttered once, twice, like a held breath, then went out entirely. In the sudden dark of the keeper's cottage, Jonah Reyes made a sound low in his throat—not quite a laugh, not quite a groan. "Well," he said. "That's that."

Mara found the matches by memory, her fingers walking the shelf above the sink where her grandmother had kept them for forty years. The oil lamp caught on the third strike, and its light bloomed across the room, across Jonah standing in the middle of her grandmother's parlor with snow melting off his shoulders and a look on his face she couldn't afford to study too closely.

"The fireplace works," she said, all business, because business was safer than anything else. "There's wood in the box. If you're going to be stranded here, you can at least be useful."

He knelt at the hearth without argument, and she hated how easily he fit there, how naturally his hands moved over kindling and match, as if he'd done this a hundred times in a hundred rooms. As if he belonged in one.

* * *

They cooked by firelight because there was nothing else to do. Mara had flour in the cottage—of course she did; she was her grandmother's granddaughter—and there were eggs, cream, a wedge of hard cheese, apples going soft in a bowl. Enough for something. Not enough for much.

"Confiture," Jonah said, holding up the last jar of blackberry preserves she'd put up in August. He'd shed his wet coat and rolled his sleeves, and the firelight turned his forearms bronze. "You still make it with the lemon zest."

It wasn't a question, and that was the problem. It meant he remembered. It meant he'd eaten it—once, ten years ago, in another kitchen, in another life—and carried the taste of it around like a stone in his shoe ever since.

"Lemon zest cuts the sweetness," she said, taking the jar from him more sharply than necessary. "My grandmother's rule."

"Your grandmother's galette," he said, "was the best thing I ever ate. And I've eaten in Paris, Mara. I've eaten everywhere."

"You ate my grandmother's galette while your family was drawing up papers to take her bakery." She cracked an egg against the bowl's rim, harder than the egg deserved. "So forgive me if I don't treasure the review."

The words landed between them like a dropped pan. Jonah went still, the apple knife paused mid-slice, and when he spoke his voice had gone quiet and careful, the way voices go before storms or confessions. "You think I don't know what my family took from you?"

"I think you signed the lease renewal yourself. I've seen the signature. *Jonah M. Reyes, Managing Partner.*"

"I was twenty-four years old and my father handed me a stack of documents and told me which page to sign." The knife came down through the apple, clean. "I read what they let me read."

"That's not an excuse."

"No," he said. "It's an explanation, and you're allowed to hate both."

* * *

The galette went into the Dutch oven over the coals, and while it baked they fought—or rather, the fight they'd been postponing for a decade finally found them, the way weather finds a house with a bad roof.

She told him about the year after he left. About the winter the bakery nearly failed because the Reyes development company bought the building's debt and doubled the rent overnight. About the customers who drifted away, priced out or frightened off, about the notice taped to the door that read CLOSED FOR RENOVATION when really it meant closed for good. About how she'd sat on this very floor, in this very cottage, with her grandmother's hand in hers, listening to the old woman say, *Hold on, little gull. Hold on.*

"Hold on to what?" Mara had asked her then, and her grandmother had smiled with half her mouth, the way she did when she knew something no one else knew yet.

And now, sitting across the fire from the man who had helped break her life open, Mara heard her grandmother's voice again, clear as the wind against the shutters: *The tin, little gull. You never looked in the tin.*

She stood up so fast the lamp flame shuddered.

"What is it?" Jonah was on his feet too, alarm in every line of him.

"The flour tin." Her heart was hammering somewhere up near her throat. "In the bakery. There's a false bottom—I found it when I was a girl, I used to hide things in it, letters, secrets—but I haven't opened it in years, I keep the lease notice in there now, God help me—" She was already moving toward the door, toward the coat hooks, and Jonah caught her arm.

"It's a whiteout out there. You are not walking to the bakery."

"It's two hundred yards—"

"It's two hundred yards of zero visibility and drifts past your knees." His grip was firm but not cruel, and his eyes in the lamplight were doing something complicated. "Mara. What could possibly be in a flour tin that matters enough to die for?"

* * *

She waited until the storm eased near midnight, and he came with her—because of course he did, because whatever else Jonah Reyes was, he was not a man who let a woman walk into a blizzard alone. They roped themselves together with clothesline like something out of a rescue story, and the wind screamed, and the snow came sideways, and the bakery's dark shape rose out of the white like a shipwreck.

Inside, by flashlight, she pried up the false bottom of the tin.

Letters. Dozens of them, tied with string gone brittle, in her grandmother's small, slanted hand—and others, typed, on letterhead she recognized with a sick lurch: **Hale Development Group**. Victor Hale. The man whose name was on every development project on the coast, the man whose money moved through the town like tide through a channel.

Her grandmother had been writing to him for years. Before the rivalry. Before everything.

"Oh," Mara said, and the sound came out of her like something wounded. "Oh, no."

Jonah took the top letter from her shaking hands and read aloud, his voice growing rougher with each line:

*"Mr. Hale—you will stop sending men to my granddaughter's shop. Whatever arrangement you believe you have with the Reyes family does not concern me. I know what you're doing. I know you approached their son first, and I know you approached mine second, and I know you told each one lies about the other. You want this harbor, Mr. Hale. But you will not get it by breaking two families who might otherwise have stood together against you."*

Dated eleven years ago. Two months before Jonah left town.

Mara sank onto the flour-dusted step stool, letters spilling across her lap, and the truth assembled itself piece by terrible piece: Hale had gone to the Reyes family first, dangling the harbor development, telling them the Eastwicks were already negotiating to sell. Then he'd come to her grandmother, claiming the Reyeses intended to raze the bakery regardless, so she'd better take his offer while it was decent. Each family had believed the other was betraying them. Each had armored up. And in the middle of it, a young man named Jonah had chosen exile over choosing sides—and a young woman named Mara had chosen pride over chasing after him.

"There was never a rivalry," she whispered. "We invented it. No—we didn't even invent it. *He* invented it, and we believed him, because believing him hurt less than doubting each other would have."

Jonah crouched in front of her, and she watched him read letter after letter, watched his face do what hers must have done—the dawning horror, the grief, and underneath it, rising like bread in a warm oven, something that looked unbearably like hope.

"Mara." His voice cracked on her name. "Ten years. Ten years I thought you chose the bakery over me. That you looked at me and saw the enemy."

"I thought you chose the money over me."

"I wrote to you."

"He intercepted them." She lifted a letter dated three weeks after Jonah's departure, her grandmother's handwriting furious now, pressing hard enough to tear the paper. "*Victor Hale has admitted to me that letters have been 'misdirected.' He calls it protecting his investment. I am keeping every document. One day someone will need them.*"

"One day," Jonah repeated softly.

The storm rattled the windows. The flashlight beam trembled between them. And Mara looked at this man—this man she had spent a decade hating on false evidence, whose hands were still dusted with her flour, who had knelt in her grandmother's kitchen and sliced apples like he'd never left—and felt the anger drain out of her so completely that what rushed in behind it frightened her far more than any blizzard ever had.

"We have to show these to someone," she said. "A lawyer. The paper. Anyone."

"Yes." He didn't move. Neither did she. The space between them had shrunk to almost nothing, and his breath mingled with hers in the cold air. "But first—Mara—can we just—"

She kissed him. Or he kissed her. Later neither of them would be able to say, only that ten years collapsed into a single point of warmth in a freezing room, and when they broke apart, foreheads touching, Jonah laughed—a broken, wondering sound.

"My father doesn't know any of this," he said quietly. "But Victor Hale does. And if my family's name is attached to what Hale built on those letters—"

"Then you're about to lose everything," Mara finished.

Jonah straightened, and in the flashlight's glow she saw something settle in his face, something that looked like a decision being born.

"Not everything," he said. "Not quite."
