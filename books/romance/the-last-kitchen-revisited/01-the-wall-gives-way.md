## 1. The Wall Gives Way

The pipe chose a Tuesday to burst, which Noor Joubert would later decide was typical of pipes. They never failed on a Saturday when you had time and a good mood. They waited until the day the new stock arrived, forty-two boxes of spring titles stacked in the back room like a small city awaiting its citizens, and then they let go all at once behind the plaster of the kitchen wall with a sound like a long, wet sigh.

He heard it at seven in the morning, before he'd even unlocked the front door, standing in the back kitchen with his coat still on and his thermos in one hand. The sound was not dramatic. That was the strange part. He had expected, in the half-second before he understood what he was hearing, something cinematic—a geyser, a crash. Instead it was a soft, persistent seeping, water finding the path of least resistance through sixty years of lath and lime, the way water always did, patient as a reader working through a long novel.

"No," Noor said to the pipe, reasonably, the way he said *no* to customers who tried to negotiate the price of hardcovers. "No, we discussed this."

The pipe did not discuss anything. It wept.

By eight he had shut off the mains, moved the boxes, called the plumber—who could come Thursday, maybe Friday, did he want to be on the list?—and stood surveying the damage with the particular helplessness of a man whose entire livelihood depended on paper staying dry. The wall above the little sink had bloomed a stain the shape of a country on no map he knew, brown at the edges, spreading. A blister of plaster hung from it like a ripe fruit.

His daughter Amira appeared in the doorway in her school uniform, toast in hand, and assessed the situation with the flat gaze she'd inherited from her grandmother.

"You're going to have to open it," she said.

"I'm going to call someone."

"You already called someone. They said Thursday." She took a bite of toast. "Water doesn't wait for Thursday, Papa."

"Where do you learn to talk like that?"

"Books." She kissed his cheek, leaving a crumb, and was gone down the alley toward her school, twelve years old and certain about everything, and Noor stood alone in the ruined kitchen holding a thermos of coffee he no longer wanted.

* * *

The bookshop had been his for eleven years, and the building had been old before that. Marchand & Fils had been a haberdashery once, then a notions shop, then nothing much, and when Noor signed the lease at twenty-nine with more hope than money, the back kitchen had come with the deal: a sink with two taps, a hot plate, a table scarred by a century of elbows, and walls so thick with paint layers they seemed to be wearing coats. He had made tea there every morning since. He had eaten lunch there while reading manuscripts nobody had asked him to read. He had, on exactly one occasion he never spoke of, sat at that table until three in the morning with a bottle of wine and a letter he couldn't finish writing.

He thought about that occasion now, absurdly, as he stood in the hardware store buying a pry bar.

"You'll want a dust sheet too," said the man at the counter, an old customer, M. Ferrand, who bought westerns exclusively and considered this a personality. "Plaster's filthy work. And goggles. You've only got the one face, I assume."

"One face," Noor agreed. "It's served me well enough."

"Then protect it." Ferrand wrapped the pry bar in newspaper with the tenderness of a man handling a first edition. "What happened?"

"Burst pipe. Behind the kitchen wall."

"Ah." Ferrand nodded with the gravity such news deserved. "Old buildings. They hold their water like grudges. You get it out or it ruins everything from inside." He handed over the parcel. "Good luck, Joubert."

Noor walked back through streets still damp from last night's rain, past the café where Madame Osei was setting out chairs, past the fountain with its chipped mermaid, and let himself in through the shop's back door into the smell of paper and dust and, underneath it now, the mineral smell of standing water. The shop proper was quiet. It would fill at eleven with the regulars—the retired teacher who read mysteries in strict alphabetical order, the teenagers who came to use the Wi-Fi and stayed to argue about fantasy series—but for now it was just him and forty-two boxes and a wall that needed opening.

He spread the dust sheet. He put on the goggles, which fogged immediately. He took up the pry bar and felt, briefly, like a man about to commit a small burglary against his own life.

The plaster gave easier than he expected. Sixty years of paint had stiffened the surface, but beneath it the material had gone soft as stale cake, and the first swing of the pry bar opened a hole the size of a fist, exhaling a breath of cold, wet air that smelled of earth after rain. Water ran down his wrist. He worked methodically, the way he shelved books, left to right, top to bottom, widening the wound in the wall until the lath showed through, pale wooden slats like ribs, and behind them the dark cavity where the pipe lived and, apparently, had been leaking for longer than today.

He pulled away a section of lath with a crack like a knuckle cracking, reached in with his flashlight, and swept the beam across the cavity looking for the source of the leak.

The light found the pipe, greened with age, weeping at a joint. Fine. Thursday's problem, or Friday's. But the light also found something else, wedged between two studs at shoulder height, tucked into the gap the way people tuck things into cracks in train station benches, or library shelves, or the spaces between the pages of books they mean to return to.

An envelope.

Noor lowered the pry bar. He took off the fogged goggles because he wanted to see clearly, and seeing clearly seemed suddenly very important.

It was yellowed. Not brown, not stained by the water that had soaked the studs around it—it had been placed high enough, dry enough, sheltered enough—and the yellowing was simply the yellowing of paper that has waited a long time in the dark. The edges were soft. There was a stamp in the corner, and a postmark, and the postmark was legible, and Noor read it twice because the first time his eyes refused to cooperate.

Eleven years ago. The third week of April.

And the handwriting on the front—his own. He knew it the way you know your own voice on a recording: with immediate recognition and immediate denial. The careful, slightly formal script he used for addresses, each letter standing straight as if afraid to lean on its neighbor. His own hand had written the name on this envelope, and the name was not his.

**Delphine Marchand.**

The shop was silent. Somewhere a clock ticked; somewhere outside a delivery truck changed gears. Noor stood in the wreckage of his kitchen wall with his arm still half-raised toward the cavity, and the envelope sat in the beam of the flashlight like a thing on display in a museum of one exhibit.

He remembered writing it. That was the terrible part—he remembered it completely, the way you remember a fever. Eleven years ago, the week Delphine left town, the week the house on Rue Verte went dark and the train platform saw the whole of what he had to give, which was silence. He had written a letter instead. He remembered the bottle of wine, the three in the morning, the words coming out of him in a flood he hadn't known he'd been damming for years. And then—

And then morning. And then the sober light of morning, which is the great enemy of courage, and the letter folded into an envelope, addressed, stamped, carried to the post office—and not mailed. He could not remember deciding not to mail it. He remembered standing at the counter. He remembered the clerk asking if that was all. After that, memory declined to testify.

But the letter had gone somewhere. And here it was. Here it had been, all this time, ten feet from where he made his tea every morning, sleeping in the wall above the sink while he raised a daughter and built a life and told himself, with increasing fluency, that the past was the past.

How? How had it gotten into the wall?

He turned the envelope over. Unopened. Sealed. On the back flap, in the same upright handwriting, a small notation in pencil, faint: *to be delivered.* As if he had meant to leave instructions for some future self, or some other person entirely, and had never done either.

Noor reached in and lifted it from the studs. It weighed almost nothing. Letters weigh almost nothing; that is how they get to be so heavy.

He held it under the light for a long moment. He could feel the single folded page inside, the thickness of it, the particular density of words written fast and meant seriously. His thumb found the sealed flap. One motion. One tear. Eleven years of not knowing, ended in a second.

He put it in his shirt pocket instead.

This was, he would reflect later, the most Noor Joubert thing he had ever done. A man who had spent a decade rehearsing a sentence he never delivered had now recovered the sentence itself, in his own handwriting, delivered by a burst pipe—and his response was to button it against his chest and stand in a demolished kitchen telling himself he'd open it later, when things were calmer, when the plumber had come, when he'd had time to think. Later. Later was the word his whole adult life had been built on, load-bearing later, and here it was again, arriving right on schedule.

The water dripped. The clock ticked. At eleven the bell over the front door rang and Mme. Aubert, the retired teacher, called out, "Monsieur Joubert? I finished the Simeon. Do you have the next one?" and Noor wiped his hands on the dust sheet, checked that the envelope was secure in his pocket, and went out to sell a book, because that was what he did, that was who he was, that was the whole architecture of the life he'd built on the far side of a train platform.

* * *

All day the letter rode in his pocket like a coal.

He sold books. He recommended the new novel to the book club ladies, who trusted him absolutely and were therefore dangerous. He ordered tea for M. Ferrand, who had come in to check on the wall situation and offered, unprompted, to come by Thursday with his brother and a crowbar, "in case the plumber's the delicate sort." He climbed the ladder to reshelve the poetry section, which no one ever disturbed, and caught himself standing at the top of the ladder with his hand pressed flat against his chest, checking, the way you check for a wallet in a crowd.

At four, Amira arrived with her backpack and her opinions and installed herself at the big table near the window to do homework among the shelves, which was her habit and his favorite feature of the shop—that low constant rustle of her pencil, the sighs of a girl wrestling with mathematics, punctuated by declarations like "Nobody needs to know what x is, that's why they made it a letter."

"How's the wall?" she asked without looking up.

"Open."

"Did you find anything?"

"Rats' nest. Very disappointing."

She looked up then, briefly, with that glance she had—the one that weighed statements and found them underweight. "You're touching your pocket," she said, and went back to her math.

Noor took his hand off his chest.

That evening he closed the shop, counted the till, turned the sign, and stood in the middle of the floor in the blue hour light with the letter finally laid on the counter in front of him. Outside, the streetlamps came on one by one, and the fountain murmured, and the town settled into itself the way it did every night, a town that had watched him arrive with a suitcase and a dream and had never once asked him what he'd left behind.

He picked up the envelope. Turned it over. The pencil note caught the lamplight: *to be delivered.*

"Not tonight," he said aloud to the empty shop, and the empty shop, being full of books, said nothing, having seen this kind of thing before.

He locked the letter in the bottom drawer of his desk—the deep drawer, the one with the broken lock he'd never fixed because he'd never had anything worth stealing—and went home to make dinner for his daughter, and slept badly, and dreamed of trains.

In the morning the wall waited, open and dripping, and the drawer waited, and the letter waited inside it, patient as water, patient as paper, patient as everything in that old building except the pipe, which had finally run out of patience for both of them.

Thursday, the plumber would come. Thursday, Noor told himself, he would think about it properly.

But that night, before locking up, he wrote on a scrap of paper and pinned it above the desk, where Amira would be sure to see it, though he didn't know yet why that mattered:

*Some things wait in walls.*

Amira would find the drawer within the week. But that is getting ahead of the story.
