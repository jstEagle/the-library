## 14. Amira's Rebellion

The letter lived in the bottom drawer of her father's desk, under the tax receipts and a dead pen and a rubber band that had given up on being elastic sometime around the invention of the internet. Amira knew this because she had gone looking for stamps.

That was the thing nobody warned you about snooping: you never found what you were looking for. You found what you weren't.

She had read it twice now. Once standing at the desk with her heart going like a woodpecker, once later in bed by flashlight, slower, the way you reread a passage on a test you knew was coming. It was eleven pages of her father's handwriting — his actual handwriting, the careful slanted script he used for inscriptions inside book covers when someone bought a gift for somebody else — saying things she had never once heard him say out loud to anyone, including her.

*I loved you then. I love you now. I am asking you to stay.*

He had written that. Her father. The man who said "hmm" when asked about feelings. The man whose entire emotional vocabulary consisted of *tea?*, *careful*, and *your grandmother would have liked that*.

And it was postmarked. That was the part that made her want to scream into a pillow. He hadn't just written it — he had addressed it, stamped it, carried it to the post office, and then apparently lost his nerve somewhere between the counter and the mailbox, because it had come back undelivered and he had wedged it into a wall like a squirrel hiding a nut from itself.

Eleven years. Delphine Marchand had been gone eleven years, and the whole time the answer to everything had been sitting in plaster.

Amira was twelve, which meant she was old enough to know exactly how unfair the world was and young enough to still believe you could do something about it. This is a dangerous combination. Historians agree.

* * *

The problem, as Amira saw it during the walk home from school on Thursday, was that her father was going to die of politeness.

Not literally. But spiritually. He was going to stand behind his counter for another eleven years, dusting the poetry section and making soup for sad people, while Delphine Marchand signed papers across town and got on a train again, and this time there wouldn't even be a letter in a wall. There would be nothing. Just Noor Joubert, seventy years old, saying "hmm" at the ocean.

Because here's what she'd figured out, watching them over the past weeks: they were both doing it. Both circling. Delphine came into the shop now — sometimes to look at lease documents, sometimes for no reason she could name, standing in Biography with a book upside down — and her father would materialize beside her offering tea like a man defusing a bomb. They talked about square footage. They talked about Gerard. They talked about the roof, which was apparently a character in their relationship now.

They did not talk about the letter. Because he didn't know she'd read it, and because he was her father, and because adults, in Amira's considerable experience, would rather chew glass than say a true thing out loud if a safe false thing was available.

Meanwhile Gerard had put a date on the calendar. Six weeks. She'd seen the paper on the counter, all legal language and a number with too many zeroes, and underneath it her father's face doing the thing where it went smooth and closed like a shop shutter.

So. Someone had to act. And since the two people involved appeared to have been struck collectively mute sometime around 2013, that someone was going to be a seventh grader.

* * *

The copying took some doing.

Mr. Okafor at the print shop on Delacroix Street had known her since she was small enough to ride in a papoose sling between the shelves, so when she walked in after school with eleven crumpled pages and asked for copies "for a school project," he didn't even blink. He did ask what the project was.

"History," Amira said, which was true in a way she chose not to examine.

"Local history?"

"Very local."

He made the copies. Eleven pages, plus one more — the note. She wrote the note three times on scrap paper before committing, because words mattered; her father had taught her that without ever meaning to. The first draft said *MY DAD WROTE THIS AND HE'S A COWARD*, which was accurate but tactically unwise. The second draft said *PLEASE COME BACK*, which gave away too much leverage. The third draft, the one she copied out in her best handwriting, the handwriting that looked unnervingly like her father's, said:

*He wrote this eleven years ago. He still means it. I know because I've watched him not mean anything else since. — A.J.*

She read it over four times. It was good. It was maybe too good. There was a brief moment where she considered becoming a lawyer instead of a writer, then remembered lawyers had to talk to Gerard-types all day and abandoned the plan.

The envelope was harder. She couldn't use the shop's address, obviously. She used the café's return address — Madame Sylvie owed her for three straight Saturdays of bussing cups — and she addressed it to Delphine Marchand at her aunt's house on Rue des Lilas, which she got off the estate notice her father kept forgetting to throw away. Then she stood at the blue mailbox on the corner of the square for a full ten minutes, holding the envelope, feeling its weight, which was absurd, because it weighed nothing. Paper weighs nothing. It was the heaviest thing she had ever held.

A pigeon watched her with what she decided was judgment.

"He started it," she told the pigeon. "Sitting on a letter for eleven years. Some people deserve consequences."

The pigeon left. Even the pigeon wanted no part of this.

She posted it. The slot swallowed the envelope with a sound like a small door closing, and Amira Joubert stood on the corner of the square and felt, for approximately four seconds, like the hero of something.

Then the four seconds ended.

* * *

Here is what she had not thought about, walking home with her hands in her pockets and her stomach slowly filling with cold water:

Her father didn't know she'd read the letter.

Which meant that when Delphine read it — and she would read it, Amira had no doubt about that, because who throws away a letter addressed in a child's hand that says *he still means it* — Delphine would know things no one had told her. She would know the letter existed. She would know Amira had found it. She would know Amira had copied it, and mailed it, and written *I've watched him* — which implied watching, which implied reading, which implied that Noor Joubert's daughter had gone through his desk.

And then Delphine would come to the shop. And she would look at her father. And her father would say, "What?" the way he said it, all eyebrows and innocence, and Delphine would either tell him or not tell him, and either way, somewhere in that conversation, someone was going to say the word *privacy* or the word *snooping* or possibly the word *grounded*, and every single road led directly to Amira Joubert standing in front of her father explaining why she had interfered in the single most important thing that had ever happened to him without asking anybody first.

By the time she reached the shop door she had constructed, with the efficiency of a very anxious mind, seventeen separate versions of the disaster. In six of them her father didn't speak to her for a week. In two of them he cried, which was worse. In the worst one — the one she kept returning to, the one that made her stop on the sidewalk outside her own home — Delphine read the letter, felt ambushed, felt managed, felt *handled by a child*, and got on the train early. Out of spite. Out of embarrassment. Out of whatever grown-ups felt when they realized everyone around them had been discussing them like weather.

The letter was supposed to fix things. Letters fixed things. That was the whole economy of her household; she'd been raised on the principle that words, written down, could cross any distance. Her father sold that idea daily. She had just bet her family on it.

But her father had also written a letter once, and it had sat in a wall for eleven years, and maybe — the thought arrived slowly, like a bruise surfacing — maybe letters didn't fix things. Maybe letters were just words waiting for someone brave enough to say them, and she had mailed her father's bravery to a stranger without asking him.

She went inside. The bell rang. Her father looked up from the counter, where he was repairing the spine of a cookbook with tape and the patience of a saint, and smiled at her, tired and ordinary and completely unaware.

"Good day?"

"Fine," said Amira Joubert, liar, traitor, saboteur, child. "Homework."

"Tea?"

"Sure."

She drank the tea. She did her homework. She laughed at something he said about a customer who'd tried to return a book because "the ending was unrealistic," and the whole time a copy of his heart was traveling through the postal system toward Rue des Lilas, and there was nothing on earth she could do about it.

* * *

That night she lay awake doing arithmetic.

Two days for delivery, probably. One day for Delphine to open it, or five, depending on how she felt about mail from children. Then some number of days for fury, or grief, or — please — the other thing. Then a confrontation, the shape of which Amira could not predict and therefore imagined in every possible configuration until three a.m., at which point she got up, drank milk straight from the carton like a criminal, and made a decision.

She would not tell him.

This was not a good decision. She knew it was not a good decision even as she made it. But telling him meant confessing, and confessing meant the letter might get intercepted somehow — except it couldn't be, it was already gone, it was *in the system*, which was somehow worse — but mostly telling him meant watching his face while he learned that his daughter had read the most private thing he'd ever written and then distributed it without permission, and Amira had seen her father's face go closed exactly once, the day the estate notice came, and she could not bear to be the cause of it.

So: silence. She would let the letter land wherever letters land, and she would live in the gap between mailing and consequence, and if it worked — if it *worked* — then no one ever needed to know, and if it didn't work, well.

Well.

She thought about the sentence again, the one from page nine. *I am asking you to stay.* He'd written it and never sent it, and she had sent it and never told him, and between the two of them they had produced one complete act of courage, split down the middle like a choreography neither had agreed to.

"Sorry, Papa," she whispered to the ceiling, which was not sorry enough to confess and too sorry to sleep.

Down the hall, her father's floor creaked once, the way it did when he couldn't sleep either, and stood a long time by the window facing the direction of the station, though of course no trains ran at that hour, and hadn't for years, and the last one that mattered had left long before Amira was born.

Three streets away, an envelope lay in a canvas sack, beginning the slow journey across town.

It would arrive on Saturday.
