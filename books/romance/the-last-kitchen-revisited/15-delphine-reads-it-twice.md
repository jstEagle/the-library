## 15. Delphine Reads It Twice

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which Delphine would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days her aunt had always said nothing important ever happened, and here was the universe disproving her posthumously.

It was thin. That was the first thing she noticed—the way you notice a person's hands before their face. A single sheet folded twice inside an envelope with no return address, only the town's name in a child's careful cursive, and beneath it, in smaller, more hurried letters: *You should read this.*

Delphine stood in her aunt's kitchen—her kitchen now, technically, though she still couldn't say the word *inherited* without hearing it as *orphaned*—and turned the envelope over twice before opening it. The handwriting inside was not the child's.

She knew that handwriting. She had known it for eleven years the way you know a scar: not by looking at it, but by the weather it predicted.

*Noor.*

Her name at the top of the page. Her name in his hand, dated the week she left, and below it a sentence she read once before the room tilted slightly and she had to sit down at her aunt's table, the one with the wobble she'd never gotten fixed because fixing it would have meant admitting she was staying.

*Delphine—I have started this letter nine times. This is the tenth. I am sending it or I am burning it, and I find I cannot do either, so I am doing the coward's third thing, which is hiding it in a wall where even God will have to renovate to find it.*

She laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

*You asked me once why I never say what I mean, and I told you I was saving it. That was a lie. I don't save things. I bury them. My mother did it, and her mother before her—we are a family of excellent masons and terrible speakers. What I should have said on the platform, while the train was making up its mind, is this:*

*Don't go.*

*Two words. I have carried them around for months like stones in my shoe. Don't go. Not because your life here is more important than your life there—you were right about Paris, you were right about everything, you were always right, which is its own kind of unbearable—but because I love you, and I never learned how to say that sentence out loud without feeling like a man asking for something he hasn't earned.*

*I am not asking you to stay. I am telling you, too late and in the wrong direction, that if you went, you would be taking the shape of my whole future with you, and I would spend the rest of my life walking around inside a house with one wall missing, pretending the draft was character.*

*If you are reading this, then either Amira has disobeyed me—which is likely—or the wall has given way, which is what walls do eventually when you ask them to hold things they were never built to hold.*

*Don't come back for me. But if you ever come back for any reason at all, look for me in the kitchen. It's where I keep everything true.*

*—N.*

Delphine read it again.

Not because she hadn't understood it the first time. She understood it perfectly, in the way you understand a diagnosis. She read it again because the first reading had happened to someone standing up and the second needed someone sitting down, and by the third reading she was furious.

Furious at him—eleven years, eleven *years*, he had known, he had written it down, he had *dated* it, he had put a date on his love like a man stamping a document he intended to file and forget. Furious at the child who had mailed it without asking, though the fury there kept collapsing into something dangerously close to gratitude. Furious at her aunt, who had surely known—had kept clippings, had kept photographs, had kept watch over a life Delphine had abandoned and never once said *come home, child, look what's still here*. Furious most of all at herself, at twenty-six, boarding that train with her chin up and her heart already rehearsing its own obituary, waiting—*waiting*—for the platform to give her a reason, and receiving instead a wave and a smile and a sentence so polite it could have been printed on a menu.

He had written *don't go* and mailed it to a wall.

She read it a fourth time, sitting in the dark because she'd never turned on the lights, and by then the fury had burned down to coals, and under the coals was the thing she'd actually crossed the ocean carrying: not grief for her aunt, not paperwork, not a building. Hope. Hope she'd dressed up as duty. And hope, it turned out, could be wounded worse than grief, because grief expects nothing and hope had spent eleven years expecting exactly this letter and had received, until Tuesday, nothing at all.

She got up and put on her coat.

* * *

The bookshop's lights were still on at nine, which meant Noor was doing the closing ritual she remembered: straightening the poetry first, because poetry left out overnight got lonely, or so he'd claimed once, laughing, and she'd thought *I will love this ridiculous man forever,* and then she'd boarded a train to prove otherwise.

The bell over the door did its small bright thing. He looked up from the counter with a stack of returns in his arms, and she watched the recognition move through him like weather—surprise, alarm, and underneath both, so briefly she might have imagined it, relief.

"Delphine." Her name in his mouth after eleven years. It sounded the same. That was somehow the worst part. "We're—actually we're closed, but—"

"I know you're closed." She held up the letter between two fingers, like evidence at a trial, which was fair, because she'd come to hold one. "I know you're closed, Noor. I've been closed for eleven years."

His arms tightened around the books. "Amira," he said quietly. Not a question.

"She signed it *you should read this.* Which, for the record, is the most honest sentence anyone in your family has produced in a decade."

"She's twelve."

"She's twelve and braver than you, and she knows it, and someday that's going to break her heart, so we may want to discuss that, but first—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated that her body refused to conduct this as the clean, cold tribunal she'd planned on the walk over. "First you're going to explain to me why this was in a wall."

"It wasn't supposed to be found."

"No. Clearly. You addressed it to the studs." She stepped further in, past the biography table, past the reading chairs, into the territory of the argument. "Nine drafts, Noor. Nine. And the tenth you hid in the architecture. Do you know what I did on that train? Do you have any idea?"

"I've imagined it."

"You haven't." The words came out flat and certain, because they were. "I know you haven't, because if you had—if you had spent one hour honestly imagining it—you would have gotten on the train yourself. I sat in seat 14C and I watched the platform through the window for six minutes. Six minutes, Noor. I gave you six minutes. All you had to do was run. People run in films constantly; it's practically a civic duty. And you stood there with your hands in your pockets being *polite at me.*"

"I was going to say it." His voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice of a man setting down something heavy. "I want you to know that. I walked to that station having decided. I had the sentence. I'd rehearsed it for three weeks."

"And?"

"And then I saw your face, and you looked so *certain*, Delphine. You looked like a woman who had already grieved the leaving and packed it. And I thought—who am I to stand in front of a train with two words? Who am I to make my fear weigh more than her ambition? So I swallowed it. I told myself it was generosity." He set the books down on the counter, carefully, one at a time, like he was buying time in installments. "It took me about four years to understand it was cowardice wearing generosity's coat."

"Four years," she repeated. "I gave it eighteen months before I stopped checking whether your letters were coming. Except you didn't write letters, did you. You wrote *a* letter. Singular. To drywall."

"The wall was safer."

"Everything was safer! That's the whole tragedy of you! You safe-proofed your entire life until there was nothing left in it that could hurt you except the hole where I used to be!" She heard herself and stopped, breathing hard, and realized distantly that she was crying, which she had strictly forbidden herself to do on the walk over, and that Noor had come around the counter and was standing three feet away with his hands open at his sides like a man showing he was unarmed.

"Say it," he said softly. "Whatever the next sentence is. Say it. I've waited eleven years; I can wait through the ugly part."

"The ugly part?" She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "Noor, the ugly part is that I read your letter tonight and I *understood every word of it*, because I wrote the same one. In my head. On the train. For years. *Come find me. Admit it was a mistake. Give me a reason to be the woman who stayed.* And you never did, and so I built a whole life on the theory that you hadn't loved me enough, because that theory, at least, let me keep my dignity. And now I find out you loved me the entire amount and just—" her voice broke clean through— "just *filed it*."

"In a wall," he agreed, wrecked and gentle. "I'm aware of the filing system. It's not my best work."

And despite everything—despite the fury still banked in her chest like a stove that would take hours to cool—she felt the laugh escape, one broken note, and saw him catch it, and understood that this was how it was going to be with them: everything arriving tangled, grief and laughter sharing a coat pocket, the truth coming out sideways in an argument because neither of them had ever been able to build a straight road to it.

"Why didn't you burn it?" she asked. Quieter now. "You wrote that you would send it or burn it. Why the wall?"

He was silent long enough that the clock over the door ticked into the silence and out again.

"Because burning it would have meant admitting it was finished," he said. "And some stubborn part of me—the part that hid it instead of destroying it—was keeping it as evidence. That I'd felt it. Even if I never said it. Even if no one ever knew." He looked at her then, fully, and she saw the boy from the platform still living in his face, older, penitent, unextinguished. "I suppose I was waiting for a renovation."

"Eleven years," she whispered. "You let eleven years stand between us because saying a sentence felt like drowning."

"Yes."

"And you're sorry."

"Not for loving you. Never for that. For everything else—all of it, every day, in a language I'm better at now. Ask the town. They'll tell you I apologize compulsively. It's become a regional landmark."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and something in her chest shifted—not closed, not healed, but *moved*, the way a locked joint moves the first time after a long winter. The fight hadn't fixed anything. But it had aired the house. Eleven years of sealed rooms, and now windows open, draft everywhere, and the draft, God help her, felt like the thing he'd promised in the letter: a house with one wall missing, finally admitting where the wall had gone.

"There's a hearing," she said. "Gerard's deadline. The clause. *Continuing service to the town.*"

"I know."

"I hold the pen, Noor." She said it steadily, though her hands weren't. "And I came here tonight ready to hate you, and it didn't work, and I need you to understand that the letter changes nothing legally and everything else entirely, and I don't know yet what I'm going to do, because I have spent eleven years learning how to live without you and apparently I was bad at it the whole time."

He nodded slowly. Then, in the voice of a man who had finally learned which sentences mattered and refused to lose another one:

"For what it's worth—I'm not going to hide in a wall this time. Whatever you decide. I'll be standing on the platform. Both feet. Saying it out loud, in front of whoever shows up."

She looked at him—at this absurd, maddening, faithful man among his lonely poetry—and felt the last coal of anger gutter into something warmer and far more dangerous.

"Don't rehearse it," she said, pulling her coat tighter, moving toward the door. "I've heard your rehearsals. Just say it."

The bell rang its small bright thing behind her, and outside, the night air hit her face cold and clean, and Delphine Marchand stood on the pavement of a town she'd left at twenty-six and thought: *he's going to say it. He's actually going to say it. And I have no idea what I'll answer—and for the first time in eleven years, I cannot wait to find out.*
