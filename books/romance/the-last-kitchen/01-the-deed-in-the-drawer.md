## 1. The Deed in the Drawer

The papers smelled of cedar and time, and Lena had been breathing them in for three hours when she found the thing that would undo her.

She'd set up in the back room of the bookshop, the one with the good north light and the radiator that knocked like a polite ghost. October pressed its face against the window—Maple Hollow in full amber dress, the sugar maples along Wren Street going up like slow fires—and around her feet rose the archaeology of her grandmother's life: seed catalogues from 1987, a receipt for a pressure canner, letters tied with butcher's twine, photographs curling at their edges like leaves deciding to let go.

The library wanted an exhibit. *A Century of Willow Orchard*, they'd called it, and Ruthie at the front desk had said it with such bright civic hope that Lena hadn't been able to refuse. So every evening after June went to bed, after the *CLOSED* sign was flipped and the last customer's bell had gone quiet, Lena sat cross-legged on the floor with white cotton gloves and sorted her grandmother's seven decades into tidy archival boxes.

It was good work. It was the kind of work she had built her whole life out of—the invisible kind, the keeping-things-in-order kind. Catalogue the past. Shelve the books alphabetically. Water the orchard rows before anyone was awake to see you doing it. If you moved carefully enough through the world, the world mostly left you alone, and Lena Zhou had spent seven years becoming very, very careful.

June was at Priya's house doing homework that was probably not homework. The kettle had gone cold an hour ago. Outside, a truck ground past with a load of pumpkins, and somewhere down the block the bakery's exhaust fan pushed the smell of tomorrow's cinnamon into the street.

Lena opened the third drawer of the oak filing cabinet, the one that stuck and had always stuck, and pulled out a folder labeled in her grandmother's square, unhesitating hand: **SURVEYS & BOUNDARIES — DO NOT LOSE**.

Her grandmother had never lost anything in her life. Edith Zhou had run Willow Orchard for forty years with a ledger book, a grafting knife, and the moral certainty of a woman who believed weather was a personal negotiation. Lena had inherited the orchard the way you inherit a heartbeat—without asking, without being able to imagine the silence after.

Inside the folder: plat maps soft as cloth from handling. A 1998 boundary agreement signed by two neighbors long dead. A soil survey with marginalia in pencil (*clay here — stubborn like your grandfather*). And then, near the bottom, a document Lena didn't recognize. Newer. A certified survey from eleven years ago, commissioned by the county, with a cover letter clipped to it.

She read the plat map first, because maps were honest in a way people weren't. The orchard spread across the page in inked lines, the creek stitching along its western edge, the old kitchen sitting in the northeast corner like a comma. She knew every line of this land by feel—knew where the Gravensteins gave way to the Baldwins, knew which row flooded in March, knew the exact spot where June had learned to ride a bike and the exact spot where Lena went to cry where no one could hear her.

Then she looked at the parcel lines, and the world tilted one degree off true.

The line did not run where she had always believed it ran. It ran forty feet east of the creek—a sliver, no, more than a sliver, a whole rib of the orchard, nearly six acres including the western rows of Baldwin apples and, God, the kitchen itself, the old stone kitchen with the wood stove where her grandmother had made cider donuts every October until her hands got too stiff to knead.

Six acres. Half the heart of the place.

Lena's first thought was absurdly practical: *the fence is wrong.* They'd strung that fence wrong for years, then. Her second thought was colder: *who owns it?*

She unclipped the cover letter. The county's language was dry as August grass, all *whereas* and *hereinafter*, but one paragraph snagged her like a fishhook:

*...in accordance with the recorded subdivision of 1974, the westerly parcel (Parcel B, approx. 6.1 acres) remains titled to the Marchetti estate, current holder of record: Theodore J. Marchetti...*

The room went very quiet. Not actually quiet—the radiator still knocked, the bakery fan still hummed—but inside her chest, everything stopped moving at once, the way a forest goes silent before weather.

*Marchetti.*

She said the name without sound, just shaped it with her lips, testing whether it still burned. It did.

Theo Marchetti. Who had left Maple Hollow at nineteen with his mother's recipe box and Lena's heart in his jacket pocket, though he'd only known about one of those. Who had promised, the summer before he left, that the orchard kitchen would be theirs someday—he'd cook, she'd grow—and who had then vanished so completely that for years Lena checked the obituaries whenever she passed the newspaper stand, just to know.

Seven years ago she'd come back to Maple Hollow pregnant and grieving her grandmother, and everyone in town had been tactful about the father the way small towns are tactful, which is to say loudly silent. She had built a life here out of secondhand shelves and orchard rows and June's laugh, and she had told herself the story so many times it had worn smooth as river glass: *I chose this. I am happy here. I need nothing that isn't already mine.*

And now a piece of paper in a sticking drawer said that half of what was hers had never been hers at all—that it belonged to a man who was a stranger now, or worse than a stranger, because strangers didn't have history, and Theo Marchetti had nothing but.

Lena sat down hard on the floor among her grandmother's papers. A photograph slid loose from the folder and landed face-up in her lap: the orchard kitchen, younger, its stone walls uncracked, and two teenagers in the doorway squinting into the sun. The boy had flour on his jaw. The girl was laughing at something he'd said, caught mid-laugh, unguarded in a way Lena could no longer remember how to be.

She turned the photo over. On the back, in her grandmother's hand: *T & L, harvest day. He'll be back. They always come back for what's theirs.*

Lena laughed—one short, cracked note with no humor in it—and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Outside, the light was failing. The maples along Wren Street had gone the color of embers, and the first real cold of the season was sliding down from the ridge, the kind of cold that meant frost by morning, the kind that meant the last of the apples needed picking. She should call Ruthie. She should call the county clerk. She should call a lawyer, someone sensible, someone who dealt in deeds and easements and not in seventeen-year-old summers.

Instead she sat in the darkening room holding a photograph of a boy with flour on his face, and did the arithmetic she'd spent seven years refusing to do: if Parcel B was his—if it had always been his—then Theo Marchetti owned the kitchen. The kitchen where her grandmother had taught her to pit cherries. The kitchen where June had taken her first steps on the worn brick floor. The kitchen that was, in every way that mattered, the center of everything Lena had rebuilt.

He could come back and take it. He could come back and—

The phone buzzed on the floor beside her. A text from Priya: *June wants to sleep over, ok? Also someone was asking at the diner today if the Marchetti boy ever came back. Small towns, am I right?*

Lena stared at the screen until the words blurred.

Then, from the front of the shop, faint through the closed door, came the sound of the doorbell chiming—though she had locked it an hour ago—and footsteps crossing the creaky board by the poetry section, unhurried, familiar in some way her body recognized before her mind allowed it.

Lena stood up in the dark, the survey trembling in her gloved hands, and listened to someone walk toward her through the shop that smelled of old paper and apples, knowing—with the terrible certainty of a woman reading her own plat map—who it would be.

* * *

She didn't move. That was the coward's arithmetic: if she stayed still among the boxes, maybe the footsteps would pass, maybe the past would browse the fiction section and leave. But the footsteps stopped outside the back room door, and a shadow filled the frosted glass—broad-shouldered, hatless, patient—and then knuckles rapped twice, softly, the way he used to knock on her grandmother's porch rail.

"Lena?" The voice was lower than memory, roughened by years and other kitchens, but it was his. "I saw the light. I know it's late." A pause, and then, almost gently, almost like an apology: "I'm back. And I think we need to talk about the orchard."

In her hands, the county letter seemed to burn straight through the cotton gloves, and Lena understood that the careful, invisible life she had built was over—had ended, probably, the moment she opened the drawer—and that whatever walked through that door next, she would have to meet it standing up.
