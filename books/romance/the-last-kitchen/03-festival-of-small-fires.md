## 3. Festival of Small Fires

The smoke reached Theo before the music did—woodsmoke and cider and something charred-sweet drifting down from the town square like a hand pulling him forward by the collar. Maple Hollow's autumn festival had been small when he was seventeen: a few fire barrels, a fiddle player who was also the mailman. Now it spilled across three streets, lanterns strung between the brick faces of buildings that hadn't changed at all, and the whole town glowed amber against the early dark.

He'd told himself he came for the orchard business. The developer's people wanted to meet here, in public, where the town could see them shaking hands. *Good optics*, Priya had said on the phone, her voice doing that thing it did when she meant *be strategic*. But standing at the edge of the square with his hands in his coat pockets, Theo knew the truth he wouldn't say aloud even to himself: he had come because Lena Zhou would be here, and because seven years of silence had taught him nothing about how to stay away from her.

She was at a stall near the bookshop, stringing paper stars onto a line with June, and the sight of them together hit him like cold water. June had his stubbornness in her jaw—he saw it now, in the set of her mouth as she argued about knot placement—and Lena laughed at whatever the girl said, and the sound carried across the square and undid something in his chest he'd spent two decades welding shut.

*That's your daughter,* he thought. *That girl selling paper stars is yours.*

He didn't go over. He bought a cup of mulled cider from a man wearing a fox hat instead, and burned his tongue on it, and stood there like an idiot watching his family by lantern light while his phone buzzed in his pocket with messages from people who thought land was just money waiting to be liquidated.

***

The meeting happened in the back room of the Grange Hall, which smelled of varnish and old apples, and it went exactly the way Theo should have known it would go.

"Three point two million," said Whitaker, the developer's acquisition man, sliding a folder across the folding table. "Cash. Thirty-day close. And frankly, Marchetti, you'd be a fool not to take it."

Priya leaned in beside him, all sharp elbows and sharper numbers. "He's not taking it yet, but he's listening, which is more than we got last week. Theo, walk me through your hesitation again? Because from where I sit, you're sitting on half an orchard in a dying town, running a restaurant group that's bleeding cash in two markets, and this deal saves all of it."

Theo turned the folder without opening it. Through the window he could see the square, the fires, the paper stars Lena had strung catching the light like a constellation someone had hung too low.

"The hesitation is that it isn't my land alone," he said. "There's a co-owner."

"Lena Zhou." Priya said the name flatly, like reading it off a spreadsheet. "The bookshop woman. We've done the research. She can't match this offer—not in ten years. You buy out her half or you force the sale. Either way, the deal closes."

"She won't sell," Theo said. "Not to me. Not to anyone."

"Then you force partition." Whitaker shrugged, as if he were discussing weather. "Courts do it every day. Land gets divided, sold at auction, everyone walks away paid. It's not personal."

It wasn't personal. Theo almost laughed. Nothing in this room was personal—that was the whole architecture of it, the whole comfort of it. Numbers didn't bleed. Spreadsheets didn't remember the taste of cider stolen off a porch rail in September, or a girl with flour on her wrists teaching him to fold dumplings in a kitchen that no longer existed except in the deed drawer of a dead woman's house.

"I need until the end of the festival," he heard himself say. "Monday. I'll give you an answer Monday."

"Theo—" Priya started.

"Monday," he repeated, and closed the folder, and walked out into the smoke and the music before either of them could make the argument he was already losing to.

***

He found the bonfire crowd gathered around the big pit behind the church, where the festival's namesake fires burned in iron rings and children ran with sparklers like comets on short leashes. Someone pressed a roasted apple into his hand. A woman he vaguely remembered as Mrs. Okafor from the hardware store squinted at him and said, "Marchetti boy. Back from the dead," and he said, "Something like that," and she said, "Hm," in a tone that contained multitudes, none of them welcoming.

He was looking for Lena. That was the simple, humiliating truth of his evening. He'd been looking for her since the moment he arrived, and he found her finally at the edge of the firelight, sitting on a hay bale with June's head on her shoulder, both of them watching the flames. He was three steps away—close enough to see the firelight move in Lena's eyes, close enough to speak—when he heard his own name.

"—and then what?" June was saying. "If he sells?"

Lena was quiet for a moment. A log collapsed in the nearest ring, throwing sparks up like startled birds.

"Then he sells," she said. "And I'll be angry, and I'll fight it, and probably lose, because men like Theo Marchetti always have lawyers and I have a bookshop." She said it lightly, but Theo heard the steel under it, the same steel that had once told him *don't come back* through a screen door in the rain. "But you know what the strange thing is, June-bug?"

"What?"

"He's still the mistake that saved me."

The world went very quiet around those six words. The fiddler kept playing; the children kept running; the fire kept eating its wood. None of it reached Theo. He stood in the dark past the edge of the light with a roasted apple going cold in his hand and felt the sentence go through him like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

*The mistake that saved me.*

Not *the love of my life.* Not *the one who left.* A mistake. Something that happened to her, that she survived, that she'd built a life on top of the way you build on rubble—grateful for the foundation, hating the earthquake. Seven years of raising his daughter alone, and this was the story she told about him, softly, by firelight, to the child they'd made.

He didn't hear the rest. He was already walking, back through the square, past the stalls, past the lanterns, his breath coming fast and shallow in the cold. His phone was in his hand before he knew he'd taken it out. Whitaker's number. The folder was in his car on the passenger seat, and inside it was a signature page, and thirty days from now Willow Orchard would be a rendering—a rooftop bar, a farm-to-table restaurant with a concept name, *Orchard & Main*, God, they'd already mocked up the logo—and none of it would be a mistake, because mistakes required that you had loved something first.

His thumb hovered over the contact.

Behind him, across the square, the fires burned on. Somewhere in that glow was a woman who had called him salvation and ruin in the same breath, and a daughter who tied bad knots, and a kitchen that existed only in memory and in half a deed. Ahead of him was a pen.

He got in the car. He sat in the dark with the engine off, the folder on his knees, and the signature page open to the light of the dash, and his hand was not steady.

That was when he saw, tucked into the windshield wiper where someone had left it while he was in the Grange Hall, a single paper star, folded crooked, with writing on one wing in a child's careful hand:

*You forgot to say goodbye the first time. — J.*

Theo Marchetti sat in the freezing car with a contract in his lap and a paper star in his fist, and understood that Monday was too far away, and too near, and that whatever he decided in the next forty-eight hours would decide everything else for the rest of his life.
