## 5. Arriving as Someone Else

**Emrys**

The car smelled of rain and Wren Halloway, which was to say: rain, and something warmer underneath it—bergamot, maybe, or the ghost of hospital soap, or simply the particular warmth of a person who had spent the evening holding dying strangers' hands and had not yet learned to be afraid of touch.

Emrys kept both hands on the wheel and both eyes on the road, which was a lie, because at least once a mile their eyes slid sideways to the passenger seat, where Wren sat with the napkin contract smoothed flat on their knee like a relic, like scripture, like a joke that had not yet finished being told.

"Okay," Wren said. "Run it again. From the top."

"We met on Platform Nine."

"Wrong."

Emrys glanced over. "That's what happened."

"That's what happened to *us*. Nobody falls in love on a platform. Platforms are where people say goodbye." Wren's voice had a lilt to it, a performer's instinct Emrys had not expected from a hospice pianist, though perhaps hospices were the last great stages—every performance a premiere and a farewell at once. "We met at the station *café*. You were reading. I was late for my shift. I asked what you were reading and you said—"

"*The Anatomy of Melancholy*."

"And I said, 'That sounds like a diagnosis,' and you said—"

"'It is.'"

"And I laughed." Wren demonstrated the laugh, low and genuine, and Emrys felt it land somewhere beneath the sternum, in the territory the cardiologists had mapped and failed to secure. "And then I missed my train on purpose. Which is the true part, so it's easy to remember."

"The true parts are easy to remember," Emrys agreed. "That's the problem with them."

They drove on. The coastal road unspooled ahead of them, black and gleaming with rain, the sea invisible to the left but audible, a long slow breathing in the dark. Streetlights arrived at intervals like thoughts Emrys hadn't invited: *you have known this person for eleven hours. You have paid them. You are driving them toward your family like a lamb toward a very polite slaughter.*

"Ask me something," Wren said suddenly. "Something a partner would know. Something your mother will ask me over the soup course, because mothers always interrogate over soup. It's how they establish dominance."

Emrys's mouth did something unauthorized. "You've met my type of family before."

"Honey, I've played piano in hospices for six years. I have sat with more families than I can count. I know exactly how they work. The ones who love each other show it in the silences. The ones who don't show it in the *food*." Wren turned in the seat to face them, and the dashboard light caught the fine bones of their face, the small scar above the eyebrow Emrys had been trying not to catalog. "Soup course. Go."

"She'll ask how we met. She'll ask what I'm like when I'm—"

"When you're what?"

"When I'm *not performing*." The words came out flatter than Emrys intended. "She'll want to know if I'm kind. She'll want to know if I'm happy. She'll want to know if I eat enough, because in her cosmology all unhappiness is a dietary deficiency."

"And what do I say?"

Emrys watched the road. The wipers kept their patient metronome. "You say I'm quiet. You say I'm kind when I remember to be. You say I make you tea in the mornings and you've never seen me eat breakfast, which is true of everyone who has ever known me, including me."

Wren was quiet for a moment. When they spoke again, the lilt had gone out of their voice, replaced by something gentler and more dangerous. "You rehearse your own failures very fluently, you know that?"

"It's a skill."

"It's a *symptom*."

The word sat in the car between them. Emrys's hands tightened on the wheel. Somewhere under the ribs, the old traitor muscle gave a small arrhythmic cough, and Emrys pretended not to notice, the way they had been pretending for two years, the way pretending had become a full-time occupation with no salary and no pension.

"Landmines," Emrys said, because the alternative was to answer. "You wanted the landmines."

"Hit me."

"My father does not hug. He shakes hands. If he shakes your hand, it means he has accepted you provisionally, the way one accepts a tenant. Do not try to convert the handshake into an embrace. He will short-circuit."

"Noted."

"My brother Caspian is a vascular surgeon and will find a way to mention it within four minutes of meeting you. My sister Odile runs a foundation and will find a way to mention *that* within three. There is a wall in the main hall with all their achievements on it. Mine is—" Emrys heard their own voice go carefully, professionally neutral. "Mine is a small shelf. It has a trophy from a youth competition on it. I was twelve."

"And the cello?"

"What about it?"

"Is there a cello on the shelf?"

Emrys said nothing. The wipers swept. The sea breathed.

"There's no cello on the shelf," Wren said softly. "There's no cello anywhere, is there. Not in your workshop. Not in your car. You restore them but you don't—"

"Landmines, Wren."

"Right. Sorry." But they didn't sound sorry; they sounded like someone filing something away, which was worse. "Continue. Caspian, Odile, the wall, the shelf. What else?"

"Aunt Marguerite will ask when we're getting married. This is not a question; it is a weather report. You may answer with anything. 'Eventually' works. 'We're taking our time' works. Do not say 'never,' she will take it as a challenge and produce a list of available venues."

"God, she sounds wonderful."

"She's a nightmare and I love her, which is the Almeida family condition in miniature. Everyone is a nightmare and everyone is loved and nobody is *known*." Emrys heard the sentence leave their mouth and was surprised by it. It was truer than most things they said aloud. "And there's—there's one more. My father's retirement. That's the occasion. Forty years at the conservatory. There will be toasts. If someone toasts to *legacy*, look at your plate. If someone toasts to *the children*, look at your plate harder."

"Because you're the child the legacy missed."

Emrys's jaw tightened. "Because I'm the child the legacy *dropped*, and everyone in that room remembers the sound it made."

The car hummed. Rain needled the windshield. And then Wren did the thing Emrys would think about later, much later, in rooms with better lighting and worse odds: they reached across the console and rested their hand, lightly, on Emrys's forearm. Just that. No pressure. The warmth of it bled through the sleeve like a slow dye.

"Okay," Wren said. "Your turn to learn something. Watch my face."

"What?"

"Smile for me."

"That's absurd."

"It's rehearsal. Everything tonight is rehearsal. Smile."

Emrys smiled the way they smiled at clients when a restoration quote came in higher than expected—brief, closed, professionally retractable.

"No." Wren's voice was not unkind. "That's a door closing. Try again. Think of something that isn't a performance. Think of—" They paused, and Emrys watched them hunt for it, watched the rain move in the window behind their head. "Think of the platform. Last night. When I said yes. What did your face do before you could stop it?"

Emrys thought of it. The rain on the platform, the last train sighing away without either of them, the stranger with the hospice lanyard and the tired, amused eyes saying *how much are we talking about* as though being bought for a weekend were the most reasonable thing offered to them all year. And Emrys's face, before the discipline arrived, had done something it hadn't done in two years. Something unguarded. Something that had felt, in the moment, like a window opening in a house long shuttered.

"There," Wren said. "That one. Keep that one."

Emrys hadn't realized they were doing it. The smile sat on their face like a bird that had landed without permission, and they let it stay, three seconds, five, while the road unspooled and the sea breathed and the traitor under their ribs beat its small stubborn rhythm.

"That's the one your mother will believe," Wren said. "That's the one that's going to cause problems."

"What kind of problems?"

"The kind we agreed not to have." Wren withdrew the hand, retrieved the napkin, held it up. "Rule three. Read it back to me."

Emrys read. "'No feelings. This is a performance, and performances end.'"

"Good. Rule four."

"'No history. We are strangers who fell in love recently and conveniently.'"

"Rule five."

"'Either party may end the performance at any time, no questions asked, no refunds issued.'" Emrys lowered the napkin. "You know these rules are insane. You wrote half of them."

"I wrote the *escape* clauses. You wrote the fantasy." Wren folded the napkin with great care, along its original creases, and put it in their breast pocket, over the heart, which Emrys chose to read as a joke. "I'm a professional, Almeida. I know how performances end. I've sat through the last bars of a few hundred of them."

* * *

**Wren**

The house appeared out of the rain the way wealth always did to Wren—suddenly, and with the confidence of something that had never once had to explain itself. Wrought iron gates. A drive lined with bare plane trees. The Almeida house rose at the end of it, honey-colored stone going gold in the lamplight, windows lit warm against the wet dark, and it was beautiful the way a museum was beautiful: everything polished, nothing lived in.

"You okay?" Emrys asked. The car had stopped. Wren hadn't moved.

"I'm running lines."

"You've known the lines for three hours."

"And I've been nervous for three hours. I'm very efficient." Wren looked at the house, at the lit windows behind which a family was assembling like an orchestra tuning—each instrument confident, none of them in key. "Can I tell you something true? Off the record. Not in the script."

Emrys's hands went still on the wheel. "Off the record."

"I've spent six years in rooms where people are dying, and the thing they all say at the end—the thing that comes up every single time, in every variation—isn't *I wish I'd worked more* or *I wish I'd been braver*. It's some version of *I wish I'd said it*. Whatever *it* was. To whoever it belonged to." Wren turned to look at Emrys, at the fine tired architecture of their face, the way they held themselves like an instrument in its case—protected, silent, worth more than anyone around them suspected. "So here's mine, off the record: whatever happens in there, whoever you have to be for them—you were a stranger who bought me a weekend, and you were also the first person in years who looked at me like I was a person and not a service. That part isn't in the script. But it's true. So when I smile at you across the dinner table tonight, some of it will be acting." Wren picked up their bag. "And some of it won't. And I'm telling you now so you don't spend the weekend doing that thing you do, where you analyze everything until it's safe and dead."

Emrys stared at them. The rain ticked on the roof. Somewhere in the house, a door opened; lamplight spilled down the front steps; a silhouette appeared, tall and straight-backed, unmistakably a father waiting to take the measure of his child's guest.

"That," Emrys said finally, quietly, "was not in the script."

"No," Wren agreed, and opened the door into the rain. "It was worse. It was the truth. Ready?"

Emrys did not answer right away. Wren watched them look at the house, at the lit windows, at the waiting father, and watched them do the thing they had apparently done their whole life: the long slow inhale of a performer stepping into the light, gathering the self they had built to survive the room.

"No," Emrys said. "But I never am. That's the family condition."

They got out of the car together into the rain, and Emrys's hand found Wren's—rehearsed, contractual, a prop for the audience on the porch—and held on a beat longer than the script required.

The front door opened. A tall man in a cardigan the color of good whiskey stood in the light, and behind him Wren could already see it: the wall of achievements, the framed photographs, the whole museum of a family's carefully curated love.

"Emrys," the man said. "You came."

"I came," Emrys said, and Wren felt the hand tighten around theirs, felt the tremor in it that had nothing to do with the cold, and understood that the weekend's most difficult performance would not be Wren's at all.

It would be watching Emrys Almeida walk back into the house that had taught them to disappear—and trying, with everything Wren had, to keep them visible.
