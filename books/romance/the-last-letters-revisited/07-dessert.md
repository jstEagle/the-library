## 7. Dessert

The dining room at Almeida House was built for acoustics, though no one in the family seemed to know it. Wren noticed these things the way a pianist notices them—automatically, professionally—the way the ceiling's coffered height would carry a whispered confession to every corner of the table, the way the long windows threw afternoon light across the linen like a stage wash. Twenty-two people seated along a table that could have hosted a string quartet and its entire audience. Crystal that rang when touched. And beneath it all, running under the conversation like a bass line too low to name: the hum of a family performing itself for itself.

Wren had spent six years playing for the dying. They knew performance intimately—knew it as a sacred act, a last gift, the thing you offered when there was nothing left to offer but sound. But this table performed for different reasons. This table performed the way walls hold up roofs: structurally, without thinking, because if they stopped the whole house would come down.

They were seated between Emrys and a cousin named Beatrice who had already told Wren three times about her daughter's acceptance to Juilliard, each telling slightly more triumphant than the last. Across the table, Emrys's father sat at the head like a tuning fork struck once decades ago and still faintly ringing—Augustin Almeida, retired after forty years of cardiac surgery, his hands folded on the white cloth with the stillness of a man who had held a great many hearts and never once been asked how his own was doing.

And beside Wren—beside Wren, close enough that their knees had been touching under the table since the soup course—Emrys Almeida sat with the rigid grace of someone holding a difficult note past the point of comfort.

Wren had learned things about Emrys in thirty-six hours that no contract should have contained. They'd learned that Emrys buttered bread in small, precise quadrants, as if even carbohydrates required structure. That they went quiet—not relaxed-quiet, but vigilant-quiet—in rooms where their siblings were praised. That their left hand, the one not resting on the tablecloth, had found Wren's knee twice during dinner: once when Beatrice mentioned the word *concerto*, and once when their father laughed at something the eldest brother said, a laugh Wren suspected had been rare currency in this house, spent mostly on other children.

The lemon cake arrived on a silver stand, carried by a woman Wren guessed had worked here longer than any of the siblings had been alive. It was tall and pale as a wedding cake, glazed so the light slid off it, candied zest scattered across the top like small yellow confessions.

"Marion's recipe," said Emrys's mother, Eleanor, from her place near the father. She said it the way people say the names of saints. "She made it for every family occasion for thirty years. She passed two winters ago, but we kept the recipe." A pause, delicate as porcelain. "Some things you don't let go of."

Wren felt Emrys go still beside them. Not visibly—Emrys did invisibility beautifully, had clearly practiced it for years—but Wren felt it the way you feel a string stop vibrating in the next room. Under the table, Wren's hand moved without consulting policy. It found Emrys's knee and stayed.

*Rule three,* the napkin had said, in Emrys's narrow handwriting: *No unnecessary touching.* Rule four: *No real history.* Rule five, added by Wren in looser script: *We reserve the right to amend.*

They were amending.

Plates were served. Forks were lifted. And then—Wren saw it coming the way you see a chord change coming, four bars out—Eleanor Almeida turned her soft, terrible attention down the table and said:

"Wren. We've hardly heard from you at all. Tell us how you and Emrys met. I want *everything*. Emrys is so private." A smile that was entirely loving and entirely a scalpel. "We have to take our intelligence where we can get it."

Twenty-two faces turned. The room's acoustics, Wren thought absurdly, really were excellent. Every breath would carry.

Beside them, Emrys had stopped breathing altogether.

Here is what Wren Halloway knew about improvisation, learned over six years of playing at bedsides where the request was always *play something, anything, whatever you remember*: the secret was never invention. The secret was honesty deployed at speed. You didn't make something up. You reached for what was true in your own chest and let it wear the costume the moment required. Patients could tell the difference. Dying people especially—they had no patience left for fabrication. They wanted the real thing, even dressed wrong.

So Wren didn't invent. Wren reached.

"The platform," Wren said. "At Halden Cross. The last train on a Thursday night."

It wasn't even a lie. That was the first miracle of it—that the truest version and the fictional one shared a skeleton. They *had* met on Platform Nine. They *had* missed trains. Everything else was architecture built on ground that actually existed.

"I'd come off a shift," Wren went on, and heard their own voice do something it rarely did at tables like this—soften, drop into the register they used at hospice, the one that didn't perform. "I'm a musician. I play piano—at a care facility, mostly. For people who are—" They paused. Chose. "*For people nearing the end. It's strange work to describe at dinner. But I'd played for eleven hours that day, and I missed my train, and I was sitting on the bench feeling sorry for myself in a very professional way, and this person came storming down the platform arguing with their phone about a family reunion."

A ripple of laughter—nervous on some faces, delighted on others. Beside Wren, Emrys made a small sound that might have been protest or surrender.

"They were furious," Wren said. "Beautifully furious. Like the anger had been composed in advance, with dynamics marked. And I remember thinking—" and here Wren made the mistake, or the non-mistake; here Wren reached too deep into the well and came up with water instead of set dressing—"I remember thinking, *there's someone who has been carrying something alone for a very long time, and everyone around them has just gotten used to watching them carry it.*"

The table had gone quiet. Not politely quiet—actually quiet. Even the crystal had stopped ringing.

"And I thought, I know that look. I see it every day at work. It's the look of people who've decided they're easier to love from a distance." Wren turned then—had to turn, couldn't not—and looked at Emrys directly, which violated approximately four rules at once. "And I'm a selfish person in one specific way. When I see that look, I want to sit next to it. I can't help it. It's the whole reason I do the work I do. I sit next to it and I stay until it lets me."

Somewhere down the table, an aunt made a sound like a held breath being released.

"We talked for twenty minutes," Wren said, and now they were describing the actual platform, the actual rain starting, the actual phone call about this very reunion, and the fiction and the truth had fused so completely that Wren honestly could not have told a court of law which parts were assigned. "About nothing. Trains. Weather. Whether the vending machine was a crime against humanity. And when my train came—" Wren smiled, and the smile hurt, because the memory underneath it was real, was the single most real thing in Wren's recent life, "—I almost didn't get on it."

"You got on it," Emrys said quietly. First words in ten minutes. Their voice had a crack in it, hairline, audible only to someone trained to hear fractures.

"I got on it," Wren agreed. "Because I'm an idiot. Fortunately, the universe gives idiots second platforms."

Laughter broke the surface tension, warm and widespread, and Wren watched the table soften—watched shoulders drop, watched Eleanor press her napkin briefly to her mouth, watched even Augustin Almeida's folded hands loosen by a degree. The story had done what stories do in families: it had given them a new room in the house of themselves, and they moved into it immediately, gratefully, the way families accept any furniture that promises less pain than the old pieces.

But Wren wasn't watching the table anymore. Wren was watching Emrys.

Emrys was looking at their plate, at the untouched wedge of lemon cake, and their eyes were—Wren searched for the word and kept coming back to the same impossible one—*wet*. Not crying. Emrys Almeida did not cry; Wren would learn that later, would learn exactly how much pressure it took. But there was a shine there, a tremor at the lower lid, the look of a person standing in a doorway of a house they'd sealed shut years ago and finding, to their horror, that someone had opened a window somewhere and the air was moving through again.

*So does something in me,* Wren thought, surprised. *So does something.*

It was Beatrice—of course it was Beatrice, third glass of wine, Juilliard daughter fully exhausted as a topic—who leaned forward on her elbows and asked the question.

"But when did you *know*?" she said. "That it was love. Everyone always says you just know, but nobody ever says *when*, or what it feels like. Was it the platform? Was it later?" Her eyes were genuinely kind beneath the wine. "Emrys will never tell us. So you have to."

The table waited. Emrys had gone statue-still beside Wren, and Wren understood suddenly, completely, the shape of the trap: answer glibly and wound the person whose knee was under their hand; refuse and confirm every suspicion this family had ever harbored about Emrys's coldness; deflect and leave Emrys standing alone in the doorway again.

There was only one honest exit, and it led straight through the fire.

"It was the second day," Wren said.

Not the platform. Not the meet-cute. The second day—which hadn't happened yet, which was tomorrow, which was fiction—but the words came out of Wren's mouth with the unmistakable weight of testimony, and Wren realized, with a lurch like a missed stair, that they weren't fabricating at all. They were prophesying. They were describing something they already knew was going to be true.

"It was the second day," Wren said again, steadier. "When I saw them do something small and unguarded. Anyone can be charming on a platform—it's dark, the lighting's forgiving." Laughter, gentle. "But the second day, I watched Emrys talk about their work—restoring instruments, old cellos, bringing broken things back to playing condition—and their whole face changed. They talked about a cello the way I talk about music. Like it was a patient. Like it deserved gentleness. And I thought—" Wren's voice caught, actually caught, an imperfection no coach would have approved, "—I thought, *oh. There it is.* That's the moment. It's not fireworks. It's watching someone be tender with something that can't reward them for it, and realizing you want to be in the room while they do it. Forever, ideally."

Silence. The excellent acoustics carried everything: the tick of the mantel clock, the sea beyond the windows, the small wet sound of Eleanor Almeida setting down her fork.

"That's how you know," Wren finished, quietly. "You know when tenderness stops being a performance. When you catch yourself meaning it."

*I mean it,* Wren did not say. *Right now, at this table, in front of your lemons and your crystal, I mean every word, and I don't know when that started, and I'm terrified, and I'm going to keep talking anyway.*

Beside them, Emrys exhaled—a long, controlled release, the kind of breath that is the last structural element holding a building upright. And under the table, Emrys's hand moved. Found Wren's. Not the knee this time. The hand itself, fingers sliding between fingers, deliberate, public-by-proxy, hidden only by the tablecloth and the fact that no one was looking below the salt cellars.

Their grip shook. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Well," said Eleanor Almeida, and her voice had gone soft in a way that rearranged the whole room's weather. She looked at her child—looked at Emrys with an expression Wren suspected had been in storage for years, wrapped in tissue, waiting. "That may be the finest answer anyone has ever given at this table. And I have listened to forty years of answers at this table."

"Most of them mine," said Augustin, dry, and the table laughed with relief, and the moment crested and began its long roll toward shore.

Dessert resumed. Conversation restarted in patches—Beatrice launching gratefully into a story about a recital disaster, an uncle demanding to know whether the vending machine at Halden Cross truly was a criminal enterprise. Wren answered, smiled, passed the cream, performed the role with ease now, because the hardest scene was behind them and the audience had gone gentle.

But under the table, neither of them let go.

And when Wren finally glanced sideways—casually, between bites of cake they couldn't taste—they found Emrys already looking at them. Not with the wary gratitude of an employer. Not with the calculation of someone assessing a performance. With something rawer and more frightened and more awake, the look of a person who has just heard their own eulogy delivered warmly and discovered they're not ready to die to that version of themselves after all.

"You improvised," Emrys murmured, barely above the room's hum. "All of it."

"Mm," Wren said. "Professional habit."

"Which part was invented?"

Wren held their gaze. Held it past the point of safety, past the edge of the contract, out over open water.

"Ask me tomorrow," Wren said. "Day two. I'll tell you which part was invented on day two."

Something crossed Emrys's face then—fear and hope in the same instant, indistinguishable, twin birds flushing from the same cover—and they looked away quickly, down at the cake, at the candied zest, at anything. But their hand did not move from Wren's. If anything, it tightened.

Down the table, Eleanor Almeida was watching them both over the rim of her coffee cup, and her expression was no longer soft. It was knowing. It was the expression of a mother who had raised four children and missed nothing, ever, and who had just seen her most guarded child hold a stranger's hand like a rope thrown to someone drowning.

She said nothing. She only smiled—small, private, terrible in its tenderness—and lifted her cup a half inch, a toast to no one, meant for everyone.

Under the table, Emrys's pulse beat against Wren's palm. Fast. Too fast, maybe, Wren thought, filing the observation away with the others—the pallor at dinner, the careful way Emrys climbed stairs, the pill bottle Wren had glimpsed in the coat pocket that morning. A pianist's habit, cataloguing rhythms.

The cake stood unfinished. The evening stretched ahead of them, golden and dangerous, full of rooms they'd have to survive together.

*Tomorrow,* Wren thought, and squeezed the shaking hand once, and let go only when the plates were cleared—as little as possible, and not a moment sooner.
