## 9. Sunday, Departure

**Emrys**

Sunday arrived the way verdicts do—inevitable, and arriving too soon regardless.

Emrys woke before the light did, in the room the family had booked for the two of them, and lay still in the grey half-dark listening to Wren breathe. Four feet away, maybe five. A whole night of that sound and they had not once crossed the distance between the beds. The napkin rules sat folded in Emrys's jacket pocket like a confession they couldn't burn. *No feelings. No history. One performance.* They had written those rules on a rainy platform with a stranger's pen, and now the stranger's breathing was the most familiar sound in the house, and the rules had gone soft at the creases, illegible in any language Emrys trusted.

The Almeida house was already stirring below—the clatter of the good silver being gathered, the hum of the coffee machine that cost more than Emrys's first cello. Somewhere down the hall, their father's voice, measured and pleasant, saying goodbye to someone. Emrys had lain awake for an hour in the dark cataloguing the weekend the way they'd once catalogued recordings: the moment Wren's hand had found theirs under the dinner table, thumb moving once across their knuckles like a bow drawn gently over a string; the way Wren had said *lemon cake* with such conviction that Aunt Beatriz had nearly wept; the terrible, unguarded second at dessert when Wren had answered the cousin's question—*how did you know it was love?*—with something so honest it had stopped the whole room, and Emrys had felt the floor of their chest give way.

*It was love because it was quiet. Because for the first time I wasn't performing.*

They hadn't meant it. That was the thing Emrys kept returning to, the splinter under the skin. Wren hadn't meant it, and it had been true anyway, and true things spoken by accident in front of witnesses cannot be taken back, only survived.

Wren stirred. Emrys closed their eyes fast, a child's trick, and heard the rustle of sheets, the soft pad of feet, the pause—Emrys felt it like heat on their face—of someone standing over them, deciding something. Then the bathroom door, the shower running, the weekend beginning its long exhale.

They dressed in the clothes they'd arrived in. Downstairs, the family was assembled in the front hall in a tableau Emrys knew by heart from thirty years of departures: mother with her hands folded, father with his handshake ready, siblings collecting their children like instruments after a recital. The goodbyes were already happening to other people.

"There you are." Their mother crossed the hall first, and to Emrys's astonishment, hugged them—brief, fierce, smelling of lavender and the particular soap she'd used their whole childhood. "Wren is lovely," she murmured against their ear. "Bring them again. Or don't bring them, and come anyway. Either would make me happy. Both would make me happier."

"Mother."

"I'm allowed one sentence per visit. I've used it." She pulled back, and her eyes did the thing they always did—saw too much, said nothing. "Drive carefully. Both of you."

Their father offered his hand. Emrys took it. The grip was firm, correct, a handshake calibrated to the exact temperature of professional respect, and Emrys felt the old familiar arithmetic run itself: *still not enough, still the one who left the stage, still the one whose wall in the hall of achievements is bare.* But then, as Emrys turned, their father said, quietly, not quite meeting their eyes:

"The cello. The way Wren described you playing for the guests on Friday—" he paused, recalibrating. "You used to make it sound like weather. I'd forgotten."

It was a lie, or a kindness, or both. Emrys had not played all weekend. But their father's voice had a crack in it, hairline, barely audible, and Emrys carried it out to the car like something fragile they were afraid to set down.

Wren was already at the passenger door, bag over their shoulder, smiling the smile that had gotten them through the weekend—warm, easy, slightly crooked, a smile that should have been a lie by now and wasn't.

"Ready?" Wren said.

"No," Emrys said honestly, and Wren laughed, and the sound went through Emrys like a tuning fork struck against bone.

* * *

**Wren**

The drive to the station was forty minutes, and Wren spent thirty-nine of them composing eulogies for the weekend.

They were good at this. Hospice work taught you the shape of an ending—how it announces itself in small administrative gestures, the gathering of cups, the folding of blankets, the way conversation starts circling the safe subjects. Emrys drove with both hands on the wheel and a face like a locked instrument case, and Wren knew, the way they knew when a patient had decided it was time, that the performance was over and the closing of accounts had begun.

They had rehearsed this part too, in a way. On the drive up, two days and one geological era ago, they'd built a story: met on a train platform, which was true; fell for each other, which had been the fiction. Wren had delivered the fiction flawlessly for a weekend. The trouble with acting, they'd learned from the dying, is that you can only sustain a role if some part of you wants to live in it.

"So," Wren said, watching the hedgerows flick past, the sea appearing and disappearing between them like a thought you can't hold. "Invoicing. Do you want an itemized bill? I could break it down. Aunt interrogation: four hours. Lemon cake testimony: one hour, pro bono. Hand-holding under table: billed at—"

"Stop." Emrys's voice was quiet. Not angry. Worse than angry—careful, the voice of someone handling glass. "Please."

Wren stopped. The car hummed. The sea came back, then went.

"Okay," Wren said. "No invoice. It was—"

"Don't say it was fun."

"It was fun."

Emrys's jaw tightened. "It was a transaction. You did a job. You did it—" a breath, "—extraordinarily well. My family adores you. My mother will ask about you at Christmas. And on Monday I go back to the workshop and you go back to—" they gestured vaguely, a conductor's hand cutting off a phrase, "—your life. And none of it has to mean anything. That was the deal. I wrote the deal."

"You wrote the deal on a napkin," Wren said. "In the rain. While shaking."

"I was not shaking."

"You were shaking. I could feel it when we shook on it." Wren turned in their seat to look at them properly—Emrys's profile against the grey water, the fine tension in their throat, the pulse ticking there a little fast, a little wrong, and Wren's chest did the thing it had been doing all weekend, the thing they had no billing code for. "Emrys. Look. We can do this however you want. We can shake hands at the platform like colleagues. We can say 'nice working with you.' I'll say it in a way you'll believe, because I'm good at that, you said so yourself. But I'm not going to be the one who pretends the weekend was nothing, because it wasn't nothing, and we both—"

"Platform's here," Emrys said, and pulled into the station.

The station was small and salt-scoured, the kind of place where the timetable is a single board and the wind gets into everything. The last Sunday train was already announced in a flat recorded voice. Wren got out, took their bag from the back seat, and stood on the platform in the cold while Emrys stood by the car, and the distance between them was eight feet and two years of practiced solitude and one napkin's worth of rules, and it might as well have been the width of the stage where Emrys had fallen, or the length of a hospice corridor Wren had walked too many times.

"So," Wren said. "Nice working with you."

"Nice working with you," Emrys said, and their voice held, and Wren hated them a little for it, and loved them a great deal more, and said neither.

They shook hands. Wren's hand was shaking. Emrys's wasn't, which meant Emrys had practiced this, which meant it had cost them something, which meant—

The train came. Wren boarded. Found a seat by the window because they were a sentimentalist and a fool. And as the platform began to slide away, they pressed two fingers to the glass—not a wave, not a gesture anyone would read, just a pianist's fingers resting on a surface the way they rested on keys before the first note—and watched Emrys Almeida stand alone on the emptying platform with their hands in their pockets, watching back, until the curve of the track took the station, and the sea, and all of it, away.

Then Wren took out the napkin—the rules, which they had stolen from Emrys's jacket on Friday night while Emrys slept, a theft they did not regret—and unfolded it on their knee, and read it one more time, and did not throw it away.

* * *

**Emrys**

The platform took eleven minutes to empty.

Emrys counted. It was the kind of thing they did now, since the collapse—counting things, measuring things, keeping the world in numbers because numbers didn't flutter or skip or fail without warning. Eleven minutes for the last passengers to disperse, for the tea kiosk to close its shutter, for the wind to take the newspaper someone had left on the bench and carry it out over the rails like a slow grey bird.

They stood there the whole time. They did not decide to. Their body simply declined to perform the action labeled *leave*, and Emrys had learned, in the hard curriculum of the last two years, that some instructions the body will not take.

*It was a transaction,* they told themselves. *You paid them. They performed. The performance is over.*

But the heart—the treacherous, undisciplined, untrainable heart—kept replaying the weekend in fragments, against their will, like a phrase of music stuck in the ear. Wren's laugh in the kitchen at two in the morning, both of them unable to sleep, stealing biscuits. Wren's hand under the table. Wren at dessert, saying the true thing by accident, and the room going soft, and Emrys's own eyes going soft, and the specific terror of being seen and the specific, worse hunger for it.

They thought about the way Wren had said goodbye. *Nice working with you.* Perfect. Convincing. Emrys had taught them that line, practically, in the car, in the script of the thing, and Wren had delivered it flawlessly, and it had been the worst moment of the weekend, worse than the handshake, worse than the father's handshake, because it had worked. Because Emrys had built a fiction so well that it had closed over the true thing like water over a stone.

The platform was empty now. The rails ran off into the grey toward the town where Wren played piano for people who were dying, and Emrys stood there with their hands in their pockets and their pulse doing something irregular that they noted, clinically, and ignored, and they understood with terrible clarity that they had just done the thing they were best at in all the world.

They had performed. They had performed the role of a person for whom nothing had happened, and they had performed it to a full house, and the house had believed it, and the silence after the final note was theirs alone.

Emrys got in the car. Drove home with the windows down in the cold. Unpacked nothing. Sat in the workshop among the cellos in their various states of undress—ribs open, bellies off, waiting to be made whole again by patient hands—and picked up the napkin rules, which were still in their jacket, which meant Wren had not taken them, which meant—

Which meant Wren had left them. On purpose. A thing left behind is a reason to return, or an accusation, or both.

Emrys sat down at the workbench and looked at the napkin for a long time. Then they smoothed it flat, and pinned it above the bench, next to the tuning charts, where they would have to see it every day.

*Rule one: no feelings.*

The workshop was quiet. Outside, far off, too far to hear but present anyway, the way a note is present after it stops, the last train went north.

Emrys reached for a client's cello, and their hands, which could restore any instrument put in front of them, were not steady, and they did not play, and they did not cry, and the evening came down over the coast like a lid.

On Monday, they told themselves, they would invoice nothing, call no one, and forget the whole weekend, because that was the deal.

On Monday, the mail arrived at eleven, and among the bills was a letter, handwritten, on paper that smelled faintly of piano felt and rain.

The postmark was Wren's town.

Emrys did not open it. They set it on the workbench, face down, next to the napkin, and walked away, and got four steps before they turned around and came back.
