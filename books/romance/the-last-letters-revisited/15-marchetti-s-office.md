## 15. Marchetti's Office

The clinic sat on the fourth floor of a building that had once been a bank, and it still smelled faintly of money—polished marble in the lobby, brass fittings gone soft with age, an elevator that hummed upward like a held breath. Emrys stood outside the doors of the Hartwell Cardiac Institute for eleven minutes before going in. They counted the minutes because counting was what they did instead of panicking. Sixty seconds at a time, sixty times over, until the fear had been measured into something with edges.

*You're here for a consultation,* they told themselves. *That's all this is. A second opinion. People get second opinions every day.*

The lie fit poorly, like a coat tailored for someone else's shoulders.

They had dressed carefully—not too well, not too poorly. A gray sweater Wren had once said made them look "like autumn decided to be a person." Dark trousers. The canvas bag over one shoulder contained a folder of medical records they had photocopied twice: once to study, once to leave in a locker at the train station in case things went wrong. They had no clear idea of what *wrong* would look like. Only that it was possible, and that possibility deserved contingency.

Inside, the reception area was quiet in the way expensive places are quiet—sound absorbed rather than absent. A water feature trickled behind glass. A woman with silver-streaked hair sat behind the desk, typing with two fingers, and when Emrys gave their name she smiled the smile of someone who processed forty names a day and remembered none.

"Dr. Marchetti will see you shortly. New patient intake?"

"That's right."

"Any referrals?"

"A friend recommended her." This was even true; the friend was dead, but Dr. Okafor had spoken warmly of Marchetti's surgical reputation before Emrys had learned what lived underneath reputations like sediment under clear water.

They were led down a corridor lined with framed certificates. Emrys walked slowly enough to read them. *Ilse Marchetti, MD, PhD.* Fellowships in Zurich and Boston. Awards for innovation in valve reconstruction. And there—one frame slightly crooked, as if hung in haste or never adjusted after a deep clean—a photograph of Marchetti at perhaps forty, standing beside a patient in a hospital bed, both of them smiling, the patient holding a violin.

Emrys looked away from it the way you look away from a car crash you can't help seeing.

* * *

The consultation room was small and warm, with a window that looked out on a parking structure. There was an exam table draped in paper, a desk, two chairs arranged at a conversational angle—the geometry of reassurance. Everything in medicine, Emrys had learned, was designed to make you feel that nothing bad could happen here, which was either kindness or camouflage depending on the day.

Marchetti entered without knocking.

She was smaller than Emrys remembered. Memory had inflated her to operatic proportions—the tall figure leaning over the operating notes, the voice that had said *we'll have you back on stage within the year* with such certainty. In person she was a compact woman in her late fifties with reading glasses pushed up into iron-gray hair and the posture of someone who had spent decades bending over open chests. Her hands were remarkable. Long-boned, steady, the hands of a person who had never once trembled at the wrong moment.

"Emrys Almeida." She said the name before Emrys could offer it, and the room changed temperature.

There it was. Not surprise exactly—something more controlled than surprise, a flicker behind the eyes like a curtain moving in a closed house. She knew. Of course she knew. She had operated on them; she had read the file; she had, if the discrepancies were true, rewritten parts of it afterward. And now the patient whose heart she had broken stood in her consultation room wearing a gray sweater and a stranger's name on the appointment book.

"You've done your homework," Emrys said. "Most doctors don't remember patients from two years ago."

"I remember the ones who matter." She extended her hand. Her grip was cool and dry and perfectly calibrated—not firm enough to dominate, not soft enough to seem uncertain. "Though I confess I expected this visit sooner."

Emrys sat. Their own hands, they noticed distantly, were folded in their lap with the deliberate stillness of someone preventing them from doing anything else. "Expected?"

"You were a cellist. Cellists are romantics about their bodies. When something goes wrong, they come back wanting a narrative." She settled into the chair opposite, crossing her ankles. "So. Tell me why you're really here."

*Tell me why you're really here.* It was almost generous, the opening she offered—a door left ajar, as if to say *we can do this honestly or we can do this politely, your choice.* Emrys felt the old reflex rise, the trained instinct of a lifetime spent being the family's careful disappointment: smooth it over, smile, retreat.

But they had spent three weeks with these files. Three weeks of nights in the workshop with the space heater ticking and Wren's letters in a drawer and photocopies fanned out like tarot cards. They knew what a falsified chart looked like. They knew that the operative report dated the discovery of the torn annulus forty minutes later than the anesthesia record suggested. They knew that a note about intraoperative complications—an entire paragraph—had been added in different ink, different pressure, a hand writing faster than a hand writes when remembering and slower than a hand writes when inventing.

"I'm here," Emrys said, "because my heart is failing again. And I wanted to ask the surgeon who repaired it why the repair failed."

Something moved through Marchetti's face then—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. Not guilt. Guilt would have been easier. It was closer to recognition, the look of a person hearing a sound they'd hoped never to hear again.

"The repair didn't fail," she said. "Degeneration occurred. These are different things."

"Are they?" Emrys opened the canvas bag and drew out the folder—not the copies, the originals they'd requested from records months ago, annotated in pencil so fine it was nearly invisible. "Because I've been comparing documents. The anesthesia record says the tear was identified at 11:40. Your operative report says 12:20. Forty minutes is a long time in cardiac surgery, Dr. Marchetti. That's the difference between a complication and a catastrophe."

The silence that followed had texture. Emrys could feel it against the skin like wool.

Marchetti removed her glasses. It was a small gesture, unhurried, and Emrys understood suddenly that everything this woman did was unhurried—that speed was for people who still believed events could outrun consequences. She polished the lenses on her sleeve, replaced them, and looked at Emrys with an expression of clinical interest that did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

"You've been busy," she said.

"I've been dying," Emrys corrected quietly. "Slowly, and on schedule, apparently. Busy came afterward."

"And what is it you want, Mr.—" she paused, delicately, "—Almeida? An apology? Those are worth less than the paper they're printed on. A settlement? I don't handle those matters personally. Revenge?" She tilted her head. "I've read enough charts to know how hearts actually break. Very few of them break from malice. Most break from arithmetic. Case loads. Fatigue. Systems that reward speed and punish doubt. You want a villain, and what you'll find if you dig far enough is a tired woman with excellent hands and a scheduling error."

"Two other patients," Emrys said.

The words dropped into the room like stones into still water.

"Kestrel Voss. Died fourteen months post-op, aortic insufficiency, listed as idiopathic progression. And Daniel Reyes-Okafor—" they caught themselves on the name, the coincidence of it landing strangely even now, "—left hemiparetic after his 'routine' mitral repair. Both operated on by you. Both with the same pattern in their files. Complications noted late. Notes amended. Ink that doesn't match its own dates."

For the first time, Marchetti went still in a way that wasn't performed. Emrys watched it happen—the fractional drop of her chin, the way her thumb pressed white against the armrest—and understood that beneath the polish, beneath the Zurich fellowships and the awards and the tired-woman-with-excellent-hands speech, there was fear. Real fear. The kind that lives in the body and doesn't consult the face first.

"You should be careful," she said softly, "with other people's medical records. There are laws."

"There are," Emrys agreed. "I've been reading those too."

"This becomes harassment, I'll have no choice but to involve counsel."

"Yes," Emrys said. "You will. That's rather the point."

They stood. Their legs were unsteady—not from fear, or not only from fear, but from the sheer metabolic cost of holding a human heart steady while it hammered against a wall it couldn't escape. Somewhere under the sternum, the failing repair fluttered its broken-winged flutter, and Emrys breathed through it the way they'd taught themselves, slow and low, four counts in, six counts out, a rhythm borrowed from music and repurposed for survival.

At the door, they turned back. Marchetti hadn't risen. She sat in the warm little room with its reassuring geometry, hands folded, watching them with that smile that stopped at the border of her mouth like a tide that had forgotten how to come in.

"For what it's worth," she said, "your case was difficult. Difficult cases have difficult outcomes. If you take this path, you'll lose years of your life to it. Years you may not have many of, given your current function." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "Is that how you want to spend whatever time remains?"

It was cruel, and it was expertly aimed, and it found its target—Emrys felt it sink in below the ribs where all the old fears lived, the fear of wasted time, of a life spent in waiting rooms and courtrooms instead of workshops and concert halls.

But something had changed in the last year. Something with warm brown eyes and a habit of humming show tunes while making soup, something that had missed a train on purpose once and stayed.

"I used to think time was something you saved," Emrys said. "Like money. Like breath." They pulled the strap of the canvas bag higher on their shoulder. "Then I met someone who plays piano for people who are running out of it, and she taught me that time isn't saved. It's spent. The only question is what you buy with it."

They let that sit for a moment.

"So no, Doctor. I don't want an apology. I don't want revenge. I want the truth in a room where it has consequences. And I want you to know that I know." They opened the door. "Thank you for the consultation. I won't be needing a follow-up."

* * *

The corridor seemed longer on the way out. The certificates watched from their frames. Emrys walked past them with their pulse loud in their ears, past the receptionist who wished them a good afternoon with genuine warmth, past the water feature trickling its indifferent music, into the elevator that hummed downward like an exhale.

In the lobby, they stopped at the marble counter and pressed both palms flat against the cold stone until the shaking stopped. It took longer than they would have liked.

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold and thin, that particular coastal light that made even parking structures look briefly beautiful. Emrys walked four blocks without deciding to, then found themselves at the train station, at the locker, retrieving the second set of photocopies as planned. Their hands were steadier now. Fear, they were learning, didn't leave. It just became fuel if you fed it purpose.

Their phone buzzed. Wren.

*How did it go? Call me when you're free. Also I found a recording of the Brahms we talked about and I have OPINIONS.*

Emrys stared at the screen. Here was the fork in the road, laid out plain: tell her everything, and watch worry settle into her face the way damp settles into walls—or say *fine, routine consultation, second opinion stuff,* and carry this alone, the way they carried everything, the way carrying alone had very nearly killed them once already.

They thought of Chapter One of their own private philosophy: the heart as a muscle, discipline over feeling. They thought of how thoroughly that philosophy had failed, and how much better the newer one worked: *tell the truth to the people who stay.*

They dialed.

"It went," they said, when Wren picked up. "She knew who I was before I said my name. And Wren—she threatened me. Politely. With lawyers and with—" their voice caught, steadied, "—with my own mortality. She asked if this was how I wanted to spend whatever time I have left."

A silence on the line. Then Wren, quiet and fierce: "And what did you tell her?"

Emrys leaned against the station wall, watching the departure board flicker its destinations, feeling the fear and the resolve sitting together in their chest like two passengers who'd discovered they were bound for the same place.

"I told her the truth has consequences," they said. "And then I called you. Because apparently I'm learning."

On the other end, he heard Wren exhale—relief and pride braided into a single sound—and somewhere across town, in an office smelling of antiseptic and old ambition, Ilse Marchetti was reaching for her phone as well.

The board meeting was in five weeks. The race, Emrys understood with sudden clarity, had already begun.
