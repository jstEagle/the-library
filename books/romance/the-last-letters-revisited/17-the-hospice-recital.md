## 17. The Hospice Recital

*Wren*

The invitation happened the way most true things happen with Emrys—sideways, in a doorway, disguised as something smaller than it was.

They had come to the hospice on a Thursday, ostensibly to pick Wren up for dinner, and had gotten trapped. That was the only word for it. Sister Agnes, who ran the place with the gentle tyranny of a woman who had outlived three bishops, had spotted Emrys in the corridor—the tall figure, the careful hands, the way they stood slightly apart from the world as if waiting to be asked to leave—and had simply decided.

"You're the cellist," she said. Not a question. Wren had told her, of course; Wren told her everything, the way you tell a person who has heard every kind of confession and filed them all under *human*. "Wren plays for us on Fridays. But we haven't had strings." She said it the way you might say *we haven't had rain*, an absence noted without complaint.

Emrys looked at Wren over her head. Their face did the thing it did—closed like a shop pulling down its shutters—but slower now, these days, as if the shutters had grown heavy from disuse.

"I don't perform anymore," Emrys said.

"No one here performs," Sister Agnes said. "We just make sound until it's time to stop."

And then she walked away, because she was a woman who understood that the last word should be left alone in a room to do its work.

---

That night, over the cold noodles neither of them had bothered to heat, Wren watched Emrys turn the moment over like a stone found on a beach, testing its weight.

"She's right, you know," Wren said. "About the sound thing. It's not a performance. Half the time I'm playing and nobody's listening, or everybody's listening and nobody's *here*, and it doesn't matter either way. The music isn't for them exactly. It's just—company."

"Company," Emrys repeated, in the voice they used when a word had teeth.

"For people who can't leave the room anymore. For people whose rooms are getting smaller." Wren set down their fork. "I'm not going to push you. You know I won't. But I'll say this once and then I'll let it lie: there are eleven people on my floor right now, and every single one of them would rather hear an ugly cello played by a frightened man than hear nothing at all."

Emrys's jaw tightened. "I'm not frightened."

"Wren Halloway," Emrys said, after a long time, "you are the worst liar I have ever met, which is remarkable in someone who agreed to be my fake boyfriend."

"I was terrible at that too. I fell in love inside of four days. No discipline whatsoever."

The joke landed the way their jokes did now—softly, in the space between them that had stopped being a gap and started being a home. Emrys looked down at their hands. Wren knew those hands. Knew how they hovered over things they wanted to touch: instruments, doorframes, Wren's face. Knew that two years ago those hands had been famous, and that fame had died on a stage in front of nine hundred people along with, briefly, the body attached to them.

"Friday," Emrys said finally. "One hour. And if my hands shake—"

"They'll shake. That's what hands do when they're alive."

---

*Emrys*

The hospice smelled of lavender and something underneath the lavender—antiseptic, maybe, or just the honest smell of bodies being kept comfortable at the end of keeping. Emrys noticed smells the way other people noticed faces. It was a restorer's habit: every room had a finish, a varnish, a history soaked into its grain.

Wren had set up a chair for them in the common room, near the window where the afternoon came in low and gold through glass that needed cleaning. There was no stage. That was the first mercy. A stage was a place where you could fall and be seen falling. This was just a corner of a carpet, a borrowed chair, and a cello that belonged to the hospice itself—a student instrument, plywood-bellied, its bridge cut too high by some well-meaning volunteer, its strings old enough to have opinions.

Emrys tuned it anyway. Tuning was prayer for people who didn't pray.

The residents gathered the way weather gathers—not all at once but by degrees, a wheelchair here, a woman in a quilted robe there, a man named Harold who announced he'd come for the free entertainment and then sat down in the front row and closed his eyes before a note was played.

Eleven people, Wren had said. Emrys counted twelve, counting Wren, who sat against the wall with their hands folded, not watching, giving Emrys the gift of an unobserved back.

Emrys's heart did its flutter—that small wrong bird beating against the wrong side of the cage—and Emrys breathed through it the way Dr. Okafor had taught them, four counts in, six counts out, and thought: *if it stops here, at least it stops somewhere honest.*

Then they began to play.

Not the concerto. Never the concerto, not yet, maybe not ever. They played Bach—the fifth suite, the sarabande, that slow dark thread of a melody that is less a song than a confession. The cheap cello fought them. Its tone was narrow, nasal, a voice with a cold. Emrys adjusted, softened the bow pressure, let the instrument be what it was instead of punishing it for what it wasn't. This, at least, the restoration work had kept alive in their hands: the patience to meet a thing where it stood.

And something happened that Emrys had not anticipated, though perhaps should have.

It was easy.

Playing for the dying was easier than playing for the living, and Emrys understood why within eight bars. The living listened with expectations. The living remembered the reviews, held you up against your own history, measured the present performance against the ghost of the young cellist you used to be. The living applauded, and applause was a bill presented for payment.

But these listeners asked for nothing. Harold with his eyes closed. The woman in the quilted robe—her name was Marguerite, Wren had said, she'd been a music teacher for forty years—who mouthed the phrasing as Emrys played it, following like a swimmer following a current. A man near the window who might have been asleep or might have been past the reach of waking, his breathing slow and tidal.

They were not an audience. They were witnesses. And a witness asks only that you tell the truth.

Emrys played the sarabande twice through, then moved without deciding to into the sixth suite's gavottes, lighter, almost dancing, and heard Marguerite laugh—a dry leaf of a sound—and felt something unlock in their chest that was not the heart's electrical misfiring but something older and less dangerous: joy, arriving unannounced, like a friend who still has a key.

When they finished, nobody clapped. Sister Agnes had been right about that too. There was just a silence, full rather than empty, the silence of a room that has been given something and is holding it carefully.

Except Harold opened his eyes.

Harold leaned forward in his chair with the effortful deliberateness of a man negotiating with his own body, and looked at Emrys with eyes gone pale as watered milk, and said, in a voice like gravel settling:

"You play like someone apologizing."

The words went through Emrys like a needle finding the exact nerve.

"I—" Emrys started, and stopped. Around the room, the others had begun to drift, wheels whispering on carpet, Sister Agnes already steering Marguerite toward tea. But Harold stayed, and Wren stayed against the wall, very still, and the window light had gone the color of honey left too long in the jar.

"My father used to say that about my brother," Harold went on, unprompted, unbothered, a man with nothing left to lose by honesty. "He'd play piano in the parlor and my father would say, *you play like you're sorry to be taking up the room.* Forty years I remembered that." He settled back. "Whatever it is you're sorry for—I'd stop. Nobody in this room is keeping score. We're all just glad for the company."

Then he raised one knuckled hand—not quite a wave, more a benediction—and let himself be wheeled away by a nurse with kind forearms, and Emrys sat alone in the gold light with a cheap cello in their lap and the truth sitting across from them like a visitor who intended to stay the night.

*You play like someone apologizing.*

For existing. For surviving when the music hadn't. For taking up the room.

Wren crossed the carpet and knelt beside the chair, and Emrys expected comfort, expected the warm-mouthed reassurance that was Wren's native language. Instead Wren just said, quietly:

"He's right, isn't he?"

"Yes," Emrys said. It came out steady, which surprised them both. "Yes. I think I've been apologizing since the stage. Every scale, every restored scroll, every time I touch an instrument. Like I owe music an apology for what my body did to us both."

Wren took their hand—the bow hand, still faintly trembling—and pressed it between both of theirs.

"So stop," Wren said. "Not forever. Just—start with this room. Start with people who never wanted anything from you except the sound."

Outside the window, the light failed by degrees, gold to copper to ash. Down the hall, someone had turned on a radio, and a voice sang something old and half-remembered, and Emrys sat in the hospice that smelled of lavender and endings and felt, for the first time in two years, witnessed—not reviewed, not diagnosed, not pitied, but *seen*, the way you see a tree or a tide or any other large slow thing that has survived its own weather.

"Next Friday?" Wren asked.

Emrys looked at the doorway where Harold had gone. At the cello, ugly and honest in their arms.

"Next Friday," they said.

---

*Wren*

Wren drove them home along the coast road with the windows cracked, salt air moving through the car like a third passenger. Emrys was quiet, but it was a different quiet than usual—not the locked-door silence Wren had learned to read in October, but something open-ended, a silence that was thinking rather than hiding.

At the workshop, Emrys didn't go straight inside. They stood in the yard looking at the building where forty instruments slept in their cases, and Wren watched them decide something.

"There's a file," Emrys said suddenly, to the dark, to the sea, maybe to Wren. "In my office. Marchetti's charts. I've been—" A breath. "There are things I haven't told you. About why I've been tired. About what I've been doing at night."

Wren's chest went tight and cold, the way it did when a patient's family asked the question they weren't ready to have answered. But they kept their voice level, warm, the voice that had carried them through a hundred deathbeds.

"Okay," Wren said. "Tell me tomorrow. Or tonight. Whenever you're ready. I'm not going anywhere."

Emrys turned, and in the porch light their face was stripped of everything—no performance, no apology, just fear and love in equal measure, finally sharing a room.

"That's what frightens me," Emrys said. "How much I want to believe that."

They went inside together, and behind them the last train sounded its long note across the water, passing, as it always passed, while neither of them moved.
