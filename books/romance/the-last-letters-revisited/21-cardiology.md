## 21. Cardiology

The cardiology wing smelled of hand sanitizer and lilies, which struck Emrys as a strange combination—the antiseptic smell of things being kept at bay, and the funeral-adjacent sweetness of things already being mourned. They sat in the waiting area with their coat folded over their knees like a shield, watching the receptionist's fingers move across the keyboard with the brisk indifference of someone who typed death sentences all day and had long since stopped reading them.

*Almeida, Emrys. 10:40. Dr. Okafor.*

They had told no one about this appointment. Not their mother, who would immediately begin arranging casseroles and phone trees. Not the patient allies from the Marchetti file, who would look at them with that particular softening of the eyes that Emrys had come to dread—the look people gave the dying when they still had breath left to waste on pity. And not Wren.

That last one was the lie that sat heaviest. Wren, who had taught them that secrets were relapses, that hiding was just another word for disappearing. Wren, whose letters arrived in blue ink on heavy cream paper, whose handwriting slanted forward like a person leaning into a conversation. Wren, who had moved a toothbrush into the workshop bathroom three weeks ago without asking permission, without making it an event, as if staying were simply a thing that happened now, the way weather happened.

Emrys had told Wren it was a routine follow-up. A valve check. A formality. The words had come out smooth and easy, and afterward Emrys had stood in front of the bathroom mirror and practiced saying them again, and again, until the lie wore grooves deep enough to walk through blindfolded.

The nurse called their name. Emrys rose, and their heart did its small treacherous flutter—not pain, exactly, but a reminder. A tap on the shoulder from something inside that wanted to be acknowledged.

* * *

The echocardiogram room was cold and dim, lit only by the monitor's glow. Emrys lay on the table with their shirt open and gel cooling on their chest while a technician pressed the transducer against them with small, impersonal firmness. On the screen above, their heart appeared in shades of gray and ghost-blue—a chambered, pulsing thing, working away in the dark of them without any regard for how it looked from outside.

"There's my little overachiever," the technician said cheerfully, and Emrys almost laughed. Almost. It was strange, the intimacy of it—this stranger seeing the exact mechanism of their survival, the machinery that had failed once on a stage in front of four hundred people and been patched by a surgeon who, it turned out, had lied about the patching.

Dr. Okafor came in afterward, a compact woman with silver threading her close-cropped hair and reading glasses pushed up into it. She pulled a stool close and studied the screen for a long time before she spoke, and Emrys knew—had known since the moment she walked in, the way you know weather is turning—that the news was not going to be kind.

"The original repair is failing," she said. No preamble. Emrys appreciated that, in the way you appreciate a clean cut. "The annuloplasty ring has partially dehisced. There's significant regurgitation—blood flowing backward through the valve with every beat. Your ejection fraction is holding, but it's working harder than it should. That's why the fatigue. Why the fluttering. Why you've been waking up short of breath." She glanced at the chart. "You didn't mention the nocturnal dyspnea on your intake form."

"I forgot," Emrys said.

"You didn't forget." Okafor removed her glasses and looked at them directly, and there was something in her face that wasn't pity—it was respect, or its clinical cousin. "You're a musician. I read your file. You have perfect control over what you present to the world. But your body keeps its own score, Mr. Almeida, and it's been keeping it for two years."

She turned the monitor toward them. "Here. See this?" Her stylus traced a pale seam along the ring's edge. "This is where it's pulling away from the tissue. It's not sudden. It's been loosening for months. Possibly since the infection last winter."

"Can it be fixed?"

"Yes. But not the same way. The first repair was a salvage job—we both know that, given what we now know about how it was done. This time it would be a full replacement. Mechanical or bioprosthetic. Open chest. Six to eight hours on bypass." She paused. "And I won't lie to you: because of the scarring from the original surgery, because of the dehiscence pattern, this is a higher-risk procedure than a first-time replacement. There's a real chance of complications. Bleeding. Stroke. Failure to wean off bypass." She said each outcome plainly, without drama, the way one reads a grocery list. "I'd put your odds at seventy percent uncomplicated recovery. Maybe seventy-five. The rest is—" she spread her hands, "—the rest."

Seventy percent. Emrys turned the number over. It was such a small thing, seventy percent. Less than a B-minus. A grade that got a musician passed over for solos.

"When?"

"If we do it—and I strongly recommend we do, because the regurgitation will only worsen—I'd want to schedule within six weeks. Before the spring. Your heart is compensating now, but compensation is a loan, not a gift. Eventually it comes due."

Emrys nodded. Their hands were perfectly still in their lap. Somewhere beneath the sternum, the traitor muscle went on beating, oblivious, faithful, failing.

"I need copies of everything," Emrys heard themselves say. "All imaging. The report. I'm building a case against the original surgeon, and I'll need documentation of the current damage as evidence of downstream harm."

Okafor raised an eyebrow. "You're the Almeida cellist. The Marchetti case—I've been following it in the journals." Something shifted in her expression, a hardening that looked almost like approval. "Then take care of yourself, Mr. Almeida. Dead plaintiffs make poor witnesses."

It was the kindest terrible thing anyone had said to them in weeks.

* * *

The drive back along the coast took forty minutes, and Emrys spent thirty-eight of them not thinking about it, which is to say thinking about nothing else. The sea was the color of hammered tin. Gulls hung motionless over the breakwater like notes held past the end of a measure. At some point Emrys realized their hands were gripping the wheel at ten and two with the rigidity of a student mid-examination, and made themselves loosen, finger by finger.

The report sat in a manila envelope on the passenger seat. They could feel it the way you feel a sleeping animal—present, warm, requiring nothing, and capable at any moment of waking up and changing everything.

Six weeks. Seventy percent. *The rest.*

What astonished Emrys, turning onto the gravel drive, was not the fear. Fear was an old acquaintance; they had shared a house with it for two years, had learned its footsteps on the stairs. What astonished them was the arithmetic happening underneath the fear, quiet and relentless: *six weeks minus hearing prep minus testimony minus recovery means the surgery comes after the medical board. Good. The timeline holds.* Even now, even with mortality handed a date and a shape, some part of them was still conducting—still insisting that everything fit inside the bar lines, that the downbeat would come where it was supposed to come.

The workshop lights were on. Through the window Emrys could see Wren at the workbench, bent over a client's cello, turning it slowly under the lamp, checking the seam with a fingernail. They had learned to do that—Wren, the pianist, learning the vocabulary of another instrument's body the way they learned everything: gently, thoroughly, with their whole attention. Watching them through glass, Emrys felt the envelope's weight double.

There were two kinds of silence, Emrys had learned. The kind you kept to protect yourself, and the kind you kept to protect someone else. The second kind was heavier. It always had been.

They sat in the car until the engine ticked cool.

* * *

"You're home late." Wren didn't look up from the cello, but their voice did the thing it always did—arrived a half-second before their eyes did, warming the room in advance. "How was the valve check?"

"Routine," Emrys said, and hung their coat on the hook, and the manila envelope went into the drawer beneath the repair invoices, beneath the spare bridge blanks, beneath the box of replacement strings, in the place where things went to be forgotten on purpose.

Wren looked up then. Brown eyes, patient, a smudge of hide glue drying on one knuckle. For a moment—one long, suspended moment—Emrys thought they saw something flicker there, some instrument in Wren tuned to frequencies others couldn't hear, registering a wrong note somewhere in the room.

"Routine," Wren repeated, softly. Not a question. An echo. Testing the resonance of the word the way they'd test a string—plucking it, listening for the false overtone.

"Mm." Emrys crossed to the bench and picked up the client's cello, needing their hands occupied. "The varnish on this Guarneri copy is drying beautifully. Another week and I can start the setup."

Wren watched them for a while. Then they let it go—or appeared to let it go, which with Wren was sometimes the same thing and sometimes very much not. They washed the glue from their hands and said, "I brought bread. The good bakery, the one that opens at five. I stood in line with the fishermen."

"You hate standing in line."

"I hate standing in line alone." They tore the loaf in half and held out a piece, and steam curled up between them like something alive. "Eat. You look pale."

"I'm always pale."

"You're *more* pale. It's a relative pale. I'm tracking it." Wren smiled when they said it, easy and warm, and Emrys ate the bread and smiled back and felt the lie settle into their chest alongside everything else, one more thing the failing muscle had to carry.

That night they lay in bed in the loft above the workshop, a foot apart in the old habit that had never quite broken even after the foot closed to nothing, and Wren fell asleep first, breathing slow and even, one hand open on the sheet between them as if in sleep they still reached. Emrys lay awake doing the arithmetic again. Six weeks. The hearing was in five. Recovery, if there was recovery, would take months of not lifting, not restoring, not playing—which meant that if they were going to play the concerto at all, if they were ever going to stand on that stage again, the surgery had to happen now, in this narrow window, or perhaps not at all.

*Mortality given a date and a shape.* That was what the report was. Not a diagnosis—a schedule. The heart, that undisciplined muscle, finally submitting to a rehearsal calendar.

Emrys slid out of bed, careful of the third stair's creak, and went down to the workshop in the dark. They did not turn on the lamp. By moonlight through the salt-fogged window they opened the drawer and took out the envelope and read the report again, all of it, every clinical phrase, letting the words do their work: *dehiscence, regurgitation, ejection fraction, risk stratification.* Then they took out a pen and wrote on the back of the last page, in the margin, small:

*70%. Tell Wren?*

And beneath it, after a long time, in smaller letters:

*After the hearing. When it can't change anything.*

They put the report back under the strings and stood in the dark among the silent cellos, their hand pressed flat against their sternum, feeling the uneven rhythm underneath like rain against a roof—irregular, insistent, counting down.

Above them, the floorboard sighed. Footsteps, soft, crossing the loft. Pausing at the top of the stairs.

"Emrys?" Wren's voice, sleep-roughened, drifting down into the dark. "What are you doing down there?"

Emrys closed the drawer without a sound.

"Nothing," they called up, steady as a tuning fork. "Go back to sleep."

But they heard Wren cross to the top of the stairs anyway, and stand there, and stay.
