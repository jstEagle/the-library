## 22. Evidence in the Dark

The call came at 4:17 in the afternoon, on a Tuesday so gray the workshop lamps had been burning since noon.

Emrys knew before they answered. They had learned the shape of these calls—the way the phone seemed to hesitate before ringing, as if even it understood the weight of what it carried. This one was from a number they'd come to know by heart: Dana Okafor, the former patient from Leeds whose husband had died eleven months after Marchetti's scalpel went looking for a valve and found something else instead.

"Emrys." Dana's voice was low, careful, the voice of someone who had learned to carry secrets like water without spilling. "We have her."

Emrys set down the purfling knife they'd been holding and pressed their palm flat against the workbench, against the cool grain of unfinished spruce, because for a moment the room tilted.

"Have who?"

"The nurse. Priya Raghavan. She was in the OR for six of the procedures—six, Emrys, including yours. She left St. Aldric's last spring. She's been sitting on this for a year because she was terrified, and I don't blame her, but she watched what happened after your surgery. She saw the charts change hands. She saw them rewritten."

The spruce was smooth under Emrys's hand. They focused on that. Smoothness, coolness, the small honest facts of wood. "Is she willing to testify?"

A pause on the line, long enough that Emrys could hear Dana breathing, could hear behind her the ordinary sounds of a kitchen—a kettle, a radio murmuring, a life continuing while other lives hung suspended on paperwork.

"She's willing to give a sworn statement. She won't do media, she won't do a press conference, but she'll sign. She said—" Dana's voice caught, steadied. "She said she'd been waiting for someone to ask. That's what broke me, Emrys. A year. She was waiting for someone to ask."

Emrys closed their eyes. In the dark behind their lids they saw the operating theater they had never seen—had been unconscious for, had reconstructed only from fragments and nightmares—the green of the drapes, the beep of monitors, a woman in scrubs watching a chart being carried out of the room and coming back different.

"Tell her," Emrys said, and their own voice sounded strange to them, scraped thin, "that I'm asking. Formally. Whatever she needs—lawyer present, notary, whatever makes her safe—we'll make it happen."

"There's more." Papers rustled. "She kept copies. Annotated schedules, medication logs, the post-op notes from your second day—the ones that were amended twice in forty-eight hours. She has the originals in her handwriting, dated. Emrys, she has *your* file. The real one."

The kettle sound in the background reached a whistle and was pulled off the heat. Somewhere in Emrys's chest, beneath the scar tissue and the failing repair and the pills lined up on the bathroom shelf like small pale soldiers, something turned over—not pain, exactly. Recognition. The feeling of a lock that had been rusted shut for years giving way all at once.

"Thursday," Dana said. "She can meet Thursday evening. Her solicitor's office, in town. Can you be there?"

"I'll be there."

"And Emrys?" A gentleness entered Dana's voice that Emrys hadn't earned and couldn't refuse. "Bring Wren. You shouldn't sit in a room alone with the ghost of your own death. Trust me on that one."

* * *

Wren arrived at seven, carrying soup the way other people carried flowers—in both hands, slightly ceremonially, steam curling up past their chin into the cold air of the doorway.

"You look," Wren said, taking in Emrys's face, "like you've been told something enormous."

"I have." Emrys stepped back to let them in. The door—the door that had stayed locked through so many seasons of Emrys's life—swung wide and stayed wide, and Wren passed through it without ceremony now, the way they passed through everything, as if belonging were simply a fact about them rather than an achievement.

They ate at the workbench because the table was buried under paper. That was the truth of the last three weeks: the workshop had become a war office. Client commissions pushed to the corners; in their place, folders. Photocopies. Timelines drawn in colored ink on butcher paper taped to the wall, the dates marching across it like a military campaign—which, Emrys supposed, is what it was. Marchetti's career rendered as a series of incisions, each one annotated.

Wren set down their spoon halfway through the soup. "Tell me."

So Emrys told them. About Priya Raghavan, about the annotated schedules, about the original post-op notes with the amendments circled in red—two revisions in forty-eight hours, the first changing the dosage record, the second changing the *time* of a critical event, shifting it by ninety minutes, enough to move the complication outside the window where protocol required certain interventions. Enough, if anyone ever asked, to make it look like Emrys's heart had failed on its own schedule rather than under supervision.

Wren listened the way they always listened—completely, their whole body oriented toward the speaker like a plant toward light—and when Emrys finished, they were quiet for a long moment.

"Ninety minutes," Wren said finally. "Someone sat down and decided that ninety minutes was the difference between their career and your life. And then they wrote it down. In ink. In a hospital that's supposed to—" They stopped. Pressed their lips together. Emrys watched them choose, deliberately, not to finish the sentence with anger.

"It doesn't feel like winning," Emrys admitted. "I thought it would. When we started finding the others, when Dana called tonight—I thought I'd feel vindicated. Instead I just feel like I'm reading my own autopsy."

"That's because you are, a little." Wren reached across the bench and took Emrys's hand, turning it over, tracing the callus at the base of the fingers where bow and strings had once lived. "But autopsies are how the living learn things. Come on. Show me the timeline again. If we're going to sit across from a solicitor on Thursday, I want every date in my head."

And so they worked.

It went on past ten, past eleven. Wren cleared a space on the big table and they spread the files out in chronological order, and something happened in the doing that Emrys could not have predicted and would never afterward forget: the horror became navigable. Not smaller—never smaller—but *ordered*. Wren had a gift for this, a hospice worker's gift, the ability to stand inside terrible rooms and find where the light switches were. They read aloud in a steady voice. They asked questions Emrys hadn't thought to ask. When Emrys's breath went shallow over a particular page—the anesthesia record, the one with the unexplained gap—they noticed before Emrys did, and put down the papers, and made tea, and didn't say anything about it at all, just stood beside them at the counter until the shaking stopped.

"This one," Wren said much later, holding up a page. "Marchetti's operative report. Listen to the language." They read: *'Procedure completed without incident. Patient stable.'* And then, underneath, in the same hand but different ink, added later: *'Minor intraoperative event, resolved.'*

"Minor intraoperative event," Emrys repeated. "That's my cardiac arrest. That's me dying on the table. She called it a minor event and resolved it into the record like a typo."

"We'll use her words against her." Wren's voice had gone quiet and hard, a register Emrys had heard only rarely, only ever on Emrys's behalf. "Her own reports. Her own timeline. We don't have to accuse her of anything. We just have to lay the pages side by side and let them disagree with each other."

Outside, the wind had come up off the water, worrying at the windows, and the lamp above the workbench threw their two shadows long across the floorboards, overlapping at the shoulders so that for stretches it looked like one person working alone with four hands.

At half past midnight, Wren stood and stretched until their spine crackled, and looked at the wall of butcher paper with its campaign of dates, and said, "Do you know what I keep thinking about? At the hospice, when families go through a loved one's things—there's always a moment where someone finds a document. A bill, a letter, a diagnosis nobody mentioned. And they hold it and they say, *this changes everything*, and it does, and it doesn't. The person is still gone. But knowing the shape of it—" They turned. "Knowing the shape of it lets you grieve something specific instead of something infinite. That's what you're doing. You're making it specific."

Emrys looked at them across the wreckage of paper and lamplight and felt the braid pull tight—justice and tenderness, wound around each other so thoroughly now that pulling either strand free would unravel the whole. There was no version of this fight anymore that did not include Wren's steady voice reading dates aloud. There was no version of Wren that did not include this table, this hour, this shared custody of a grief.

"Thursday," Emrys said. "Dana wants you there. She said I shouldn't sit alone with the ghost of my own death."

"Dana sounds wise." Wren came around the table. "Was she right?"

Emrys considered lying, out of old habit, and found the muscle for it had atrophied. "Yes."

"Then I'll be there." Wren gathered the files into order with quick, capable movements, squaring the edges the way they squared sheet music on a stand. "But first—you're going to sleep. Actual sleep, in an actual bed, not the chair in the corner you pretend I don't know about."

"The chair is ergonomically—"

"The chair is a lie you tell yourself, like the napkin rules were a lie you told me." Wren smiled as they said it, but their eyes didn't soften, and Emrys felt seen straight through, the way the hearing would see them, the way the courtroom would—seen and not destroyed by it. "Come on, cellist. Even instruments get put in their cases at night."

They turned off the lamps together. In the dark, walking toward the stairs, Emrys's hand found Wren's without being sent for, and Wren's fingers closed around theirs with the easy certainty of a chord resolving home.

And on the workbench, in the darkness, the phone lit once with a message from Dana: a photograph of a signed statement, fourteen pages, a name at the bottom in careful, frightened, courageous script.

*Priya Raghavan.*

Below it, a single line of text: **She says there's one more thing. She says Marchetti wasn't acting alone.**
