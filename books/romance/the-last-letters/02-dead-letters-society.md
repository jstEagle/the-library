## 2. Dead Letters Society

The invitation arrived the way all my work arrived: in someone else's envelope.

It was a Tuesday in October, and I was three weeks into ghosting a footballer's autobiography—a book about glory written by a man who had never once considered what glory cost anyone else—when my editor, Beatriz Serra, appeared in my doorway holding two coffees and an expression I'd learned to distrust over eleven years of working together.

"You look like a woman about to ask me for something," I said.

"I'm always about to ask you for something. That's the job." She set one coffee on the stack of manuscripts beside my desk—the footballer's draft on top of a chef's memoir on top of a widow's letters to her dead husband, each one unfinished, each one mine now—and lowered herself onto the only chair in my apartment that didn't have paper on it. "The Corvo Literary Society is doing their winter exchange again."

I kept typing. "Congratulations to them."

"Xanthe."

"Beatriz."

"They want you."

That made me stop. Not because it flattered me—flattery was currency I'd learned to count quickly and spend faster—but because the Corvo Literary Society did not want people. It wanted names, and it wanted them quietly, and it had spent forty years being the kind of institution that literary Lisbon mentioned the way you mention weather: constant, ambient, slightly ominous.

"The society doesn't want me," I said. "The society wants whoever wrote *The Salt Year*. And *The Salt Year* was written by Inês Cardoso, who is currently very happy in Porto and has no idea her sentences have been living a second life."

"Inês Cardoso is on the committee," Beatriz said. "She recommended you personally. Which means she knows, which means everyone knows, which means—" she spread her hands, "—welcome to the club nobody admits exists."

I turned in my chair. Outside the window, Lisbon was doing its October trick, rain arriving sideways off the Tejo, turning the tiled rooftops the color of old pewter. My apartment was one room plus a kitchen that had never cooked anything but coffee, and every surface carried the weight of other people's stories: boxed drafts, annotated galleys, a shelf of published books whose spines bore names that were not mine. Eleven of them. Twelve, if you counted the cookbook, which I preferred not to.

"What does the exchange actually involve?" I asked, because Beatriz had that patient look of a woman prepared to sit through an entire winter.

"Every year, the society pairs its members anonymously. You write letters. Real letters, by hand, posted through the actual mail—they're precious about it. You don't give your name. You don't say what you do. You get assigned a number and a post office box in some village or other, and between November and February you correspond with a stranger." She sipped her coffee. "This year's theme is 'the voice beneath the voice.'"

"That's obscene."

"It's perfect. It's research." She leaned forward. "You've been complaining for months that your dialogue is going flat. That everyone in these books sounds like a press release. You said—I'm quoting—'I've forgotten how people talk when they think no one is writing it down.'"

"I said that at three in the morning after a bottle of vinho verde. It wasn't testimony."

"It was diagnosis." She set down the cup. "Here's my thinking. You spend your life ventriloquizing. Footballers, chefs, widows, founders, one very tedious admiral. You take what people tell you and you sand it smooth until it sounds like them on their best day. But you haven't been a person on the page in—" she counted on her fingers with theatrical slowness, "—ever. The exchange gives you a correspondent who doesn't know who you are. No agenda. No advance. No one performing for a ghostwriter because they can smell one. Just a stranger, writing honestly, because they think no one important is reading." She shrugged. "You want authentic voice? Go find out where it lives."

"And then what? I steal it?"

"You learn from it. There's a difference." She stood, brushed biscuit crumbs from her coat, and delivered the killing blow with the mildness of a woman discussing lunch. "Also, the society pays a stipend. Small. But the footballer's second installment isn't due until March, and I know for a fact your mother's clinic raised its rates again in September."

I looked at her. She looked back with the serene confidence of someone who had already won, which was accurate, because Beatriz Serra had been winning arguments against me since 2013 and treated each victory as further evidence of her method.

"When do I have to decide?" I said, which we both understood as surrender.

"By Friday. First letters post the first of November." At the door she paused. "For what it's worth—and I know you'll pretend it isn't worth anything—I think this will be good for you. Not just the prose. You."

"Don't ruin it."

* * *

I decided by Wednesday, which surprised no one, least of all me.

Not because of the stipend—though I did the arithmetic twice, sitting at my kitchen table with the clinic's invoice propped against the fruit bowl, and the arithmetic was not generous. And not entirely because of the research argument, though it was a good argument, the kind of argument that lets a man feel professional about his curiosity.

It was the phrase. *The voice beneath the voice.*

I'd built a career on the voice beneath the voice. That was the whole trade, if you wanted to be honest about it, which I generally avoided being. People came to me with lives too large or too tangled or too wounded to write themselves, and I listened—not just to what they said but to what they circled, what they skipped, what they repeated like a tongue returning to a broken tooth. Then I wrote the book they couldn't. Their name went on the cover. Mine went nowhere, or into the acknowledgments under some anodyne formula—*invaluable editorial assistance*—a phrase designed to be read by no one, which was the point.

People assumed ghostwriters resented the invisibility. Most articles about the profession were built on that assumption: the bitter man behind the curtain, seething while someone else collects the applause. And maybe some of us were like that. But resentment requires wanting the thing you didn't get, and here was the truth I'd stopped saying out loud years ago: I didn't want the applause. I had wanted it once—badly, at twenty-four, with a byline and a future and a father who told the whole freguesia his son was going to be a writer—and I had learned, in the space of a single ruined summer, exactly what wanting things publicly cost. Invisibility wasn't my punishment. Invisibility was my architecture. You cannot burn down a house that has no address.

So when the society's registration form arrived—cream paper, a fountain-pen-friendly weight, questions typed in a typeface that suggested both tradition and surveillance—I filled it out the way I did everything: carefully, completely, and as someone else.

Name: **Tomás Reis**. A name common enough to be furniture. I gave him an age four years younger than mine, a profession adjacent to mine but unremarkable—*translator*, which had the advantage of explaining any oddity of phrasing without inviting scrutiny—and a hometown I chose by closing my eyes and pointing at a map of the interior: Fundão, cherry country, far from the sea. Tomás Reis would have no father whose name appeared in cooperative records. Tomás Reis had never testified before a tribunal. Tomás Reis was a man with a clean ledger, and I confess I felt something dangerously close to relief as I wrote him into existence, the way you feel taking off a coat that's been weighing on you so long you'd forgotten you were wearing it.

There was one question near the end of the form that stopped me longer than the others:

*What do you hope to find in your correspondent?*

I sat with my pen above the line for a long time. Rain ticked against the window. On the desk, the footballer's manuscript waited with its chapters of triumph-over-adversity, adversity having been, in his account, mostly the inconvenience of fame. Somewhere under that stack was the widow's letters, and somewhere under those was the next contract, the one Beatriz hadn't told me details about yet beyond a single sentence in an email—*big one, memoir, island, good money, complicated subject*—which I'd flagged for December because December was when I'd be free.

What did I hope to find?

A person who didn't perform, I almost wrote. Someone who'd forgotten they were being read. Raw material. Voice.

But the form was anonymous, and anonymity is a strange gift: it makes lying pointless and honesty cheap, and I found my pen moving before I'd authorized it.

*I'd like to be known a little,* I wrote, *by someone who doesn't know what I do. Just a little. Just for practice.*

Then I crossed out *just a little* because it sounded needy, then uncrossed it because crossing it out felt needier, then left it, sealed the envelope, and told myself the whole exercise was craft. Fieldwork. An anthropologist among the honest.

Practice, nothing more.

* * *

The first letter I received came on the ninth of November, forwarded through the society's post office box in a plain brown envelope, my number—**Correspondent 47**—typed on a label in the corner.

I almost didn't open it that night. I had six hundred words of the footballer's chapter twelve to restructure—he'd described a championship match as "a journey," and I was trying to locate the actual human being somewhere inside the sponsorship language—and the letter sat on the corner of my desk for two hours, a small pale rectangle, before I surrendered and slit it open.

The handwriting hit me first. It was not pretty. It was fast and slanted and impatient, letters shouldering into each other like commuters, ink blotted where the pen had been pressed too hard at the turns—the handwriting of someone thinking faster than they could move their hand. I have strong opinions about handwriting; I've forged enough of it to have earned them. This was handwriting that had never once considered being admired.

The letter was one page. It began mid-thought, as if continuing a conversation we'd been having for years.

> **Correspondent 47 —**
>
> They told me you'd write first, and you didn't, so I suppose I'm the one breaking the ice, which figures. I'm told we're supposed to discuss "the voice beneath the voice," which sounds like something a committee invented after too much port, so let's skip it.
>
> Instead I'll tell you something true, since apparently that's the currency here. I live on an island where the winter ferries run three times a week and the wind comes off the Atlantic like it holds a grudge. I am stuck on the first chapter of a book I did not want to write, and today I wrote four hundred words and deleted four hundred and ten, which even accounting for the words I salvaged is a net loss, and I mention this not for sympathy but because you struck me, from your registration card—which the society shares, shamelessly—as someone who would appreciate the mathematics of failure.
>
> Your turn. Tell me something true. Not your name—we're past names, or rather we never got to them, which is better. Something true. I'll know if it isn't.
>
> — **Correspondent 12**

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing up, which I never do.

There was no performance in it. That was the thing Beatriz had promised and I hadn't quite believed: no positioning, no charm offensive, no awareness of an audience worth impressing. Correspondent 12 had written the way people talk at two in the morning to someone they'll never see again—sharp, a little defensive, funny in a way that was clearly accidental and clearly real. *I'll know if it isn't.* As if honesty were a language she could hear accents in.

She. I'd decided she, arbitrarily, from the handwriting's impatience, and I noted the arbitrariness and did it anyway.

I sat back down and pulled a fresh sheet toward me, and here is where I should record, for accuracy's sake, that I hesitated. That some part of me—the part that had survived 2013 intact by learning exactly how much of himself to show and to whom—raised a hand and said, carefully: *you are a professional. This is a laboratory. Do not put anything in the glass you can't take back out.*

And I meant to obey. I composed a first paragraph of pure technique—anecdote, deflection, a wry line about the weather in Lisbon—and it was fine, it was competent, it was exactly the kind of letter a man writes when he is practicing rather than corresponding.

Then I looked at her line again—*tell me something true, I'll know if it isn't*—and I thought about the last time anyone had demanded truth from me, and what the truth had done when I finally handed it over, and how since then I had dealt exclusively in other people's truths, laundered through my hands until they came out smelling like somebody else's courage.

I tore up the competent paragraph. Wrote instead, quickly, before the caution could catch up:

> **Correspondent 12 —**
>
> Something true, then. I make my living listening to people tell their lives and writing them down better than they could, and none of the books have my name on them, and I have told myself for eleven years that this is a choice I made freely rather than a wound I dressed as one. Some mornings I believe it.
>
> There. Now you know the worst thing about me, or at least the most embarrassing. Your turn again. And for the record, four hundred deleted against four hundred ten written isn't failure—it's erosion, and erosion is how coastlines get interesting.
>
> — **47**

I read it over once. Every instinct I had honed across eleven books screamed at me: *too much, too fast, too true—you've shown the mechanism, you've shown the wound, pull it back.* And underneath the scream, quieter, a voice I barely recognized as mine said: *she said she'd know.*

I folded the letter, addressed it to the society's forwarding box, and walked it to the post office in the rain rather than leaving it for morning pickup, because I had learned the hard way that unsent things have a way of becoming burned things, and I wasn't ready to burn this one.

On the walk home, Lisbon gleaming wet and orange under the streetlamps, I caught myself composing her reply before it existed—imagining her laugh, her irritation, the exact angle of her pen—and I stopped in the middle of the Rua da Prata and made myself say it aloud, quietly, like a man reciting terms of a contract:

"Practice. Nothing more."

A passerby gave me the wide berth Lisbon reserves for men who talk to themselves, and I couldn't blame her. I turned up my collar and walked on, telling myself the lie with such fluency that for nearly a block I believed it.

Three days later, her answer arrived, and the first line undid a week of discipline in a single stroke:

> *Erosion. God help me, 47, I think you might be the first person on this earth who knows how to talk to me—and you don't even exist.*

I sat down to prove her wrong.
