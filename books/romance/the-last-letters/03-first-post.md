## 3. First Post

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which on Corvo meant the boat came and half the island stood at the harbor pretending they hadn't been waiting for it.

Marisol Ferreira was not among them. She had learned years ago that standing at the harbor waiting for things to arrive was a posture of defeat, and she had sworn off postures of defeat somewhere around the age of nineteen, when her father's cooperative collapsed and the whole village watched to see how the fisherman's daughter would carry it. She carried it, as it turned out, by never being seen to wait for anything again.

So when Inês from the post office climbed the hill to the house with a padded envelope in one hand and gossip in her face, Marisol was outside repainting the gate, which needed no repainting, because a woman with a brush in her hand could not be accused of waiting.

"From Lisbon," Inês said, holding it out like an offering or a summons. "The literary society."

"I know what the society is. I'm the one who signed up."

"You signed up?" Inês's eyebrows performed their entire repertoire. "You? Letters to strangers?"

"It's for the book." The lie came easily now; she'd been telling versions of it since spring. *Research into epistolary voice. A chapter structure built around correspondence.* Her editor had loved the phrase *epistolary voice* so much he'd repeated it back to her twice, as if she'd handed him a gift instead of an excuse. "Writers do research, Inês. It's a known practice."

"And the book itself? Chapter one? Is that also a known practice, or does it still not exist?"

Marisol dipped the brush and painted a slow, deliberate stripe over rust that wasn't there. "Tell João his mother called for him twice this morning and he pretended deafness."

Inês laughed and went, and Marisol stood alone with the envelope, and only then—only when there was no one left to perform composure for—did she notice her own heartbeat doing something unprofessional about it.

She took it inside. She made coffee first, because a woman should not be eager, and then she sat at the kitchen table where the memoir's first chapter lay under a stone paperweight like something dead she hadn't yet buried, and she opened the envelope with a knife, slowly, the way you open anything you've decided not to care about.

*Dear V.,*

*They tell me I'm to write to you all winter, and that by spring we may know each other better than some people know their spouses, which says less about letters than it does about spouses.*

*You should know up front that I don't believe in this exercise. The society calls it "authentic voice," but authenticity is mostly a costume people wear when they want to be forgiven for something. What I believe in is practice: doing a thing until the doing wears the performance away. So consider these letters practice. If something true slips through, blame the postage.*

*A few facts, since the form requires them. I live in a city full of other people's unfinished sentences. I make my living saying things in voices that aren't mine. I have a view of a roof and a chimney that smokes only when it rains, which strikes me as exactly backwards. I am told I'm wry. I suspect this is what people say when they mean guarded but are too polite to say it.*

*I won't ask you questions yet. Questions are how people take your measure without giving you theirs. But I'll leave you this much: today I watched a man on the street argue with a pigeon for a full minute, lose, and pretend he'd been talking on his phone. It occurred to me that most of us are doing some version of that all day long.*

*Yours under protest,*
*R.*

Marisol read it twice.

Then a third time, slower, the way she read manuscripts she intended to take apart, which was her professional habit and her worst one, because some things deserved to be read the way they were written and she had forgotten how.

It was a good letter. That was the irritating part. It was funny without trying too hard, guarded in a way that admitted it was guarded—which was either honesty or a more sophisticated guard—and underneath the wit there ran something dry and tired and real, like a riverbed in August. *I make my living saying things in voices that aren't mine.* She knew that condition. She'd diagnosed it in herself often enough, though in her case the voice that wasn't hers had always been her father's—the way he'd say *the sea gives and the sea keeps accounts*, the way his silences had taught her more grammar than any school.

And: *Questions are how people take your measure without giving you theirs.*

"Well," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the dead first chapter, to the man on the page. "Aren't you careful."

She got out paper. Good paper, because if she was going to answer a stranger who wrote like that, she would not do it on the back of an electricity bill, though she noticed, with some annoyance, that she was already composing before she'd decided to compose, sentences assembling themselves in the back of her mind while she was still telling herself she might not reply at all.

*Dear R.,*

*Under protest accepted. I write under several protests myself, though I'll ration them—one per letter, so neither of us drowns.*

*You're right that authenticity is a costume. You're wrong that practice wears it away. Practice makes the costume fit better. Ask any actor. Ask any liar with a good work ethic.*

*Facts, since the form requires them. I live on an island with one road that matters and four hundred neighbors who know my business before I do. I have a view of the ocean, which sounds like a gift and is sometimes a bill. I am stuck on the first chapter of a book I agreed to write and now resent daily, the way you resent a guest who arrived charming and has stayed nine months.*

*You didn't ask questions, which I notice you're proud of. So here's mine, and you can refuse it: the pigeon man—when he lost, did he feel foolish, or did he feel seen? Because those are different failures, and I've had both this year, and I find I mind the second one more.*

*V.*

She read it over. It was sharper than she meant to be—that was the thing about her, everyone said it, her editor said it gently and her mother had said it not gently at all: *you come out swinging, Mari, even at hands that aren't raised.* There was a line in there about liars with good work ethics that she could soften. There was the accusation folded into *you're proud of*, which she could sand down.

She did none of it. She addressed the envelope, stamped it, and set it by the door with a satisfaction she distrusted immediately, because satisfaction was a door and doors opened onto expectations and expectations were just disappointments with better marketing.

* * *

That night the fog came down off the caldeira and settled over the village like a lid, and Marisol sat at her desk with the lamp on and the first chapter open and fought it the way she'd fought it every night for two months.

*Chapter One. The Water Remembers.*

She'd written the title last spring and hated it by summer. Underneath it sat four hundred words, each one polished to a shine that fooled no one, least of all her. The problem was not craft. The problem was that every sentence she wrote about her father required her to decide what the truth of him was, and the truth of him depended entirely on where you stood—in the village or outside it, in 1998 or now, in his daughter's memory or in the testimony of a young journalist whose name she still kept like a stone in her shoe.

Because that was the wound under everything, the one the memoir was supposed to lance and wouldn't: the cooperative hadn't died of weather or markets alone. It had died in print. A piece in a Lisbon weekly, sourced from inside, quoting a witness who'd grown up on these docks and sold the story of his neighbors' ruin for a byline. Her father had read it at this very table. He hadn't shouted. He'd folded the paper along its creases, smaller and smaller, until it was a square he could hold in one fist, and then he'd gone out to the boat and stayed past dark, and when he came back he said only, *Some people write to be seen. Some people write to be paid. Learn the difference before you pick up a pen.*

She'd been twenty-two. She'd wanted to be a writer. She'd spent fifteen years learning the difference, and what she'd learned was that the difference was mostly luck and timing, and that she herself had taken money to begin a book she didn't want to write, and that the distance between her and the boy with the byline was narrower than she could bear to measure.

The pen-pal exchange had been her editor's idea, actually—a colleague's mention, a flyer, *good for the voice, keeps the prose honest*. She'd signed up telling herself it was research. Now, rereading R.'s letter by lamplight, she caught herself doing something worse than research. She was curious. Curiosity was the one appetite she'd never managed to discipline.

*I make my living saying things in voices that aren't mine.*

What kind of living was that, exactly? Ghostwriting, maybe. Speeches. Advertising copy. Or journalism—the respectable kind, the kind that wore other people's lives like coats. She felt a small cold finger of something move through her at the thought and couldn't name it. Coincidence, probably. The world was full of people who wrote for hire. Lisbon alone must hold thousands.

She went back to the chapter. Wrote a paragraph about the smell of the drying sheds, deleted it, wrote it again worse, deleted it again. At midnight she gave up and did the thing she'd been circling all evening: she took out fresh paper and started a second letter, unsanctioned, ahead of schedule, with no justification she was willing to examine.

*Dear R.,*

*I answered too fast yesterday and I know it. My sharpness is a reflex, like flinching—I do it before I've checked whether there's anything to flinch from. Consider this letter the check.*

*Here is what I didn't say: I liked your letter. Not the wit—wit is cheap, I can buy it by the kilo at any writers' table in Lisbon. I liked the tiredness under it. You write like someone who has been careful for a long time and is tired of the weight of it, and doesn't know yet that putting it down isn't the same as dropping it.*

*About the pigeon man: I've decided he felt seen, and that's why he reached for the phone. Being laughed at is survivable. Being accurately observed is not. Which raises the question I actually want to ask, the one underneath the pigeon: what are you practicing for? People don't drill a muscle without a fight coming. What's yours?*

*You may refuse. Refusals tell me plenty.*

*V.*

She sealed it before she could soften it. Then she sat a while in the lamplight, listening to the fog press against the windows, aware that she had done something tonight she hadn't done in years: she had told a stranger the truth about herself, or near enough, and the sky had not fallen, and the island had not found out, and some locked room in her chest had let in a draft of air that smelled—she searched for the word and resented it—smelled like possibility.

* * *

Winter in Corvo was a season of small logistics: wood, oil, bread, the boat when it came. Marisol moved through hers competently and alone, and told no one—not Inês, not her editor, not the blank page—that she had begun checking the time against the mail.

His second letter came eleven days later, and she read it standing in the post office doorway, in public, breaking her own rule about composure, because the first line undid her:

*V.—*

*You asked what I'm practicing for. I've started three answers and burned them all, which tells you the question landed where questions shouldn't. Here is the fourth, and it's the true one, and I'll thank you not to look at it directly: I'm practicing for the day I have to say something in my own voice and be believed.*

*That's all you get. The rest is postage.*

*But I'll answer your pigeon theory, because you're right, and being right should be rewarded with honesty: yes. Seen, not laughed at. The phone was so he could stop existing for a moment. I've reached for that phone myself more times than I'll admit in writing.*

*Your turn, when you're ready: tell me about the guest who's overstayed. The book. What is it about—not the subject, books are never about their subjects—but the fear underneath it?*

*R.*

Marisol read it three times in the doorway while Inés sorted parcels behind her and the wind worried the loose sign above the door, and by the third reading she had stopped pretending this was research.

*Practicing for the day I have to say something in my own voice and be believed.*

She knew that day. She was scheduled to have it sometime next spring, when the memoir came out with her name on the cover and her father's story in her mouth, and the whole world—four hundred neighbors, a handful of critics, whoever remained of the old cooperative families—would decide whether the woman who wrote it was his daughter or his traitor. She had never once said this out loud. She had barely said it to herself. And here was a stranger in a city of chimneys handing the shape of her fear back to her across three hundred miles of sea, without knowing he was doing it.

That night she wrote him about the book—or around it, close enough to warm her hands on the flame without naming what burned. She told him about a father who folded bad news into smaller and smaller squares. She told him about a first chapter that behaved like a locked door. She did not tell him about the testimony, or the byline, or the name she kept in her shoe. Some stones you didn't show anyone. Some stones were load-bearing.

She mailed it Thursday. On Friday she sat down at the desk, looked at *The Water Remembers*, and wrote six hundred words that were, for the first time in two months, not polished, not safe, and not dead.

She stared at them with suspicion.

Then she got out paper and began, already, composing her reply.
