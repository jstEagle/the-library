## 5. Ink and Salt Air

**Xanthe**

The interviews had a rhythm now, and the rhythm was war.

Every morning at ten I climbed the hill to Marisol Ferreira's house—a stone cottage with blue shutters and a view of the harbor that could make a postcard weep—and every morning she met me at the door like a customs officer.

"Sit."

I sat. The kitchen table between us, a tape recorder that neither of us trusted, and two cups of coffee she poured without asking whether I wanted any. She didn't ask because asking would have been a courtesy, and courtesies were for people who hadn't helped dismantle her father's life.

"Chapter three," I said. "The winter your mother—"

"We're not doing my mother today."

"You said Tuesday was—"

"I said what I said." She opened her notebook anyway, which meant we were doing her mother today, and she hated herself for it, and I pretended not to notice. This was the dance. She gave me the story in pieces small enough to swallow without tasting them, and I assembled the pieces into something true enough to hurt her later, when it was bound and shelved and hers on the cover but mine underneath, the way everything good in my life had always been mine underneath.

"The light," she said suddenly, staring past me at the window. "You'll want to know about the light. Everyone writes about Corvo like it's gray. It isn't gray. It's the color of wet slate before rain, and then the sun comes through and the whole island turns the color of a struck match. My father used to say you could set your watch by the weather changing its mind."

I wrote it down. *Struck match.* It was better than anything I'd have found on my own, and she knew it, and knowing it made her angrier.

"Don't put that in the book," she said.

"It's the best line you've given me all week."

"Then find worse ones." She stood, collected my cup, and carried it to the sink like evidence. "Same time tomorrow. Bring the draft of chapter two. If you've made me sound sentimental, I'm feeding it to the gulls."

At the door I paused. "For what it's worth—the light stays."

She didn't turn around. "Everything stays, Almeida. That's the problem with this island."

* * *

That night, in the rented room above the café where the ceiling sloped so low I had to write sitting on the floor, I took out the letter from my pen-pal—*Rosa*, of the Corvo Literary Society's winter exchange, correspondent number forty-one—and read it again for the fourth time since the ferry brought it over.

*Rosa* wrote like someone arguing with God and enjoying it. Her last letter had taken apart my careful paragraph about loneliness—my careful, ghostwritten, committee-approved paragraph—with the precision of a woman dismantling a bomb.

*"You describe solitude like it's a room you've decorated. Nobody decorates a room they plan to leave. Either you live in your loneliness or you're squatting in it. Which is it?"*

I laughed out loud, alone, at midnight, in a room the size of a coffin. Then I wrote back:

*Rosa —*

*Squatting. Obviously. I keep meaning to renovate and instead I hang more pictures.*

*You asked what I do when a day goes wrong. Here is the honest answer, which I will deny having written: I rewrite other people's days until they go right. It's my trade. I take the mess of a life and sand it into sentences. People pay me to disappear into their voices. I've done it so long I sometimes answer to names that aren't mine.*

*There's a woman here on this island—I told you about her, the one I'm working for—who fights me like the truth owes her money. Today she gave me one perfect sentence about the light and then forbade me to use it. I think she's the bravest coward I've ever met. I think she'd hate hearing that. I think that's why I'm telling you.*

*Do you ever feel like two people? Not metaphorically. Literally. Like the version of you that exists in letters is truer than the one that answers doors?*

*— Your squatting stranger, No. 37*

*I sealed it, walked down to the box by the church in the dark, and posted it before the honesty could cool.*

* * *

**Marisol**

He came at ten o'clock sharp, this ghost they'd sent me, with his careful questions and his careful face, and I spent the hour trying not to notice that his careful face was doing something to me.

Not that. Nothing like that. What it was doing was *familiar*. He listened the way no one had listened to me in years—not waiting for his turn, not composing his rebuttal, just listening, pen moving, as if my father's fishing cooperative and my mother's slow vanishing were the only weather in the world.

I hated him for it. I hated him specifically, personally, with the special fury reserved for people who are good at their jobs.

"Tell me about the letters," he said. "Your father's letters. From the Lisbon years."

"He wasn't in Lisbon."

"No. But he wrote to someone there. There's correspondence in the archive. Someone he trusted with the cooperative's accounts, near the end."

I hadn't known that. Twenty-nine years on this island and a stranger from the mainland knew my father's paper trail better than I did. I looked at him—at Xanthe Almeida, the boy whose testimony had helped gut the cooperative, grown into a man with tired eyes and ink-stained fingers—and felt the old wound open its mouth.

"You've done your homework."

"It's the job."

"Is it." I closed my notebook. "Interview's over, Almeida."

"Marisol—"

"*Over.*"

He gathered his papers with those infuriatingly gentle hands and left, and I stood in my kitchen hating him, and then I did the thing I always did now, the thing that had become as involuntary as breathing: I went to the drawer, took out the letter from correspondent number thirty-seven, and reread it.

My stranger. My anonymous man, somewhere out there in the world, who wrote to me under a false name because I'd insisted on one, and who nevertheless told me things no one had ever told me. Last week he'd written: *"I have made a career of being believed and never known. Some mornings I wake up and can't remember which face is the loaner."*

Tonight I wrote back:

*No. 37 —*

*Two people. Yes. Though in my case it's less two faces than one face held underwater.*

*You asked once if I'd ever been seen clearly. Here is my answer, which I also deny: there is a man on this island writing my life back to me, and he's the enemy—or he was, once, in a way that matters—and he listens like my voice is the only sound worth transcribing. I give him a sentence about the light and I can see him hold it like it's made of glass. And I despise him. I want to be very clear about the despising. It is load-bearing.*

*But some mornings I catch myself saving up things to tell him. Good sentences. Memories I'd forgotten I kept. And I think: what does it mean that the person who understands my father best is the person who helped destroy him?*

*What do you do, No. 37, when the person you should hate is the only one paying attention?*

*— Rosa*

*I posted it before I could soften it. Then I sat with my tea going cold and admitted the thing I'd been not-admitting for weeks: I checked the mail twice a day now. Once for his letters. Once for the other kind, the ones from the stranger, and I could no longer tell you which errand mattered more.*

* * *

**Xanthe**

Her reply arrived on Thursday, and I read it standing in the lane because I couldn't wait the walk home.

*What do you do when the person you should hate is the only one paying attention?*

I read it four times. Then I read it a fifth time and understood, with a sensation like a floor tilting, that I wasn't reading a question. I was reading a mirror.

Because that was Marisol in the kitchen, giving me her father's letters and her mother's silences, hating me with her whole chest while handing me glass after glass after glass. And it was also Rosa—whomever Rosa was—writing to a stranger she couldn't see, telling him truths she wouldn't tell a friend.

Two women. Two rooms. One question.

*You answer it,* I wrote back that night, *by letting yourself be paid attention to. That's all anyone wants, Rosa. To be transcribed accurately by one witness. The tragedy is we usually hand the job to people who weren't listening.*

*As for your ghostwriter: be careful. Men who listen like that usually want something. Sometimes it's just the story. Sometimes it's the storyteller. In my experience the second kind is rarer and considerably more dangerous.*

*I say this as a professional.*

*— No. 37*

* * *

**Marisol**

His reply came Saturday, and I read it in bed with the lamp on, and by the third paragraph I was smiling, which annoyed me so much I had to put the letter facedown on the blanket like it had misbehaved.

*Sometimes it's the storyteller,* he'd written. *In my experience the second kind is rarer and considerably more dangerous.*

Dangerous. As if I were in danger. As if anything on this island could reach me except weather and memory.

And yet.

And yet at ten o'clock Monday morning, when Xanthe Almeida knocked on my door with chapter two folded in his jacket, I caught myself checking—before anger, before suspicion, before the old testimony and the drowned cooperative and twenty years of grievance—I caught myself checking whether he'd slept badly. He had. There was a crease on his cheek from a page, actual paper pressed into actual skin, and something in my chest did a thing I refused to name.

"Chapter two," he said. "I made you sound sentimental in exactly one place. I've marked it. You can feed that page to the gulls."

Against my will, against every vow I'd made at my father's grave, I laughed.

It was a small laugh. Barely a laugh. But he heard it, and something crossed his face—relief, maybe, or fear, or the look of a man who has just realized he's carrying water in both hands and can't set either load down.

"Almeida," I said, because someone had to say something sane. "Why do you do this? Ghostwriting. Hiding inside other people's books."

He was quiet long enough that the clock in the hall got loud.

"Because," he said finally, "it's easier to be honest in someone else's voice."

Then he opened the manuscript, and we worked until dark, and neither of us mentioned the laugh, and I went home and wrote to my stranger about the light, and he went home and wrote to his, and the island turned on its axis, patient as paper, holding both our secrets in the same salt air.

* * *

**Xanthe**

November came down the chimney like smoke.

The storms started on a Tuesday, and by Friday the ferry schedule thinned to a rumor, and Marisol and I fell into a new pattern: interviews running long because neither of us wanted to walk home through the horizontal rain, arguments that had lost their teeth, coffee refilled without either of us tracking the cups.

That Friday she read a page I'd written about her mother's hands—"hands that had gutted ten thousand fish and still folded around a teacup like it might break"—and went silent for so long I thought I'd finally crossed the line.

"That's her," she said at last. "That's exactly her." And then, quieter, almost angry about it: "How do you do that? How do you get inside a life that isn't yours?"

I thought of a sloped-ceilinged room and a letter sealed in the dark. *Easier to be honest in someone else's voice.* I thought of Rosa's last letter, unanswered on my desk, asking me what I was afraid of.

"Practice," I said.

She studied me with those eyes that missed nothing, that had been missing nothing since the day I stepped off the ferry and she recognized me across twenty years.

"You know what I think, Almeida? I think you're the most honest liar I've ever met."

"And what do you think about that?"

"I think," she said, gathering her coat, "that I need to check the mail."

She left. I sat in her kitchen another minute, in the warm ghost of her, and let myself feel the full weight of what I was doing: two letters owed tonight, two voices to sustain, one woman to deceive in stereo.

I walked home through the storm and wrote first to Rosa, quick and light, about nothing—about the weather, about a dog that had followed me for a mile demanding nothing. Safe ground. Then I sat over the second letter for an hour, because Rosa had asked what I was afraid of, and I had promised myself I'd stopped lying to her.

*What am I afraid of?*

*That the two halves of my life are going to touch. That the woman I'm lying to by day and the woman I'm honest with by night are going to compare notes. That when they do, there won't be enough of me left over to survive the arithmetic.*

*— No. 37*

*I posted it in the rain. Somewhere across the island, I suspected, Rosa was posting one too.*

* * *

**Marisol**

I stood at the postbox longer than I needed to, my own letter already swallowed, watching the rain hammer the little red door.

*Compare notes,* my stranger had written. *There won't be enough of me left over to survive the arithmetic.*

Arithmetic. I turned the word over all the way home, and it snagged on something, the way a hook snags on weed. Two relationships. One man each. A ghostwriter who listened like transcription was devotion, and a correspondent who wrote like confession was a craft he'd practiced in the dark.

Both of them honest in the exact same way. Both of them afraid of the same thing.

I told myself it was coincidence. I told myself the world was full of men terrified of being known; it practically manufactured them.

But that night I took out all of No. 37's letters and laid them across my bed in order, and I read them straight through, and somewhere around the fourth one I stopped reading as a woman in love—which I was not, I told the ceiling, I was *not*—and started reading as a writer.

Checking phrasing. Checking rhythm. Checking the particular way a certain mind circled a truth before landing on it.

And at the bottom of the pile, in the earliest letter, I found a sentence I'd read a dozen times without hearing: *I've done it so long I sometimes answer to names that aren't mine.*

I lay in the dark with the letters rising and falling on my chest like tide, and I did not sleep, and I did not yet understand, and the storm outside kept saying the one word storms know how to say.

*Soon,* it said. *Soon. Soon.*
