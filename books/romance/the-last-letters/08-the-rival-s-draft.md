## 8. The Rival's Draft

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone except Marisol, because Tuesdays were when the mail boat from Flores arrived, and the mail boat was the only clock she still trusted.

She was carrying it up from the dock—brown parcel, her publisher's franking, heavy as a brick—when Tomás the postman called out from his doorway that she had a look on her like a woman carrying a bomb.

"Maybe I am," she said, and meant it more than she wanted to.

The parcel contained the first three chapters of her own life, typed and bound, with a cover memo from her editor in Lisbon that said, in the tone of a man trying not to sound excited: *Read this. Then tell me you still want to fire him.*

She had, in fact, wanted to fire him. She had composed the letter twice. She had a drawer full of letters she'd composed and not sent—letters to editors, letters to her father, letters to the stranger she now wrote to every Sunday night—and this one would have joined them, because firing the ghost meant going back to chapter one alone, and chapter one alone had defeated her for a year.

So she made coffee she didn't drink, sat at the kitchen table with the sea going grey and silver outside the window, and opened the manuscript the way you open a door you're afraid is standing on your life.

---

*My mother taught me to read from the tide tables.*

That was the first line. Not the line Marisol had given him. She had given him, in their second interview, a careful anecdote about her grandmother's recipe book, sanitized, decorative, the kind of thing you put in a memoir to avoid the real thing. He had taken it and set it aside and gone digging for something else entirely.

*My mother taught me to read from the tide tables, and she taught me arithmetic from the ledger of the cooperative, because that was the only arithmetic that mattered in our house: how much the boats brought in, how much the middlemen kept, how little was left for the men who did the dying.*

Marisol read the line three times. Then she put the manuscript face-down on the table, stood up, and walked to the window, because her hands were shaking and she did not want to see them shake.

He had gotten it exactly right. Not the facts—she could have corrected the facts. The *weight* of it. The way the ledger had sat on the kitchen table like a fourth person, the way her mother's finger had moved down the columns and her father's silence had moved with it. She had never said any of this out loud. She had said the recipe book. He had heard the ledger underneath.

*She sat me on her knee and made me read the numbers aloud before I could understand what they were numbers of. I thought it was a game. It was a will. She was teaching me the only inheritance the sea ever leaves a fisherman's daughter: the ability to count what's been taken from you.*

She sat back down. She read for two hours without moving, and the coffee went cold, and the light changed, and somewhere in the middle of it she forgot to be angry, which frightened her more than anything else in the manuscript.

---

Because that was the thing. She had gone into this arrangement armed. She remembered the young journalist—she had never learned more than his first name, a boy with a recorder and a notebook who had sat in her father's kitchen in the spring she was nineteen, asking gentle questions, writing gentle notes, and then testifying—testifying, in the inquiry, in the articles, in the court record—that the cooperative's books had been falsified. Her father had not denied it. Her father had not been given the chance to explain that the falsification was survival, that the middlemen had structured the contracts so that honesty meant drowning, that every fisherman on the island cooked his books the way every fisherman salted his fish: because otherwise everything spoiled.

The cooperative had dissolved within the year. Her father had lasted four more, most of them silent, and then the sea had taken him the way the sea takes men who have stopped arguing with it.

And now a stranger was writing her life, and the stranger was good. That was the unbearable part. Not a hack. Not a hired voice smoothing her into something marketable. A writer who understood that a memoir about a fishing island was not about fish.

*The tragedy of the cooperative was never the money. The money was the excuse. The tragedy was that a village full of men who trusted each other with their lives every morning on open water could not trust each other with an accounting. And the ones who broke it first were the ones who had least to break—the small boats, the fathers, the men the sea was already collecting. The system made liars of the poor and called it corruption, and then made orphans of the liars' children and called it justice.*

She read that paragraph standing up. She read it with the manuscript held at arm's length like a document she was cross-examining.

*How does he know,* she thought. *Who told him. Who is he.*

Because here was the suspicion that had been growing in her all along, quietly, like rust: nobody wrote like this from nothing. Nobody understood the interior of a fisherman's kitchen this well without having stood in one. He asked questions in their interviews that no ghostwriter asked—questions about what her mother did with her hands when she was worried, questions about the smell of the ledger paper, questions that were less like research and more like recognition. Once, when she had described the night the inquiry verdict came, he had said, very quietly, "And after the verdict—what did the room do?" and she had answered before she understood that the question had a strange shape, as if he were asking about a room he'd been in.

She had told herself: he's researched other cooperatives. There were dozens. They all died the same way.

She had told herself: it's craft. Good ghosts do this. They find the load-bearing wall of a life and they build against it.

She had told herself a great many things, and then she had read chapter three.

---

Chapter three was the chapter she had refused to give him. She had told him, in their fourth interview, flatly, that she would not discuss the inquiry, that it was off the page, that if the book needed it he should write around it. He had nodded and written nothing in his notebook, which she had taken for agreement and now understood to be something else—patience, or reconnaissance, or the particular discipline of a man who knows a thing can't be avoided and has decided to wait.

Chapter three was titled, simply, *The Testimony.*

Her hands were cold. She noticed this the way you notice weather.

He had written it from the outside—her outside, the daughter's outside, the nineteen-year-old in the kitchen doorway while the men from the mainland took her father's statement in the front room. He had written the sound of the recorder. He had written the way her father had looked at the wall above the journalist's head, at nothing, at the horizon he couldn't see, while the boy with the notebook asked his gentle questions. He had written:

*She stood in the doorway and understood, with the terrible clarity of the young, that testimony is a kind of weather: it forms somewhere far from you, it travels, and it arrives at your house regardless. No one had lied, exactly. Everyone had selected. The truth, disassembled into exhibits, reassembled into a verdict, came out the other side a different animal entirely—and it had her father's name in its teeth.*

She put the manuscript down.

She went outside. She walked down to the water because there was nowhere on Corvo you could walk that wasn't toward water eventually, and she stood on the black stones where the boats used to launch and she let the wind do what the wind does, which is make it impossible to tell crying from weather.

Because the passage was true. It was true the way almost nothing written about her father had ever been true. It was compassionate without being soft. It did not excuse him and it did not condemn him. It put the machinery on the page—the machinery, the system, the weather—and left her father a man inside it, a man with a name and a kitchen and a daughter in a doorway.

No one had ever done that. Not the newspapers, not the inquiry, not the sympathetic profiles that still called it *the Ferreira scandal* as if he were the scandal and not the man. And now a ghost she had hired under protest, a ghost she had tried to fire by letter, a ghost whose face she studied in their interviews for the flicker of the boy from the kitchen—had written her father back into being a person in three chapters flat.

*Which means,* she thought, standing on the stones with the spray coming up cold, *either he is the finest ghost on the Atlantic seaboard, or he knows something.*

---

She went back up and read the rest in one sitting, the way you finish a meal you distrust.

And then, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother had taught her to count what's been taken, she did the accounting.

Gratitude: real. Disarming. She sat with it and let it work on her, this warmth toward a man whose name she kept forgetting to hold on to—Xanthe, an odd name, a name like a key that didn't fit any lock she knew. He had given her father back his dignity in print. He had heard the ledger under the recipe book. Whatever else he was, he had done that, and no one had ever done that, and she would not pretend otherwise.

Suspicion: also real. Growing. Because the questions kept having that strange shape. Because he watched her when she spoke of the inquiry with an attention that went past professional into personal, like a man checking a wound he recognized. Because in their last interview, when she had said *they took everything*, he had closed his eyes for half a second—half a second, she'd have missed it if she hadn't been studying him for exactly that—and when he opened them he had said, "Not everything. They didn't take the ledger." And she had thought: how do you know what they didn't take? How do you know what my mother kept?

And underneath both, a third thing she was slower to name, because naming it made it real: the ghost's prose and the stranger's letters were starting to sound alike.

She had noticed it the week before and dismissed it. The letters came on Sundays, from a man named—she smiled at the absurdity of it—*a man named* nothing, a pseudonym, the society's rules, an invented signature in careful blue ink. The letters were wry and guarded and then, lately, not guarded at all. The last one had contained a passage about his father, about a man who had worked with his hands and been failed by the institutions that were supposed to hold him up, and she had read it at her kitchen table and thought: *this is how the manuscript sounds. This cadence. This way of putting the machinery on the page and leaving the man whole.*

Coincidence, she told herself. Writers sound like writers. The whole island's mail probably sounded like that in winter.

But she had been raised on ledgers, and a ledger does not care what you tell yourself. She got a fresh sheet of paper and wrote at the top of it, in her mother's accounting hand: *Facts.*

Under *Facts* she wrote:

*1. The ghost writes like he was in the kitchen.*
*2. The stranger writes like he was in the kitchen.*
*3. I have never told either of them about the ledger. Both of them know about the ledger.*

She looked at the list for a long time. The fire ticked. Outside, the last light went off the water, and the first star came up over the ridge the way it always did, indifferent and exact.

There was a fourth fact, and she made herself write it, because her mother had taught her that an accountant who flinches is finished:

*4. Whoever he is, I am in danger of loving him twice.*

She sat with that until the stove burned low. Then she took out her Sunday letter—early, this once—and began to write to her stranger, testing him, laying small facts on the page like bait in clear water: a detail about the ledger she had never told anyone, a phrase from her childhood she had never written down. If he was who she was beginning to fear he was, he would use them. They would surface, sooner or later, transformed, in the manuscript, and she would know.

She sealed the letter and set it by the door for the morning boat.

Then she picked the manuscript up again, opened it to the first page, and read the dedication he had included at the front, unsigned, in his own voice, the only part of the book that was not her:

*For the ones who kept the books, and the ones who were kept by them.*

She read it twice. And in the silence of the kitchen, with the sea working the stones below like a slow hand, Marisol Ferreira admitted to the empty room the thing she had been refusing since the first interview:

*Whoever you are, she thought, I hope to God you're not who I think you are. And I hope to God you are.*

The mail boat would come Tuesday. The letter would go out. And three hundred miles away, in a Lisbon apartment stacked with other people's unfinished books, a man who did not exist would open it and know, before he reached the second line, that the ledger had been sprung—that she was counting now, and that every word he wrote was a figure in her columns, and that the account was coming due.

He would write back anyway. She was sure of it. That was the most frightening fact of all.
