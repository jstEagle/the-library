## 10. The Editor's Knife

The call came at seven in the morning, which was how I knew it would be bad. Editors who like you call at noon. Editors who need something from you call early, before you've had coffee, before your defenses are up, the way a vet approaches a dog that bites.

"Xanthe." Duarte's voice was bright in the way that meant trouble. "You're on the island still? Good. Good. Listen—"

I listened. I stood at the window of my rented room above the café and watched fog peel off the harbor while he talked, and somewhere around the word *numbers* I understood what was coming, and somewhere around the word *scandal* I understood it was already done, decided in some Lisbon conference room weeks ago, and this call was not a request but a courtesy extended to a man they considered furniture.

"The cooperative chapter," Duarte said. "We want it as the spine of the book. The father, the collapse, the whole rotten business. Ferreira senior taking the loans, hiding the books, letting his own men drown in debt while he sailed out clean. That's the story. That's the *story*, Xanthe."

"That's not the story," I said. "That's a story. It isn't hers."

"It's the one that sells."

The fog was lifting off the water in long gray ribbons. Somewhere below, Marisol was probably awake too—I'd learned her rhythms by now, the light in her kitchen window at six, the walk down to the dock at seven whether or not there were boats worth looking at. Two months of interviews had taught me things no contract required me to know: that she took her coffee bitter enough to strip paint, that she read standing up, that she still said good morning to old men who hadn't spoken to her since her father died.

"She didn't hide any books," I said. "I've seen the ledgers. Her father kept honest ones. Badly, but honestly."

"Honestly." Duarte laughed, short and dry. "You've been on that island too long. You're starting to sound like them."

Maybe I was. That was the danger of the work nobody warned you about—the ghosting, the living inside another person's grief for a year until their grief fit you like a coat. I'd built a career on wearing other people's coats and giving them back uncreased. But this one had started to fit in places I didn't have.

"Duarte," I said. "The advance."

And there it was. I heard him breathe in, the small satisfied breath of a man who has found the soft place.

"Yes," he said gently. "The advance. Let's talk about the advance."

* * *

Here is what the advance had bought, itemized in the ledger I kept in my head because writing it down felt too much like keeping score against myself:

My mother's hip surgery, the good surgeon, the one who didn't make her wait eleven months. The eleven months she'd waited the first time, back when I was twenty-three and bylines were things that happened to other people, and I'd learned what waiting does to a body—to watch pain calcify into personality, into the way she moved through rooms apologizing for the space she took. The second surgery had happened eight days after the money cleared. Eight days. I had cried in a hospital corridor in Coimbra over an invoice, over the beauty of an invoice, and I was not ashamed of it. Or I told myself I wasn't.

Rita's tuition, final two years. My sister studying marine biology in Porto with the seriousness of someone who intended to save the ocean personally, and who called me every Sunday to tell me about plankton and ask if I was eating. The advance had bought her a degree without loans. It had bought her a future where she never had to learn what I did for a living.

The flat payment that got my mother out of the building with the broken elevator, into ground-floor light.

That was the knife's handle. Duarte held it and knew he held it, and we both knew I knew, and the conversation proceeded with the smoothness of all transactions between people pretending not to threaten each other.

"We're not asking you to invent anything," he said. "We're asking you to frame what's there. The man borrowed heavily. The man's cooperative failed. Two hundred families lost everything. She can grieve him all she wants—we'll give her room for that, we're not monsters—but the reader needs a villain, Xanthe, and the reader has chosen. The documents support us. Your testimony supports us."

There it was. Said plainly at last, after years of both of us circling it the way you circle a hole in the floor.

"My testimony," I repeated.

"You were there. Twenty-six years old, first real assignment, and you filed the piece that broke it open. *The Cooperative That Ate Its Young.* God, what a headline. What a debut." He said it almost fondly, the way you'd praise a man for a war he'd fought before you knew him. "It made you. And now it makes the book. Symmetry, Xanthe. Publishers dream about symmetry."

"I was wrong," I said. "What I wrote. Half of it was wrong. The loan structure came from the bank, not from Ferreira. He was the one fighting the terms. I know that now. I've held the ledgers, Duarte. I've held *his* ledgers."

"And whose fault is that? Yours. You did the research late instead of early. That's not a reason to kill the chapter; it's a reason to be grateful the truth is flexible." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into its reasonable register, the one he used for authors he was managing rather than arguing with. "Look. I understand. You're out there, you're embedded, Stockholm syndrome sets in—it happens to everyone, it's practically a feature. But think about what you're actually proposing. You want to gut the sales engine of this book to protect the reputation of a dead fisherman, based on documents only you have verified, in a market that has already decided what it wants to believe. And in exchange you're willing to forfeit—" he named the number, and the number filled the room like weather "—and possibly your position with us, and frankly, Xanthe, given how thin the wall is between ghostwriting and ghosting entirely, your position anywhere. Because here's the other thing, and I say this as someone who respects you: if this book comes out soft, people will ask why. People will dig. And do you know what they'll find? They'll find that the ghostwriter of the definitive Ferreira memoir is the same Xanthe Almeida who testified—testified, on the record, under his own name—to the cooperative's fraud. They'll find that he then took money to whitewash it. Whether or not that's what you've done—and I believe you that it isn't—that's what it will look like. That's the headline *they'll* write. So really, the scandal chapter isn't just good business. It's your alibi."

I stood very still at the window. Below me, Marisol emerged from her door and stood a moment in the blue early light, looking at the water, and even from three floors up I could see the way she squared herself against the day, shoulders set like a woman boarding a ship she expected to sink.

"Give me a week," I said.

"Five days. We go to layout Monday." And then, softer, almost kind, which was worst of all: "You're a good writer, Xanthe. Best ghost I've worked with. Don't let a dead man's ledger take food off your family's table. Your mother didn't wait eleven months so you could develop a conscience at forty."

He hung up. I stood in the fog-cleared light holding the phone against my chest like something I'd fished out of the sea.

* * *

That afternoon I sat in the archive cottage with the drowned records spread across the table, because if I was going to betray anyone I wanted to do it looking at exactly what I was betraying.

The cottage smelled of salt and mildew and lamp oil. The cooperative's papers had spent thirty years in a flooded storeroom before Marisol's aunt rescued them, and the pages bore the damage like scars—ink bloomed into brown flowers, edges furred soft as moth wings. Joaquim Ferreira's handwriting marched across the ledgers in a strong, unschooled cursive, numbers corrected and re-corrected, margins crowded with notes in a dialect I needed Marisol to translate half the time.

*Paid Ribeiro brothers full share though boat lost. Their pride won't let them ask.*

*Advanced winter oil to Costa widow. No interest. Tell no one, she has sons.*

*Bank meeting. They want the boats as collateral. Refused. Refused again. Must find third way.*

Third way. There was no third way. There never is. The bank took the boats, the paper I'd written at twenty-six had called it fraud, and the town had needed someone to blame, and Joaquim Ferreira—proud, stubborn, drowning quietly in obligations he'd never once recorded as debts to himself—had been the shape the blame fit.

I had gotten it wrong. Not maliciously. Worse than maliciously: lazily. I'd taken the bank's filings at face value because the bank's filings were typed and legible and arrived promptly, and the cooperative's counter-evidence was handwritten by dying men in a village four ferries from anywhere. I'd been twenty-six and hungry for a byline and the story I filed had been true the way a photograph is true—one angle, one light, everything outside the frame deleted.

And the frame had killed him. Not directly—Marisol would say that, had said it, her voice flat as slate: *he died of his heart, not of you*—but the hearing, the testimony, the public unwinding of a life's work while the whole island watched: his heart had known the difference.

Now his daughter's publisher wanted me to do it again. Same story, better typeset. And this time I wouldn't even get a byline. This time I'd commit the betrayal invisibly, professionally, with the craft of a man at the height of his powers, and she would read the chapters and feel understood, because that was the horror of it—I *was* good. If I wrote the villain's version, it would be convincing. She might even doubt her own ledgers. That was the power Duarte was paying for, and I had spent fifteen years renting it out without once asking what it cost the people on the receiving end.

My phone buzzed. Rita.

*Did you eat? Mom says you sound weird on Sundays. Weird-er.*

I looked at the message for a long time. Then I typed: *Eating fine. Big deadline. Love you.* And put the phone face-down, because if she called I would hear the plankton in her voice and the Porto rain behind her and the entire future the advance had purchased, and I knew—with the terrible clarity that arrives sometimes at four in the afternoon in a damp cottage on the edge of the Atlantic—which way the knife would fall.

* * *

Marisol came by at dusk, unannounced, carrying bread wrapped in a cloth, because that was the ritual now: interviews ended, hostility formally suspended, and she fed me the way islanders feed anyone stranded among them, without warmth exactly, but without cruelty either. Something had shifted between us since the storm night neither of us mentioned. Not softened—shifted, like ballast moving in a hull. We could sit in a room together now. We could almost talk.

"You look like a man reading his own autopsy," she said, setting the bread down.

"Work."

"Your work is me. So either I'm dying or you are." She pulled a chair around backward and straddled it, arms folded on the backrest, watching me with those eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Which chapter?"

I should have lied. Fifteen years of practice and I couldn't produce a single lie; that was the strange rot the island had worked into me. "The cooperative."

Her face went still. Not angry—still, the way water goes still before it freezes. "And?"

"And I'm deciding how to write it."

"There's nothing to decide." She said it lightly, reaching for the bread, tearing it, handing me half. "You write what happened. That's the job, isn't it? Isn't that what you keep telling me? *Trust the record, senhora Ferreira. The record doesn't flatter anyone.*" She smiled, and the smile had an edge I couldn't read. "Unless the record's changed."

"The record hasn't changed."

"No," she agreed. "But publishers do." She bit into her bread and chewed, looking past me at the darkening window, at the last gray light on the water. "My aunt warned me about Lisbon publishers. She said they'd come out here wanting my father's bones for the soup. I told her no—this one's different, this one listens, this one writes my father like he was a man." She turned back to me, and her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Was I wrong?"

The question hung there. I could feel the whole architecture of my life balanced on my answer—the advance, the surgeries, the ground-floor light, Rita's unpoisoned future, fifteen years of invisible competence, all of it stacked on one side of a scale. On the other side, a loaf of bread and a woman waiting.

"Not yet," I said.

She studied me for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she nodded slowly, stood, and left the rest of the bread on the table between us like a test I hadn't passed.

"Five days," she said at the door. "Islanders count faster than editors."

When she was gone I sat in the lamplight with the ledgers and the phone and the number Duarte had named, and I took out a clean sheet of paper—not the laptop, paper, the old discipline—and wrote at the top, in my own hand, the one thing I'd sworn never to write in this book:

*My name is Xanthe Almeida, and I am the reason your father's name is mud.*

I looked at it for a long time. Then I folded it into my notebook, next to a letter I hadn't sent yet, and began, very carefully, to decide who I was going to be on Monday.
