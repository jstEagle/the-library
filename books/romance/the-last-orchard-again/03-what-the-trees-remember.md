## 3. What the Trees Remember

The morning of the last day before the festival, I woke before dawn and lay in the dark listening to the orchard breathe.

That's a foolish thing to say, maybe. Trees don't breathe, not in any way a man can hear. But my grandmother used to claim you could stand in the rows at first light and feel the whole place inhale — the leaves taking up the dew, the roots drinking deep, two hundred acres of Gravensteins and Pinot Noir all sighing together like one enormous sleeping animal. I'd believed her when I was eight. I'd stopped believing it somewhere around the second foreclosure notice.

I got up quietly so as not to wake Marisol in the guest room down the hall, and I went out to check on the festival preparations, because that was easier than checking on myself.

By seven I'd counted the string lights twice (we were short two strands), argued gently with old Tomás about whether the cider press needed a new gasket (it did), and eaten half a stale biscuit standing over the sink. By eight, Marisol was in the kitchen, hair still damp, wearing my grandmother's apron because hers had a wine stain from the reunion dinner and she refused to be photographed in it.

"You look like a man who slept," she said, squinting at me over her coffee. "Liar."

"I slept fine."

"Your left eye is twitching. It's been twitching since Thursday. Either you're having a stroke or you're hiding something, and honestly, at this point, either would explain a lot."

I laughed, because with Marisol you laughed or you bled, and I wasn't ready to bleed yet. She went back to her notes for the tasting tent — she'd taken over the wine program for the festival with the terrifying efficiency of someone who could not bear to watch amateurs butcher a pour — and I went upstairs to my desk, where the contract sat in the bottom drawer under a folder labeled GRANDMOTHER'S RECIPES, because I had always been a coward with excellent filing instincts.

The contract. Hollis & Vane Development. Ninety-one acres, the heart of the orchard, the oldest trees, the ones Grandmother had planted herself with dirt under her nails and a cigarette behind her ear. The signature line waited for me like an open mouth. Signing date: October 14th. The day after the festival.

I hadn't told anyone. Not my mother, not my sister Dana, not the workers who'd stayed on through three years of shrinking paychecks because they loved this place more than they loved being paid properly. And not Marisol, who had spent the past week falling in love with the orchard in a way I recognized too well, because I'd done it once myself, thirty years ago, in utero, presumably.

She found me at noon, in the barn, coiling extension cords.

"Silas." Her voice had a strange flatness to it, the calm-before kind. "Why is there a sale contract in your recipe folder?"

The cord slipped out of my hands and pooled on the dusty floor like something dead.

"It's not what—"

"Don't." She held up the papers. Ninety-one acres. Hollis & Vane. October 14th. "Don't do the thing where you talk for five minutes and say nothing. I've watched you do it to your family all week. You're very good at it. It's almost beautiful."

"Marisol—"

"The trees, Silas." Her voice cracked on the word, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "You told me about these trees. How your grandmother planted them. How the Gravenstein row leans south because she said even apple trees deserve a good view. And you're going to sign them away the day after we—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "The day after the festival."

"It's ninety-one acres," I said, and heard how small it sounded. "It keeps the house. It keeps the north vineyard. It erases everything I owe. It's the only way anybody survives this."

"Survives." She said the word like she was tasting a bad vintage, hunting for the flaw. "Who survives? You? Alone in this big empty farmhouse with your twitching eye and your lies? Because that's what you're buying, Silas. That's the price. You get to keep the shell of this place and lose every living thing in it."

"The alternative is losing *all* of it."

"The alternative," she said, stepping closer, "is telling the truth. Which I know is hard for you, so let me help: tomorrow night, two hundred people are going to come to this festival and toast the Underhill family and its thriving orchard, and you are going to stand there and let them. And then Monday morning you're going to sign away the oldest trees while everyone's still hungover on your generosity. That's not survival. That's just a slower way of dying, with better catering."

"That's easy for you to say!" It came out louder than I meant, loud enough to echo off the tin roof. "You don't carry it. Three generations, Marisol. My grandmother buried a husband and two harvests and kept this place alive with her bare hands. My father worked himself into an early grave trying to do the same. I'm the last Underhill. If I let it go, it doesn't matter how honest I was about it. It's gone. Forever. Do you know what that costs?"

"Yes," she said quietly. "I think I might."

And that's when I noticed we weren't alone.

Dana stood in the barn doorway with a box of festival flyers in her arms, frozen mid-step, wearing the expression of someone who has just walked into a room where the temperature dropped forty degrees. Behind her, half-hidden, my mother. Of course my mother. The woman had the stealth of a barn owl and the discretion of one too, in that she saw everything and said nothing until precisely the worst moment.

"How much did you hear?" I asked.

"Enough," said Dana. She set the box down slowly. "Silas. Tell me she's wrong. Tell me you're not signing away Grandma's trees the day after the festival."

The silence answered for me. Silence was, after all, my native language.

"Oh my God." Dana pressed her hand over her mouth, then took it away, because Dana had never once in her life hidden a feeling successfully. "You were going to let us all stand up there tomorrow and celebrate. You were going to let Mom give her toast about the hundredth anniversary—"

"I was going to fix it," I said. "Quietly. Cleanly. Nobody needed to—"

"That's what you said last time." Dana's voice went strange. "God, you're doing it *again*. You're doing exactly what you did with Elena."

The name landed in the barn like a dropped glass.

Marisol looked between us. "Elena?"

"Nothing," I said.

"He gave up Elena," Dana said, talking over me, over eleven years of my asking her not to, "to save the farm the first time. Did he tell you that? When we almost lost it after Dad died — the bank, the loans, all of it — the Delgado vineyards offered to buy the east parcel, and Mr. Delgado's daughter Elena wanted to marry him. He could have married her and sold the parcel and been set for life. Instead he broke it off, because if he married into the Delgados, selling to them would look like — what did you say, Silas? Say it. I've never forgotten it. You said, 'I won't be a man who marries the farm's rescuer.' So he chose the debt instead. He chose to carry it alone. He was twenty-six years old."

"Stop," I said.

"And now here you are," Dana went on, tears streaming freely now, furious and grieving at once, "twenty years later, about to do the same thing in reverse. Choosing the debt over the person. Choosing silence over everybody who loves you. You didn't save anything, Silas. You just taught this whole family that love is something you sacrifice to the land, like it's hungry."

The barn was very quiet. Somewhere outside, Tomás was testing the cider press, and the sound of it — that slow wooden groan, the same groan it had made my whole life — filled the space where none of us could find words.

Marisol set the contract down on a hay bale, carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

"I owed you a favor," she said, and her voice was steady in the way that costs the most. "One weekend. No feelings. That was the deal." She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she was looking for, she didn't find it. "I broke my end of the deal somewhere around the second night, Silas. I'm starting to suspect you never intended to keep yours."

She walked out past my mother, who stepped aside without a word, and my mother came into the barn and looked at the contract on the hay bale, and then at me, and said only:

"Tomorrow night, you'll stand up in front of this town and tell them the truth. All of it. Or I will." She smoothed her cardigan, the way she did before delivering verdicts. "Your grandmother hid debts too, you know. For six years. It nearly killed her, and it very nearly killed this farm. Honesty isn't the risk, Silas. It's the harvest. Everything else is just rotting politely on the branch."

Then she left, and I stood alone in the barn with the contract and the sound of the cider press, and through the open door I could see Marisol crossing the yard toward the far row of trees — the Gravensteins, the leaning ones, the ones with the good view — walking fast, wiping her eyes, getting smaller and smaller between the rows.
