## 1. Five Safe Steps

Edmund Okonkwo had a theory about safe steps, and like most of his theories, he had developed it alone, in the dark, with a cup of cold tea going bitter beside him.

The theory was this: a man could survive almost anything if he limited himself to five safe steps a day. Not literal steps — though God knew he took those too, the same ones, in the same order, until the flagstones between the farmhouse and the winery had worn pale depressions shaped like his boots. He meant the other kind. The decisions. The small, survivable motions of a life kept deliberately small.

Step one: read the ledgers before coffee, so the numbers could ruin the morning while it was already ruined anyway.

Step two: walk the vines.

Step three: answer correspondence — which, for five years, had amounted to bills, rejection letters from banks with increasingly apologetic phrasing, and one memorable postcard from a soil scientist in Montpellier who wanted to know if his *terroir* was for sale and whether the answer could be sent collect.

Step four: fix something broken. There was never a shortage.

Step five: sleep, if the vineyard permitted it, which it usually did not.

Five steps. No sixth. A man who took a sixth step was a man who believed in things again, and belief, in Edmund's experience, was how vineyards went bankrupt in the first place.

His father had believed in six steps. His father had believed in sixty. Joseph Okonkwo had arrived in the valley with nothing but a suitcase, a degree in agronomy, and the unshakeable conviction that a Nigerian-born winemaker could put a bottle on every table in Europe, and for twenty years — against frost, against fashion, against the murmured skepticism of neighbors who pronounced his name carefully at first and then stopped trying — he had nearly done it. Nearly. The word hung over Okonkwo Vineyards like the mist off the river some mornings: *nearly*. Nearly solvent. Nearly famous. Nearly enough.

Then came the loans, then the bad winter, then the distributor who folded owing them everything, and then, on a gray Tuesday five years ago, a solicitor's letter informing Edmund that his father was dead and his inheritance was a beautiful, drowning thing.

He had been twenty-eight. He had been working in Lyon, doing something sensible with spreadsheets that he could no longer quite remember, and he had come home for the funeral intending to stay two weeks.

Two weeks had become five years, and Edmund had learned to count his steps.

* * *

On the first Tuesday of October, he woke at five-forty without an alarm, because the vineyard had trained him the way it trained everything else — patiently, and through suffering.

The farmhouse was quiet in the particular way it had been quiet for ten years now. He knew all its sounds the way you know the silences of an old friend: the tick of the boiler deciding whether to commit to the day, the settling groan of beams his grandfather had hauled up the hill, the wind finding the loose shutter on the north side that he had been meaning to fix since — he checked the mental ledger — March. Two years of Marches, actually. It was possible the shutter had become less a repair and more a companion.

He made coffee in the dented pot because the good French press had cracked the second winter and he had never replaced it, and he sat at the kitchen table with the ledgers spread out before him like a patient awaiting diagnosis.

The news was not good, but it was not bad, and after five years Edmund had developed a connoisseur's palate for the space between. He turned the pages with a pencil behind his ear — he had given up pens, because pens implied confidence — and did the arithmetic he could do in his sleep.

Harvest in three weeks. If the weather held. If the fruit set as well as it looked set. If he could find pickers, which was its own annual comedy, because the valley's young people had all gone to cities where the work didn't stain your hands purple. If the yields came in at even the modest number he'd penciled into column four, and if he could sell the grapes at even the modest price anyone would pay, then Okonkwo Vineyards would survive another year.

Another year. Five years running, the entire ambition of the estate had contracted to that phrase, the way a starving body contracts around its organs. *Survive another year.* It was not a dream. It was a pulse.

He allowed himself, as he always did, thirty seconds of imagining what it would be like to want something larger. Thirty seconds, timed against the cooling coffee. In those seconds he let himself picture the cellar full instead of echoing — barrels actually aging wine instead of standing empty like furniture in a house waiting for owners — picture a label with his father's name on it sitting in shops in Lyon, in Paris, in places Joseph Okonkwo had never been able to afford to visit. Picture someone across the table in the evenings. Anyone. Someone who laughed at the right moments and argued at the better ones.

At thirty-one seconds, he closed the ledger, and the wanting folded itself away into the drawer where he kept such things, and he stood, and he took step two.

* * *

October had come early and gold to the valley. The morning fog was burning off the river in long ribbons, and the vines — old, gnarled, half of them older than Edmund — were turning the color of struck matches. He walked row seven first, because row seven was the oldest planting and therefore, in Edmund's private theology, owed a certain deference, the way you visit the eldest relative first.

"Morning," he said to them. He talked to the vines. He was aware this was the sort of thing that, reported back to Lyon, would confirm every suspicion the city held about country people, but the vines were the only audience he had who never interrupted, and besides, his father had talked to them too, in three languages, and the fruit had never once complained.

He walked the rows with his hands behind his back, checking. Leaf roll on the far block — mild, manageable. Netting intact on the south slope, mostly; there was a tear near the end post where some bird of considerable determination had spent the summer filing a complaint, and he crouched and mended it with the twine he carried in his coat pocket for exactly this purpose. His fingers did the work while his mind wandered, which was the arrangement they'd had for years.

It was during this wandering that he thought about the letter.

The letter had come Saturday. He had read it eleven times since, which by Edmund's standards was practically reckless behavior, and it was sitting upstairs on the kitchen table under the salt cellar, weighted down as though it might otherwise blow away — or as though pinning it down might make it real.

Callum Reyes. Regional sales director, Meridian Distribution. Edmund had heard the name before, the way you hear a weather report about somewhere else. Reyes was a rising man in wine distribution — everyone said so, even people who said it through their teeth — the kind of salesman who could talk a sommelier into stocking a wine the sommelier had sworn off, and then thank him for the privilege. Younger than Edmund by a few years, probably. Charmer, certainly. The letter itself was charming, which was how Edmund knew it had been written by the man personally rather than a secretary, because secretaries wrote correct letters and charmers wrote letters that made you feel like the only vineyard in the valley.

*Your father's wines were ahead of their time,* the letter had said. *I believe yours are arriving exactly on time.*

Edmund had read that sentence perhaps forty times. He was not proud of this. But a man who has received five years of letters explaining why no one can help him develops a certain hunger for a letter that says otherwise, and Reyes's letter said otherwise in confident, well-fed prose, and then it said the thing that had kept Edmund walking row seven longer than usual:

*I will be traveling through your region the third week of October and would welcome the opportunity to taste what you're making. I have in mind a distribution arrangement that could be significant for us both.*

Significant. The word had a shine to it. Edmund had held the letter up to the window light on Sunday evening, half expecting to find the fine print of a joke, but there was only the paper, and the watermark, and his own reflection looking foolishly hopeful over the top of it.

A distribution contract. A real one. Not the hand-to-mouth grape sales, not the co-op's charity pricing, but someone putting Okonkwo wine in front of people who bought wine on purpose. It was the door his father had beaten his fists against for twenty years, and a stranger was proposing to open it with a smile.

Which was, of course, precisely when Edmund's caution woke up, stretched, and asked its usual questions. What did Reyes want in return? Exclusivity, surely — distributors always wanted exclusivity, and exclusivity meant dependence, and dependence meant that a man in a nice suit three valleys away could decide whether the lights stayed on here. And why now? Why this vineyard, this year, this bankrupt little holding that every other distributor had driven past without slowing down?

There was no answer in the letter. There was only the invitation, and a phone number, and a signature that looped its R's like a man enjoying himself.

Edmund finished mending the netting, straightened, and looked out over the valley. From row seven you could see the whole shape of the place: the river, the village with its church spire poking up like a periscope, the mountains beyond going white at the tips earlier every year. Somewhere out there was a road that led over the pass, and somewhere along that road, in the third week of October, a car would come carrying Callum Reyes and whatever significant things he intended.

"You hear that?" Edmund asked the vines. "Somebody's coming."

The vines, being vines, said nothing, but the wind moved through them in a long shiver that he chose, generously, to interpret as interest.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing step four, which today meant the tractor, which had developed a noise in second gear like a bagpipe being murdered, and by the time the light went amber over the western ridge he had grease to the elbows and a temporary peace treaty with the gearbox and a mood that was, remarkably, close to good.

It was while washing up at the kitchen sink, watching the water run black off his forearms, that he caught himself doing the arithmetic of hospitality. Guest room. When had he last aired the guest room? The honest answer involved a number with more digits than he cared to face. Sheets. Did he own guest sheets, or only the sheets currently in rotation? There was a bottle of his father's last good vintage in the cellar — three bottles, actually, guarded like state secrets — and wouldn't that be the move, opening one for a man who might hold the estate's future in his polished shoes?

He dried his hands and stood in the middle of his kitchen, and realized that he was, for the first time in years, preparing for something rather than merely enduring it. The feeling was unfamiliar and slightly alarming, like a muscle remembered from childhood.

"All right," he told the empty room, because talking to vines apparently opened the door to talking to kitchens. "One dinner. One tasting. One conversation." He pointed the dish towel at the ledger on the table. "And if he wants the farm for nothing, I'll show him the pass myself."

But that night, after the lamp was off and the house had settled into its familiar dark, Edmund lay awake doing the thing he did not do on ordinary nights, which was remember.

Not the ledgers. Not the debts. The other inheritance — the one that had no line item and no column, the one he kept in the drawer of the desk in the guest room, of all places, hidden in the least visited room of the house like a wound kept in a glove compartment.

Ten years. Ten years this autumn. A summer that had started with her laughing at his terrible French and ended with an unsigned postcard and a silence so complete it had its own acoustics. Marguerite. Even now, even after a decade of disciplined not-thinking, the name arrived whole, with weather attached — heat, dust, the smell of crushed grapes on both their hands, her voice saying his name with the R rolled wrong and the meaning entirely right.

She had left in September. No goodbye that explained anything. Just the postcard, unsigned, unsentimental, a picture of the valley itself as though she couldn't resist one last look, and on the back, in her slanted handwriting, a single line: *Some summers are the whole story. Thank you for mine.*

He had been twenty-three and he had taken it like a verdict. He had waited for an explanation the way you wait for rain in a drought — attentively, then hopefully, then with a bitterness that passed, eventually, into something quieter and worse: acceptance. She was gone. People were gone. His father used to say the valley taught you that early or it broke you late, and Edmund had learned it at twenty-three and built his five safe steps on top of the lesson like a house on a fault line.

Now a stranger was coming over the pass with a contract and a smile, and Edmund found, lying in the dark, that hope and dread had arrived together, holding hands, the way they always did.

He slept badly, and dreamed of a postcard he could not read, and woke at five-forty without an alarm, and put the kettle on, and reached for the ledgers.

They were still there. The numbers were still the numbers. But on the table beside them, pinned under the salt cellar, Callum Reyes's letter waited with its shiny word — *significant* — and outside, the fog was lifting off the river in ribbons, and somewhere over the white-tipped pass, the third week of October was already on its way.

Edmund poured his coffee, sat down, and allowed himself — cautiously, briefly, at his own considerable risk — a sixth step.

Then he went to air out the guest room.
