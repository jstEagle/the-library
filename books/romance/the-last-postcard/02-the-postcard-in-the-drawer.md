## 2. The Postcard in the Drawer

The guest room had been a shrine for ten years, though Edmund Okonkwo would have denied it under oath.

He preferred to think of it as *storage*. That was the word he used when his mother called from Lagos and asked, in the careful tone she reserved for questions about his heart, whether he'd done anything with the spare room yet. *Storage*, he'd say. *Boxes. The good linens.* And his mother would make a small sound, the sound of a woman who had raised three children and buried a husband and was therefore fluent in the language of men lying to themselves, and she would change the subject to the weather in Napa Valley, which was always either perfect or catastrophic, never anything in between.

But this morning, standing in the doorway with a stack of fresh towels against his chest like an offering, Edmund admitted — privately, briefly, the way one admits to a doctor that yes, one does drink slightly more coffee than recommended — that the room had not been touched since Marguerite Laurent slept in it.

Ten years. The bed made with hospital corners he hadn't bothered to learn anywhere else. A jar of wildflowers on the sill, replaced every few weeks by whatever the hillside offered, which meant right now it held the dry brown skeletons of November fennel. He'd told himself once, early on, that he'd stop doing it after the first year. Then after the second. Somewhere around year five he stopped telling himself things about that room at all, which was its own kind of surrender.

Callum Reyes was arriving tomorrow. Callum Reyes, of Reyes & Associates Distribution, whose letter sat on the kitchen table downstairs like a lit fuse. *Dear Mr. Okonkwo, I've tasted your Chardonnay — the '19, the one you made yourself, God knows how, given your situation — and I think we should talk.*

*Given your situation.* Edmund had read that phrase four times. It was either condescension or honesty, and with distributors it was usually both. But the man was offering a meeting, and meetings with distributors were to a bankrupt vineyard what rain was to a cracked hillside: insufficient, probably, but you didn't turn it away.

So: fresh towels. Clean sheets. Windows opened to let out a decade of held breath.

He stripped the bed first, and that was where the trouble started, because the mattress was heavy and old and shifted against the wall, and behind the headboard there was a gap — two inches of shadow between the frame and the plaster — and something fell out of it.

A postcard.

Edmund stood very still, holding the corner of the sheet, listening to the house settle around him the way it did on cold mornings, timbers ticking like a clock that kept no particular time.

He knew what it was before he turned it over. His body knew. Some animal part of him, some older and less disciplined Edmund, had gone quiet and attentive, the way a dog goes still when it hears a car come up a drive it hasn't heard in years.

He picked it up anyway. Of course he picked it up. Ten years of careful order, five years of ledgers balanced to the cent and pruning schedules color-coded by varietal, and all of it undone by a rectangle of cardstock falling out of a wall.

The front was a photograph of the valley itself — the vineyard in high summer, rows running down the hillside like green script, the farmhouse small and white in the lower corner. It was the kind of postcard sold at the general store in town for forty-five cents, the kind tourists bought because it was pretty and forgot in glove compartments. On the back, in handwriting he could have identified blindfolded, by touch, underwater:

*I keep thinking about the morning we picked the Muscat. You had purple stains on your hands for three days and you pretended to be embarrassed but you weren't. I wasn't either. — M*

No date. No address. No signature beyond the initial. Just that, in her slanted, impatient hand, the letters leaning forward as if they were trying to get somewhere faster than the sentence allowed.

Edmund sat down on the bare mattress, which complained beneath him, and read it again. And again. Not because the words changed, but because some part of him kept hoping they might, kept hoping that this time the card would contain the rest of it — the reason, the goodbye, the explanation she'd owed him and never sent.

It didn't. It never did. He'd read it perhaps four hundred times over ten years and it remained stubbornly, maddeningly incomplete, a letter with the middle torn out.

Because here was the thing — the thing he'd told almost no one, the thing that lived in him like a stone in a shoe: the postcard had arrived *after* she left. Three weeks after. Long enough that he'd begun to believe she'd simply erased him, that the summer had been a season she'd packed away with her clothes. And then this. One card, unsigned, unaddressed, mailed from nowhere he could trace — he'd checked the postmark so many times the ink had smudged — saying nothing except that she remembered his hands stained purple.

Not *I'm sorry*. Not *I had to go*. Not even *goodbye*. Just the memory, offered like a coin tossed into a well, with no way to know if she wanted a wish back.

* * *

That last summer came back the way it always did — not in order, but in pieces, the way a barrel gives up its wine one glass at a time.

They were twenty-four and twenty-three, and she had come to the valley for a job she quit within a month because the winemaker she'd been hired to assist turned out to be, in her words, "a tyrant with a forklift." She stayed anyway. Nobody asked her why. It was obvious why. The valley in June does that to people; it makes liars of everyone's plans.

She'd shown up at the farmhouse the first week with a bottle of somebody else's terrible rosé and announced that she'd read in a book that you could tell a vineyard's soul by tasting its worst wine. Edmund, who had inherited the place only that spring along with his father's debts and his father's stubbornness, had said, "Then you'll be drinking a lot of my father's mistakes."

"I have time," she said, and she did, that summer. They both did. That was the unbearable luxury of it — time, whole afternoons of it, spent walking the rows while she asked questions no one had ever bothered to ask him. Not *how many tons per acre* but *why do you talk to the vines when you think nobody's watching?* Not *what's your production model* but *what did your father smell like?*

Nobody had ever asked him that. His father had been dead eight months and Edmund had been carrying the estate like a man carrying a piano downhill, and this stranger with dirt under her fingernails and a laugh that startled birds asked him what he smelled like, and Edmund had said, "Sulfur and pipe tobacco," and she'd said, "Perfect," and that was that. That was the whole catastrophe, right there. He was finished by July.

The Muscat harvest — the one on the postcard — had come in early September, hot and fast, and they'd picked until their fingers went purple and then kept picking past dark with the tractor lights on, giddy and stupid, eating more grapes than they saved. She'd crushed one against his cheek in a moment of what she claimed was accident and he claimed, correctly, was assault, and the stain had taken three days to fade, and he'd caught himself checking the mirror each morning with something dangerously close to hope.

Hope. That was the crop he'd planted that summer without meaning to. It grew faster than anything else on the property.

And then October came, and with it a phone call she took in the other room, voice dropping low, and a visit from her brother that Edmund only half-witnessed through a window — two figures by a car, speaking with a urgency that didn't look familial so much as *legal* — and then, on a Tuesday with nothing wrong about it that he could later point to, she was gone. Room emptied. Job abandoned. The winemaking tyrant reported she'd left no forwarding address.

He'd driven to her apartment in town. Empty, keys left with the landlady, deposit forfeited. He'd called everyone they knew. He'd done everything a man does except the one thing he couldn't do, which was stop.

Three weeks later: the postcard. And then nothing, forever, except the room upstairs that he did not call a shrine.

* * *

Downstairs, the kettle had gone cold on the stove. Edmund realized he'd been sitting on the bare guest bed for the better part of an hour, holding a piece of cardstock worth forty-five cents, and that the light had moved across the floorboards the way it did when the day was getting away from you.

He laughed — one short, humorless note — at himself. At the picture he must have made. Thirty-four years old, a man who could recite his debt schedule from memory, sitting in a museum of one summer.

"Right," he said aloud, to the room, because talking to empty rooms was another habit he'd stopped explaining to people. "Towels."

He worked. That was the mercy of routine; it gave your hands something to do while the rest of you recovered. Fresh sheets, hospital corners — he'd learned those from his mother, actually, not the army, though everyone assumed army. The wildflowers he replaced with the last of the dried lavender from the bundle hanging in the pantry, because lavender at least looked intentional. He wiped the windowsill. He beat the dust from the curtains and decided, generously, that the room now smelled like a place people visited rather than a place someone had fled.

The postcard he carried downstairs.

Here was the ritual, and he performed it now with the grim efficiency of long practice: the bottom drawer of the kitchen dresser, third drawer down, beneath the spare batteries and the manual for a food processor he'd won at a raffle in 2019. There was a tin in that drawer — an old shortbread tin, blue, dented — and inside the tin, wrapped in a page of the local paper dated the week she disappeared (he'd kept the newspaper too; there was no defending this), lay the postcard's siblings.

Or rather, its predecessors. Because the postcard was not the only thing she'd left in his keeping. Over that summer, Marguerite had written letters — real ones, stamped and addressed to her family back east, to friends, to the job she'd quit — and she'd written them at his kitchen table, and several times she'd written them, sealed them, and then decided not to send them, tearing them open again with a fingernail and starting over. He'd teased her about it. *You know mail is optional,* he'd said. She'd thrown a dish towel at him.

When she vanished, he'd found three of those unsent drafts tucked into the farmhouse — one in the flour canister, one under the loose brick by the hearth, one folded inside the pages of his father's pruning manual, which was either carelessness or a message, and after ten years he still couldn't decide which. He'd never read them. That was the line he'd drawn, early, the one clean thing: her words addressed to other people were hers, even unsent, even abandoned. He'd kept them the way you keep a stranger's lost glove — carefully, uselessly, waiting for a claim that never comes.

The postcard joined the others in the tin. He looked at the little collection — one card, three letters, one newspaper — and felt the familiar double weight of it: the ache, and underneath the ache, the stubborn tenderness. Grief, he'd learned, was mostly love with nowhere to put itself.

"You could burn them," he told the tin. "You know that, don't you. Any reasonable person would."

The tin said nothing. Tins are famously tight-lipped.

He closed the drawer. And that was when the phone buzzed against the counter — a text from a number he'd entered yesterday with the deliberate neutrality of a man signing a contract: **Callum Reyes.**

*Looking forward to tomorrow, Edmund. Bringing my fiancée if that's alright — she's the real palate in the family. She'll want to try everything.*

Edmund stared at the screen. Fiancée. Fine. People had fiancées; people had lives; the world was full of people pairing off with great efficiency while he organized batteries in drawers. He typed *Look forward to it* and deleted *it* because it seemed too warm, then retyped it because *Look forward to* alone seemed curt, then sent the original version and put the phone face-down on the counter like a man closing a door on a noise.

Fiancée. He'd set out two glasses for tomorrow. He supposed he'd need three.

Upstairs, the guest room waited, lavender-scented and innocent, corners crisp, holding nothing now but sheets and the shape of a memory. Edmund stood in the doorway a moment longer than the task required.

Outside, the first clouds of the week were stacking up over the pass, gray and unhurried, the way weather gathers when it has somewhere to be and all the time in the world to get there.

He didn't think anything of it. Why would he? Storms came and went. So, he'd believed for ten years, did everything else.

He went down to make dinner for one, and the valley got ready for snow.
