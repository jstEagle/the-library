## 5. Stranded

*Edmund*

The knock came at four in the afternoon, when the light had already gone the color of weak tea and the wind had begun to say serious things to the shutters.

Edmund opened the door expecting no one. The pass had been closed since noon — he'd heard it on the radio while he stacked firewood with the grim satisfaction of a man whose pessimism was about to be vindicated. So when he saw Callum Reyes standing on his step in a city coat far too elegant for snow, shaking flakes from his hair like a man who found weather personally offensive, Edmund's first thought was not *hello*. His first thought was a small, cold arithmetic: *the guest room is ready, and I will have to be charming for two days.*

His second thought arrived one heartbeat later, over Callum's shoulder, out of the falling white.

She was standing by the car.

Ten years is a long time. It is long enough for a boy to become a man with calloused hands and a ledger full of other people's debts. It is apparently not long enough to prepare a person for Marguerite Alba turning around in a snowstorm with her scarf half unwound and her mouth forming his name like a word she'd been told she wasn't allowed anymore.

"Edmund," Callum was saying, breezy, stamping his boots. "Terrible luck, the pass. The plows won't run until morning at the earliest, maybe later if the wind keeps up. We saw your chimney smoke from the bend and thought — well, we hoped you'd take pity on us."

Edmund heard himself say something hospitable. He did not know what it was. Some part of him had gone on autopilot — the same part that handled tax inspectors and broken pumps — while the rest of him stood very still inside, watching Marguerite come up the path through the snow, each step deliberate, as though the ground between them were something that could be crossed carefully or not at all.

"Marguerite," he said, when she reached the step. Her name fit his mouth the way an old key fits a lock: rusted, but true.

"Hello, Edmund." Her voice was lower than he remembered. Steadier. Except at the very end of the word, where it wobbled like a candle flame in a draft.

Snow gathered on all three of them. Nobody moved.

"Well," said Callum, into the silence, with the tone of a man dropping a match into a room he suspected was full of gas, "this is cozy already."

* * *

*Margarite*

The farmhouse smelled exactly the same, which was the cruelest thing about it.

Marguerite had prepared herself for changes. She had rehearsed changes on the drive up — the way one rehearses a difficult phone call — imagining the house repainted, renovated, made strange so that it couldn't reach into her chest and squeeze. Instead: the same coat hooks by the door, the same crooked shelf where someone had once propped a bottle of the estate's worst vintage as a joke, the same kitchen with its long wooden table where she had eaten peaches in August and laughed until her ribs hurt.

The same man, taller, broader through the shoulders, with ten new years carved into his face — and looking at her now as if she were a ghost who had the poor manners to be solid.

"You remember where everything is," Edmund said. It wasn't a question. He was filling the kettle with the economy of motion of someone who needed his hands busy.

"I remember everything," she said, before she could stop herself, and then wished she'd said something safer, like *vaguely*, like *it's been a while*.

Callum, mercifully, was being enormous. He had installed himself at the table and was admiring the room with genuine warmth, asking after the vineyard, the frost, the well, complimenting the iron stove as though stoves were a personal interest of his. Marguerite loved him a little for it, in the way you love a person who stands between you and a falling wall.

"The roads," she said, when the kettle began its low complaint. "Surely they'll clear by morning."

"Maybe." Edmund didn't look at her. "The radio says another front behind this one. Could be two days. Could be three."

Three days. The number landed in the room and sat down among them like an uninvited guest.

Callum whistled. "Well. I've survived worse company." He grinned at Edmund, easy and unbothered, and Marguerite watched Edmund return the grin a half-second late, and understood that the two men had already begun some private negotiation she hadn't been invited to — a fencing match conducted entirely in hospitality.

"I'll make dinner," she said, because doing nothing was unbearable. "If there's anything to make."

"There's eggs," Edmund said. "There's always eggs. The hens are the only part of this operation that's never let me down."

Something in her chest cracked open, warm and awful. Because that was the joke — *his* joke, from the first summer, when the vines had frozen and the bank had circled and he'd stood in this kitchen holding an egg aloft like a trophy and declared the hens the only honest workforce in the valley. He remembered it. He'd kept it. All these years, the joke had been sitting here in the kitchen waiting for her the way the crooked shelf waited, the way the coat hooks waited.

"Still telling people the hens are your best employees?" she managed.

"They unionized last spring," Edmund said. "I gave in on the mealworms."

And she laughed — really laughed, startled out of her — and the sound seemed to surprise all three of them. Callum looked between them with bright, unreadable eyes, and Marguerite turned quickly to the eggs, and outside the storm leaned its whole weight against the house like something impatient to be let in.

* * *

*Callum*

Callum Reyes had built a career on reading rooms, and the room he was currently sitting in was the most interesting room he'd entered in five years.

It wasn't that either of them was doing anything. That was the remarkable part. Nothing was happening — and it was happening loudly. Edmund Okonkwo set the table like a man defusing something. Marguerite asked where the salt was and then, upon being told, looked at the shaker as if it might testify against her. When their hands met reaching for the pepper mill — briefly, accidentally, in the way hands do — both of them flinched back with such synchronized politeness that Callum nearly applauded.

He kept the conversation running himself, generous as a host at his own ambush. Wine regions. Freight costs. The scandalous price of oak barrels. He told a story about a sommelier in Lyon who had once mistaken tap water for a Burgundy, and got a real laugh out of Edmund, and caught Marguerite watching Edmund laugh with an expression she probably believed was neutral.

*Oh, you two,* he thought, spearing an egg. *You absolute disasters.*

He wasn't angry. Not yet. What he felt was more like a chess player's alertness — the pleasant, sharpened attention of a man who has just realized the game is being played on a board he hadn't known existed. He had proposed this trip for business; Marguerite had insisted on coming for reasons she'd dressed up as logistics; and now the mountain had done what mountains do, which is keep people where they are until they stop pretending.

He should have been jealous. He registered, distantly, that jealousy was the appropriate response and that he would get to it. But first there was fascination. Ten years of history sat at this table wearing borrowed manners, and Callum wanted to know all of it.

"So," he said pleasantly, refilling Edmund's glass with the cheap local red they'd opened — decent, actually, better than decent, which annoyed him on principle because it meant the contract mattered even more — "how long has this place been in your family?"

"Grandfather planted it," Edmund said. "My parents ran it. I inherited the debts along with the dirt."

"And you stayed."

"It stays with you whether you stay with it or not." Edmund turned his glass. "Vineyards are like that."

"Like dogs," Callum agreed. "Or mortgages." He smiled, and let the pause stretch just slightly past comfortable. "Marguerite says you knew each other. Before."

"We grew up in the same valley," Edmund said evenly.

"That's all?" said Callum, lightly, terribly.

Marguerite's fork stopped moving. Edmund looked at the candle flame for a moment, and Callum watched him decide — visibly, the way a man decides at a high card table — how much truth the pot was worth.

"That's the geography," Edmund said at last. "The rest is weather."

Callum laughed, delighted despite himself. "That's the best non-answer anyone's ever given me. I'm going to steal it for distributor meetings."

Outside, the wind rose to a register that made the windows hum. The lights flickered once, twice — and died. The refrigerator sighed into silence. In the sudden dark, before the candles were lit, Callum heard Marguerite's breath catch, and heard Edmund say, quietly, from somewhere across the black table:

"It's all right. It does this. Stay where you are — I know the way."

*I bet you do,* Callum thought, striking the match, and watched the small flame stand up between the three of them and give each face back to the others.

* * *

*Marguerite*

They washed the dishes together because there was nothing else to do with their hands, and because Callum had announced he'd inspect the generator and disappeared into the dark of the utility shed with a flashlight and the cheerful courage of a man who had never once fixed anything himself.

So it was just the two of them at the sink, shoulder to shoulder, the way they had been a hundred times in another life, and the storm pressing at the windows, and the candle on the counter throwing their shadows huge against the ceiling where they mingled and would not separate.

"You've kept the house well," she said.

"You've kept—" He stopped. Whatever he'd been about to say, he swallowed it. She watched him choose a different sentence the way a man steps around ice. "You look well."

"Liar," she said softly. "I look like a woman who drove into a blizzard to avoid—"

"To avoid what?"

The question came out sharper than either of them expected. The water ran on between them, indifferent. Marguerite scrubbed a plate that was already clean.

"I don't know," she said. "I've spent ten years not knowing. It's practically a profession."

Edmund dried his hands. He did it slowly, and when he spoke his voice was careful, level, and underneath the levelness something moved like a current under ice. "I used to imagine this. Meeting again. I had versions. In one of them I was calm and gracious and asked about your life like a civilized man. In another I'd rehearsed things I wanted to say." He set the towel down. "None of the versions had a blizzard and your fiancé fixing my generator."

"He can't fix a generator."

"No," Edmund agreed. "I know."

And there it was — the terrible, gentle intimacy of shared knowledge, the shorthand of two people who had once catalogued each other completely. She felt it land in her like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom.

"Why are you here, Marguerite?" he asked. Not cruelly. Worse than cruelly — kindly, patiently, as though he had all night, as though the mountain had given them permission.

"For the wine," she said. "For Callum's contract. For—"

"Marguerite."

"Don't," she whispered. "Please don't ask me questions tonight. If you ask me anything tonight I'll answer it, and I can't — there are reasons, Edmund, good ones, old ones, and if I say them out loud in this kitchen I'll never be able to take them back, and—" Her voice failed. She gripped the edge of the sink. "I need the lie to hold a little longer. Just until the roads open."

The silence went on so long she dared to look up. He was watching her with an expression she had never seen on him before — not the boy's open hurt, but something older and stranger. Recognition, almost. As if she had confirmed something he'd suspected for years.

"The roads," he repeated quietly.

"Yes."

"All right." He picked up the candle. "I'll show you your rooms. There's wood in every grate. The house creaks at night — it's only the house."

In the hallway, Callum reappeared, snow-dusted, triumphant, having gotten the generator to cough twice and die again. "We're on candles till morning," he announced. "Romantic, honestly. Like a holiday."

Neither of them answered him. And as Marguerite climbed the stairs behind the two men, past the landing where a certain loose floorboard still sang its one note under her foot — *he never fixed it,* she thought, dizzily, *ten years and he never fixed it* — she caught Edmund glancing back at her once, briefly, with a look she couldn't decode.

Later, lying awake under quilts that smelled of cedar and of summers, she heard the storm turn its great slow pages outside, and she admitted the thing she had been refusing to admit since the driveway:

He hadn't looked surprised to see her.

Shocked, yes. Wounded, yes. But underneath it — in the half second before his face closed — there had been something else. Something that looked almost like a man finding a letter he had long ago stopped expecting, but never quite stopped listening for.

Down the hall, a floorboard creaked. Someone else was awake, too.

And in the cellar below them all, forty empty oak barrels stood in the dark, waiting — the way certain truths wait — to be filled.
