## 6. What the Cellar Remembers

The lights died at 7:42 in the evening, and Edmund knew it before the darkness arrived — he felt it in the way the house exhaled, a small mechanical sigh from the boiler that had been his grandfather's and would outlive them all.

"Storm's found us," Callum said from the armchair by the window, where he'd been watching the snow come down like static on an old television. He said it with the tone of a man narrating weather for other people's benefit, as though someone might be taking notes.

Marguerite stood at the kitchen counter with a candle already lit. Of course she did. Some things didn't change in ten years; she had always been the one who moved first in a dark room, who found matches in drawers no one else thought to search. The flame caught her face from below, and Edmund looked away too quickly, caught himself looking at all.

"There's a lantern," he said, "in the pantry. Behind the—"

"I know," she said. "Behind the flour."

She was already moving toward it.

He stood there in the sudden blue half-light of the storm window, feeling absurdly like a man in his own museum. Ten years of living alone in this house had taught him exactly where everything was, and now every one of those certainties was being gently, competently contradicted by a woman holding a candle.

"The cellar's warmest," he said to the room in general. "Stone holds the heat. And if the power stays off, the pipes up here will freeze before the ones down there do."

"Sensible," said Callum, rising, brushing nothing off his sleeves because there was nothing on them, which was somehow its own kind of elegance. "Lead the way."

* * *

Edmund went down first with the lantern, and the cellar received him the way it always did: coolly, familiarly, smelling of damp stone and raw oak and the particular mineral breath of ground that had been growing grapes since before anyone alive could remember. He'd spent five years restoring this room — new racks for the old bottles, swept floors, the south wall cleared of the ivy roots that had been quietly strangling the foundation. What he hadn't done was fill it.

The barrels stood in their rows, French oak, bought secondhand from a château in Burgundy going through its own hard times. Empty. All of them empty. He ran his hand along the nearest stave as he passed, the way you'd touch the shoulder of an old friend you owed money to.

"You can fit how much down here?" Callum asked behind him, voice dropping into the hush the way voices do underground.

"Six thousand liters, if I fill everything." Edmund set the lantern on the tasting table his father had built from a door laid across two sawhorses. "I've filled none of it."

Marguerite came down the stairs last, candle still in hand, and stopped three steps from the bottom.

He watched her take it in. The rows of barrels receding into shadow. The bottle racks along the east wall, mostly bare, a few dusty survivors from his grandfather's last good vintage standing sentinel. The low ceiling, the iron hooks where hams used to hang, the chalkboard on the far wall where his father had kept the fermentation notes in handwriting like a seismograph reading.

"Oh," Marguerite said softly.

It wasn't much of a word. It was, Edmund thought, the most eloquent thing anyone had said in this house in a decade.

She walked past him without seeming to see him, straight to the third barrel in the second row — and he knew, with a certainty that made his chest hurt, which barrel she would go to, because it was *his* barrel, the one directly beneath the old trapdoor, the one whose position corresponded to nothing except that it was where his father had always kept the newest wine, the wine still becoming itself.

Her fingers spread against the oak stave. Flat palm, fingers wide, the way you'd greet a horse.

"They're empty," she said. Not accusing. Wondering.

"Not forever."

"No," she agreed, and her hand stayed where it was. "No, I don't suppose they are."

Callum had drifted to the bottle racks. Edmund watched him read the labels by lantern light — the man had the focused, courteous attention of someone appraising a house he intended to buy, or a person. He lifted one of his grandfather's bottles, turned it, hummed something appreciative and noncommittal.

"Nineteen ninety-eight?" Callum said.

"The last year my grandfather had more money than sense," Edmund said. "He bottled everything, even the batches that weren't ready, because he couldn't bear to see a barrel sit full and unprofitable."

"And your father?"

"My father couldn't bear to open them." He heard his own voice flatten. "Between the two of them, we ended up with a cellar full of wine nobody drinks and barrels nobody fills. There's probably a proverb in that."

"There's a business plan in that," Callum said lightly, setting the bottle back with exaggerated care. "Which is why I'm here."

But he said it while looking at Marguerite, or rather at Marguerite's hand on the barrel, and Edmund saw the exact moment the observation landed — the small stillness in Callum's shoulders, the recalibration behind his eyes. A distributor's instinct, Edmund supposed. You learned to read a room. You learned to notice when two people stood in the same silence comfortably, when silence between strangers would have been awkward but between these two was something else entirely. Something worn-in. Something with a shape.

Callum said nothing about it. He was too well-mannered, or too strategic, or both. He simply turned back to the racks and began asking intelligent questions about soil drainage that Edmund answered on autopilot while some older, deeper part of him stood in the second row of empty barrels beside a woman touching oak like an old friend.

Because that was what she was doing. That was the thing he couldn't stop seeing. Everyone else who came down here touched the barrels the way you'd touch furniture — checking, assessing, measuring capacity. Marguerite touched them the way you'd touch a scar. Familiarly. With apology.

* * *

They settled around the tasting table because there was nowhere else to sit, and because the alternative was standing in a cold cellar performing casualness, and everyone had apparently reached their limit of performance for the day. Callum produced a bottle from his own bag — naturally he traveled with wine, Edmund thought, the man probably slept with a corkscrew under his pillow — a Rioja, good, not showy, and opened it with a waiter's friend in four clean movements.

"Glasses?" Callum asked.

Edmund reached under the table and brought up three. They were his grandfather's tasting glasses, thin-stemmed, mismatched, one with a chip on the rim that he gave himself without comment. He poured Callum's first, then his own, then held the bottle over Marguerite's glass.

"Please," she said, and covered the rim briefly with her fingertips — an old habit, a gesture he hadn't seen in ten years and recognized the way you recognize a song from your childhood playing in a stranger's shop.

The wine was good. It was also irrelevant. Edmund drank it and tasted nothing but the room.

"So," Callum said, settling onto the sawhorse bench with the ease of a man making himself at home in any cellar on earth, "how long has this place been in the family?"

"Three generations. My grandfather planted the south slope in '61. My father ran it into the ground slowly enough that it looked like dignity." Edmund turned his glass by the stem. "Then he died, the bank sent letters, and I discovered I'd inherited forty hectares, a farmhouse, and a number with a minus sign in front of it."

"And you stayed."

"I stayed."

"Why?"

It was a fair question, the kind a business partner asks, and Edmund had a rehearsed answer — something about terroir and legacy and the valley's untapped potential — that he delivered at trade fairs and to bank managers. He opened his mouth to deliver it.

"It's the only place I know how to be," he said instead.

The lantern flickered. Somewhere above them the wind took a long drag on the house and let it go.

Marguerite was looking at him across the table. In the low light her eyes were almost black, and there was something in her expression he couldn't parse — not pity, not nostalgia, something more uncomfortable than either. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone hearing their own secret said aloud by another mouth.

"That's rarer than you'd think," Callum said into the pause, smoothly, filling it the way he filled every pause, "and worth more than most people realize. Passion doesn't show up on a balance sheet, but it shows up in the glass. Eventually." He raised his glass an inch. "To eventually."

"To eventually," Marguerite echoed.

Edmund drank. Under the table, his boot found a loose stone in the floor and worried at it, an old nervous habit he'd thought he'd outgrown.

"Tell me about the vintages you're planning," Callum said. "If the contract goes through. Walk me through it — what do you put in those barrels first?"

And this, at least, was safe ground. This was the country Edmund had mapped obsessively for five years, every contour memorized. He talked. Tempranillo from the south slope, because the heat units were right and the old vines were still sound if you pruned them mercifully. A white from the north terrace his grandfather had always said was wasted on red. Small batches. Honest wine. Nothing clever until the estate could afford to be clever.

He talked for ten minutes and Marguerite listened, chin propped on her hand, and twice she asked questions — precise, technical, better than polite — and each time her voice came out of the dark it landed somewhere under his ribs.

"She knows wine," Callum observed, with a smile that was either proud or probing. "Don't let her modesty fool you. She grew up around vineyards."

"Do I?" Marguerite said lightly. "I mostly remember the smells. Sulfur and wet dog. Every vineyard I've ever been in smelled like sulfur and wet dog."

"This one smells like stone and empty promises," Edmund said, and immediately wished he'd phrased it differently, because Marguerite laughed — really laughed, head tipped back, the sound bouncing off the low ceiling — and Callum looked between them with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't enjoy.

"Wet dog," Callum repeated, deadpan. "That's going in the marketing materials. *Estate-bottled, notes of sulfur and wet dog.*"

"You'd sell it," Marguerite said. "You could sell anything."

"I intend to," Callum said, and clinked his glass against hers, and the sound was bright and small in the big dark room.

* * *

Later — an hour, perhaps two; time moved strangely underground, in candlelight, in company — Callum climbed the stairs to fetch his phone from the coat he'd left in the hall, wanting to check whether the pass report had updated. His footsteps faded overhead. Floorboards announced his progress room by room, faithful as a narrator.

The cellar went quiet.

Edmund began collecting glasses because his hands wanted occupation. Marguerite rose from the bench and drifted along the barrel rows again, trailing her fingertips along the staves, one after another after another, a slow scale played on wood. He watched her sideways while pretending to stack glassware that was already stacked.

"You keep it well," she said. "The cellar. Better than—" She stopped herself.

"Better than the rest of it," he finished. "Yes. Well. It's the part I understand."

"You always did." She turned, and the lantern light made a small gold world around her, and outside that circle the barrels went on into the dark like a congregation. "You used to say the cellar was the only honest room in the winery. Everything upstairs is theater — the tasting room, the labels, the stories. Down here it's just..." She searched for the word.

"Waiting," Edmund said.

"Just waiting," she agreed softly.

He set down the glass he was holding. He was aware of his own heartbeat, absurdly loud, a drum in a quiet church. There were things in his throat that had been there ten years, packed down like sediment, and the whole evening they had been loosening, particle by particle, stirred by her voice, her laugh, her hand on his father's barrels.

"Margo." The old name was out before he could stop it. Her head came up sharply. Nobody had called her that in a decade; he could see her deciding whether to flinch. "Why did you come on this trip?"

The question hung there. Above them, faintly, Callum's footsteps crossed the kitchen and stopped.

"For the wine," Marguerite said.

"Right," Edmund said.

"Edmund—"

"It's fine." He meant it to sound fine. It came out sounding like a door closing carefully so as not to wake someone. "You don't owe me an answer. You never did. I've just—" He picked up the rag on the table and wiped a spotless stretch of wood. "I've wondered. For a long time. That's all."

Overhead, the floorboards creaked. Callum, standing in the kitchen, listening to a silence he couldn't hear the contents of but could certainly hear the weight of.

Marguerite looked at him across the rows of empty barrels — six thousand liters of capacity, not one drop of courage in the room — and whatever she was about to say, or not say, was interrupted by the sound of Callum's boots starting back toward the stairwell, unhurried, deliberate, a man giving them fair warning of his approach.

"We should talk about the contract terms tomorrow," Edmund said loudly, to the whole cellar, to the past, to whoever was listening. "While the roads are closed. No hurry anywhere."

"Good idea," Callum's voice floated down the stairs ahead of him, pleasant as ever. "Nothing like a blizzard for due diligence."

He came down into the lantern light carrying his phone and his smile, and glanced — briefly, professionally, the glance of a man taking inventory — from Edmund to Marguerite to the barrel her hand still rested on, and Edmund watched him file it away, wherever men like Callum filed the things they noticed.

"More wine?" Callum asked cheerfully. "I feel like we're finally getting somewhere."
