## 10. Harvest Looms

The phone rang at seven in the morning, which meant it was either bad news or Abuela Rosa, and in this family the two categories overlapped more often than anyone admitted.

Mirabel let it ring twice while she stood at the kitchen counter with her coffee going cold and her laptop open to a document she hadn't touched in nine days. Nine days since the platform. Nine days since she'd watched the last train pull out of the little coastal station with Theo Marchetti inside it, both of them performing relief so badly that a stranger waiting on the bench had actually laughed.

Nine days of blank pages.

She picked up on the third ring because she knew better than to make Abuela Rosa wait for a fourth.

"Mi nieta." Her grandmother's voice came through warm and slightly windblown, as though she were already outside among the vines, which at seven in the morning during the week before harvest, she absolutely was. "You're awake."

"I'm awake."

"You sound guilty."

"I sound like I just woke up."

"You sound like someone who has been avoiding my calls for nine days and is now caught." A pause, the particular pause of a woman who had raised four children and outlasted two husbands and did not lose negotiations. "Good. I need you."

Mirabel leaned against the counter. Through the window above the sink she could see the lighthouse, white and patient at the end of its spit of land, the way it had been patient her whole life. She'd been looking at it differently lately. Everything, lately, she'd been looking at differently.

"Harvest starts Saturday," Abuela Rosa said. "We're short-handed. Your cousins are useless—Marisol has the baby, Daniel threw his back out moving furniture he shouldn't have been moving alone, and Tomás claims he has a conference, which means a woman whose name he won't tell me."

"That's very on brand for Tomás."

"Don't defend him. I need hands. I need you, and I need—" and here her grandmother's voice took on the specific brightness that Mirabel had learned, over thirty years, to fear, "—that nice young man. The archivist. Theo."

Mirabel set down her coffee cup with exaggerated care, as if the saucer might shatter if her hand betrayed even a tremor.

"Abuela."

"He was lovely," Abuela Rosa said, steamrolling directly through the warning in Mirabel's voice. "He helped me carry the chairs. He asked about the malbec block like he actually wanted to know the answer. And when I told him about your grandfather keeping the light through the '62 storm, he listened. Do you know how rare it is, mi nieta, to find a young man who listens?"

"He was being polite."

"He was being *interested*." Another pause, shorter, sharper. "And then he left on Sunday night like Cinderella, and you came back to the house looking like someone had unplugged you from the wall, and you have not called me since, and you think I don't notice things."

"I've been working."

"On what? You haven't sent me a chapter in three weeks. You used to send me chapters like other people send postcards. Now nothing. So either you're writing something so brilliant it can't be shared, or you're writing nothing at all, and I know which one it is because I know you."

Mirabel closed her eyes. This was the problem with grandmothers who had actually read all your books: they could do this. They could find the exact seam in you and press it.

"The reunion weekend was a lot," she said finally, which was true, and also a lie, because what it had been was the best forty-eight hours she'd had in years, followed by the worst nine days, and she was not going to say any of that out loud on a Tuesday morning to a woman standing in a vineyard with dirt under her nails and X-ray vision.

"A lot," Abuela Rosa repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. Well. Harvest is also a lot, and we do it anyway. Saturday, six a.m., both of you. Tell your young man."

"He's not my—you can't just—"

"Six a.m.," said Abuela Rosa, and hung up.

* * *

Mirabel stood in her kitchen for a long time after the call ended, holding the phone like it might detonate.

Here was the thing she had not told anyone, including herself, in any language she could bear: she had thought about texting Theo every single day for nine days. She had composed the messages. That was the humiliating part—not the wanting, but the drafting. She was a novelist; she drafted everything; she could not stop the machine in her head that turned every impulse into seventeen revisions before it ever reached air.

*Hi, random question, do you like grapes?* No. Idiotic.

*My grandmother has requested your presence at a mandatory agricultural event.* Too formal. Sounded like a summons from a small hostile nation.

*The vineyard needs volunteers and apparently you made an impression.* Better. True. But it led, inevitably, to the question underneath, which was: *and I thought about you every day and I don't know what to do about that,* and she was not sending that one. She would die before she sent that one.

Because here was the other thing, the thing she resented with a clean bright fury: the rules had worked. That was the unbearable truth. They had written their rules on a napkin—no real feelings, no backstory questions, leave before the last train—and she had followed them, and he had followed them, and the train had left on schedule, and she had gone home to an empty house exactly as designed, and the system had functioned flawlessly.

And it had felt like amputation.

She kept thinking about dessert. Not the flan, though the flan had been excellent. She kept thinking about the moment Marisol's toddler had put an entire grape in his nose and the whole table dissolved, and Theo had laughed—not his archive laugh, the polite contained one, but a real one, unguarded, head tipped back—and Mirabel had looked at him across her grandmother's long table surrounded by her loud impossible family and felt something crack open in her chest that she had spent six years welding shut.

She'd sworn off being known. That was the vow. After Daniel—after the betrayal she still couldn't say aloud even in the privacy of her own skull—she had trained herself the way athletes train, daily, disciplined, no skipped sessions. Don't tell them the real thing. Don't let them close enough to use it. Edit yourself out of the photograph before anyone else gets the chance. It had worked for six years. It had kept her safe and whole and alone, and she had told herself those were the same thing.

Then one archivist with ink-stained fingers and a way of listening like your words mattered had walked into her research archive, and the training had fallen apart in a single weekend.

So no. She was not going to text him. She was going to call her grandmother back and explain that Theo had returned to the city, that whatever the reunion weekend was, it had concluded per the agreed terms, that Mirabel Quintana did not chase—

Her phone buzzed in her hand.

She looked down.

It was a text from her cousin Lucía, a photo of the crush pad being hosed down, captioned *pray for us*, and Mirabel exhaled and almost laughed at herself, at the jolt of stupid hope that had fired through her chest at a buzz, at the fact that some traitorous part of her had seen *Theo Marchetti* flash behind her eyes before she'd even looked.

That was the moment she understood she was in trouble. Not because she missed him—missing was manageable, missing was a muscle too, she could train that—but because her body had started answering phones before her mind gave permission. Containment required consent between the self and the impulses. Hers had collapsed.

She put the phone face-down on the counter. She opened her laptop. She looked at the blank document, cursor blinking like a lighthouse with nothing to guide home.

She lasted eleven minutes.

* * *

The text she finally sent was not any of her drafts. Drafts were for people with composure. What she sent, thumbs moving fast before the machine in her head could intervene, was:

*My grandmother has mobilized. Harvest starts Saturday, 6am, and you have been formally summoned by name. She used the phrase "that nice young man" twice. I want you to understand the gravity of what you've gotten yourself into.*

She hit send and immediately held the phone away from her body like it was a live coal.

Casual. Good. It was casual. It was funny-casual, the kind of message you'd send to a person who was nothing to you, a colleague, a guy from the archive. There was nothing in it that a reasonable person could read as *I have thought about you for nine days and the silence has been eating me alive.*

She set the phone down. She picked it up. She put it in the other room.

This was absurd. She was thirty years old. She had published two novels. She had survived the worst thing a heart could survive and built a life on the ruins with load-bearing walls and everything. She did not pace kitchens over text messages.

She went back into the kitchen.

Nothing. Four minutes. Five. Fine. He was an archivist; archivists worked; he was probably elbow-deep in some mislabeled box of 1950s correspondence, gloriously happy, not thinking about her at all, because why would he be, because the rules had been *his* idea as much as hers, because men like Theo Marchetti—careful, quiet, self-contained—did not spend nine days composing drafts they didn't send.

Unless.

No. Stop.

She was rinsing her coffee cup for the second time for no reason when the phone buzzed.

Eight seconds. Later she would do the math—she had sent it at 8:14 and change, his reply landed at 8:15—and she would realize he had answered in nine seconds, which meant he had been holding his phone, which meant—well. It meant something. She refused to calculate what.

*I accept the summons,* the text read. *But I have conditions.*

She stared at the screen. Then typed: *Conditions.*

*1. I get to actually learn the difference between the grape varieties instead of pretending.*
*2. Nobody tells me the story about the '62 storm again unless I'm allowed to ask follow-up questions.*
*3. You have to show me where you go when you disappear from dinner parties. I noticed, by the way. You're not as subtle as you think.*

Mirabel read the third condition three times.

He'd noticed. Of course he'd noticed. Leaving parties was her signature move, her great art—the slow fade to the porch, the garden, anywhere the noise wasn't. She'd done it at the reunion too, twice, and both times she'd come back and found him watching her with that expression he had, the one that wasn't a question but wasn't not one.

She should deflect. She had a whole arsenal of deflections, professionally sharpened. Instead her thumbs wrote:

*Condition accepted. But fair warning: the place I disappear to is usually the roof of the equipment shed, and it requires climbing, and if you fall Abuela Rosa will never forgive either of us.*

*Then I'll be careful,* he wrote. *I'm good with fragile things.*

She put the phone down on the counter, gently, and pressed both palms flat against the cool stone, and breathed.

There it was again. That thing he did—said eight words, harmless words, and somehow got under the armor like water through stone. *I'm good with fragile things.* He restored damaged letters for a living. He handled paper older than the vineyard, torn and water-stained and half-destroyed, and he coaxed them back into legibility without tearing them further. She had watched him do it once through the archive window, hunched under a lamp with tweezers and infinite patience, and she had thought: *that's a man who believes broken things are worth the work.*

She had decided, right then, that she liked him. She had decided it the way you decide to step off a ledge—without permission from the part of you that knows better.

Now she stood in her kitchen with his nine-second reply glowing on the counter and admitted the rest of it, privately, in the locked room of her own head where she kept the things she'd never say: she didn't want him to come for the harvest. She wanted him to come for her. She wanted six a.m. among the vines and the shed roof at dusk and the whole golden weekend stretched out ahead, and she wanted it so badly it frightened her, because wanting was the first step of the fall, and she had sworn, she had *vowed*, after Daniel, that she would never again hand someone the power to gut her.

And yet her thumbs had already typed the next message:

*Saturday. 6am. Wear shoes you don't love.*

His reply came in four seconds this time, and it was just a single emoji—a bunch of grapes—and she laughed out loud, alone in her kitchen, a real laugh, the kind she hadn't managed in nine days, and the sound of it startled her like a stranger's voice in the house.

* * *

Friday night she packed, and packing became its own confession.

She caught herself holding up two sweaters—one practical, one the soft blue one that her cousin Elena had once said brought out her eyes—and realized she was dressing for him, and put both back, and then packed the blue one anyway, because lying to her family was one thing but lying to herself mid-sweater was a bridge too far.

At the bottom of her bag, folded into her notebook, went the napkin. She'd kept it—of course she'd kept it, she kept everything, that was half her problem. *RULES OF ENGAGEMENT,* in Theo's careful archivist print, her own additions scrawled beneath in purple ink. *No real feelings. No backstory questions. Leave before the last train Sunday.*

Every rule broken or about to be. No real feelings—she'd failed that by dessert. Leave before the last train—he'd left, she'd stayed, and the leaving had hollowed her out so thoroughly that she'd spent nine days staring at a blank page. And now she was inviting him back into the exact situation the rules were built to contain, with no reunion as cover, no fiction as scaffolding. Just harvest. Just work. Just the two of them among the vines with her entire family watching.

It was a terrible idea. She knew it was a terrible idea the way you know weather is coming—pressure behind the eyes, the dogs going quiet.

She packed the napkin anyway.

Before bed she checked the weather, out of habit, because harvest people checked weather the way sailors did. Clear through Tuesday, the app promised. Blue skies, mild nights, perfect picking conditions.

She almost believed it.

Almost—because out the window, past the dark shape of the hills, the lighthouse beam swept its old steady arc across the water, and she thought about her grandfather's logs stacked in the tower, and about promises, and about how the sea around here had a long memory and no respect whatsoever for forecasts.

She turned off the light. In the dark, her phone lit up one more time on the nightstand.

*One more condition,* Theo had written. *This weekend, when you disappear—I want to be the one who comes looking.*

Mirabel lay in the dark with her heart going like a bird against a window, and did not answer, and did not sleep, and did not delete it either.
