## 11. Second Weekend, Unscripted

### Mirabel

There were no rules this time. That was the first thing Mirabel noticed, standing at the edge of the vineyard at seven in the morning with dirt already under her fingernails and Theo Marchetti three rows over, sleeves rolled to the elbow, holding pruning shears like he was afraid of them.

The first weekend had been a production. Costumes, essentially. She'd coached him on her family—Uncle Emilio's politics, Cousin Dani's interrogation techniques, Abuela Rosa's sixth sense for nonsense. They'd rehearsed in her kitchen with index cards. *How did you two meet?* had a scripted answer, polished until it shone.

This weekend, she'd sent him a text that said only: *Harvest prep. Bring boots you don't love. There will be mud.*

He'd replied in nine seconds, and she'd resented how fast her heart had answered.

Now the vineyard stretched gold and green under a sky scrubbed clean by last night's wind, and Theo was learning that pruning was not, in fact, just cutting things.

"You're murdering that vine," she called over.

"I am *editing* it," he said, without looking up. "There's a difference. It's a difference archivists understand."

"You're cutting the fruiting cane."

He stopped. Looked at the vine. Looked at her. "I was going for *thinning*."

"You were going for a massacre." She crossed the row to him, close enough to smell the cut-green scent of the vines and, under it, whatever soap he used that she had absolutely not spent any time cataloguing last weekend. "Here. Like this." She took the shears from his hand—her fingers brushing his knuckles, an accident she did not correct—and angled the blade above a bud. "You cut here, at an angle, so the water runs off. You leave two buds. You're not punishing the vine, Theo. You're telling it where to grow."

He was quiet for a moment. "That's either viticulture or a metaphor."

"Could be both."

"Which one are you doing right now?"

She handed the shears back and stepped away before her face could answer for her. "Work, Marchetti."

He worked. And here was the thing she kept noticing, the thing that made the morning feel like walking on a frozen lake and listening for cracks: he was *good* at this. Not the pruning—he was hopeless—but at the being-there. He didn't fill silence with noise. He didn't ask questions she'd have to dodge. When her back ached and she sat down hard on the row's end post, he sat down too, a careful arm's length away, and said nothing at all, and the nothing was so comfortable it frightened her.

"You know," he said eventually, "last weekend I had a script."

"I know. I wrote it."

"And this weekend there's no script."

"No."

"So what I say right now is off the record."

Mirabel looked at him. His profile against the brightening sky, the small scar through one eyebrow she'd noticed the first day in the archive and never asked about—no backstory questions, that had been the rule, and the rule was dead now, they'd both killed it without a funeral.

"Off the record," she said.

"I'm having a better time than I've had in years." He said it to the vines, not to her. "I wanted you to know that. In case you were keeping score."

She was keeping score. She'd been keeping score since the platform, since the last train pulled out and left her with a blank document and an ache she'd diagnosed, correctly, as missing him. She opened her mouth to say something equally honest and equally dangerous—

"¡MIRABEL!" Dani's voice carried across four rows like a weather system. "Is the archivist surviving, or are you still teaching him which end of the plant is up?"

"The end with the grapes," Theo called back, deadpan, and Dani's laugh rang off the hills.

Mirabel stood, grateful and ungrateful at once. "Come on. If we don't show up at breakfast, she'll come find us, and she's worse at a distance."

"Is she?"

"Dani once followed a boyfriend of mine to the bathroom to ask if he was 'serious about the family.' He was not. He moved to Portugal."

Theo fell into step beside her, and their hands brushed—once, twice, a third time that lasted a half-second too long to be accidental—and neither of them moved away, and Mirabel spent the whole walk to the farmhouse thinking about the difference between an accident and a decision.

* * *

### Theo

The farmhouse kitchen smelled like bread and woodsmoke and coffee that could strip paint, and Theo understood, sitting at the long table with flour on his forearms because Abuela Rosa had drafted him into rolling dough without asking his opinion, that he was in trouble.

Not the vineyard kind. The other kind. The kind where a woman laughs with flour on her cheekbone and you think, unhelpfully and permanently, *oh.*

He'd come back for the harvest. That was the official version, and it was even true, in the way that a label is true of what's in the box. But the fuller truth—the one he'd written into a letter and not sent, the one sitting folded in his bag in the guest room—was that he'd spent six days in the city and had thought about her approximately every eleven minutes, which he'd calculated on the train, which told him something about how he was spending his attention, which was the currency, his mother used to say, that tells you what you love.

He didn't finish that thought. He rolled dough instead. Abuela Rosa watched him with the expression of a woman reading a book she'd already read and enjoying it anyway.

"You knead like a man thinking about something else," she said, in Spanish-accented English that tolerated no nonsense.

"Is that bad?"

"It depends what you're thinking about." She tapped his dough. "Too much. Again."

Mirabel came in with a basket of eggs, hair escaping its braid, and the whole kitchen reorganized itself around her without her permission, and Theo wondered if she knew. She probably didn't. Containment, she'd called it once, half-joking, the theory that a heart was a muscle you could train into stillness. He'd wanted to say: muscles remember. That's the whole point of training them. One day you reach for something and the old motion is still there, waiting.

"Abuela," Mirabel said, setting down the basket, "why is Theo rolling dough?"

"Because you were taking too long."

"I was *harvesting*."

"You were talking." Abuela Rosa's eyes were shrewd and warm and entirely without mercy. "Talking is also harvest. Different crop."

Dani arrived then, in a flurry of cold air and commentary, dropped into the chair beside Theo, and looked at him with the frank assessment of a customs officer. "So. Weekend two."

"Dani," Mirabel said, in a warning tone.

"I'm just making conversation."

"You're conducting an audit."

"Same thing." Dani turned back to Theo. "Last weekend you were very polished. Very rehearsed. 'We met at the coastal archive, she knocked a box of letters off a shelf, I caught them, it was cinematic.'" Dani shrugged. "Very nice. Very fake."

"Dani—"

"No, let me finish, this is a compliment." Dani leaned in. "This weekend you're terrible at pruning. You let Abuela boss you around. You laugh at her jokes even though half of them are in Portuguese and you don't speak Portuguese. You're *worse*, is what I'm saying. You've gotten worse at performing."

Theo glanced at Mirabel, who had gone very still by the counter, an egg in each hand like she'd forgotten what eggs were for.

"And that," Dani said, sitting back, satisfied, "is how I know you two are disgustingly real."

The kitchen went quiet except for the tick of the old clock and the sound of bread cooling. Theo felt the truth of it land in his chest like a dropped stone—*disgustingly real*—and watched Mirabel's face do something quick and complicated, a whole weather system crossing it in two seconds: panic, denial, and then something softer that she shut down before it finished arriving.

"He's an archivist," Mirabel said finally, to the eggs. "He's good with damaged things."

"That's not a denial," Dani said.

"It's not a confirmation either."

"It's a Mirabel," Abuela Rosa said, and the old woman laughed, and Theo laughed, and Mirabel—cornered, floured, beautiful—cracked a smile that she tried very hard to make look like surrender and did not entirely achieve.

* * *

### Mirabel

The afternoon broke them into crews: some to the north rows to lay out harvest bins, some to the barn to service the old press. Mirabel and Theo got assigned the far slope, the oldest vines, the ones her grandfather had planted the year the lighthouse was decommissioned—as if, she'd always thought, he needed something else to keep alive.

They worked through the gold part of the afternoon into the amber part, and the conversation went where conversations go when nobody's steering it.

"My mother used to say the old vines make the best wine," Mirabel said, tying back a cane, "because they've stopped trying to impress anyone. They just make what they make."

"Your mother says a lot of true things, I think."

"You've known her one weekend."

"I've known you two weekends," Theo said, "and you quote her like scripture."

Mirabel's hands stilled on the tie. It was true. She quoted her mother the way exiles quote a country—constantly, longingly, and never to her face. She hadn't let herself see the shape of that until he said it, plain as weather.

"She and I are—" She tried three sentences and discarded all of them. "It's complicated."

"You don't have to explain."

"That's the thing." She turned to look at him, really look, dirt to his elbows, sun in his hair, patience in his whole posture. "You keep saying that. You don't have to explain, you don't have to answer, you don't have to— and I keep almost telling you things anyway. Do you know how annoying that is? I spent years getting good at not telling people things. I'm very good at it. I have a *system*. And you just stand there not asking, and the whole system falls over."

Theo was quiet for a moment. A bird crossed the sky above the rows.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I have a system too."

"Does yours work?"

"Mine's better built. But it's failing at the same rate."

She laughed—really laughed, the unedited laugh, the one that last weekend had felt like a door opening and this weekend felt like a door that had never quite closed—and the sound of it seemed to surprise them both. They stood among the old vines, two people with excellent systems in ruins, and Mirabel thought: *this is the moment.* This is the moment where I could tell him about Daniel, about the letter he'd sold to the tabloids, about the six years of editing herself down to a woman with no history and no wounds and no weight. He's right here. The door is open.

"Theo," she started.

He waited. He was so good at waiting. That was the cruelest thing about him.

"The vines on this slope," she said instead, "were the last thing Grandpa Joaquín planted. The same year they turned off the light."

Something flickered across his face—not disappointment exactly. Recalibration. "The lighthouse," he said. "The one from the logs."

"You've read them."

"Professionally." A half-smile. "They're in my care, technically. Along with a box of correspondence I haven't finished cataloguing."

"From the decommission?"

"From the decommission." He said it lightly, but there was a weight under the lightness, a stone under snow, and Mirabel—who was expert in stones under snow—heard it and filed it away in the growing drawer of things she wasn't asking about.

"Come on," she said. "I want to show you something before the light goes."

* * *

### Theo

She led him up the field path as the sun dropped, past the barn, past the stone wall, to the rise where the old lighthouse stood white and blind against the darkening east. The vineyard fell away below them, rows running to the sea like lines on a page.

"My grandfather kept this light for thirty-one years," Mirabel said. "The last night before the decommission, the whole town came out. And he made a promise. That the light would never really go out. That as long as any Quintana was here, something would burn." She shrugged, self-conscious. "Family legend. Probably embroidered."

"Most legends are," Theo said. "That doesn't make them false. It makes them load-bearing."

She looked at him. "That's a very archivist thing to say."

"It's a very true thing to say that I've disguised as an archivist thing."

The wind came up off the water. She hugged her arms, and he took off his jacket and held it out, and she looked at it for a long moment—the jacket, the offer, the whole quiet grammar of it—and took it.

And Theo stood there in his rolled-up sleeves in the cooling air and understood that the fiction was over. Had been over, probably, since the platform, since the flan, since the first time she'd laughed at something he hadn't rehearsed. There was no fake date anymore. There was just a woman in his jacket on a hill she loved, telling him the story of a promise, and him memorizing it the way he memorized everything that mattered, which was the problem, which had always been the problem, because he was keeping a drawer of his own locked—his mother's name, his brother's silence, the letter in his bag—and you couldn't ask someone to open their doors while you stood in the hallway of yours.

"Mirabel," he said.

"Don't," she said, but gently, looking out at the water. "Not yet. I'm almost ready. I'm— I need it to be the right moment, and I keep almost making it the right moment, and then I get scared, and I show you a vineyard instead."

"The vineyard's a very good vineyard."

She laughed, wet-eyed. "It is, isn't it."

They walked down in the blue dusk, and at the wall by the barn she stopped him. "Tomorrow," she said. "Tomorrow I'll tell you. The real thing. Not the edited version."

"Okay," Theo said. "Tomorrow."

He meant it. He'd have told her everything tomorrow too—had already, in his head, begun drafting the confession he'd failed to mail, his brother's name, the estrangement, the whole architecture of his own silence.

He didn't know, then, that tomorrow would arrive early, and sideways, in the form of a letter in a mislabeled box—a letter about her grandfather's promise, and about her mother, and about a truth that had been waiting thirty years in the archive for someone patient enough to read it.

He didn't know that by tomorrow night, the question would no longer be whether to tell her his secret.

It would be whether he could survive telling her hers.
