## 13. First Harvest Morning

The alarm went off at four-forty, and Mirabel was already awake.

She'd been awake since three, actually, lying in her childhood bed listening to the vineyard breathe—the irrigation lines ticking, a dog barking two properties over, the particular silence of a place that had never once needed her to perform for it. She'd forgotten, over the years she'd stayed away, how loud the quiet was here. How it asked nothing of you and somehow gave everything back.

Theo was sleeping in the guest room down the hall. Or he wasn't sleeping; she had no idea. That was the strange new fact of her life: there was a man in her mother's house whose sleep schedule she could not account for, and instead of finding this alarming, she found herself lying in the dark wondering about it. Wondering if he lay awake too, cataloging the sounds of a family that wasn't his, rehearsing whatever version of himself he planned to wear into the day.

She'd texted him the harvest call time last week—*5:15, dress in things you don't love*—and he'd replied with a single thumbs-up, which she'd stared at for an embarrassing length of time trying to decode. Nine seconds, that first reply after Abuela Rosa's invitation. He was getting faster. They were both getting faster. It was becoming a problem she refused to name.

By five, the kitchen smelled like coffee and pan dulce, and her mother was already at the counter in rubber boots, slicing oranges with the efficiency of a woman who had done this every September of her life and would do it every September until the vines outlived her.

"You're up," Elena Quintana said, without turning around. "You used to sleep through harvest. Your father had to carry you to the truck."

"I was nine."

"You were twenty-two." Her mother set a knife down and looked at her then, really looked, the way she did maybe twice a year—a look that went through the sweater and the smile and the carefully arranged face underneath. "You look different this year."

"I'm thirty. Thirty looks different."

"Mm." Elena slid a plate of orange slices across the counter. "Eat. The boy will need feeding too. Theo." She said the name like she was testing its weight. "He's coming?"

"He said he'd be here."

"Did he." Not a question. Her mother turned back to the oranges, and Mirabel stood there holding a plate of fruit like evidence in a trial she hadn't been told about.

* * *

The east rows were silver when they got to them, fog still pooled in the low ground between the vines, and the whole Quintana operation was already scattered through it like notes on a staff: Tía Carmen calling orders nobody followed, cousin Marco on the tractor making far more noise than necessary, Abuela Rosa moving down Row 12 with a pair of clippers and the unhurried authority of a woman who had personally selected every cutting in this vineyard since 1974.

Abuela Rosa spotted Theo before anyone else did.

She stopped mid-row, looked him up and down once—he was wearing flannel Mirabel had bought him at the hardware store, sleeves rolled, hair pushed back, looking less like an archivist and more like someone who belonged to a place—and said something in rapid Spanish that made Carmen laugh so hard she had to lean on the trellis wire.

"What did she say?" Mirabel asked, catching up.

"She said I have good hands." Theo held up his own, faintly bewildered. "For the grapes, I think. I hope."

"It's never just about the grapes with her. What else?"

He hesitated. "Something about dessert."

Mirabel felt her ears go warm. "We're not discussing dessert."

"We discussed dessert extensively at dinner Tuesday."

"That was *your* doing—"

"Mirabel!" Marco bellowed from the tractor. "Is this him? Is this the archivist?" He pronounced it *ar-kee-VOOR-ist*, with relish. "Carmen says you met him among dead people's mail. Very romantic. Like a museum ghost."

"The letters weren't dead people's—"

"They were definitely dead people's letters, Mira." Marco grinned, jumped down, and stuck out a hand to Theo. "Marco. I'm the handsome one. You'll figure out the family hierarchy fast because everyone will tell you they're the handsome one and none of them are."

"I'm the handsome one," said Carmen, arriving.

"See? Fast learner," Marco said, and Theo laughed—openly, easily, the laugh of someone who had decided to stop being careful—and something in Mirabel's chest turned over and refused to right itself.

They picked.

That was the whole morning, and it was everything. The fog burned off by seven and the sun came through the rows in long gold blades, and the family worked the way families work when they've done a thing ten thousand times—half competence, half chaos, all of it underlaid with talk. Arguments about whether the Pinot block should've come off last week. Remonstrations about proper clipper technique. Abuela Rosa correcting everyone's grip and letting Theo's slide entirely because, apparently, he had *good hands*.

And Theo fit.

That was the thing Mirabel couldn't stop watching, even while her own hands moved through the canopy on muscle memory older than her grief. He fit the way water fits a glass—no performance, no strain, just taking the shape of what surrounded him. He learned Marco's trash-talk cadence within an hour. He let Carmen load him with buckets until his forearms shook and thanked her for it. When little Sofía, six years old and officially Too Young For Harvest But Present Anyway, appointed herself his supervisor, he crouched down in the dirt and listened to her entire lecture on grape selection with the gravity of a man receiving state secrets.

"You're very good at this," Mirabel said, when Sofía finally released him.

"At picking grapes?"

"At being *here*. At—" She gestured vaguely at the whole golden, noisy, impossible scene. "This is my family. They're a lot. Most people need a weekend and a debrief."

Theo looked down at his bucket, at the fruit stacked dark and heavy in it, and when he looked back up there was something guarded behind his eyes again, a door closing partway. "Maybe I'm practicing," he said.

"For what?"

"For a family I might want someday." And then, before she could ask anything, before she could even decide whether she wanted to ask anything: "Your grandmother says the old vines on the north slope are your grandfather's. Show me?"

It was deflection. She knew deflection; she'd written whole chapters of it. But she let him have it, because her chest was still doing the thing, and because the north slope meant her grandfather, and her grandfather meant the lighthouse logs, and those were waters she could navigate.

* * *

At mid-morning the crew broke for agua fresca and tamales in the shade of the equipment barn, and Mirabel sat on a hay bale between Theo and Sofía, sticky-handed, sun-drunk, laughing at Marco's impression of Tío Rafael arguing with the weather app—and it happened.

Her mother looked at them.

Not glanced. *Looked.* From across the barn, standing with the thermos, Elena Quintana watched her daughter sitting shoulder-to-shoulder with a stranger, watched Mirabel laugh with her whole face unguarded, watched Theo say something quietly to Sofía that made the child beam—and her expression did something Mirabel had no word for.

It wasn't suspicion. It wasn't warmth either. It was recognition. It was the look of a woman seeing a shape she'd seen before, long ago, in a mirror or a photograph or a younger version of herself—and grieving it, or bracing against it, or both.

Then Elena caught Mirabel watching her, and the expression folded itself away, smooth as a page turning, and she smiled and raised the thermos in a small salute, and everything was normal again.

Except it wasn't.

Because Mirabel knew that look. She'd spent eleven years perfecting the art of unreadable faces; she could read one in her sleep. And what she'd just seen on her mother's face wasn't a mother sizing up a daughter's boyfriend.

It was a woman who already knew something.

*You keep editing yourself out of the photographs,* her mother had said once, years ago, in a fight they'd never finished. *Every Christmas card, every reunion, you stand at the edge like you're waiting for permission to leave.*

Mirabel had thought it was a complaint. Sitting on the hay bale now, watching her mother pour coffee for Tía Carmen with steady hands and that same closed, knowing calm, Mirabel understood—with a cold drop through the center of her—that it had been a diagnosis. Her mother had known. Maybe not the details, maybe not the name of the person who'd broken her, but the shape of it. The architecture of the retreat. Eleven years of Sunday phone calls where Mirabel gave the weather and the word count and nothing else, and her mother had sat on the other end of the line, holding the silence, *letting* her hide.

Why?

What did her mother know that she had never said?

"Hey." Theo's voice, low, beside her. "You went somewhere."

"Nowhere," Mirabel said, and heard how practiced it sounded, and hated it, and couldn't fix it—not here, not with Sofía leaning against her arm and the whole family twenty feet away and the truth sitting in her throat like a stone she'd swallowed at nineteen and never passed.

Theo studied her. He didn't push. That was the trouble with him—he never pushed, which meant every inch he gained, she'd given him, and she was starting to give him inches she couldn't account for.

Across the barn, her mother set down the thermos and crossed to them, wiping her hands on her apron.

"Mirabel. A word inside, when the picking's done." Her tone was light. Harvest-light. Nothing-light. "There's something we should discuss."

"About the crush schedule?"

"No," her mother said, and looked at Theo—kindly, steadily, in a way that made him go very still—"not about the crush schedule."

She walked back toward the house, and Mirabel sat in the sun with her heart going hard, feeling the day tilt beneath her like a deck shifting at sea.

Beside her, Theo said quietly, "Mirabel. What does she know?"

And Mirabel, looking at the north slope where her grandfather's old vines stood in their long gold rows, heard herself answer with the only honest words she had left:

"I think she's known all along."
