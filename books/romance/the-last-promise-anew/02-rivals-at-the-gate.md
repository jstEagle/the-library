## 2. Rivals at the Gate

The road to the Sandoval vineyard—though he did not yet know it was hers—climbed through the valley like a sentence being written slowly, each curve adding a clause to the landscape: first the poplars, then the river's silver seam, then the terraced rows rising in dark green arithmetic against the hillside. Elias Thorne drove with the window down despite the October chill, because the air here smelled the way he remembered it from twenty years ago, when he had been young enough to believe that a life could be planned like a symphony and executed without a single wrong note.

He had come to buy a vineyard.

That was what he told himself, anyway, as his car took the last switchback and the farmhouse appeared below him, shuttered and patient, its stone walls the color of old honey. The parcel was listed simply as *Domaine de l'Aube*—the Estate of Dawn—a name that struck him now, as he read it again on the agent's dossier folded on the passenger seat, as either an accident of history or a joke the universe had been waiting two decades to tell.

The seller was listed only by initials. *Z.S.*

He had assumed a widower. A retired banker, perhaps, someone who had bought the land for tax reasons and let the vines go feral out of indifference. He had prepared himself for neglect: broken trellises, mildew in the rows, a cellar that smelled of regret and standing water. What he had not prepared himself for was the order of the place—the vines pruned with something close to tenderness, the gravel raked, the late roses along the wall still blooming stubbornly against the season, red as spilled wine.

Someone loved this ground. That was the first thing he felt, before he even stepped out of the car, and it unsettled him more than any disrepair could have. Land that is loved does not sell easily. Land that is loved has a way of knowing who comes for it, and why.

Elias stood beside his car for a long moment, breathing the cold mineral air, listening to the wind move through the rows with a sound like distant applause—or like a hall emptying after a performance, which was a sound he had never stopped hearing, in one form or another, since the night everything ended.

Then the door of the farmhouse opened, and the universe finished telling its joke.

She came out carrying a basket of cuttings, her hair bound back carelessly, a smear of earth along one forearm, and for three full seconds Elias Thorne—who had negotiated across tables in four countries, who had stared down men twice his age over contracts worth fortunes—could not speak at all.

Zephyrine Sandoval.

The name arrived in him like a stone dropped into still water, and the rings went outward and outward. She was older, of course; they both were. Her face had been carved by weather instead of stage lights, and there were lines around her eyes that had not been there at twenty-four, and her hands—her hands, those hands that had made audiences weep in Vienna, in Prague, in that final glittering hall whose name he still could not say aloud—those hands were stained green at the fingertips and rough at the knuckles, and they held grape cuttings the way she had once held a bow, with an authority so complete it looked like rest.

She saw him. He watched the recognition cross her face the way frost crosses a window—not all at once but in a slow crystalline bloom—and he watched her choose, deliberately, visibly, to become someone else. Her spine straightened into courtesy. Her mouth arranged itself into the polite neutrality of a businesswoman greeting a stranger.

"Monsieur Thorne," she said. "You're early."

"You know who I am." His voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw by something he refused to name.

"I read the file." She set the basket down on the wall with a small, precise click. "The agent sent ahead your details. Thorne Family Estates. You're expanding south." A pause, thin as a knife's edge. "I didn't realize you knew this valley."

"I grew up an hour from here," he said. "Before Paris. Before—" He stopped himself. Before everything. Before her. "It's been a long time since I came back."

"Has it." It was not a question. She looked past him at the hills, at anything that was not his face, and the wind lifted a loose strand of her hair, and Elias thought absurdly, unbearably, of the way she used to push that same strand behind her ear between movements, a gesture so private he had once believed no one else in the world would ever see it.

Twenty years. Twenty years since the night the strings broke—since the sound that would live in his ears until he died, that wet, splintering crack beneath her bow, followed by another, and another, the instrument coming apart in her hands like something slaughtered—and the audience's confusion curdling into that terrible rustling silence, and Zephyrine standing alone on the stage holding the wreckage of her career while three thousand people waited for her to explain herself, and she could not, because she did not know either.

And he had known. Not then—not in the moment—but soon after. Soon enough that his silence became a choice, renewed daily, for twenty years.

He had told himself a hundred stories about it. That he'd had no proof. That Marguerite Vasseur's confession, delivered years later in a hotel bar in Lyon, half-drunk and weeping, was unreliable. That going to Zephyrine with an accusation against a rival would look like jealousy, like spite, like a lover trying to wound. That the truth would hurt her more than the mystery. Every story had been a brick in the wall he built between himself and the thing he had actually done, which was nothing—which was stand in the wings and watch, and afterward hold her shaking body in a green room that smelled of lilies and panic, and say, *we'll find out what happened*, and then quietly, over months, stop looking.

She had left the city within the year. He had told everyone—including himself—that she needed space. The truth was simpler and uglier: she had left, and he had let her go, because letting her go meant never having to say the sentence that lived under his tongue like a splinter working toward the heart.

*I think I know who did it, and I said nothing, because I was afraid of what it would cost me.*

Now she stood six feet away from him among her own roses, offering him her hand, and the splinter moved.

"Welcome to Domaine de l'Aube," she said, and her hand in his was brief and cool and professional, and if it trembled at all he could not be sure whether it was the wind or twenty years arriving all at once.

* * *

They walked the vineyard together because the inspection required it, and the walk was the strangest hour of Elias's life: two people fluent in each other's oldest silences conducting a conversation conducted entirely in the present tense.

"The merlot is on the north slope," she said. "The soil there is limestone over clay. It drains fast, stresses the vines, concentrates the fruit."

"And the chardonnay?"

"Lower field, near the river. Fatter soil, gentler wines." She moved through the rows without looking at her feet, the way she had once moved through backstage corridors in the dark. "My aunt planted most of it. I've kept her methods. They're unfashionable."

"Unfashionable often means correct."

A ghost of a smile. "You always did like to agree with me in public."

The words landed harder than she probably intended—or exactly as hard as she intended; he could no longer tell with her, could never tell, even at the beginning when he'd convinced himself he could. He looked away, down a row of vines heavy with the last fruit, grapes gone amber-gold in the low sun, and made himself speak about brix levels and canopy management and the drainage problem on the eastern terrace, and she answered in kind, and they were two professionals walking a property, except that every few minutes some small detonation went off in the space between them—a shared reference, an old cadence of speech, the particular way she said *obviously* when she was about to demolish an assumption—and the smoke of those detonations hung in the cold air between them, unacknowledged, acrid, sweet.

At the top of the slope she stopped. Below them the whole valley opened: the river, the village with its church spire, the far hills going blue with distance. The light was doing something shameless to the landscape, gilding everything, forgiving everything.

"My aunt called this the listening place," Zephyrine said. "She said you could hear the whole valley breathe up here, if you stood still long enough."

Elias stood still. He listened. What he heard was the wind in the vines, and beneath it, faint and relentless, the memory of a cello mid-phrase, the opening of the Elgar, the way Zephyrine had played it that last night—so beautifully that the sabotage, when it came, had seemed impossible, obscene, a violation not just of her but of music itself.

"She taught you?" he asked. "The vines?"

"She taught me everything except how to leave." Zephyrine turned to face him, and the professional mask slipped—not entirely, but enough that he saw underneath it the exhaustion of a woman who had spent two decades pretending a wound had healed. "Why this vineyard, Monsieur Thorne? You have estates in three regions. You don't need this one."

"It's a good parcel."

"That isn't an answer."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

The wind moved through the rows below them, thousands of leaves turning silver all at once, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them was the same silence that had filled the green room twenty years ago, after the wreck, when she had sat with the broken instrument across her knees and he had stood in the doorway unable to cross the room, unable to leave it, and finally she had said, without looking up, *Say something, Elias,* and he had said, *We'll find out what happened,* and they had both known, even then, that it was a lie of omission, the first brick.

"I should show you the cellar," she said at last, turning away. "If you're serious about the purchase, you'll want to see the barrels."

* * *

The cellar stairs descended into cool darkness, and the smell hit him before his eyes adjusted: oak and earth and something older, something almost animal, the deep fermentation smell of a place where time is kept honestly. Barrels lined the walls in ranks. And against the far wall, beneath a canvas tarpaulin secured with twine, stood a shape too tall and too narrow for a barrel, a shape his eye recognized before his mind consented to.

An instrument case. A cello case, dust-furred, its clasps oxidized green, leaning against the stone as if placed there by someone who had intended, once, to come back for it.

Zephyrine saw him looking. He watched her entire body change—the shoulders drawing in, the chin lifting, the whole architecture of her reassembling itself into fortification.

"My aunt's," she said. Too quickly. "She played a little. Church music, mostly."

The lie was so transparent it was almost generous—an offering, a signal: *do not ask.* And Elias, who had spent twenty years not asking, heard himself say, with a softness that startled them both:

"Does it still get tuned?"

The question hung in the cellar cold. Somewhere above them, a shutter banged in the wind, once, twice, and went quiet. Zephyrine looked at him for a long moment, and in her eyes he saw the frost finish blooming, complete, opaque—and beneath it, deep down, something moving, something alive and furious and grieving, like a flame seen through ice.

"The tasting room is this way," she said, and walked past him into the dark.

He followed. And behind them, in the corner of the cellar, the case leaned against the wall with its dead green clasps, keeping its promise, keeping its silence, keeping—as he was beginning to understand, with a dread that tasted like hope—its time.

Because tomorrow, according to the schedule the regional committee had circulated that very morning, the third judge for the winter competition would arrive to inspect the estate's entries.

Marguerite Vasseur was due at dawn.
