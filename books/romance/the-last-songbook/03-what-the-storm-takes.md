## 3. What the Storm Takes

The power died at 4:17 in the afternoon, and Petra knew it to the minute because she had been watching the clock the way a prisoner watches a door.

Not that she wanted to leave. The roads were gone — buried under a white roar that turned the windows of the keeper's cottage into something abstract, like frost on the inside of a lens. The sea below was a rumor. She could hear it more than see it, a long animal breathing against the rocks.

What she wanted was for time to stop doing what it was doing, which was thinning. Three days ago there had been twenty years of silence between her and Callum Rennick, and silence was a wall she knew how to live behind. Now the wall had windows in it. Now he sat across her kitchen table by candlelight, his hands wrapped around a mug of tea he wasn't drinking, looking at her with an expression she recognized from her own mirror on bad nights — the look of a person deciding whether to tell the truth or survive another decade without it.

Marisol had retreated to the window seat with a blanket and her phone, which had no signal but which she checked anyway, every few minutes, like a woman counting rosary beads that didn't work.

"The generator will hold," Petra said, mostly to fill the room. "It always does."

"I remember." Callum's voice was low, roughened by two days of storm and confession. "You used to say that about everything. The roof. The boat. Yourself."

"Everything held."

"Did it?" He set the mug down. The ceramic made a small, definite sound against the wood, and in the candlelight his face did something complicated. "Because I've been sitting here for two days trying to work out how to say this, and I keep coming back to the fact that you think I left because I stopped loving you. And I need you to know that's not what happened. That's never what happened."

Petra's hands went still around her pen. She hadn't been writing; she'd been holding the pen the way other people hold cigarettes, something to do with her fingers while her heart misbehaved.

"Then what happened," she said, "because I've had twenty years of versions. In some of them you were a coward. In some of them I was unbearable. In one memorable draft you were secretly married." A breath. "I'd like the true one, if it's available."

* * *

Callum had rehearsed this in the car, before the car died. He had rehearsed it on the causeway road, wipers failing, thinking absurdly that if he crashed at least he'd crash within sight of her light. But rehearsal was useless now. The truth didn't want structure. It wanted out.

"In my last year of university," he said, "my father's business went under. Not struggling — under. Gone. There were debts with names attached, and one of the names was mine, because I'd signed things I didn't read at nineteen because he was my father and fathers don't ruin you." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I came here that summer to tell you we couldn't be together. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. If I married into money — and there was a family who'd arranged it, God help me, it was that archaic — the debts would be absorbed. It was presented to me as arithmetic. You'd have hated the arithmetic."

Petra said nothing. The candle between them bent in a draught and righted itself.

"But I couldn't do it either," he went on. "So I did the coward's version. I wrote you a letter explaining everything, and I asked someone to make sure you got it — someone you trusted, someone who'd make sure you understood before you burned my name off your life entirely." His jaw tightened. "And then I waited in London for a reply that never came, until the debt collectors taught me what waiting costs, and I told myself you'd read it and chosen silence. That you'd closed the account. It was easier than believing the letter never arrived."

The wind took the cottage in its teeth and shook it once, gently, like a dog with a rag.

"Marisol," Petra said. Not a question. A stone dropped down a well, waiting for the sound of water.

"She intercepted it," Marisol said from the window seat.

Both of them turned. She had lowered her phone. Her face in the candlelight looked older than her years and very tired, the way faces look when they've been carrying something up a long stair.

"He asked me because I was your best friend and he was too proud to post it and risk it getting lost," she said. "I read it. I read it standing in your doorway, and I understood exactly what it would do if you read it — you'd have waited for him. You'd have spent your twenties waiting for a man being sold into a marriage to buy himself free, and he might never have come, and you'd have hollowed out doing it. I'd watched you already start to hollow over him. So I put the letter in my coat pocket and I let you believe he simply left." Her voice cracked and she let it. "I chose for you. I decided which pain you could survive, and I picked the one that would hurt fastest and end soonest. And I have been wrong about that every single day for twenty years, and I am telling you now because the storm has taken away every exit I usually take."

"You let me hate him," Petra whispered.

"I let you hate him because hating is faster than hoping. I've watched you since. You write love like a woman defusing a bomb. Every book, you get them to the edge of it and then you find a reason — a train, a war, a misunderstanding — anything but letting them stay. I did that. I built that." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I'm not asking forgiveness tonight. I'm asking you to know the shape of what happened, because you've both been living inside a lie I built, and it's -20 degrees outside and none of us can leave, so."

Silence. The generator coughed somewhere beneath the floorboards, caught, steadied.

Petra looked at Callum across the candle. Twenty years of versions, and the true one was worse and better than all of them: no one had stopped loving anyone. Someone had simply moved the letter.

"I wrote you," she heard herself say. "All this time. Every novel. There's a man in all six of them — the lighthouse keeper's son, the cartographer, the violinist who leaves — different names, same man. Same hands. Same way of listening like the other person is the only weather that matters. My readers have a name for him. They write to me about him. They don't know they're writing to me about you."

Callum made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite anything else. "Which one am I?"

"All of them. None of them. The one who comes back in the last chapter and it's too late, so I kill him gently instead." Her voice failed and she rebuilt it. "I thought writing you was survival. It turns out it was just drowning with better vocabulary."

* * *

The generator failed at midnight.

Petra knew it first as a change in the floor's hum, a note dropping out of the world, and then the candles guttering in the sudden cold rush of the heating stopping, and then — worse — the memory arriving whole: the lamp. The light above them, dark for the first time in a hundred and forty years, on a night when any fishing boat fool enough to be out would be running blind toward these rocks.

"There's a hand crank," she said, already moving. "Backup for the backup. It's in the lamp room. Without power the rotation's manual — someone has to turn it, all night, or the flash pattern's wrong and it's worse than nothing."

"I'll go." Callum was up, pulling his coat.

"It's ninety-one steps and the tower sways in wind like this—"

"Then I'll sway with it." He was already at the door, and something in his face stopped her argument mid-flight. "Twenty years, Petra. Let me keep one light burning."

She went with him anyway. Of course she went with him.

The stairwell was a black throat. Their torches made small trembling circles on the granite, and the wind found the tower and played it like a pipe, and at step sixty-something the whole structure leaned — a slow, sickening tilt, the sea reaching up through the stone — and Callum's foot skated on wet iron, and Petra grabbed his arm and the rail screamed and for one full second he hung between her grip and the drop of the central well, legs kicking over darkness.

Then he was up. Then they were both pressed against the curving wall, breathing hard, her heart slamming against his shoulder blade through two coats.

"Don't," she said, and her voice broke on it. "Don't you dare fall. I've written your death six times. I refuse to publish the seventh."

He turned in her grip. In the torchlight his eyes were wet and furious and alive. "I'm not going anywhere," he said. "That's the whole confession, Petra. That's the entire letter. I never went anywhere."

They reached the lamp room. The great lens stood dark and patient as a sleeping eye, and Callum took the crank with both hands, and together — because she refused to leave, because keeping a light had apparently always been a two-person job — they began to turn it. Flash. Sweep. Flash. Out over the drowned world, the old rhythm resuming, one white heartbeat every seven seconds, saying *here, here, here* to whatever was lost out in the dark.

Below them in the cottage, Marisol stood at the window watching the beam sweep the snow, and took out her phone, and began — signal or no signal, storm or no storm — typing the message that would, by morning, summon half the village to testify for a lighthouse its keeper didn't yet know she was losing.
