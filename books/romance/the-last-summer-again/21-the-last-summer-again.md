## 21. The Last Summer Again

The pen had been sitting on my desk for three days.

It was a good pen — a fountain pen, black lacquer, the kind I'd bought in another life when I still believed that important things deserved important instruments. It had signed court documents and lease agreements and, once, a divorce settlement. It had never signed anything that mattered to me. Not really.

For ten years I had written to Lena Dravenmoor with the cheapest ballpoints I could find, on paper bags and receipt backs and hotel stationery from towns I passed through on cases I didn't want to remember. Anonymous letters required anonymity, and anonymity, I had learned, lived in cheap ink. You don't sign your soul with a fountain pen. You scrawl it and run.

But tonight the letter would be signed. Tonight there was nowhere to run, and more importantly — nowhere I wanted to go.

I sat at the desk by the window of my apartment above the old tailor shop, the one I'd moved into eleven months ago with forty boxes of paper and three pieces of furniture, telling myself it was coincidence. The late August light came through sideways, gold and thick as honey, catching the dust motes above the tin box where her letters lived — every letter she'd ever sent me back, because after everything, after the hearing and the confession and the kiss on this very fire escape, Lena had gone into her own storage and found the letters she'd never answered. Forty-one of them. She'd answered them all in one furious week, each envelope dated with the year it should have arrived, so that I now possessed a decade of her voice delivered all at once, like a flood breaking through a dam I'd spent ten years building.

I had read them all twice. Some of them four times.

The case was moving. That was the word everyone used — *moving* — as if justice were traffic, as if five years of a man's life could be measured in forward motion. Judge Osei had ordered a full review of the conviction. Tomas Reyes's statement sat in a prosecutor's hands like a lit match. Halvard's stock had dropped nine points the week the story broke, and Marcus Vey had stopped calling Elias Wren entirely, which somehow frightened me more than the threats had. A man who stops threatening you has either given up or found a better weapon. I was trying — trying hard — not to spend this last week of summer wondering which it was.

That was the thing about joy. I kept waiting for it to be taken back. I kept writing clauses into my own happiness, escape hatches, disclaimers. *This is provisional. This is revocable. Do not get attached.*

Lena had caught me at it once, two weeks ago, when I'd said something careful about the fall — *if things go well, if the review goes our way* — and she'd put down her rolling pin, crossed the café floor with flour still on her hands, and taken my face in both palms like I was a bun that hadn't risen properly.

"Elias," she'd said. "You are allowed to be happy on a Tuesday. Nothing bad happens because you noticed."

I thought about that now, watching the light move across the desk. *You are allowed to be happy on a Tuesday.*

So: the letter.

I pulled a clean sheet toward me — real paper, heavy stock, bought from the stationer on Delancey who wrapped everything in brown paper and string — and I held the fountain pen over it for a long time before I wrote anything at all.

*Dear Lena,* I wrote. And then I stopped, because for ten years I had started letters exactly that way and then torn them up, and my hand remembered the tearing. My hand expected the tearing.

I made myself keep going.

---

The café was full.

I could hear it through the wall — the low roar of the Saturday afternoon crowd, Marisol's laugh cutting through it like a bell, Dev's voice too loud on purpose, Mrs. Okafor holding court at the corner table where she'd held court since before any of us understood what the corner table meant. The community land trust papers were signed; the building belonged, in the strangest and truest sense, to itself, to the people in it, to a future nobody could buy out from under them. Lena's father had been out on bail for six weeks and had already reorganized her kitchen twice, which she pretended to hate and did not hate at all.

And I sat upstairs with a pen that trembled slightly, writing:

*Dear Lena,*

*I have started this letter four hundred times over ten years. I know because I kept the drafts — all of them, in boxes you have seen and laughed at and called "a fire hazard with feelings." Most of them began with an apology, and most of those apologies were for the same thing: that I watched, and said nothing. That a nineteen-year-old clerk with a lanyard and a fear of his own shadow saw what happened to your father and went home and wrote to a stranger instead of raising his hand.*

*Here is what I never managed to say in any draft: I didn't write to you out of guilt alone. That's the coward's version, and I told myself the coward's version for years. But the truth is that after the trial I went home and I couldn't stop thinking about the daughter. The girl in the second row who sat straight-backed through every day of testimony like her spine was the only structure left standing in the world. I wrote to you because you were the bravest person I had ever seen and I wanted, selfishly, unforgivably, to be near that bravery. Even anonymously. Especially anonymously.*

*Ten letters got sent. Four hundred drafts didn't. Every year I told myself next year I'd sign my name. Every year I chose the version of love that couldn't be refused.*

*So this is the letter I owe you. Signed. No escape hatch. No clause. No revocation.*

*I loved you through a decade of paper before I ever heard your laugh. I will love you through whatever comes next — the retrial, the fall, whatever Vey is building in the dark, whatever the world does with the truth we handed it. Not provisionally. Not in careful increments. The whole thing, given, not lent.*

*Yours — actually, finally, legally, irrevocably yours,*

*Elias Wren*

I read it four times. I changed nothing. For once in my life, I changed nothing.

Then I folded it, put it in an envelope, wrote her name on the front in the handwriting she'd recognized under her door eight months ago — the handwriting that had made her furious before it made her mine — and I carried it downstairs.

* * *

The fire escape between our doors had become, over the summer, a kind of room. Neither of us could have said when. It was three feet wide and iron-cold in winter and it held two folding chairs and a candle stub in a jam jar and, currently, a plate of cardamom buns Lena had carried up an hour earlier, "for research purposes," she'd said, though the recipe hadn't changed in months and didn't need research and we both knew it.

She was already there when I came out, hair loose, feet bare, reading one of her father's old baking journals with her knees drawn up. The sun was going down behind the water tower, turning everything the color of the inside of a cardamom bun — which is to say, the color I now associated permanently with being home.

"I have something," I said.

She looked up. She saw the envelope, and I watched her whole body change — not tense, exactly. Attentive. Like a person hearing the first note of a song they know.

"Is that—" she started.

"It's signed," I said. "For the first time. I want to read it to you. Out loud. If that's—"

"Yes." Fast. Then, softer, putting the journal down: "Yes."

I sat in the folding chair across from her. Our knees almost touched. Below us the street did its evening business — someone's radio, a dog, the shuttered tailor shop with its new paint, because the community had boarded the broken window in April and painted it in June and someone had stenciled a small cardamom bun on the shutter, which made Lena cry the first time she saw it and made her pretend she hadn't.

I unfolded the pages. My hands were steadier than I expected.

I read it aloud. All of it. The coward's version and the truth underneath it. The four hundred drafts. The girl in the second row. *The whole thing, given, not lent.* When I reached the signature I said my full name out loud like a vow — *Elias Wren* — and heard how strange and enormous it sounded in the open air, my name attached at last to the words it had been carrying for ten years.

Lena didn't speak when I finished. The light had gone from gold to rose, and her eyes were bright, and she was doing the thing she did — holding very still so the feeling wouldn't spill.

"You wrote four hundred drafts," she said finally.

"Four hundred and twelve. I rounded down."

"And every year you almost signed."

"Every year I almost signed."

She nodded slowly, looking out at the water tower, and I braced myself — old habit — for the question underneath, the one about wasted years, the one about why. Instead she reached over and took the letter from my hands, carefully, like it was dough at the proofing stage, and read the signature again in silence.

"I used to imagine you," she said. "The person writing them. In the worst year, after Dad went in, after the bakery closed — I invented a whole person. Old, maybe. Kind in a tired way. Someone who'd been through something." She looked at me. "You're not him at all. You were twenty-three and terrified and you showed up anyway, in ink, for ten years. I think I might have loved him a little. The imagined one. So the math isn't hard."

"The math isn't hard," I repeated, uselessly, because my throat had closed.

"But I'm not answering tonight with talk," she said, and stood, and disappeared through her door, and left me on the fire escape with my heart going like a teenager's.

* * *

She came back with paper and a pen of her own — a cheap ballpoint, I noticed, and something in my chest turned over at that, at her choosing the cheap pen, the honest pen, the pen of paper bags and receipt backs — and she sat down cross-legged facing me and said, "Don't read until I'm done. And don't talk. If you talk I'll lose my nerve, and I've only lost my nerve four hundred times in my life and I'm retiring the habit."

So I watched her write. The rose light fading to violet, the first moth arriving at the jam-jar candle, her pen moving fast, crossing things out, starting sentences over, muttering. It took her eleven minutes. I counted. I counted everything that summer — buns, days, payments, heartbeats — and this was the eleven minutes I'll remember longest.

When she finished she folded the page, hesitated, and then handed it to me unfolded, which I understood was its own kind of signature.

*Dear Elias,*

*I kept your letters in a shoebox under my bed for ten years. I moved them to a tin when the shoebox got moldy in the apartment after the bakery, and I moved the tin three times, and I never threw out a single one, even the year I decided I hated hope, even the year I decided hope was just pain with better marketing. I told myself I kept them because whoever wrote them had earned the shelf space. That was a lie. I kept them because they were the only voice in my life that asked nothing, and some starving part of me needed one voice that asked nothing.*

*You ask nothing. That's the first thing I loved. Here's the rest: you show up. Badly, late, terrified, in cheap ink, but you show up. You stood up in a courtroom and confessed to a felony to save my father because you couldn't stand to watch me carry it alone. Nobody taught you how to do that. You just did it.*

*So here is my answer, signed, no clauses: I choose you. Not because of the letters. Because of the man who wrote them and then walked next door and ordered coffee and complimented my cardamom buns with unsettling specificity like a total weirdo. I choose the Tuesday happiness. I choose the fire escape. I choose the version of my life where the door between our apartments stays unlocked forever.*

*Write to me anyway. Even though you live thirty feet away. Especially then.*

*Lena Dravenmoor*

*P.S. The buns don't need research. You just wanted an excuse. I've known for months. Keep coming anyway.*

I read it twice. On the second pass my vision blurred, and I let it, because I was done being a man who managed his own face.

"Keep coming anyway," I said aloud.

"That was the important part," she said. "The P.S."

"It was a very good P.S."

"I workshopped it."

We sat there while the sky went dark and the candle did its small brave work and the sounds of the café below faded to Marisol locking up, to Dev's bike bell, to the street exhaling into night. At some point her hand found mine on the iron railing, flour-rough and warm, and neither of us moved for a long time.

"We should keep writing," she said eventually. "Even though we don't need to."

"Especially though we don't need to."

"One letter a week. Minimum. Real paper. Signed."

"Where do we put them? We'll drown in tins."

She smiled at the dark. "We'll need a bigger tin."

And that was it — that was the whole ceremony, no vows, no audience, just two people on a fire escape agreeing to a correspondence that had already lasted a decade and would now, absurdly, wonderfully, continue across a distance of thirty feet. Joy chosen deliberately. Not stumbled into. Not hoped for quietly in case hoping loudly jinxed it. Chosen, out loud, in ink.

Later — much later, after the candle drowned and the moths gave up and we had migrated inside through her door, the unlocked one, the one that stays unlocked — I lay awake listening to her breathe and thinking about the morning.

Because tomorrow there was a meeting. The prosecutor's office had called that afternoon, voice carefully neutral, and asked Mr. Wren and Ms. Dravenmoor to come in Monday morning regarding "new developments in the matter of State v. Dravenmoor." New developments. Three words that could mean the review was accelerating or collapsing, that Vey had found his better weapon or that the walls were finally closing on Halvard the way they should have closed five years ago.

Lena stirred against my shoulder. "You're thinking loudly," she murmured.

"There's a meeting tomorrow."

"I know." A pause. "Whatever it is, we walk in together. That's the whole plan. That's the entire strategy."

"Together," I agreed.

But sleep didn't come easily, and near midnight I heard it — faint, from the street below: a car idling where no car idles at that hour, engine running, lights off, patient as a held breath. I got up without waking her and stood at the window with the signed letter in my hand, hers and mine, and watched the dark shape sit there for eleven minutes before it pulled away, slow, unhurried, like something that knew exactly where everyone in this building slept.

Monday suddenly felt very far away.
