## 1. Counting Turtles

The bus from the airport let Beatrix Coldharbour off at a crossroads with no cross, just a leaning sign that read COLDHARBOUR VINEYARD — 2 MI and, beneath it in someone's ancient marker pen, *last chance for decent coffee*, which was a lie, because there had been no coffee since the gas station two counties back.

She stood on the shoulder of the road with one suitcase, one duffel bag of field equipment, and a cardboard box labeled FRAGILE — MOM'S THINGS in her own handwriting, and she counted to ten. It was an old habit. Marine biologists counted things. Fish, turtles, tide pools, the seconds between lightning and thunder. Counting made the world legible. Counting meant you were observing, not drowning.

One. Two. Three.

By seven she had admitted the bus wasn't coming back, and by ten she had started walking.

The road rose and fell through hills gone gold with late summer, and every rise gave her a little more of the valley: the river flashing silver in its bend, the oak trees standing around like patient uncles, and then—there it was—the vineyard, laid out along the south-facing slope in long green rows that looked, from this distance, exactly as they had when she was nine years old and certain the whole world fit inside them.

It looked smaller. Everything did, after a decade of counting whales in the North Pacific. But it looked *alive*, which surprised her, because according to the eleven voicemails from Rosa Delgado, the foreman, alive was not a word anyone had used about Coldharbour Vineyard in some time.

*The bank called again,* Rosa had said in voicemail number six, in the flat tone of a woman who had stopped expecting miracles sometime around the second drought. *Your mother's program is still costing us money we don't have. The crush pad pump died. Also I miss you, and also I'm sorry about your mom, and also come home before I say something meaner.*

Beatrix had not cried at the funeral. She had filed the death certificate, closed the apartment lease, sold the car, settled the estate with the efficiency of a woman performing a necropsy on her own life, and gotten on a plane with a one-way ticket and a plan: one season. One harvest. Get the vineyard's books into something resembling order, hand Rosa whatever she needed to keep it breathing, and go back to the coast, where grief could be spread out over a very large ocean instead of concentrated in forty acres of grapes.

One season. She had said it out loud on the plane, to the stranger in 14C who had asked if she was going home. "Just temporarily," she'd said, and the word had tasted like a promise she intended to keep.

The gravel crunched under her boots. The box grew heavier with every step, or perhaps her arms simply grew more honest about it. Halfway up the drive, a pickup truck rattled past her going the other direction, braked hard, and backed up in a cloud of dust with the confidence of a vehicle whose driver had never once considered that backing up might be a bad idea.

Rosa Delgado leaned out the window. She was sixty-three and built like the fence posts she'd been setting for forty years, with forearms that could crack walnuts and a face that had decided, somewhere around 1998, to stop wasting energy on pretense.

"You walked," Rosa said.

"The bus schedule and I have irreconcilable differences."

"Two miles with a box." Rosa looked at the box, then at Beatrix's face, and something in her expression went carefully neutral, the way people's faces did around Beatrix lately, like hers was a room they were afraid of knocking something over in. "Get in."

"I'm fine."

"Nobody's fine. Get in anyway."

Beatrix got in. The truck smelled of diesel and dried lavender, which was Rosa's answer to everything, including, possibly, mortality. They rolled up the drive past the first block of vines, and Beatrix watched them pass her window—Chardonnay, the old block, planted by her grandfather—and felt nothing, which she noted with clinical interest, the way she'd note a water sample that should have shown salinity and didn't.

"How is she?" Rosa asked, eyes on the road. "I mean—" A muscle worked in her jaw. "How are *you*."

"I'm practical," Beatrix said. "There's a lot to do. I made a list on the plane."

Rosa snorted. "Of course you did." She drove another hundred yards. "You look like your mother, you know. Around the eyes. It's going to be a problem for me, so fair warning."

"Understood," Beatrix said, and looked out the window, and counted fence posts until the feeling passed.

* * *

The farmhouse hadn't changed, except in all the ways that mattered. Same wraparound porch with the third step still soft in the middle. Same kitchen with the same yellow curtains her mother had sewn from sailcloth in some fit of optimism circa 1987. But the mail on the table was stacked in Rosa's tidy piles, and the calendar on the wall was Rosa's handwriting now, and there was a mug in the dish rack that Beatrix didn't recognize, and the whole house had the particular silence of a place where someone used to hum.

Her mother had hummed constantly. Terribly. Off-key renditions of sea shanties while pruning, while bottling, while burning dinner. Beatrix stood in the kitchen doorway holding her box and listened to the absence of humming, and put the box down on the table very gently, as though it might bruise.

"There's soup," Rosa said behind her. "It's terrible. I made it."

"That tracks."

"It has kale in it. Your mother always said if you're going to fail at something, fail with conviction."

Beatrix almost smiled. It got about halfway before it turned into something else, and she turned toward the sink and ran the tap so she'd have an excuse for her face being wet. Behind her, Rosa pretended very loudly to find bowls.

They ate the terrible soup at the table where Beatrix had done her homework, and Rosa filled her in with the unsentimental efficiency Beatrix had specifically chosen her for, back when Beatrix was nineteen and leaving and needed to know the vines would be in good hands.

The numbers were bad. Not dead-bad, but sick-bad, the kind of bad that crept. Three consecutive short harvests. The distributor dropped them last spring. The bank had extended the loan twice out of what Rosa suspected was pity and once out of what the loan officer had openly admitted was fondness for Beatrix's mother, who had brought him soup when his wife was ill, because that was the kind of woman she'd been, and soup apparently carried collateral value.

"And the letter program," Rosa said, and here her voice changed, went careful again. "She kept it running. Even after. Cost us postage and paper and half of Nan's Tuesdays, but she wouldn't let it go. Said it was the only part of this place that ever made anybody feel less alone."

Beatrix set down her spoon. The Coldharbour Correspondence Club. Her mother's invention, born the winter Beatrix's father went away, when the whole valley had split into camps and stopped speaking to each other and her mother had decided, with the stubbornness that ran in the family, that if people wouldn't talk face-to-face, they could talk on paper, anonymously, safely, names withheld until both parties agreed otherwise. Strangers paired by lottery. Letters collected in a crate at the farm stand. It had sounded naive to nineteen-year-old Beatrix, hope dressed up as stationery.

It had also, she remembered now with a small reluctant jolt, actually worked. For a while. People had written. The valley had softened, a degree or two.

"She designed the pairing system herself," Rosa said. "Little envelopes with numbers. There's a whole crate of it in the barn. Unopened mail, too—people kept writing even after she got sick. Didn't have the heart to tell them to stop." Rosa pushed her bowl away. "Point is, it costs us maybe two hundred dollars a year and it's the only thing in this valley that still likes us. I'd think twice before killing it."

"I don't kill things," Beatrix said. "I count them."

"Well. Count gently."

Later, unpacking in her childhood bedroom—the single bed, the glow-in-the-dark stars she'd never gotten around to removing, the window facing the vineyard—she found her mother's field notebook on the desk, left open, as if she'd only stepped out. Inside, in her looping hand, notes on the Chardonnay block, and then, on the last written page, unrelated to anything:

*B. comes home soon. Must remember: do not fuss. Fussing is a form of drowning her. Let her count her turtles.*

Beatrix read it three times. Then she closed the notebook, set it down, opened it again, and read it a fourth time, because her mother had been dead four months and could not, by any mechanism known to science, have known that Beatrix still counted turtles—that she'd done it on the beach at Monterey as a child, counting the heads popping up in the kelp, and had done it on the research vessel whenever the data came in wrong, and had done it on the plane, silently, one turtle per row of seats, all the way across the country.

*Let her count her turtles.* Meaning: let her have her way of surviving it.

Beatrix sat on her childhood bed in her childhood room and, for the first time since the hospital, cried—not the clean grief of the funeral but the ugly, hiccuping kind, the kind with no dignity in it whatsoever, into a pillow that smelled faintly of the lavender sachets her mother had tucked into every closet in the house like a woman laying siege to sadness itself.

Then she washed her face, went downstairs, and made a list.

* * *

The list took up two pages. Rosa reviewed it over coffee the next morning—real coffee, Beatrix had located the good beans her mother hoarded like state secrets—and made small approving noises, and large disapproving noises at item three.

"Sell the north parcel? To *who*?"

"Whoever's buying. Someone's buying land in this valley; the trucks have been going past all week with survey stakes."

Rosa's mouth flattened. "That'll be the Merrick outfit, most likely. Aldous Merrick's been buying up everything on this side of the river since your mama got sick. Got his eye on our water rights, if you ask me, which nobody does, because I'm not important enough to ask."

"Merrick." The name rang a faint bell—her father's era, her father's trouble—but Beatrix filed it under *later*, where she was filing most things. "Noted. Item three stays on the list."

"Item three can stay on the list the way a hornet stays on your arm."

"Noted," Beatrix said again, and Rosa muttered something in Spanish that was probably about hornets, and went out to check the pump on the crush pad, and Beatrix sat alone at the kitchen table with her coffee and her lists and the quiet house, and told herself firmly that this was fine. This was manageable. One season. Ninety-odd days. She had survived storms at sea and a grant review panel; she could survive a vineyard.

She spent the morning doing what she did best: inventory. She walked every block with a clipboard, counting vines, noting dead ones, missing trellis wire, irrigation lines held together with what appeared to be prayer and electrical tape. The crew nodded at her as she passed—a dozen or so of them, weathered and wary, watching the daughter come home the way cats watch a new piece of furniture. She knew their faces from summers long ago. She did not know how to be their boss, and they clearly knew it too, and everyone politely pretended otherwise.

At the far end of the Chardonnay block she found the old wooden stand where the correspondence crate lived, half-hidden behind the farm stand's shuttered counter. The crate was cedar, gray with age, stuffed fat with envelopes. Some were addressed in careful schoolroom cursive. Some were smudged. One, near the top, had a pressed flower taped to it, which seemed optimistic given the postal conditions involved.

On the front of the crate, burned into the wood in her mother's wobbly hand-crafted lettering, were the rules:

*WRITE TO A STRANGER. NO NAMES UNTIL BOTH SAY YES. EVERYONE IS LONELY. THAT'S THE WHOLE SECRET.*

Beatrix stood there a long moment, clipboard against her chest, reading the last line twice.

*Everyone is lonely. That's the whole secret.*

"Hm," she said aloud, in the tone she used for anomalous data, and moved on.

* * *

That evening, the books defeated her.

She sat at the kitchen table with ledgers spread around her like fallen soldiers, and the picture they painted was worse than Rosa's summary had suggested. Not bankrupt—yet. But the margin between solvency and ruin was roughly one bad harvest wide, and the harvest was six weeks out, and the crush pad pump was dead, and the north parcel sale would cover the loan payment but gut next year's capacity, and every option ended in some version of *and then what?*

And then what. Ten years of grants and fieldwork and publish-or-perish, and she had never once faced a question with no methodology.

She was rubbing her eyes when she heard the crunch of tires, and looked up to see headlights sweeping the yard, and a moment later Rosa came in without knocking, which was Rosa's constitutional right, holding an envelope.

"This came for the program today. Well—one of eleven came. But this one's different." She set it on the ledger pile. "It's addressed to the *program*. No pairing number. Just—" She tapped it. "Read the front."

Beatrix picked it up. The envelope was good stock, expensive, the handwriting bold and slanted, a lawyer's hand or a doctor's. Addressed simply to:

**THE COLDHARBOUR CORRESPONDENCE CLUB**

And beneath that, in smaller letters: *Attention: whoever keeps the lottery.*

"Whoever keeps the lottery," Beatrix repeated.

"Nan usually handles new pairings. Nan's in Fresno visiting her sister. And your mama used to draw the envelopes herself, on the first of the month, out of the shoebox." Rosa jerked her chin toward the shelf, where a battered shoebox sat, stuffed with small numbered envelopes, her mother's whole architecture of anonymous hope. "First of the month was yesterday."

Beatrix looked at the shoebox. Looked at the letter. Outside, the light was going amber over the vines, and somewhere down the slope a crew member was laughing at something, and the house was quiet, and she was so tired, tired in a way sleep had never touched, tired down in the marrow where the counting couldn't reach.

"One season," she reminded herself quietly. "Practical. Bounded scope."

She slit the envelope open.

Inside was a single page, typed, formal, and strange. It read:

*To the administrator of the Coldharbour Correspondence Club: I understand the program pairs volunteers anonymously, by lottery, without disclosure of identity. I am requesting enrollment. I am aware the program was designed for members of this community, and that I may not qualify. I am asking anyway. I have something I need to say to someone, and I find that I cannot say it to anyone whose face I know. If a partner can be found for me, I will write honestly. That is the only term I offer: honesty. Please respond by whatever channel you use. —V.*

Beatrix read it twice. It was an odd letter. It was a proud letter and a wounded one at once, and she recognized the shape of it the way she recognized weather fronts—from long study, from the inside.

"He requested enrollment," she said slowly. "People *request* this? I thought it was locals dropping slips in a fishbowl."

"Locals drop slips in a fishbowl," Rosa said. "Nobody requests. Nobody writes a formal application to be lonely." She studied Beatrix. "Well?"

"Well what?"

"You're the Coldharbour here. Your mama drew the envelopes. You want me to write back and tell him the program's closed?"

Beatrix looked at the shoebox of little numbered envelopes. Her mother's handwriting on every one. *Everyone is lonely. That's the whole secret.*

The vineyard needed two hundred dollars' worth of reasons to keep this thing alive, and here was a stranger offering the one thing the program had never had: proof it still mattered to somebody. And besides—it cost a stamp. She could afford a stamp. Probably.

"Draw him a partner," Beatrix said. "From the box."

Rosa raised an eyebrow. "Whoever's in the fishbowl?"

"Whoever's in the fishbowl. House rules." Beatrix reached into the shoebox, and her fingers found an envelope near the bottom, worn soft at the corners, a slip that had been waiting a long time. She drew it out, opened it, and read the number inside.

"Who is it?" Rosa asked.

Beatrix looked at the number, then at the ledger of active pairings pinned inside the crate lid, and found the line, and read the entry, and paused.

"What?" said Rosa.

"Nothing. It's just—" Beatrix set the slip down. "This one's been in the box for years. Never drawn. Whoever wrote it must've nearly given up." She copied the pairing number onto a fresh card in her neatest handwriting, and slid it into an envelope, and addressed it to *V.*, care of the club, and thought, distantly, that her mother would have fussed about the choice of stationery, and that fussing was a form of drowning, and that this was not fussing, this was administration.

"Congratulations," Rosa said dryly. "You're a matchmaker."

"I'm a bookkeeper with a rubber stamp."

"Uh-huh." Rosa headed for the door, then stopped, hand on the frame. "For what it's worth—that letter. Man sounds like he's carrying something heavy. Your mama always said those were the ones you matched fastest."

"Rosa."

"Yeah?"

"Don't make it weird. It's a mailing list."

"Sure it is." Rosa grinned and went out into the dusk, and Beatrix sat alone with the ledgers and the lamp and the envelope, and told herself—firmly, practically, in the voice she used for recalcitrant data—that she had just performed routine administrative work for a struggling nonprofit, and that it meant nothing, and that she would be gone in one season regardless.

Down the hill, the last light caught the tops of the vines, row after row after row, and Beatrix counted them out of habit, and lost track somewhere past two hundred, and found, to her mild irritation, that she didn't mind.

She sealed the envelope. First of the month, first of the season, first letter.

Somewhere out there, a stranger named V. was waiting to be honest with someone.
