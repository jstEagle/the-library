## 10. Theo's Silence

Mara Voss had learned, over the years of her marriage and its slow unraveling, to read the silences of men. Her husband Daniel had been a man of loud silences — the kind that filled a room like smoke, pressing against the windows, daring you to open them. He went quiet when he wanted something. He went quieter when he'd already taken it.

Theo Lindqvist was nothing like Daniel. That was the trouble. His silences were small and tidy, folded away like clean laundry, and for the first weeks of their truce she had mistaken them for simplicity. A man who pruned trees and fixed fences and said little — she had catalogued him as harmless, as background, as part of the landscape itself, no more capable of concealment than the barn or the creek.

She should have known better. Landscapes kept secrets better than people did. The valley proved it every day.

It began with small things, the way rot begins in an orchard — not in the crown where everyone looks, but down at the graft line, where the wood meets the soil.

The first time, they were sitting on the cottage porch after a long morning of thinning fruit, the three of them sharing a pitcher of well water and the last of a loaf of bread Jonah had brought over. The light was going gold and long across the rows, and Mara, loosened by exhaustion and something dangerously close to contentment, had asked the question she'd been circling for days.

"Do you remember when we left?" she said. "The summer of '09. You must have been— what, twenty?"

Theo's hand paused on the bread. Just a pause, half a second, the kind of thing you'd miss if you weren't watching. She was watching.

"I remember the trees," he said. "That summer was hard on them. Late frost, then the heat came early." He tore off a piece of crust and offered it to her. "You want the last of this?"

"Theo."

"Mm?"

"You didn't answer my question."

He looked out at the orchard then, at the rows going shadowy and blue in the evening, and his face did something she couldn't name — not guilt exactly, but the shape of it, the way a glove keeps the shape of a hand after it's taken off.

"It was a long time ago," he said gently. "People leave towns. It happens."

"My mother didn't *leave*," Mara said, and heard the edge come into her own voice before she could blunt it. "We were driven out. There's a difference, and you were here for it."

Jonah had gone very still beside her, the way she always did when the old wound came up between them — which was less often now, but never never. Theo only nodded, slow, and rose to collect the plates.

"Your mother loved those trees more than anyone I ever met," he said, with his back to them both. "That's what I remember about that year."

And that was all. That was all he ever gave.

* * *

After that, Mara started counting.

She counted the way she used to count in the bad months of her marriage — not because she wanted to find evidence, but because the not-knowing was worse than anything she might learn. She counted the way Theo's jaw tightened when the county clerk's name came up over dinner. She counted how he changed the subject whenever Elias Eriksen's name surfaced, steering conversations toward irrigation schedules and sulfur sprays with the deftness of a man who'd had practice. She counted the fact that in six weeks of shared work, shared meals, shared storms, he had never once spoken of the year 2009 except to talk about weather.

Everyone remembered 2009 through some personal catastrophe. It was the year the recession gutted the valley, the year half the farms along the county road went up for sale, the year the Voss family loaded a truck at dawn and drove out of town with their windows up. Everyone had a story about that year. Theo Lindqvist, who had lived in this valley his whole life, who had worked for her mother for eleven years before they left and somehow stayed on afterward, tending trees that legally belonged to a family that had abandoned them — Theo had no story at all.

That was the thing she kept turning over at night in the narrow bed of the cottage, listening to the roof tick and settle. Not that he lied. That he had *nothing*. A man with no story about the worst year of your family's life is a man holding one very close to his chest.

"He's just private," Jonah said when Mara raised it, carefully, one evening in the farmhouse kitchen. Jonah stood at the counter with her back half-turned, washing glasses she'd already washed, a habit Mara had learned meant discomfort. "He was here before either of us. He doesn't owe us his whole history."

"No," Mara agreed. "He doesn't." She turned the wineglass in her fingers. "But he owes us the truth if it touches this land. So do you. So do I. That's the deal we made."

Jonah set the glass down harder than necessary. "You think there's something he's hiding."

"I think there's something everyone's hiding," Mara said. "I think it's this valley's chief agricultural product."

Jonah almost smiled at that. Almost. But she didn't argue, and Mara noticed that too — noticed that Jonah, who argued about everything, who had fought her over fence lines and water rights and the correct depth for cover crops, let this pass without a fight. As if she knew something. As if she were carrying her own folded silence under one arm and didn't trust herself to set it down.

Mara filed that away too.

* * *

She found him at the burn barrel on a Tuesday evening in late August, when the air had finally cooled enough to breathe and the first real hint of autumn moved through the valley like a rumor.

She'd gone looking for him because the pump in the east block had lost pressure and he hadn't answered when she called from the gate. She cut through behind the equipment shed instead of taking the long way around, and that was why she saw him before he saw her — Theo standing at the old steel barrel near the barn, feeding papers into it one at a time, unhurried, watching each page catch and curl before adding the next.

He wasn't burning trash. Mara knew what burned trash looked like; she'd grown up on farms. Trash got thrown. This was ritual. This was careful. He had a stack of documents squared neatly on an overturned crate beside him, and he was working through them top to bottom, and the firelight moved on his face in a way that made him look older than his fifty-some years, older than the valley itself.

For a moment she simply stood in the shadow of the shed and watched.

Then a page caught wrong, flared bright, and in that flash of light she saw writing — dense, handwritten, columns of figures — before the paper blackened and lifted as ash.

"Theo."

He didn't startle. That was its own kind of answer. He turned slowly, and his expression when he faced her was the most open she'd ever seen it: tired, and sorry, and absolutely closed.

"Mara."

"What are you burning?"

"Old records." He said it evenly, the way he said *late frost* or *boron deficiency*. "Invoices. Spray logs. Paper accumulates."

"Invoices don't have handwriting like that." She crossed the yard toward him, gravel sharp under her boots, and stopped an arm's length from the barrel. The heat pressed against her shins. On the crate, the remaining stack sat squared and patient, maybe thirty pages, the top one bearing a date in faded ink that her eyes snagged on even upside-down: *June 2009.*

Something went cold in her chest, colder than the fire was hot.

"That's my mother's handwriting," she said quietly.

Theo looked at the stack. Then at her. And Mara watched — actually watched — a man decide something. She saw the weighing happen behind his eyes, the scales moving, and she understood with sudden terrible clarity that whatever was in that stack, he had carried it for fifteen years, and that he had been waiting, all this time, for something. For her to be ready. For the right season. For permission from someone who was no longer alive to give it.

And he had decided, tonight, standing alone at a burn barrel, that the wait was over — that it would end in ash instead of words.

"Theo," she said, and her voice cracked on his name, and she hated it, hated being seventeen again in front of this man. "Please. Whatever it is. My mother is dead. I have nothing left of her but this land and a box of letters. Don't take the last of her out of the world while I stand here."

The wind moved through the orchard behind them, and the trees made the sound they always made, that long exhale, and Theo closed his eyes briefly like a man praying or grieving or both.

Then he reached past her, took the stack from the crate, and held it against his chest — not offering it, shielding it.

"Ask your mother's lawyer," he said.

"*What?*"

"Halloran," he said. "In Corvallis. Ruth Halloran. Your mother retained her in March of 2009, and paid her retainer every year until she died, and I know because I drove her to the appointments." His voice was steady but his hands, wrapped around that stack of paper, were not. "I'm not destroying anything, Mara. I'm moving it. There's a difference, and you of all people should know there's a difference." He looked at her then with something fierce and sorrowful in his face. "I promised her I'd keep certain things safe until you came home. I've kept that promise for fifteen years. I'm not breaking it tonight, and I'm not breaking it in a burn barrel conversation. Go see Halloran."

"She's *dead*, Theo. Mom is dead. Whatever promise you made her—"

"—was to her, not to you," he finished, not unkindly. "And I intend to keep it. All of it. Even the parts that hurt."

He walked past her toward the barn, the stack held tight, and stopped at the door without turning around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your mother never doubted you'd come back. She told me once the tide always comes back to the same shore. She just thought it would be sooner."

The barn door rolled shut behind him.

Mara stood alone in the yard with the burn barrel ticking as it cooled, ash lifting on the wind and settling on the rows like a gray snowfall, and felt the ground shift beneath everything she'd built these past weeks — the truce, the partnership, the fragile, unspoken thing growing between her and Jonah and this stubborn man who kept the trees. Trust, she thought, was like grafting. You could bind two things together and they might take, might grow into one body — but if the rootstock underneath was rotten, no amount of binding saved it.

She didn't know yet whose roots were rotten. Hers. Jonah's. Theo's. Her mother's. Maybe everyone's.

But she knew one thing as she walked back through the darkening rows, ash in her hair and her heart hammering: in the morning she was driving to Corvallis, and Ruth Halloran was going to tell her what her mother had spent fifteen years and a fortune in retainers trying to keep safe.

And she was suddenly, desperately afraid that she already knew — that it would rhyme with a ledger, with a torn page, with the way Jonah washed glasses that were already clean.

The tide, her mother used to say. It always comes back.

Mara just prayed that when it did, it wouldn't take the whole shoreline with it.
