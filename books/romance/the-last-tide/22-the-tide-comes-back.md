## 22. The Tide Comes Back

The buds came first on the old Gravensteins, the way they always did — the trees nearest the creek waking before anything else in the valley, as if they'd spent all winter listening to the water and couldn't wait any longer to answer it.

Jonah saw them from the farmhouse window on a morning in late March, the light still low and gold along the eastern ridge, and she stood there with her coffee going cold in her hands for longer than she would have admitted to anyone. Five years ago she had stood at this same window and seen nothing but arithmetic: what a crop might fetch, what a loan would cost, what she could lose by lunchtime if the weather turned mean. Now she looked out at the orchard and saw — simply — trees. Budding. Doing what they had always done, with or without her permission, with or without her grandfather's ledgers, with or without the whole tangled history of who owned which row of roots.

It was Theo who had told her once, back in those first raw weeks of pruning side by side, that apple trees keep time differently than people do. *They don't count the years,* he'd said, his knife moving through a water sprout with the ease of long habit. *They just decide, every spring, whether to try again.* She hadn't understood him then. She thought she was starting to.

She set down her cup, pulled on her jacket, and went out to walk the fence line.

* * *

The gate at the orchard's edge hung open.

That was the thing that still caught in her chest, even now, even after a winter of it — the gate swinging loose on its hinges, no chain, no padlock, nothing but the wind's occasional push to move it. For most of her life that gate had been locked. Her grandfather had kept it chained since before Jonah could remember, and when Mara's lawyers had made the boundary dispute official, someone — Jonah never learned who, though she suspected Theo — had added a second lock, brass and stubborn, so that the half of the orchard that wasn't hers was also, pointedly, not reachable.

She had taken the locks off herself, the week after the hearing. It had felt like a small, absurd ceremony, standing there in the mud with bolt cutters she'd borrowed from the barn, cutting through metal that had outlived its purpose. The padlocks were still in a drawer in the kitchen. She kept them the way you keep a cast after your bone heals — proof of something broken, proof of something mended, and no particular use for either.

Now she walked through the open gate without slowing, because that was the whole point of an open gate.

The ground was soft from last week's rain, and her boots left prints that filled slowly with water, each one a small mirror holding a piece of sky. The grass between the rows was coming up green and unruly; Theo had argued for letting it grow this year instead of mowing it flat, said the clover would feed the soil and the bees both, and Jonah had agreed before he finished making the case, mostly because she liked watching him talk about dirt. He had a way of describing the orchard's underground life — the fungal threads, the slow commerce of roots — that made it sound less like agriculture and more like a neighborhood where everybody owed everybody favors and nobody minded.

She walked the boundary line slowly, the way she used to walk it defensively. This stretch of fence had been the war's front for two years: the survey stakes, the letters from the county, the mornings she'd come out and found Mara standing on the other side of it like a sentinel, arms crossed, jaw set, every inch of her a wall built out of seventeen years of silence. Jonah could map the whole history of their estrangement onto this fence line if she let herself — the padlock, the shouting match in the rain, the terrible quiet afterward, the night of the frost fires when they'd passed smudge pots hand to hand across the property line and neither of them had said a word but both of them had stayed until dawn.

She didn't need the map anymore. But she walked it anyway, touching the top rail here and there, feeling the wood gone smooth under her palm, worn by weather and by her own hand and, she suspected, by Mara's too. There were places where the fence had been repaired over the winter — new posts, tight wire, work done carefully by someone who intended for it to last. Not a wall anymore. Just a line, the kind neighbors agree on, the kind that says *this is yours and this is mine and we can talk across it whenever we like.*

At the far corner, where the Voss parcel met the creek, she stopped.

The water was high with snowmelt, running quick and brown-green over the stones, carrying twigs and last year's leaves toward wherever the valley's water went. Her grandfather had fished this bend. He'd taught Jonah to cast here when she was nine, his big hands closing over hers on the rod, and she had loved him so completely in those years that loving him had felt less like a feeling than like a fact of the landscape — as fixed as the creek, as permanent as the hills.

Then the ledger. Then the quitclaim, forged in his careful hand, dated three days after Elias Voss's funeral while the ink of grief was still wet on the family. Then the payments marked *V.* stretching across a decade, hush money dressed up as farm expenses, and the torn page where the truth had been ripped out — except that Theo had kept the original draft all those years, faithful to a dead woman's wish, waiting for Mara to be ready to come home.

Jonah crouched at the bank and watched the water move.

Her grandfather had believed, right up until the end, that what you took and held was yours. That possession was a kind of truth stronger than paper. He'd died in this house believing the orchard was his, all of it, every tree, and he'd left it to her like a sealed box, and she had spent five years defending a lie before she ever knew it was one.

*Tides go out,* she thought, watching the current shoulder past the bend. *That's what they do. They go out so they can come back.*

Mara had said it first, actually — or almost. On the cottage porch during the storm, rain hammering the roof so hard they'd had to raise their voices, Mara had talked about her mother walking this same creek with her, teaching her the names of things, and how the tide of it rose and fell with the seasons and never once failed to return. *Everything here comes back,* Mara had said, and her voice had cracked on *here*, and Jonah had understood that Mara wasn't talking about water at all. She was talking about herself. About leaving at seventeen and taking twenty years to find her way home.

Jonah had never left. That was the strange symmetry of them — Mara had gone and ached to return; Jonah had stayed and ached to belong. Two women on opposite sides of the same fence, each convinced the other had the easier life. Neither had.

She straightened, brushed the damp from her knees, and kept walking.

* * *

By mid-morning the valley had warmed enough that she shed her jacket and tied it around her waist, and the orchard had filled with the small busy sounds of spring — bees working the early blossoms, a flicker drumming somewhere on a dead snag, the creek keeping its low constant commentary beneath everything. Theo's truck was parked by the barn when she came back around, and she found him at the end of the third row with a ladder and a pruning saw, doing the last of the winter cuts before the sap ran heavy.

"You're late," he said, without looking up. "Trees don't wait."

"I'm not on the payroll."

"Nobody's on the payroll. That's the problem with this operation." But he was smiling — that dry, sideways smile that had taken her two full seasons to learn how to read, and that came easier to him now than it once had, the way the gate swung easier now that it carried no lock.

She took the second ladder off the truck bed and carried it down the row. Working beside him required no conversation; that was one of the gifts of him, one of the reasons her chest had done its slow reluctant opening over the months of shared labor. They moved through the trees together, sawing and clipping, dragging brush to the pile, and the rhythm of it was older than either of them — the oldest rhythm there was, maybe, people and trees and the turning year.

Halfway down the row he paused, saw in hand, and looked up into the canopy of a Gravenstein so old its trunk had hollowed and healed over itself twice.

"This one's older than both deeds," he said. "Older than Elias. Older than your grandfather, probably." He touched the bark gently, the way you'd touch the shoulder of someone sleeping. "Whatever we wrote down about who owns what, this tree never signed anything. It just kept making apples."

"That's very mystical for a Tuesday, Theo."

"I'm a caretaker. Mysticism is in the job description." He climbed down, folded the ladder, and slung it over his shoulder. Then, more quietly: "Mara's coming by this afternoon. She wants to walk the new rows, look at the rootstock order before you send it in."

"I know. She called me at six."

"At six?"

"She's always been a six-in-the-morning person. You'd know that if you'd known her before—" Jonah stopped. Before, in this valley, meant before everything: before the feud calcified, before the forgery, before the vanishing. "You know what I mean."

"I know what you mean." He settled the ladder against the truck bed and looked at her for a moment with that steady gray attention of his, the look that had unnerved her the first year and steadied her ever since. "For what it's worth," he said, "her mother used to say the same thing about the tide. That it goes out so it can come back. She wrote it in a letter once. I kept that letter too."

Of course he had. Jonah laughed — short, surprised, warm. "You're a vault, Theo Lindqvist."

"I'm a caretaker," he said again, and got in the truck, and drove off toward the equipment shed with the ladder rattling behind him.

* * *

Mara arrived at four, as promised, in the truck she'd bought used after the divorce finalized — a good truck, Jonah had noted approvingly, the first purchase Mara had made purely for herself in fifteen years. She came up the drive with a folder of rootstock printouts under one arm and a thermos in her other hand, and she didn't knock, just shouldered the farmhouse door open the way she'd been doing since November, calling out that the coffee in this house was still an insult and she'd brought her own.

They sat at the kitchen table with the printouts spread between them, arguing companionably about rootstocks — Mara wanted something more drought-tolerant for the south slope; Jonah worried about cold pockets near the creek; they went back and forth until Theo wandered in, listened for ninety seconds, and said, "Split the difference. Plant both," with such finality that they both shut up and realized he was right.

Afterward Mara lingered, turning her cup slowly on the table, and Jonah knew the look. It was the look Mara got when she was circling something she didn't know how to say yet — the same look she'd had on the courthouse steps after the hearing, when the ruling had come down restoring her title and dissolving the lender's fraudulent liens, and justice had arrived so much quieter than either of them had braced for.

"The deed came back from the recorder's office," Mara said finally. "The new one."

Jonah's hands went still around her cup.

"I picked it up yesterday." Mara reached into the folder — not the rootstock section, the other pocket — and drew out an envelope, and set it on the table between them without opening it. "I haven't looked at it yet. I wanted—" She exhaled, annoyed at herself, at the sentimentality of it. "I wanted to do it here. At this table. With bad coffee."

"It's my coffee or no coffee, Mara."

"Exactly. An insult." But she was smiling, and her eyes were bright in a way she'd have denied under oath, and she slid the envelope across the table.

Jonah opened it with care, the way you open something you've waited a long time for.

The document inside was crisp and official and utterly ordinary-looking — heavy paper, embossed seal, the county's indifferent typography. But the names stopped her breath anyway, the way they had stopped it the first time she'd read them in the clerk's office two years ago, when the discovery had felt like a wound. Two names. Joint tenancy. The whole parcel — vineyard and orchard, creek and slope, every row and every tree — held together, undivided, by two women who had spent most of their lives on opposite sides of a fence.

**Jonah Eriksen. Mara Voss.**

Neither name above the other. Neither estranged.

She read it twice. Three times. Her throat worked around something that wouldn't come out as words.

"My lawyer says it's unusual," Mara said, studying her own hands. "People don't usually do this. Co-own a working farm with somebody they sued." A pause. "With somebody they used to love."

"Used to," Jonah managed.

"Oh, shut up." Mara laughed, wet-eyed and furious about it, and reached across the table and gripped Jonah's wrist hard, the way she had at seventeen, the way she had across the smudge-pot smoke on the frost night, the way she apparently always would — like a person holding on to proof. "You know what I mean."

"I know what you mean."

Outside, the light was going long and amber across the orchard, and the buds on the old Gravensteins had opened another fraction of a millimeter since morning, imperceptible and unstoppable, the trees deciding — every single one of them, without being asked — to try again.

* * *

That evening, after Mara had gone home to the cottage and Theo had gone wherever Theo went when he wasn't materializing exactly when needed, Jonah walked the fence line one last time.

The sun was setting behind the western ridge, laying bars of gold and shadow across the rows, and the air smelled of wet earth and blossom and the green mineral smell of the creek. She walked the whole boundary — hers, theirs, the line that was only a line now — and at the corner where the parcels met she stopped and looked back the way she'd come.

Five years ago she had inherited a failing vineyard and a stranger's debts and a lifetime habit of bracing for abandonment, and she had thought the task ahead of her was survival. Keep the land. Pay the loans. Trust no one. She had thought belonging was something you could inherit if you just held on tightly enough — to the property, to the name, to the version of her grandfather she'd needed to believe in. She had gripped so hard her knuckles had gone white, and it had nearly cost her everything: the harvest, the truth, Mara, herself.

But tides go out. That's what they do. They go out so they can come back — the water, the friendship, the honesty, the spring. Nothing in this valley stayed by force. It stayed because it chose to, season after season, the way the trees decided every year to bloom again, the way Mara had come home, the way Theo had kept faith through decades of silence, the way Jonah herself had stayed — not because she was trapped, not anymore, but because staying had finally become a choice instead of a fear.

She put her hand on the fence rail one last time, feeling the smooth worn wood, the new posts solid in the ground.

Then she turned and walked back through the open gate, leaving it open behind her, and headed for the farmhouse where the lights were on and the kettle was waiting and, folded in the drawer beside two useless padlocks, lay a copy of a deed with two names on it — neither one alone, neither one estranged, both of them home.

In the morning there would be rootstock to plant, and a label to design, and the first bottles of Last Tide to fill when autumn came around again. In the morning the tide would come back, the way it always did.

Tonight, Jonah slept without bracing for anything at all.
