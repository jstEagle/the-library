## 1. The Ghost's Contract

The manuscript weighed four pounds and eleven ounces. Beatrix knew this because she had weighed it on the kitchen scale that morning, standing barefoot in her flat with the city still gray outside the window, holding a year of her life in her hands like something she'd fished out of a river. Four pounds, eleven ounces. Two hundred and ninety-one pages of Julian Ashe's life as he wished it had happened.

She wrapped it in brown paper the way you'd wrap a gift for someone you didn't particularly like — neat, correct, without ribbon.

The publisher's offices occupied the top three floors of a building on Bedford Square, all glass and pale wood, the kind of place where the coffee came with a story about its origin. Beatrix signed in at reception under a name that wasn't hers. Not quite a lie — *B. Thornbury* was legally hers, after all — but not the truth either, because the truth was that the book upstairs bore only one name, and it wasn't hers, and it never would be.

"Ms. Thornbury." The publicist met her at the elevator, a young woman named Priya whose enthusiasm had not yet been sanded down by the industry. "We're so excited. Julian's people said the final chapters are—"

"They're in the bag," Beatrix said. "All of them. The arc closes where we discussed."

"You must be so proud."

Beatrix smiled the smile she kept for occasions like this, the one that cost nothing and revealed less. "I'm proud of the book."

It was even true, in its way. She was proud of it the way a seamstress is proud of a gown someone else will wear down the aisle. She had stitched every seam, set every sleeve, and when the bride turned in the mirror, glowing, no one would look at the hands that had made the glow possible. That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

* * *

The NDA lived in a drawer in her desk at home, and she thought of it sometimes the way other people thought of an old injury — not painful anymore, exactly, but present in cold weather. Forty-two pages. She had read every one of them before signing, which made her nearly unique among Julian's collaborators; his agent liked to joke that nobody read them, they just felt their weight. Forty-two pages specifying that the words were his, the experiences were his, the voice was his, and any suggestion to the contrary, in any forum, at any time, forever, constituted breach.

She had signed it six years ago, when ghostwriting Julian Ashe's memoirs had seemed like a ladder rather than a cage. He'd been charming then too — he was charming now, that was the whole architecture of him — leaning across the table at some long luncheon, saying, *You have a way with a sentence, Bea. Lend me your eyes.* Lend. As if language were a coat he could borrow and return. As if she wouldn't notice it never coming back.

And she hadn't noticed, or hadn't let herself. That was the trick of it. You could give away pieces of yourself indefinitely if you kept the whole locked somewhere no one asked to see. A sentence here, a memory there, a way of turning grief so it caught light — she had been lending herself out for eleven years, first under three different pennames, then under none at all, until the byline on her own ambitions had gone soft from disuse, like a word repeated too many times.

In the taxi back across the square she watched the plane trees shedding early leaves onto the pavement and thought: *I am very good at this.* And beneath it, quieter, in a voice she did not permit often into the room: *Good at this is not the same as good.*

* * *

Her flat smelled of paper and yesterday's tea. It was not a lonely flat, exactly. It was a flat arranged against loneliness — books double-shelved, a kettle always warm, the radio murmuring conversation into rooms she moved through alone. She had built it the way she built everything: competently, carefully, with no load-bearing walls left exposed.

She made tea she didn't drink and sat at the window with the contract copy open on her knees, not reading it, just feeling its presence. Clause 14.3: *The Author acknowledges that the Collaborator shall have no right, title, or interest in the Work.* The Author. Julian. She was, in the legal grammar of her own life, a footnote.

Her phone buzzed. Julian himself, because he had a sixth sense for the moments she almost thought about him clearly.

**Julian:** Heard the chapters landed. Brilliant. Dinner Friday? I want to talk about the tour. They want *me*, which means they want *us*. Wear something devastating.

She typed and deleted three replies. The first was yes. The second was a question about the fire chapter, which he had cut in revision — *do we really lose the orchard entirely?* — deleted because she already knew the answer, had watched him delete it himself with two fingers on the trackpad, saying, *Nobody wants the sad part, Bea. Sad parts don't sell hardbacks.* The third reply she actually sent:

**Beatrix:** Congratulations. Friday works.

Two sentences. She could feel how much of herself was not in them, the way you can feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

* * *

That night she dreamed of an orchard.

She dreamed in green and gold, in rows of trees heavy with fruit she couldn't name — apples, but also not apples, something sweeter and more specific that existed only in the dream and in a summer twenty years gone. In the dream she was seventeen and barefoot between the rows and someone was calling her name from the far end of the orchard, a boy's voice cracking on the second syllable, and she woke with her heart going hard and the word *Elias* dissolving on her lips like sugar in rain.

She lay in the dark and did not say it. She had gotten very good, over twelve years, at not saying it.

There was a box in her bedroom closet, top shelf, behind the winter coats. She had not opened it since the funeral. She knew what was in it the way you know what's in a sealed room: her father's papers, his ledgers, the deed to a piece of land in the country that the solicitor's letter last month had called *an asset requiring attention.* Thornbury Orchard. Sixty acres of apples and cider press and one corner, she remembered, that her father never replanted, year after year, though the soil there grew nothing but nettles and a silence he never explained.

*I should deal with it,* she thought, the way she'd thought it every month since the letter came. *I should go up, sign whatever needs signing, sell it, be done.*

She fell asleep again near dawn, and the dream did not come back, and in the morning the manuscript was gone from her table and the space where it had sat looked, for a moment, like a held breath.

* * *

Friday dinner was at a restaurant where the menus had no prices and the waiters knelt slightly when they spoke. Julian arrived late, which was punctuality for Julian, in a coat that probably had a name, and kissed the air beside both her cheeks and settled in like a man arriving at his own biography.

"There she is," he said. "The best-kept secret in publishing."

"That's one way to put it."

"It's the *only* way to put it, darling, that's the entire point of you." He ordered for them both without consulting her, which he had done for six years and which she had allowed for six years, and Beatrix watched him do it now with the detached interest of a woman observing weather. He was beautiful, Julian, in the lacquered way of expensive furniture — you could see your reflection in him if you looked, but it came back warped.

"The tour," he began, and talked for some time. Cities, festivals, a podcast whose name meant nothing to her. Then, over the fish course, lightly, the way he laid down anything that mattered: "I've had my people look at that property situation. The orchard."

Beatrix's glass stopped halfway to her mouth. "What property situation?"

"Your father's little empire. Apples." He waved a hand, dispersing the subject like smoke. "A tax nuisance, honestly. But there's a wrinkle — some old co-ownership clause, apparently. My solicitor says the deed is oddly drafted." He smiled at her over the candlelight, easy and golden. "I may need to be involved. Isn't that funny? Me, a farmer."

The restaurant noise receded. Somewhere under her ribs, an old floorboard creaked.

"I don't understand," she said carefully. "Why would your solicitor be looking at my father's deed?"

"Because it's *my* father's deed too, apparently." He laughed, delighted with the absurdity of it, and reached across to touch her wrist, a gesture calibrated to look affectionate to any passing stranger. "Small world, Bea. I'll have the papers sent over. Don't worry your lovely head about it."

He went back to the tour. He talked about Milan. Beatrix ate her fish and tasted nothing and understood, in the slow cold way of ice forming, that the past tense of her life — the sealed room, the closeted box, the name she did not say in her sleep — had just walked through the door of the present wearing an expensive coat and ordering for two.

Afterward, walking home through streets slick with autumn, she stopped at her building and did not go in. She stood on the step a long moment, keys in hand, looking at nothing.

Then she went upstairs, dragged the chair to the closet, and took down the box.

The string tying it was stiff with age. It gave on the second pull, and the smell of her father rose out of the cardboard — pipe smoke and pencil shavings and cold cellars — and Beatrix sat down on the floor of her careful, competent life and began, at last, to sort through what he had left her.

The deed was near the bottom, folded twice, the wax seal cracked like old skin.

She read the names once. Then she read them again, slower, her finger under each line as if the finger might catch an error the eye kept making.

*Edmund Thornbury,* of course. Her father.

And beneath him, in the section titled *Joint Tenants and Survivors,* two names she had no business seeing together anywhere in the world, much less on a single line of legal parchment:

*Elias Wren.*

*Julian Ashe.*

The lamp hummed. The radiator ticked. Outside, London went on being loud and indifferent, and Beatrix Thornbury sat on the floor with the document trembling very slightly in her hands, understanding that everything she had spent twelve years keeping locked away had just been unlocked by a piece of paper — and that neither of the two men whose names shared it with her dead father had any idea what they owned.
