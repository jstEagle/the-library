## 1. Closed on Sundays

There are three kinds of people who come into a dying bookstore at six minutes to close on a Saturday evening. The first kind wants directions. The second kind wants to argue about the price of a used hardcover, as though I set it personally out of spite. The third kind wants to talk, and they want to talk about grief, and they want to talk about it standing between the register and the door so I can't escape.

The man currently breathing on my counter was the third kind.

"It's just that my wife read everything," he said, holding a paperback of *The Bridges of Madison County* like it was a small injured bird. "Everything, you know? And now I don't know what she would have read next."

I did know. That was the terrible part. That was the whole job, if I was honest, which I tried not to be. Twelve years running the last bookshop in Millbrook, and what I'd actually become was a grief counselor with a card reader and a cat that showed up when it pleased.

"She'd have liked Kent Haruf," I said. "*Our Souls at Night.* It's quiet. Two old people, mostly talking. It won't hurt you."

"Nothing hurts anymore," he said, which was the saddest sentence I'd heard all week, and I'd heard my mother leave a voicemail.

I rang him up, gave him the good bag — the ones with the shop's name stamped on them, which I'd ordered back when I still believed in ordering things — and watched him walk out into the blue-gray evening of a town that had been closing, gently, for twenty years. The bell over the door did its little tin complaint. I turned the sign to CLOSED and stood there a moment with my palm flat against the glass, watching the street.

Millbrook at dusk, October: three streetlights working out of seven, the hardware store's window display unchanged since the Obama administration, and down at the end of Main, the old train station sitting behind its chain-link fence like a widow who'd stopped opening her mail. The station was my favorite ugly thing in town. Brick gone the color of an old bruise, windows boarded, the platform clock frozen at 4:17 — some afternoon in 2003 when the last train pulled out and nobody official ever came back to wind it. Kids said it was haunted. Kids said that about the library too, and the feed store, and Mrs. Okafor's house, because when a town starts dying, everything in it becomes a ghost by association.

I was, technically, one of the things it had become a ghost of. Voss & Daughters, Books, the sign said, though there were no daughters in the partnership and hadn't been since my father died. Just me. Jude Voss, thirty-eight, keeper of eleven thousand books nobody in a five-mile radius wanted badly enough to keep the lights interesting.

My phone lit up on the counter. MOM. I watched it ring with the particular calm of a man watching a weather system approach from across a valley. She'd want to talk about the reunion. The Voss family reunion, three weeks out, held every other year at the estate like a shareholders' meeting with casseroles, and this year — this year being the twentieth anniversary of the station closing, a coincidence my mother insisted was not a theme — Eleanor Voss had decided it would be *special*. Special meant seated. Special meant place cards. Special meant I was expected to arrive with a person.

The voicemail came through while I was counting the register, and I listened to it the way you take a castor oil dose.

"Jude. It's your mother. I've spoken with your aunt Carol about the seating and we need to finalize your guest situation by Friday. I'm not going to make a thing of it. Your cousin Denise is bringing her husband. Even Denise, Jude. Call me. Also the gutters at your shop — I drove past, they're hanging. It reflects on all of us."

It reflects on all of us. The Voss family motto, roughly. We were, as far as I could tell, the last old money in the valley, which is to say we were money-shaped and the money had mostly drained out, leaving the shape: the estate, the name, the opinions, the reunion. My mother presided over all of it from a kitchen the size of my entire shop, drinking decaf at nine a.m. like it was a sacrament.

I did not call back. I had a system for my mother and the system was: survive until Friday, then produce a guest. The guest was the problem. The guest was the reason I'd spent two weeks doing the arithmetic of humiliation. Because the truth was that if I showed up alone, my mother would spend the entire evening performing Concern, and if I showed up with Wren only, she'd perform Concern with a different inflection, and if I showed up with anyone at all, the family would perform Delight, which was worse, because Delight meant questions, and questions meant the locked room.

Here is the thing I believe, and I believe it the way some people believe in gravity: the heart is a muscle, and muscles are for containment. You hold. You don't spill. I learned it at twenty-six, holding my wife's hand in a hospital room while a machine breathed for her, and I learned it harder at the funeral when my mother gripped my elbow and said, "You'll want to be strong for the family," and I understood that in her language, strong meant *sealed*. I have been sealed ever since. It's not a tragedy. It's a practice. Like piano. Some days I'm very good at it.

The trouble with a practice is it only works when nobody watches too closely.

"Dad?" Wren's voice came down the stairs from the apartment above the shop, our apartment, all low ceilings and book smell. "Is it pizza night or are we sad?"

"We're never sad on pizza night," I called back. "That's the whole point of pizza."

"Because you have a face."

"I always have a face. It's one of my features."

She appeared on the stairs, twelve years old, all elbows and judgment, wearing a hoodie she'd decorated with a constellation pattern in fabric paint that had, over two years, faded into what looked like a rash of small accusations. She stood at the bottom step and looked at me with her mother's exact level stare, and I did what I always do with that stare, which is to meet it for one second and then become extremely busy with the register.

"Mom's mom called Grandma," she said. "Grandma called me. About the reunion."

"Your grandmother calls everyone about the reunion. It's how she metabolizes."

"She asked if you were seeing anyone. She asked *me*, Dad. She asked me like I'm a source."

"You are a source. You're my primary source. You leak constantly."

"I don't leak." She came and sat on the counter, which was against shop policy and also the only place she'd willingly sit near me lately, so I allowed it, the way you allow a cat on the piano. "I just don't lie."

"There it is," I said. "The family talent, skipping a generation."

She didn't laugh. She was in the phase — I'm told it's a phase — where my jokes landed on her like weather, noted and unimproved. She picked at the counter's old scar, a burn mark from a candle I'd lit the week her mother died and never lit again.

"Dad. Are we okay? Money-wise."

The question went through me like a splinter finding its depth. Because the honest answer was *we're okay the way a town is okay, technically open, structurally tired*, and the answer I gave was the one I'd been giving for a year, smooth as a practiced lie gets:

"We're fine. October's slow. Christmas fixes October."

"You said that last year."

"And here we are, fixed."

"Grandma said the shop's a hobby. She said real Vosses don't keep shops, they keep *holdings*."

"Your grandmother," I said, "thinks the word 'hobby' is a diagnosis. Ignore her. Pizza."

I made the call to Sal's — the pizzeria, one of nine businesses left on Main, and the only one that still delivered — and Wren went up to set the table, and I stood alone in the shop with the lights half-off doing the thing I do every night, which is walk the aisles and straighten what doesn't need straightening. It's not tidying. It's inspection. It's me checking that the room is still holding, that the spines are still aligned, that the world inside these walls is still a world I can govern. Outside the walls, the town was going under one storefront at a time, and my family had spent twenty years pretending that was weather, and I had spent twenty years pretending I didn't know what my family knew.

I want to be clear that I didn't know, not exactly, not the whole shape of it. I knew the station closed in 2003 and the town's spine went with it. I knew the Voss name was spoken differently by the older people in town — not warmly, not coldly, but carefully, like a word in a language they weren't sure I still understood. I knew my father, in his last year, had gotten strange about the archive room in the shop's basement, the one with the town ledgers and the old county records, and had said once, drunk on two fingers of rye, "You'll want to know where things are buried, Jude, before you decide to dig." I'd filed it under fatherly eccentricity, the way you file everything when your heart is a locked room and the room is full.

The pizza came. Wren and I ate at the kitchen table under the one bulb that worked in the fixture, and she told me about school — a boy named Theo who could do a backflip, a science unit on decomposition that she was taking personally — and I told her nothing, the way I always told her nothing, and I could see her noticing that I was telling her nothing, and I could see her deciding, at twelve, to start keeping her own locked room, and I could not figure out how to stop it without opening mine, and so I passed the parmesan.

"Can we go by the station tomorrow?" she said, mouth full. "Priya says there's a truck there. A big truck. Somebody's leasing the concourse, my friend's mom works at the county office and she saw the paperwork."

"Somebody's leasing the concourse," I repeated. "Of the station."

"That's what I said."

Nobody had leased anything at the station in twenty years. The Voss family owned the land — a fact stated in my mother's kitchen the way you'd state a blood type — and the family had let it sit like a held breath. A truck at the station was a pebble in a shoe. Small. Impossible to ignore.

"Maybe it's the county," I said. "Maybe it's surveyors."

"Maybe it's a person," Wren said, with the terrible simplicity of twelve. "Priya says it's a woman. A chef. She's opening a *bakery*, Dad. In the station. With ovens."

"A bakery," I said, "in a train station that hasn't had a train in twenty years," and I laughed, because it was funny, a stranger arriving with ovens and hope into a town that had run out of both, and I laughed a little too long, and Wren looked at me across the pizza box with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to.

After she went to bed I sat in the shop's back room with the ledger of the shop itself — the actual ledger, paper, my father's habit — and ran numbers that came out the way they always came out, which was: fine for now. Fine for now was my whole financial philosophy. Fine for now and a locked heart and a town that smelled like cold brick and dead leaves. I wrote the day's total in the book. I listened to the building settle.

Then I took out my phone and did the thing I'd been avoiding for two weeks: I opened the town's community board, the one Mrs. Okafor's granddaughter maintained, and scrolled past the lost cat and the leaf schedule and the notice about the autumn festival — the festival, which now that I thought about it was always scheduled to honor the station, though in recent years it had thinned into a craft fair with a train theme, like a memorial service that had forgotten who died — and there it was, third from the top, posted that afternoon:

*Coming soon to the Old Millbrook Station: Marisol Reyes, pastry chef. Grand opening at the Autumn Festival. Yes, the station. Yes, really. Come hungry. — M.R.*

Below it, a photo: a woman I didn't recognize, flour on one forearm, standing in front of the station's boarded windows with a grin like she'd already won something. She looked, in the flat light of the photo, like a person who had never once in her life practiced containment. She looked like a lit match walking into a room full of what we kept in this town.

I locked the phone. I told myself it was nothing. A bakery fails here the way everything fails here, gently, within the year; the ovens would go cold and the concourse would go back to its pigeons and the town would have one more ghost to scare its kids with.

I turned off the lights. I stood a moment in the dark of my shop, in the dark of my town, in the dark of my well-practiced heart, and I thought: fine for now.

Upstairs, Wren's light was still on under her door. I almost knocked. I stood there with my hand raised and heard her inside, talking to someone — the phone, probably, Priya, probably telling her everything I wasn't telling anyone — and I lowered my hand and went to bed.

Friday, I thought. I have until Friday to produce a person.

Outside, the wind came down Main Street the way it always did, from the direction of the station, carrying the smell of brick and rust and, for the first time in twenty years — faint, impossible, probably nothing — cinnamon.
