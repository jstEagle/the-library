## 9. Almost

### Jude

The storm came in off the ridge around four o'clock, the way October storms do in Millbrook — without announcement, without apology, dropping the temperature ten degrees in an hour and turning Main Street into a river of leaves. By five, the lights flickered. By six, they gave up entirely, and I stood in the middle of my darkened bookshop holding a candle I'd bought for exactly this purpose and never once believed I'd use.

"Great," said Wren, who was doing her math homework at the front table by the light of her phone. "Now I can fail algebra in atmospheric lighting."

"You're not failing algebra."

"I'm failing algebra *aesthetically*, then."

I lit two more candles and set them on the counter, and the shop did that thing it does in low light — went soft, went secret, all those spines turning to shadows and gold leaf. It occurred to me, as I watched Wren squint at fractions, that I had never done inventory night by candlelight before. Inventory night was supposed to be Thursday. It was Tuesday. The schedule had been Marisol's idea originally — she'd offered to help, back when helping was still a thing she did casually, before the letters and the ledger and everything we were both carefully not saying.

She knocked at seven.

I knew it was her before I opened the door, because who else knocks at a bookshop during a blackout? The whole town was home eating dinner by flashlight. But there she stood on the step, rain-damp, flour dust still somehow on her sleeve despite the storm, holding a paper bag against her chest like a shield.

"I heard the power's out," she said. "I brought rolls. The ones that survived the generator failure. There were casualties."

"How many casualties?"

"Four dozen. We don't speak of them." She stepped inside, shaking rain off her jacket, and stopped when she saw the candles. Something moved across her face — surprise, or something adjacent to it. "You look like a novel," she said. "Like the cover of one. *Candlelit Bookshop, Woman Runs From Her Past.*"

"That's not a genre."

"It could be. I'd buy it." She set the bag on the counter and started unpacking rolls with the efficiency of someone who needed her hands busy, which is a thing I recognized because it is also a thing I do. "So. Inventory. Show me how it works."

And that's how it began: the three of us in a candlelit bookshop while the storm leaned its shoulder against the windows. Wren abandoned algebra — "inventory is basically math, Mom, I'm being educational" — and appointed herself counter of the poetry section, which meant she counted to forty-seven and then declared the section complete. Marisol worked the cooking shelves, calling out titles in a voice that had gone warm and unhurried, and I climbed the ladder with a clipboard and tried very hard not to notice how the room had changed shape.

It wasn't the candles. Candles are just fire behaving itself. It was the way she'd fit herself into my space without asking permission, the way she'd tucked her hair behind her ear with a floury hand and left a white streak at her temple like a streak of premature wisdom. It was the sound of her laughing at something Wren said — really laughing, head tipped back — and the way my chest did something unprofessional about it.

Here is what you need to understand about me. I have spent eleven years treating my heart like a muscle, and muscles, if you train them, do what you tell them. You tell them to hold. They hold. You tell them not to reach. They don't reach. Grief made a gymnasium of me, and I have been lifting ever since — the shop, the mortgage, Wren, the slow daily weight of a town that keeps forgetting itself. I am strong in the specific ways that matter. I am not built for this: a woman in my shop calling out *Julia Child, second edition, one copy* while rain writes longhand down the glass.

"Jude." She was looking up at me on the ladder. "You've been staring at *Mastering the Art* for a full minute. Either you love it very much or you've forgotten how clipboards work."

"Inventory face," I said. "It's a professional condition."

"Mm." She didn't look convinced. She looked, in fact, like a woman filing something away for later, and I climbed down one rung and told my heart to hold, and it held, mostly.

### Marisol

There is a version of this story where I stayed home. Where I ate leftover conchas alone in my apartment above the station and listened to the storm rattle the platform lights and thought about my father and did not walk six blocks through horizontal rain to a bookshop that smelled like old paper and cedar. That version exists. I chose not to live in it, and I'd like the record to show I knew exactly what I was doing.

Because here's the thing about Jude Voss: they are so careful. Careful with words, careful with distance, careful with that clipboard held like a small bureaucratic shield between us. And careful people, when they finally crack, crack interestingly. I wanted to see it. I'm not proud of that. Or maybe I am. Maybe wanting to see someone come undone is just another word for wanting to be let in.

We worked through the fiction section, then biography. Wren fell asleep at nine-thirty, folded into the armchair by the window with a volume of poems open on her chest like a fallen bird, and neither of us moved to wake her. The storm got louder. The candles burned lower. And somewhere around the history shelves, the work stopped being work.

"Tell me something true," I said. I was sitting on the floor; Jude was cross-legged across from me, a stack of uncatalogued local histories between us like a border checkpoint. "Since we're trapped here. One true thing. House rules."

"This is my house. I make the rules."

"Fine. Then rule one: one true thing."

Jude looked at me for a long moment. Candlelight is generous and merciless at once — it showed me every line of that guarded face, and the exact moment the guard shifted weight from one foot to the other.

"My father used to read to me in this room," Jude said. "Right there, that corner. He died when Wren was three months old, and I kept the shop because closing it would have been the second worst day of my life, and I'd already had the first." A pause. "That's two things. I overshot."

"That's the truest thing anyone's said to me since I got to Millbrook."

"Low bar. The last true thing you heard was probably from the health inspector."

"The health inspector is incapable of truth. He communicates exclusively in citations." I pulled a book off the stack — a slim blue thing, water-stained, *A History of the Millbrook Line* — and turned it over in my hands, and felt the old ache shift in its sleep. My father's handwriting lived somewhere in that subject. My father's whole ruined career lived in that subject. "My turn. True thing: I didn't come here for a fresh start. I came here because of the station. Everything else — the bakery, the lease, even—" I gestured vaguely at the space between us, at whatever this was becoming, "—that's all built on top of it. Like a house on a foundation nobody inspected."

Jude went very still. Stillness, I'd learned, was their tell. When the truth got close, Jude Voss became furniture.

"I know," Jude said quietly.

"You know?"

"Some of it. Your father. The closure." Those eyes came up and met mine, and there was something in them I hadn't seen before — guilt, wearing the costume of caution. "Marisol—"

Thunder cut them off. The whole shop shuddered, and a candle guttered, and in the half-second of darkness I heard Jude move — not away. Closer. When the flame caught again they were nearer than they'd been, close enough that I could smell rain and old paper and something underneath, soap or skin or just them, and neither of us had retreated to our respective sides of the book-stack border.

"Rule two," I said. My voice had dropped without permission. "No more true things tonight. They're dangerous in this lighting."

"Agreed," Jude said, and did not move away.

I want to be honest about what happened next, or almost happened, because honesty is supposedly the theme of my year. We leaned in. Both of us. It was mutual, it was slow, it was the kind of slow that has been waiting weeks for permission it will never formally receive. I watched their eyes drop to my mouth. I felt my own breath go shallow. The storm pressed at the windows like a crowd pressing toward a stage, and the candle flames leaned, and the distance between us was maybe two inches and shrinking, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *his father read to him in that corner, and mine drove the last train out of Millbrook, and we are both just children of this town's wreckage, reaching—*

"Wren," Jude said.

Not waking. Not yet. But stirring — a small sound from the armchair, a dream-noise, the kind that precedes the real thing. Jude was on their feet before I'd finished processing, moving toward their daughter with the reflexive speed of eleven years of practice, and the two inches stayed two inches forever.

### Jude

The nightmare came the way Wren's nightmares always come: all at once, no build-up, a small body bolt upright and crying before the mind fully arrives. I gathered her up. She was hot and damp and saying something about a map, about a map that wouldn't stay flat, which made no sense and perfect sense, because dreams don't owe us coherence.

"It's okay," I said into her hair. "It's the storm. Storms are loud but they're just weather. They can't get in."

"You were gone," she mumbled against my shoulder. "In the dream everybody was gone."

"We're all here. Look." I turned so she could see the room: the candles, the books, Marisol standing by the history shelf with her hands clasped, looking like she wished she could make herself smaller and couldn't decide whether to leave. "See? Everyone's here."

Wren looked. Her eyes found Marisol and stayed there, heavy-lidded, and then she did the thing that undid me: she lifted one arm. Toward Marisol. The universal toddler-to-trusted-adult gesture, deployed by a nine-year-old who should have outgrown it years ago, aimed like a compass needle at the woman I'd almost kissed ninety seconds earlier.

Marisol crossed the room like it was nothing, like it was the most natural motion in the world, and took her. Settled her against one shoulder with a practiced ease that suggested either extensive babysitting experience or something older, something cellular. Wren's fist closed in her collar.

"Storms are just weather," Marisol murmured, echoing me, and Wren was asleep again inside a minute. Of course she was. Children trust the person who doesn't hesitate.

I stood there with the clipboard I had apparently never put down, holding it against my sternum like a breastplate, and performed the arithmetic I'd been avoiding all night. My daughter, asleep on the shoulder of the woman whose family my family ruined. My almost-kiss, hanging in the candlelit air between us, unclaimed. The ledger in the archive drawer upstairs, hidden, complicit, waiting. Every thread of my life pulling tight in one small room, and me standing in the middle pretending to be a bookstore.

"She's out," Marisol whispered. "Where do you want her?"

"Upstairs. I'll take her."

But when I reached for Wren, Marisol didn't hand her over immediately. For a moment we stood there, the three of us connected — my daughter between us, my hands on Wren's back, Marisol's breath close enough to feel — and I watched Marisol's face do something complicated and then settle into kindness.

"Go on," she said softly. "I'll blow out the candles."

I carried Wren up the back stairs and put her in bed, and she curled around a pillow and slept the boneless sleep of the innocent, and I stood in her doorway longer than necessary because downstairs was a door I wasn't ready to walk back through.

The heart is a muscle. That's the creed. Muscles hold. Muscles lift. Muscles do not lean two inches across a stack of local histories and ask for what they want. If I let go of the weight now — after eleven years of training, after building a life that functions precisely because nothing in it reaches — everything I've held up comes down. Wren first. The shop. Myself, probably, in some structural way I couldn't afford to investigate.

When I came back down, Marisol had banked the candles and was buttoning her coat by the window. The storm had softened to steady rain. She looked up at me, and her face was composed and warm and completely unreadable, and I understood that she had done her own arithmetic while I was upstairs, and arrived at her own answer.

"Inventory's nearly done," she said lightly. "Same time Thursday? Assuming the power cooperates."

"Assuming," I said.

She paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and for a second I thought she might say something true — rule one, house rules, one true thing before the weather releases us. Her mouth opened. Closed. Whatever it was, she swallowed it, and I watched it go down, and hated myself for being relieved.

"Goodnight, Jude," she said.

"Goodnight."

The bell over the door gave its small silver cough, and she was gone into the rain, and I stood in my dark shop surrounded by forty thousand books, every one of them containing people brave enough to say the thing, and blew out the last candle myself.

Upstairs, I did not sleep. I lay listening to the rain and thinking about the ledger in the archive drawer, and about Eleanor's voice on the phone that afternoon — *some strangers ask too many questions, Jude, be careful who you let close* — and about two inches of candlelit air that I had left exactly as they were, because leaving things as they were is the only skill I have ever mastered.

Thursday, I told myself. We'll finish inventory Thursday. Everything can wait until Thursday.

It was a lie, and I was fairly sure we both knew it, and the rain kept falling on the roof like applause for a performance only one of us believed.
