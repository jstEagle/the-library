## 14. The Festival Opens

### Marisol

The morning of the grand opening, I burned the first batch of croissants and considered it an omen I could live with.

"Everything's fine," I told Dot Priddy, who had arrived at six a.m. ostensibly to help and actually to stand in my kitchen doorway narrating the town's expectations like a sports commentator. "It's a sacrifice to the oven gods. They appreciate a tithe."

"They appreciate a croissant," Dot said, peering at the blackened tray. "That's what they appreciate."

I slid the tray into the sink and started over. The truth was my hands had been unsteady since dawn, and not because of the ovens. The concourse of the old Millbrook station had never held this many people, not in twenty years, and today it would hold all of them — every soul in town who had a memory of a train, which was every soul in town above the age of thirty. My father's station. My father's ruin. My father's name, which I had spent a week deciding whether to print on the banner that now hung over the ticket windows: **REYES BAKERY — GRAND OPENING — MILLBROOK AUTUMN FESTIVAL**. I'd printed it. My hands had decided before my head did.

The ovens were new but the building remembered. I'd spent two months learning its moods — the way the east windows went gold at four in the afternoon, the way the floorboards over the old platform sang when you walked them, the way the wind came through the gaps in the freight doors and sounded, if you let yourself imagine it, like a whistle. I had not let myself imagine it much. Imagination was a luxury for people whose fathers hadn't died with a cardboard box of belongings and a pension that evaporated like steam.

But today the freight doors were propped open, and the wind was coming through, and I was letting it.

"Marisol." Dot again, lower now, her hand on my elbow. "They're coming."

I looked up from the second batch — risen, golden, forgiven — and saw what she meant. Not a crowd. A trickle. Then not a trickle. People came up the old ramp from the parking lot where the platform used to extend toward the coast, and they stopped at the threshold the way people stop at the doors of churches they were raised in but haven't entered in years. An old man in a canvas jacket took off his hat. An old woman touched the ticket window frame with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head.

"That's Walter Ives," Dot murmured. "He drove the 7:42 to Hartwell for nineteen years. He used to let my Tommy ride in the cab on Saturdays."

Walter Ives crossed the concourse slowly, reading the walls — the framed photographs I'd hung of the station in its working years, borrowed from the historical society and from shoeboxes in people's attics. He stopped at one: a man in a stationmaster's cap, arm around a boy of maybe ten, both squinting into a sun that had set two decades ago.

"That's Rafael," Walter said. Not a question.

"My father," I said. "Rafael Reyes."

Walter turned and looked at me for a long moment. Whatever he saw in my face — the same face, people kept saying, the same face — made him take my hand in both of his, his palms rough as brake linings.

"You have his chin," he said. "And his way of standing. Like you're waiting for a train that matters."

I laughed, which was a surprise, and blinked, which was more of one. "I'm waiting for an oven timer that matters. Close enough."

And that was how it began. By nine o'clock the concourse was full, the cinnamon rolls were gone, the sourdough was going, and the old-timers had arranged themselves along the benches under the departure board — the board I'd restored, blank where the destinations used to be, and someone had already chalked *HARTWELL* into the first slot in handwriting that shook with feeling.

I moved among them with a tray of coffee and a notebook in my apron pocket.

I want to be honest about what I was doing, because I've decided to be honest about most things now. I was working. Every story they told — about the 7:42, about the freight schedule, about the winter of '98 when the line iced over and Rafael Reyes slept in the station two nights running to keep the switches thawed — every story went into the notebook, with a name and a date and a *may I call on you if it comes to that* asked so softly it barely counted as asking. They all said yes. They said it the way people say yes to doctors and priests, with the particular relief of the long-silenced.

My father had been blamed for the closure. That was the story Millbrook told itself: the stationmaster who couldn't save the line, who must have done something, who must have failed somehow, because the alternative story — that the line was taken away on purpose, that the land was wanted more than the town was — that story required villains, and nobody wants to live next door to villains. Especially not when the villains have a name on the library wing.

But the men on the benches remembered things. They remembered the rerouting study that appeared and vanished. They remembered a county meeting in 2003 that was rescheduled twice and then held at two in the afternoon on a Tuesday, when everyone who worked for a living was, well, working for a living. They remembered that the land under the station changed hands within the year, cheap, to a buyer nobody could quite name, and that the name, when it finally surfaced, was one everyone knew.

They didn't say *Voss*. They said *you know whose*, and looked at their coffee.

I wrote it all down. And every time the guilt came — and it came, it came in waves, because these were kind people handing me their grief like I was collecting for something — I reminded myself of the letter in my office, the one my father wrote my mother in the last hard winter: *They took the line, mija, and they took my name with it, and I could not fight a thing I could not see.* I could see it now. I was the seeing.

The bell over the freight door — my bell, new brass, but hung on the old hook — rang, and I looked up expecting another customer and felt the particular drop in my stomach that I have stopped pretending is anything other than what it is.

Jude Voss came in with Wren on one side and a stack of folding chairs on the other, and the whole concourse did a thing — a ripple, a held breath, a hundred small calculations — because everyone knew whose daughter that was, and everyone knew whose land this had been, and nobody knew what to do with the arithmetic.

Jude set the chairs down, looked around the room full of people who had just stopped talking, and said, clearly, in the voice of someone announcing a book title: "It's about time somebody put the lights back on in here."

The room exhaled. Walter Ives laughed first, and the sound broke the spell like ice going out of a river. And Jude — Jude looked at me across the crowd, and there was something in their face I hadn't seen before, or had seen and refused to name: the look of a person who has chosen a side and is standing on it, in the open, in the wind.

Wren broke away and came straight at me at a dead run.

"Marisol. Marisol. Do you have the ones with the sugar leaves? The little maple ones? You said."

"I said I might have them."

"You *said*."

I crouched to her level. "Behind the case. Top shelf. Don't tell your parent I showed you where the good ones live, or they'll never trust me again."

"They already trust you," Wren said, with the devastating precision of eight-year-olds, and went to collect her bribe.

Jude arrived in the space she'd vacated, and for a moment we just stood in the middle of my father's station, with the crowd's noise rising around us like water coming back into a dry riverbed.

"Nice banner," Jude said.

"Nice chairs."

"I brought forty. Dot says we'll need sixty."

"Dot's been right about everything since six a.m. It's insufferable."

Jude smiled — the real one, the one I'd only seen a handful of times, the one that rearranged their whole face — and then said, quieter, "People are talking to you. The railway people. I saw Walter."

"I'm not spying, Jude."

"I know." And then, and this is the part I keep turning over even now: "I'm not either. Anymore. I just want you to know that if anyone asks me whose bakery this is, I'm going to say the Reyes bakery, and I'm going to say it loudly, and I'm going to keep saying it."

The oven timer went off in the back. I have never been so grateful to an appliance in my life.

"Go be charming," I said. "You're good at it. It's alarming."

### Jude

I have spent my whole life being a Voss in public, which means I have spent my whole life being watched by people who are doing arithmetic. Today was the first day I've ever done the arithmetic myself, out loud, in front of everyone, and gotten a different answer than the one I was raised on.

The answer was: this is Marisol's bakery, and it is a good thing, and it is in a building my family helped empty.

I worked the crowd the way I work a slow Saturday in the shop — recommendations, small talk, the gentle art of making people feel seen. But today the recommendations were cinnamon rolls and the small talk had a current running under it. Old-timers kept drifting up to me, testing.

"You're Eleanor's," said a woman I half-recognized from the garden society, not unkindly.

"I'm Wren's," I said, and she laughed, and something in the room shifted a degree warmer.

That was the whole strategy, if you could call it a strategy: I'm Wren's. I'm the bookshop's. I'm the one who brought the folding chairs. Every Voss association I could not renounce — my mother, my name, the wing of the library — I buried under the ones I could. And the strange thing, the thing I'll be unpacking in therapy for years if Millbrook ever gets a therapist, is that it didn't feel like betrayal. It felt like taking off a coat I'd forgotten I was wearing.

By noon the concourse was standing-room only. The departure board read HARTWELL in its shaky chalk, and beneath it someone had added *7:42 — ON TIME* in a different hand, and Walter Ives stood under it telling a story about a blizzard in '99 with the whole crowd leaning in, and I watched Marisol move through them — refilling coffee, laughing, notebook out, writing — and understood that she was doing two things at once, and that one of them was a kind of work I'd only ever seen done in novels.

"She's good at this," Dot Priddy said, appearing at my elbow with the unerring timing of a woman who has made eavesdropping a civic duty.

"She's good at everything. It's insufferable."

"That's what I said." Dot sipped her coffee. "You know her father ran this station like it was a ship. Strict. Kind. Knew everyone's name." She paused. "She has his chin."

"So I've heard."

"And your mother's lawyer was in the hardware store this morning," Dot continued, in the same tone, the way you'd mention rain, "asking Mr. Okafor whether he'd kept records from when he worked the freight office. Asking real friendly. Real friendly like a subpoena."

I felt the floor of the day tilt a half-degree.

"Which lawyer?"

"The tall one. The one with the cufflinks. The one who came to the shop last spring and tried to return a book that was clearly water-damaged and blame it on my cat." She patted my arm. "Anyway. Lovely opening. Lovely rolls. Wren's got icing on her chin."

I found Marisol at the counter, boxing the last of the maple-leaf cookies for the school table, and told her quietly, watching her face do the thing it does — going very still, very bright, like a lamp turned up to hide the wiring.

"Okafor," she said. "He was freight clerk in '03. He'd have seen the transfer paperwork."

"He'd have seen everything."

"Yes," Marisol said. She closed the pastry box with a small, deliberate click. "So would my father. That's rather the point."

The crowd noise swelled behind us — Walter had reached the blizzard, and the blizzard was going well. Marisol looked out at it, at all of them, at the chalk on the board, and when she turned back to me her voice had dropped into a register I hadn't heard from her before. Not the bakery voice. Not the fake-date voice. The voice from the night she showed me the letter, the one that sounded like a woman holding a door open against a wind.

"Jude. If they start leaning on people before the hearing — if Okafor gets a visit, if Walter gets a call —"

"Then we get to them first."

"That's not a small thing you're offering."

"No," I said. "It's really not."

She held my eyes for a moment longer than the room's noise could excuse, and then Wren arrived between us with icing on her chin and a cookie in each hand, and the moment folded itself away like a letter returned to its envelope.

But I carried it with me the rest of the day, through the last tray of rolls, through the closing toasts, through the crowd draining out into the gold October afternoon. And when the freight doors were finally shut and the concourse went quiet — that particular station quiet, the quiet of a room built for departures — I helped her stack the chairs, and neither of us mentioned the tall lawyer with the cufflinks, because we were both thinking about him, and neither of us wanted to be the one to say that the festival had only just opened, and the real season was about to begin.

Marisol locked the freight door behind us. Then she stopped, keys in hand, and looked back through the glass at the departure board — HARTWELL, 7:42, ON TIME — and said, mostly to herself:

"He'd have loved this."

"Your father?"

"He used to say the station was the one room in town where everybody was going somewhere." She turned to me. "I intend to find out who decided they shouldn't be. All the way down."

The keys made a small bright sound in her hand, like a bell rung once, at the start of something.
