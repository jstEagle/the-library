## 19. Aftermath

There is a particular silence a bookstore makes when nobody comes in. Most people never hear it. Most people never have occasion to. It's the sound of a thousand conversations happening without you — spines murmuring to each other on the shelves, dust settling on the front table like snow on a field no one will walk across. I had heard it before, in the lean years, the years when the mill closed and the town held its breath. But I had never heard it sound quite like this: not poor, exactly. *Judged.*

The hearing was four days ago. Word travels fast in a town the size of Millbrook, and word of what I did travels faster, because it came attached to the name Voss, and in Millbrook the name Voss has always been a kind of currency. I had spent it. Publicly. At a folding table in the community hall, in front of the council, the county clerk, three former railway men with their good jackets on, and my mother, who walked out before I finished the second sentence and did not look back, which was its own kind of testimony.

The council voted to reopen the land grant inquiry. The Reyes family's claim — Marisol's claim, her father's claim, the claim he carried like a stone in his chest until the stone killed him — was restored to the docket. Justice, it turns out, does not arrive like a train. It arrives like a zoning procedure.

And the town, being a town, divided itself the way towns do: cleanly, along lines nobody had drawn before but everybody seemed to already know the location of.

* * *

The morning after the vote, I opened the shop at nine as always, and Mrs. Halloran was waiting outside. Mrs. Halloran had bought her mysteries from me for eleven years. She liked the ones where the detective was a cat. She stood on the sidewalk with her purse clutched in both hands and looked at me the way you look at a house that used to belong to a friend.

"I'm sure you had your reasons, Jude," she said.

"I did."

"Mm." She adjusted her glasses. "The Hallorans shopped at Voss Hardware for forty years. My Arthur bought his first hammer there. When the Voss family does a thing, there's generally a reason, and the reason is generally the Voss family." She said it without malice, which was somehow worse. "I'll get my books in Corning for now. The drive does Arthur good."

She did not come in. She did not come in the next day, or the day after that, and by the end of the week I had a small constellation of absences I could chart from the register: the Hallorans, the Pruitts, Dale from the feed store who had never once in his life bought a book but came in to argue about weather and had apparently decided I no longer deserved his weather. Dale. I lost *Dale.*

Wren noticed before I said anything. Wren notices everything; she is nine and she has the surveillance capabilities of a small government. She was doing homework at the reading table by the window, the one with the wobbly leg I keep promising to fix, and she looked up on Thursday morning when the bell over the door stayed silent for the third hour running.

"Nobody's coming," she said. Not an accusation. An observation, delivered in the flat tone she'd inherited from me, God help us both.

"Some people are upset with me."

"Because of the hearing? Because you told the truth?"

"That's the general theory, yes."

She chewed the end of her pencil, considering this with the seriousness she brought to long division. "That seems like a bad system," she said finally. "Where you get punished for the true thing." And then she went back to her fractions, and I stood there in my own shop, in the dust-moted light, feeling like the true thing and the punished thing were both sitting in my chest, taking up more room than they used to.

* * *

Saturday morning was the worst of it. Saturdays had always been our day — story hour at ten, the register humming, Wren stationed at the children's table like a tiny employee of the month. I opened the doors at nine and the bell rang its little brass hello and then nothing. The street was bright and ordinary. People walked past the window. A few of them looked in. One woman — I won't say who, though she has a laugh I used to enjoy — saw me looking and turned her face away with the deliberate speed of someone who has rehearsed it.

Wren appeared at my elbow. She had put on the cardigan Marisol gave her, the yellow one with the cinnamon-roll-colored buttons, which I suspect was a tactical decision on her part, though I couldn't have said how. She took my hand.

Not grabbed it. Took it, the way you take something that has been left out in the rain and needs bringing inside. Her hand was small and warm and slightly sticky, because she is nine and her hands are permanently slightly sticky, and we stood together behind the empty register like the last two employees of a company that had folded.

"You don't have to stay," I said. "You could go to Dee's. Watch cartoons. It's going to be a slow one."

"I'm staying," she said. "Somebody has to hold the shop."

"Sweetheart, the shop doesn't need holding."

"Everybody needs holding." She said it the way children say enormous things — lightly, already moving on — and then she climbed onto the stool behind the register, which was my stool, and began arranging the bookmarks by color, and I understood that I had been dismissed by a person half my height, and that she was right.

We passed the morning that way. She read aloud from a picture book about a lonely lighthouse to an audience of zero, doing the voices. I shelved returns. The silence in the shop changed quality somewhere around eleven — stopped being the silence of judgment and became something else, something almost companionable, the silence of two people keeping a vigil. I put on the kettle. I let her have cocoa at an hour that would have scandalized her pediatrician. The register rang once, at noon, for a man I didn't recognize, passing through, who bought a paperback western and had no idea he was the most welcome customer of my entire career.

"You know what I think?" Wren said, around one o'clock, feet swinging on the stool.

"Tell me."

"I think the people who aren't coming are the ones who never really liked books anyway. They just liked that the shop was here." She shrugged. "The books don't care. The books are still good."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I had to turn and face the biography section for a moment, because my eyes were doing something I did not want witnessed, even by a lighthouse audience of zero.

* * *

The bell rang at four.

I knew before I looked up. Not because the bell is distinctive — it rings the same for everyone — but because of the smell that came in with it, ahead of it, like a herald: yeast and butter and something dark and sweet, burnt sugar and rosemary. The smell of the bakery. The smell, specifically, of the back room of my own shop, where two nights ago Marisol and I had baked the festival batch by lamplight after someone flooded her kitchen, our hands dusted white, our shoulders touching, saying nothing that mattered and everything that did.

She came in carrying two loaves and a paper bag, her hair tied back with what appeared to be a pencil, flour on one cheekbone like a beauty mark she'd been issued at birth.

"Before you say anything," she said, "these are not a thank-you."

"Okay."

"I don't thank people for doing what they were supposed to do. That's how you get a whole town full of people who expect thanks for basic decency." She set the loaves on the counter — one dark rye, one something studded with olives — and put the paper bag next to them. "These are an *inventory adjustment.* My ovens are still condemned, my kitchen is still a swamp, and if I don't bake I get strange. Ask anyone."

"I don't think anyone would describe you as strange."

"You haven't seen me on day three without a starter to feed." She glanced past me at the register, at the empty shop, and something moved across her face — not pity. I'd have thrown her out for pity. Something more like recognition, the look of someone surveying a battlefield they'd fought on themselves. "Slow day?"

"Slow week," I said. "Slow month, if the trend holds. Mrs. Halloran buys her cat mysteries in Corning now. Dale from the feed store has taken his weather elsewhere."

"Dale," she said, "is an idiot."

"He's a *loyal* idiot. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed. "And it's not worth much." She pulled out the stool beside Wren's and sat, uninvited, the way she does, the way she has done since roughly the second week I knew her, colonizing my furniture and my routines like she'd been issued a deed. Wren looked up from her book and lit up in a way I pretended not to catalog.

"Hi, Marisol."

"Hi, troublemaker. Did you save me a seat or do I have to share with the lighthouse book?"

"You can share. The lighthouse is very lonely."

* * *

She stayed through the afternoon. She didn't announce it; she just didn't leave. She helped Wren with the last of the fractions — Marisol measures everything in weights and volumes and found the whole concept of "improper fractions" personally offensive, which led to a twenty-minute tangent about why American recipe writers should be tried at The Hague. She reorganized the front table without asking, pulling the local history titles I'd buried and stacking them face-out. *The Rails of the Chenango Valley. Millbrook: A Pictorial History.* The station, in the cover photograph, whole and gleaming and full of people.

"People took these out before," she said, quiet, not quite to me. "When it was just a nice old book about trains."

"They'll take them out again now," I said. "For different reasons."

"Is that better or worse?"

"Ask me on a different day."

She laughed, and the sound went through the empty shop and filled more of it than I would have believed sound could fill, and I turned away to water the ferns that don't need watering, because I am a grown man with a reputation to neglect.

At five I flipped the sign. At five-fifteen she was still there. At six, when the light went long and amber through the front windows and Wren had migrated to the floor behind the register with her book and her cocoa refills, Marisol was still there, sitting at the reading table, reading one of the local histories herself with a pencil in her teeth, making small notes in the margin of a napkin. She had not asked to stay. I had not asked her to. There was a whole negotiation we were both declining to have, and the silence where it should have been was the most comfortable silence I had ever stood in.

I locked the till. I turned off the overheads and left the lamps on, the good ones, the amber ones. The shop went soft and small around us.

"You know they're going to keep at it," I said, finally. The words had been waiting all day, patient as bookends. "Eleanor's lawyer. The boycott isn't organic — half those people got a phone call. There will be more calls. The health inspector who came to your kitchen came fast, Marisol. Fast like someone dialed for him. When the inquiry gets teeth, they'll come after the lease, the insurance, me. They'll come after Wren's school, if they can find an angle. I've seen my mother fight. She doesn't fight to win. She fights until the other side is too tired to stand."

"I know." She didn't look up from the napkin. "My father fought the railway company for nine years. I've read every letter. I know exactly how it ends when one person fights an institution." She turned a page. "I also know he was right. That's the part they never put in the ledger. You can be right and broke. You can be right and tired. You can be right for nine years and die with the file still open. But you were *right*, and the file was *open*, and twenty years later your daughter walks into a dead train station with a folder under her arm and finishes it." She looked up then, and her eyes were dry and steady and absolutely without self-pity, which is the most frightening thing a person can be. "I'm not tired, Jude. I'm the opposite of tired. I've been tired my whole life about this. Now I'm something else."

"What's the something else called?"

"I'll tell you when it's over."

Wren had fallen asleep on the floor, book open on her chest, rising and falling. Marisol looked at her for a long moment, and then at me, and something passed between us that had no words attached, that I am not going to insult by attempting them here.

"I should go," she said, and didn't move.

"You should," I said, and didn't either.

She gathered her napkin notes, kissed the top of Wren's head on the way past — Wren, asleep, smiled like it was the most natural thing in the world, which I suppose it was becoming — and paused at the door with her hand on the frame.

"The bread," she said. "It's not a thank-you. But eat it anyway. The rye's better tomorrow. Everything I make is better tomorrow. It's my one flaw as a businesswoman."

The bell rang her out. The smell of her stayed.

* * *

I carried Wren up to bed — she is getting heavy, she is getting heavy so fast, the years have their hands on her already — and she surfaced halfway up the stairs just enough to murmur, "Marisol stayed past closing," with the smug precision of a child logging evidence.

"She did."

"You didn't ask her to."

"No."

"And she didn't ask to."

"No."

Wren nodded once, gravely, like a judge confirming a ruling, and went back under. I stood in the hallway outside her room for a minute, in the dark, holding the day in my hands and turning it over. Empty register. Lost customers. A mother's silence, which is louder than her anger ever was. And bread on the counter, and a pencil in a sleeping girl's hair that wasn't mine, and a woman who stayed past closing without being asked.

The cost, I decided, was high. The cost was going to get higher; I'd seen the weather coming off my mother's side of the family my whole life, and I knew a front when I felt one.

But the worth — I looked down the stairs at the dark shop, at the loaves sitting on the counter like two small patient suns — the worth was not in question. The worth had walked in at four o'clock smelling of rosemary and stayed until the lamps burned low.

I went down, cut a slice of the rye even though she'd said to wait, and ate it standing in the dark of my own shop, and it was, annoyingly, perfect even tonight.

On the counter, under the register drawer where I kept things I wasn't ready to file, I found a note I hadn't seen her leave. Her handwriting, quick and slanted, flour-smudged at one corner:

*The county clerk posts the inquiry schedule Monday. First session is the 14th. They'll try to move it. Watch the paper. — M.*

I read it three times. Then I got out the calendar, the old paper one by the register, the one with Wren's drawings in the margins, and I circled the 14th, and the circle came out darker than I meant it to.

Monday was coming. And whatever they tried to move, I intended to be standing in its way.
