## 3. Dessert

**Lior**

The Fairweather reunion began, as it always did, with an ambush disguised as a hug.

Aunt Prudence caught Lior at the vineyard gate before they could even find Wren's hand, folding them into a cloud of lavender and judgment. "There you are," she said into their ear. "Fifteen years, and you finally bring someone. What's her name again? Wendy?"

"Wren," Lior said. "Mayor Halloway."

"Ooh." Prudence pulled back to look Lior over the way she might inspect fruit at the market, checking for bruises. "Ambitious. Your mother will have opinions."

"My mother has opinions about weather."

"Yes, but these will be *load-bearing* opinions."

Lior located Wren across the courtyard, standing beside Juniper with the easy posture of someone who had never once been ambushed in her life. She was wearing a linen dress the color of late afternoon and listening—actually listening—to Juniper explain something at length about the resident peacocks, nodding as though the mating habits of Almeida poultry were municipal business.

*This is fine,* Lior told themselves. *She's a professional. She performs for a living.*

The trouble was that she performed so well.

At lunch, when Cousin Dale asked how they'd met, Wren said, "He reorganized my entire understanding of poetry," without missing a beat, and Lior nearly choked on their wine because it was *true*, or close enough to true that the difference felt like a technicality.

"And what does Lior call you?" asked Great-Aunt Bettina, who was ninety-one and missed nothing.

Wren considered. "They haven't settled on anything yet. I think they're waiting for me to earn it."

The table laughed. Lior did not laugh, because something in their chest had gone strange and warm and unmanageable.

It got worse. It kept getting worse all afternoon. Wren remembered that Uncle Raymond had served in the navy and asked him about the Mediterranean. She let Prudence win an argument about hedge trimming with visible grace. When Lior's mother appeared—small, silver-haired, armed—Wren stood up straighter and offered her hand like a woman reporting for duty.

"So you're the reason my child finally came home for this," their mother said.

"I'm the reason they were brave enough," Wren said. "The coming home was theirs."

Lior's mother looked at Wren for a long moment, then at Lior, and her eyebrows performed an entire conversation that Lior chose not to translate.

By the time dinner was cleared, Lior had developed a headache shaped exactly like hope.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren had given a lot of performances in her life. Ribbon cuttings. Budget hearings. The annual turkey pardon, which involved holding a bird named Gerald and pretending he wasn't plotting against her.

None of it had prepared her for the Fairweathers.

They were sharp in a way that reminded her of her own family before the estrangement—love expressed primarily through interrogation—and she understood within ten minutes why Lior had needed armor. So she became armor. She answered every question, deflected every barb, absorbed Aunt Prudence's third attempt to establish whether Wren intended children ("I intend a new crosswalk on Mill Street; we all contain multitudes") until even Prudence seemed charmed despite herself.

What she hadn't planned for was how easy it would be.

That was the part that unsettled her, sitting in the golden hour light among strangers who were becoming less strange by the minute: how naturally her hand found Lior's shoulder, how easily she said *they* and *us* and *when we*, how little acting any of it required. Somewhere between the soup course and now, the performance had stopped being a performance, and nobody had informed her.

Juniper climbed onto the bench beside her after dinner and leaned against her arm, boneless with sugar and evening, and Wren found herself absently smoothing the girl's hair the way she'd seen Lior do it, and thought: *oh no.*

Because here was the thing about Wren Halloway. She had spent eleven years in public life learning to want things carefully. Wanting carefully meant wanting things you could get—votes, budgets, a second term. It did not mean wanting a bookshop owner with flour on their sleeves and a daughter who fell asleep against your arm at family dinners. That kind of wanting was not careful. That kind of wanting was a liability with excellent taste in cardigans.

"Lior tells me you're running for reelection," said Lior's mother, settling across from her with the serenity of a chess player three moves from checkmate.

"I am."

"And do you usually win?"

"Usually."

"Mm." The old woman smiled. "And what happens to people who beat you?"

"They send very gracious concession letters," Wren said, "and then they move to the coast and take up watercolors."

Her mother laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and Wren felt the victory land somewhere deeper than victories usually landed, and understood that she was in genuine trouble.

Under the table, Lior's hand found hers.

Not staged. Not for the audience. Their fingers simply threaded through hers, tentative, asking a question, and Wren realized with a lurch that neither of them was performing anymore, and that everyone at this table probably knew it, and that she did not move her hand away.

Dessert arrived—some plum thing with a crust that crackled—and Wren tasted none of it. She was too busy cataloguing the exact pressure of Lior's thumb moving once, slowly, across her knuckle, and deciding not to examine what it meant that she'd memorized it on the first pass.

* * *

**Lior**

The drive home was quiet in the way that good days end quiet—Juniper asleep in the back seat with a peacock feather clutched in one fist, the vineyard shrinking gold in the rearview, the radio off because neither of them reached for it.

"You were extraordinary today," Lior said finally.

"I was working."

"You held Aunt Prudence's hand during grace. You called her 'Pru.' She's hated that nickname since 1987. She let *you* say it."

"She likes me," Wren said, sounding faintly astonished by it. "Your mother invited me to Sunday supper. Indefinitely. I believe I've been adopted by treaty."

"It's reversible. There's a process. Forms."

"Don't you dare." But she was smiling, turned toward the window so the streetlights strobed across her face, and Lior watched her instead of the road for one dangerous second.

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable, which was the problem. Comfortable silences were not in the contract. The contract had rules—hand-holding permitted for photographic purposes, terms of endearment limited to surname-based irony, no kissing under any circumstances including mistletoe, *especially* mistletoe. Lior had drafted those rules themselves, in their own handwriting, in a moment of what they'd assumed was prudence.

Their hand was still resting on the gearshift. Hers was two inches away. Neither moved.

"She asked me if it was real," Lior said. "My mother. While you were getting more pie. She asked if it was real, and I said—" They stopped.

"What did you say?"

"I said yes." The word came out smaller than intended. "I don't know why I said yes. It isn't. We signed paperwork."

"Contracts can be amended," Wren said lightly, too lightly, eyes still on the window.

The town rose up around them then—the dark shopfronts, the clock tower, the bookshop's window glowing where Lior had left the lamp on for the cat, who did not live there and paid no rent. Lior pulled up outside Wren's place on Cordelia Street and put the car in park and did not turn off the engine, because turning off the engine would make this a moment, and it was already a moment, and they both knew it.

In the back seat, Juniper slept on, feather and all.

"Today was—" Wren started.

"Don't," Lior said. "If you summarize it, it becomes a thing that happened. If we just sit here for another minute, it's still happening."

Wren turned to look at them then, fully, and the streetlight caught the edge of her jaw and the exact place where her smile went uncertain, and Lior discovered that uncertainty looked devastating on a woman who was never uncertain.

"The line's blurring," Wren said quietly. "On my side too. In case the paperwork needs updating."

Lior leaned in—or Wren leaned in, the physics were disputed later—and for one suspended breath they were close enough that Lior could smell the vineyard air still clinging to her collar, close enough that the next inch would be a point of no return.

Juniper sighed in her sleep and said, distinctly, "the peacock knows what he did."

They broke apart, laughing, foreheads nearly touching, hearts hammering in a register only dogs and liars could hear.

"Goodnight, Mayor," Lior managed.

"Goodnight, bookseller." Wren got out, paused at the gate, and looked back. "Sunday supper. Don't forget. Your mother strikes me as someone who keeps records."

She was halfway up the walk when Lior's phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, forwarded from the town listserv, some routine notice about a special session of the council Thursday night:

*Agenda item 4: Land disposition — Almeida parcel — private consultation.*

Lior frowned at it, reread it, decided it was nothing—councils discussed parcels all the time, it was practically a hobby—and drove home through streets that smelled like ripening grapes, telling themselves the small cold feeling in their stomach was just too much dessert.
