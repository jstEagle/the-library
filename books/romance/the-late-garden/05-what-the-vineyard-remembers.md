## 5. What the Vineyard Remembers

The letter from the Fairweather family had finally stopped arriving, which should have been a relief, but Lior found they missed the ritual of it—the crisp envelope, the guilt folded inside like linen. In its place now was something better and far more dangerous: Sunday mornings when Juniper woke them by jumping on the bed announcing plans, and Wren's truck idling in the bookshop's back lot because Wren had "happened to be in the neighborhood," though the mayor of Hollow Creek had no business being in the neighborhood of anything before ten on a Sunday.

"Come on," Juniper said now, tugging Lior's sleeve in the vineyard parking lot. "You said the garden remembers people. You said."

"I said your great-grandmother used to work here," Lior corrected, locking the car. "I didn't say it remembers anyone. It's a garden, not an elephant."

"Gardens are better than elephants." Juniper had inherited her stubbornness from somewhere Lior preferred not to examine too closely. "Grandma Rosa said so. She said you plant a thing and it keeps saying yes after everybody stops asking."

Lior's throat did something complicated. Fifteen years since Rosa Almeida died—fifteen years since Lior had stood in these rows at the funeral, holding a child-sized bunch of grapes someone had pressed into their hands, and sworn never to come back to a place that could hurt like that. And yet here they were, on a bright September morning, with dust on their shoes and their daughter already halfway to the heritage gate.

Wren was waiting by the low stone wall, out of her usual armor of blazers, in a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled up. She looked younger without the mayoral posture. She looked, Lior thought with a small internal alarm, like someone you could grow old with.

"You're staring," Wren said.

"I'm assessing. There's a difference."

"Mm." Wren handed over a paper bag. "Scones. From Dot's. I bribed my way past the tasting room staff. Apparently the mayor still has to pay for baked goods, which I told them was an outrage."

"A scandal," Lior agreed, and felt the warmth of it settle somewhere under the ribs where they kept all the things they weren't ready to name.

* * *

The heritage garden sat behind the old winery building, walled in stone gone gold with lichen, and it was smaller than Lior remembered—or perhaps Lior had been smaller, and everything had seemed vast then. The fig tree was still there, enormous and slightly drunken in its posture, shading a bench whose paint had surrendered decades ago. Rosemary ran along the south wall in a gray-green hedge that smelled like every summer of Lior's childhood compressed into one breath.

"This is where she worked?" Juniper asked, spinning slowly.

"Right here. She kept this whole garden when the vineyard went mostly to grapes. Everybody said it was a waste of space." Lior touched a rosemary sprig and crushed it between their fingers, letting the scent rise. "She said a place that only grows one thing forgets how to grow anything else."

Juniper nodded solemnly, as if receiving scripture, and took off toward the fig tree with the scone bag.

Lior laughed despite themselves and turned to say something to Wren—and stopped.

Because Wren wasn't looking at the garden. She was looking at the ground near the east wall, at a small cluster of orange-tipped stakes driven into the soil, each one flagged with surveyor's tape, each one planted precisely along the line where Rosa's old vegetable rows used to run.

Something cold moved through Lior, slow as groundwater.

"Wren." They kept their voice light. Careful. "What are those?"

Wren turned. For half a second—less than half a second—something crossed her face. Lior had spent fifteen years reading faces across a bookstore counter; they knew the micro-weather of expressions, the flicker before the front came down. What crossed Wren's face was recognition. Calculation. Then the smooth, easy smile of a woman who had given a hundred speeches and never once stumbled.

"Survey markers," Wren said. "County business. We've been talking about doing some infrastructure assessment on the property—drainage, access roads, that sort of thing. Boring municipal stuff. Nothing for you to worry about."

"Infrastructure assessment." Lior looked at the stakes again. They were laid out in a grid. A grid meant parcels. Parcels meant—

"Mom!" Juniper called from the fig tree. "There's more of them! Way more!"

There were. Dozens of them, marching off through the old rows in neat orange ranks, flagging tape fluttering in the breeze like tiny surrender flags. Lior walked out among them, and the cold thing in their chest spread. Here, where Rosa had grown tomatoes that tasted like August itself. Here, where the peach trees stood that Lior had climbed at seven years old and been rescued from, weeping and sticky, by a grandmother who smelled of thyme and cigarettes and patience.

Stakes in all of it. Orange flags in all of it.

"Lior." Wren had followed. Her voice was gentle now, careful, the voice she probably used at council meetings when citizens got emotional about zoning. "It really is routine. Drainage studies. Half the valley's getting assessed."

"Drainage studies don't need parcel grids." Lior crouched beside the nearest stake. The tape was new. The stake was new. The soil around it was freshly disturbed, dark and turned. "These went in recently. Within the last few weeks."

Wren said nothing for a moment. When Lior looked up, she was watching them with an expression that was almost—almost—honest. Almost pained.

"The county hired a firm," she said. "Vane Development Group is handling the contract work. It's aboveboard, Lior. I'd tell you if it weren't."

And there it was. *I'd tell you if it weren't.* Said smoothly, warmly, convincingly. Said exactly the way someone would say it if they were lying.

Lior stood. Brushed the dirt from their knees. Smiled, because what else was there to do in front of a child?

"Well," they said brightly. "Bureaucracy. Can't fight it. Juniper! Scone check!"

* * *

They ate lunch on the bench under the fig tree, and Wren told a story about a zoning dispute involving a man who had painted his house in horizontal stripes to protest a fence ordinance, and Juniper laughed until she hiccupped, and Wren caught Lior's eye across the bread and cheese and smiled the smile that had started undoing Lior somewhere around dessert at the reunion, three weeks and a lifetime ago.

And Lior smiled back, and hated how easy it was, and hated more that they couldn't stop checking her face for the flicker.

* * *

Juniper fell asleep in the truck on the drive home, cheek against the window, scone crumbs on her chin, and Wren drove slowly over the gravel roads to keep from jostling her, and it was such a tender, unguarded thing to do that Lior almost forgave her for whatever it was she hadn't told them.

Almost.

They dropped Wren at her office—"paperwork," she'd said, kissing Lior's cheek at the curb, quick and warm and gone—and Lior sat in the idling car outside the bookshop for a long time, watching the light go amber on the storefront glass.

It was Juniper who broke the spell, waking cranky and demanding juice, and it was Juniper, later, sprawled on the bookshop floor doing homework among the biography section, who said the thing that cracked everything open.

"Mom? Why'd you keep that old letter in the garden book?"

Lior looked up from the register. "What letter?"

"The one in Grandma Rosa's book. The green one. It fell out when I was looking for pictures of the garden for my project." Juniper held it up, a thin envelope gone soft and yellow at the edges, Rosa's handwriting slanting across the front: *For whoever needs it next.*

Lior's hands went still on the counter.

Rosa had kept a journal—a garden journal, part almanac, part diary—that Lior had inherited and shelved years ago in local history, unable to read it and unable to give it away. They had never noticed a letter tucked into its pages.

"Give it here, bug."

Juniper brought it over, and Lior opened it with fingers that had suddenly become clumsy. Inside, on a single sheet in Rosa's strong, sloping hand:

*To whoever finds this —*

*If the vines ever come up for sale, remember: land isn't dirt, it's memory with roots. Men with money will come calling with maps and promises. They always do. The Almeidas sold once before and regretted it a hundred years. Don't let them talk you into believing a signature is just paperwork.*

*And if it's the Halloway girl doing the talking — watch her. Ambition's a fine horse but it runs away with you.*

*— R.A., August, the year the peaches failed*

Lior read it twice. Three times. The shop sounds receded—the tick of the register, the hum of the cooler, Juniper's pencil scratching—and there was only the letter, and the orange flags behind their eyes, and Wren's smooth voice saying *I'd tell you if it weren't.*

*The Halloway girl.* Wren's grandmother had been on the town council forty years ago. Had run against an Almeida cousin for the seat, once. Had known Rosa, surely. Known her well enough to be warned about.

Or well enough for Rosa to warn others.

Lior set the letter down very carefully, as if it might go off.

"Mom? Are you okay?"

"I'm fine, bug." Their voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of its own. "Just an old note from Grandma Rosa."

But that night, after Juniper was asleep, Lior stood at the shop window with the letter in one hand and their phone in the other, Wren's contact page glowing on the screen—*Wren 🌿*, because three weeks ago Lior had been foolish enough to add the emoji—and typed and deleted *can I ask you something* four times.

Outside, the streetlights came on. Somewhere across town, in an office with a view of the valley, the Vane contract was either signed or waiting to be.

Lior put the phone face-down on the counter, next to Rosa's letter, and did not sleep.
