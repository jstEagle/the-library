## 7. The Reading of the Letter

Lior didn't sleep. That was the first thing. The second was that they opened the bookshop anyway, because grief and betrayal were no excuse for disappointing the Tuesday morning crowd, which consisted of Mrs. Okafor wanting her large-print westerns and a man who came in every week to read the gardening magazines without buying them.

Lior had always been good at this—functioning with a cracked interior. Fifteen years of practice. They made the coffee, straightened the poetry table, and did not think about Wren Halloway's face on the phone screen, lit up with *Marcus Vane* at eleven o'clock on Harvest Eve, one hour after she'd kissed Lior under paper lanterns like a woman in a novel.

Except that wasn't quite true. They thought about nothing else.

The letter sat in their apron pocket all morning, folded into eighths now, soft as cloth from handling. They'd found it Sunday, tucked inside the heritage garden's stone wall where Grandmother Almeida used to leave notes for the seasonal workers—a habit from another century, a hiding place only someone raised on her stories would know to check. Lior had gone there looking for peace after finding Vane's survey stakes among the old rows. Instead they'd found a printed email, creased twice, dated eight months ago.

*Wren — Vane's people are serious about the north parcel. Keep this between us until we have the council votes locked. Your name stays off anything until then. Smart move keeping the Fairweather connection quiet. — M.*

The Fairweather connection. Which meant he knew about Lior. Which meant Wren had told him—or he'd found out—and either way, Lior had been a variable in somebody's equation while they were falling asleep holding her hand.

At noon, Lior flipped the sign to CLOSED FOR INVENTORY, which was a lie, but a small one, and small lies had never been their sin.

* * *

Wren arrived at four, because of course she did. She had a sixth sense for it, or maybe she'd simply known since the festival—known the moment Lior had gone still against her on the dance floor, the music still playing, everything else stopping.

The bell over the door chimed and there she was: mayor's blazer traded for a soft gray sweater, hair down, carrying two coffees like an offering. Like a woman walking into a church she suspected had already excommunicated her.

"Juniper said you closed early," Wren said. "She said you looked—" She stopped herself. Set the coffees on the counter with exaggerated care, as if the cups might detonate. "You saw the phone."

"I saw the phone." Lior came out from behind the counter slowly. "And then I went to the garden."

Something flickered across Wren's face—the precise expression of a person realizing the hiding place has been found. "Lior."

"Eight months." Lior took the letter from their apron and unfolded it, laid it flat on the counter between them like evidence at a trial, which, they supposed, it was. "Eight months you've known. Before the reunion. Before the rules. Before you held my hand under my sister's dessert plate and told me—" Their voice caught; they hated that it caught. "What did you tell me? That you'd never seen me laugh like that?"

Wren didn't look at the letter. She looked at Lior, which was somehow worse. "I can explain."

"That's what he said." The words came out flat and cold, and Lior watched them land. "Fifteen years ago. Theo Aldous, standing in my kitchen, saying *I can explain*, and then explaining very eloquently how taking my savings and my grandmother's recipes and my whole stupid trusting heart had actually been *for my own good*. He had a plan. He had numbers. He had a future he needed funding for." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "He even cried. Do you cry, Madam Mayor? Is that part of the toolkit?"

"You're not being fair."

"No," Lior agreed. "I'm not. Fairness is for negotiations. This is just me, reading you a letter."

They read it. Out loud, every word, watching Wren stand very still in the middle of the shop she'd started visiting last spring, back when she'd claimed she was there for the local history section. *Keep this between us.* *Your name stays off anything.* *Smart move keeping the Fairweather connection quiet.*

When they finished, the silence had a texture to it, dense as wet wool.

"The school roof is failing," Wren said finally. Quietly. Not defensive—worse, reasonable. "The town budget can't cover it. Vane's offer funds the school, the clinic, the road repairs, three years of everything we've been scraping for. I ran on fixing this town, Lior. I meant it. I still mean it."

"And the vineyard?"

"There are compromises. Conservation easements. He keeps the heritage rows if—"

"He put survey stakes through Grandmother Almeida's roses." Lior's voice didn't rise; it dropped instead, down to somewhere quiet and terrible. "You stood in that garden with me. You let Juniper show you the sundial. You said—" A breath. "You said some places hold more than they grow."

"I know what I said."

"Do you? Because here's what I keep coming back to, Wren. Every single thing you've said to me, I now have to audit. The reunion—that was convenient, wasn't it? Charming the rural voters, you said. And then the lunches. The walks. Was that also campaigning? Was I a constituency?"

"No." It came out fast and raw, the first unpolished thing she'd said. "No, that was—"

"Was it real?" Lior asked. And then, because they were who they were—someone who had learned the hard way that you check the fine print—they shook their head before she could answer. "Don't. Don't answer that yet. Because here's the thing about Theo: every word he ever said to me was real when he said it. That's what nobody tells you about betrayal. It doesn't require lying. It just requires loving something else more, and doing the math quietly, and letting the person find out last."

Wren's jaw tightened. "So that's it. I'm Theo now. Eight months of knowing me and I get filed in the same drawer."

"You signed nothing yet?" Lior asked. "That's your defense? That the knife is still in the box?"

"It's not a defense, it's a—" Wren stopped. Pressed her fingers to her eyes. When she lowered her hand, she looked, for the first time, not like a mayor but like a tired woman in a sweater standing in a bookshop that smelled of coffee and old paper. "If I walk away from this deal, I lose the election. I lose everything I've built for nine years. And the school roof doesn't fix itself out of sentiment."

"So choose," Lior said. "Right now. Me and this town's memory, or Marcus Vane and your career. Choose out loud, in this room, and I'll believe you whichever way you go. That's all I've ever wanted from anyone. A choice made in daylight."

And Lior watched—watched with a horrible, familiar clarity—as Wren Halloway, who could speak fluently at any podium, who had charmed a table of hostile Fairweathers into applause, stood silent in front of forty shelves of books and could not produce a single sentence.

"I need time," Wren said at last.

"You had eight months."

"Lior—"

"The door's behind you." Lior turned away, began squaring the poetry table that didn't need squaring. "I'm not going to ask you again. I learned that lesson too. Nobody gets to explain twice."

For a long moment there was only the tick of the old wall clock, the hum of the refrigerator behind the counter. Then footsteps, unhurried, and the chime of the bell, and the door closing with a softness that felt deliberate—like she couldn't even give Lior the satisfaction of a slam.

The coffees sat untouched on the counter, going cold side by side.

* * *

Juniper appeared at five-fifteen, as she always did, backpack thumping, calling out that Danny's mom had gotten the wrong kind of granola bars again. Then she stopped in the doorway of the back room, where Lior was sitting on the floor among unsold remainders, not reading any of them.

"You closed early," Juniper said. "And the mayor's coffee's still on the counter. And you have your sad face, but the old kind. The pre-mayor kind."

"Fifteen years old and already a detective," Lior managed.

Juniper came and sat down cross-legged on the carpet, which she hadn't done since she was small enough to fit between the shelves without knocking things over. She studied Lior with her grandmother's eyes, sharp and unfooled.

"She lied to you," Juniper said. Not a question. Kids heard everything; kids assembled everything; kids were merciless little archivists. "About the vineyard. The stakes. I saw them too, you know. Weeks ago. I didn't say anything because she seemed—" Juniper searched for the word. "Happy. You both seemed happy."

"We were."

"Were you pretending?"

"No, bug. That's the whole problem. Nobody was pretending. That's how it gets you."

Juniper absorbed this with the gravity of a supreme court. Then, quietly, in the small voice she saved for genuinely important matters—lost pets, dead grandmothers, whether Santa scaled down for apartments:

"But was she real? The mayor. When she was here. With us." Her fingers picked at the carpet seam. "Because the pancakes on Sundays were real. I was there. I ate them."

Lior's whole chest cracked open along an old fault line. They pulled Juniper close, into the smell of pencil shavings and playground dust, and rested their chin on her head.

"I don't know yet," they said honestly, because they had promised themselves, fifteen years ago, never to lie to this child. "I think parts of her were real. But parts aren't enough, sweetheart. Parts are how you end up on the floor of a bookshop at fifty-one, starting over."

"You're not fifty-one."

"Forty-eight."

"Grandma says you round up when you're dramatic."

Despite everything, Lior laughed—a broken, wet sound—and held on tighter.

Outside, the light was going amber over the rooftops, the way it did on the eve of harvest, the whole valley turning gold as if the season itself were showing off. Tomorrow the town would gather in the vines. Tomorrow Wren Halloway would stand somewhere with a contract in her bag and a pen in her hand, and whatever she decided would decide everything else.

Lior looked at the letter, still lying open on the counter upstairs. Then at their daughter, warm and solid and asking nothing except to be told the truth.

"Go wash up," they said gently. "I'll make pasta."

And when Juniper had gone, Lior sat alone in the dimming shop, surrounded by ten thousand stories in which people chose bravely, and understood—with a calm that frightened them more than rage ever could—that tomorrow they would watch Wren sign, or they wouldn't.

Either way, the whole heart was going back in its box. They'd survived it once. They knew exactly where the box was kept.

They just hadn't expected, after all these years, how much it would hurt to reach for it.
