## 8. Signature

The pen had been sitting on the contract for eleven minutes.

Wren knew because she'd been watching the second hand of the wall clock make its slow, indifferent circles, and she'd counted every one of them the way you count the seconds between lightning and thunder—except she couldn't decide whether the storm was coming or already here.

Marcus Vane's signature sat at the bottom of the page, black and confident, a man who signed things before lunch and never lost sleep over them. Above it, the line waiting for her name was blank as fresh snow. All she had to do was write *Wren Halloway* in her own careful hand, and everything would be settled. The vineyard would become Vantage Point Estates. Forty acres of vines and memory would become forty acres of luxury units with "vineyard-inspired landscaping." The town would get a tax base. Her campaign account would get a number with two commas in it. And Lior Fairweather's bookshop—

She set the pen down.

It rolled slightly on the blotter, came to rest against the edge of the contract, like even the pen wanted no part of this.

"Due diligence," she said aloud to the empty office. It was the phrase she'd been using for six weeks, the phrase that had let her sleep, mostly, if you didn't count the nights she'd woken at three a.m. staring at the ceiling while her heart did something arrhythmic and shameful. *Just due diligence.* As if knowing exactly what a thing would cost was somehow different from buying it.

The trouble with due diligence, she thought now, was that it worked. She'd done hers thoroughly. She knew the numbers cold: what Vane was paying, what the trust fund for the school renovation would yield, what her polling numbers did when she stood in front of a room and promised results instead of hopes. She'd built the whole case like a lawyer building a defense, and the defense was airtight, and that was precisely the problem. Airtight cases were for people who already knew they were guilty.

Her office at this hour was all amber light and dust motes, the last of the afternoon slanting through the blinds in bars. Downstairs, faintly, the town was assembling itself for the harvest festival—she could hear the pop of tent poles going up in the square, someone testing a PA system that produced a long, wounded feedback whine, children shrieking with the particular joy of children who could smell fried dough from a hundred yards. First harvest eve. The whole town turning out to celebrate the thing she'd spent six weeks quietly arranging to bury.

On the shelf behind her desk, between a binder of zoning ordinances and a framed photo of her being sworn in, sat a cardboard box she hadn't opened since moving into this office four years ago. Her grandmother's papers. Edith Halloway had been mayor of this town for eleven years, back when the population sign still said 2,300 and everyone complained about it like weather. Wren had run partly because of her—the family name, the family gravity—and partly, if she was honest, to prove she could do it better, faster, with fewer compromises.

She pulled the box down now, mostly to have something to do with her hands that wasn't signing.

The letters were bundled with rubber bands gone brittle, and the first one snapped apart in her fingers like an old promise. Edith's handwriting was tall and slanted, written fast, the handwriting of a woman who always had three more things to say than the page could hold. These were drafts—campaign letters, unsent, kept anyway. Edith had never thrown away a sentence in her life.

Wren unfolded the top sheet and read:

*"To the people of Harrow's Bend — I've been asked lately why I won't take the Delacroix money. Let me tell you why. Money like that doesn't come with strings; it comes with scissors. You take it, and one day you look down and notice your hands are empty and someone else is holding the shears..."*

Wren read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you reread a diagnosis.

Delacroix. She remembered the name dimly—a resort developer, sometime in the nineties, who'd wanted the riverfront. Her grandmother had killed that deal too. Wren had grown up hearing about it as a kind of family legend, the year Edith stood up at the grange hall and told a man with a briefcase full of futures exactly where he could file them. It had cost Edith the next election. Wren had always suspected that was the moral of the story: idealism was expensive, and sentimentality got you voted out.

But the letter didn't read like a woman mourning her career. It read like a woman sharpening a knife she intended to enjoy using.

*"...They will tell you growth is inevitable. Nothing is inevitable. That word is a sales pitch wearing a lab coat. What is inevitable is that once the vines are gone, they are gone, and no amount of 'vineyard-inspired' anything will bring back the rows my mother planted with her own hands..."*

Wren sat down slowly.

Because there it was, in her grandmother's slanting hand, the exact architecture of the deal on her desk. *Vineyard-inspired.* She'd heard Marcus Vane use those words himself, three weeks ago, across a table at the Blue Roan, sliding renderings toward her: heritage aesthetics, preserved facades, a community garden space—"we're not erasing the character, Mayor, we're curating it." And she'd nodded along, professional, receptive, doing her due diligence, while somewhere in a box behind her desk her grandmother's ghost was already writing the rebuttal.

She dug deeper into the bundle. There were more letters—one to the editor of the county paper, one that appeared to be addressed, furiously, to a state senator, and then one that stopped her cold, dated the autumn before Edith died, in shakier handwriting:

*"To whoever sits in this chair after me — I hope it's you, Wren, though I'd never say so to your face because you'd only argue. A few things I learned late and paid full price for: The town is not a problem to be solved. It is a garden. You don't get to redesign a garden every time a man with money tells you it's underperforming. Also: never sign anything you wouldn't read aloud at the harvest festival. If you'd blush, don't sign. I love you. Go home earlier than I did."*

Wren put the letter face-down on the desk, very carefully, the way you handle something that might go off.

*Never sign anything you wouldn't read aloud at the harvest festival.*

Downstairs, right on cue, the festival was tuning itself. She could hear the fiddle players warming up, a ragged run of notes rising through the floorboards, and beneath it the murmur of half the town gathering in the square to celebrate the first harvest of the Almeida vines—the vines her deal would bulldoze by spring.

She thought about reading the contract aloud. All nine pages. The easement clauses. The demolition schedule. Section 14, subsection B, which transferred "all water rights appurtenant to the parcel" and which she had read four times and understood completely and had been carefully not thinking about. She imagined standing at the festival microphone, the fiddles going quiet, reading Marcus Vane's confident black signature out loud to Juniper Fairweather, who was eight years old and had recently informed Wren—with the devastating precision of eight-year-olds—that the vineyard was where Great-Grandma Alma used to work, and also that Wren smelled like coffee and important papers.

She imagined reading it to Lior.

That was the one that undid her. Not the school funding, not the polling numbers, not even the water rights. Lior, standing in the bookshop doorway with that guarded look finally gone soft, saying *I haven't let anyone past the front counter in fifteen years, and here you are, wandering around the stockroom like you pay rent.* Lior, who had been betrayed once by someone who smiled and made promises and left, and who had taken Wren's hand under a reunion table anyway, trembling slightly, choosing again.

And Wren had spent the entire time holding a secret shaped exactly like the old wound.

"I'm not him," she said to the empty office. It was true. She wasn't. She was something arguably worse: she was the person who knew better. Lior's betrayer had at least had the decency of ordinary selfishness. Wren had dressed hers up in due diligence and called it leadership.

She looked at the pen. Still resting against the contract, still refusing.

Here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for six weeks, laid out suddenly and without mercy: the deal bought her a term, maybe two. It cost the vines, the water, the shop's block—which Vane's plans had marked, in cheerful architect's blue, as "future commercial frontage"—and it cost Lior, who would find out, because Lior always found out, because people who had been betrayed once developed radar. And when Lior found out, Wren would lose the only thing in years that had felt like it was actually hers—not the title, not the office, but the hand under the table, the walks that ran too long, the way Juniper had started saving her a seat at story hour without being asked.

Power, she discovered, tasted exactly like everyone said it did. Like nothing. Like a pen cap.

Edith had gone home early. Wren picked up the letter again—*I love you. Go home earlier than I did*—and laughed, once, wetly, alone in her office on the most consequential afternoon of her life.

Then she picked up the contract.

Tearing it was easier than she'd expected. Nine pages, and the paper gave with a sound like a small clean rip, once per page, and she tore each page twice more for good measure, lengthwise, until Marcus Vane's signature was in pieces and the water rights were confetti and Section 14, subsection B, was linguistically impossible. She did not feel noble. She did not feel relieved. She felt the full weight of the cost arriving all at once, honestly itemized: the campaign account, the endorsements, the election in five weeks that she would now lose to Dale Pruitt, who thought the town's comprehensive plan should be "whatever comes next," and who owned a boat named *Fiscal Responsibility*. She counted every piece of it, deliberately, the way you count your fingers after an accident, and found she was still standing.

There was one more thing. She took a clean sheet of the town letterhead, and wrote, in her best careful hand:

*"To the Elections Office: I am withdrawing my candidacy for Mayor of Harrow's Bend, effective immediately. To the people of the town: I owe you an explanation, and you'll have it tonight, at the festival, in person, because some things shouldn't be read aloud unless you're willing to stand in front of the people they concern. — W. Halloway"*

She signed it. This pen stroke felt entirely different from the other one would have. Lighter. Terrifying. Hers.

* * *

The festival had swallowed the square whole by the time she got down there—strings of lights just coming on against the blue dusk, cider steam, the fiddle band finding its feet, kids weaving between adults with glow sticks like tiny comets. Someone had hung a banner over the stage: FIRST HARVEST FESTIVAL — HARROW'S BEND KEEPS WHAT IT GROWS. Wren read it and almost laughed. The universe had a flair for staging.

She scanned the crowd for Lior and found them near the bookshop's stall, helping Juniper arrange a display of picture books with the seriousness of museum curators. Lior was laughing at something Juniper said—that unguarded laugh Wren had spent weeks earning—and the sight of it hit Wren like weather.

She had no speech prepared. She had a pocket full of torn contract and a resignation letter filed and a grandmother's letter burning a hole in her mind. She had, she realized, absolutely nothing to offer except the truth, delivered publicly, at cost, with no request attached.

Lior looked up then—some instinct, some radar—and their eyes met across the crowd, across the lights and the fiddle music and the banner about keeping what you grow.

Wren took a breath, and stepped forward.
