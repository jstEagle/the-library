## 3. Last Train to Harbor Bluffs

The train announced Harbor Bluffs in a voice like a woman apologizing for waking you, and Jude Underhill sat very still and let the name wash over him the way he imagined cold water must feel to a man who has been on fire.

Twelve years. He had done the arithmetic on the ride down from the city, because arithmetic was what he did instead of feeling: twelve years, four months, eleven days since he had stood on this same platform with two suitcases and his father's hospital bill folded into his jacket pocket like a letter he couldn't bear to open. He'd been nineteen. He'd promised someone he wouldn't leave without her, and then he'd left without her, and he had built an entire career — an entire self — out of the principle that leaving was survivable if you never looked back.

He was looking back now. That was the whole problem with this assignment.

Through the window, the town assembled itself in pieces: the water tower first, rust-streaked, still wearing its faded mural of a leaping fish; then the shingled roofs of Cannery Row, now boutiques and a wine bar where the sardine packing house used to be; then the harbor itself, gray-green and patient, holding its little fleet of boats the way it always had, as though nothing that happened on land could possibly concern it.

Jude had spent three weeks telling himself this was research. Marcus Vane's memoir required immersion, Vane had insisted — *you can't write a place you haven't stood in, Underhill* — and Vane was paying enough that Jude could almost believe the money was the reason. Almost. But there was a moment somewhere past the county line when the hills went gold and the eucalyptus started throwing their blue shadows across the road, and Jude had understood, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own autopsy, that he would have come here even if no one were paying him. That some part of him had been circling this coastline for twelve years like a gull that couldn't find its wind.

The train sighed into the station. He gathered his bag — one bag, deliberately; ghosts traveled light — and joined the trickle of passengers onto the platform, which smelled of salt and creosote and, underneath it all, of grapes. Harvest was coming. He could feel it in the air the way you felt weather coming, a sweetness at the edge of things, ripe and slightly rotten, the smell of a season about to be decided.

The station hadn't changed. That was the cruelty of small towns: they didn't change so much as accumulate, adding a coat of paint here, a new sign there, until the place became a palimpsest of every year you'd tried to escape it. The same wooden benches, slatted and sun-bleached. The same clock above the ticket window, its hands frozen at 4:17 for as long as anyone could remember, a joke the town told about itself. The same bulletin board by the door, layered with flyers for lost dogs and guitar lessons and, pinned dead center, a glossy card that read:

**CALLOWAY ESTATE — HARVEST FESTIVAL & FAMILY REUNION — ALL WELCOME**

Jude looked at it for exactly as long as it took to memorize the date, then made himself look away.

*Research,* he reminded himself. *You're here for the book.*

Marcus Vane's manuscript-in-progress sat in his bag like a stone. Three hundred pages so far, dictated in Vane's rolling, self-mythologizing voice while the man paced his office in Napa with a glass of his own reserve in hand. *I was born with dirt under my nails and ambition in my blood,* the opening line ran, and Jude had already rewritten it twice, because the truth — which Jude alone seemed interested in — was that Vane had been born with a silver spoon that someone else had pried from his hand, and everything he'd built since was a forty-year argument with a family that pretended he'd never existed.

A family named Calloway.

Jude had known before he signed the contract. Of course he had. He'd read the name in the source materials his agent sent over — *Vineyard interests, Harbor Bluffs; estranged relations; the Calloway estate* — and he had sat with the folder open on his desk for a long time, watching the word *Calloway* the way you watch a lit fuse. Then he had signed anyway, because the advance was the largest of his career and because he had a rule about coincidences: they only mean something if you let them.

He was beginning to suspect his rule was wrong.

The platform was thinning out. A fisherman argued cheerfully with a duffel bag. Two teenagers took a selfie against the frozen clock. And down at the far end, near the ramp to the parking lot, a woman stood with her phone pressed to her ear, her free hand gesturing at the sky as though the sky had personally disappointed her.

Jude saw her the way you see a fire across a valley — a flicker first, then recognition arriving all at once and too late to be prepared for.

She was wearing canvas work pants and a white shirt with the sleeves rolled, and her hair was shorter than it had been at nineteen, cut to her jaw, and she was arguing with someone on the phone in a voice that carried — *no, Nonna, I am not bringing a date, I'm bringing myself, which is apparently a tragedy* — and none of that mattered, none of it registered beyond the first half-second, because the first half-second was just: *Wren.*

Wren Calloway. Standing thirty feet away on a train platform in a town he'd fled, in a week he'd chosen, for a book that would gut her family.

His body did something stupid and honest. It started toward her.

He caught himself after four steps — four steps he had no memory of taking — and converted the motion into something plausible, angling toward the bulletin board as if he'd been walking there all along, studying the harvest festival card with the intensity of a man who cared deeply about municipal events. His heart was going like a fist on a door.

*Don't.* He said it to himself the way he said everything to himself, quietly, in the margins. *You are here to work. She is a variable. Variables get controlled or they get removed.*

But she had turned. He felt it before he saw it, the way you feel weather change — a shift in the pressure of the platform — and when he glanced up from the flyer she was looking at him. Not recognizing. Not yet. Looking the way people look at strangers who stare too hard at bulletin boards, with mild wariness and the beginnings of apology, as if she might have blocked his view.

Then her head tilted. Slightly. The way a bird's does.

Jude knew that tilt. He had spent one entire summer learning the vocabulary of it — the tilt that meant she was deciding whether to say the thing she was thinking, and usually did. Nineteen years old, sitting on the breakwater with their shoes off, feet in cold water, saying outrageous things to make her laugh. He knew the exact angle of her head when she was about to speak.

She didn't speak. Her eyes moved over his face — quick, thorough, the way she'd once catalogued constellations for him, pointing out that everyone learned the shapes but nobody learned the spaces between them — and whatever she found there, she filed it away without opening the drawer. Her gaze slid off him like rain off wax. Back to the phone. Back to her argument with the sky.

Relief hit him so hard it felt like injury.

And then, because the universe had a sense of humor that had never once been kind to him, she hung up the phone, turned, and walked directly into him.

It shouldn't have happened. There was a whole platform's worth of empty space between them, and she was walking one way and he was walking the other and physics had no interest in a collision. But at the last second she glanced back over her shoulder — at the board, maybe, or at nothing — and her shoulder caught his chest, and his bag swung wide, and the folder inside came loose and fanned pages across the boards like a losing hand of cards.

"Oh — God, I'm sorry—" She dropped to a crouch, gathering pages, and he dropped too, and for one absurd moment they were both kneeling on the platform of Harbor Bluffs station, heads nearly touching, collecting the scattered anatomy of Marcus Vane's life.

"I wasn't watching," she said. "I'm sorry, my grandmother has the ability to turn a phone call into a contact sport—"

"It's fine." His voice came out lower than he intended. Rougher. Twelve years of not speaking to her had left sediment in his throat. "Really. No harm."

Their hands reached for the same page. Hers stopped an inch from his. He watched her look at it — at the typescript, at the header — and he watched something cross her face, brief as a fish turning under water.

*VANE, M. — CHAPTER 14 (DRAFT)*

"Marcus Vane," she said. Reading aloud. Testing the name. "You're not a reporter, are you? Because if you're writing about the harvest festival, I should warn you our town's idea of news is Mrs. Okonkwo's zucchini winning again."

"Not a reporter." He gathered the rest of the pages quickly, squaring them, hiding the headers against his chest. "Writer. Sort of. Research."

"For a book?"

"For a client." The lie came smoothly, which was its own kind of shame. Lying to Wren Calloway had never come smoothly before. At nineteen he'd been constitutionally incapable of it; she'd once said he had the face of a man being interrogated whenever he tried. Apparently twelve years had fixed that. Apparently twelve years could fix anything, which was the most depressing thing he'd learned all day, and he'd spent the morning doing arithmetic about abandonment.

"A ghostwriter," she said, and he felt the word go through him like a needle finding a nerve.

"Something like that."

She stood, brushing off her knees, and offered him the last page — the top sheet, the title page, which he took care to keep face-down. Up close she smelled of sunscreen and crushed grape leaves and something underneath that was just her, unchanged, and it undid him more thoroughly than any question could have. People changed. Smells didn't. Somewhere in his brain a room he'd sealed shut for twelve years quietly unlocked itself, and he heard — actually heard, in memory's merciless fidelity — her voice saying *if we don't leave together in September, we're cowards,* and the tide going out beneath them, and the lighthouse sweeping its arm across the dark like it was clearing the table.

"You look familiar," she said.

There it is, he thought. Right on schedule.

"Do I?" He kept his face pleasant, blank, the professional mask he wore through interviews and airport lines and every other situation where being recognized would cost him. "I have one of those faces. People are always asking me if we went to school together."

"Maybe." But the tilt of her head stayed, that bird-tilt, and her eyes held his a beat longer than strangers' eyes needed to. He watched her decide not to push — watched the decision happen, actually happen, a small visible surrender behind her expression — and understood that she was practicing the same discipline he was. Whatever she thought she saw, she was choosing not to reach for it.

People did that, he'd learned. They sensed the shape of a thing and declined to name it, because naming made it real and real things demanded responses. He had built a whole industry on other people's willingness not to ask.

"Well," she said, stepping back, hitching her bag higher on her shoulder. "Welcome to Harbor Bluffs. Watch the seagulls outside the bakery — they're organized now. It's a whole thing."

"Thanks." He smiled, and hated how easy the smile was, how it arrived like muscle memory, like it had been waiting twelve years in storage. "Enjoy your reunion."

Her face did something complicated. It was only a flicker — a tightening around the mouth, a shadow crossing the eyes — but Jude had made a living for fifteen years reading faces, catching the micro-expressions clients didn't know they gave, and he read hers fluently: the word *reunion* landed on her like a fine print clause she'd agreed to without reading.

"Yeah," she said. "Working on it."

She turned and walked up the ramp toward the parking lot, and Jude stood there among his reassembled pages and watched her go, because he was weak, and because some animal part of him needed to confirm she was real. She got halfway to a battered pickup truck — faded green, a dent in the bed, a stack of folding chairs roped down under the camper shell — and stopped. Turned partway back. Looked at him again, longer this time, with an expression he couldn't parse: not suspicion exactly, and not recognition either, but something between them, something that lived in the space between constellations that nobody learned the names of.

Then she shook her head — at herself, he thought, at whatever she was letting herself think — and got in the truck, and the engine caught, and she pulled out of the lot and was gone, taillights shrinking down the coast road toward the vineyards, toward the estate, toward the family whose oldest wound he was carrying in his bag in triplicate.

Jude exhaled.

Around him the platform resumed its business. The frozen clock said 4:17. The gulls wheeled over the harbor, screaming their one note. Somewhere down the street the bakery was closing, and the smell of bread drifted out and mixed with the salt and the ripening vines, and it was all so exactly, unbearably the same that he had to grip the strap of his bag just to have something to hold.

*Research,* he told himself again, walking out of the station into the late gold light. *Three weeks. You interview Vane, you walk the ground, you keep your head down, and you leave before the harvest. In and out. Nobody sees you. Nobody ever sees you.*

That was the rule. Fifteen years of rules, and every one of them existed to prevent precisely what had just happened on a train platform: a collision, a conversation, a woman looking at him like he might be somebody she used to know.

He checked into the Anchor Inn — the same inn, under new management, the same creaking third stair — and set up his laptop at the desk by the window, and opened Vane's draft to Chapter 14, and worked until the light failed. This was the discipline. This was the whole trick of his life: you put your head into someone else's story and you kept it there, and your own story stayed shut in the drawer where you'd locked it, gathering dust, harmless.

At some point after dark he got up and stood at the window. From the third floor he could see the harbor, black and glittering, and beyond it the dark shoulders of the hills where the vineyard rows climbed in pale stitched lines, and one lit farmhouse on the ridge that he knew — knew without checking, knew the way you know a scar is yours — was the Calloway estate.

Somewhere up there, Wren Calloway was probably still arguing with her grandmother about whether she'd bring a date to the reunion.

Jude stood in the dark a long time, watching that single lit window on the hill, and told himself the pull in his chest was just guilt, just the ordinary dread of a man working too close to the flame.

He was almost able to believe it. Then his phone buzzed on the desk — a text from an unknown local number, and he stared at it, because he hadn't given the number to anyone in this town:

*This is going to sound strange. But do I know you? — W*

* * *

He typed and deleted six replies. *No. I don't think so. Have we met? You have me confused with someone else.* All lies, and worse than lies — clumsy ones, the kind she'd see through, because she'd always been able to read him and twelve years evidently hadn't blunted her.

In the end he wrote: *I don't think so. But I'll take that as a compliment.*

He watched the message deliver. Watched the three dots appear, hover, vanish. Appear again. He stood there with his heart in his ears, a grown man terrified of a typing indicator, until finally the dots disappeared for good and no answer came.

Down the hill, the light in the farmhouse window went out.

Jude closed the curtain, sat back down at the desk, and pulled Chapter 14 toward him — the chapter where Marcus Vane, in his rolling voice, finally began to talk about the fire.

Outside, the tide came in over the harbor, patient as a debt, and the last train of the day pulled out of Harbor Bluffs without him, heading back to the world he'd come from, and he listened to it go and felt — for the first time in twelve years — like the one who'd been left behind.
