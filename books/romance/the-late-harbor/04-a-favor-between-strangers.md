## 4. A Favor Between Strangers

The invitation had arrived in a cream envelope with her grandmother's handwriting on it, the kind of looping, ironed script that made even *RSVP* sound like a summons. Wren had read it three times on the porch of her rented cottage above the harbor, coffee going cold in her hands, and each time the same line snagged her like a nail in a sweater:

*Please bring your fiancé. Daniel is of course welcome.*

*Of course he was welcome.* Daniel, who had moved out eleven weeks ago with his record collection and his sensible opinions and his apology that sounded like a press release. Daniel, whose absence at the Calloway reunion would be the only topic of conversation more interesting than the wine.

Wren set the envelope down on the rail and looked out at the water, which was doing what the water always did in Harbor Bluffs — holding its color like a secret, silver where the light hit it, green-black everywhere else. The town hadn't changed. That was the problem with coming home. Everything waited for you exactly as you'd left it, patient as a creditor.

She had told herself she wouldn't come this year. She'd written the decline in her head a dozen times: *work commitments, harvest season, so sorry.* But Grandmother Calloway did not accept declines; she absorbed them and then appeared at your door wearing pearls and disappointment, and Wren had learned long ago that it was easier to attend the execution than to postpone it.

So she would go. She would wear something flattering and drink something dry and smile at her aunt Carol's questions about the wedding that wasn't happening anymore, and she would feel the whole table's pity settle over her like a fine mist of vinegar. Everyone would be kind. That was the worst part. The Calloways were devastatingly kind when they wanted to be, kindness deployed like a tarp over something they didn't want to look at.

Unless.

Wren turned the thought over, examining it from underneath the way she examined a contract before an event — looking for the flaw, the hidden clause, the reason it wouldn't work.

Unless she brought someone.

Not someone real. Real people came with their own weather systems, their own histories, their own tendency to fall in love with bridesmaids or get drunk and quote podcasts. Someone real would require managing. But a stranger — a handsome, plausible stranger who owed her nothing and knew no one — a stranger could be anything she needed him to be for one weekend. A shield. A decoy. A man at her elbow whose mere existence said *I'm fine, look, I'm fine,* louder than any speech could.

It was a terrible idea. She catalogued the ways it could fail while she rinsed her coffee cup: he could refuse; he could be married; he could be a serial killer; he could turn out to be boring, which was somehow worse. And yet the idea didn't dissolve the way bad ideas usually did. It sat there, warm and reckless, like a shot of something poured at noon.

Because here was the thing she couldn't say out loud, not to her sister, not to her therapist back in the city, not to anyone: she was tired of being the woman things happened to. The engagement had ended and everyone had rushed in to narrate it for her — *you're better off, he never deserved you, time heals* — until her own breakup felt like a story other people were telling, in a voice that wasn't hers. She wanted, just once, to walk into that house holding the pen herself.

And there was the other thing. The smaller, stranger thing. The man on the platform.

He'd knocked into her two days ago, literally — shoulder to shoulder, her tote bag spinning off onto the boards, a paperback skidding toward the tracks. He'd caught it before it went over the edge, and when he handed it back to her their eyes had met for one second too long, and something in her chest had done a thing she refused to name. He'd looked familiar. Not familiar like *I know you.* Familiar like a song from childhood — a melody you can't place but your body remembers, some chord struck years ago still ringing faintly in the walls.

Tall, dark-haired going gray at the temples early, a face that seemed deliberately arranged to be forgotten — pleasant, unremarkable, forgettable — except that it wasn't, because she'd been thinking about it for two days, which was the opposite of forgetting. He'd apologized, helped her gather her things, and vanished down the platform with a walk that looked like a man leaving a room he didn't want to be in.

She'd half convinced herself she'd imagined the recognition. But Harbor Bluffs was small. Small towns return what you lose.

* * *

She found him on Thursday evening at the Copper Kettle, which was less a café than a community institution with espresso machines. It was the kind of place where the chalkboard menu hadn't changed since 2009 and the owner, Deb, knew everyone's order and everyone's business in roughly equal measure. Wren came in for the wifi and stayed for the anonymity of the corner table by the window, the one spot where the town's attention slid off her.

He was there. Of course he was there — the universe, having decided to meddle, apparently intended to see the job through.

He sat alone at the counter with a notebook open in front of him, writing in longhand, which struck her as either romantic or pathological. A cup of black coffee, untouched, going the way of all neglected coffee. He wore a jacket that had been good once and a shirt that had given up trying, and he wrote the way some people pray — head bent, entirely elsewhere.

Wren stood in the doorway longer than was normal. Long enough that Deb glanced up from the register and gave her a look that said, in fluent small-town, *whatever you're about to do, I will be watching.*

She crossed the room.

"Excuse me," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "You're the man from the station."

He looked up. Up close, the forgettable face did its trick again — pleasant, ordinary, and then, underneath, something that caught the light like a coin at the bottom of a fountain. His eyes were a brown so dark they read almost black, and they held hers with a stillness that made her aware of her own pulse.

"I'm sorry," he said. "About the bag. And the book."

"You saved the book. You're forgiven." She heard herself smiling and didn't stop it. "You're not from here."

"No." Just that. No elaboration, no *visiting my aunt*, no filler. People from cities filled silence like potholes. This man let it sit there between them, unpaved.

"I'm Wren," she said, and watched to see if the name landed anywhere. Nothing moved in his face. Either he was a very good liar or the familiarity ran one direction only, which stung a little more than she wanted to admit. "Wren Calloway. My family has the vineyard up on the ridge."

Something flickered then — quick, gone, like a fish turning under the surface. "Calloway Hill," he said. "I saw the sign."

"That's us." She pulled out the stool beside him without asking, which was bold, and sat, which was bolder, and told herself that recklessness was just courage that hadn't been audited yet. "Can I ask you something strange?"

His pen came to rest on the notebook. "That depends."

"Do you have plans this weekend?"

Deb, wiping the counter six feet away, slowed her cloth by approximately forty percent.

"Why?" he asked. Not suspicious, exactly. Careful. Like a man testing ice with one foot.

"Because I need a date," Wren said, "to a family reunion, and I'd rather chew glass than go alone, and you—" she gestured at him, at the notebook, at the entire aura of *passing through* — "are perfect. You don't know anyone. You have no history here. Nobody at that party will be able to say *oh, I remember when you—* because there is no *when*. You're a blank page."

"A blank page," he repeated. There was something odd in his voice, almost like pain, quickly folded away. "Most people pay to avoid being called that."

"Most people aren't being offered free wine and a view of the harbor." She leaned her elbows on the counter. "Here's the situation. My family throws this reunion every year. It's part reunion, part shareholders' meeting, part trial. This year I'm the main event, because my engagement ended in June and nobody in my family has mastered the concept of *not mentioning it*. If I show up alone, I'll spend four hours being looked at the way you look at a car with its hood up. If I show up with someone—" she shrugged — "they'll still talk, but they'll talk about *him*, and I can live with that."

"And what do I get," he said, "besides free wine?"

"What do you want?"

It was the wrong question to ask a stranger, maybe, or the right one, because he went quiet and looked at the window, at the harbor going pewter in the dusk, and for a moment he seemed very far away, farther than the few feet between them could account for.

"Honesty?" he said finally. "A weekend where I'm someone. Anyone. Where nobody knows what I do for a living."

"What do you do for a living?"

"Consulting," he said, smooth as poured cream, and something about the smoothness made her file it away rather than swallow it whole. Everyone in this town lied about something; at least his lie had manners.

"Fine. Consultant. Even better — vague professions are catnip at family functions. My uncle Rodger will talk to you about markets for an hour and think you're brilliant."

He laughed. It transformed him — the careful face cracking open, younger suddenly, and for one disorienting second Wren was nineteen again and laughing on a dock with a boy whose name she couldn't—

No. That was nothing. That was nostalgia doing what nostalgia did, draping old summers over new strangers.

"Terms," he said, recovering, turning to face her fully. He had a negotiator's posture; she recognized it from a hundred vendor meetings. "If I do this, we agree on the rules first."

"Go on."

"One: I don't use my real name. Not because I'm hiding from the law," he added, and the corner of his mouth twitched, "but because I'd like to stay a blank page, as advertised. You can call me whatever you like."

"Jude," she said, before she could stop herself.

He went still. Actually still — the kind of still that has weight, that makes the air around it heavier. "Why Jude?"

"Lucky guess. You look like a Jude. Literary. Slightly tragic." She was lying, mostly. The truth was the name had simply surfaced, the way names do from deep water, and she chose not to examine why. "Unless you'd prefer Chad."

"Jude is fine," he said quietly, and wrote nothing down, though she had the sense the name had been recorded somewhere permanent.

"Two," he continued. "We keep the story simple. How we met, how long we've been together — simple enough that we both remember it under pressure. Families interrogate. Yours sounds especially well-equipped."

"Oh, you have no idea. My grandmother should have run interrogations at The Hague."

"Noted. Three—" and here he paused, and something in his tone shifted, became almost formal, almost sad — "no real feelings. This is a performance. When it ends, it ends clean. No complications."

Wren looked at him. He was watching the window again, jaw tight, and she understood suddenly that rule three wasn't for her benefit. It was a fence he was building around himself, and she wondered what was inside it that needed containing.

"Agreed," she said. "No real feelings. I've had enough of those this year to last a decade."

Something passed over his face at that — regret? recognition? — and was gone.

"So that's it?" she said. "You'll come?"

He looked at her for a long moment. She watched him weigh it — she could practically see the scales, the sensible side loaded with reasons to walk away, and then the other side, whatever lived over there, whatever hunger had made him say *a weekend where I'm someone.* She recognized that hunger. It was the same one that had put her on a stool next to a stranger with a plan that would have made her mother faint.

"Yes," Jude said. "I'll come."

Just like that. No deliberation worth the name, no *let me think about it.* He agreed the way a man steps off a ledge — decisively, and perhaps already regretting it, and perhaps already past regretting it.

"Okay," Wren said, and realized she was grinning like a girl, and let herself. "Okay. Saturday, four o'clock, Calloway Hill. Wear the jacket. It photographs well."

"It's falling apart."

"All the better. My family distrusts anyone too polished." She stood, pulled a napkin from the dispenser, and borrowed his pen — his hand closing briefly around it as she took it, a small electric negotiation neither acknowledged — and wrote her number on the napkin in looping script that was, she noticed with distant horror, starting to resemble her grandmother's. "Text me tonight. We'll build our story. How we met, first date, the works. Keep it simple, right?"

"Simple," he agreed.

She got three steps toward the door before turning back, because there was one more thing, and she'd learned not to leave important things unsaid — life was short and her evidence was Daniel.

"Jude. Why are you saying yes? Really. You don't know me. This is objectively insane."

He considered the question with the seriousness of a man being asked to explain fire to a child.

"Because you asked," he said. "Nobody asks me for anything. They hand me assignments." A beat. "This felt like being asked."

It was the most honest sentence anyone had spoken to her in months, and it came from a man using a fake name, and that — that was Harbor Bluffs all over. Truth arriving in costume.

"Saturday," she said, and pushed out into the blue evening, heart going like a girl's, telling herself the whole way home that this was strategy, pure and simple, a calculated maneuver, nothing more.

She did not quite believe herself. But she was learning that believing yourself was optional. Showing up was the whole job.

* * *

At home, the cream envelope still lay on the porch rail where she'd left it, and the wind had turned it face-down. She picked it up, carried it inside, and set it on the table, and looked at her grandmother's ironed script for a long moment.

Then she found a pen and, beneath *Please bring your fiancé*, wrote in the margin, small and defiant:

*Bringing someone better.*

She texted the number she'd given him — *it's me. Story time. Start with how we met* — and set the phone face-up beside the envelope, and waited, feeling reckless and rescued in the same breath, like a woman who has jumped and found the net rising to meet her.

The phone buzzed.

*I walked into a wedding I was working and saw the planner talking down a collapsing tent in heels. I decided on the spot I'd marry you. Then the tent fell on you instead.*

Wren laughed out loud, alone in her kitchen, at a story that hadn't happened yet.

*Good,* she typed back. *But make it less dramatic. My grandmother hates drama.*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

*Too late,* Jude wrote. *I already told her the true version.*

She stared at that message for a long time, the laughter draining slowly into something else — a cold thread of curiosity, of déjà vu, of a melody she still couldn't place.

*What true version?* she typed.

But the reply, when it came, was only:

*Saturday. Four o'clock. I'll bring the jacket.*
