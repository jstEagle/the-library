## 5. The Rules of Pretending

### Jude

The wine was terrible, and Jude was grateful for it.

It came in a bottle with a hand-drawn label — a cartoon seagull wearing sunglasses, the words *Bluff Point White* arched over it in a font that had been popular when they were nineteen — and it tasted like green apples and regret. Wren had bought it from the gas station at the edge of town, paying with crumpled bills she'd shaken out of her coat pocket, and now she sat across from him at the kitchen table of her apartment above the boathouse, pouring it into two mismatched mugs because neither of them had thought to bring glasses.

"You realize," he said, turning his mug slowly on the scarred wood, "that this is how every bad decision in history has started. Cheap wine and a handshake."

"We haven't shaken on anything yet." She lifted her mug. "That's what tonight is for."

Outside, the harbor did what it had always done: breathed. Water slapping pilings, a gull complaining somewhere near the fuel dock, the far-off clank of a sailboat's halyard against its mast. Twelve years since he'd last heard that particular music, and his body remembered all of it — the way the sound got under your skin and made a home there, so that any other silence felt like a room with the furniture missing.

He should have been on the ferry back to the mainland by now. He'd told himself that on the walk over, told himself again when she'd held the door open, told himself a third time as she'd rummaged through a drawer for a corkscrew and come up with a butter knife instead. But here he was, drinking gas-station wine out of a mug shaped like a lighthouse, negotiating the terms of a lie.

"Okay." She set down her pen — an actual pen, poised over an actual napkin, because apparently Wren Calloway negotiated everything this way, in writing, with witnesses. He was the witness. He was also the counterparty. It occurred to him that this was structurally unsound. "Rule one. No real names. In public, you're—"

She stopped. Looked at him properly for the first time since they'd sat down, her eyes doing that thing they'd done on the station platform, that flicker of *I know you* quickly shuttered behind something more practical.

"What do I call you?" she asked.

The truth was sitting right there, as it always was, patient as a debt collector. *Jude. Jude Underhill. You knew me before you knew anyone; you knew me before you knew yourself.* He picked up his mug instead.

"Jude," he said. "Jude... Hale."

"Hale." She wrote it down. Her handwriting was still the same — quick, left-leaning, the letters slightly drunk. "Why Hale?"

"It was my mother's name before she married." This was true. It was also, he noted with a kind of distant professional admiration, exactly the kind of detail a liar offers to make a smaller lie load-bearing.

"Jude Hale." She tried it aloud, tasting it. Something moved across her face — not recognition, not quite, but the shadow of its cousin. "Fine. And I'm Wren. That part's real, unfortunately. Rule one-A: you don't ask why I need this, and I don't ask why you said yes so fast."

"That's not a rule, that's a mutual hostage situation."

"All the best arrangements are." She took a sip and grimaced with her whole body, an unguarded motion that hit him somewhere beneath the ribs. God, she was still *like that* — still willing to be ugly-faced about bad wine while wearing a sweater that probably cost more than his monthly rent, still incapable of pretending to enjoy something she didn't. He had spent twelve years around people whose entire lives were performed enjoyment. She was going to ruin him.

"Rule two," she said, recovering. "Physical contact is... situational. Hand-holding acceptable. Arm around shoulders acceptable. Anything beyond that we improvise only if someone's watching and it's necessary."

"Necessary," Jude repeated.

"My grandmother will be watching. My grandmother watches the way weather satellites watch. If she thinks for one second this is staged, she'll take it apart in front of everyone, politely, with dessert forks." Wren turned her mug a quarter-turn, then back. "Which brings me to rule three. We met—"

"At the farmers market," he said. "In the city. You were buying peaches, I was standing too close to the honey stand, you asked me if the lavender honey was worth eleven dollars, I said no honey was worth eleven dollars, you bought it anyway."

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, a boat engine coughed to life at the marina and faded down the channel.

Wren was looking at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere. Not suspicion. Something older than that, and more tired.

"That's oddly specific," she said.

"I'm good at specific. It's the job."

"And what job is that?" she asked, lightly, the way you'd toss a stone into a well to hear how deep it went.

"Consulting," he said, and heard how it sounded — smooth, sealed, a door closing gently in a house where all the doors closed gently. She let it go. He watched her let it go, watched her decide not to pull the thread, and understood that this was the first real thing they'd given each other tonight: not honesty, but the agreed-upon shape of each other's silences.

"Farmers market it is," she said, writing. "Peaches and overpriced honey. It's good. It sounds like something that could have happened to actual people."

*It did happen,* he almost said. *Minus the peaches. Minus the city. Add a pier at low tide and a stolen bottle of champagne and your laugh echoing off the breakwater at one in the morning.* He drank his wine instead.

### Wren

There was something wrong with him, and Wren couldn't stop cataloguing it.

Not wrong-wrong. Wrong like a picture hung a half-inch crooked — nothing you could name, everything you kept noticing. He knew the harbor. When a foghorn sounded, low and mournful off the point, he'd gone still in a way no tourist goes still; he'd listened to it the way you listen to a voice on the phone from someone you buried. And just now, when she'd said "farmers market," he'd looked at the table like it had spoken.

But asking felt expensive. Asking was how you found out things you couldn't unknow, and Wren had spent the last year learning exactly how much a person can carry before the carrying shows. So she wrote *peaches, honey* on the napkin and moved on.

"Rule four," she said. "Exits. Either of us can end this at any time, no explanation owed. One word — 'done' — and we both walk away clean. No follow-up, no awkward run-ins engineered by either party."

"You've done this before," he said.

"I plan events for a living. Weddings mostly. You learn that every contract needs a fire door." She tapped the pen against the napkin. "My sister's wedding, three years ago. The best man and the maid of honor did the fake-flirting thing to make their exes jealous, except neither of them had exes there, and by the end of the night they were slow dancing to the playlist I'd built and crying. Real crying. It ruined the timeline."

"And that taught you..."

"That pretending has a shelf life." She said it lightly. She didn't feel light. "So we set the term now. The reunion is Saturday. Two days. After that, we're strangers again, and nobody has to be graceful about it."

"Two days," Jude said. He was turning his mug again, that slow quarter-rotation, like a man winding something or unwinding it. "And after Saturday, I'm gone anyway. Back to the city Sunday morning."

Something in her chest did a small, traitorous drop at that, which annoyed her so much she wrote *TERM: SATURDAY* on the napkin and underlined it twice.

"Rule five," she said, maybe too fast. "No real feelings. None. This is a business arrangement between two adults who are both too proud to show up alone. Whatever either of us feels — nostalgia, gratitude, boredom, indigestion — stays in this kitchen. We do not confuse proximity with anything else. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He said it easily. Too easily? She searched his face and found it pleasant, open, professionally agreeable — the face of a man who had agreed to many things in many kitchens. For some reason that irritated her more than reluctance would have.

"Say it back," she said. "I want it on record."

"No real feelings ever." He raised his mug toward her. "Cross my heart, hope to die, et cetera."

"You're mocking the process."

"I'm honoring the process. Mockery is a form of rigor."

She laughed before she could stop herself — a real one, startled out of her, the kind she hadn't produced in months, maybe since before Daniel had stood in her doorway with his duffel bag and his apology and his new life already packed around him like insulation. The laugh changed the temperature of the room. She watched it land on Jude, watched something flicker behind his eyes that wasn't in the rules, and firmly pretended she hadn't seen it.

Because here was the thing she wasn't writing on any napkin: she hadn't chosen him at random on that platform. She'd seen his face and felt the floor tilt — that dark-haired boy from the summer she kept in a locked room of her memory, the one who'd promised her a train ticket for two and boarded alone — and then this man had opened his mouth with a stranger's voice and a stranger's name, and she'd thought, *fine, fine, the universe is playing a joke, and I'll play along until I figure out the punchline.*

Maybe he wasn't that boy. Maybe he just had that boy's stillness, that way of watching a room like he was taking dictation from it. People's faces rhymed across decades; she'd read that somewhere. Or maybe he was exactly who he said he was: a consultant named Hale passing through, unlucky enough to resemble a ghost.

Either way, she needed a date on Saturday, and he needed — what? She'd get that out of him eventually. Everyone wanted something. She poured them both more of the terrible wine.

"Your turn," she said. "You must have terms. Nobody agrees this fast without terms."

### Jude

He had one term, and it was the size of the ocean.

*I need to be in this house.* That was the truth under everything: Marcus Vane's manuscript open on his laptop forty miles away, chapter six circled in red — *the fire, the insurance money, the brother no one would name* — and Jude needing, with a hunger that frightened him, to see the inside of Calloway House. To sit at its tables. To learn whether the people in Vane's story had faces, voices, a grandmother with sharp eyes. To find out how much of what he'd been paid to write was true, and what it would cost the woman across the table when it printed.

So instead he said: "One condition. At the reunion, I work."

"Work."

"I'm better in rooms when I have a function. Give me a job. Carrying chairs, pouring wine, wrangling your uncle's hearing aid. A man with a tray is invisible. A man with no purpose gets interrogated."

Wren studied him, and he felt the study like a lamp being angled. "You don't like being interrogated."

"I don't like being *interesting*," he said. "Interesting people get remembered. Remembered people get asked questions. I've built a whole life on being forgettable. It's the most underrated luxury there is."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard someone say proudly."

"It's not sad. It's efficient." He smiled to make it land as a joke, and she didn't smile back, and the smile sat there between them, exposed, until he picked up his mug and hid behind it.

*Forgettable.* His agent used a different word — *discreet*, said with reverence, like a vintage. Twenty-two memoirs in fourteen years. Politicians' wives, fallen CEOs, a tennis champion, a reality star with a ghostwritten soul. His name appeared nowhere. His voice appeared everywhere. He'd told himself once, young and romantic, that he was a craftsman, that anonymity was a discipline like any other. Lately the word that fit better was *buried*. And now here he was, digging himself deeper — into a fake name, a fake relationship, a family's living room — all to protect a book that would detonate under this woman's feet.

Complicity, he thought, always dresses itself as caution. You tell yourself you're being careful. You're actually just choosing which person you're betraying first.

"Fine," Wren said. "You can carry chairs. Rule six: you carry chairs, you stay near me at dinner, and when my aunt Carol asks what you do, you say—" she squinted at him, "—something boring. Say you're in logistics."

"I am in logistics," Jude said. "Of narrative."

"Perfect. Boring. Write it down." She slid the pen across the table, and their fingers touched — briefly, accidentally, the static-cling touch of two hands that had both flinched toward the same object — and they both pretended it hadn't happened, which was, he was beginning to understand, going to be the central activity of the next two days.

She pulled the napkin toward her and read the whole document aloud, her voice taking on the bright administrative tone she probably used with brides: *"Terms of Engagement. One: public names only — Jude Hale, Wren Calloway. Two: contact situational, tasteful, minimal. Three: origin story — farmers market, peaches, overpriced honey. Four: exit clause, one word, no questions. Five: term ends Saturday night. Six: no real feelings, ever, signed in blood or cheap wine."*

She dipped her finger in her mug and pressed it to the napkin. A small purple thumbprint bloomed next to rule six.

"Your turn," she said.

Jude looked at the thumbprint. A fingerprint. The one mark on the whole document that was undeniably, forensically *her* — while his entire identity sat above it in fiction. There was a version of him, a braver version, who would dip his own finger in the wine and press it down beside hers and say, *before I sign, you should know my real name, and you know it already, and I've missed you since 2:14 on a Tuesday afternoon twelve years ago.*

Instead he pressed his thumb beside hers and said, "Signed."

"There." She held the napkin up to the light like a treaty, and the two prints sat side by side, purple and ordinary, and outside the foghorn sounded again, closer now, rolling in off the water. "Witnessed by the state of bad decisions."

"To bad decisions." They touched mugs. The ceramic clink was soft, domestic, damning.

They drank. The wine was still terrible. Neither of them mentioned it again.

### Wren

Later — after the second bottle was half gone and the negotiation had decayed into trading stories that were each ninety percent true — Wren walked him to the stairs that led down off her deck to the beach path, and the fog had come all the way in, erasing the harbor, so that the world ended three feet past the railing.

"Well," she said. "Partner."

"Partner." He stood on the top step, which put them nearly eye to eye, which she hadn't accounted for. Fog did that — collapsed distances, made the safe space between two people seem suddenly negotiable. "For the record, I think your rules are excellent."

"You think they're ridiculous."

"I think they're excellent *and* ridiculous. Like a seatbelt on a lawn chair." He put his hands in his pockets. "Saturday. Peaches and honey. Chairs carried. Feelings strictly prohibited."

"Strictly." She hugged her arms against the damp. "Thank you, Jude Hale. Whoever you are."

Something crossed his face then — quick, there and gone, a fish under ice — and for one suspended second she was certain he was going to say something that would change the whole shape of the weekend. She waited. The fog waited. Somewhere below them, unseen, the water kept working at the pilings.

"Goodnight, Wren," he said.

She watched him descend into the white until the fog took even the sound of his footsteps, and then she went back inside and looked at the napkin lying on her table, two purple thumbprints side by side, and thought: *rule six is going to be the one we break.*

She just didn't yet know which of them would break it first — or that the biggest lie on that napkin wasn't the name *Hale* at all, but the word *ever*, sitting there in her own handwriting, already obsolete.

Across the harbor, in a rented cottage with the lights off, a man sat up late with a laptop, reading a dead man's secret by screen-glow — and in the morning, Marcus Vane's car would come up the coast road, and the reunion would begin.
