## 18. Date Number Six

The harbor fair came to Harbor Bluffs the way it had come for a hundred years: on flatbed trucks, in the smell of frying oil and salt, in strings of bulbs strung between the fish cannery's rusted ribs and the new marina's white railings. By six o'clock on Friday evening the boardwalk was a river of strollers and teenagers, of toddlers with sugar on their chins, of old men who stood at the railing as if the water might finally tell them something it hadn't told them yesterday.

Wren Calloway arrived at the ticket gate at six-fifteen, wearing a dress she'd bought three years ago for an engagement party that never happened, and found Jude Underhill already waiting under the string lights, holding two paper cones of fried dough like a man holding peace offerings.

"You're early," she said.

"You're punctual," he said. "I'm learning your family's habits."

"Careful." She took one of the cones. The dough was hot through the paper, dusted with cinnamon sugar that caught the light like something precious. "That's how they get you."

* * *

Jude had counted the dates now — not out loud, never out loud, but in the private ledger he kept behind his ribs. There had been the reunion itself, which didn't count except that it did; then the walk along the bluff path where she'd shown him where the lighthouse used to be; then dinner at the chowder shack where she'd argued with him about whether clams were better steamed or fried until the owner, who knew her since she was small, had refused to settle it and comped their beers instead; then the afternoon at the vineyard when he'd helped her move crates and she'd laughed at him for holding them wrong; then last Tuesday, when she'd called him — called, not texted — just to say there was a heron standing in the shallows by the pier and he should come see it before it left, and he had come, and it had still been there, and neither of them had said anything about what it meant that he'd dropped everything for a bird.

Five. This was six. And this one had been planned, which made it different, which made it dangerous.

He'd agreed to the fair because Wren had proposed it in front of her cousin Priya, who had raised an eyebrow and said, "Date number six?" in a tone that suggested she was keeping score, and Wren had gone pink and said, "It's not — we're pretending, remember," and Jude had watched her pretend and thought: *she's getting worse at this, and so am I.*

The rules still stood. They'd written them on a napkin at the Anchor Inn three weeks ago, both of them laughing, both of them lying. No real names in public — though everyone in Harbor Bluffs already knew hers, so the rule protected only him, and she had never once asked why. No real feelings ever — though the napkin was folded in his wallet now, soft as cloth, and he took it out sometimes just to look at the grease-spotted ink and feel like a man guarding evidence against himself.

"Fried dough first," Wren said, "or games first? Because I have a strategy, and it involves winning you a goldfish you don't want."

"I want the goldfish."

"Nobody wants the goldfish. That's the point of the goldfish." She bit into her dough, closed her eyes, and made a sound of such unguarded pleasure that Jude felt it land somewhere below his sternum. "God. Twelve years and they haven't changed the recipe. Same oil, probably. Same fryer. Maybe the same guy."

"The same guy?"

"Sal. He ran the ring toss when I was nine. He let me win a stuffed shark because I cried." She pointed across the boardwalk to a booth where a broad gray-haired man was separating a teenager from five dollars with the serene cruelty of long practice. "He's still here. Everything's still here. That's the thing about Harbor Bluffs nobody tells you — it doesn't move. You leave and come back and it's exactly where you left it, like a dog waiting at the door."

"And is that good or bad?"

She considered the question seriously, the way she considered everything, chewing. "Both," she said finally. "Mostly both."

They walked. The fair unspooled around them in noise and light — the clang of the high-striker, the wheeze of a calliope that had been out of tune since the Clinton administration, the barkers calling *three throws for five, everybody's a winner.* Wren greeted people constantly. That was the thing about her here: she belonged to the town and the town belonged back. The woman selling beeswax candles caught her arm to show her a photo of a grandchild. Two teenage boys working the dunk tank called out, "Hey, Wren, you gonna get in the tank again this year?" and she shouted back, "Only if you do!" and they grinned like she'd given them a gift.

Jude walked beside her and felt the strange double exposure of his life. In the city he was a ghost; here, attached to Wren's arm, he was becoming a person. People looked at him. People asked his name. He gave them *Jude,* just Jude, and each time he said it, it felt less like a lie and more like a down payment on one.

At the ring toss, Sal looked up from his booth, squinted, and said, "Calloway girl. Still tall. Who's this?"

"This is Jude," Wren said, and her hand found his without either of them deciding it. "He's with me."

"With you," Sal repeated, weighing it. Then, to Jude: "Three rings, friend. Dollar apiece. Nobody wins, but everybody likes trying."

Jude paid. He threw three rings with the focused incompetence of a man who had genuinely never done this, missed all three, and heard Wren laugh — really laugh, head tipped back, the sound carrying over the calliope — and decided the three dollars were the best money he'd spent in twelve years.

"One more dollar," he said. "One more try."

"Jude—"

"For the goldfish."

"There's no goldfish at ring toss, you absolute—"

There was, in fact, a goldfish. It sat in a plastic bag on the top shelf among the stuffed sharks, orange and doomed, and when Jude's fourth ring — thrown with his eyes half closed, purely on faith — dropped over the neck of a bottle, Sal sighed the sigh of a man betrayed by probability and handed it over.

Wren held the bag up to the light. The fish swam a tight, bewildered circle.

"We can't keep it," she said. "The cottage has no—" She stopped. "You'll keep it. At your place. Name it something terrible."

"Barnaby," Jude said immediately.

"That's not terrible, that's distinguished."

"Barnaby Vane," he said, and heard himself say it, and watched her laugh again, and hated himself for the ease of it — the way Marcus Vane's name slid out of him disguised as a joke, the way everything did lately. She didn't know the name meant anything. She couldn't know. He carried the fish through the fair like a man carrying a small glowing secret, which was, he supposed, exactly what he was doing.

* * *

Wren had been to this fair every year of her life, including the year of the fire — she could remember being seven, being handed a candy apple by an aunt whose hands smelled of smoke, being told everything was fine, everything was fine — and she knew its geography the way she knew the vineyard rows. So when she saw the fortune teller's tent at the end of the pier, purple velvet and hand-painted stars, she almost steered them past it.

Almost.

"Oh no," she said. "Madame Serafina."

"Is she real?"

"She's Dottie Pell, retired schoolteacher, seventy-four, charges five dollars, and once told my cousin Priya she'd marry a man with a boat, and Priya married a man with a boat, and Dottie has never let anyone forget it." Wren hesitated. The tent flap breathed in the sea wind. Inside, a bulb glowed amber. "She's a fraud, obviously."

"Obviously," Jude said.

"But she's never wrong."

They went in. It was cramped and close-smelling — incense over salt air — and Dottie Pell sat behind a folding table draped in the same purple velvet, wearing enough scarves to upholster a chair, her reading glasses pushed up into a cloud of white hair. She looked at Wren with the flat appraisal of a woman who had once given her a C-minus on a book report.

"Wren Calloway." Dottie's eyes moved to Jude, and stayed. "And company."

"Dottie, this is Jude."

"Mm." Dottie patted the table. "Sit. Both of you. No — you." She pointed at Wren. "Go buy more fried dough. You've been staring at that boy's cone all night and it's embarrassing."

"I have not—"

"Five minutes," Dottie said. "I read him alone. House rule."

Wren looked at Jude. Jude, absurdly, felt his ears go warm. "I'll be fine," he said. "Probably."

Outside, the fair noise dimmed to a murmur, the canvas walls trembling with light. Dottie shuffled a deck of cards that looked older than the town, laid five of them face down, and studied Jude over her glasses with the patience of someone who had taught fourth grade for thirty-one years and could detect a lie at forty paces.

"So," she said. "You're the one."

"The one what?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She turned the first card. A tower, struck by lightning, figures falling. "You came here on a train you weren't sure you should take. You're carrying something heavy in a bag with someone else's name on it." She tapped the card. "And you're telling yourself you'll put it down after. After the money. After the job. After it's too late to matter."

Jude kept his face still. It was a professional skill; he'd interviewed senators. But his pulse ticked up. "Cold reading," he said lightly. "You're guessing. Everyone arrives by train. Everyone's carrying something."

"Everyone's carrying something," Dottie agreed. "Not everyone's carrying someone else's." She turned the second card. A moon, pale and doubled. "Two lives. One you show and one you write. And here's the trouble, young man — you've gotten so good at writing other people that you've forgotten which one is yours."

The tent felt smaller. The bulb buzzed.

"You think you're here for a book," Dottie said. "Or for her. Or for the summer you owe a debt to. And maybe some of that's true. But I'll tell you what I see, and you can pay me your five dollars and call it a fraud." She leaned forward. Her voice dropped, not mystical anymore — just an old teacher, tired and kind. "You're waiting for permission to be seen. You've been waiting so long you've started to think the waiting is who you are. It isn't. It's just longer than it needed to be."

Jude said nothing. Outside, the calliope wheezed through a song he almost recognized.

Dottie gathered the cards. "The girl knows something's coming. Not what. She's braver than you about it — she always was, even in fourth grade, she'd raise her hand before she knew the answer." She slid the deck aside. "Whatever you're carrying — decide soon. Secrets don't keep. They ferment. And by harvest, this town will be drinking whatever you've brewed." She waved a beringed hand. "Send her in. And tell her the boat thing stands."

Jude stepped out into the noise and light, five dollars lighter, feeling flayed and strangely clean. Wren was waiting with two fresh cones and an expression of deep suspicion.

"What did she say?"

"She said you owe her five dollars and the boat thing stands."

"Dottie." Wren rolled her eyes, but she was smiling. "What else?"

He looked at her — the sugar at the corner of her mouth, the string lights doubled in her eyes, the whole improbable fact of her standing in front of him asking nothing, absolutely nothing, about who he was or why he was here — and the truth rose in his throat like a tide.

"Nothing else," he said. "Come on. I hear the ferris wheel calling."

* * *

The ferris wheel was the oldest ride at the fair, a white lattice wheel that groaned like a ship in weather, and Wren loved it with a love she'd never examined. They rode near the top, the car swaying, the whole harbor spread below them — the boats, the breakwater, the darkening vineyard hills inland, the fair a spill of gold light on the boards.

"Okay," Wren said. "Confession time. Since we're apparently doing honesty tonight."

"Are we?"

"Dottie seems to think so." She tucked her feet up under her. "Here's mine. This is the best date I've ever been on, and it's fake, and I don't know what to do with that information."

The car swayed. Below, a child screamed with joy on the tilt-a-whirl.

"It's not fake," Jude said.

"You signed a napkin."

"I know what I signed." He was quiet a moment. The wheel turned; the horizon rocked slowly past the window of their car. "Can I tell you something true? Just one. Small. You don't have to match it."

Her breath caught — he saw it, the small hitch — and she nodded.

"The night before I left here. Twelve years ago. I stood on the platform and I almost didn't get on the train." His voice was low, pitched under the machinery. "I want you to know that. Whatever else — I almost stayed."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. The lights moved across her face. "Why didn't you?"

"Because staying felt impossible and leaving felt temporary." He shook his head. "Nineteen-year-olds are idiots about arithmetic."

"Yes," she said softly. "They are."

The wheel reached the top and paused — it always paused at the top, some old mechanical courtesy — and the harbor lay still beneath them, and Wren thought: *he knows. He knows something. He's been carrying it since the station, since the reunion, since the napkin, and he just gave me one true piece of it like a coin through a slot, and I could ask — right now, at the top of the wheel, I could ask him everything—*

She didn't. She was afraid of the answer, and more afraid that the answer would end this, whatever this was, this golden suspended minute at the top of the town she loved with the man she—

With the man she.

Below them, someone recognized them. A cluster of Wren's cousins, lit by the midway glow, began to chant — faintly at first, then louder, delighted, merciless: *"Kiss! Kiss! Kiss! Kiss!"*

Wren laughed, mortified, hiding her face. "Oh God. The rules—"

"Screw the rules," Jude said, "it's for the crowd," and took her face in both hands and kissed her.

It was meant to be performance. It lasted approximately one second of performance and then became something else entirely — became her hand fisting in his collar, became the sugar-sweet taste of fried dough, became the wheel creaking back to life beneath them while neither of them noticed, became the chanting cousins fading into the roar of blood in her ears. When they broke apart, the crowd was cheering and the wheel was descending and Wren's heart was doing something structurally unsound.

"For the crowd," she managed.

"For the crowd," he agreed, hoarse.

The wheel set them down. The cousins high-fived. They walked the length of the boardwalk with the goldfish bag swinging between them, and at the gate where the fair ended and the dark pier began, away from the lights, away from everyone, Wren stopped.

"No one can see us here," she said.

"No," Jude said.

"So this next part's off the books."

She kissed him again — slower, unhurried, with the whole black harbor breathing beside them — and this one wasn't for anyone. This one was a signature on a different document, unwitnessed, binding. When it ended she rested her forehead against his and felt the goldfish bump gently against her knee and thought, with a clarity that frightened her: *I'm falling. I know exactly what I'm doing. I'm falling anyway.*

"Date number seven," she murmured, "is going to be a problem."

"Date number seven," Jude said into her hair, "is going to be a disaster," and he meant it as a joke, mostly, the way he meant most things.

They walked home along the water road, shoulders touching, the fish riding in Jude's jacket pocket like a small orange conscience. Neither of them saw, three booths back, a man in a linen jacket raising his phone — not at them, at nothing, idly, the way men do — nor the photograph he'd taken an hour earlier inside a rented office on Cannery Row, where a manuscript page lay on a desk under a lamp, one paragraph sharp and legible in the frame:

*The fire at Calloway Vineyards was no accident. I have the letters to prove it.*

By morning, it would be everywhere.
