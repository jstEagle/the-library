## 19. The Draft Leaks

The photograph arrived the way ruin always arrives: casually, on a Tuesday, disguised as something else.

Jude was in the cottage kitchen, standing at the counter with a mug of coffee he'd forgotten to drink, when his phone lit up with a message from his agent. *Call me. Now.* Two words, no emoji, no warmth. In fourteen years of working together, Dana had sent that exact message exactly three times, and each time it had preceded either an enormous opportunity or an enormous disaster. He set down the mug and braced himself for one of them.

She answered on the first ring.

"It's out," she said. No greeting. "Part of the Vane manuscript. One page, photographed on his desk — you can see the edge of his blotter, the corner of a framed photo, everything. Someone in his office took it and sold it to a gossip outlet. It went up forty minutes ago."

Jude's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, like an elevator whose cable had been cut. "Which page?"

"You know which page."

He did know. He knew before she said it, knew from the way her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone handling something sharp. He crossed to the window over the sink, where the harbor was doing its morning business — gulls arguing over the tide line, a fishing boat chugging out toward the open water, all of it ordinary, all of it oblivious — and he listened while Dana read him the paragraph that was about to end several lives.

*The old man didn't die in his sleep, whatever the headstone says. The fire at the north barn wasn't lightning, whatever the insurance settlement says. I was fifteen years old and I watched my father's half-brother's family collect on a lie, and I have waited thirty years to say it out loud: Elias Calloway burned so the Calloways could be paid.*

"Jesus," Jude said, uselessly.

"The outlet is framing it as an exclusive excerpt from an upcoming memoir by 'a member of the extended Calloway family.' They don't name Vane yet. That won't last until lunch." A pause; he could hear her deciding how much mercy to spend. "Jude. Is this your draft? Word for word?"

He closed his eyes. The page swam up behind his eyelids — his own typesetting, his own careful line edits, the printed copy he'd left on Vane's desk two weeks ago during their last interview session, because Vane insisted on reading physical pages, because Vane said screens made him feel like a guest in his own life. *A guest in his own life.* The man had staged his own betrayal with a framed photograph in the background for authenticity.

"It's mine," Jude said. "My edit. My line breaks."

"So you're the ghost."

"I'm the ghost."

Another silence, longer this time. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up. "Okay," Dana said finally, in the tone she used for triage. "Here's what happens now. By noon, every outlet that covers wine or wealth or scandal will have the page. By tonight they'll have Vane's name. By tomorrow they'll have yours, because ghostwriters are only anonymous until they're leverage. You need to decide what you are when that happens. Are you the hired pen, or are you the source?"

"I'm neither."

"You're both, sweetheart. You've been both since you signed." Her voice softened by a degree, which from Dana was practically an embrace. "I'm not asking you to decide right now. I'm telling you the clock started forty minutes ago. And Jude — does the girl know yet?"

The question landed in the center of his chest like a dropped anchor. Wren. Wren who had kissed him at the harbor fair with flour sugar still on both their mouths. Wren who had asked him once, gently, terribly, what he did for a living, and had accepted *consulting* with a nod that he'd mistaken for trust and now understood was patience. Wren who was, at this exact moment, probably walking the vineyard rows with a clipboard, checking ripeness numbers, planning a harvest that this photograph was about to burn down around her ears.

"Not yet," he said.

"Then you have a choice about who tells her first. Choose fast."

She hung up. Jude stood at the window holding the phone against his sternum as if he could press the whole morning back inside it, and watched the fishing boat grow smaller, and understood with perfect clarity that the life he'd been carefully not-living for twelve years had just sprung its lock.

* * *

He drove to Calloway Vineyards too fast, windows down, salt air whipping through the car, the harbor road unspooling ahead of him in the gray-gold light. He rehearsed sentences the whole way. *Wren, there's something you need to see. Wren, I wrote those words. Wren, I can explain.* Every version collapsed under its own weight. There was no sentence that could outrun a photograph.

The tasting room parking lot held maybe a dozen cars — harvest crews, the accountant, his aunt's ancient Volvo. He parked at the far end and walked toward the crush pad, where he could hear the clatter of bins being stacked, and it was there, halfway across the gravel, that he saw her.

Wren was standing alone beside the equipment shed, phone in both hands, absolutely motionless.

She was wearing her work clothes — jeans, boots, a flannel shirt with the sleeves pushed up, her hair tied back with whatever was nearest, which today appeared to be a length of twine. She looked, from a distance, like every other morning of the last six weeks. But nothing about her posture was ordinary. People move when they read bad news; they shift weight, they exhale, they do small involuntary things. Wren was not moving at all. She stood the way stone stands, and Jude felt the distance between them — thirty yards of gravel and morning light — become the longest distance he had ever crossed.

He was still twenty feet away when she looked up.

Her face did something he would remember for the rest of his life. It didn't crumple. It assembled. He watched her take the shock and the hurt and the dawning, horrible arithmetic and pack all of it away behind her eyes with a speed that came, he realized, from practice — from a family that had been packing things away for thirty years.

"You wrote this," she said. Not a question. She turned the phone toward him, and there it was: his paragraph, his punctuation, the em-dash he'd fought Vane to include, photographed slightly crooked on a desk he recognized.

"Wren—"

"The fire. My great-grandfather. You wrote that we—" Her voice caught on the word *we*, and she hated it, he could see her hating it, and she pushed through anyway. "You wrote that my family killed him. For money. And then you came here. To my town. To my *reunion*. You sat at my grandmother's table."

"Yes." There was no other honest word, so he said it and let it stand there, naked and insufficient.

"When?" The question cracked out of her like a whip. "When did you write it? Before or after the station? Before or after I asked you — God, Jude, I *asked* you. I picked a stranger off a train platform because he looked kind, and you already knew. You already knew everything."

"I didn't know everything. I knew there was a book. I didn't know what was in it until later, I swear to you, the chapter about the fire—"

"Don't." She held up one hand, and the twine at her wrist slipped down and hung there, absurdly domestic, a thing she'd grabbed that morning without thinking, back when the morning had been innocent. "Don't swear to me. Not today."

Down at the crush pad, someone laughed at something. The sound carried across the gravel, bright and oblivious, and Wren flinched as if it were a gunshot. Jude understood then that she wasn't just hearing the crew. She was hearing the future — every laugh, every whisper, every stranger with a phone, the entire machinery of public attention beginning to grind toward her family's oldest wound.

"How many people have it?" she asked. Quieter now. Worse.

"One page. So far. It'll spread by tonight. They'll have Marcus's name by tomorrow, probably mine soon after."

"Marcus." She said the name like she was testing whether it still fit in her mouth. "Your client. Your *consulting* client." She laughed — one syllable, no humor in it, a sound like glass under a boot heel. "I let it go. Do you know how many times I let it go? The interviews you took calls about. The nights you disappeared to his office. The way you flinched whenever anyone mentioned the fire, the way you never once asked me about it directly, because you already knew the answers. I thought I was being generous. I thought I was giving you room." Her eyes were shining now, furious and wet, and she blinked hard rather than let anything fall. "I was giving you rope."

"Wren, listen to me. The manuscript isn't finished. Nothing is published. What leaked is a draft — my draft, yes, but drafts change. If you tell me what actually happened, if your family tells its own story before he does—"

"*Before he does.*" She stared at him. "You're still working for him. Right now, standing here, you're still his writer. You came running over here to manage me. To protect the asset."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" She stepped closer, and up close he could see how exhausted she was beneath the fury, how many nights of almost-sleep were sitting behind her eyes. "Then tell me what you'd be doing right now if you'd never met me. If I were just the event planner and you were just the consultant. Would you be here warning me? Or would you be on the phone with your agent, figuring out how to spin a leak into publicity?"

He opened his mouth and found it empty. Because the terrible truth was that both answers lived in him simultaneously — the man who loved her and the man who had built a career on other people's pain, and they had shared a body for twelve years without ever properly meeting. Until now. Until this gravel lot, this morning, this woman looking at him like a locked door.

"That's what I thought," she said softly, when he didn't answer fast enough.

"Wren." His voice came out rough. "Twelve years ago I left this town on a train and I've regretted it every single day since. I am not going to stand here and let you believe I'm the same coward twice. Whatever you need me to do — burn the contract, go public, stand in front of your grandmother and confess — I will do it. But please don't decide right now, in the next five minutes, that I'm only the worst thing I've done."

For a long moment she just looked at him. A truck rumbled past on the county road. Somewhere a bin of grapes was set down hard, and the smell of crushed fruit drifted over — sweet and green and ending, the smell of things at the moment they stop being what they were.

"The reunion is Saturday," she said at last. Flat. Businesslike. The event planner surfacing, because the event planner was the part of her that knew how to survive. "Half the county will be here. My grandmother will have seen that page by tonight, and she will know — she has *always* known — that a book is coming. And now there's a photograph." She tucked the phone into her back pocket with hands that were almost steady. "So here's what's going to happen. You're going to leave. You're going to walk back to your car, and you're going to drive away, and you're going to figure out whose side you're actually on. And when you know — really know — you can come find me. But Jude?" She met his eyes, and hers were dry now, which was somehow more devastating than tears. "If you come back still holding his manuscript, don't bother knocking."

She turned and walked toward the crush pad, toward the noise and the work and the family whose story was escaping its cage behind her, and Jude stood alone in the emptying light of the morning and understood that the clock Dana had warned him about hadn't started forty minutes ago.

It had started twelve years ago, on a train platform, and he had spent every hour since pretending otherwise.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He didn't look at it. He already knew who it was — Marcus Vane, calling to savor the morning, to talk strategy, to ask his biographer what they should do next.

*What we should do next.*

Jude stood in the gravel with the vineyard humming around him, the harvest three weeks out, the woman he loved walking away, and for the first time in his invisible life, the answer to the question arrived fully formed, demanding only that he say it out loud:

Burn it all down. Starting with himself.
