## 20. Interrogation at Breakfast

The morning after the leak, the Calloway house did not feel like a house. It felt like a courtroom that had been furnished by someone with excellent taste and no understanding of mercy.

Wren came down at seven-fifteen to find the long table in the dining room already set — not for breakfast, exactly, but for something with the architecture of a tribunal. Her grandmother sat at the head, spine straight as a trellis post, a cup of black coffee steaming before her untouched. The silver had been laid out. The silver was never laid out for breakfast. The silver meant witnesses.

Aunt Delia arrived next, wearing the particular armor of a woman who had done her crying in private and intended to do her fighting in public: pressed linen, lipstick reapplied, eyes still swollen underneath it all. Then Uncle Peter, who owned eleven percent of the vineyard and all of its anxiety. Then cousins, spouses, the younger generation drifting in with the wary faces of people summoned to a meeting they hadn't been told the agenda of.

Jude came down last.

He was dressed carefully — soft gray sweater, sleeves pushed up, the costume of an unthreatening man — and he carried himself carefully too, the way you carry a full glass across a moving room. He caught Wren's eye on his way to his chair and something passed between them, quick and wordless: *we'll get through this*, or maybe *I'm sorry*, or maybe just *here*. She wanted to believe all three. That was the problem. She was discovering she wanted to believe him so badly that believing had stopped being a thing she did and become a thing she performed.

"Sit," said Grandmother Calloway, though everyone was already sitting. "Eat if you can. We have things to discuss."

Nobody ate.

* * *

The photograph had gone up at nine o'clock the night before. By ten it was everywhere that mattered — the wine trade boards, a gossip aggregator with a taste for old-money ruin, three regional papers whose editors had apparently been waiting years for a reason to spell Calloway correctly. The image showed a single manuscript page photographed on a desk in Vane's rented office above the chandlery, angled so the header was legible: *Chapter Seven — The Fire.* Beneath it, two paragraphs. Enough to name Elias Calloway. Enough to say *insurance*. Enough to say *the body was found after the structure collapsed, and the claim was filed within the month.*

Enough to gut them.

The family had spent the evening in triage — lawyers called, statements drafted and redrafted, Aunt Delia's husband driving into town at midnight to buy every physical copy of one paper from the gas station on Route 1, as if scarcity were a strategy. Wren had stood at the kitchen window for a long time watching the dark vineyard, thinking about fire, about how a family could be a building that burned slowly for sixty years and called itself standing.

Now her grandmother folded her hands on the white cloth and began.

"We will not perform grief for one another," she said. "We will not rehearse outrage. What we will do is establish facts, because facts are the only currency we have left that anyone might still accept." Her eyes, pale and precise, moved around the table like a lighthouse beam. "Marcus Vane is writing a book. A page of it has surfaced. It accuses this family of burning our own packing house in 1963, with my father-in-law inside it, to collect on an insurance policy. I want to know how much of that page is true, and I want to know who in this room already knew."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren could feel it against her skin.

"You first, Delia," Grandmother said.

Aunt Delia set down her fork with exaggerated care. "Mother, I found out about the photograph the same time everyone else did. From Peter's text."

"And your reaction?"

"My reaction?" Delia's voice cracked open. "My reaction was that I threw up in the guest bathroom and then called our attorney. Is that the reaction you were looking for? Because I can describe the bathroom in detail."

"I'm looking for truth, Delia. It's in short supply."

Uncle Peter leaned forward. "For the record, I've known about the book since August. Vane's people contacted the winery association asking about historical records — permits, insurance filings, anything from the sixties. I thought it was a title search. I didn't think—" He stopped, rubbed his jaw. "I didn't think anyone would actually write it down."

"You didn't think," Grandmother repeated, without inflection, letting the words sit there like stones in a field. "Thank you, Peter. That's more honesty than I expected before eight a.m."

He went around the table then, methodical, merciless in her mildness. Cousin Amy, who admitted she'd seen Vane at the harbor fair and spoken to him for twenty minutes and found him, God help her, *charming*. The younger ones, who knew nothing and said so with the defensive volume of the innocent. Delia again, when the beam swung back, admitting through tears that their father — Wren's grandfather, dead twelve years now — had once gotten drunk on port wine at Christmas and said, *ask your mother what happened to the packing house,* and that Delia had asked, and been told never to ask again, and had obeyed for forty years.

"You obeyed," Grandmother said softly. "That was always your gift."

Delia flinched as if struck.

Wren watched it happen and felt herself splitting into two women seated in the same chair. One of them was a Calloway, doing the grim arithmetic of a family discovering its own foundation was ash. The other was a woman in love — or in whatever this was, this terrifying gravity well with gray eyes and careful hands — and that woman kept glancing sideways at Jude, cataloguing every micro-expression, hunting for the thing she'd know how to read if only she'd ever learned to read him.

He was listening the way he always listened: completely. Still. His attention a held breath. When Delia wept, his hand moved an inch toward hers on the table and stopped, respecting some border Wren couldn't see. When Peter confessed, Jude's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly, and Wren thought: *he pities us.* And then, immediately, treacherously: *or he's taking notes.*

* * *

"Wren."

Her grandmother's voice arrived like a hand on her shoulder. The whole table turned.

"Yes."

"You've been quiet."

"There's been a lot of talking."

"There has." Grandmother Calloway took her first sip of coffee, unhurried, a woman who understood that silence was a tool with a handle shaped however she needed it. "You brought a guest to this reunion. A man none of us had met. A man who appeared in Harbor Bluffs—" she paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses over the incision line, "—in June. And I find myself wondering what you know about him that the rest of us don't."

"He's my boyfriend, Gran. That's what I know."

"That is what you've *told* me. You told me he was in consulting. You told me he grew up inland. You told me—" the old woman's voice didn't rise; it narrowed, "—that you met through friends. And yet last week, at dinner, he described the day you met, and it was the pier, and the gulls, and the boy who lost his hat to the wind. Which is a lovely story, Wren. It's also a story with details no friend-of-a-friend would retain for a decade. Details like that are retained by someone who was there."

The room had gone very still. Somewhere in the walls, the old house ticked and settled, keeping its own counsel.

Wren's heart was doing something complicated — hammering and sinking at once, love and doubt arriving in the same mouthful, indistinguishable, like salt in a wound. Because her grandmother was right. Of course she was right. Wren had known it since dessert, since Jude told the story of the summer they'd actually shared with the fluency of a man reciting scripture, and she had chosen — *chosen*, deliberately, with her eyes open — not to ask the obvious question. She had let it go. She had told herself it was kindness, giving him room to explain in his own time. Now, under her grandmother's lighthouse gaze, the letting-go looked less like kindness and more like complicity. Like two liars holding hands.

"He was here before," Wren said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Years ago. We knew each other when we were young. He didn't tell you because—"

"Because why?" Grandmother asked. "Because the truth was inconvenient? Because he was ashamed? Or because he had another reason to be in this town, in this season, among this family, and 'boyfriend' was the most useful disguise available?"

"That's not fair."

"No," her grandmother agreed. "It isn't. Very little happening at this table today is fair. But fairness is a luxury for families whose history hasn't been photographed on a stranger's desk." She turned her head, slow as a turret. "Mr.—I'm sorry, I realize I still don't know your surname. Isn't that remarkable? Three weeks in my house and I know your coffee order and not your last name."

"Underhill," Jude said. His voice was level. Wren gave him enormous credit for level. "Jude Underhill."

"Underhill." Grandmother Calloway tasted it like wine she suspected of being corked. "And what is it you consult on, Mr. Underhill?"

"Organizational narratives," he said. "How institutions tell their histories."

Wren heard it and felt the floor tilt a degree. *How institutions tell their histories.* It was true — she believed it was true — and it was also the most elegant non-answer she'd ever heard delivered at a breakfast table. He had a gift for that. For answers that were technically honest and functionally hollow, polished until you could see your own hope reflected in them.

"How timely," said her grandmother. "Given that our institution's history is currently being told by Marcus Vane."

"Gran," Wren said, "stop."

"I haven't started, dear. I'm simply noting coincidences." She counted them off on her fingers, unhurried, terrible. "One: Mr. Underhill arrives in Harbor Bluffs in early June. Two: within days, Marcus Vane leases an office above the chandlery and begins interviews for a book about this family. Three: Mr. Underhill attends our reunion as your escort, gaining access to every wing of this house, every elder, every story. Four: he is, by his own account, professionally interested in how families tell their stories. Five: he tells you a true story about your shared past while concealing that it *was* your shared past." She folded her hands again. "I am seventy-nine years old. I have survived two wars' worth of aftermath, one husband, and four decades of harvests. I do not believe in coincidence at this density."

The word landed in Wren's chest and detonated quietly: *biographer.* No — she hadn't thought it yet. Not quite. But the shape of it was there now, hovering, and once a shape like that hovers it never fully leaves.

She looked at Jude. He was looking at his hands.

"Say something," she said to him. Just to him. The rest of the table fell away, or tried to.

"I came here for you," he said. "Before I knew anything about any of this. Before Vane. I came because of the train schedule, Wren. I saw the seasonal line was running again and I bought a ticket. That's the whole of it."

It was a good answer. It was possibly even a true answer. And it answered nothing, because it slid past the question her grandmother had actually asked — *what do you do, and when did you start doing it here* — the way water slides past a closed fist.

"The whole of it," Grandmother repeated. "What a complete thing to claim. Most men need several lifetimes to manage the whole of anything."

Delia blew her nose. Peter studied the ceiling. Someone's phone buzzed and was silenced with the guilt of a caught animal.

* * *

They broke at half past eight, because the vineyard does not care about your scandals — the nets needed checking, the tanks needed scrubbing, the fruit was coming whether the family survived it or not. Chairs scraped. People escaped in twos and threes, murmuring, and the dining room emptied like a tide going out, leaving behind cold eggs, untouched silver, and wreckage.

Wren stayed. So did Jude. So, pointedly, did her grandmother, who remained at the head of the table drinking coffee that must have gone cold an hour ago, watching them both with the patience of a woman who had outlasted everything else and could certainly outlast two liars in love.

"Go on, Mr. Underhill," she said at last, gently, which from her was worse than shouting. "Wren and I will manage the dishes. Consider it a chance to be useful somewhere the questions are simpler."

Jude hesitated. Wren felt him hesitate — felt the weight of him deciding, the way you feel weather change. His hand found hers under the table, one squeeze, warm and brief and utterly insufficient. "I'll be in the east rows," he said. "The crew starts thinning at nine. I told Marco I'd help."

Of course he did. Of course he'd learned the crew's names, memorized the work schedule, made himself indispensable to everyone except the person who most needed him to be legible. He kissed the top of her head — a gesture that three weeks ago had melted her and now felt like a signature on a document she hadn't read — and went out through the screen door, which clapped shut behind him twice, like applause from someone who knew how the play ended.

Then it was just the two of them and the ticking house.

"Well," said Grandmother Calloway.

"Don't," Wren said.

"I wasn't going to say I told you so. I was going to say something far less comfortable." The old woman rose, gathered her cup and saucer, and stood looking out the window toward the vineyard, where a tall figure in a gray sweater was already walking toward the east rows, shoulders squared, a man heading toward work like a man heading toward an alibi. "I was going to say: check the dates, Wren. Write them down if you have to. The week he stepped off that train is the week that book began. Whatever he is to you — and I believe he is something, I watched him watch you at dinner, and men don't look at women that way unless it costs them — whatever he is, he is also *something else*, and the something else got here first."

Wren stared at the window. At the vines. At the small, retreating figure of the man she loved, growing smaller between the rows, absorbed by the green.

"And when you've checked your dates," her grandmother added, moving toward the kitchen, "ask yourself the only question that matters. Not whether he lied. Everyone at this table has lied, including me, including you. Ask instead: what was he writing down?"

*Writing down.*

The phrase went through Wren like a blade finding the seam of an envelope.

Because she had seen it — hadn't she? The notebook. Black, battered, always in his jacket pocket, appearing at odd moments: at the reunion dinner, at the storm cleanup, at the harbor fair while she waited in line for fried dough. She'd assumed — she'd *wanted* to assume — letters, sketches, grocery lists, the private debris of an ordinary man. But ordinary men did not write the way Jude wrote: fast, covert, head bowed, pen moving like it was being paid by the word.

She stood alone in the dining room amid the ruins of the silver breakfast, and the two women inside her finally spoke to each other. The one in love said: *he squeezed your hand, he came back for you, he stayed through the storm, he learned Marco's name.* The other one said nothing at all. She just opened an imaginary notebook and began, very calmly, to check the dates.

June 3rd: Jude's train.

June 5th: Vane's lease.

Wren picked up her coffee, found it cold, drank it anyway, and went to find out what else she had failed to ask.
