## 21. The Question She Doesn't Ask

The morning after the leak, Harbor Bluffs held its breath.

Jude felt it in the way the light came through the cottage windows — not the gold of the last two weeks but a thin, washed-out silver, like the town had drawn a curtain over itself. Down on the main road, he could hear the gulls arguing over something, and beneath them the low diesel murmur of the first press truck arriving early for harvest prep. Normal sounds. Harvest sounds. But everything underneath them had changed pitch since that photograph surfaced: a single page of Marcus Vane's manuscript, blown up and passed around like contraband, its typeset accusation — *the fire was no accident* — sitting in every phone in town.

He made coffee he didn't drink. He sat at the little table under the window with his laptop open to nothing, cursor blinking in an empty document, and listened to the cottage settle around him the way it did every morning, beams ticking as the sun warmed them, as if the house were clearing its throat before saying something difficult.

His phone had buzzed twice already. Both times, his stomach dropped before he saw the screen. Both times it was only Vane's assistant confirming the afternoon interview slot, as if the world hadn't tilted, as if they were still simply two men working on a book about a self-made vintner's rise. *Mr. Vane would like to do today's session at the winery rather than the office. He feels the setting will be productive.*

Productive. The word sat in Jude's chest like a swallowed stone.

He should have quit last night. He knew that. He'd sat on the edge of the bed with his agent's number glowing on the screen, thumb hovering, and then Wren's face had come to him — not angry, not yet, just *watching*, the way she'd watched him across the storm-lit rows two nights ago when they'd almost kissed and the almost had been more honest than anything either of them had said aloud — and he'd put the phone down. Because quitting meant explaining, and explaining meant confessing, and confessing meant watching her understand, in real time, that the man who'd stood beside her through the storm and the fair and her grandmother's needle-sharp scrutiny had been taking notes the whole time.

There were good reasons to stay silent. He had a list of them, actually; he'd drafted it mentally the way he drafted everything, in clean declarative sentences. The penalty clause would bankrupt him. The book wasn't finished; nothing was decided until it was finished. Vane controlled the material, not him. He was a ghost, and ghosts didn't choose what haunted houses.

All true. All beside the point. He'd learned long ago that the most dangerous lies were the ones built entirely out of true sentences.

At nine-fifteen there was a knock at the cottage door, and his whole body went still before his mind caught up and identified the rhythm — two knocks, a pause, one more — Wren's knock, the one she'd established without discussion sometime during the second week, when she'd started coming by with crew schedules and vineyard gossip and excuses so thin he could have read newsprint through them.

He opened the door and she was standing on the step holding a paper bag and wearing the expression of someone who has rehearsed being casual all the way up the hill.

"Breakfast," she said. "The kitchen at the house is a war zone. My aunt is interrogating the staff about who talked to whom, and my cousin Dev is on his third espresso and nobody should be near him. I figured your cottage was the only neutral territory left in Harbor Bluffs."

"Neutral territory," Jude repeated. "Like Switzerland."

"Like Switzerland." She stepped past him into the cottage, and he caught the scent of her — rain still, faintly, from the storm two nights ago, or maybe from this morning's fog — and something in his chest pulled tight, the way a rope does when both ends are being held by people who won't let go.

She unpacked the bag on his counter with the ease of someone who had done it before: croissants from the bakery on Front Street, two of them, plus a jar of the blackberry jam she claimed was the only thing her family's estate couldn't ruin because the brambles grew wild where nobody tended them. She talked while she did it — small, bright talk about the crew, about the sorting table needing repair, about how her brother Theo had tried to fire the new intern twice before nine a.m. Her voice was steady. Her hands were steady. Only her eyes gave her away, flicking once, quickly, to the corner of the room where his notebook lay face-down on the armchair, and away again.

He saw her see it. She saw him see her see it. Neither of them said anything, and the silence between those three facts was the widest thing in the room.

They ate at the little table. She sat across from him with her croissant torn into pieces she mostly moved around the plate, and he watched the morning sharpen outside the window, fog burning off the harbor in slow rags, and waited for the question. It was coming. He'd known it was coming since the photograph surfaced, since he'd seen her face at the tasting room door when someone showed her the page — that terrible stillness of hers, the stillness he'd first fallen for twelve summers ago and now recognized as the sound of a person deciding how much truth they could afford.

"So," she said finally, and wiped jam from her fingers with a napkin, folding it into smaller and smaller squares. "Can I ask you something?"

Here it is, he thought. Here is the train leaving the station, and you can still get on it or you can watch it go.

"Anything," he said, which was a lie so large it deserved its own chapter.

"What are you going to do about the harvest dinner seating chart? Because my grandmother has put Marcus Vane directly across from me, and I need an ally within reaching distance."

It took him a full second to reroute. Then he laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and she smiled, and for a moment the cottage was warm again, the two of them conspiring over place cards like the stakes were china patterns and not the excavation of her family's oldest grave.

But the question hadn't come. And now he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that it was still coming. That this was the approach run. That Wren Calloway, who planned events down to the candle, who could read a room the way sailors read weather, had simply chosen to circle before landing.

* * *

They walked down to the vineyard together because there was work to do and because neither of them wanted to sit still. The morning had turned bright and hard-edged, the kind of coastal light that shows everything — every netting tear from the storm, every broken trellis wire, every face in the crew that looked up too quickly when they passed, conversations dying mid-sentence. The leak had done that. The town had always watched the Calloways; now it watched them with its mouth open.

Jude worked beside Wren in Block Four, checking fruit for rot where the storm had bruised the clusters. It was close, careful work, hands moving through leaves, shoulders nearly touching in the narrow rows. She taught him without teaching him — he'd gotten better over two weeks, clumsy-earnest, and the crew had stopped laughing at him somewhere around day four and started handing him things instead, which he understood was a promotion.

"You know," she said, tipping a cluster toward the light, inspecting it, "my grandmother asked me about you again last night."

"Let me guess. She wants references."

"She wants *everything*. She's like a customs official. She asked where you went to school, and I realized—" Wren paused, set the cluster down, brushed a leaf off her wrist. "I realized I didn't actually know. Isn't that strange? Two weeks, and I don't know where you went to school or what you—"

And there it was. Not the question he'd feared, but the doorway to it, and he felt every muscle in his back tighten against the row behind him.

"—do," she finished. "What you do. For work. You've never said. You're here all hours helping us, which is lovely and slightly suspicious, and when I asked Theo if you'd told him anything about yourself, Theo said, and I quote, 'He asks great questions and answers none.' Which is a very charming quality in a man and a very alarming one in a houseguest."

The leaf in Jude's hand had become very interesting. A pinot noir leaf, five-lobed, veined like a map of somewhere he couldn't go.

"Consulting," he said.

The word came out smooth. That was the horror of it — how smooth it came out, after all these years. He'd said *consulting* to taxi drivers and aunts and a woman in a bookstore who'd asked why he looked familiar, and each time the word had come out polished, frictionless, a stone worn round by repetition. It was technically even true. He consulted. He consulted famous people on the shape of their own lives and then erased himself from the record.

Wren turned to look at him fully. In the row's green half-light her eyes were the color of the sea past the headland, gray-green, changeable, and right now they were doing the thing he dreaded most, which was nothing at all. No narrowing. No suspicion. Just attention, patient and total, the attention of someone who had grown up reading people at reunions full of people lying about money.

"Consulting," she repeated.

"Management consulting. Sort of. It's — freelance. Communications work, mostly. For companies. People." He heard himself adding detail, the amateur's mistake, the liar building rooms onto a house that needed none. "Confidential clients, mostly, which is why I'm vague. Contractually vague. There are clauses."

"I see."

"It's boring," he said. "Genuinely. You'd fall asleep."

Something moved through her face then — fast, gone before he could name it. Disappointment? Relief? He would think about that flicker for days afterward, turning it over like a stone looking for what lived underneath, and never be sure.

"Okay," she said lightly. "Consulting. Mysterious, contractual, boring consulting. That explains the notebook, I suppose. Notes for clients."

"Notes for clients," he agreed, and the lie went through him like a needle finding a vein, quick and clean and poisoning everything downstream.

* * *

They worked another hour. The sun climbed. The crew moved up the rows ahead of them, and the ordinary machinery of harvest absorbed them both — crates, clippers, the shouted jokes in Spanish and Portuguese that he only half understood and loved completely. For stretches of it, Jude managed to forget. That was the seduction of physical work; it forgave you hour by hour. His hands knew what to do even when his conscience didn't.

But Wren had gone quiet in a new way. Not cold — she laughed at Marco's impression of Dev on espresso, she argued cheerfully with her cousin about whether Block Four would come in at twenty-two or twenty-three brix — but between the laughter there were these intervals, these small silences, and in them he could feel her thinking. He could feel the question she wasn't asking stacking itself up behind the questions she was. Where are you really from. Why do you know so much about writing. Why did you flinch when the manuscript page showed everyone Vane's handwriting, when everyone else just got angry.

She was assembling him. Piece by piece, politely, without ever letting him see the blueprint. And the worst part — the part that kept surfacing in him like a body in water — was that she was being *kind* about it. She was giving him room. Every question she declined to ask was a door she chose not to open, and each unopened door felt less like mercy than like debt accruing. Interest compounding daily.

At midday they sat on the flatbed of the crew truck sharing water and the last of the bakery's almond croissants, legs dangling, the whole valley below them terraced down toward the harbor, the water hammered flat and bright by noon.

"My father used to say," Wren said, out of nowhere, squinting at the horizon, "that in this family you could tell who was hiding something by who stopped asking questions. Everyone else gets louder. The guilty ones get quiet and helpful." She took a sip of water. "You've been extremely helpful lately."

The words landed softly and cut deep, the way she seemed to do everything.

"That's a terrible way to live," Jude managed. "Assuming everyone who helps you is guilty."

"No," she said. "The terrible way to live is assuming they're not." She hopped down off the tailgate, brushed off her jeans, and smiled at him — a real smile, warm, devastating — and walked back up the row to where Marco was waving a clipboard. "Come on, consultant. Block Five isn't going to check itself."

* * *

That evening the interview happened, because the world did not pause for the state of Jude Underhill's soul.

Vane had taken over the estate's small barrel room for the session, and he was magnificent in it — candlelight he'd requested, the barrels stacked in their dark ranks like sleeping animals, the man himself in a linen shirt the color of cream, gesturing as he talked about his years of exile with the practiced cadence of a story told many times to many listeners. Jude sat with the recorder between them and transcribed, and nodded, and felt each word go into the manuscript like a brick into a wall being built around Wren.

"And when I wrote to my brother after the funeral," Vane was saying, "after the fire — do you have that, the letter? — he returned it unopened. Unopened, Jude. Twenty-two years old, and my own blood wouldn't even break the seal. Whatever else goes in this book, that goes in. Let them explain the seal."

"I have it," Jude said quietly. "It's in chapter six."

He walked home in the dark with the recorder heavy in his pocket like a confiscated weapon. The cottage lights of the estate glowed below him, and in one window — the kitchen — he could see figures moving, the family gathering for some late meal, and among them a silhouette he knew by posture alone, Wren, pouring wine, laughing at something, her head tipped back.

She hadn't asked. All day, she hadn't asked. And he understood now that her letting it go was not the reprieve he'd wanted it to be. An accusation he could fight. An accusation had edges; you could push back against it, deny it, survive it. But this — this patient, loving silence, this door held shut out of respect — this was worse. This was her trusting him with the benefit of the doubt, spending her own credibility on him in front of a family that was already bleeding, and every hour he didn't correct her, the bill grew.

He sat down at the table under the window. He opened the laptop. The cursor blinked in the empty document, patient as a pulse.

On his phone, Vane's assistant had sent tomorrow's schedule. Three sessions. *Mr. Vane feels we're entering the final chapters.* And beneath it, a second message, sent an hour later, from Vane himself this time — the first time the man had texted him directly:

*Good session tonight. One more thing I need from you before we finish: everything you can find on the granddaughter. The events planner. Public sources only, of course — but a book like this needs texture, and she's the emotional heart of the next generation. Readers will want to know who she is.*

*Who she is.*

Jude stared at the screen until it dimmed, then lit it again, reading the words a second time as though they might rearrange themselves into something he could live with. They didn't. Somewhere below him, the kitchen window went dark, the family drifting to bed, Wren somewhere in that house believing — generously, expensively, against all her better instincts — that the man on the hill was exactly what he said he was.

He typed *consulting* into the search bar of his own life and found nothing there worth keeping.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would fix it. Tomorrow he would find the sentence that saved them both.

Outside, far down the coast, the evening train sounded its horn at the trestle bridge — one long note over the water — and Jude closed the laptop without writing a word.
