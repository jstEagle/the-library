## 34. What He Should Have Said

The tasting room smelled of oak and dust and the ghost of a thousand afternoons that had nothing to do with him.

Jude stood in the doorway for a long moment before he entered, the printed pages trembling slightly in his hand despite the stillness of the morning. He had rehearsed this on the train — the whole four hours of it, watching the coastline unspool past the window like a film he'd once starred in and abandoned halfway through. He had rehearsed it walking from the station, rehearsed it standing outside the Calloway gates until a field hand who remembered him from harvest waved him through without a word. He had rehearsed every sentence, every pause, every place he might break down and every place he might be tempted to soften.

And now, standing in the empty tasting room with the morning light falling in long amber bars through the windows, he understood that none of the rehearsal mattered. Because Wren was already there.

She sat at the long cedar table where, twelve summers ago, they had shared stolen glasses of the estate's worst vintage and laughed until the owner of the vineyard — her grandmother, though he hadn't known it then — had chased them out with a dish towel. She sat with her hands folded on the wood, not touching anything, not looking at anything, wearing the particular stillness of a person who has decided to hear something out and has not yet decided what she'll do afterward.

She'd come because he'd asked. That was all. No promise of forgiveness, no promise of anything. His message had been six words: *I'm here. The tasting room. Whenever you're ready.* And she had come, which was either mercy or curiosity or the last courtesy owed to someone who was about to lose everything, and Jude found he couldn't tell which, and suspected it didn't matter.

"You came back," she said. Not an accusation. Just a fact, set down on the table between them like a stone.

"The train let me off," he said. "It didn't say I could stay."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, not quite its absence. "Sit down, Jude."

His name in her voice. Not *the stranger from the platform*, not *my date*, not the careful nothing-name they'd agreed to use in public during those first weeks of pretending. Just his name, plain and unadorned, and it landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to reach the bottom.

He sat across from her. The table was wide enough that their hands, if they'd reached, would not have touched. He was grateful for that. He did not trust himself to touch her and keep talking.

"I wrote something," he said. "On the train. After I left." He set the pages down but kept his hand on them. "It's not the book Vane hired me to write. It's not even close to that book. It's — me. All of it. Everything I should have told you the night you asked me to be your date, everything I should have told you at the reunion, at the storm, at the fair, at breakfast when you asked what I do and I said *consulting* like a coward."

"I remember," Wren said quietly. "I remember all of it."

"Then you know how much there is to confess." He lifted the pages. "I'm going to read it out loud. I thought about just giving it to you, letting you read it alone, but that felt like one more way of not being present for it. I've been absent my whole life, Wren. Professionally absent. Personally absent. I ghostwrite other people's lives so I never have to live mine out loud. So I'm going to sit here, in front of you, and read it in my own voice, and you can leave whenever you want. You can laugh. You can throw the wine decanter at my head. There's no negotiation here. I'm not asking for anything. I just need you to hear it from me before you hear it anywhere else."

"Vane's press conference is tomorrow," she said.

"I know."

"And excerpts are already leaking. My family's name is trending. My grandmother hasn't slept." Her voice stayed level, but he saw the cost of the levelness in the white of her knuckles. "So whatever you have to say, it's arriving late."

"It is," he agreed. "Late by twelve years, late by two months, late by every single day I chose the contract over you. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. But late isn't never. So." He looked down at the first page. His handwriting, scrawled in the rocking dark of the train car, was barely legible in places. He'd transcribed it at three in the morning in his rented cottage, the same cottage where he'd first read Vane's manuscript and learned what the Calloways' oldest wound really was. The lamp had buzzed. His hands had shaken then too.

"Wren," he began, and stopped, and started again. "Wren. My name is Jude Underhill. You've known that since the platform, even when we pretended we didn't. But here's what you didn't know, and what I didn't say: I came to Harbor Bluffs that first time as Marcus Vane's biographer. His ghostwriter. I write memoirs for people who want their lives told without doing the telling themselves. I've done eleven books. None of them carry my name. I chose that work because invisibility felt like safety, and safety felt like the only thing I could afford."

She didn't move. The light moved instead, crawling slowly along the cedar grain, marking time.

"I knew your name before I ever got on that train," he read. "It was in Vane's source material — a family in Harbor Bluffs, a vineyard, a fire. And under the family tree, in the genealogy section his researcher compiled, there it was: *Calloway*. And underneath that, in my own memory, older than any file: *Wren*, nineteen years old, sitting on a stack of harvest crates, telling me she'd leave this town someday if only someone would leave with her."

He heard her breath change. Just slightly. He kept reading.

"We were nineteen. You know this part, or part of it. We met in June, at the harbor fair — you were working the wine booth for your family, and I was supposed to be passing through on my way to nowhere in particular, and I bought a glass of the worst rosé either of us has ever tasted and stayed for the whole summer anyway. You showed me the coves. You taught me the difference between pinot noir and pinot gris by making me guess blindfolded until I got it right nine times out of ten, and you said that was the moment you knew I was worth keeping around. We made plans. Real ones. A notebook full of them — I still have the notebook; you'll see it later, or you won't, that's your choice now. We were going to leave together in September. You were going to study event design in the city. I was going to write — actually write, fiction, my own name on the cover. We shook hands on it like children, which we were, which is the whole tragedy in a sentence."

The words were coming easier now, or harder — he couldn't tell the difference anymore. His voice had found a register below performance, somewhere in the chest, and it carried whether he wanted it to or not.

"On the last night of August, my father called. You never met him. He was a man who borrowed money the way other men breathed — constantly, involuntarily, without ever quite meaning to. He'd been diagnosed that spring and hadn't told me, because telling me would have meant admitting he needed something, and needing things was the one sin he never committed twice. Except he committed it that night. He called and he said, *Jude, I'm sick, and I'm broke, and I don't know how to ask you for help, so I'm asking.* And I got on the train the next morning."

Wren's hands unfolded. Refolded. She said nothing. She had promised herself nothing, he realized — not silence as punishment, but silence as discipline, holding herself to the same standard she was holding him: *no interruptions, no absolution, no shortcuts.*

"I didn't call you," he read. "That's the part I've never said out loud to anyone, including myself, in plain language. I didn't call you because I was ashamed, and because I told myself I'd send for you once things stabilized, and because shame is very good at writing letters it never mails. My father lived eleven more months. I spent them working three jobs and learning, in the margins of his hospital forms, how to make sentences disappear into other people's mouths. A nurse's aide read my father's story back to him once — I'd written it for her, free, the first thing I ever ghostwrote — and he cried, and I understood that I had found a trade. I also understood, somewhere beneath that, that I had traded. I had taken the summer we planned and exchanged it for a dying man's comfort, and I told myself for twelve years that it was a fair price, and some nights I still believe it, and most nights I know it was also cowardice dressed up as devotion. Both things are true. I've stopped trying to make them add up."

He turned the page. The paper whispered. Outside, far off, a tractor coughed to life and faded — harvest prep, the crew, the life of the vineyard continuing without pausing for the two people sitting in its empty heart.

"When Vane offered me the contract," he continued, "I almost refused. You should know that. I read the outline — *a vintner's rise, a family's fall, the truth about Harbor Bluffs* — and I saw your family's name and I felt the floor go out from under me. I told my agent I'd pass. Then she told me the number. And the number was the exact number that would pay off the last of my father's debts, the ones I inherited, the ones I've been carrying since I was twenty years old. So I said yes. I told myself it was just research. I told myself I'd steer the book away from anything that could hurt you. I told myself a great many things, and every one of them was a lie I signed my initials to, because that's what ghostwriters do — we sign away our names and our objections in the same stroke."

"And then I met you again," he read, "on the platform, and you didn't recognize me, or pretended not to, and God, Wren, the relief of it. The relief of being a stranger to you. Of getting to start over without the leaving attached. When you asked me to be your date — you, asking me, the universe handing me a second chance wrapped in a lie — I said yes before I could think, exactly the way you described it later, recklessness as self-rescue. I told myself I'd keep the two lives separate. The biographer and the boyfriend. The contract and the kiss. I actually believed, for a while, that a man could hold those apart, the way you hold two glasses at a tasting and never let them touch."

"They touched," Wren said. Very quietly. Not a question.

"They touched," he said. "At dessert, when your grandmother cornered me and I told her how we met — the true story, the fair, the blindfolded guessing game — and I watched your face while I did it. At the storm, when we worked until dawn and nearly kissed and I pulled back because kissing you felt like spending money I hadn't earned. At the fair, when we kissed twice, once for the crowd and once for no one, and the second one undid me completely. Every day at harvest, hauling bins beside your crew, being adopted by people whose family I was secretly helping to betray. They touched, and bled into each other, and by the time I understood what I'd become — a man in love with a woman whose ruin he was drafting, chapter by chapter, for another man's byline — the manuscript was half done and the penalty clause was a guillotine over my neck and I told myself, one more week, I'll fix it next week. Cowardice with good reasons. I'm very good at cowardice with good reasons. It's practically my signature style."

He paused. Drank water from the pitcher on the table because his throat had gone to sand. Wren waited. She was very good at waiting; he'd forgotten that, or maybe he'd never known it, maybe the girl of nineteen had grown into a woman who could sit inside another person's confession like it was weather.

"The chapter about the fire," he said, and his voice finally cracked, and he let it. "You know what Vane claims. That your grandfather died in a fire your family set for the insurance. I read that chapter in my cottage at midnight and I was physically ill. I thought about burning the draft. I thought about sending it back with a letter telling Vane to find another ghost. I did neither. I kept reading, and I kept the money moving toward my father's creditors, and I told myself the truth wasn't mine to judge — that a ghostwriter's job is transcription, not conscience. That was the night I became, fully and formally, the kind of man I'd spent my whole life promising myself I wouldn't be. Not a liar, exactly. Worse. A man who tells true things for false reasons, in service of someone else's revenge, and calls it work."

"So when the draft leaked," he went on, "when your family fractured and your grandmother started interrogating everyone and you looked at me across that breakfast table with suspicion blooming behind your eyes — I deserved every degree of it. And when you asked what I did for a living and I said *consulting*, and you let it go — that kindness was the cruelest thing anyone has ever done to me. Your mercy was worse than your anger would have been. Anger I could have answered. Mercy just sat there, patient, waiting for me to be worthy of it, and I wasn't, and I knew I wasn't, and I stayed silent anyway. That silence is the second-worst thing I've ever done. The first was the train, twelve years ago. I've spent my adult life perfecting departures."

He reached the last page. The sun had climbed; the amber bars had gone white and hard. Somewhere in the vines, the crew was singing one of the old harvest songs, muffled and off-key, and the ordinariness of it made the room feel more exposed, not less.

"Here is what I should have said," he read, "at the platform, the first day. *I know you. I loved you. I left and I was wrong to leave the way I left.* Here is what I should have said when you asked me to be your date: *I can't, because I'm here to write a book that will hurt you, and you deserve better than a bodyguard who's also the arsonist.* Here is what I should have said at breakfast: *I'm a ghostwriter. Vane is my client. I've known since before I arrived.* Here is what I should have said the night of the storm, and the night of the fair, and every night after: *I love you, and loving you while doing what I'm doing is a betrayal of the word itself.*

"Instead I said nothing, and then I said too little, and then Vane said everything, in the worst possible voice, and you put me on a train. Which was correct. You were right to do it. I'm not here to argue with the sentence you handed down."

He set the pages flat on the table. His hands were steady now — the strange calm that comes after the shaking is finished.

"But here is what I did after the train," he said, no longer reading, looking at her directly for the first time since he'd begun. "I terminated the contract. Not renegotiated — terminated. The penalty clause takes everything I have and then some. I will owe my agent's agency money for years. I sent you the confession chapter because I wanted you to have my version before Vane's version finished burying your family. And I came back here with no money, no career, no mask, and one printed chapter, because I finally understood that the invisible man doesn't get to ask to be seen. He has to stand in the light first and let it hurt. So. Here I am. In the light. It hurts."

Silence filled the tasting room the way wine fills a glass — slowly, completely, up to the brim.

Wren looked at him for a long time. He let himself be looked at. That was the whole point; that was the entire tuition he was paying.

"You wrote that my mercy was cruel," she said at last. "It wasn't mercy. It was evidence-gathering." A beat. "But you've made it unnecessary. Congratulations. You convicted yourself better than I ever could."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned forward, and her eyes were dry and bright and terrible. "Because here's what I heard in all of that, Jude. I heard a beautiful confession. Genuinely — it's beautifully written; of course it is, it's what you do. And underneath the beauty, I heard a man who is still narrating himself. Still choosing the words. Still controlling the frame. You confessed to everything except the one thing I actually need to know."

"Ask it," he said. "Anything."

"Why now?" Her voice didn't rise. That was the frightening part. "Not why did you take the contract — poverty explains that; I understand poverty better than you think. Not why did you lie — fear explains that too. Why *now*? What changed between the breakfast table and the train? Because a man who betrays someone for twelve years and two months doesn't wake up honest by accident. Something made honesty cheaper than lying. And I need to know what it was, because if it was losing me — fine, I can live with being the reason, I suppose. But if it was the penalty clause, if it was the money turning bad, if you're here because the math changed and not because you did — then this whole speech is just one more manuscript, and I'm just one more client, and you should get back on the next train before I start believing you."

The question landed exactly where she'd aimed it, in the soft place under his ribs where he kept the answer he'd been avoiding since the platform.

"That's fair," he said. "And I've thought about it, on the train and before it, and I'll give you the true answer even though it's ugly. It was both. It started as the money — I drafted the resignation email the night of the storm and deleted it because I needed the payment, and I've never hated myself more cleanly than I hated myself in that moment. But it ended as you. Because somewhere between the storm and the fair and the harvest rows, the arithmetic inverted. Losing the money became survivable and losing you didn't. And I know how that sounds — like I'm telling you that you outbid Vane. Maybe I am. Maybe love is always partly an auction, and the best any of us can do is be honest about what we bid and when. But I'll tell you the difference, Wren, and the difference is this: Vane paid me to tell his truth. You never asked me for anything except to show up. And showing up — staying, being seen, being *known* — turned out to be the only currency I ever actually wanted. I burned the contract for you. I'd burn it again. That's the whole testimony."

Wren held his gaze for a long, long moment. Then she reached across the cedar table and picked up his pages — the confession, the whole ruined architecture of his honesty — and read the final paragraph silently, her lips moving slightly, the way she used to at nineteen over the harvest ledger.

When she looked up, her eyes were wet, but her voice was not.

"My grandmother wants to convene the family tonight," she said. "Everyone. The aunts, the cousins, the ones who haven't spoken to each other since the leak. She's going to decide what the Calloway name does next — fight Vane, settle with him, or something no one's thought of yet." She folded his pages once, precisely, and held them against her chest like a document entered into evidence. "I'm going to bring your confession to that table. All of it. Including the parts about me, including the part where you say you love me in front of a room full of people who have every reason to hate you."

"Wren—"

"You wanted truth without negotiation," she said, standing, tucking the pages into her jacket. "Fine. Here's yours: I believe you. I believe every word, and I still don't know if I forgive you, and those are both going to be true for a long time, so don't ask me to resolve them. But my grandmother taught me that a family that controls its own story survives, and a family that lets strangers write it dies. Vane thinks he's holding the pen." She walked to the door, then stopped, her hand on the frame, not looking back. "Tonight, at seven, the family votes on what happens next. Be in the courtyard. If my grandmother asks for you — and she will; she already knows you're back, she knows everything, she's terrifying — you'll tell her what you told me. Word for word. And Jude?"

"Yes."

"If they offer you what I think they're going to offer you — don't you dare say yes for my sake. Say yes because it's yours. Or don't say it at all."

The door swung shut behind her. Jude sat alone in the tasting room, in the hard white light, listening to the harvest song drift in from the rows, and understood that whatever happened at seven o'clock, the invisible man's trial had already begun — and for the first time in his life, he was glad to be the defendant, present and named, awaiting the verdict of a family that had every reason to close the book on him forever.
