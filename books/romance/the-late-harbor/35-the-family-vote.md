## 35. The Family Vote

The Calloways had always fought in the parlor.

It was the room her great-grandmother had chosen for it, Wren thought as she stood at the window that Sunday morning and watched the cars come up the drive — the long gravel sweep that curved past the oldest vines on the property, the ones planted before anyone alive could remember who had put them in the ground. The parlor had high ceilings and low light and a fireplace that smoked if you looked at it wrong, and every important argument in the family's history had happened beneath its water-stained plaster. Weddings were held in the garden. Funerals in the church. But verdicts — verdicts happened here.

Wren had dressed carefully for a war. Not armor exactly; she'd learned by now that armor read as guilt. She wore the soft gray sweater her mother used to say made her look like weather, and she'd pinned her hair back with the clip from the harbor fair, the one Jude had won off a ring-toss booth by missing four times and then, with the whole crowd watching, not missing at all. She touched it once, briefly, like checking a pulse.

He wasn't here. That had been the condition, hers, delivered through the door of the cottage where he was staying — *I'll tell them everything, but they need to hear it from me first, without you standing there being the thing they're angry at.* He'd agreed too easily, which either meant he trusted her or he'd already accepted that trust was not something he could ask for yet. Maybe both. He was good at both: the waiting and the believing anyway.

Her uncle Robert arrived first, because Robert was always first to anything with a vote in it. Then her aunt Dee and her husband, whose name Wren forgot every single time and covered with *sweetheart* so smoothly no one had ever caught her. Her cousins Theo and Priya came together in Theo's ridiculous yellow car. Her father came last, alone, which said more than any of them would say out loud today. Marcus Vane was not invited. Marcus Vane, Wren suspected, was currently sitting in his rented office above the hardware store refreshing a news feed, waiting to see whether the family chose surrender or suicide, and enjoying either option.

And Grandmother Calloway — Edith, though no one had called her that to her face since 1987 — was already in the parlor when Wren walked in, seated in the wingback chair by the cold fireplace with her hands folded in her lap like a woman who had been awake since four and had used every hour of it.

"Sit down, Wren," she said. "You look like your grandfather the day the bank called."

"That's charming, Gran."

"It wasn't meant to be." But something moved behind her eyes — the old sharpness, the one that missed nothing, softened by a degree Wren had never seen before. "It was meant to be honest. Sit."

Wren sat. The others filed in and arranged themselves in the loose geometry of family crisis: Robert by the window because windows were exits, Dee on the sofa with her hands already wringing a tissue that didn't need wringing yet, Theo and Priya on the piano bench like children summoned for a lecture, her father standing until Dee patted the cushion beside her and he sat like a man lowering himself into cold water.

The manuscript page was still taped inside the pantry door where Wren had found it three days ago. Someone — she never learned who — had photographed it and sent it to everyone, and now it lived in all their phones, that typeset fragment with its terrible calm: *the fire of October 1974 was not an accident, whatever the insurance settlement and the county record agree upon.* Vane's prose. Vane's patience. Twenty years of exile distilled into two paragraphs designed to be read in a waiting room.

"You've all read it," Grandmother Calloway said. It wasn't a question. "Some of you have read worse this week, I imagine, since Mr. Vane has been generous with his excerpts. So I'll skip the part where we pretend we don't know what this is about."

"Mother—" Robert began.

"I'm speaking, Robert. You'll get your turn to be wrong in a moment; I know how much you enjoy it."

Dee made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. Even now. Even here. Wren loved her aunt desperately for it.

"The facts," Grandmother Calloway continued, "are these. In October of 1974, my husband set fire to the east barn, which was insured for more than it was worth and mortgaged beyond even that. Your grandfather — most of your grandfather, the man some of you remember — was in that barn. Whether he went in to stop the fire or to save the ledgers or because forty years of habit carried him toward trouble before his mind could argue, I do not know. I have never known. I stopped letting myself know, which is its own kind of answer." Her voice did not waver. It had been sanded down over decades until nothing rough was left to catch. "Harold Vane — who worked for us, who was my husband's half-brother by a father none of us acknowledged — died in that fire. His son Marcus was nine years old. We paid the claim. We buried Harold quietly. And we told the town a story about faulty wiring, and we told ourselves the same story until we believed it, which is what families are for, mostly. Telling each other stories until the truth gets tired of knocking."

No one spoke. Outside, the wind moved through the vines with the dry rattle of leaves deciding whether to let go. Harvest was six days away.

"So," Robert said at last, clearing his throat with the confidence of a man about to be useful. "The strategy is obvious. We deny it. There's no proof — a photographed page proves nothing, it's hearsay dressed up in italics. We retain counsel, we threaten Vane with defamation, and we—"

"We do what we've always done," Wren said.

Every head turned. She hadn't meant to speak yet. She'd planned to wait, to let them exhaust themselves, to deliver her speech from the rubble. But Robert's voice had done something to her — that smooth, boardroom certainty, the same tone that had managed the family's silence for fifty years as if silence were an asset class.

"Say that again?" Robert asked, pleasant and dangerous.

"No," Wren said, and stood. "You heard me. We do what we've always done. We hire lawyers and we deny and we bury it deeper and we make sure whoever pays is someone who can't fight back — that's the family business, isn't it? Grapes and grief management. Harold Vane has been dead fifty years and we're still managing him." She looked around the room, at the faces she loved, faces that had fed her and failed her in equal measure. "Marcus isn't doing this because he hates us. I've met him. I've listened to him. He's doing this because he was nine years old and nobody pulled out a chair for him. Nobody ever said *you belong here* — and the reason nobody said it is the thing in that manuscript. Every year we keep the secret is another year we prove him right."

"And what's your alternative, sweetheart?" Dee asked gently. "Air the worst thing this family ever did in front of the whole town? Let the vineyard — let *all of this*—" she gestured at the walls, the land, the generations stacked in the wallpaper, "—become a headline?"

"Yes," Wren said.

The word landed in the middle of the room like a dropped glass.

"My grandmother just told you the truth herself. Did the roof fall in? Is anyone bleeding?" Wren's heart was going hard but her voice held, and she realized with a strange floating clarity that she wasn't performing anymore — hadn't been since the interview, since she'd sat in front of a camera and given the fire back its real shape. "I already told the story. On Tuesday. With grief instead of accusation, because that's what it deserves — grief, and Harold Vane's name said out loud, and an acknowledgment that a boy grew up fatherless because of us. And you know what happened? The town didn't burn down. People brought casseroles. Mrs. Okafor from the hardware store — Vane's landlord — told me her mother had always wondered about that fire and was glad somebody finally said it. Half this town has been carrying a corner of this secret for fifty years without knowing what it was. They're relieved. Being lied to for decades is heavy, even when you don't know you're carrying it."

"The vineyard contracts—" Robert started.

"Are holding. Two buyers called to say they respect it. One canceled. One. We'll survive one."

Theo raised his hand like they were in school. "Okay but — and I love you, Wren — there's a second problem, and it's got brown hair and it's been sleeping in the cottage." A few uncomfortable shifts around the room. "The ghostwriter. Vane named him. In front of God and everybody. If we go public with our version, the first question out of every reporter's mouth is going to be about the guy who was writing the other version while dating our cousin under a fake job title. We can't control that narrative while he's—"

"He's not the enemy."

"He lied to you for months."

"He lied to me for months," Wren agreed, and felt the old wound open and breathe, "and I haven't forgiven him, and I may not, and that's between him and me and it is *not* the same question as what we do about the book. Stop conflating them because it's easier than looking at the fire."

Silence again. Her father spoke for the first time, quietly, from the end of the sofa. "What is it you actually want, Wren?"

She'd asked herself that at three in the morning, more than once, staring at the ceiling of her childhood bedroom. What did she want? Not vengeance — Vane could keep his. Not absolution — the family didn't deserve a clean bill, and neither, honestly, did she; she'd spent weeks lying by omission with a smile on her face, and she knew exactly how easy it was, how warm the lie felt from the inside.

"I want us to tell it before he does," she said. "All of it. Properly. Not a press release — a book. Our book. The fire, Harold, Marcus, the silence, all of it, told by us, with our names on it. Because if we write it, we choose the words. We choose what Harold's life means instead of letting it only mean our worst night. And—" she took a breath, because this was the part she'd rehearsed and the part she feared, "—I want it written by someone who knows how to tell a true story. Which means Jude Underhill. By name. On the cover."

The reaction was immediate and various — Robert's scoff, Dee's soft intake, Theo's eyebrows achieving orbit — but Wren kept her eyes on her father, and her father kept his eyes on his hands.

"Absolutely not," Robert said. "The man is compromised. He was Vane's biographer. He's the villain of this chapter, and you want to hand him the pen?"

"He returned Vane's contract. He tore it up knowing the penalty would take everything he has — his agent told Priya's friend at the paper, it's public now, the man is functionally bankrupt because he wouldn't put his name on Vane's version. And he wrote something else." Wren reached into her bag and set the notebook on the coffee table — his notebook, the one he'd left behind in the cottage, the one she'd read twice and then sat with for an hour just holding it. "Notes for a different book. Ours. Written before any of this broke, when he still had every reason to protect himself. Read the margins. He wrote *tell it tenderly* next to Harold's name. He wrote *she deserves the choice her grandmother never got* next to mine. This is who he is when he thinks no one's watching. I want that man telling our story, because he's the only person alive who knows all of it — the fire, the family, and what it costs to hide."

"And if he betrays us?" Robert pressed. "One more time?"

"Then I'll have been wrong about him in public, and I'll live with it. But I don't think I am." She finally turned to the wingback chair. "Gran. You've been quiet."

Grandmother Calloway had been watching Wren throughout with an expression Wren couldn't parse — not warmth, not approval, something older and more complicated, like a woman reading a letter she'd written to herself fifty years ago and never mailed.

"I've been thinking," she said, "about chairs."

"...Chairs?"

"Marcus Vane wants a chair pulled out for him. That's all any of this has ever been about. And I have spent fifty years making sure there was never a chair in this house with his father's name near it." She unfolded her hands. Wren saw, for the first time, how old they were — how the sharpness had been holding up a structure that age was finally collecting on. "When your grandfather died I told myself I'd protected this family. I hadn't. I'd protected a story. And stories, Wren, are like vines — you can train them anywhere, but they draw from the root, and ours has been poisoned at the root for half a century. Everything that's gone wrong since — your father and his brother not speaking, Marcus's lawsuits, the way this family can't sit through a reunion without someone leaving early — it all draws from that root."

"Mother," her father said carefully, "what are you saying?"

"I'm saying I side with Wren." The room went very still. Even the wind seemed to pause outside. "We publish. All of it. Our names on it, including mine — I was married to the man who struck the match, and I will sign my confession in ink if that's what it takes. And we put the boy's chair at the table. Both boys." Her eyes flicked toward the window, toward the road that led down into town, toward a rented office above a hardware store. "Marcus too. When this is done — if he can stand it — there's a seat for him. Not because he earned it. Because withholding it is how we got here."

Robert made a noise of pure disbelief. "You're the one who built the wall, and now you're handing out sledgehammers?"

"I'm the one who knows exactly how heavy the stones are," Grandmother Calloway said, "because I carried every one of them up myself." She rose — slowly, one hand on the arm of the chair, and Wren moved to help her and was surprised to be allowed to. Standing, small and straight-backed in the low light, she surveyed her descendants like a general reviewing troops she intended to lead personally into an unwinnable battle. "Here is what happens next. Wren coordinates the book. The Underhill man writes it, credited, bylined, no ghosts — I want his name so large on the cover that no one can ever accuse us of hiding behind him again. Robert, you'll call the insurers' archivists and the county records office and get us every document we can legally obtain, because if we're telling the truth we tell it with receipts. Dee handles the town — the church, the historical society, the Okafor woman, everyone whose trust we'll need. Theo, the harvest does not stop. Six days. If the fields fail while we're busy confessing, we'll have traded our name for nothing. And someone—" her voice caught, just once, a hairline fracture in fifty years of masonry, "—someone should tell Marcus that his father's name will be in the book. Spelled correctly. With his photograph, if we can find one."

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Dee laughed — wetly, helplessly, wiping her eyes with the tissue she'd finally needed — and said, "God help us, we're really going to do it," and the room broke apart into motion, voices overlapping, Robert already arguing terms he'd lost, Theo on his phone to the vineyard manager, her father standing at the window with his back to all of them and his shoulders shaking in a way Wren pretended not to see so that he could keep pretending he wasn't.

Grandmother Calloway squeezed Wren's hand — hard, brief, a signature at the bottom of something.

"You understand what you've done," she said quietly. "Forgiveness isn't mercy, child. It's strategy. The family that tells its own truth first never has to buy it back later. I learned that fifty years too late. You learned it in time to save us." She paused at the doorway, small against the bright hall. "Now go tell your writer. Tell him the vote was unanimous — after Robert stopped voting." The ghost of the old wit crossed her face. "And tell him, Wren, that if he hurts you again, I'm ninety-one years old and I have nothing left to lose, and I know where the matches are."

Then she was gone down the hall, and Wren stood alone in the parlor where every verdict in the family's history had been handed down, holding the notebook that contained a stranger's tenderness for her family, feeling the strangest thing of all:

hope, with paperwork.

Outside, the light had turned gold and slanted, harvest light, the kind that made even the damaged rows look like they were trying. Six days. There was so much to do — the documents, the interviews, the outline she'd already started sketching in her head at three a.m., chapter by chapter, the fire and the boy and the chair.

She picked up her phone and typed the message before she could lose her nerve, her thumbs moving fast across the glass:

*They voted. It's unanimous — well, eventually unanimous. We're publishing the truth ourselves, our names, all of it. And Gran says the book is yours. Byline and everything. Come to the tasting room tomorrow — bring the notebook. Bring yourself.*

She hit send, and stood watching the screen, and watched the three dots appear almost instantly, as if he'd been holding the phone since the moment she'd walked out of his cottage days ago, waiting.

His reply came in one line, and she read it three times, and then she looked out the window at the vines, at the long gold afternoon, at the first pickup truck already rumbling up the drive with crates rattling in its bed — the crew, early, eager, the season refusing to wait for anyone's healing —

*Tomorrow. I'll be there. Wren — thank you. For the chair.*

And beneath it, a second message, arriving a heartbeat later, that made her breath catch and her heart do the reckless, hopeful, unforgivable thing it had been threatening to do for weeks:

*Also — the crew asked if I'm coming back for harvest. I told them I'd ask you first. So. Are you asking?*
