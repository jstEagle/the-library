## 38. Bylines

The email arrived at 6:47 in the morning, which was how Jude knew it was real. His agent, Priya, had spent twenty years perfecting the art of the dramatic silence — the held breath before good news, the long pause before bad — but she had never once been able to wait for a civilized hour when something actually mattered.

**Subject: IT'S DONE**

He read it three times before he got out of bed, the screen's light pale against the gray-blue dark of the cottage. The cottage had been his for eight months now, rented month to month from a widow named Mrs. Okonkwo who left tomatoes on the porch and never asked what he did for a living. Outside, the vineyard rows ran down toward the harbor in long silver lines, the last of the harvest still hanging on the late-season vines, and the light was doing the thing it did every morning — the thing that had ruined him for every other place he'd ever lived.

*The Calloway book. Announced. Your name on the cover. UNDERHILL, Jude — not "with," not "as told to." YOURS. Publisher is thrilled. Advance renegotiated in your favor, don't argue with me. Congratulations, you beautiful idiot. You finally did it.*

He sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, bare feet on the cold floorboards, and let the words settle over him like weather.

His name. On a cover.

For fifteen years he had lived in the white space of other people's stories. He had written the memoirs of a retired senator, a disgraced tennis champion, a pop star who cried beautifully on cue, a hedge fund founder who wanted his childhood poverty rendered in lyrical prose and his second wife rendered not at all. He had learned to disappear so thoroughly that at parties — the rare ones he attended, standing near the hors d'oeuvres — people would ask him what he did and he would say *consulting* and they would nod and drift away, satisfied, because *consulting* was a word shaped like a closed door.

He had told himself, all those years, that invisibility was a discipline. A craft. The ghostwriter's gift was that he could walk into any life, absorb it, and walk out again without a trace — and the world called that professionalism, and he called it safety, and neither of them called it what it was, which was fear wearing a very good suit.

And now his name was going to sit on a book jacket in twelve-point type, and the world would know that Jude Underhill had written the truth about the Calloway fire, the Calloway debt, the Calloway silence — and about his own father, who had come to Harbor Bluffs with a debt of his own and left on a stretcher, and about a nineteen-year-old boy who had promised a girl with paint on her wrists that he'd be on the train with her, and then boarded it alone.

*You finally did it,* Priya had written, and the strange thing, the thing he kept turning over like a stone in his pocket, was that it didn't feel like an arrival so much as a landing. The difference between the two, he decided, was that an arrival is something you celebrate, and a landing is something you survive.

* * *

By nine he was in the tasting room, which had become his office by unspoken consensus, at the corner table by the window where the morning light came in low and gold across the barrels. The tasting room was empty at that hour — the public hours didn't start until eleven — and Wren had given him a key in September with the same offhand grace she gave everything, as if handing a man the keys to your family's sanctuary were the most natural thing in the world.

She was there when he arrived, up on a ladder rehanging the string lights that had come down in the last storm, and she looked at his face and then at his phone, still in his hand, and she said, "It happened, didn't it."

It wasn't a question. She had been there for the whole negotiation with the publisher — had sat across from him at this same table while he drafted the announcement, had watched him hover over the author line the way a man hovers over a signature on a confession.

"It happened," he said.

"Say it out loud."

He set the phone down. "Jude Underhill," he said, and the name sounded foreign and enormous in his own mouth, "is going to be on the cover of a book."

Wren came down the ladder slowly. She was wearing her work jacket, the one with the frayed cuffs, and there was a smudge of dust along her jaw, and she looked at him the way she had looked at him across twelve years and one burned bridge and one long, stubborn season of repair — like he was a thing worth reading all the way to the end.

"The ghost," she said, "has a byline."

"The ghost has a byline."

She crossed the room and kissed him, brief and sure, tasting faintly of coffee and the cold morning air, and then she pulled back and looked at him with an expression he was learning to read as *I'm proud of you and I'm also going to make you work for it.*

"Say it again," she said.

"Jude Underhill."

"Louder. The barrels should know."

"Wren—"

"The barrels," she said, "have heard every other version of you. The consulting version. The ghost version. The version who sat in this room at two in the morning writing about my family and couldn't tell me his own name." She took his face in both hands, her palms rough from the vines, and her voice gentled. "Say the name like it's yours."

And he did. He said it to the barrels and the string lights and the low gold light and the woman holding his face like something she'd already decided to keep, and the strange thing was that by the third time, it stopped sounding foreign. By the fifth, it sounded like a key turning in a lock he'd been carrying around for fifteen years without knowing it was his.

* * *

The calls started at noon.

He should have expected them. He had, actually — had spent a sleepless hour after Priya's email cataloguing exactly who would call and in what order — but knowing a wave is coming does nothing to keep you dry.

The first was Priya herself, wanting to walk him through the announcement cycle: the trade announcement, the excerpt placement, the *question* of morning television, delivered with the word *question* wrapped in enough cotton to smother it. He let it go to voicemail, because he already knew his answer and wanted to say it to her live, once, without the chance to be talked out of it.

The second was the senator. Not the senator — the senator's people. An assistant with a crisp voice informing him that Senator Harlan was preparing his own memoir of the administration years and hoped to secure "the same collaborative team," and would Mr. Underhill — *Mr. Underhill,* said with a faint surprise, as though the name had surfaced from somewhere unexpected — be available in the new year.

Jude watched the phone ring itself out on the corner table, next to his notebook and a cold cup of coffee, and felt something in his chest that took him a moment to identify. It wasn't satisfaction, exactly. It was closer to the feeling of closing a door on a room you'd lived in for a long time — not relief, not grief, just the specific weight of a thing ending because you had ended it.

The tennis champion called at two. The pop star's manager called at three, and then the pop star herself, leaving a voicemail that began *Jude, honey, it's Delphine, don't be a stranger* and lasted four minutes, and he listened to all four minutes because Delphine had cried beautifully on cue but had also, once, at three in the morning in a hotel in Lisbon, told him the true story of her mother, the one that never made it into the book, and he had carried it for her ever since, unwritten, like a stone she'd asked him to hold.

He let every call go to voicemail. Not out of cruelty — or not only. It was that he had spent fifteen years answering, instantly, eagerly, the moment anyone dangled a life at him. *Come write my father's story. Come write my scandal. Come disappear into my marriage.* And he had always gone, because going was easier than being somewhere, and being someone, and having a name that could be found.

The voicemails piled up like weather reports from a country he had emigrated from. He listened to them that evening, all of them, standing at the window of the tasting room while the harbor went bronze and then violet, and he noticed something that made him laugh out loud, alone, into the empty room: not one of the callers had ever said his name before. Not once, in fifteen years. It had always been *our writer,* or *the collaborator,* or, in Delphine's case, *honey.* The name *Jude Underhill* had been a dead letter, an address with no occupant — until a vineyard family with a fire in its past had needed someone to tell the truth, and had decided the truth deserved a living author.

* * *

Wren found him there at closing time, a stack of glasses under one arm, and read his face the way she'd learned to all season.

"Voicemails?" she said.

"Eleven."

"Good ones?"

"Offers." He turned his phone over on the table, face down, like closing a book. "The senator. The tennis player. A streaming company that wants a limited series about — and I quote — *the untold story behind the book.*"

"And?"

"And I keep thinking about my father." He said it plainly, because he had learned, this year, to say plain things. "He spent his whole life being good at things nobody saw. The best carpenter in three counties, and he died with his name on nothing. Not a building. Not a sign. Just his hands, and what they made, and then not even that." He looked at the window, at the last light on the water. "I used to think invisibility was what I inherited from him. Now I think it's the thing I have to give back."

Wren set down the glasses. She came around the table and stood beside him at the window, shoulder to shoulder, and for a while they watched the harbor do its evening work of turning everything to gold and then letting it go.

"You know what my grandmother said," she said finally, "when we voted to put your name on the cover."

"You told me. She said—"

"She said, *A ghost wrote the first version of this family's story, and look what it cost us. The next one gets a name on it.*" Wren smiled, small and crooked. "She also said you'd get fan mail and it would go to your head, and I'm contractually obligated to tell you she's watching."

"Your grandmother," Jude said, "terrifies me more than the streaming company."

"Good. Balance."

* * *

He wrote the announcement post himself that night — Priya had wanted to handle it, had sent him three drafts, and he had read them and found them all written in a voice that wasn't his, which was, he supposed, fitting, and also exactly the thing he was done with. So he wrote his own, four sentences long, and read it to the empty cottage, and pressed send at 11:52 at night with his heart going like a boy's.

*My name is Jude Underhill. For fifteen years I wrote other people's stories and let them keep the credit. This spring, a book with my name on the cover will tell the story of the Calloway family of Harbor Bluffs — the fire, the silence, and the long work of telling the truth. I wrote it standing in their vineyard. I'll be there when it comes out.*

He expected, after that, not to sleep. Instead he slept like the dead, and woke to a notification count that made him put the phone face-down on the kitchen table and go out to the porch, where Mrs. Okonkwo's tomatoes were ripening in the early light and the harbor was doing its morning silver trick, and he stood there a long time breathing like a man who has set something heavy down and is remembering what his shoulders are for.

The phone buzzed against the table inside. Once. Twice. A third time, insistent, the particular triple-buzz of an actual call.

He went in and looked at the screen.

**MARCUS VANE.**

He stared at it. The name sat there, glowing, patient, a man he had spent a season transcribing and a chapter redeeming and a chair, at the family table, pulling out. Vane had been given his acknowledgment — a chapter in the Calloway book, his exile told in his own words, his grievance heard at last. He had accepted it, stiffly, at the harvest table, with the expression of a man who had come to a war and been handed a seat, and who was still deciding whether a seat could hold everything he'd brought to burn.

The phone buzzed a fourth time. Then a fifth.

Jude picked it up and answered, and Marcus Vane's voice came through, warm and roughened, the voice of a man who had rehearsed something and abandoned it halfway.

"Underhill." A pause. Wind on the line — Vane was somewhere outside, somewhere high, the harbor maybe, or the bluff road. "I read the announcement. Your name. On the cover." Another pause, longer, and when he spoke again the charm had thinned and something older showed through it, something that sounded almost like the nineteen-year-old who had once been erased from a family tree. "I need to show you something. Before the book goes to print. Something about the night of the fire." His voice dropped, and the wind took the edge off it. "Something that isn't in any of the versions. Not theirs. Not mine. Not yours."

Jude stood in the doorway of the cottage, the morning light on the water, his name — his actual name — still glowing on the screen where the announcement post lived, and felt the ground tilt, gently, the way it always did when a story he thought was finished opened one more door.

"Tell me," he said.
