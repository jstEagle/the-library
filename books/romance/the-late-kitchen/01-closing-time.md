## 1. Closing Time

The rain came in off the harbor the way it always did on Tuesdays, sideways and without apology, and by four o'clock the windows of Fairweather Books had gone soft with it, the street outside blurred into watercolor — the cannery's dark hulk at the end of the block, the lampposts bleeding their light into puddles. Ivo Fairweather stood behind the counter with a rag in one hand and a mug of tea going cold beside the register, and watched Mrs. Pell read the last forty pages of a mystery she'd already confessed to having guessed.

"You could just skip to the end," Ivo offered.

"That," said Mrs. Pell, "would be cheating." She turned a page with the deliberation of someone who had all the time in the world and intended to use it. "The ending is earned, dear. You don't get to take shortcuts with an ending."

Ivo smiled and said nothing, because there was no arguing with that, and because Mrs. Pell was eighty-one and had buried two husbands and a son and still came every Tuesday for her mystery, and Ivo had privately decided years ago that whatever she believed about endings was probably true.

At five-fifteen the school bus hissed to a stop across the street and Wren came down the steps with her hood up and her backpack held over her head like a shield, taking the crosswalk at a dead sprint, and burst through the door trailing rain and October.

"Dad. Dad. We're doing the play."

"Hello to you too."

"The *play*, Dad. Ms. Ferreira finally told us what it is. It's *The Tempest*. But shortened. I'm Ariel."

Ivo set down the rag. "Ariel. The spirit of the air."

"I'm mostly invisible and I do everyone's chores," Wren said, shrugging out of her wet coat and hanging it on the peg with the precision of a person who had been told approximately nine hundred times. "But I get to fly. There's a harness. Maybe. If Mr. Okafor can figure out the rigging. He said maybe, which means yes but he hasn't told the principal yet."

"Ariel," Ivo repeated, and something in their chest went warm and complicated, because eleven years ago — almost exactly eleven years ago, though Ivo did not let themselves think in those terms, not usually, not on ordinary Tuesdays — they had sat in a lecture hall at the university while a woman with ink-stained fingers had argued that *The Tempest* was a play about forgiveness, about whether you could ever really give back what had been taken, and Ivo had fallen in love so hard and so fast it had felt less like falling than like being struck.

Wren didn't know that. Wren knew that Shakespeare was the thing Dad got weirdly quiet about, the way some kids' parents got quiet about old songs or dead relatives.

"Ariel's good," Ivo said. "Ariel gets the last word, sort of."

"Ariel gets *freed*," Wren corrected, with the moral seriousness she brought to everything. "That's the whole point. Everybody thinks it's about the storm but it's actually about letting people go." She paused, considering. "Or keeping them. Prospero keeps everybody for like five acts and then feels bad about it."

"And how do you feel about it?"

"I think he should've apologized sooner." She was already moving toward the poetry section, dropping her backpack behind the counter in the spot that had been hers since she could walk — the fortress of unsold remainder stacks where she did homework among the discounted Rilke. "Can we have dinner here? Please? Adaeze's working late and she said she'd bring soup if we stayed."

Mrs. Pell looked up from her mystery. "That child negotiates like a union lawyer."

"She gets it from her grandmother," Ivo said, which was true, and also a way of not saying anything else.

* * *

By six the shop was empty except for the three of them — Ivo, Wren, and the rain, which had settled into a steady drumming against the glass like a patient visitor who intended to stay the night. Ivo flipped the sign to CLOSED, locked the front door, and began the ritual: lamps dimmed to the amber settings, the register counted, the returns cart wheeled to its corner, the cat-shaped bookend on the history shelf righted after some customer had inevitably turned it to face the wall.

Fairweather Books occupied the ground floor of a narrow brick building on Cordage Lane, wedged between a shuttered tailor's and the annex building where Adaeze Okonkwo had lived for thirty-four years and where Ivo now lived upstairs, in the apartment above the shop, with Wren. The cannery loomed three doors down — Harrow & Sons Seafood, once the largest employer on this stretch of coast, closed eleven years ago this autumn, its windows boarded, its brick stained black with a century of smoke. The town had been trying to figure out what to do with it ever since. Lately the answer, according to the notices stapled to the plywood, was: nothing good.

Ivo tried not to look at it. This was easier some days than others.

In the back of the shop, past biography and past the cramped bathroom, was the kitchen — a room barely wider than its stove, with a counter scarred by decades of cutting boards and a window that looked out onto the alley, where somebody's umbrella lay abandoned and filling with rain. It had come with the building. So had the smell of old grease and older bread, and Ivo had never managed to renovate it, partly for lack of money and partly because there was something in the room that resisted improvement, the way certain faces resist smiling.

It was here that dinner happened, on Tuesdays and most other nights: Wren's homework spread across the counter, a stack of poetry collections repurposed as a booster seat and then, when she grew, as a table, and whatever Ivo could make from what was in the fridge.

Tonight it was soup — Adaeze's soup, delivered at five-thirty in a dented pot with a lid that didn't match, along with instructions ("heat it slow, don't you dare microwave my egusi") and news (the annex building had been sold; the new owners were "developers, whatever that means when it's your home"). The soup was ground melon seed and spinach and dried fish, thick enough to stand a spoon in, and Wren ate hers standing up, reading aloud from a library book about deep-sea creatures between bites.

"The anglerfish," she announced, "lures its prey with a light. It grows the light itself. Out of its own body."

"Mm."

"It's a lie the fish tells with its face."

"That's — one way to put it."

"And the little fish go right up to it even though it's obviously a trap, because they're hungry, and then—" She made a sound like a vacuum cleaner. "Gone."

"Cheerful bedtime reading."

"I'm not reading it at bedtime, I'm reading it at dinner. Bedtime is *A Wrinkle in Time*." She pointed her spoon at Ivo. "You promised. Chapter seven."

"I remember what I promised."

"You forget things," Wren said, not cruelly, the way children state facts. "You forgot picture day. You forgot that Tuesday is trash night twice last month."

"I did not forget trash night."

"You put it out Wednesday morning in your robe and Mr. Delgado saw."

Ivo conceded the point with a nod and refilled her bowl, and thought, not for the first time, that eight years old was a dangerous age — old enough to keep records, young enough to say anything.

* * *

Later, when the soup pot was soaking and Wren was upstairs in the bath, conducting the elaborate negotiations with water temperature that preceded every bedtime, Ivo walked the shop.

This was the part of the day Ivo loved most, if love was the right word for it — the part where the store belonged to no one, where the spines threw long shadows under the lamp, where the whole place smelled of paper and rain and the ghost of coffee. Eleven years Ivo had kept this shop. Had signed the lease at twenty-six with money that was mostly inheritance and mostly a mistake, had painted the shelves themselves because finished furniture cost more than lumber, had learned the register, the wholesalers, the strange alchemy by which a bookstore survives in a town whose cannery has died. Had done it alone, except not alone, because the town had adopted the shop the way towns adopt things — gradually, then completely, until Fairweather Books was simply part of the grammar of the place, like the church and the diner and the cannery's ruined silhouette.

Eleven years of careful, small living.

Ivo stood in the doorway of the poetry section, where the alphabet ended at V — Vale, Juniper. *Field Notes from a Drowning Season.* One copy, spine uncracked, ordered by accident eleven years ago from a wholesaler's catalog and never shelved with the stock, never sold, never returned. It lived flat on the top shelf, face-down, where no one would find it unless they knew to look.

Ivo had never read it. That was the discipline. Eleven years of not reading a ninety-page book of poems, which is either madness or devotion, depending on the day.

The letter had come the week after Juniper left — a postcard, actually, from a press in another state, announcing the publication. No note. No return address that meant anything. Just the announcement, and then nothing, for eleven years, and Ivo had built a life around the shape of that nothing the way a tree builds around a fence wire.

There had been a man, once, three years in — Daniel, who taught at the middle school, kind and patient and bewildered by Ivo's talent for being present and absent in the same moment. There had been the fertility clinic, and the decision made alone and terrified and certain, and then Wren, eight pounds two ounces, screaming in a delivery room while rain hit the window, and Ivo had understood with total clarity that this — this child, this shop, this small exact life — was the whole of it now, and that was not a tragedy. That was a choice. Choices could be good.

And it *was* good. Ivo needed only to inventory it, some nights, like stock: a daughter who negotiated like a union lawyer and flew invisible in school plays; a neighbor who brought soup in dented pots and called Ivo *nwa m* — my child — in a voice that left no room for argument; a shop that smelled like paper and held the town's whole memory in its back room, where Ivo kept the archives nobody had asked them to keep: photographs of the cannery floor in 1962, the union minutes, Mrs. Okonkwo's recipe box copied out in her own hand. A careful life. A good one. Safe.

Safe, and not whole. The way a sentence can be grammatical and still be missing its verb.

Upstairs, the bathwater drained with a long gargling sigh. Small feet hit the floorboards. Ivo straightened the cat bookend one last time, touched the flat spine of the unread book — a habit, a superstition, a wound patted twice daily — and climbed the stairs to hear about chapter seven.

* * *

Wren fell asleep mid-sentence, somewhere between Mrs. Whatsit and a question about whether stars could be sorry for things, and Ivo sat on the edge of her bed for a while in the dark, listening to the rain work the roof.

Below, through the floor, the shop ticked and settled. Three doors down, the cannery stood in the rain like a held breath. And in the wall of its old workers' kitchen — though no one alive knew it yet, though the demolition notice had been posted and the crew scheduled and the permits stamped — something waited, sealed in tin, patient as a pressed flower, carrying a date from eleven years ago and a handwriting Ivo would know anywhere, asleep or awake, drunk or sober, alive or dead.

The rain kept on. Ivo checked the locks out of habit, kissed a sleeping forehead, and went to bed in a life that was safe, and small, and about to be neither.
