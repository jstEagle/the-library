## 2. The Wall Comes Down

The storm arrived the way trouble always did in Merrow's Point — sideways, off the water, without any intention of apologizing.

Ivo Fairweather heard it coming all afternoon, a low grumble out past the breakwater, and by four o'clock the rain was hitting the front windows of Fairweather Books so hard that the lettering — FAIRWEATHER BOOKS, hand-painted by his grandfather in 1961, the R's gone soft as old fruit — seemed to swim. He'd propped the door open anyway, because Wren liked to stand under the awning and catch rain on her tongue, and because there was nothing else doing on a Tuesday in October. The shop had seen three customers all day. One of them had bought a bookmark.

" Dad." Wren appeared from behind the poetry shelf, where she'd been building her usual after-school architecture: a fort of slim volumes, Neruda load-bearing against Dickinson, a roof of Mary Oliver. She was eight years old and had her mother's dark eyebrows, which meant she could make an argument look like a question and a question look like a verdict. "Can we have dinner on the counter again?"

"We had dinner on the counter last night."

"That was breakfast-for-dinner. This would be dinner-dinner. It's different."

"It's not different."

"It's *completely* different." She planted herself in front of him, rain still beaded in her hair, wearing the expression she'd inherited from no one he could name — some fierce little ancestor, maybe, someone who had once stood in a doorway in another country and refused to move until the world rearranged itself. "Also Mrs. Okonkwo said soup weather is sacred weather, and you can't argue with Mrs. Okonkwo."

This was true. Nobody argued with Adaeze Okonkwo, who had cooked for two hundred men a day at the cannery for nineteen years and who now, at seventy-eight, could silence a room full of city councilmen with one raised spoon. She lived two doors down in the cannery annex, in a third-floor apartment that smelled permanently of stockpot and cardamom, and she had appointed herself Wren's grandmother sometime around Wren's second year, without consulting anyone, least of all Ivo.

"Soup it is," Ivo said. "But we're eating at the table like people."

Wren considered this. "Counter," she countered.

"Table."

"Counter, and I get the good bowl."

Ivo looked at her — this small person he had made, this whole entire person, who had never once in eight years made him feel like half of anything — and felt the familiar ache arrive right on schedule, the one that came every evening around closing time, when the lamplight went gold on the spines and the rain got loud and the shop settled into its bones. He loved this life. That was the thing nobody understood about grief, the part he could never explain to anyone who hadn't lived inside it: you could love your life completely and still feel it missing a wall. A room you never went into. A door you kept meaning to paint over and never did.

"Counter," he conceded. "Good bowl. Go pick out tonight's reading."

She lit up and vanished into the stacks, and Ivo went through the closing ritual he'd performed eleven hundred times: register drawer counted, till reconciled, the brass bell above the door given a small polish with his sleeve because his grandfather had polished it and his father had polished it and because some things you did not let go soft. Outside, the streetlamps were coming on down Harbor Street one by one, halos in the rain, and past them — filling the whole eastern end of the block like a ship run aground and left there to rot — stood the cannery.

The Merrow's Point Packing Company had been dead for thirty years. Everyone knew the shape of it the way you know a scar on your own hand: the long brick face, the sawtooth roofline, the smokestack leaning like a man listening hard for something. The windows were boarded. The chain-link around it had been patched so many times it looked like quilting. Kids dared each other to touch the fence; gulls owned the roof. And now, finally, after decades of petitions and surveys and a developer named Corliss-Hale Partners who wanted to put up luxury condos with names like *The Landing* and *The Saltery*, the cannery was coming down. The demolition permits had posted in June. The fencing had gone up in August. The crews had started gutting the interior two weeks ago, and every morning Ivo opened the shop to the sound of it — the groan of steel, the cough of machinery, the occasional crack of brickwork letting go — and every morning he thought the same thing he'd thought since June:

*About time.* And then, quieter, in a voice he didn't recognize as his own: *Careful.*

Because buildings held things. He knew that better than most. His grandfather had found a wedding ring in a returned book in 1974 and kept it in the register drawer for forty years waiting for the owner to come back. His father had pulled love letters out of a box lot at an estate sale and spent a summer tracking down the descendants of a woman named Ruthie who had written to a soldier named Albie every week for three years and gotten back exactly none of them. Buildings held things. Walls especially. People wrote things down when they couldn't say them, and then they hid what they'd written where only the building would know.

"Dad! Found it!" Wren's voice rang off the rafters. She emerged holding a battered copy of *A Child's Garden of Verses* that had been in the shop since before Ivo bought it, before his father ran it, possibly since the flood of '52. "This one. It's my favorite. Also I'm starving."

"Then let's feed you before you start eating the inventory."

* * *

They ate at the counter, because the counter won, as the counter always did. Ivo ladled out Mrs. Okonkwo's soup — she'd sent over a thermos that morning with instructions written on masking tape: *EAT ALL OF IT, THE BOY IS TOO THIN* — and they sat side by side on the tall stools with their bowls balanced on a stack of poetry collections, because the counter was covered in special orders and the table was buried under a shipment of atlases, and because some traditions you surrendered to.

"Read the one about the wind," Wren said, mouth full.

Ivo read the one about the wind. Then the one about the shadow. Then, because she gave him the look, the one about going up in a swing, which he had read to her perhaps four hundred times and which still caught in his chest at the same line, the way it always did — *up in the air and down* — because that was childhood, wasn't it, the whole swinging arc of it, and here he was at the bottom of the arc watching his daughter rise.

Thunder rolled in off the harbor, close enough to rattle the window glass.

"The cannery's coming down," Wren said, apropos of nothing, watching the lightning flicker through the front windows. "Tommy Delgado says there are ghosts in it."

"Tommy Delgado also says he can breathe underwater."

"He *can*. He showed us a video."

"Videos lie." Ivo collected her empty bowl. "There aren't ghosts in the cannery. There are pigeons, and asbestos, and probably forty years of lost hats. That's all."

Wren looked at him for a long moment with those dark eyebrows doing their work. "You used to work there," she said. "In the kitchen. Before I was born. Mrs. Okonkwo told me."

"I shelved books there for one summer, when the historical society rented the kitchen wing. It was not dramatic."

"You carried flour."

"I carried flour."

"Mrs. Okonkwo says you were very strong then." A pause, surgical in its precision. "She says you were stronger then."

Ivo laughed despite himself, and ruffled her hair, and sent her upstairs to brush her teeth while he turned down the lamps. Stronger then. Eleven years ago, sure. Eleven years ago he had been a different animal entirely — twenty-six years old, working days at the shop and nights learning to cook in the apartment over the garage, saving for a trip he never took, in love with a girl who laughed like a struck match. Juniper Vale, who waited tables at the Anchor Line and read Whitman out loud in the dory on slow Sundays and talked about leaving Merrow's Point the way other people talked about weather — constantly, casually, with no real expectation anyone would do anything about it.

Juniper Vale, who had disappeared on a Tuesday night in October, eleven years ago this month, without a note, without a call, without so much as a returned letter. Who had left behind a duffel bag of clothes at the Anchor Line and a hole in Ivo Fairweather exactly the size of herself, which he had learned, over eleven years of careful practice, to walk around without looking at.

He hadn't told Wren about her. Of course he hadn't. What was there to tell? *Once, before you existed, I loved someone who chose to vanish, and I built my whole life in the space she left, and you are the best thing that space ever held.* No. Some rooms stayed shut. He'd painted over that door years ago.

Or believed he had.

The storm hit its stride around nine. Ivo stood at the front window after Wren was asleep, the shop dark behind him, and watched the rain come off the harbor in gray sheets. Across the street, the demolition site was a black mass with a single work light burning somewhere deep inside it, and the wind was making the scaffolding sing — a low, eerie note, like breath across a bottle. He should go to bed. He had a school run in the morning and a shipment to unpack and a leak in the storeroom ceiling that announced itself in weather like this.

Instead he watched the cannery, the way he'd been watching it all autumn without admitting why, and when the lightning came he saw it happen.

It happened fast. A flash — white, total, the whole street printed on his eyes like a photograph — and in that frozen instant the east face of the old kitchen wing seemed to shudder, and then the thunder arrived and swallowed everything, and when the darkness came back there was a new sound underneath it: a heavy, grinding slide, brick on brick, and then a crash that he felt through the floorboards more than heard.

The work light went out.

Ivo stood very still in his own dark shop, heart going hard, and listened. Rain. Wind. The scaffolding singing. And then — faint, human, muffled by water and distance — voices. Shouting. Flashlight beams swinging wild inside the fence line, cutting the rain into bright threads.

He grabbed his coat and the big flashlight from under the register and went out into it.

* * *

The foreman's name was Deshawn Pike, and he was sixty if he was a day, built like the pilings under the old wharf, and he had been pulling men out of the weather and toward the site office when Ivo came splashing up the street in his coat and unlaced boots.

"Nobody hurt!" Pike called before Ivo even asked, holding up both hands, rain streaming off his hard hat brim. "Before you ask — nobody hurt. Brick let go on the east face, took a chunk of parapet with it. Came down inside, thank God, not onto the street. We're done for the night anyway. Storm's got opinions."

"What fell?" Ivo swept his light across the fence. Through the chain-link he could see the east wing of the cannery, the old kitchen block, and where the parapet had been there was now a ragged gap, a wound in the silhouette he'd known his whole life. Rubble fanned out across the interior floor, catching the beams of the remaining flashlights.

"Parapet, some brick, part of an interior wall." Pike squinted at him through the rain. "You're the book fella. Fairweather."

"Ivo."

"Pike." They shook, gloved and brief. "Your granddad sold me a fishing almanac in eighty-nine. Charged me fair, which I respected."

"He'd have liked to hear it."

Pike looked back at the broken wall, and something in his face shifted — the professional mask slipping just enough to show the man underneath, and the man underneath looked unsettled. "Funny thing," he said. "That interior wall that came down. Crew's been gutting that wing two weeks. Kitchen block, old cookhouse. And that wall comes down tonight, of all nights, and behind it—"

He stopped. Rubbed the rain off his face with a forearm.

"Behind it there's a cavity," Ivo said quietly. He didn't know why he said it. He knew exactly why he said it. Buildings held things.

Pike studied him. "You want to see it?"

* * *

Inside, the cannery smelled the way abandoned places smell — cold iron, wet brick, pigeon droppings, and beneath it all something older and stranger, something almost sweet, like the ghost of brine and boiler steam. Their lights swung across the gutted floor of the cookhouse: stripped counters, a rusted range the size of a truck, tile gone to rot. And against the east wall, where the collapse had torn the plaster open, there was a gap in the brickwork — a deliberate gap, Ivo could see that even from ten feet away, a recessed niche maybe eighteen inches wide, hidden behind a skin of lath and plaster that generations of painters and inspectors had never questioned.

Sitting in the niche, patient as an egg, was a tin.

It was a biscuit tin once — Ivo could make out the faded lithograph of a sailing ship under the corrosion — and it had been sealed with care, wrapped in oilcloth, tied with a length of butcher's twine that had gone brittle as hair. Someone had wanted it to last. Someone had wanted it to be found, or had at least wanted it to survive, and had chosen this wall, this kitchen, this hiding place, and had walked away and let the building keep the secret.

"My guy Ruiz spotted it soon as the wall dropped," Pike said. "Wouldn't touch it. Superstitious as a cat, that one. Says you don't open other people's mail." He shrugged, rain dripping off him onto the ruined floor. "State law says finds like this go to the property owner, and Corliss-Hale's lawyer says everything in that building belongs to them, and honestly? I don't much care what a condo company does with a rusty tin. But the town charter says finds of historical interest get reported to the town archivist, and near as anybody can figure, that's you."

"I'm not the archivist. I just keep the shop."

"You keep the *records*, don't you? The ledgers, the photo collection, all that?" Pike waved a hand. "Close enough. Town's too broke to pay a real one. Congratulations."

Ivo barely heard him. He had crouched at the edge of the rubble, and his light was full on the tin now, and there was something about it — the careful wrapping, the twine tied in a flat seaman's knot, the way it sat centered in its niche like a thing placed rather than lost — that had reached into his chest and closed a hand around something in there.

Buildings held things. Walls especially. People wrote things down when they couldn't say them.

"Mr. Fairweather." One of the crew, a young woman in a slicker, had come up beside them, her light angled low. "There's writing on the bottom. You can just see it where the oilcloth's worn through."

Ivo leaned closer. Under the corrosion, scratched into the tin's base with something sharp — a nail, a key, a determined thumbnail — in a hand that slanted hard to the right, quick and confident, letters pressed deep enough to survive thirty years of damp:

**FOR THE KITCHEN.**

And below that, smaller, the ink long gone but the scratches legible, a date. October. A year.

Eleven years ago.

The sound of the rain changed. Or Ivo changed, inside it. The cookhouse seemed to tilt, the rusted range swimming, Pike's voice going distant and underwater — *you okay, fella?* — and Ivo Fairweather knelt in the ruins of a kitchen he had carried flour through when he was twenty-six and in love, staring at handwriting he would have known anywhere, anywhere on earth, asleep, drowning, at the bottom of the sea.

He knew that slant. He had watched it cross a hundred order pads at the Anchor Line. He had kissed the wrist that made it.

"Dad?"

He turned. Wren stood in the doorway in her raincoat over her pajamas, bare legs soaked, having followed him the six blocks from the shop the way she followed him everywhere, silent as a cat, stubborn as gravity. Her hair was plastered to her forehead and her eyes were enormous.

"You're supposed to be asleep," he said. His voice came out wrong.

"You forgot your keys." She held them up, jingling, defiant. Then she saw his face, and whatever she saw there made her come to him fast across the broken floor, small and fierce, and put her hand on his arm. "Dad. What is it? What's wrong?"

Ivo looked at the tin. At the scratch of that impossible handwriting. *For the kitchen.*

"Nothing's wrong," he heard himself say, which was the first lie he had ever told his daughter, and it tasted like rain.

Behind them, Pike was saying something about bringing it by the shop in the morning, about proper procedure, about getting out of this weather — and Ivo nodded at all of it, nodding and nodding, while the storm hammered the broken wall and the building that had held its secret for eleven years finally, finally let it go.
