## 14. Conflict of Interest

Dana Reyes had a way of assigning work that made it sound like a gift.

"Vale." She didn't look up from her screen when Juniper appeared in the doorway of her corner office, forty floors above the harbor, the cannery site reduced to a gray smudge in the model city spread across her desk. "Close the door."

Juniper closed it. The office went quiet in the particular way that meant money was about to change hands or lives were about to be rearranged, and often both.

"The annex," Dana said. "Four holdouts. Okonkwo, the Petrossians, the widow Marsh, and a kid named Alvarez who thinks he's going to out-lawyer us with a paralegal certificate he got off the internet." She finally looked up, and her expression was the one she used for closing arguments — warm at the edges, iron underneath. "I want you on them. Personally."

"Personally."

"You're from there." Dana said it like an asset on a balance sheet. *Homegrown talent understands local markets.* "You know these people. You know how they think. You can sit across a kitchen table and make them feel heard, and then they'll sign, because people sign when they feel heard. That's not manipulation, Juniper. That's communication."

The word sat wrong in Juniper's mouth. *Communication.* Eleven years of depositions and she still couldn't say the word *pressure* without someone dressing it in softer clothes first.

"What exactly am I communicating?" she asked.

Dana leaned back. Behind her, the window held the whole glittering fiction of the city — cranes, glass, the harbor shining like something that had never once been lied to. "That the offer on the table is generous, that eminent domain is coming whether they like it or not, and that the difference between a settlement and a condemnation award is usually eighteen months of litigation and thirty percent off the top for attorneys' fees. That's what you're communicating." A pause, surgical. "And you're the only one who can communicate it without it sounding like a threat."

*Because it will sound like a homecoming instead.*

"I can do it," Juniper said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did in conference rooms — flatten, professionalize, become a surface nothing could stick to. "I'll start with Okonkwo. She's the anchor tenant. If she moves, the others follow."

"That's my girl." Dana was already turning back to her screen, the audience over. Then, as if it were an afterthought, which it never was: "Juniper. These people are going to try to make you feel like the villain. Don't take the bait. You're not the villain. You're the mechanism. Villains have choices."

Juniper stood in the elevator afterward, watching the floors count down, and thought: *that's exactly the problem.*

* * *

She told herself she'd stop at the annex just to look at it.

It was a lie so transparent she didn't even bother believing it. The annex building crouched behind the cannery like an old dog chained to a dying master — three stories of brick gone soft at the corners, fire escapes rusted into lace, window boxes where somebody, against all odds and probably all regulations, was still growing geraniums. Fourth floor, left side. Adaeze Okonkwo's geraniums, blazing red against the gray October sky like a small act of civil disobedience.

Juniper parked two blocks away, which she told herself was discretion and knew was cowardice. She walked the distance slowly, rehearsing. *Mrs. Okonkwo, I'm Juniper Vale, I represent Meridian Development Group, and I wanted to talk to you face to face about your options—*

Your options. As if the woman had any. As if *options* weren't just the word lawyers used for doors they'd already locked from the outside.

The stairwell smelled like soup. That stopped her on the second-floor landing with her hand on the rail — chicken stock and thyme and scorched onion, the smell of every kitchen she'd ever loved, rising up through the building like the place itself was cooking for her. For eleven years she had eaten conference-room catering and airport sushi and the kind of restaurant food that arrived arranged like a landscape painting, and none of it had ever smelled like this, like someone's actual hands had made it for someone's actual hunger.

Adaeze Okonkwo opened the door before Juniper knocked.

She was smaller than Juniper remembered — everyone from that town was smaller now, the way places shrink when you leave them — but her eyes hadn't shrunk at all. They were the eyes of a woman who had fed two hundred workers a day for thirty years and could tell by looking at you whether you'd skipped lunch.

"You're the Vale girl," she said. Not a question. "Ivo's Vale girl, from before. Come in before the hallway eats you alive."

"I'm actually here on behalf of—"

"I know who you're here on behalf of. Your name is on the paper they slid under my door like a summons." Adaeze turned and walked into the apartment, leaving the door open behind her, which was either hospitality or a trap and Juniper genuinely could not tell which. "Shoes off. I just mopped."

Juniper took her shoes off. Her heels — four hundred dollars, chosen that morning as armor — looked absurd on the worn linoleum, and she set them by the door next to a pair of men's rubber boots and a child's sneaker, size small, long outgrown but kept anyway.

The apartment was narrow and immaculate and full of photographs. Photographs on every wall, every shelf, taped inside cabinet doors — decades of them, curling at the edges. Cannery picnics. Workers lined up on the loading dock, arms slung over shoulders. A younger Adaeze in a white apron standing at a stove the size of a car, wooden spoon raised like a scepter, laughing at something outside the frame.

"You cooked there," Juniper said. It came out before she could stop it. "In the cannery kitchen."

"Thirty-one years." Adaeze was already ladling soup into a bowl Juniper hadn't asked for. "You sit. You eat. Nobody negotiates with me on an empty stomach — that's rule one, and it applies to corporate lawyers double."

"Mrs. Okonkwo, I appreciate the offer, but I should be clear that I'm not here socially—"

"You're here because your boss sent you, and your boss sent you because you're from that town, and you're from that town because—" she set the bowl down in front of the chair she'd clearly decided was Juniper's, "—because kitchens raise us whether we like it or not. Sit down, Juniper Vale. The soup doesn't care whose name is on your paperwork."

Juniper sat. It was that or stand in the middle of the room looking like what she was, which was a woman holding a folder full of other people's evictions.

The soup was chicken and dumplings, and it was, without competition, the best thing she had eaten in eleven years. She didn't say so. She didn't have to; Adaeze watched her take the first spoonful and nodded once, like a judge accepting evidence.

"My mother worked the line," Juniper said quietly. "Before everything. Before the debts."

"I know. Ruthie Vale. Fastest labeler on the summer crew, and she'd give her own lunch to anybody who forgot theirs." Adaeze sat down across from her, folded her hands. "So. Say what you came to say, and then we'll see whether you've earned the rest of the bowl."

Juniper set the folder on the table. Her hands were steady. She'd trained them to be.

"The relocation package Meridian offered is final," she said. "But I want to walk you through what it actually provides, because the summary letter understates some protections you're entitled to. Moving costs, obviously. Six months' rent differential. And if you sign within thirty days, there's a goodwill supplement that won't be available after condemnation proceedings begin."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then the county initiates eminent domain, and the court determines compensation. In my experience, courts award less than negotiated settlements, and the process takes a year to eighteen months. During which you'd be living in a building the county has already condemned."

All true. Every word load-bearing and accurate. She'd built the sentence herself that morning, tested it for weak joints, and now she heard it in the room — this room, with its photographs and its geraniums and its soup — and it sounded like what it was: a very polite description of a wall falling on someone.

Adaeze listened to all of it without blinking. Then she reached across the table, took the folder, and did something Juniper had never seen a holdout tenant do in eleven years of practice.

She read it. All of it. Slowly, running one finger down each page, lips moving slightly, occasionally grunting. It took nine minutes. Juniper timed it against the clock on the stove and drank her soup and let the silence do whatever silence was going to do.

Finally Adaeze closed the folder and looked up.

"This clause here." She tapped page six. "Section nine. Says the six months' rent differential is void if I refuse 'comparable replacement housing' identified by the developer. Who decides comparable?"

"The firm's housing consultant."

"So they could offer me a box in some tower across the river, call it comparable, and take the money back." Adaeze pushed the folder across the table like something spoiled. "And this — section twelve. Arbitration. Binding. In Delaware." She almost smiled. "Delaware. I've never been to Delaware. My knees have certainly never been to Delaware."

Juniper said nothing. There was nothing to say. Section twelve was hers — she'd drafted it, three years ago, for a different client, boilerplate she'd carried forward like a stone in her pocket. *Efficient,* Dana had called it, when someone once questioned it. *Arbitration keeps costs down for everyone.*

"Here's what I know," Adaeze said. She rose, took Juniper's bowl, and refilled it without asking, which ended the negotiation more thoroughly than anything in the folder. "I know that building next door fed this town for sixty years. I know my husband laid brick on that annex with his own hands, and died in the bed in that room there, and I promised him I'd die in it too, and I don't intend to break a promise to a dead man because a Delaware clause says I should. And I know that you sat down at my table and ate my soup while carrying papers that would put me on the street." She set the bowl down gently. "So here's my question, and think hard, because I'll know if you lie. Do you believe what's in that folder is fair?"

The clock ticked. Downstairs, a radio played something old. The geraniums burned red in the window.

"No," Juniper said.

It came out small. She hadn't authorized it. Eleven years of training and the truth had simply walked past security.

Adaeze nodded, unsurprised, unsurprised in a way that was somehow worse than anger. "I didn't think so. You read section nine and your face did a thing. Cooks learn faces. Thirty years at a serving window, you learn which ones are hungry and which ones are hurting." She sat back down. "Now. Are you going to tell me why a smart girl like you is carrying a folder she doesn't believe in?"

And there it was — the question, the exact question, the one Juniper had spent eleven years and one entire career making sure no one would ever ask her, sitting across a kitchen table in a soup bowl's worth of steam.

Because her father had signed things he didn't read. Because she'd been nineteen and watching the house go and had sworn, on the train platform she never stood on, that she would learn every clause in the world so that no one could ever do to her family what the fine print had done to them. Because she'd gotten so good at the fine print that now she was the one writing it, and the girl in the photographs on the wall — laughing, aproned, generous — would have looked at the woman Juniper became and asked her the same question Adaeze was asking.

"I can't discuss internal matters," Juniper said. Privilege. The oldest wall she owned. "But I can tell you this much. Don't sign anything yet. Not until you've had independent counsel review it. Not the lawyer the firm offers to pay for — your own. There's a legal aid clinic in the county seat. Tell them I sent you."

"They'll ask me who sent me."

"Tell them the truth. Tell them the developer's own lawyer told you to call."

Adaeze studied her for a long moment. Then she laughed — a real laugh, low and rolling, the laugh of a woman who had fed two hundred people through every crisis a town could produce. "Oh, you are Ivo's girl. He does that too. Says the honest thing in the middle of the dishonest situation and then looks surprised when the room goes quiet."

"I'm not—" Juniper started.

"You were. Maybe you still are. That's between you and him and whatever's eating him these days — and don't tell me nothing's eating him, child, I live across the hall from his shop and I've watched that man carry grief around like a lunch pail for eleven years." She stood, collected the empty bowl, and waved off Juniper's protest before it formed. "You ate twice. That means you're coming back. House rules."

At the door, putting her ridiculous heels back on, Juniper paused. "Mrs. Okonkwo. If I come back, I won't be able to promise I'm bringing better news."

"Good," Adaeze said. "I don't trust news that arrives promising. Now go on. And Juniper—" she caught her at the threshold, one hand light on her arm, "—the girl who wrote those letters in that wall. The whole town figured it was you, eventually. We figured it the same week you left. Nobody thought less of you. We just wondered when you'd come back and finish the sentence."

* * *

The hotel bar was quiet and expensive and wrong, and Juniper sat in it for two hours without drinking, the folder unopened on the table in front of her like a dead thing she'd brought home.

Section nine. Section twelve. Delaware. She'd written those clauses or carried them forward or defended them in rooms full of people who called themselves efficient, and tonight they had a face — a small fierce woman with geraniums and a dead husband's bricks and a memory of her mother handing out lunches on the loading dock.

Her phone buzzed. Dana.

*Progress? Okonkwo softening?*

Juniper looked at the message for a long time. Below it, the cursor blinked, patient as a bill collector.

She typed: *She's reading everything. She's smarter than the file suggests. She won't move quickly.*

*They never do. That's why we sent you. Push the timeline — mention the demolition schedule moved up. Fear accelerates signatures.*

Juniper set the phone face-down. Across the bar, a man in a good suit was laughing too loudly at something a woman hadn't really said, and the whole room hummed with the sound of deals being closed, and none of it — none of it — smelled like soup.

She picked the phone back up, and instead of answering Dana, she opened the notes app, where she kept case memos, and started a new one. She titled it *Okonkwo — Annex*, and under it, where the strategy notes should go, she found herself typing:

*Section 9 is coercive. Section 12 is unconscionable. Both are mine.*

She stared at those last three words for a long moment. Then she locked the phone, gathered the folder, and headed for the elevator — and it wasn't until the doors closed that she admitted what she'd already known on the second-floor landing of the annex, breathing in chicken stock and thyme:

She was going back tomorrow. Not for Meridian.

For the soup. For the photographs. For the sentence Adaeze said she'd left unfinished.

And somewhere between the fourth floor and the lobby, another thought arrived, unwelcome and punctual as a subpoena: *Ivo Fairweather lives across the hall from that woman. Which means Ivo Fairweather is going to hear, by morning, exactly whose name is on the eviction notices.*

The elevator doors opened onto the marble lobby, and Juniper stepped out into it carrying the beginning of a choice she couldn't yet see the shape of — only its weight, gaining, like water filling a hull.
