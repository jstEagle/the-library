## 23. Privilege

The resignation letter was four sentences long when Juniper finally stopped typing.

She had started it at eleven, in the hotel room the firm still paid for, on hotel stationery because her laptop kept going to sleep and she wanted to feel the weight of a pen. She had typed it instead — muscle memory, eleven years of memos and motions — and now the cursor blinked at the end of a paragraph that said everything and committed to nothing.

*Dear Dana,* it began. *After careful consideration—*

Careful consideration. As if this were a matter of consideration at all. As if she hadn't known since Tuesday night, standing in Adaeze Okonkwo's kitchen with a bowl of egusi soup going cold between them, exactly what she was going to do. The letter was theater. She was drafting her own eulogy and pretending it was a rough draft.

*After careful consideration, I am resigning my position at Reyes & Associates, effective immediately. This decision is not made lightly.*

Not made lightly. She almost laughed. It had been made with a heaviness that sat in her chest like wet concrete, but lightly? Nothing about the last three weeks had been light. She deleted the sentence and stared at the wall.

Here was the problem, laid out as cleanly as any brief she'd ever written:

Exhibit A: The structural reports for the Cannery Row annex building, dated March and June of last year, both signed by Harlan Pike & Associates, both concluding that the building posed an imminent hazard to occupants. Exhibit B: The internal memo from Harlan Pike to Dana Reyes, forwarded to Juniper by accident — or not by accident; she'd never decided which — noting that the "hazard" findings were based on a preliminary visual assessment only, that the load-bearing walls showed no measurable deterioration, and that the report language should be understood as serving "the client's acquisition timeline." Exhibit C: The eminent domain petition filed with the county, citing those same reports as its factual foundation.

She had photographed all forty-one pages on Wednesday night, alone in the conference room after everyone else went home, her hands steady in a way that frightened her more than shaking would have. The flash of her phone against the paper, page after page, quiet as a confession.

And here was the rule, the one they taught you in your first semester of professional responsibility and again in bar prep and again, implicitly, every day of your working life: attorney-client privilege protects communications between lawyer and client. Work product belongs to the firm. Documents obtained in the course of representation are not hers to distribute, disclose, or deploy — not even, *especially* not even, when the client used them to lie to a county board and displace four elderly tenants from their homes.

If she handed those photographs to the tenants' side, she wasn't a whistleblower. Whistleblowers reported crimes through channels. She would be a lawyer breaching privilege to sabotage her own client, and the bar association would eat her alive, and Dana would make sure of it, and she would deserve it — that was the part that kept her up, the part she couldn't argue away at two in the morning. There were rules for a reason. The whole edifice stood on lawyers who kept confidences even when keeping them curdled their stomachs. Break the rule for a good cause and you'd broken it, full stop, and every bad actor who came after you would point at you and say *see, discretion is optional.*

But if she didn't—

If she didn't, Adaeze Okonkwo would be out of her apartment by spring. Mrs. Okonkwo, who had cooked for two hundred workers a day in that cannery kitchen for thirty years, who fed Ivo soup like feeding people was a form of prayer, who had looked at Juniper across her table and said, without accusation, only observation: *"You have the look of someone carrying something heavy and calling it a briefcase."*

Juniper set down the phone.

The letter glowed on the screen. Four sentences. She read them once more, then selected all the text and watched it turn blue, and her thumb hovered over delete, and hovered, and hovered.

She didn't send it. She didn't delete it either. She saved it to a folder called DRAFTS — old habit, the folder where indecision went to live — closed the laptop, and got dressed.

* * *

The cannery at midnight was a different animal than the cannery by day.

By day it was a construction site: orange fencing, hard hats, the foreman Ruiz shouting over generators, the smell of dust and diesel. By night, with the work lights off and the storm fence sagging where Wren Fairweather's classmates had cut through it months ago — kids always found the soft places first — it was just an old building holding its breath.

Juniper knew the gap in the fence. She'd walked the site a dozen times, officially, clipboard in hand, documenting conditions for the demolition schedule. Tonight she carried no clipboard. She carried a flashlight, a thermos of coffee she'd made herself for the first time in years — the hotel didn't have a kitchen worth the name — and the folder of photographs, printed, because paper felt more like evidence and less like a secret.

The workers' kitchen was on the second floor of the east wing, the section slated for phase two. Ruiz's crew hadn't touched it yet. The demolition schedule had moved up twice since the town meeting — Dana pushing, always pushing, the timeline compressing like a fist closing — and phase two was supposed to begin Monday. Which meant this room, the room where the letters had lived inside the wall for eleven years, the room Adaeze described like a cathedral — *we pinned everything to that wall, child, complaints and recipes and love letters, the wall remembered what we couldn't say out loud* — had maybe five days left.

Juniper climbed the stairs with her flashlight cupped low. The treads groaned. Somewhere below, water dripped in a rhythm too slow to be a leak and too regular to be anything else, and she let it keep time for her.

The kitchen door stood open on darkness.

Her beam swept across it: the long steel counters, rusted at the seams. The industrial stove with its burners gone gray. The walk-in with the door hanging crooked. And the wall — the south wall, the famous wall, the one the tin had come out of — scabbed with decades of paint layers, corkboard squares, pushpin holes like constellations. Someone had pinned things here for fifty years. Adaeze said the practice predated her. Cooks wrote what they couldn't say. Workers answered. The wall was the town's unconscious, and the development firm had scheduled its demolition for Monday morning.

Juniper set her coffee on a counter, clicked the flashlight to its widest beam, propped it against a mixing bowl so the light fanned upward across the wall, and sat down on an overturned milk crate like the world's loneliest juror.

She took out the photographs. Forty-one pages. She spread them along the counter in order, report pages alternating with memo pages, and read them again from the beginning, because reading them a third time might change what they said. They didn't change. Harlan Pike had measured nothing. The hazard was fiction. The eminent domain claim — the legal instrument that would take Adaeze's home, take the Vasquez family's home, take the Okafor brothers' shop — rested on numbers a consultant had invented to please a client.

*Efficient,* Dana had called the relocation packages, back when this was all abstract, back when Juniper could still say the word without tasting metal. Dana Reyes, who had taken a twenty-six-year-old associate with a dead father's debts and a chip on her shoulder the size of a courthouse and built her into this — into a woman sitting alone in a condemned kitchen at one in the morning, weighing her license against four old people's houses.

"You should see your face," Juniper said aloud, to nobody, and her voice cracked the silence badly enough that she flinched.

But the room seemed to want talking. Rooms like this did. Fifty years of pinned notes — the place where honest things got said, Ivo had called it, that night across the bookshop counter, flour on his hands, the late kitchen. She understood the name now. Kitchens after hours were where the truth came out, because there was no audience left to perform for.

So she talked. Quietly, to the wall, to the dark, to whatever was listening — the building, the ghosts, the version of herself who had stood at a train station eleven years ago and chosen shame.

"I found it," she told the wall. "I found what they did. And I can't tell anyone."

The drip below kept its time. The flashlight buzzed faintly.

"That's the joke, right? Eleven years ago I didn't tell you the truth because I was ashamed, and now I can't tell anyone the truth because of a rule." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Ivo thinks I delivered those notices because I wanted to. He gave the letters back unopened. Did he tell you that? He put them in my hands at the hotel like returning a defective purchase." Her throat tightened. "And I stood there and let him believe the worst, because the truth was *I've been building a case against my own client for a week and I can't say so out loud.* Privilege. You know what privilege means? It means the client's secrets are safer with me than my own are with anyone."

She picked up the top photograph — the March report, cover page, the firm's letterhead crisp and confident — and held it in the fan of light.

"There's a way," she said slowly, because saying it slowly made it real in stages, survivable in stages. "There's always a way. I resign first. I'm not their lawyer anymore. Privilege belongs to the client, not the attorney — fine, but once I'm out, once I've withdrawn, the documents I *personally* observed, the ones that show fraud on the court—" Her voice caught on *fraud.* She'd written the word in a hundred motions against other people. "Fraud vitiates everything. Even privilege, arguably, if the communication was in furtherance of the fraud. There's case law. There's always case law."

She knew she was lawyering at the dark, building the argument the way you build a fire in the rain — small, protected, feeding it one twig at a time. But the argument held. Barely. Narrowly. The kind of argument that wins at two a.m. and gets shredded at nine by opposing counsel with better facts and no conscience.

It didn't matter. That was the thing she kept circling and finally landed on, somewhere past two o'clock, the thermos empty and the cold coming up through the milk crate into her bones:

Even if the argument failed. Even if Dana burned her professionally, even if the bar opened a file, even if she ended up doing wills in some strip-mall office in a town that wasn't this one — the photographs existed. The truth existed. She could put it in front of the county clerk, in front of the judge, in front of the tenants' new lawyer, and let it do what truth does when you finally stop sitting on it.

The cost was calculable. She was very good at calculating costs. Eleven years of training.

What she'd never been good at was deciding what she was willing to pay.

* * *

Sometime after three, the rain started.

She heard it before she saw it — a hiss on the roof, far off, then closer, then the sound arriving all at once like a crowd. Water found the broken windows of the east wing and came through in threads, ticking on the steel counters, pooling in the low spots of a floor that had been level once. Juniper gathered the photographs quickly, tucked them inside her coat, and stood at the window watching the yard below turn silver and shine.

Across the yard, visible even in the rain, the old loading dock where the trucks used to idle. Beyond the fence, the dark shape of Cannery Row itself, and beyond that — she knew the geography without looking, had memorized it in three weeks the way you memorize the map of a place you're afraid to leave — Main Street, and the green awning of Fairweather Books, and above it the lit window of the apartment where Ivo was either awake or lying awake, and beside him the smaller bed where Wren slept with her ferocious little list of questions taped inside her closet door.

*Did you make my dad cry? Will you leave again?*

"No," Juniper said to the glass, to the rain, to the girl. "I mean — yes, the first one. God, yes. But not the second one. Not again. Never again."

The trouble was that staying and leaving had stopped being simple. Staying meant finishing what she'd started, which meant the choice, which meant burning down the only life she'd built since the one she'd abandoned. Leaving meant proving Wren's list right, and she would rather be disbarred.

She thought about the train station. She thought about it every night now; it had moved in like a tenant. Nineteen years old, one suitcase, her father's creditors' letters folded in her jacket pocket like knives. The 11:40 to the city. And Ivo — twenty-two, already talking about the bookshop like it was a person they were waiting to meet — standing on the platform in the rain, because she'd asked him to meet her there and then hadn't gone, hadn't been able to walk out of her family's collapsing house and toward the one good thing she'd ever been offered, because going would have meant admitting she deserved it.

She'd watched him wait from behind the ticket office. Forty minutes. She'd counted. He'd stood under the awning getting soaked, checking the clock, checking the street, and she'd stood in the dark hating herself with a purity of feeling she'd never managed since, and when the train came she hadn't boarded it, and when it left she hadn't crossed the platform, and she'd gone home and applied for the scholarship and told herself the story everyone tells themselves: *it was the only choice. Anyone would have done the same.*

Eleven years to learn that it hadn't been a choice between him and her family. It had been a choice between telling the truth and hiding, and she'd hidden, and hiding had a compound interest rate.

The rain thickened. Water sheeted down the window, warping the lights of Main Street into long gold smears, and one of those smears was the bookshop, and she thought: *he's there. Right now. Eleven years of Tuesdays and he's there tonight, probably reading, probably listening to the same rain, probably not thinking of me at all, and I don't deserve for him to be thinking of me, and I want it anyway.*

That was the hour. Three-something in the morning, in a condemned kitchen, rain hammering the roof of the building she was paid to destroy. The loneliest hour, when every defense files out of the room and leaves you alone with the plain arithmetic of yourself.

She took out her phone. No signal worth using, but she didn't need signal. She opened the drafts folder. The resignation letter waited, four polite sentences, patient as a loaded gun.

She didn't send it. Not yet. Not from here, not half-frozen, not in the dark where it might be fear dressed up as courage. Tomorrow. Tomorrow she would print it, sign it, walk it into Dana's office, and watch Dana's face do the thing faces do when the efficient plan stops being efficient. Tomorrow she would call the county clerk's office and ask, in her most boring procedural voice, about the filing requirements for supplemental evidence in pending proceedings. Tomorrow she would make copies — certified, timestamped, undeniable — and put them where they could not be disappeared.

Tomorrow she would stop being the kind of lawyer who kept this kind of secret.

But tonight — tonight she allowed herself one thing. She took out the returned letters, the bundle Ivo had pressed back into her hands, and untied the string, and read the first page of the first letter by flashlight, just the opening lines, the way you test bathwater.

*Ivo. Year one. The cannery's been closed eight months and I still drive past it every day like it owes me money. Today I saw smoke from the chimney and nearly drove into a ditch. It was just fog. I'm writing to the kitchen because the kitchen was where we were honest, and I don't have anywhere honest left—*

She stopped. Pressed the pages flat against her chest, over her heart, like stopping a wound.

"One more day," she whispered to the kitchen, to the wall, to the girl she'd been on the platform. "Then everything. I promise. Everything."

The rain answered, drumming the roof like applause, or like a countdown.

Down in the yard, headlights swung through the gate — Ruiz, early, or security, or the beginning of Monday arriving ahead of schedule. Juniper killed the flashlight and stood in the dark with forty-one photographs against her ribs and a letter unsent in her pocket, and listened to the building breathe around her, and made herself ready.

Monday was coming. So was she.
