## 34. Learning the Shelves

The first thing Juniper Vale learned about bookselling was that she was bad at it.

Not the way she'd been bad at things before — at piano, at small talk, at staying — but bad in a new and humbling register. Bad in front of witnesses. Bad in a room with a bell on the door that announced every failure.

"Excuse me," said a woman on her third morning, holding up a paperback with a cover of storm clouds and a man's silhouette. "Do you have the sequel to this? *The Salt Road*?"

Juniper had spent four days memorizing the inventory system, which was not an inventory system at all but a spiral notebook Ivo kept behind the register, organized by a logic he described as "how the shop thinks." She had read it twice. She had cross-referenced it against the shelves. She believed she was prepared for anything.

She checked the notebook. Fiction, S through T. She walked to the shelf, ran her finger along the spines, and found nothing.

"I'll check the back," she said, with the confidence of a woman who had once managed due diligence teams across three states.

The back was a closet containing a ladder, a box of bookmarks, and no sequels to anything. She returned empty-handed. The woman was still there, patient in the way of people who have lived in one town long enough to find patience cheaper than irritation.

"Oh, honey," the woman said. "It's on the staff picks wall. Dot read it in March and wouldn't shut up about it. Third row, under the blue card."

Juniper found it there — third row, under a handwritten card reading *DOT: trust me* — and rang it up wrong twice before the woman leaned over the counter and gently moved her thumb off the tax-exempt key.

"You're new," the woman said, not unkindly.

"I'm — yes. Temporarily."

"Mm." The woman took her bag of books and paused at the door. "I'm Harriet Pell. I taught fourth grade for thirty-one years. You'll get it. Nobody's born knowing where Dot's picks live."

The bell chimed her out. Juniper stood behind the counter holding the receipt she'd printed incorrectly and felt something she hadn't felt since law school orientation: the specific vertigo of being a beginner among people who already knew things.

It should have been intolerable. It was, instead, strangely clean.

* * *

The arrangement had been Ivo's idea, delivered in his careful way, three days after Adaeze's dinner. He'd waited until Wren was upstairs doing homework and then stood in the middle of the shop turning a pen over in his fingers like a man rehearsing testimony.

"The apartment's yours either way," he said. "Rent or no rent, that doesn't change. But the shop could use help. Tuesdays are brutal, and I can't do story hour and the register at the same time, and—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't want you sitting up there all day reading case files that don't exist anymore. That's not rest. That's just waiting."

"And shelving books is rest?"

"It's the opposite of waiting. Books don't care what you were. They just need to be somewhere findable."

She had laughed — actually laughed, surprising them both — and said yes before the fear could catch up.

Now, two weeks in, the fear had caught up several times, usually around ten a.m., when the morning regulars arrived and looked at her with frank, unhurried curiosity. They didn't ask about the scandal. That was the thing she kept bracing for and never got: nobody wanted the deposition version of her. What they wanted was smaller and harder. They wanted to know if she'd read the book Dot recommended. They wanted to know whether she thought the new poetry section should stay by the window or move away from the damp. They wanted her opinion on glue bindings versus sewn, and they did not accept *I really couldn't say.*

"You can say it once," Ivo told her that night, as they closed up — she'd learned the ritual now, the tilting of chairs, the counting of the till, the way he touched the doorframe on his way past like a superstition he'd stopped being embarrassed about. "After that, they'll know you're dodging, and Dodging is worse than Wrong."

"What's wrong with Wrong?"

"Wrong starts conversations. Dodging ends them." He handed her the duster. "Harriet Pell corrected you today."

"How do you know that?"

"She bought a book from your wall. Dot's wall." He smiled at the register. "That means she forgave you before she left."

* * *

Thursday afternoons were story hour, which Ivo had warned her about and which she had nonetheless failed to imagine accurately. Eight children on a rug worn thin in the shape of their sitting. Ivo reading aloud with a voice Juniper had only heard in fragments — low, unhurried, willing to be silly at the exact right moments. And Wren, who attended officially as a helper and unofficially as a warden, stationed at the edge of the circle with the gravity of a museum guard.

Juniper's job was to reshelve during story hour, which meant moving quietly through the stacks while Ivo's voice carried over her shoulder — a fox, a lighthouse, a girl who collected lost buttons. She was alphabetizing the middle-grade section when a boy of about six detached himself from the rug, wandered over, and stared up at her with the directness only small children and juries possess.

"Are you Wren's Juniper?"

She crouched down to his level. "I think so. Yes."

"Wren says you used to be a lawyer but now you're a shelf person."

"That's roughly accurate."

He considered this. "Shelf people are better. Lawyers just talk. Shelf people put things back where they go." He returned to the rug before she could formulate a response, leaving her kneeling between the B's and the C's with her chest unexpectedly tight.

*Put things back where they go.* She stayed there a moment longer than the task required.

* * *

Her education continued. Mrs. Okonkwo came in Fridays for the newspaper and stayed an hour, correcting Juniper's shelving with the serene authority of a woman who had fed this town for forty years. ("Cookbooks face out, dear. A cookbook spine is a promise. Novels can hide; recipes shouldn't.") The foreman from the demolition crew — Sal, whose name Juniper finally learned in week two — came in for westerns and called her "counselor" until she asked him to stop, then "June," which she liked better than she expected to. An old man named Ferris tested her weekly by requesting obscure maritime histories, and when she failed, he would sigh, retrieve the book himself from a location that defied all classification systems, and say, "Well. You're learning the shop's opinions. That takes time. The shop has strong opinions."

She began to understand what he meant. Fairweather Books was not organized; it was *inhabited*. The poetry lived near the window because Ivo believed poems needed weather. The local history section had its own chair beside it because people always started reading standing up and never finished that way. There was a shelf behind the register of books Ivo refused to sell — damaged first editions, a waterlogged copy of something with a 1970s price sticker — and when Juniper asked why keep them, he said, "Because someone loved them badly, and I respect that."

She wrote that down. Not in the inventory notebook. In the small green one she'd started keeping in her apron pocket, where she recorded the shop's opinions like case law.

* * *

The evening it changed — though change, she would later understand, had been happening for weeks without her permission — began with rain and a customer named Beth Ann Corliss, whom Juniper knew slightly from the town meetings: one of the annex tenants, mid-fifties, hands rough from years at the cannery before it closed.

Beth Ann stood at the counter for a long moment, working up to something, and Juniper recognized the posture instantly. She'd seen it across conference tables for eleven years. Someone about to say the hard thing.

"I want to thank you," Beth Ann said finally. "For what you did. Everybody says it different ways — some folks won't say it at all — but I wanted to say it plain. You gave up everything, and we got our homes back. My granddaughter sleeps in the same room she slept in before all this. So. Thank you."

Juniper's throat did something complicated. "You're welcome," she managed. "Though honestly, the town did most of—"

"Don't you dare deflect at me, counselor." But Beth Ann was smiling. Then her expression shifted, softened into something more careful. "Can I ask you something? Off the clock?"

"Off the clock."

"Do you regret it? The firm, the partnership. People say you must. Dana Reyes's name comes up in the county paper like a villain in a serial, and folks assume you're up there in that apartment eating your heart out."

Juniper looked at her hands on the counter. She thought about lying, out of habit, and found she'd somehow lost the knack.

"No," she said. "I keep waiting to regret it. I check, most mornings. Like checking a wound." She looked up. "It's not there. I miss pieces of my old life — the certainty, mostly. But I don't miss the woman I had to be to hold onto it. Does that make sense?"

Beth Ann studied her for a long moment. Then she reached across the counter and squeezed Juniper's wrist, brief and firm, the way you'd test a rail fence.

"It makes sense," she said. "You'll do fine here." At the door she turned back. "Oh — and tell Ivo the mystery club wants the back table Thursdays now, not Wednesdays. He'll pretend it's fine. Make him actually move the table."

The bell chimed. Juniper stood alone in the warm lamplight of the shop, rain ticking against the glass, and realized that her eyes were wet and that she had been, for some minutes now, simply and completely happy — a condition so foreign she'd nearly failed to diagnose it.

* * *

Closing time. Ivo counted the till; she tilted the last chairs. Wren was upstairs, ostensibly asleep, audibly reading by flashlight. Rain had settled in for the night, soft and steady, and the shop smelled of paper and the coffee Ivo made too strong.

"Harriet says I'm improving," Juniper offered, hanging her apron on the hook — *her* hook, a distinction that had happened sometime last week without ceremony.

"Harriet says everyone's improving. It's the teacher in her." He glanced up. "But you are. You found the maritime history yesterday. Ferris didn't even have to rescue you."

"Ferris watched me the entire time. I could feel him."

"That's how he teaches." Ivo set the till drawer shut and leaned against the counter, and there was a look on his face she was learning to read — the one that meant he was about to say something true and was giving himself a running start. "Can I tell you something?"

"You usually do."

"The first week you worked here, Mrs. Okonkwo came in and asked me — very casually, which is how she asks things — whether I knew that you alphabetized the whole biography section overnight because it was bothering you." He smiled. "And I said yes. And she said, 'That girl is going to be fine. People who fix things that bother them are people who plan to stay.'"

Juniper went still. Outside, the rain moved through the guttering like a slow page turning.

"I didn't correct her," Ivo said quietly. "About the 'staying' part. I hope that's — I hope that's all right."

She crossed the small distance between them. Eleven years ago she had measured her life in billable hours and exit strategies; now she measured it in hooks and aprons and a green notebook full of a shop's opinions, and the arithmetic had never once come out in favor of the old ledger.

"It's more than all right," she said.

They stood there in the closing-time quiet, foreheads almost touching, the lamp humming, the rain keeping its own hours. Upstairs, the flashlight clicked off. Somewhere below the floorboards, beneath the old wood, the town held its breath the way it always did at day's end — not anxious, just attentive. Waiting to see what its people would do next.

Which was when the phone rang.

Ivo frowned. The shop line, at nine at night, in the rain. He picked it up, listened, and Juniper watched his face change — the ease draining out of it, replaced by something older and warier.

"Yes," he said slowly. "This is he." A pause. His eyes came up and found hers across the counter. "When did she arrive?"
