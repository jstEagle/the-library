## 10. Dessert

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from being charming for six hours straight, and I want it noted that I discovered this while pretending, which means the real thing must be lethal.

By the time dessert was served at Hollow Pines — on the long veranda, under strings of Edison bulbs that Eleanor had clearly ordered installed by someone with a ladder and no fear of heights — I had survived: three cousins asking when we met (answer: he came in for a book of Neruda, and yes, I know how that sounds), one uncle asking what I intended to do about the shop's "numbers" (answer: a smile with all the warmth of a bank vault door), and Aunt Carol asking Eli directly whether he was "serious" about me, which is the Whitlock version of a pat-down at airport security.

He'd said, without missing a beat, "I'm serious about whatever she'll let me be serious about," and Aunt Carol had actually blushed, at sixty-eight years old, into her chardonnay.

I could have killed him. I could have kissed him. At that point in the evening those felt like roughly the same gesture.

* * *

Dessert was lemon posset, because Eleanor believes a menu should be decided by one woman in 1987 and never revisited. The tables had been dragged out onto the veranda so everyone could watch the sun go down over the orchard like it was a performance they'd personally funded. Junie had fallen asleep an hour earlier on the window seat in the library, one shoe off, clutching a paperback she'd smuggled from home, and I had covered her with somebody's discarded cardigan and felt, briefly, like a mother who had things under control.

The string lights buzzed faintly overhead. Moths threw themselves against the bulbs with small, hopeful thuds. Somewhere down the lawn, someone's kid was shrieking about fireflies, and the sound carried up through the dark like a reminder that not everything at Hollow Pines was varnished.

Eli sat beside me, his chair angled toward mine, his knee a polite two inches from my knee. He had been performing all evening — the easy laugh, the attentive lean, the way he remembered that Cousin Dana's daughter was applying to Vassar and asked after it unprompted, which made Dana love him instantly and me feel vaguely conned. He was good at this. That was the problem. I kept catching myself forgetting it was a performance.

"You're doing that thing," he murmured, low enough that only I could hear, while across the table Uncle Richard held forth about municipal zoning to a table of people who would rather have discussed anything else.

"What thing."

"The thing where you catalog the exits." His eyes stayed forward, his smile fixed pleasantly on Richard's zoning monologue. "Door's behind you. Window seat's got Junie. There's a trellis on the east side that looks load-bearing."

"I don't catalog exits."

"You've checked the trellis twice."

I picked up my spoon and addressed the posset with more violence than it deserved. "It's a nice trellis."

"It's a great trellis." He turned his head then, finally, and looked at me, and the string light caught the tired creases around his eyes — the ones I'd noticed the first day, in the shop, before any of this. Underneath the polished evening version of him there was still that same man who'd bought poetry he didn't seem to want and lingered like he did. "You're doing fine," he said. "Better than fine. They like you."

"They're supposed to like *you*. You're the garnish."

"August." He said my name quietly, and something in the way he said it — not the reunion voice, not the performance voice, some third voice I hadn't heard before — made me stop stirring. "They've always liked you. They just like you better when you're happy, and they don't know how to ask for it. So they audit instead. It's a family disease."

I stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Lucky guess." He smiled, and it didn't quite reach all the way up, and I let it go, because the band was tuning up on the far end of the veranda and Eleanor was rising from her chair with the expression of a woman about to conduct a liturgy.

* * *

My late husband's mother does not give speeches. She gives *addresses*. She stood at the head of the veranda with her glass of water — Eleanor hasn't drunk at these events since 2009, a fact she mentions annually — and thanked everyone for coming, and spoke about Daniel.

She does this every year. Four minutes, timed internally, about the boy she lost. And every year I stand there holding my glass and performing the face: the soft grief, the grateful nod, the widow-shaped composure that I sewed onto myself four years ago and have worn so long I sometimes forget it's a costume. Daniel died and the whole town gave me a role, and I learned my lines so well that even I stopped hearing them as lines.

But tonight, standing next to Eli, feeling the warmth of him radiating off his sleeve like a small dishonest sun, I heard it. Every sentence. *Daniel would have wanted the family together. Daniel believed in this place. Daniel understood that legacy is a responsibility.* All true, probably. None of it the whole truth. The whole truth was sitting in a locked tin in my stockroom four hours away, and I hadn't opened it, and I wasn't going to, and the distance between those two facts suddenly felt like the width of the entire state.

Eleanor finished. Applause, soft and reverent. Then she said, in the tone of a woman unveiling a fountain: "Now. Some music, I think. Richard, you promised."

And Uncle Richard — who apparently had been taking guitar lessons in secret for two years, a fact the entire family had conspired to protect from him — shuffled to the band setup, and began to play.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fake dating: it has choreography. We'd rehearsed the hand-holding. We'd agreed on the story. What we had not agreed on was dancing, because dancing had not come up in the terms and conditions, and when the first slow song started and Cousin Dana grabbed her husband and Aunt Carol grabbed whoever was nearest, and Eli turned to me with his hand already half-extended and an eyebrow raised in silent question — *terms and conditions?* — I should have said no.

I want that on the record. I am a woman who reads contracts. I once returned a toaster over a warranty dispute. I should have said no.

I put my hand in his.

The veranda floorboards were old and warm from the day, and the string lights turned everything the color of weak tea and honey, and Eli pulled me in with a hand at my waist that was careful in a way that undid me more than greediness would have. He was, annoyingly, a good dancer. Not showy. Just steady. He moved like a man who had decided, somewhere in the last hour, to stop performing.

"You're staring at my collarbone," he said.

"I'm establishing eye contact with a neutral zone."

"My collarbone thanks you for your professionalism."

The song was something old and slow, the kind of song that doesn't ask much of you, just that you sway and let it. Around us the Whitlocks danced in their pairs — Dana and her husband bickering fondly, Aunt Carol leading her partner with visible authority, Eleanor standing at the edge with her hands folded, watching us all with an expression I couldn't read and had spent twenty years trying to.

"You okay?" Eli asked. Quietly. Really asking.

"I'm fine."

"You've got the face on."

"What face?"

"The one from the speech. The museum face. Like you're a portrait of yourself hung slightly too high."

I laughed — a real laugh, startled out of me, too loud for the song — and several cousins glanced over, and I felt the strange double exposure of it: the woman they were seeing, swaying under the lights with a handsome stranger, glowing; and the woman I actually was, whose chest had gone tight and strange, whose heart was doing something it had explicitly been trained not to do.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I explained to Junie over melted ice cream on a slow Tuesday, the theory I built my whole second life on: the heart is a muscle. Muscles can be trained. You train it to hold still, to stay contained, to lift only what you can carry and set it down again. Grief is heavy, so you do reps. Loneliness is heavy, so you do reps. You get very strong at carrying it alone, and people admire you for it, and you call the strength healing, and some days you almost believe that.

But muscles have another property, one I may have failed to mention to my seven-year-old during the ice cream lecture. They remember. You can stop training for four years and the body keeps the shape of what you practiced — and the moment you pick the weight back up, it all comes back at once, every unlifted pound of it.

Eli's hand shifted at my waist. Half an inch. From *polite* to *present*. And the song went on, and the moths hit the bulbs, and Junie slept inside with her book, and I stood in the circle of a stranger's arms and felt my heart do the one thing it had never been trained to do, which was to reach for something.

Not him, exactly. Or — yes, him, but also everything he was standing in front of. Being looked at. Being asked how I was by someone who seemed to want the answer. Dancing badly at a family function with a man who noticed my exits and called them by name. The whole shape of a life I'd folded up flat four years ago and stored in the back of my chest like winter clothes.

"August." His voice, near my ear now, low. "Your heart's going fast."

"That's the exertion. Slow dancing is famously grueling."

"Right." A pause. His thumb moved, barely, against the side of my waist — a motion so small it could be denied in any court in the land. "Mine too," he said. "For the record. In case you were wondering whose fault the exertion was."

And that — that stupid, gentle, honest little confession — was what broke it.

Because it wasn't in the script. Because we had terms, we had conditions, we had a handshake agreement forged over coffee with the solemnity of arms control negotiators, and rule three had been *no falling*, and he'd said it jokingly and I'd said it jokingly and neither of us had laughed when we said it, and now here we were, swaying under borrowed light, both hearts hammering like teenagers, and the lie had gotten so comfortable that the truth was starting to move in without paying rent.

I stepped back.

Mid-song. Mid-sway. Mid-everything. I stepped back and his hands fell away and the space between us filled immediately with veranda air and string-light hum and the sound of Uncle Richard's guitar wobbling through a chord change, and I stood there with my heart slamming against its training like a moth against a bulb, saying *no, no, you'll hurt yourself, you always hurt yourself.*

"Bathroom," I said. Brilliant. Devastating. Four years of widowhood and grief-work and rebuilding a life from splinters, and my contribution to the moment was *bathroom.*

"Okay," Eli said. He didn't push. He didn't do the wounded look or the smooth recovery line. He just nodded, once, and stepped aside to let me pass, and somehow that was worse — the steadiness of it, the way he made room, like he'd been making room all night and I'd only just noticed.

I walked the length of the veranda past every cousin, every knowing glance, every glass of wine, with my spine straight and my museum face bolted on, and I did not look back at him, because I was afraid of what my face would do if I saw his.

* * *

In the bathroom — Eleanor's guest bathroom, all clawfoot and lavender and towels nobody is allowed to touch — I gripped the sink and looked at myself in the mirror and conducted a brief internal tribunal.

Exhibit A: it was a dance. People dance at reunions. It meant nothing.

Exhibit B: I had left mid-song like a woman fleeing a fire, and my hands were shaking, and nothing means nothing if it makes your hands shake.

Exhibit C, entered quietly, from the back of the room: I had liked it. Not the dance. The being known. Two days of knowing him and he'd already learned where I keep my exits, and I had spent four years in a town full of people who loved me and had never once been seen, and I hadn't realized how starved I was until someone set down a plate.

The marriage — I thought, and then made myself think it, all the way through, which I almost never allowed — the marriage had gone quiet years before the accident. Not cruel. Just quiet. Two people in a house, each waiting for the other to speak first, each with a whole speech prepared and never delivered. I used to tell myself silence was peace. I told everyone that. I told the trust lawyers that. I told Junie, in gentler words, that Daddy and Mommy loved each other very much.

And now a stranger had held me under string lights for the length of one song and my traitor body had responded like the famine was over.

I pressed my palms flat against the cool porcelain and breathed until the shaking stopped, mostly.

When I came out, the song had ended and the party had migrated toward the dessert table for seconds, and Eli was standing at the veranda rail looking out at the dark orchard, alone, a glass of untouched wine in his hand. He wasn't performing. Nobody was watching him. His shoulders had come down from wherever he kept them all evening, and in the low light he looked — tired. Not reunion-tired. Something older than that. Like a man carrying a file folder full of something heavy, though God knows where I got that image, and why it arrived just then, uninvited, wearing the exact shape of dread.

He sensed me and turned, and the polish came back up like a shopkeeper flipping the sign to OPEN, quick and practiced, and I watched him do it and filed the motion away in the drawer where I keep things I'm not ready to examine.

"Hey," he said. "You okay?"

"Peachy. The bathroom has excellent acoustics for panic attacks, for future reference."

"Noted." He hesitated, then offered his arm — elbow only, strictly platonic, fully within terms. "Walk you back? I believe Aunt Carol is about to lead a conga line and I'd like witnesses when I refuse."

I took his arm. Of course I took his arm. My heart was still rattling around in its cage like a thrown stone, and I took his arm anyway, because the alternative was standing alone at a rail in the dark with a feeling I couldn't afford, and I have always been practical.

We walked slowly along the veranda's edge, past the string lights, past the moths throwing themselves hopefully at all that bright, unreachable heat.

"For the record," Eli said, eyes forward, voice even, "we can recalibrate. If the dancing was too much. We can go back to hand-holding. Elbow-holding. Aggressive nodding from across rooms."

"Eli."

"I'm just saying the terms are negotiable—"

"Eli." I stopped walking. He stopped too. Down the lawn a child screamed joyfully about fireflies again, and the string lights buzzed, and I looked up at this man I'd hired for two hundred dollars and dinner and said the truest thing I'd said all weekend: "It wasn't too much. That's the problem."

Something moved through his face then — fast, there and gone, like a fish under dark water. Not triumph. Not relief. Something closer to guilt, though I couldn't imagine what he had to feel guilty about, and I was too raw and too tired to chase it.

So instead I squeezed his arm, and we went back inside, and I laughed at Aunt Carol's conga line, and I said all the right things to all the right cousins, and I performed the happiest version of myself anyone at Hollow Pines had ever seen.

And all the while, in the back of my chest, the muscle I'd spent four years training sat up on its haunches and began, patiently, methodically, to test the walls.
