## 21. Real, Unannounced

**August**

The rain started at four o'clock, which was the kind of timing that felt personal, like the sky had been eavesdropping on my week and decided to provide atmosphere.

I'd spent the afternoon pretending to do inventory. What I'd actually done was stand in the poetry section holding the same Neruda collection for forty minutes, rereading the inscription Eli had written on the flypaper of my memory — no, that's not right, there was no inscription, I was rereading the *memory* of him standing exactly where I was standing, three weeks ago, buying a book he clearly wasn't going to read so much as use as an excuse to linger. That was the whole problem with him. He turned ordinary objects into evidence.

"Mom." Junie appeared at the register, homework folder clamped under her arm like a briefcase. "Your face is doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thinking-about-Eli thing. It looks like heartburn."

"I'm doing inventory."

"You've been on the same page since I got home from school. I checked." She dropped her backpack behind the counter with the authority of someone who owned the place, which, in fairness, she sort of did — she'd been raised under these shelves the way other kids were raised under kitchen tables. "Dana's mom is here to pick me up for the sleepover. Dana's mom says hi. Dana's mom also says you should just kiss him already because it's painful to watch, and she doesn't even know him, she's just extrapolating."

"Dana's mom needs to extrapolate elsewhere."

"She brought brownies as a bribe. They're on the counter."

I looked. They were on the counter. Traitorously good-looking brownies.

Junie hugged me around the waist, quick and fierce, the way ten-year-olds hug when they're embarrassed to be hugging but can't not. "Love you. Don't do anything weird while I'm gone."

"No promises," I said, and meant it more than I intended.

The door chimed shut behind them, and then the shop did what the shop always does when it empties out: it got honest. The quiet settled into the corners. Rain ticked against the big front window, soft and insistent, like fingers testing whether anyone was home.

I looked at the clock. Six-forty. Eli was coming at seven.

That was the arrangement now — or one of the arrangements, because we'd accumulated arrangements the way a ship accumulates barnacles, each one attached to something that used to float freely. Tuesday closings, because two sets of hands made inventory faster. Thursday dinners, because the cover story required maintenance, and town gossip required feeding. A standing joke about hand-holding rates. A growing list of things we didn't say.

The bell over the door hadn't rung yet, but I already knew how he'd arrive: slightly early, slightly damp, apologizing for both. He was a man who apologized for weather he didn't cause. Four years of widowhood had taught me every possible way a person could be absent; Eli Marchetti was teaching me, against my will and without his knowledge, all the ways a person could be present.

And that was the trouble. That was precisely, catastrophically the trouble.

Because here is what I knew, standing in my dead husband's shop in the rain-light, holding a book of love poems like a rosary: the terms and conditions had been drafted over coffee six weeks ago, and rule number three had been *no falling*, and I had watched myself break it in slow motion, daily, the way you watch a glass tip off a table — with full knowledge, with time to intervene, and with some stubborn part of you refusing to move your hand.

I set down the Neruda. I flipped the sign to Closed. And I decided — insofar as any decision made by a woman whose hands were shaking counts as a decision — that tonight I would stop pretending. Not to him. God, no. To myself. One person in this town had to stop lying, and Junie was too young, and Eleanor Whitlock would rather die.

* * *

**Eli**

There are three kinds of rain. There's rain that means nothing, rain that ruins your shoes, and rain that arrives with the specific timing of a stage crew that has read your situation and decided to help.

This was the third kind. I stood across the street from Whitlock's Books under the awning of the hardware store, watching the shop glow amber through its big window, and I gave myself the same lecture I'd been giving myself for eleven days, since the client call, since Marcus said *finalize it, Eli, the recommendation's a formality and you know it.*

Here's the thing about lies: they don't stay where you put them. You tell one small lie — I'm her boyfriend, it's temporary, it's transactional — and you think you've filed it neatly, but lies are water. They seep. They find the cracks in everything adjacent. And somewhere between the reunion weekend and the lake date and the night we closed the shop together and almost — *almost*, that word doing Olympic-level work — kissed, the lie had stopped being something I told August and become something I told myself.

The lie was: this is contained.

The truth was: I had memorized the way she pushed her hair back when she was concentrating. The truth was: I'd caught myself, twice this week, drafting sentences of my report and hearing her voice correct my phrasing. The truth was: I was a man hired to assess whether her life was worth keeping, and I had fallen in love with the evidence.

I crossed the street. My umbrella stayed closed. Some part of me understood, walking through that rain, that I wanted to arrive looking like a man who'd been through something.

She opened the door before I knocked. She'd been watching for me. We both pretended otherwise — she stepped back, I shook off water, the choreography of plausible deniability performed flawlessly by two people who were running out of stage.

"You're soaked," she said.

"It's raining."

"I have eyes, Eli. I mean why are you soaked? You own an umbrella. I've seen it. It has a wooden handle. It's the most respectable umbrella I've ever met."

"I left it somewhere," I said, which was true, if the somewhere was my own better judgment.

She handed me a towel from behind the counter — she kept towels behind the counter, along with band-aids and a sewing kit and a tin of shortbread, the field kit of a woman who ran a business and a child simultaneously — and I dried my hair while she locked the door and turned the sign and killed half the lights, leaving the shop in that low gold light that made every bookshelf look like it was keeping a secret.

"Inventory?" I asked.

"Inventory," she said. But she was looking at me in a way that had nothing to do with stock counts, and I recognized the look, because I'd been wearing it in mirrors all week.

* * *

**August**

We worked. That was the maddening thing — we worked well, the way we always did, passing boxes and reading spines and arguing about whether the store needed a poetry endcap (he said yes, I said yes, we argued anyway, because arguing was our love language and neither of us had filed the paperwork to admit it).

He reached a box on the top shelf and I reached it at the same time and our hands landed together on the cardboard and stayed there one second too long, and the shop went so quiet I could hear the rain spelling something against the windows.

"We're terrible at this," I said.

"At inventory?"

"At pretending this is inventory."

He didn't move his hand. Neither did I. His knuckles were warm from the work and there was a paper cut on his thumb from the packing tape and I thought, absurdly, *I want to know how that heals.* I wanted the whole boring sequel of him. The scab and the scar and the Sunday mornings. That's how I knew.

"August." His voice had dropped into the register he used when he was being careful with words, handling them like they might be load-bearing. "Can I say something that isn't covered by the terms and conditions?"

My heart did the thing I'd trained it never to do, which was anything at all. "The terms and conditions are null and void," I heard myself say. "As of approximately three weeks ago. Possibly longer. I've been falsifying the records."

Something moved through his face — relief and fear arriving at the same intersection. "Yeah?"

"Yeah. Rule three is broken. Rule three has been broken for a while. I've just been —" I laughed, and it came out unsteady. "I've been very committed to the bit."

"The bit," he repeated softly.

"The fake dating. The charade. Whatever we're calling the structure that lets me hold your hand in public and call it performance." I finally pulled my hand back, because I couldn't say the next part while touching him; I needed both hands free to fall apart properly. "Eli, I need to tell you something and I need you to let me finish, because I'm only brave once per sentence."

He waited. He was good at waiting. It was one of the first things I'd noticed about him, back when he was a stranger buying poetry — that he listened like the silence after mattered.

"I was married to a man who was kind and distant and dying for four years before he actually died, and I built a whole philosophy about it. The muscle theory. You keep the heart trained, contained, small enough to fit through the days. And it worked. It worked for four years and it worked for four years after, and then you walked in here and bought a Neruda you clearly had no intention of reading, and the whole theory collapsed, and I have been furious about it ever since." My voice cracked on the last word and I let it. "So. There. Confessed. In my own shop, in the rain, like a scene from a book I'd mock."

The rain filled the pause. He crossed the space between us slowly, giving me every chance to retreat, and I didn't retreat, and when he spoke his voice was rough.

"For the record," he said, "I read the Neruda. Twice. I bought it as an excuse and then I read it because of you, and there's a poem in there I can't get through anymore without thinking about your hands." He exhaled. "And I need to tell you something too, and mine's worse, and I need —"

The phone rang.

The landline, ancient and black, bolted to the wall behind the register like a relic from a braver century. It shrieked into our moment with the timing of a practical joker.

"Don't answer it," he said.

"It's nine o'clock at night. Nobody calls the shop at nine unless —" Cold slid down my spine. "Unless it's Mrs. Okafor down the street, whose husband had a fall last month, or unless it's Junie, who is at a sleepover, or —"

"Answer it," he said immediately, all his own confession folding itself away, and I loved him for it and hated the timing equally.

It was Dana's mother. Junie had a stomachache. Nothing serious, probably, probably just the brownies, but she was asking for me specifically, tearfully, the way kids ask when they want their person, and I was already grabbing my keys and my coat and saying yes, yes, thank you, ten minutes.

I hung up and turned around and Eli was holding my coat open for me like it was 1954, and the look on his face was the look of a man who had carried a confession up a mountain and was now politely helping it back down.

"Go," he said. "Go be her person. This —" he gestured at the space between us, at the whole charged ruin of the evening — "keeps."

"Does it?" I asked, and it came out smaller than I wanted.

"August." He tucked a wet strand of hair behind my ear, and his hand lingered, and outside the window the rain kept up its patient drumming. "Everything about us keeps. That's the one thing I know for certain."

* * *

**Eli**

I locked up the shop for her, because she'd forgotten the second lock, the sticky one, the one that required a hip-check and a curse. I stood inside the dark bookstore breathing the smell of paper and dust and her, and I thought about the confession I hadn't finished.

*I'm the consultant. Your mother-in-law hired me. I've known since before the lake, and I've said nothing, and every day I don't say it the lie gets a floor thicker.*

I'd rehearsed it in the car. I'd rehearsed it in the shower. I'd rehearsed it standing in line at the diner while Junie — sweet, observant, terrifyingly perceptive Junie — did her homework two booths away. And then August had confessed first, laid herself open on the floor of her own shop, and my confession had risen in my throat like a tide, and the phone had rung, and now the tide was receding and I could feel it taking pieces of me with it.

Outside, the rain had softened to a drizzle. I stepped onto the sidewalk and didn't open the umbrella I'd left in the car on purpose, because apparently I was still a man who wanted to arrive places looking like he'd earned it.

And then I saw her.

She'd come back — no. She hadn't come back. She'd never left, quite. She was standing under the awning next door, where she must have doubled back after putting Junie on the phone with reassurances, or maybe she'd called Dana's mom from the car and turned around, and none of the logistics mattered, because August Whitlock was standing in the rain-shadow of her own storefront looking at me like I was a question she'd finally decided to answer.

"Junie's fine," she said. "Brownies. Dramatic stomachache. She's staying, she just wanted to hear my voice." A breath. "I lied to get out of the car. I told Dana's mom I forgot something at the shop."

"What did you forget?"

"You," she said simply. "I forgot to finish. And I realized on the highway that I don't want to finish it in order, or bravely, or once per sentence. I want to skip to the part where —"

I crossed the sidewalk in four steps. The rain came down between us and on us and I didn't care, I was past caring, past umbrellas, past terms and conditions, past every careful clause I'd ever drafted in my careful career. I took her face in my hands and she rose to meet me and we kissed in the rain outside Whitlock's Books, under the dark windows and the dead sign and the ghost of a man whose letters I'd read in secret files, and it was —

It was not fireworks. It was quieter than that and worse. It was the sound of a structure collapsing inward, gently, all its false walls folding down into a foundation that turned out, against every expectation, to be solid. Her mouth tasted like rain and the tea she'd abandoned hours ago and something underneath that was purely her, and her hands fisted in my soaked shirt like she was afraid I'd be washed away, and I heard myself make a sound I'd never made before, low and undone.

When we broke apart, foreheads touching, breath mingling white in the cool air, she laughed — shaky, wrecked, real.

"Well," she managed.

"Well," I agreed.

Neither of us said the word. We both knew the word. The word sat between us like a third person, grinning. We'd signed nothing, amended nothing, and every term and condition lay in the flooded street around our feet, dissolving in the runoff, and still we said nothing, because some truths you hold off naming the way you hold off turning on a light — because as long as it's dark, the room can still be anything.

"Come inside," she whispered. "You're dripping on the merchandise."

"So are you."

"I live above the merchandise. It's different."

And that was how I came to be standing in the doorway of the apartment above Whitlock's Books, soaked through, kissing a woman whose trust I was paid to dismantle, while downstairs in a locked tin on a shelf sat letters I'd seen referenced in files and she hadn't yet read — and upstairs, in the quiet, with her hands in my hair, I understood with total clarity that tomorrow, or the day after, or the day after that, the tide was going to come back in, and I was going to have to say the thing I hadn't said, and it was going to cost me everything I'd just been given.

Her forehead rested against mine. "Stay," she said. "For coffee. For — talk. For whatever this is."

Whatever this was. Unnamed, unsigned, unannounced.

"Yes," I said. "All of it."

And below us, in the dark shop, the rain tapped the windows like a finger on a contract, waiting for a signature neither of us knew we'd already given.
