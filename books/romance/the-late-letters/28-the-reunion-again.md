## 28. The Reunion, Again

### August

The envelope was cream-colored, heavy stock, and smelled faintly of Eleanor's stationery drawer — which is to say it smelled of money and judgment in equal measure. I knew before I opened it what it would say. You always know, with those envelopes. The weight of the paper does half the talking.

*Dear August,* it began, as if we were pen pals. *The family will convene at Hollow Pines on Saturday next to consider certain matters pertaining to the Whitlock Trust. Your attendance is expected.*

Expected. Not requested. Expected, the way rain is expected, the way death and taxes are expected. Eleanor had been summoning me with that verb for eleven years, ever since Daniel carried me over the threshold of a family I never quite learned the secret handshake for.

I set the letter down on the counter between the register and the stack of returns nobody wanted, and I let myself feel it for exactly four seconds: the cold slide of it, the certainty. Then Junie came out of the biography section asking whether Harriet Tubman had a favorite color, and I said probably blue, honey, most people pick blue, and the world resumed its ordinary shape.

But the letter sat there all afternoon like a stone in my apron pocket even though it wasn't in my pocket at all. It was on the counter. It was everywhere.

Because here's what I knew that the cream-colored paper didn't say: Eli hadn't called in nine days. Nine days of silence since I'd stood on his rental's front steps and watched him try to make *consultant* sound like a small word, a technicality, a thing that happened to other people. *I haven't filed anything,* he'd said, and I'd believed him, which was the worst part, because believing him didn't change what he was. He was the man hired to decide whether my life fit inside a spreadsheet. And I had kissed him. Or nearly. Or almost, twice, with interruptions from babysitters and weather, as if the universe itself kept filing objections.

And now Eleanor wanted the family assembled. To vote.

You don't need to be a novelist to know how votes go when Eleanor Whitlock counts the ballots.

I closed the shop early, which I never do, and walked home past the hardware store and the diner where Junie had once found Eli eating pie alone, and I thought about the tin of letters sitting in my nightstand drawer like a loaded thing. Three letters read. One more waiting. I wasn't ready for the fourth one yet. Some part of me understood — the way you understand things at midnight without wanting to — that once I read it, there would be no version of Saturday where I sat quietly in the back row and let the family do what families do, which is protect the furniture and sacrifice the people.

"Mom?" Junie was doing homework at the kitchen table, her feet hooked around the chair legs the way she did when she was thinking hard. "Is the reunion this weekend? Grandma Eleanor sent me a card."

"A card?"

"It has a check in it." She said this with the flat disgust of a ten-year-old who has learned that checks are what adults send instead of attention. "She wants me to wear the green dress."

"The green dress," I repeated.

"For the picture." Junie looked up. "Are you going to fight about it? Last time you fought about the dress."

"I don't fight about dresses, Junebug."

"You fight quietly. It's worse." She went back to her fractions. "Eli liked the blue one anyway."

I stood very still in my own kitchen, holding a dish towel I had no use for, while my daughter casually detonated the last of my composure. "When did Eli see the blue dress?"

"The reunion. I asked him which one looked better and he said the blue because it matched your eyes, but then he got weird and said the green was nice too, like he was scared of you." She shrugged. "He's kind of scared of you. In a good way."

"In a good way," I echoed.

"Yeah. Like he knows you're real." She chewed her pencil. "Grandma Eleanor isn't real. She's like a painting of a person."

Out of the mouths of babes, etcetera. I kissed the top of her head and she tolerated it, and I went upstairs and sat on the edge of my bed with the tin in my lap, and I still didn't open it. Instead I thought about Saturday. About the long table, the string lights Eleanor had installed permanently after Daniel died because grief, in her house, was a decorating opportunity. About the cousins with their careful faces. About the vote.

Here is what nobody tells you about dread: it's not loud. It doesn't announce itself. It just moves into your body like a tenant who pays no rent, and one morning you notice you've been carrying it so long your shoulders have changed shape.

Saturday was five days away. I had five days to decide who I was going to be when I walked into that room.

The old August — the trained heart, the contained heart, the muscle held obediently still — would sit in the back row. Would let them dissolve the trust with parliamentary courtesy. Would pack up the shop with dignity and tell everyone she understood, because understanding was cheaper than breaking.

But I had read three of Daniel's letters now. And the third one had said *live*, and I was beginning to suspect that living and sitting quietly in the back row were mutually exclusive activities.

* * *

### Eli

There are two kinds of silence. The kind you choose and the kind that chooses you. For nine days I'd been marinating in the second kind, and I want it noted for the record that I am a professional, and professionals are supposed to know the difference between a client relationship and a catastrophe.

My report was due Monday. Fourteen thousand words of analysis, and every one of them a lie by omission, because the truth was simple enough to fit on an index card: *The trust can afford the bookshop indefinitely. The bookshop turns a modest profit. The lease was Daniel Whitlock's dying provision. Dissolving the trust serves no purpose except the consolidation of power, and everyone in that family knows it, including the woman paying my invoice.*

I'd written that sentence six times. I'd deleted it six times. Deleting a true sentence six times is its own kind of data, if you're the sort of person who believes in data, and until Marchetti, Vermont, I had been exactly that sort of person.

Friday afternoon, Eleanor's assistant called my office — the rented office above the pharmacy, with its view of the parking lot and its single sad fern — to confirm the schedule for Saturday.

"Mrs. Whitlock asks that you present your preliminary findings before the vote," the assistant said. Her voice had the smoothness of someone reading from a card. "Ten minutes. Slides optional."

"Slides optional," I repeated.

"Mrs. Whitlock feels visuals can be manipulative."

I nearly laughed. Nearly. "Of course she does."

"And Mr. Marchetti —" a pause, the sound of paper turning, the performance of checking notes she already knew by heart — "Mrs. Whitlock also wishes to confirm that you'll be attending in your professional capacity only."

Professional capacity only. As opposed to whatever capacity I'd attended the first reunion in, hand in hand with the woman whose shop I'd been hired to appraise like a piece of furniture. Eleanor knew. Of course Eleanor knew; Eleanor had known since approximately the moment I shook her hand at Hollow Pines and lied about how August and I met. I'd told her we met at the farmers market. August had told her cousins we met at the farmers market. We'd rehearsed it in a closed bookshop after hours while her daughter eavesdropped behind the poetry shelf, delighted, and I had thought, standing there in the dark making up a love story, that the lie was the safest thing in the room.

"That's correct," I said. "Professional capacity only."

"Wonderful. Cocktails at five."

She hung up. I sat in my office with the fern judging me and did the math I'd been avoiding for nine days. If I presented findings that served Eleanor, I kept my career, my reputation, the partnership track I'd spent twelve years grinding toward, the whole architecture of a life built on the principle that analysis was neutral and analysts were clean. And August lost the shop. Junie lost the shop. The town lost the shop, and the town, I had learned, was not an abstraction; the town was Mrs. Okafor ordering her mysteries monthly and the teenagers doing homework in the window seats and a kid named Dev who came in every Tuesday for westerns.

If I presented the truth, I lost everything except the truth.

It shouldn't have been a hard equation. It was the hardest equation of my life, and the reason it was hard had nothing to do with spreadsheets.

That evening I drove past the bookshop. I want to be honest about this, since honesty is apparently the theme of my current crisis: I drove past it slowly, at the speed of a man looking for a parking spot, and there was a light on in the apartment above it, and a silhouette moving behind the curtain that could have been anyone and was August, and I kept driving. Third time that week. There's a special circle of hell reserved for men who drive past women they love at five miles per hour, and I was getting to know it intimately.

Because here's the thing I couldn't say to her on the stairs, the thing that made me fumble the apology and watch her walk away: I hadn't filed anything, but I also hadn't refused to file. I was a man standing in a doorway, and doorways are where integrity goes to die comfortably.

Saturday, I'd have to walk through it one direction or the other.

* * *

### August

Eleanor called on Wednesday, because of course she did. A letter is for logistics; a phone call is for warfare.

"August." My name in her mouth had always sounded like a diagnosis. "I trust you received the invitation."

"I received it."

"And you'll come." Not a question. Eleanor's questions were rhetorical the way her sympathy was theoretical. "Junie too. Family should be together for these decisions."

"These decisions being?"

A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "The trust requires review. Daniel's provisions were generous, perhaps overly so, and times change. The family deserves clarity." Another pause, and then, with the precision of a woman placing a chess piece: "I understand you've been seeing someone. The consultant."

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the refrigerator hum seemed to hold its breath.

"Eli," I said.

"If you like." As if his name were a costume I'd put on him. "August, I'll say this once, mother to — well. To you. Whatever arrangement the two of you have, leave it at home on Saturday. This is business. Sentiment has cost this family enough."

Sentiment has cost this family enough. I turned the phrase over while she talked about parking arrangements, and I thought: *Daniel.* Daniel was the sentiment that had cost this family enough. Daniel, who wrote letters he never sent and funded a bookshop with dying breath and married beneath himself according to everyone whose opinion mattered, and loved me anyway, or something adjacent to love, in the silences, in the margins, in the unsent pages of a tin box.

"Saturday," I said. "We'll be there."

I hung up and stood in the kitchen and realized my hands were shaking, and not from fear. From something with better posture than fear. Something that felt, alarmingly, like readiness.

Junie appeared in the doorway in the green dress, holding it against herself, making a face. "Do I have to?"

"Actually," I heard myself say, "wear the blue."

Her whole face lit up like a storefront. "The one Eli likes?"

"The one Eli likes."

* * *

### Eli

Saturday morning I put on the good suit — the one I'd worn into the shop the first day, buying Neruda like a man casing the place, which in a sense I was — and I looked at myself in the motel mirror and tried to determine which version of me would walk into Hollow Pines at five o'clock.

The consultant. That was the safe answer. The consultant presents findings, takes questions, invoices the estate, and drives back to the city on Sunday with his career intact and a hole in him shaped like a woman who reads aloud to her daughter.

Or.

I picked up the folder on the desk. Inside it were two reports. I'd printed both, because apparently I'd become the kind of man who prints two versions of his own soul and carries them around like a schizoid briefcase. Report A: fourteen thousand words concluding that the trust's obligations to the bookshop were anachronistic, emotionally driven, and ripe for dissolution. Every number true. Every conclusion bought and paid for.

Report B: eight pages. The same numbers, arranged honestly, arriving at the opposite shore. *The trust can sustain the shop in perpetuity. The shop sustains the town. Dissolution would serve the liquid interests of three family members and injure the community Daniel Whitlock explicitly chose to provide for. Recommendation: preserve.*

Eight pages versus fourteen thousand. Truth, it turns out, is concise. Lies need footnotes.

I put both versions in the folder and drove north through the hills toward Hollow Pines, past the lake where we'd had our second fake date, past the diner where a ten-year-old had once told me her mother cried at night and smiled at my name — information I had done nothing honorable with for nine days — and I thought about terms and conditions. *No falling.* We'd shaken on it over coffee, two guarded people negotiating a fiction, and I had broken clause three within a month and never told her, and now the contract was void, and void contracts have consequences.

The estate rose up out of the trees the way it always did, all stone and symmetry, beautiful the way a courtroom is beautiful. Cars lined the drive. Cousins in summer linen. Somewhere inside, a table set for judgment.

I parked at the end of the row, farthest from the door, and sat in the car for one full minute with both hands on the wheel.

Then I saw her.

August came up the drive on foot with Junie beside her, both of them in blue — the girl in a small bright dress, the woman in something darker, deeper, the color of late evening — and they were laughing about something, heads tipped together, and the sight of them hit me in the sternum like a dropped book. She hadn't seen my car yet. She stopped at the base of the steps, squared her shoulders in a way I recognized now, the way she squared them before opening a difficult delivery or facing down a slow Tuesday, and I understood that she was carrying something into that house heavier than any folder.

Whatever happened in that room tonight, we were both about to stop pretending. The only question left was who broke first, and what the breaking would cost, and whether anything survived it.

August looked back once, down the line of cars, as if she could feel me watching. Her eyes passed over mine and stopped.

Across forty yards of gravel and nine days of silence, neither of us moved. The house waited behind her. The vote waited inside it. And somewhere in my briefcase, eight honest pages weighed more than fourteen thousand lying ones, and I finally reached for the door handle.
