## 29. The Vote

Eleanor's lawyer had once told me, at Daniel's funeral reception, that grief was a process and the Whitlocks were a machine, and that I should let the machine do its work. He'd said it kindly, the way people say terrible things when they've rehearsed them. I thought about that sentence for eleven days, all the way up to the morning of the vote, and what I finally understood was that he hadn't been offering comfort. He'd been offering instructions.

The summons had come on cream cardstock, because of course it had. *The Whitlock Family Trust requests your presence at Hollow Pines, Saturday, ten o'clock, for the consideration of trust business.* Consideration of trust business. As if the shop were a line item. As if Junie's homework by the register were a footnote in someone's quarterly review.

I dressed carefully, which is to say I dressed like a woman who wanted to be underestimated. The gray dress. The small pearls Daniel's grandmother had given me, which Eleanor had always said were "surprisingly tasteful" — a compliment shaped exactly like an insult. I put my hair up. I did not wear armor, because armor would have told them I expected a fight, and I had decided — God help me, I had actually decided — to walk in there as the widow they preferred: quiet, grateful, manageable.

Junie stayed with Rosa next door, who pressed a foil-wrapped empanada into my hand at the door and said, "Come back angry if you have to, but come back." I laughed. It was the last easy laugh of the day.

* * *

Hollow Pines in October is beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful — composed, correct, and utterly indifferent to you. The maples along the drive had gone the color of struck matches. The house sat behind them with its white columns and its long windows, every pane reflecting the burning trees without holding any of their heat.

There were more cars than usual. That was my first clue. Whitlock reunions drew maybe twenty people; this gathering had drawn forty, and they weren't the fun cousins. These were the cousins with portfolios. Marcus and his wife from Hartford. Great-aunt Beatrice, ninety-one and sharp as a paper cut, installed in her usual wingback like a monarch reviewing troops. The Prasad-Whitlocks, who had married in during the eighties and had never once been allowed to forget it. And everywhere, threaded through the rooms like wiring, the particular hum of people who have already decided something and are waiting for the formality.

Eleanor stood at the center of the drawing room in deep green silk, holding court beside the fireplace where Daniel's portrait hung — painted at thirty, before he'd learned to be tired, before I'd met him even. She saw me come in and did the thing she does instead of hugging: she took both my hands and held me at arm's length, appraising.

"August. You look well. Grief agrees with you, eventually. It takes years, but it does."

"Thank you, Eleanor."

"You're sitting with us," she said, and steered me toward the front row of folding chairs that had been set up facing the fireplace, facing the portrait, facing a long table where the trust's documents lay squared and waiting under a brass lamp. Front row. Not among the family. In the seat of the person being discussed.

I sat. I folded my hands. I watched the room fill, and I did the thing I am best at in the world, better than selling books, better than anything: I made myself small and watchful and calm, and inside that calm I kept a small hot coal of dread turning over and over like a stone in a tumbler, being polished into something smooth enough to swallow.

* * *

The meeting opened at 10:14 with a prayer from Cousin Wendell, who is not religious but enjoys the gravity. Then Eleanor rose, and I will give her this: she is magnificent. Sixty-eight years old, spine straight as a mast, voice pitched to carry to the back of a church. She spoke about stewardship. She spoke about legacy. She used the word *fiduciary* four times, and each time, heads nodded around the room like wheat.

"The trust was established in 1954," she said, "by Daniel's grandfather, to preserve family assets and support family enterprises. It has done so honorably for seventy years. But a trust is not a museum. A trust is a living instrument, and living instruments must be pruned."

Pruned. I looked down at my hands. My knuckles had gone white and I made them relax one finger at a time.

She talked for twenty minutes. Diversification. Market conditions. The burden of ill-performing assets. She never once said the word *bookshop* directly until minute nineteen, and then she said it the way you'd name a tumor: "the retail property on Marsh Street, currently leased to a concern operated by the widow."

*The widow.* Not August. Not Junie's mother. The widow, a legal condition, a status, a woman-shaped gap in the ledger.

And then — because the universe is not subtle — the doors at the back of the room opened, and Eli walked in.

My whole body knew him before my mind caught up. The way he moved through a crowd, apologetic and unhurried. The gray suit I'd seen him button against the rain outside the shop. He carried a leather portfolio and wore the expression of a man arriving late to his own execution, and when his eyes found mine across forty heads, something crossed his face too fast to name and too slow to hide.

He took a seat near the back. He did not look at me again.

Eleanor continued as though nothing had happened, and perhaps for her nothing had. Perhaps she'd invited him herself. Perhaps this was the whole architecture of the day: the report delivered, the recommendation sealed, the widow seated in the front row to witness the machinery close over her with a sound like a book shutting.

* * *

They voted by show of hands, which surprised me. I'd expected proxies, ballots, lawyers. Instead Eleanor simply said, "All those in favor of directing the trustees to terminate the Marsh Street lease at the expiration of the current term, ninety days hence, and to liquidate the property accordingly — raise your right hand."

It happened fast. That's the thing no one tells you about catastrophes. You imagine them slow, cinematic, with time to stand up and shout. But a room full of people raising their hands takes four seconds. Arms went up all around me — Marcus, the Prasad-Whitlocks, Wendell, half the room, a forest of hands rising like something growing wrong. Great-aunt Beatrice raised hers with the crisp efficiency of a woman who had been signing off on other people's losses since 1962.

Against: three hands. Three. Two of them belonged to the young cousins, Tom and Priya, who had spent one afternoon in my shop in July and bought a stack of paperbacks they couldn't afford, and who now looked stricken and defiant and alone. The third hand belonged to old Arthur, Daniel's uncle, who caught my eye as he lowered his arm and gave me a small, helpless shrug that said *I'm sorry, I'm outvoted, I'm old.*

Ninety days. The number landed in the room like a coin dropped in a church. Ninety days to vacate. Ninety days of inventory, of the ladder I climb to shelve the poetry, of the doorbell Junie rings with her elbow because her hands are always full, of the window display I change on Sundays while the town walks by. Nineteen years of my life measured out and given a deadline.

Eleanor turned to me. The whole room turned to me. And here is what I did: nothing.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the only currency I have left after today. I did not stand. I did not speak. I did not tell them about the letters in the tin, or the marriage that had gone cold years before the accident, or the truth I'd been carrying like contraband. I sat in my gray dress with my small pearls and I looked at Eleanor Whitlock, and I smiled.

It was not a kind smile. I felt it happen on my face like weather. Somewhere beneath the humiliation — the hot, crawling shame of sitting in the front row while a family priced my life and found it underperforming — somewhere under all that, a door opened onto a very quiet room, and in that quiet room a decision was already made, though I didn't yet know its shape.

"August," Eleanor said gently, publicly, performing mercy for her audience, "no one doubts your devotion. But sentiment is not a business plan. You'll be provided assistance with relocation. We are not monsters."

*We are not monsters.* Four words that will follow me to my grave.

"Thank you," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "Thank you for your consideration."

*Consideration of trust business.* The cream cardstock. The machine doing its work.

* * *

Afterward, the room dissolved into coffee and murmurs, the particular relief of people who have done a hard thing and now get to feel noble about surviving it. Cousins touched my shoulder. Someone said the word *transition* to me twice. Marcus told me about a commercial space in Hartford with good foot traffic, as though I were a fern to be repotted.

I found myself standing near the fireplace, under Daniel's painted face, and that was where Eli found me.

He stopped a few feet away, portfolio still in hand, unopened. Up close he looked like he hadn't slept in a week, and there was rain still somewhere in the memory of his hair.

"August—"

"Don't." I kept my voice low, pleasant, a conversation between acquaintances. "Not here. Whatever you're going to say — I can't do it in this room, under him." I tipped my head toward the portrait. "You sat in the back. You didn't raise your hand. I noticed. I notice everything, it's my curse. But you also didn't say a word, and silence in that room is just a slower yes."

"I haven't filed anything," he said quietly. "The report isn't finished. What they voted on today — that was Eleanor moving first. That wasn't me."

"But you're the reason she could." I finally turned and looked at him straight on, and I watched him take it. "You walked into my shop and bought Neruda. You ate dinner at my table. You danced with me under string lights. And the whole time you were carrying their audit in your briefcase. Was any of it—" I stopped, because finishing the question in this house would cost me more than I had left. "Ninety days, Eli. You gave them the map and they're evicting my daughter."

"That's not what I—" His jaw worked. "August, please. Let me fix this."

"Fix it." I nodded slowly, and the smile came back, the weather-smile. "You know what the worst part is? I hired a stranger to lie to my family, and he turned out to be the one honest man in the room — honest everywhere except with me."

I walked out past him. At the doors I paused, and I don't know why I said it, except that some part of me had already left the meeting, the vote, the whole gilded machine behind, and was standing in a dark shop at midnight holding a tin.

"Eleanor told everyone we're not monsters," I said. "She's right. Monsters require imagination."

Then I walked out into the October light, past the match-colored maples, to my car, and I sat in the driver's seat with my hands on the wheel and did not start the engine for a long time.

Because here is what I knew, sitting there, humiliated and silent and already past the point of caring what they thought of me: I had brought the tin's contents with me. They were in my bag, in a folder, all three letters plus the fourth I'd found only Tuesday — the one addressed not to me but to whoever came after, the one I hadn't been able to finish reading.

I had carried them up the drive and into that room and I had said nothing. I had chosen safety one last time, and safety had handed me ninety days and called it assistance with relocation.

I started the car. I did not drive home. I drove to the end of the lane and pulled over, and I took out the folder, and I read the fourth letter all the way through, sitting in the shadow of Hollow Pines, while the leaves came down like slow fire.

When I finished, I wiped my face, put the car in gear, and headed for town — toward the shop, toward Junie, toward whatever came next.

There were ninety days on the clock. I intended to use every one of them.
