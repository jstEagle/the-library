## 17. The Rival Arrives

The roads opened on a Thursday, or what the radio called Thursday—Jude had lost track of the calendar somewhere in the middle of February, when the days had begun to blur together like watercolors left in the rain. What mattered was that a plow had come through at dawn, grinding and orange against the white, and carved a corridor down the valley road wide enough for one cautious vehicle at a time. Rowan stood at the kitchen window watching it pass with an expression Jude could only describe as bereft, as though the plow were a betrayal rather than a rescue.

"They'll want to come now," Rowan said.

"Who will?"

"Everyone." Rowan turned from the window. The light caught the gray at their temples, the lines around their eyes that twenty years had drawn like careful pen strokes. "Groceries. Mail. Marguerite's doctor, if we can get him up here. And—" They stopped, and Jude watched them decide not to finish the sentence.

"And whoever's writing the other book," Jude said.

Rowan didn't flinch. That was the thing about Rowan that had undone Jude from the very first summer: they never flinched. "You've been waiting for it," they said. "Since the letter came."

Jude had been waiting for it since Boston, if they were honest. Since the gray office where they'd signed the Vale contract and felt, for the first time in twenty years of ghostwriting, the faint electric hum of something that wasn't anonymity. Since chapter fourteen, when Marguerite had listened to Jude's full confession—the courthouse hallway, the testimony, the name Jude had protected at the cost of her family's land—and instead of rage or absolution had reached for her dictation recorder and said, *Then we'll put it in the book. All of it. Correctly this time.*

The memoir was finished but for Marguerite's final read-through. It sat on Jude's laptop and in three printed copies, and it contained things no court record held: the truth about the disputed parcel, the truth about the surveyor who'd falsified the boundary maps, the truth about why a nineteen-year-old ghostwriter-to-be had sworn false testimony to keep that surveyor's daughter—a girl named Elena Marsh, who'd once done Jude a kindness neither of them could afford—from drowning in her father's crime. The courts had failed everyone. The book would not.

Which meant, of course, that someone else wanted the story first, and wanted it wrong.

* * *

The emissary arrived at half past two, while the light was still hard and white on the snow.

Jude heard the car before they saw it—tires hissing on wet pavement, an engine laboring up the orchard road—and went to the window of the guest room, which had become their study, which had become, without anyone announcing it, the place where the book lived. A dark sedan, city-clean except for a rime of road salt along the rocker panels. It stopped at the gate because the gate was closed, and the man who got out of it did not open the gate but stood beside it and looked up at the house with the patient confidence of someone accustomed to being let in.

He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, wearing a wool coat that cost more than Jude's entire winter wardrobe. He carried a leather portfolio under one arm like a hymnal. Even at this distance, even through glass, Jude recognized the species. They had been this creature's prey for two decades; they knew the posture, the unhurried certainty, the way such people looked at houses like Vale Orchard and saw not a home but an asset with a narrative attached.

Rowan was already moving through the hall below. Jude heard the front door open, heard the cold pour in, heard voices—Rowan's level and courteous, the man's warm and carrying. By the time Jude came down the stairs, Rowan had admitted him as far as the entryway and no farther, arms crossed, snow melting off the man's shoes onto the slate in dark crescents.

"Ms.—Mr.—Almeida," the man said, extending a hand. His smile was excellent. Everything about him was excellent, in the way of professionally pleasant objects. "Daniel Okafor, Meridian Press. I hope you'll forgive the intrusion. The roads only just—"

"I know who you are," Jude said, and did not take the hand. "You're the publisher of the exposé."

Okafor's smile adjusted by a few degrees, recalibrating. "I'd call it a corrective history. *The Vale Fortune: An Accounting.* We've been working on it for eighteen months." He glanced past Jude's shoulder at the staircase, the dim upper hall, with the frank appraisal of a man pricing the chandeliers. "Is Mrs. Vale receiving visitors?"

"No," said Rowan.

"She's resting," Jude said, which was true in the way that dying is a kind of rest. "But I'm the one you came to see. Aren't I?"

Okafor turned back to them, and for a moment the professional warmth dropped away entirely, and what remained was something Jude respected more: plain appetite. "May we speak privately?"

"You may speak here," Rowan said. "I'm staying."

A flicker of irritation crossed Okafor's face, quickly smoothed. "Of course." He opened the portfolio. Inside, Jude saw printed pages, tabbed and highlighted, and a checkbook, and a slim digital recorder that he made no move to hide. "Then let me be direct, because I respect your time and I suspect you respect mine. Meridian has obtained the complete court record of the Vale land dispute. Including—" he tapped a tabbed page without looking at it, the gesture of a man who knew his material cold, "—the sealed testimony of a witness identified in the transcript only as J.A., age nineteen, whose statement regarding the boundary survey proved decisive. Whose statement, our legal review suggests, was materially false, and given under circumstances that suggest coercion or inducement by a third party."

The hallway was very quiet. Snowmelt ticked off Okafor's coat onto the slate. Somewhere upstairs, faintly, Marguerite's radio murmured its afternoon classical program, a cello working through something old and patient.

"We know who J.A. is," Okafor continued gently, as though delivering a diagnosis. "We've known for four months. Our book runs in June. The question is not whether your testimony appears in it, Mr. Almeida. The question is in what company."

* * *

They ended up in the kitchen, because Okafor refused to conduct business standing in a drafty hall and Rowan refused to let him past the kitchen doorway, and so the negotiation happened over the scarred pine table where, eleven nights ago, Jude had almost kissed Rowan among the rising smell of bread. Jude was aware of that table now, aware of the flour still ghosted into its grain, and hated how much it steadied them.

Okafor laid out his offer the way a dealer lays out cards: faceup, unhurried, each item given its moment.

"One." He set down a printed sheet. "An advance of seventy-five thousand dollars for a detailed account of your work on the Vale memoir—its contents, its claims, its sources. Delivered before our publication date."

"Two." A second sheet. "Your cooperation in characterizing the testimony accurately—as a frightened teenager manipulated by Elena Marsh's father, which our research suggests is closer to the truth than the court's version. In exchange, we frame you sympathetically. A victim, not a villain."

"Three." And here Okafor paused, and his voice dropped into a register of genuine, almost tender reasonableness. "You persuade Mrs. Vale to delay her own publication until after ours. Or better—not to publish at all. She's dying, Mr. Almeida. Everyone knows it. A posthumous memoir from a dying woman contradicting our reporting reads as sentiment. A living author retracting nothing but declining to engage—that silence serves us beautifully. In return: the sealed testimony stays sealed in our pages too. We identify J.A. only as 'a witness close to the Marsh family.' Your name never appears. You walk away whole. Ghost to the end."

He sat back. Outside the window behind him, the orchard rows ran white to the tree line, the pruned branches black and patient, holding next spring inside themselves like a secret.

Jude found that their hands were steady. This surprised them. Twenty years ago, in a courthouse hallway that smelled of floor wax and rain, their hands had shaken so badly they'd had to press them flat against their thighs to sign the affidavit. But that had been a different transaction—terror dressed up as virtue, a sacrifice made in panic and called conscience ever since. This was simpler. This was just money and a name, laid on a table between the salt cellar and the bread knife.

"No," Jude said.

Okafor nodded slowly, as if Jude had confirmed something he'd already known. "May I ask why?"

"Because the book isn't mine to sell." Jude kept their voice level. "And because your framing is wrong. I wasn't a victim. I wasn't coerced. I chose, and the choice was mine, and the harm it did—to the Vales, to the record, to the truth—was also mine. If my testimony appears anywhere, it appears with my name attached and my culpability attached, in a book I control. Not as a footnote in yours."

"That's very noble," Okafor said, without mockery, which was somehow worse. "Now let me tell you what nobility costs. We publish in June regardless. With or without your cooperation, the court records are public once unsealed, and our attorneys have already petitioned to unseal. They'll grant it—it's routine. So in June, the world learns that the Vale memoir's author committed perjury at nineteen. They learn it from us, on our terms, in our framing, three weeks before your book lands—or whenever it lands, if it lands at all, because Mrs. Vale may not survive to finish it." He gathered his sheets, squared their edges against the table, precise as a surgeon. "I'm offering you the chance to tell your own story first. Refuse me, and you've handed the first telling to strangers."

"I know," Jude said.

"Do you?" For the first time, something human moved behind Okafor's excellent composure—not sympathy exactly, but recognition, the look of a man who had watched many people arrive at this particular crossroads and knew the exact weight of what he was asking them to carry. "Because I don't think you do. I think you've spent twenty years believing that invisibility is safety. It isn't. It's just pain, deferred, with interest." He closed the portfolio. "My card is in the door pocket of your guest room—I took the liberty. The offer stands until April first. After that, the unsealing motion proceeds, and I can't protect you from what it turns up, because I won't be trying to."

He stood. Rowan opened the front door for him with a courtesy that had knives in it. At the threshold, Okafor paused and looked back—at Jude, then past them, up the staircase, toward the room where an old woman was listening to cellos and running out of time.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I read the excerpt your agent circulated last week. The chapter about the surveyor's daughter." His mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "It's better than anything we'll print. That's not flattery. It's a warning. Write the ending yourself, Almeida, or someone will write it for you."

Then the door shut, and the sedan ground away down the valley road, and the house settled back into its snowbound hush as though the visit had been weather.

* * *

Jude stood in the entryway for a long time after the sound of the engine faded.

The arithmetic was simple and terrible. Refusal meant June. Refusal meant the unsealing motion granted, the transcript published, J.A. dissected in print by people who would get it wrong—not maliciously, perhaps, but wrong in all the ways that mattered, wrong in the grain. Refusal meant Marguerite's book arriving second, shadowed, its truths discounted as damage control. Refusal meant Rowan reading their name in a stranger's exposé before Jude could find the courage to say it aloud.

And acceptance meant—what? Seventy-five thousand dollars and a comfortable lie. Silence purchased at the price of everything the last three months had built: the household, the dictations, the chapter Marguerite had written about a young person who did the right thing badly, the night in the cellar where the almost had finally become actual, Rowan's hands and Rowan's voice saying *stay* like a word they'd been rehearsing for twenty years.

There was no version of this where Jude stayed hidden. Okafor was right about that, and the knowledge landed not as dread but as something adjacent to relief—a trap springing shut that Jude had, some part of them, been walking toward on purpose. The ghost's terms had always been a cage with the door painted on. Someone else had finally opened it.

"You're going to do it," Rowan said from the kitchen doorway. Not a question.

"I'm going to do it." Jude turned. "Publish first. Under my name. Full testimony, full culpability, everything—including what I did to your family. Before he can make it his story, I make it mine."

Rowan came into the entryway slowly, snow-light from the transom window striping their face. "Marguerite will have to agree. Her name is on the cover next to yours now."

"I know."

"And she's weaker today. The doctor comes tomorrow, if the roads hold." Rowan stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Jude could see the pulse beating at their throat, quick and controlled. "So whatever you're going to ask her, you ask her tonight."

"Yes."

Rowan studied them for a long moment, and then—this was the thing, this was the unbearable, salvational thing—they reached out and straightened Jude's collar, a small domestic gesture, an act of tending performed in the wreckage of a negotiation, as if courage were simply another chore on the estate's endless list.

"Good," Rowan said quietly. "Because I've read every word you've written for her, Jude. And there is no version of you in those pages that I would let a man like that introduce to the world." Their hand lingered at Jude's jaw, brief and warm. "Go up. She's awake. She's been waiting for you since the car pulled in—she watches the gate too, you know. She always has."

Upstairs, behind the door with the brass handle worn smooth by a century of hands, the cello had gone quiet. In its place Jude could hear the small, unmistakable click of Marguerite's dictation recorder switching on.

Waiting.
