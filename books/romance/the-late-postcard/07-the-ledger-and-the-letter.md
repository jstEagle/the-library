## 7. The Ledger and the Letter

Yara dressed for the bank the way she imagined people dressed for court: the grey blazer she'd bought for her mother's funeral and worn only twice since, the good shoes that pinched, hair pinned back so severely it changed the shape of her face. In the mirror above the coat hooks she looked like a woman who had already lost something and was negotiating over the remains.

*The heart is trainable muscle,* she told herself, the old litany, the one she'd been reciting since she was twenty-four and learned what wanting someone could cost. *You train it by not giving it anything to fail at.* She straightened her collar. Her reflection did not believe her either.

The bank sat two streets up from the harbor, in a building that had once been the customs house, all granite and tall windows and the smell of cold stone even in summer. Halvard Brandt had been the manager for as long as there had been a manager — a soft-spoken man with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, who kept a model sailing ship on his desk and spoke about money the way other men spoke about weather: regretfully, as though none of it were his doing.

"Yara." He rose to shake her hand, and his grip was gentle, apologetic before a word was said. "Thank you for coming in."

"I brought the ledger." She set it on the desk between them like evidence. It was a fat book bound in green cloth, its spine cracked from eleven years of nightly arithmetic, every loaf counted, every krone entered in her small upright hand. "I thought you should see it. The actual numbers. Not just — whatever the system says."

Halvard opened it almost tenderly, turning pages. Outside the tall window a gull hung motionless on the wind, white against the grey water. "You keep beautiful records," he said.

"That's not an answer, Halvard."

He took off his glasses. That was how she knew. Men like Halvard delivered bad news with their glasses off, folded in their hands, so they couldn't hide behind the blur.

"The restructuring committee met Tuesday," he said. "I argued for you. I want you to know that. I told them about the café's history, the foot traffic in season, your margins in July and August." He laid his palm flat on the ledger, as if he could press the numbers into compliance. "But the arrears are fourteen months deep, Yara. And the building itself — the block is worth more than the business. There are parties interested in the property who can service debt you simply cannot generate selling coffee and cardamom buns at mainland prices."

"Parties interested." She made the words sound like what they were. "You mean the developer. Vik."

Halvard didn't confirm it, which confirmed it. "An auction would be scheduled for early autumn," he went on, gently, mercilessly. "Unless the arrears are cleared in full before then. That's the window. Six weeks, roughly. Through the end of ferry season."

Six weeks. The number landed in her chest with a strange familiarity, and it took her a moment to understand why: six weeks was also the length of Elin Marsh's posting. She had learned that from Jonas, of all people, chattering over her burst pipe while he soldered joints, telling her his sibling was back for one final season before Bergen, permanent, across the water, gone for good. Six weeks. Everything in her life now ran on the same cruel timetable, as if the island itself had conspired to make her losses arrive together, neatly itemized, like a bill.

"I'll find the money," she heard herself say.

Halvard looked at her with real kindness. "Where?"

"I don't know yet." She stood, buttoned the blazer. "But I've never missed a payment I could help, and I intend to help this one. Give me until the auction notice. That's fair. You'll have my answer before you need to print anything."

"It isn't printing we worry about," Halvard said quietly. "It's the bidding."

She walked out past the model ship, past the teller who wouldn't meet her eyes, down the granite steps into a morning so bright and ordinary it felt like an insult. The harbor glittered. The ferry was in, white and patient at its berth, and a knot of summer visitors queued at the kiosk for ice cream. Saltvik went on being beautiful. It always did. That was the trap of living somewhere lovely: the beauty didn't care.

In the café kitchen that afternoon she pulled the ledger toward herself and began, for the first time in years, not to record but to plan. New menu items. A tasting board, the rye crisps people asked about, the cloudberry thing she'd been making for herself every August and never charging for. She wrote figures beside each idea and crossed most of them out and wrote them again. It was absurd. It was arithmetic performed on hope. But her hands needed work the way other people needed prayer, and by closing time the page looked less like a ledger than like a battle map, arrows and numbers and one word circled three times in the margin: *enough?*

She left the question mark. She couldn't bring herself to answer it.

* * *

Elin hadn't slept properly in nine days.

The station cot was the same as it had always been — same wool blanket, same sag in the middle, same salt-stiff smell of the room where Elin had spent half the nights of their twenties — but the body lying in it was different now, older, and it refused the comfort of familiar things. Sleep came in shallow pulls and broke on the same image every time: a photograph on a phone screen, taken by an idiot brother with a contractor's flashlight, of a postcard lying on splintered floorboards, its face warped and bloomed with water damage, its handwriting unmistakable.

Elin had stopped opening the photo after the third night. Then started again. That was the shape of the whole sickness: stopping, starting, stopping.

Now, at two in the morning, with rain ticking against the station windows and the radio muttering shipping forecasts to no one, Elin lay in the dark and let the thumb hover over the screen.

*Don't,* said the sensible voice, the trained voice, the coast guard voice that assessed risk for a living. *You read it once. You know what it does to you.*

But the sensible voice had been losing arguments for eleven years.

Elin opened it.

The postcard filled the screen, glowing in the dark room. The front was a photograph of the harbor — the old one, pre-renovation, the ferry smaller then, the seawall unpatched — which meant Yara had chosen it deliberately, or had simply grabbed the only postcard Saltvik sold, which amounted to the same confession. The message side was worse. Water had gotten under the ink in places, feathering the letters into soft grey ghosts, but the handwriting survived: that upright, disciplined script Elin would have known anywhere, the hand that had written *closed for inventory* on the café door for a decade, the hand that had signed nothing personal in all that time except, apparently, this.

Elin had read it the first night standing in Jonas's kitchen while the kettle screamed unnoticed. Had read it again at three a.m. Had read it so many times the words had begun to feel less like language than like weather, something that moved through and left damage.

*I don't know why I'm writing this when I could just say it—*

That was how it began. Elin knew that much by heart now.

Tonight, though, lying flat with the phone held above their face, Elin noticed something they hadn't noticed before. Their eyes had been too full of the words, the first nights; they'd gone straight to the message like a starving person goes to bread. But tonight, in the small hours, with nothing left to want except understanding, they looked at the top of the card instead. The postmark.

The ink was smeared, the stamp swollen with damp, but the date was legible. The machine-stamped digits sat there in the glow, patient as a landmine.

Elin did the arithmetic the way you do arithmetic when you already dread the answer — slowly, twice, hoping to be wrong both times.

Three days. The postcard was postmarked three days before the ferry. Three days before the job offer in Bergen had been accepted, before the duffel bag, before the dock, before Yara had stood with her hands in her apron pockets and said *congratulations* in a voice like a door closing. Three days before everything ended, Yara Nystrom had written a confession, addressed it, stamped it —

And never mailed it.

Elin lowered the phone onto their chest and stared at the ceiling, where the rain shadows moved like slow fish.

Three days. Which meant it wasn't a letter written long after, in safety, in retrospect, when feelings had cooled into memoir. It was written in the heat of it. While Elin was still sleeping twenty meters from the harbor, still eating breakfast at the corner table, still walking past the café twice a day hoping the curtain would twitch. Yara had sat somewhere — the kitchen, probably, flour on her hands or not yet washed off — and put it all down on paper, and carried it around for three days like a coal in her pocket, and then put it in a tin box behind a wall instead of in a mailbox, and let Elin leave believing—

Believing what? That was the question that had eaten eleven years alive. Believing she didn't care. Believing the deflected proposal had been a relief to them both. Believing the flat *congratulations* was the whole truth of her.

Elin sat up in the dark. The cot creaked. Somewhere out in the channel a buoy bell knocked against its mooring, a small persistent sound, like a fist on a door.

Because here was the thing Elin had never told anyone, the thing that lived in the bottom drawer of the desk in the Bergen apartment beneath the tax documents, wrapped in a rubber band: Elin had a letter too. Written the night before leaving, on the back of a tide table, drunk on farewell beer and terror, saying everything — that the offer could be refused, that the proposal had been serious, that if Yara said one true word Elin would stay and hang the consequences. Written, sealed, never sent. Carried through four apartments and one broken engagement and two promotions like a stone in the boot.

*I came back once,* the unsent letter admitted, in ink gone brown with age. *Two years after. I stood across the street and watched you through the window and you were laughing with a customer and the café was full and you looked happy, and I decided I'd done you a favor by going. I decided my silence was a gift. I have been telling myself that ever since.*

Both of them, then. Both cowards. Both curators of unsent museums. Elin laughed — one dry note, no humor in it — at the symmetry of it, the sheer stupid craftsmanship of the disaster. Two people who loved each other had each concluded, independently, for over a decade, that the silence was mutual consent. Each had mistaken the other's fear for peace.

The radio crackled. *Wind southwest increasing to gale late tomorrow evening,* said the forecaster, to the empty room, to the sea.

Elin got up. Couldn't lie still. Pulled on the uniform shirt out of habit more than purpose and went down the corridor to the duty room, where the big chart of the Sound hung under glass, and stood looking at the waters Elin had spent a career keeping safe for strangers.

Here was the discipline, then. Here was the training. If the letter existed — if Yara had loved them, three days before the end, and buried it — then the past was not settled. It was live ordnance, and it had surfaced, and pretending otherwise was negligence of exactly the kind that got people drowned.

Fix yourself first, Elin thought. That was the rule. You didn't go into rough water carrying unresolved weight; you jettisoned or you stayed ashore. So: finish the season cleanly. Sign nothing for Bergen yet — that was new, that decision had a loose hinge — and settle the account with Yara Nystrom like a professional. Walk in. Say the plain sentences. *I saw the photo. I know what it said. Tell me if it was true, and tell me if it still is.* No ambush, no accusation, no rehearsed speeches of their own.

Tomorrow. Or the day after. When the hands stopped shaking.

Elin looked at the clock. 3:40. Opening day at the café was — Elin checked the memory, checked it again — today. Jonas had mentioned it with the elaborate casualness of a man laying a trap. Doors at seven. Cardamom buns. The whole town would drift through.

Plenty of time, Elin told the empty duty room, to decide what a brave person would say.

Outside, the rain thickened, and the buoy bell went on knocking, knocking, like something asking to be let in.

* * *

They nearly collided at seven-fifteen, and neither of them knew it.

Yara, in the doorway of the café, propping the chalkboard sign that read *REOPENED — PLEASE BE PATIENT WITH US*, glanced down the hill toward the harbor because the ferry horn had sounded, a reflex older than sense. Across the wet cobbles, at a distance of perhaps eighty meters, a figure in coast guard uniform stood at the seawall, looking not at the ferry but up the street, directly at her, motionless in the early light.

Yara's hands went still on the sign. Eighty meters of bright cold air between them. All she had to do was lift a hand. All she had to do was call a name across a harbor, the way people did in towns like this every single day, without ceremony, without consequence.

Her heart — that trainable muscle, that disciplined, proofed, eleven-years-in-the-making muscle — slammed against its cage like something drowning.

Inside, the oven timer rang.

Yara turned and went back to her bread, and told herself she simply hadn't seen anyone at all.

At the seawall, Elin watched the door close behind her, and exhaled, and turned toward the station, and told themselves the same thing.
