## 8. Storm Warning

The radio woke Elin before the storm did.

*Coast guard Saltvik, coast guard Saltvik, vessel taking water off the north skerries, two aboard—*

Elin was already moving, boots half-laced, the familiar choreography of eleven years of callouts carrying them down the stairs of the station while the wind arrived behind them like a late guest, slamming the harbor doors against their hooks. Outside, the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the sea beyond the breakwater stood up in long, ragged ranks, white-capped and furious.

"Kvist, with me," Elin said. "Everyone else on standby."

The RIB hit the first swell outside the harbor mouth and the whole world became salt. Elin's hands knew the wheel; that was the mercy of this work, that the body could be competent even when the mind was elsewhere — elsewhere being a café on the harbor road where a woman with flour in her hair had looked at Elin three days ago like a locked door looks at someone who has lost the key.

*Old letters,* Elin had asked her, too casually, watching her face.

*I don't keep things,* Yara had said, too quickly, wiping a counter that was already clean.

Elin had turned the photo over maybe four hundred times since. Jonas's message had been flippant — *found this in your girlfriend's wall lol* — but there was nothing funny about it. The postcard was soft-edged with water damage, the ink feathered, but the handwriting was unmistakable: those narrow, upright letters, the way Yara crossed her t's like she was striking them through. And the date. Elin had checked the date against every memory they owned, and the date was three days before the ferry to the mainland, three days before everything ended without ever being finished.

*Dear Elin,* the visible fragment began. The rest dissolved into blur and rot, except for one line near the middle that the water had spared as if deliberately, cruelly:

*—and I think I have loved you since the summer we were nineteen, which is not something I know how to say out loud, so I am saying it here, where no one—*

Then nothing. Then the postmark. Eleven years old.

Elin had spent three nights telling themselves it didn't mean what it meant. That people wrote things they never meant to send precisely because they didn't mean them. That Yara Nystrom had made her choice on a dock in September, clear-eyed and kind about it, and kindness was still a choice.

The radio crackled. "Vessel confirms two souls, engine flooded, drifting toward the outer shoal."

"Copy," Elin said, and pushed the throttle forward, and let the weather have all of them for a while.

* * *

It nearly went wrong at the shoals.

Later, writing the incident report, Elin would describe it in the flat language of procedure: heavy seas, reduced visibility, successful recovery of two crewmen from the fishing vessel *Måsen*, minor damage to starboard pontoon. What the report would not say was that when the second fisherman came off the deck of his sinking boat and missed the grab line — missed it by an arm's length, by a heartbeat, by the width of everything — Elin went in after him.

That wasn't protocol. Protocol said wait for the heaving line, keep the boat between yourself and the shoal. But the man's head went under between two swells and Elin was already unclipping the harness, already in the water that was cold enough to stop thought, cold enough to make the body its own animal. The swell picked them both up and slammed them together and Elin got a fist in his jacket and held on while Kvist hauled the line in hand over hand, screaming words the wind took apart.

For perhaps forty seconds — Elin would count them later, lying awake — there was nothing in the world but the weight of another person and the roar of the sea and the animal arithmetic of *hold on, hold on, hold on.*

They got him over the pontoon. They got Elin over the pontoon. The *Måsen* went down twenty minutes later with her name just visible under the foam, and the run back to Saltvik was made in a silence broken only by the two rescued men shivering and swearing softly at their luck.

And through all of it — through the drop into black water, through the holding-on — one image had burned absurdly, stubbornly behind Elin's eyes: Yara standing at the seawall.

Because Elin had seen her. In the worst moment, when the RIB came about hard and rose on a wave and for one instant the whole harbor was laid out below — the lights, the church hill, the seawall — Elin had seen a figure standing alone at the rail in the rain, motionless, and known it was her the way you know your own name. Everyone else had gone inside. The sensible town had shuttered itself against the storm. Only Yara had stood out in it, watching the rescue light swing across the water, and Elin had thought, with a clarity that had nothing to do with the rope in their hands or the man in the sea: *she's watching me. She has always been watching me.*

Eleven years of sirens. Eleven years of a woman flinching at every callout siren in a small town, pretending she didn't.

Elin wanted — needed — to know what that meant, and was terrified of knowing, and knew that tonight, soaked through and wrung out and too tired to build walls, was exactly the wrong night and exactly the right one.

* * *

The café was dark when Elin reached it, past ten o'clock, rain still coming down in ropes. Every window was black except one, low and gold above the kitchen door, and from inside came the small domestic sounds of someone closing up: a chair leg scraping tile, the clank of the oven door, water running.

Elin stood in the rain for a full minute, dripping, arguing with themselves. Go home. Dry off. Sleep. Come back tomorrow with dry hair and rehearsed sentences and the armor back in place.

But the armor had a hole in it now, torn open somewhere off the north skerries, and through the hole Elin could see only one thing clearly: that they had come back to Saltvik for one season, that the season was burning down week by week like a wick, and that if they left again without asking — without once, plainly, asking — then they deserved every silence the next fifty years held.

Elin knocked.

The sounds inside stopped. A long pause, long enough that Elin almost turned around. Then footsteps, and the door opened on the chain, and one gray eye appeared in the gap, and Yara said:

"It's after hours."

"I know." Water ran off Elin's chin. "I was in the sea today."

The eye closed. The chain rattled. The door opened all the way onto warmth and lamplight and the smell of cinnamon that never entirely left the place, that lived in the walls the way the old postcard had lived in the wall, and Yara stood there in a sweater with dough on the cuff, looking at Elin like a woman reading a letter she'd hoped never to receive.

"You were in the sea," she repeated.

"The *Måsen* went down off the skerries. Both crew are fine. One of them owes me a drink." Elin attempted a smile and felt it fail. "I'm sorry. I should have gone home. I just — I saw you. On the seawall. During the callout."

Yara's jaw tightened. "I was walking."

"In a storm."

"People walk in storms here, Elin. It's an island. We're allowed."

"Yara." Her name came out rougher than intended, stripped of all the casualness Elin had spent three weeks constructing — the polite customer voice, the old-friend voice, the voice that asked about flour suppliers and ferry schedules and anything, anything but the truth. "I saw you standing there and I thought: she's still watching. After everything. She's still—" Elin stopped, because the sentence was heading somewhere dangerous, somewhere with the word *care* in it, and the kitchen was warm and close and Yara was looking at them with an expression that was cracking at the edges, and Elin had not earned whatever was behind the crack.

"Come inside before you drown on my doorstep," Yara said finally. "That would be ironic even by our standards."

She put a towel in Elin's hands without touching them — Elin noticed that, noticed how carefully she avoided it, the way you avoid a hot pan — and put the kettle on, and stood with her back turned while Elin dripped onto her floor and tried to remember how to be a person instead of a rescue.

"You could have died today," she said to the kettle.

"That's the job."

"Don't." The word came out sharp, cracked down the middle. "Don't say it like that. Like it's nothing. Like it's — weather." She turned around. Her eyes were bright and furious and frightened, all three at once, and Elin felt the sight of it go through them like cold water, like the shoal water, like everything. "Do you know what a siren does to this town? Do you know what it does to—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter, more controlled, worse. "People have people out there. It matters. That's all I'm saying. It matters to people."

"To people," Elin said softly.

"Yes."

"Not to you."

The kettle began to sing. Neither of them moved toward it. The little kitchen held them the way the sea had held the wrecked boat — lifting, dropping, refusing to decide.

"Elin." Yara wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture Elin had watched ten thousand times across eleven years and two lifetimes, and something in her face gave way — not all the way, just a seam opening, a sliver of light under a door. "When the siren went today, I was proofing tomorrow's loaves. And I dropped everything and I stood at the window until the boat cleared the breakwater, and then I couldn't stay inside, so I walked to the seawall, and I stood there in the rain like an idiot for an hour, telling myself I'd have done the same for anyone. For Jonas. For old Sørensen. For anyone." Her voice dropped. "And then your boat went sideways on the shoal and I stopped breathing, and I thought: this is not what I do for anyone. This is what I've done for eleven years. Every siren. Every time. And I hate it. I hate that I—"

She cut herself off. Turned away. Took the kettle off the heat with hands that were not quite steady, and poured two cups of coffee that neither of them had asked for, and the seam closed, and she was controlled again, brittle as sugar work.

"Forget that," she said. "You're soaked and half-dead and I'm talking nonsense. Drink your coffee. There's cinnamon buns that failed — too much cardamom — take them home."

"Yara."

"Don't." She set the cup down in front of Elin, and her hand stopped, just above Elin's on the table — an inch of air between them, charged, trembling, the closest they had come to touch in eleven years and three weeks. Elin could feel the heat of her skin like a small sun. One centimeter. One decision. "Please," Yara whispered, and it wasn't clear whether she was begging Elin to reach out or not to, and maybe it wasn't clear to her either.

The inch of air held. Held. Held.

Then the kitchen timer went off — the oven, the night's last batch, shrill and stupid and saving — and Yara snatched her hand back like a thief, and the moment broke and scattered across the tiles like flour.

Elin drank the coffee. It was terrible, over-extracted, made by someone whose mind had been elsewhere for days. It was the best thing Elin had ever tasted. At the door, pulling on a coat that was still wet, Elin paused with a hand on the frame and said, quietly, not turning around:

"The date on that postcard, Yara. I did the math. Three days."

Behind them, silence — the particular silence of someone who has stopped breathing.

"Goodnight, Elin," Yara said, in a voice like a door closing.

Elin walked home through the dying storm with wet shoes and a heart going like an engine at full throttle, and did not sleep at all, and in the morning sent a message to the station office requesting the extra shifts nobody wanted — because if the choice was going to be made, it would not be made in that kitchen, an inch from her hand, with the whole sea still between them.
