## 13. Midsummer, On Fire

The morning of midsummer began the way all Saltvik mornings did in late June — with light that refused to end and a town that pretended it didn't notice. Yara was at the café by three-thirty, proofing dough in the blue half-dark that wasn't dark at all, listening to the gulls argue over the harbor like old men disputing a card game. The ovens ticked as they heated. The radio murmured weather: fair, fair, fair, then wind from the southwest by evening, nothing to worry about.

Nothing to worry about. She almost laughed. There was always something to worry about. The bank notice was folded in her apron pocket where it had lived for six days now, soft as cloth from handling, its edges furred. *Auction date set.* She had not told anyone. Not Jonas, who'd been coming around more often than his plumbing work strictly required, fixing things she hadn't asked him to fix — the wobbly stool by the window, the hinge on the cold cabinet, once, inexplicably, the latch on the back door, which had never been broken. Not the developer, Henrik Voss, whose calls she let ring out and whose flowers she'd stopped putting in water, letting them brown in their cellophane on the counter like small accusations.

And not Elin. Definitely not Elin.

She kneaded. That was the discipline of it, the mercy of it: dough did not ask questions. Dough only wanted what dough had always wanted — warmth, time, hands that didn't flinch. Eleven years of practice had made her hands steady even when everything behind them was shaking. She pressed her palms into the soft resistance and thought, as she always thought, *the heart is trainable muscle,* and for the first time in weeks the sentence felt less like philosophy and more like a prayer she was tired of saying.

By seven the café smelled like a promise. By nine, Jonas arrived carrying crates of strawberries and an expression of elaborate innocence.

"I brought reinforcements," he said, setting them down. "Also I may have volunteered you for the whole festival catering thing."

"You *volunteered* me?"

"The town voted. Democratically." He was already tying on one of her spare aprons, which was too short for him and made him look like a large, apologetic child. "Maja said if we don't feed the dancers there'll be a riot. You don't want a riot on your conscience, do you? On top of everything else."

Yara looked up sharply. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing," Jonas said, too fast, and busied himself hulling strawberries with the concentration of a man defusing a bomb.

She let it go. There wasn't time. There was never time anymore — every hour since the wall came down had been an hour of circling, of near-misses, of Elin appearing in doorways and disappearing before anything could be said, of the photo of a postcard Yara had burned sitting somewhere out there in the world like a splinter working toward the heart. She had spent eleven years learning to live with silence. She had not accounted for how loud silence could become when someone else was keeping it too.

* * *

The festival assembled itself through the afternoon the way it always did — the way it had done since before Yara was born, since before her mother was born, probably, since some ancestor dragged the first maypole upright and dared the sea to object. Bunting went up between the chandlery and the fish shed. Maja Halvorsen directed traffic with a clipboard and the authority of a woman who had organized this event for thirty years and would organize it from beyond the grave if permitted. Children ran wild with flower crowns askew. Someone's accordion started up, tentative, then committed.

The café's stall stood at the edge of the square: cardamom buns, strawberry tarts, coffee strong enough to stand a spoon in. Yara worked it with Jonas, and the line never quite died, and the sun hung low and golden and refused, obstinately, to set.

Elin came by at four.

Yara saw them before she saw them properly — a tall figure in the crowd moving against the current of it, coast guard uniform traded for a plain shirt, sleeves rolled, hair doing what it always did in salt air. Her hands went still on the tart tray. Eleven years, and her body still recognized Elin Marsh before her mind agreed to.

"Coffee," Elin said, arriving at the counter. "Please. If it's not too much trouble."

"Trouble is my specialty."

Something flickered across Elin's face — the ghost of a smile, quickly disciplined. They paid with exact change. Their fingers did not touch; both of them arranged it so they wouldn't, with a care that was itself a kind of touching.

"Busy day?" Elin asked.

"Every day's busy. It's a festival."

"Right." Elin looked at the tarts, the buns, the steam rising off the urn. Looked anywhere but at her. "You look—" A pause. Recalculation. "The stall looks good."

"Thanks. I built it myself. Out of spite."

That got the smile loose, briefly, before Elin caught it again. "Spite holds up well," they said. "I've noticed."

They stood there in the noise of the square while the accordion wheezed through a waltz, and the distance between them was exactly one counter wide and exactly eleven years deep, and neither of them crossed it. Then someone called Elin's name — a colleague, a flare of orange uniform at the crowd's edge — and Elin lifted two fingers off the counter in a gesture that managed to be both farewell and apology, and was gone into the current of people.

Jonas appeared at Yara's elbow holding a tray. "So," he said. "That went well."

"Shut up and carry tarts."

* * *

Evening came without darkness. At ten the sky was still the color of a bruise healing — violet at the edges, gold at the seam of the horizon, the sun a red coin hovering just below the tree line of the far islands, refusing, like everyone else in Saltvik, to actually leave. The bonfire was stacked on the point beyond the seawall: birch and driftwood, twenty feet of it, built over three days by men who took the construction of fire very seriously.

They lit it at eleven. The flames went up with a sound like a held breath released, and the heat reached the crowd in a wave, and everyone cheered, and the dancing started on the grass in the long twilight, couples turning in slow circles to music that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere.

Yara stayed at the stall long after it should have closed, wiping down surfaces that were already clean. Jonas had gone off with Maja's granddaughter, looking pleased with himself. The town had drifted toward the fire. And Elin—

Elin was standing at the seawall alone.

Yara saw them from across the square, silhouetted against the glow, hands in pockets, watching the fire the way people watch the sea: like it might give something back. And she could have gone home. That was the truth she would turn over later, at three in the morning, in the blue dark of her kitchen — she could have gone home, and locked the door, and kept her heart proofed another year, another decade, safe and sour and intact. The path home was right there. It was always right there.

She walked toward the fire instead.

The heat hit her ten meters out, pressing against her face like a hand. Up close the fire was enormous, roaring, throwing sparks up into the pale sky where they vanished without ever quite dying. Elin turned when her shadow fell across them.

"Hi," Elin said.

"Hi."

"You closed the stall."

"Maja threatened me. Something about health codes and joylessness."

Elin laughed — actually laughed, short and startled out of them, and the sound did something to Yara's chest that she chose not to examine. They stood side by side at the seawall, close enough that the fire's heat and the sea's cold met along the line between them, and watched the flames eat the driftwood.

"I used to hate this holiday," Elin said after a while. "When I was a kid. Too many people. Too much noise."

"And now?"

"Now I think—" They stopped. Started again. "Now I think I spent a lot of years being afraid of the wrong things."

Yara's throat tightened. "That sounds like a confession."

"It's a draft," Elin said. "Of one."

The sparks rose. Somewhere behind them the music changed tempo, and the crowd cheered at something, and none of it mattered. Yara became aware, gradually and then completely, that Elin had turned toward her, and that the space between them had shrunk to the width of a decision.

"Yara." Elin's voice was low, roughened by smoke or something else. "There's something I've been trying to say for—"

"Don't." But her feet didn't move. "Don't say it here. Don't say it like this."

"Why not?"

"Because if you say it, I'll have to answer, and I'm not—" Her breath caught. "I'm not sure I remember how."

"Then don't answer," Elin said. "Just—"

They kissed her.

Or she kissed them. Later, neither of them would be able to say, and it wouldn't matter, because it happened — Elin's hand coming up to cradle her jaw like something breakable and precious, Yara's fingers closing in the front of Elin's shirt, and the kiss was soft at first and then not soft at all, eleven years of it arriving all at once, tasting of woodsmoke and salt and the specific sweetness of things too long delayed. The fire roared. The sparks climbed. Somewhere very far away the accordion played on, and the town danced, and nobody saw them, and everybody would have understood.

Yara felt something in her chest crack open — not painfully. That was the terrifying part. It cracked open the way soil cracks open in spring, the way a proofed loaf splits along its scored lines, deliberately, beautifully, because it was always meant to. She made a sound against Elin's mouth that she hadn't made in eleven years, and Elin answered it, and for one whole shining moment Yara Nystrom stood at a bonfire in the town where she'd been born and let herself want things.

Then the moment ended.

It ended the way these moments always ended in her — not with a fade but with a snap, the old alarm tripping, the voice that sounded so much like sense: *this is how it starts, this is exactly how it started last time, you open the door and then they leave and you're standing in the doorway holding everything.* Elin's thumb was tracing her cheekbone and it felt like a promise and promises were the currency of people who left, and Elin had a transfer letter, everyone knew it, Bergen, permanent, abroad, *leaving*, and Yara had kissed them anyway, had opened the door, and now—

She pulled back.

Elin's face in the firelight: wrecked and hopeful and utterly unguarded, and it was that hope that did it, that undid her, because hope was a debt and she knew how those got collected.

"Elin—"

"Don't." Elin's voice had gone careful. "Whatever you're about to say—"

"You're leaving." It came out flat. Cruel in its flatness. "In three weeks. That's the plan, isn't it? That's always the plan. You people—" She heard herself saying it and couldn't stop, the words arriving like water through a burst pipe, everything she'd dammed for eleven years finding the crack. "You decide, and you pack, and you go, and the rest of us are supposed to just keep the lights on. My whole life I've watched people leave this island like it's a waiting room. And I stayed. I *stayed*, Elin. I stayed and I built something and I am not going to stand at another dock waving at another boat because you got warm by my fire for five minutes and called it love."

The words landed. She watched them land. Elin's face didn't collapse — that would have been easier — it simply closed, feature by feature, like a house being shuttered for winter, and Yara stood in the awful silence knowing she had just done the unforgivable thing, knowing it even as the last word left her, unable to reach out and take any of it back.

"You're right," Elin said quietly. "You're right about one thing. I am leaving. That was the plan." A pause. The fire popped; sparks scattered. "Goodnight, Yara."

They walked away down the seawall, unhurried, straight-backed, and did not look back, and Yara stood alone in the firelight with her hands shaking and the taste of woodsmoke still on her mouth, understanding that she had finally said the rehearsed sentence — just the wrong one.

Behind her the bonfire burned on, magnificent and indifferent, and the town danced, and the sun hovered below the horizon without ever fully setting, and somewhere out in the dark water the last ferry of the season waited at its mooring, patient as fate, three weeks from sailing.

Yara walked home the long way, past the dark café, past the new wall that still smelled of fresh timber, and did not cry until she was inside with the door locked and the oven cold.

Then she sat on the kitchen floor, in the blue midnight that never quite became night, and let herself drown a little.
