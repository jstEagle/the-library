## 1. Water Damage

The leak announced itself the way all disasters in Bellbird Bay do: politely, and then all at once.

I noticed it on a Tuesday in late September, which is the season when the sky over this town stops pretending to be blue and commits fully to a low, bruised grey that makes even the post office look like a setting from a gothic novel. I was shelving a returns cart — three cozy mysteries, one memoir by a man who'd walked the length of New Zealand to avoid his feelings, and a romance with a shirtless lighthouse keeper on the cover that I had personally hand-sold eleven copies of — when I heard it. A soft, rhythmic drip. The kind of sound you can ignore for exactly as long as it takes you to realize you've been ignoring it.

It was coming from above the romance section.

Of course it was. If the universe has a sense of humor — and eleven years of running a bookshop in a town where the annual festival features a competitive chutney division suggests it does — then of course the water would find its way through my ceiling directly above the shelves where people come looking for happy endings. There's a metaphor in there. I declined to examine it.

"Paper towels," I said out loud, to no one, because talking to myself is one of the few perks of being both owner and sole weekday employee of Nystrom's Books, est. 1998, surviving, barely.

The shop sits on Harbour Lane between the bakery that smells like forgiveness and the hardware store that smells like decisions. It's narrow and deep, with a mezzanine at the back that Wren claimed as her own territory around age nine and has defended ever since with the ferocity of a small sovereign nation. The floors are original kauri, which the real estate agent would call "characterful" and I call "expensive to fix." The ceiling above romance is not original. Nothing about that ceiling is anything but a problem.

Here's what you need to know about me, since we're apparently going to be spending some time together: my name is Juniper Nystrom, I am thirty-eight years old, and I have spent the last eleven years constructing a life so carefully ordered that even my chaos is filed alphabetically. I open the shop at nine. I make coffee at nine-fifteen — real coffee, because Dot next door says instant is how they punish people in prison and I believe her. I shelve, I sell, I recommend, I close at five-thirty, I walk four blocks home to the little weatherboard house where my daughter does her homework at the kitchen table and pretends she can't hear me when I ask about her day. On Sundays I inventory. On the first Monday of every month I pay the rent and feel a sensation best described as a small animal dying quietly behind my sternum.

This is the routine. The routine is the point. The routine is load-bearing.

So when water started dripping through my ceiling onto the romance section, I did what any reasonable person would do. I put a bucket under it, moved the stock, called a roofer who told me he could come "sometime after the long weekend," and then, because waiting is not among my talents, I climbed up into the ceiling space myself the following morning with a torch clenched in my teeth like a pirate and a profound sense that this was a bad idea.

* * *

The ceiling space above the shop is not a place for human beings. It's a place for insulation, rat skeletons, and the previous owner's abandoned opinions about wiring. I crawled along on boards laid over the joists, following the sound of dripping, and found the culprit within ten minutes: a cracked flashing around the old vent pipe, letting rain in every time the sky remembered us. The water had been pooling on the plasterboard directly above the romance section, finding a seam, and taking its time coming down. Patient water. Water with a grudge.

I patched what I could patch with the roll of flashing tape I'd brought up, which is a bit like putting a band-aid on a shark bite but made me feel competent, and then I lay back on the joists for a moment to catch my breath and listen to the building. Old buildings talk if you let them. This one mostly complains.

That's when I saw it. Below me, through a gap where the plasterboard had sagged away from a joist — water damage again, older than this week's leak, older maybe than Wren — a sliver of the space between the ceiling and the floorboards of the attic proper. And wedged into that gap, visible only from directly underneath, at an angle no reasonable person would ever be lying at:

A corner of something metal. Rusted orange. Round.

Now. Let me be honest about the sequence of events here, because honesty is going to matter later and I might as well practice.

I did not think, *oh, a tin, how curious.* I thought nothing. That was the remarkable thing. My brain, presented with a mysterious object hidden in the fabric of my own building, simply declined to process it, the way your brain declines to process the word "moist." I finished my patching. I climbed down. I sold a cookbook to a woman named Bev who is on her fourth attempt at sourdough and her first attempt at leaving her husband, and I recommended her a novel about a woman who burns her life down and opens a bakery, and I felt the small warm glow of doing my job well.

And then, at four o'clock, with the shop empty and the light going amber through the front windows, I got the stepladder, went back up into the ceiling space, and pried loose the warped floorboard above the romance section with the flat end of a crowbar I keep for exactly this kind of decision I'm going to regret.

The board came up with a groan. Underneath: dust, a dead spider of respectable size, and the tin.

It was a biscuit tin. The kind that used to hold shortbread, back when tins were tins and not shrink-wrapped disappointment. Someone had painted it once — I could see traces of faded teal under the rust — and the lid was stuck fast with eleven years of silence. I worked it loose with my thumb, expecting, I don't know. Old Christmas decorations. Someone's lost buttons. The previous owner's opinions about wiring, in physical form.

Inside was a letter.

One envelope, soft-edged with damp, warped by water that had gotten in somewhere along the way — the same patient water, maybe, or just the slow humidity of a coastal town working at it year after year. The paper had buckled. The ink had feathered at the edges of certain words, so that parts of it looked like it had been written by someone crying, which — well. Hold that thought.

And the handwriting on the envelope was mine.

I want to describe what that feels like, seeing your own handwriting on something you have no memory of, but the closest I can get is this: it's like hearing your own voice on a recording. Wrong and right at once. Familiar in a way that makes your stomach drop, because you know that hand, you know the way I cross my j's with a little flourish I picked up in Year Nine to seem interesting, you know the pressure I bear down with when I mean something.

The address line said: **Marisol Vega, c/o Mrs. Halloran's Boarding House, Harbour Lane.**

The date, in the top right corner, in my own careful hand, was eleven years and two months ago.

Three days before Marisol left Bellbird Bay for good.

* * *

I sat up in the ceiling space for a long time. Long enough for the light through the vent to shift from amber to grey. Long enough that when I finally came down the ladder, my knee cracked in a way that suggested I'd been up there making poor life choices, which was accurate.

Here's the thing about Marisol Vega: she is a closed door in the house of my history, and I am the person who painted that door shut myself, coat after coat after coat, until opening it became structurally impossible. Eleven years ago she left this town — left me, though the grammar of that sentence is contested — and I stayed, and I raised our daughter, and I built the routine, and I did not write letters, and I did not look at the boarding house, and when Wren asked questions I answered them in the warm, vague, useless language of parents everywhere: *She loved you very much. She had to go. Sometimes grown-ups have to go.*

I never wrote letters. So whose handwriting —

No. I knew whose handwriting. That was the whole problem. I knew it the way you know your own face in a dark window.

I remember now — the memory arriving like the water had arrived, politely and then all at once — I remember writing it. One night in October, eleven years ago, in the flat above what was then half bookshop, half building site, with Wren asleep in the next room at age five and the rent notice pinned to the fridge and Marisol's departure already scheduled like a train. I remember writing until two in the morning. I remember sealing the envelope. I remember standing at the letterbox on Harbour Lane at dawn with it in my hand.

And I remember deciding not to send it. Not dramatically. Not with music swelling. I remember thinking, with terrible clarity, *if I send this, she'll stay, and if she stays, we will not survive the winter,* because the shop was failing and the rent was due and love, however enormous, does not pay rates. I remember choosing survival. I remember telling myself it was the brave choice, and then telling myself it was the cowardly one, and then, eventually, telling myself nothing at all, which is the trick. That's the trick that works.

But I don't remember hiding it in the ceiling.

Or — no. Maybe I do. Maybe some part of me, the part that couldn't throw it out and couldn't send it, had climbed up here with the tin and the crowbar and buried it the way you bury something alive, meaning to dig it up later, meaning to forget, succeeding at forgetting for eleven years until the water — the patient, grudge-holding water — came to dig it up for me.

I put the tin in my bag. I did not read the letter. I want that on the record: I carried it around my own shop for the rest of the afternoon and I did not read it, because reading it felt like picking at a scab that had finally, finally stopped bleeding, and I am a person who knows better.

At five-fifteen, Wren came in through the front door with the specific energy of a sixteen-year-old who has had A Day, dropped her school bag in the middle of the biography section — a territorial act, deliberately provocative — and asked what was for dinner.

"Pasta," I said.

"We had pasta yesterday."

"It's a series."

She rolled her eyes, which is her primary language these days, and went to flick through the new arrivals, and I watched her — tall now, taller than me since March, with Marisol's exact frown and my stubborn chin — and I felt the weight of the tin in my bag like a stone, like a heart, like a thing that had been buried and was now, against all my careful work, above ground.

"Wren," I said. "Do you ever think about her? Your other mum?"

The question came out of me sideways, uninvited, the way the water had come through the ceiling. She looked up from a paperback. For a second her face did something complicated — something young and hungry underneath the armor — and then the armor came back down.

"Not really," she said, which was a lie, and we both knew it was a lie, and neither of us said so, because that is also our language, that fluent dialect of protective silence we've been speaking her whole life.

"Pasta it is," I said.

* * *

That night, after dishes, after Wren retreated upstairs with her headphones and her mysteries, I sat at the kitchen table with the tin in front of me under the one lamp I hadn't turned off yet, and I tried to burn it.

I want to be clear: I actually tried. I took it out to the yard, to the incinerator drum Dot gave me years ago for exactly this category of problem, and I struck a match, and I held the envelope over the flame, and the flame licked up the side of it and I pulled it back before it caught more than a scorch mark on one corner, because my hands, apparently, had voted independently of my head.

Fine. Fine. Some things you don't burn. Some things you put back in the tin and carry inside and set on the kitchen table and stare at while the kettle boils itself dry, which is what happened, and which Wren will tell you happens more often in our house than strictly safe.

Under the lamp, the water damage was worse than it had seemed in the ceiling space. Whole lines of the letter had bloomed and blurred, the ink feathering outward like frost on glass. But some of it was legible. Some of it was too legible.

I read the first line. Just the first line. I'm telling you this like it was restraint; it wasn't. It was fear wearing restraint's coat.

*Marisol. I'm writing this at 2 a.m. because if I say it out loud you'll hear my voice shake, and if you hear my voice shake you'll stay, and I can't ask you to stay—*

I put it down. I put it face-down, like it was a card I'd been dealt and couldn't afford to look at. I made tea I didn't drink. I went to bed and lay awake listening to the house settle and thinking about the shape of a sentence I'd written eleven years ago and abandoned mid-clause, and about how the roof above the romance section had been leaking, probably, for years — patient water, working its way down through the layers of my careful, closed-off, alphabetized life — and how it had finally, this Tuesday in late September, dripped through.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I'll decide what to do with it.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I'll burn it or read it or bury it deeper, and everything will go back to normal, and the festival will come and go, and the routine will hold, because the routine always holds.

In my bag by the door, the tin sat in the dark with its scorched letter inside, and if tins can wait, it waited.

They can. I know that now.
