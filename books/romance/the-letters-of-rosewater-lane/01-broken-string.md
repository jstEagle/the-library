## 1. Broken String

The taxi could not fit down Rosewater Lane, so Astrid Winterbourne walked the last hundred yards with a cello case on her back and two suitcases dragging through the salt-damp gravel, feeling like a woman fleeing a crime scene with the evidence strapped to her spine.

The case had not been opened in ten years. She knew this the way one knows the weight of a grudge — intimately, constantly, without wanting to. The fiberglass shell was scuffed at the shoulder where she'd once dropped it backstage in Vienna, and the latches were furred green at the hinges from a decade of attic air. Inside, if the strings hadn't rotted through entirely, was a Matteo Goffriller copy her father had bought her at nineteen, when she still believed talent was a kind of armor.

She did not look at it as she walked. She had gotten very good, over ten years, at not looking at things.

Number 9 Rosewater Lane was smaller than the photographs. That was what she got for renting from a letting agent who used wide-angle lenses and the word *charming* as a legal term. The cottage was a single story of whitewashed stone hunched between two taller neighbors like a shy child in a school photograph. A blue door. A window box containing, as far as she could tell, three dead geraniums and one defiant survivor. The sea was audible but not visible — a long exhale somewhere beyond the rooftops, patient as a metronome.

She set the cases down on the step and stood there a moment, key in hand, listening to that sound. Somewhere behind her ribs, an old muscle memory stirred — the counting-in of an orchestra, the breath before a downbeat — and she killed it the way you press out a flame with your thumb.

The door opened onto a room that smelled of other people's lives: lavender polish, old toast, the ghost of a dog. A sofa. A fireplace. A kitchen nook with a table scarred by rings from a hundred cups. Through the back window, a strip of garden gone wild, and past it, a sliver of grey water.

It would do. It would have to. Her savings had exactly eleven months left, and she had chosen this town off a map because it was far from London, far from Berlin, far from anyone who might have seen the video. Fourteen million views, the last time she'd checked, which had been four years ago, which had been the last time she'd allowed herself to check anything at all.

She carried the cello case inside and set it against the wall beneath the window, upright, facing the corner. Like a child being punished. Like a witness.

Then she unpacked the kettle first, because some rituals survive everything.

* * *

Mrs. Okonkwo arrived within the hour, which Astrid would later learn was not coincidence but infrastructure. The woman materialized at the garden gate carrying a tin of shortbread and the bearing of someone conducting a census.

"You're the cellist," Mrs. Okonkwo said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody," Astrid said.

"Mm." Mrs. Okonkwo looked at the cello case visible through the window, then at Astrid's hands — long-fingered, nail-bitten, the calluses faded to ghosts after ten years of disuse but still shaped by their history, the way a riverbed remembers water. "I'm Adaeze Okonkwo. Number 12. I've been here thirty-one years, so I'll tell you how things work, and you can pretend to resent it."

Astrid, against every instinct honed across a decade of avoiding people, almost smiled. "That's fair."

"Good." The tin landed in her hands. "Shortbread. My granddaughter's recipe, my oven, so blame is shared. Now." And from somewhere in the depths of her cardigan — Astrid never did establish where; the cardigan seemed to operate on principles unknown to physics — Mrs. Okonkwo produced a cream-colored envelope and a pen.

"What's this?"

"The Lane Letters. Community post exchange. You write to a stranger, a stranger writes to you. Names withheld, addresses handled by the postmistress, Mrs. Ferreira, who has kept every secret in this town since 1987 including two she probably shouldn't have." Mrs. Okonkwo held out the form the way one offers a contract to a person already surrounded. "Everyone on the lane does it. Well. Everyone except Mr. Prewitt, and he doesn't count, he thinks the telephone was a mistake."

"I'm not really—" Astrid began.

"It's anonymous," Mrs. Okonkwo said, with the mild implacability of a tide. "No one knows who you are. No one knows what you've done or haven't done. You write on Tuesday, you post by Friday, and whatever you are in the rest of your life, in the letters you can be something else." She paused, and for one unguarded moment her eyes were not census-taking at all but something older and kinder. "New arrivals especially need it. This town looks quiet from the outside. From the inside it's full of people who came here to be someone different. The letters are where they practice."

Astrid looked at the form. *Name (for the postmistress only):* — a small cruelty, that line. *What should your correspondent know about you?*

Nothing, she thought. Nothing at all. That was rather the entire architecture of her life now.

"I don't write letters," she said. "I don't— I keep to myself. That's why I came here."

"Yes," Mrs. Okonkwo agreed pleasantly, watching her. "So I've noticed, in the forty minutes since you arrived."

The sea exhaled beyond the roofs. Somewhere down the lane a gull screamed its one note, flat, uncorrected. Astrid became aware that she was holding the shortbread tin like a shield and the pen was already in her other hand, and she could not remember taking it.

She told herself, later, that she signed only to be left alone. That the whole thing was a transaction — one signature purchased, presumably, several months of peace, or at least a reduction in visits. She told herself the trembling in her fingers was tiredness from the train.

But there was a moment — brief, treacherous — when the pen hovered over the blank space marked *Your correspondent will know you as:* and she felt something move under the numbness. Something small and warm and horrifyingly like curiosity. Somewhere out there was a stranger who would know her only as whatever name she invented. Who would receive her words without the fourteen million views playing behind them. Who could not look at her hands and see the shaking.

She wrote: *Rosalind.*

It was the name of a character in a novel she'd loved at fifteen. It was also, she realized as she wrote it, the name of a woman who had never failed at anything, because she had never existed.

Mrs. Okonkwo took the form, folded it once, and vanished it back into the cardigan. "First letter goes out Tuesday. Mrs. Ferreira makes the matches on the first of the month, so you'll hear by the week after." She was already turning toward the gate. "There's fish on Thursdays from the van on the corner. Don't buy the mackerel, it's been looking at people."

"Mrs. Okonkwo."

The woman turned. Late light caught the silver in her braids.

"Why do you do this? The letters. Really."

Mrs. Okonkwo considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, which was more than Astrid had intended to give it.

"Because everyone in this town is carrying something they can't say out loud," she said. "And paper is very patient." Then she lifted a hand — a small, imperial wave — and was gone up the lane, her footsteps unhurried, a woman who had planted a seed and knew better than to stand around watching the soil.

* * *

That night the cottage made its unfamiliar sounds — beams settling, pipes knocking, the sea keeping its endless time below — and Astrid lay in a bed that smelled of someone else's detergent and stared at the ceiling.

Ten years ago tonight, give or take a fortnight, she had walked onto a stage in a green dress and played the Elgar Concerto before two thousand people and a jury of five, and somewhere in the second movement her hands had simply — stopped. Not trembled. *Stopped.* As if a switch had been thrown somewhere behind her sternum. The silence that followed lasted nine seconds. She had counted them. She still counted them, sometimes, in the dark: nine seconds of two thousand people learning, in real time, that the thing they'd paid to see was not going to happen.

She had finished the movement, technically. Technically was the word the reviews used. *Technically proficient, emotionally absent.* One sentence ended her: a jury chairman's verdict read aloud at the competition close, dry as a receipt. *Some performers plateau. Miss Winterbourne has, we fear, begun to descend.*

Fourteen million people watched the silence. Only a few hundred ever learned what the verdict was. She had spent ten years making sure the two groups never overlapped.

Now she sat up in the dark, bare feet on cold floorboards, and looked at the cello case in the corner. Moonlight came through the window and lay along its curve like a hand.

*I don't play anymore,* she thought, directing it at the case, at the moonlight, at the town, at whoever might be listening. *Whatever the form says. Whatever Rosalind writes. That part isn't available.*

The case said nothing. Cases are patient too.

She got back into bed. But sleep, when it finally took her near dawn, was full of a strange and specific image: an envelope moving through the dark — through sorting rooms, down lanes, over water — traveling toward someone she would never meet, carrying the name of a woman who had never failed at anything.

And underneath the dread, quieter, unwelcome, warming her like a coal cupped in both hands:

She wondered what he — or she — would be like. Her stranger. The one person alive who would know Rosalind and no one else.

On Tuesday, she discovered, she would have to write something.

She had absolutely no idea what.
