## 14. Snowmelt

**JUDE**

The thaw came on a Friday, the way good news and bad news both tended to arrive in Sandpiper Point—without warning and slightly inconvenient.

One day the town had been locked in gray ice, gulls standing on the harbor like ornaments nobody wanted to put away, and the next there was water running in the gutters, dripping off the bakery's awning in a rhythm so cheerful it felt personal. Jude stood at the kitchen window with her coffee and watched it, and caught herself smiling before she remembered she was supposed to be guarding her heart like a recipe card with no backup copy.

Ten days since the greenhouse. Ten days since Delia had swept in smelling of expensive perfume and municipal ambition, and Marcus's name had glowed on her phone screen like something radioactive. Ten days since Theo had stepped back—actually stepped back, hands raised, giving her room—and said, "Whenever you're ready. Or never. Both are fine."

He'd meant it, too. That was the maddening part. Most men would have pushed. Theo Marchetti just kept showing up at the bakery at seven a.m. sharp with pastries from the place two towns over—"because yours aren't ready yet," he'd said, which was either an insult or a promise, and she still hadn't decided.

They were ten days out from Camille Voss's final venue walkthrough, three weeks from the wedding itself, and the impossible list had somehow become merely improbable. The winter garden had been located—a glass conservatory at the old Harrow estate that Camille's people had declared "rustic-luxe adjacent" with the tone of judges awarding a mercy point. The orchestra had been booked. The cake tasting was scheduled for Monday.

Which meant today, Saturday, was the first day in six weeks that nothing was on fire.

"You're staring at the weather like it owes you money," Theo said from behind her. He'd let himself in with the spare key she'd given him for deliveries, because apparently that was a thing she did now. He was holding two coffees and wearing a scarf that suggested someone had knitted it as a dare.

"I'm appreciating the weather," she corrected. "There's a difference."

"There is. One of them involves you leaving the building." He set a cup beside her hand. "It's fifty-two degrees. In February. The entire Eastern Seaboard is confused, and I think we should be confused with it. Walk with me."

Jude looked at the beach road, where snowmelt ran silver down the center line like the town was crying with relief. She thought about all the reasons to say no—the wedding, the lease, the lawyer's letter sitting in her desk drawer with its terrible fine print, the fact that the last time she'd walked this beach with this man she'd been twenty-four and stupid in ways she was still paying interest on.

"Fine," she said. "But if this is a ploy to discuss seating charts, I'm walking into the ocean."

"Noted. I'll bring weights."

* * *

The beach at Sandpiper Point in thaw season was not the postcard version. The sand was dark and heavy with meltwater, ridged where the ice had pushed it around all winter like a slow animal turning over in its sleep. Driftwood lay everywhere, pale and stripped. The tide was doing something complicated and unbothered.

They walked with their coats open, which felt scandalous after weeks of arctic air, and for a while neither of them talked, which was its own kind of conversation. Jude had forgotten how much silence could weigh when it wasn't loaded. With Marcus, quiet had always been a held breath, a prelude to some negotiation. With Theo, it was just... space. Room to exist.

"The gulls are judging us," he observed finally.

"Gulls judge everyone. It's their whole thing. Gran used to say they were the town's original planning committee—all opinions, no permits."

Theo laughed, and the sound went through her sideways, catching on things. "I keep forgetting you're funny. You present very serious in meetings. It's intimidating. I've watched vendors apologize to you for things that weren't even mistakes."

"That's called competence, Marchetti."

"It's called terrifying, and I mean that as a compliment." He kicked gently at a piece of driftwood. Then, quieter: "Can I ask you something? Not a work thing."

Jude braced without meaning to. "That depends on the question."

"When you left here—after your grandmother's funeral, after everything—you didn't just leave Sandpiper Point. You left the whole... category. Weddings, love, the entire genre. I've always assumed that was about Marcus. But watching you these past weeks, I don't think it started with him." He kept his eyes on the horizon, giving her the courtesy of not being watched while she decided whether to answer. "When did you stop believing in it?"

The waves came in and pulled back, patient. Jude walked several steps before she trusted her voice.

"It wasn't one moment," she said. "Everyone wants it to be one moment. The broken engagement, the cold feet, whatever story makes it tidy. But it was more like..." She searched for it. "You know how a recipe fails sometimes? Same ingredients, same oven, and something just doesn't come together, and you can't ever fully explain why? That was my childhood. My parents loved each other right up until they catastrophically didn't, and everyone acted like it was weather. Nobody's fault. Just a front that moved through. And then Gran and Pops were this—" her throat tightened unexpectedly— "this forty-eight-year love story, and I kept thinking, what made them different? What's the ingredient? And I couldn't find it. So I decided the recipe was a myth. That some people got a different flour or something, and I wasn't one of them."

"And planning weddings?"

"Paying rent in a country I don't live in." She heard herself say it and winced. "God. That sounded better in my head."

"No, it didn't. It sounded exactly true, which is worse." Theo stopped walking. The wind pushed at his hair, and he looked younger suddenly, less like the polished rival who'd once dismantled her career over a single client dinner and more like the boy who'd once sat across from her at the bakery counter eating burnt shortbread and pretending it was delicious. "For what it's worth—I don't think it's a myth. I think most people just measure wrong. They look for the grand gesture, the storm-and-kiss moment, when really it's..." He gestured vaguely between them, at the beach, at the last six weeks of flour fights and midnight takeout and vendor crises solved shoulder to shoulder. "It's this. Showing up. Again. When nothing's on fire and there's no reason except that you want to."

Jude's heart did something unauthorized.

"That's a very convenient theory," she managed, "coming from a man who once showed up specifically to take things from me."

"Yes," he said simply. "It is. I'm not going to pretend I have clean hands, Jude. I've stopped trying to earn my way back to that night. I'm just trying to be worth the next one."

The tide filled the silence. Somewhere down the beach, a dog barked at the ocean on principle.

"Walk with me to the point?" she asked.

"I was going to suggest it. But you got there first, and honestly it's better this way. Your idea. Your call." He smiled. "I've learned that about you. You need it to be your idea."

"I need it to be *true*," she said. "Those aren't the same thing."

"No," he agreed softly. "But lately they've been overlapping."

* * *

**THEO**

She was doing the thing again—the thing where she got close to happy and then flinched, like happiness was a hot pan. Theo had spent six weeks cataloguing her tells the way he used to study competitor bids: the way she laughed a half-second before deciding to, the way she deflected compliments onto the croissants, the way she'd gone very still in the greenhouse before Delia's car crunched up the drive, as if joy were a sound she needed to identify before letting it in.

He wasn't going to rush her. He'd made that mistake once, ten years ago, in the other direction—he'd rushed himself, rushed the choice, chosen the job in New York because wanting her openly had felt too much like handing someone a weapon. He'd told her that in the storm, in the candlelight, and she hadn't forgiven him, exactly. But she'd stayed in the room. Lately she stayed in rooms with him voluntarily, which he'd come to understand was the highest honor Jude Brightwater bestowed.

At the point, the beach curved toward the old lighthouse path, and the meltwater had pooled in the dune hollows, each one holding a perfect piece of sky. She stopped at the largest one and looked down at it.

"Gran used to bring me here after storms," Jude said. "We'd count the pools. She called them the sky's leftovers. She said—" Jude's voice caught, and she laughed at herself, embarrassed. "Sorry. It's stupid."

"It's not stupid. Tell me."

"She said the sky leaves pieces of itself behind on purpose. So that even after the storm moves on, something blue stays." Jude stared at the pool. "I used to think that was just a nice thing grandmothers say. Now I think she was leaving me instructions."

Theo's chest ached. "Jude—"

"Don't." She said it gently but firmly, one hand half-raised. "If you say something kind right now I'm going to do something reckless, and I haven't finished being sensible yet. Give me a minute."

So he gave her a minute. He stood on a cold beach in February and watched the woman he'd loved since he was twenty-three decide, breath by breath, whether to be brave, and he understood with total clarity that this was the actual event of his life. Not the Meridian account. Not any wedding. This. This minute, this wind, this woman weighing her own heart like something breakable and precious, which it was.

"Sensible's finished," she announced finally, turning to face him. Her eyes were wet and furious about it. "Here's the thing, Theo. I want to kiss you. I have wanted to kiss you since approximately the flour incident, possibly since the storm, possibly—" her jaw tightened— "possibly since I was twenty-four and you broke my heart so thoroughly that I built an entire personality out of the wreckage. And I hate that. I hate wanting things, because in my experience wanting something is step one of losing it. My parents. Marcus. Gran. Every time I reach for something it develops a lease clause or an ex-fiancé or a cousin with a hotel proposal."

"Delia does have a gift for ruining moments," Theo offered.

"I'm being vulnerable. Do not make jokes."

"Understood. Continuing to stand here quietly. Very quietly."

She exhaled, shaky. "But you said something on this beach five minutes ago and it lodged in me like a splinter. Showing up. Again. When nothing's on fire." She stepped closer. The meltwater pool reflected her, and him, and the ridiculous blue sky, all mixed together like ingredients that might actually come together this time. "So. Here I am. Nothing's on fire. I'm showing up. Kiss me before I regain my senses, because my senses are cowards and they will ruin this."

Theo took her face in his hands like she was the last recipe card in existence.

"Jude Brightwater," he said, "I have been waiting ten years for you to give me permission, and I want the record to show that you did. Out loud. On the beach. Witnesses include several judgmental gulls."

"Theo—"

And then he kissed her.

It was not the careful, cinematic kiss he'd imagined in the long years of regret. It was hungry and clumsy and real, her cold fingers fisting in his coat, his scarf knocked askew by her elbow, both of them laughing somewhere inside the kiss without breaking it. Ten years of almosts collapsed into one warm, salt-tinged, entirely overdue moment, and Theo thought, distantly, *so this is what it feels like when the recipe works.*

When they finally parted, she kept her forehead against his, breathing hard.

"Okay," she whispered. "Okay. That was—"

"Yeah."

"We should probably—"

"Absolutely."

Neither of them moved. The sky's leftovers glittered in the hollows. A gull screamed approval or contempt; with gulls it was genuinely hard to tell.

* * *

**JUDE**

Later, Jude would try to explain to herself what happened next, and she would fail, because terror doesn't leave notes.

They were walking back, hand in hand—her hand in his, an arrangement her palm kept reporting to headquarters with increasing alarm—when she saw it. Not anything dramatic. Just the bakery at the end of the street, steam curling from its chimney, lights on, alive. Her grandmother's bakery. The place with the ninety-day clock ticking under its floorboards, the place with a lawyer's letter full of conditions, the place that would belong to whoever proved they'd stay.

*Whoever proves they'll stay.*

And there she was, holding hands with a man whose life was in New York, three weeks from a wedding that would launch them both back into the stratosphere, having just kissed him like a woman who believed in tomorrows.

Wanting something is how you lose it. The thought arrived with the gentleness of a sledgehammer. Her parents had wanted each other. Look how that ended. She'd wanted Marcus, and he'd turned out to be a man who bought leases instead of feelings. She'd wanted Gran to stay, and the sky had taken her anyway and left only leftovers.

Her hand went slack in Theo's.

"Jude?" He felt it immediately—of course he did; he'd been studying her for six weeks like a final exam. "Hey. Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere," she said, which was a lie, because she'd gone straight back to every funeral she'd ever attended. "I just—this is fast. Right? This is fast. We kissed on a beach and now we're holding hands walking past my dead grandmother's bakery like we're in a movie, and movies end, Theo. Movies *end*, and then there's credits, and then everyone goes home."

Theo stopped walking. He didn't grab her hand back, which she noted even through the panic—that restraint, that awful, wonderful patience.

"Is that what you're afraid of?" he asked quietly. "The ending?"

"What I'm afraid of," Jude said, and her voice cracked on the word *afraid* like old ice, "is that I just handed you the power to wreck me a second time, and statistically speaking, you already have a track record. And I can't—Theo, I can't lose anything else right now. Not the bakery, not the wedding, not—" she gestured helplessly at the space between them, at the kiss still warm on her mouth, "—not this. If I hold onto this and it breaks, I don't come back from it. There's no third act for me. I've checked."

"So what are you saying?"

What she was saying was *run*. What she was saying was *protect yourself*. What she was saying, in the oldest language of her oldest wound, was *leave first, want nothing, stay safe.* But what came out, because she was Jude Brightwater and honesty was the one habit she'd never managed to break, was:

"I'm saying I need time. I know that's cruel. I know I kissed you first and I'm allowed to be a hypocrite, it's in my contract. I just need you to not—don't push, don't plan it, don't put it on a timeline. If you put it on a timeline I will sabotage it myself just to prove I still can."

Theo looked at her for a long moment. Snowmelt dripped off the bakery awning behind him, counting out seconds neither of them wanted.

"Okay," he said.

"*Okay?*"

"Okay. No timeline. No pushing." He smiled, and it hurt to look at, because it was gentle and it didn't waver. "For the record, though—I'm not going anywhere, Jude. You can take all the time in the world. I'll be here. Showing up. Again. It's kind of my whole thing now."

He squeezed her hand once—briefly, warmly, unbearably—and walked her to the door, and pressed a kiss to her hair, and left.

Jude stood alone in the bakery among the ghost-smell of cinnamon and her grandmother's recipe cards, and told herself the ache in her chest was relief.

It was not relief.

She was still standing there, arguing with herself about it, when her phone rang. Unknown number. Local prefix.

"Ms. Brightwater?" A woman's voice, crisp and unfamiliar. "This is Ruth Okafor, from the Harrow estate office. I'm afraid I have some news about the conservatory—the winter garden you booked for the Voss wedding." A pause, carefully professional. "There's been significant water damage overnight. A pipe, we think. The venue is... I'm very sorry. The venue is unusable."

Jude closed her eyes. Three weeks out. Camille Voss. Everything.

Outside, the meltwater kept falling off the awning, patient as a countdown.
