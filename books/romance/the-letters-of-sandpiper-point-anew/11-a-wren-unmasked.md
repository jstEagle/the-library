## 11. A. Wren, Unmasked

The invitation came tucked into a bag of coffee grounds, which was how everything important arrived in Sandpiper Point—hand-delivered, faintly stained, and impossible to refuse.

*Harbor Lights Books & Coffee presents: An Evening with A. Wren. Saturday, seven o'clock. Wine generously donated by Vega Cellars.*

Marisol read it twice, standing in the tasting room with her hands still sticky from barrel samples. *Wine generously donated by Vega Cellars.* She had donated it herself, three days ago, to Dot Alvarez at the bookstore, before she'd known what the event was. Dot had smiled her small, knowing smile and said only, "Oh, you'll want to come to this one," and now Marisol understood that everyone in town except her had probably known for years who A. Wren was.

Or maybe they hadn't. Maybe she was the only one who'd figured it out, three weeks ago, standing in the poetry section with a paperback held open like a wound.

She could have said no. She could have sent someone else from the vineyard with the cases of rosé and stayed home with the ledger, because the ledger always needed her, because Brandt's numbers were a maze built specifically to exhaust her. But the truth—the truth she admitted to no one, not even herself, not even at two in the morning when it was most obvious—was that she would have walked through fire for an hour in a room where Ines Winterbourne was going to be.

So on Saturday evening she loaded six cases into the truck bed, changed her shirt twice, and drove down the point with the windows open, telling herself all the way that this was business. Community presence. Brand visibility.

Her brand. Right.

* * *

Harbor Lights Books had once been a chandlery, and it still smelled faintly of rope and brine under the espresso. Dot had strung paper lanterns between the shelves and arranged forty folding chairs, and by ten past seven every one of them was full, plus a ring of standers along the maritime history section, plus two children sitting cross-legged on the floor near the register like it was story hour, which in a way it was.

Marisol poured wine at the back table and watched the room assemble itself around a woman she was learning to know in pieces.

Ines stood near the front in a blue sweater, holding a paperback too loosely, the way you hold something you're not sure belongs to you. She kept touching the spine. She laughed at something Dot said and put her hand over her mouth afterward, as if laughter were a thing she'd been caught doing. Eleven years ago, at the harvest festival, she had stood exactly like that at the edge of the dance floor—present, participating, somehow never quite *in* it—and Marisol had thought, with the arrogance of nineteen: *someone should go get her.*

She had gone. That was the whole problem. She had gone, and then she had left, and eleven years later here they both were, orbiting the same folding chairs.

"Vega." A hand landed on her shoulder, heavy and proprietary, and Marisol's spine went rigid before her brain caught up. Hollis Brandt. He smelled of gin and expensive cologne fighting each other and losing. "Heard you finally got some decent juice out of those dead vines of yours."

"The vines aren't dead, Mr. Brandt. They're resting."

"That's what my accountant says about my money." He laughed too loud at his own joke, took a glass off her tray without asking, and drained half of it while looking at the crowd with the expression of a man appraising timber. "Big turnout. For a book reading." He said *book reading* the way other men said *root canal.* "You know who this Wren person even is? Some recluse lives up at the light. Writes about—" he waved his glass, sloshing— "*feelings.*"

"You're holding a glass of her feelings right now. Donated."

Brandt snorted. "Your father used to have a sense of humor too. Before."

Marisol's hand tightened on the bottle until the label creased. *Before.* There it was, the word he always reached for, the little hook he'd been setting behind his teeth for eleven years. She made herself smile. "Enjoy the reading, Mr. Brandt."

"Oh, I will." He wandered off toward the front row, and Marisol watched him go and felt the old cold move through her, the one that lived in her chest alongside everything else, patient as frost.

Dot tapped a spoon against a water glass. The room settled.

* * *

A. Wren read the way Marisol had always imagined the books were written: slowly, carefully, with pauses that seemed less like performance and more like the author surprising herself with her own sentences.

She read from the new one, *The Salt Almanac*, a passage about a woman repairing a lighthouse lens alone at dawn, and the room was so quiet Marisol could hear the harbor slapping against the pilings outside. Then Dot asked questions—gentle ones, well-rehearsed—and A. Wren answered them in that dry, self-deprecating voice that made the whole room feel like it was being let in on something.

Then Dot said, "We have time for one or two from the audience," and Hollis Brandt's hand went up like a trapdoor opening.

Marisol's stomach dropped before he even spoke.

"A question," Brandt announced, getting to his feet with the elaborate care of a man negotiating with his own knees. He was drunker than he'd been twenty minutes ago; someone had clearly been keeping his glass full, possibly himself. "For our celebrated author." He turned, found Ines at the front of the room, and grinned. "You know, I've read your work, Ms. Wren. All of it. Devoted fan."

Scattered laughter. Nervous. Ines's face had gone very still.

"There's a passage I particularly love," Brandt continued, swaying slightly, savoring it. "From the second book. The one where the vineyard girl stands in the road and the lighthouse girl can't say what she means, so she just—stands there. Like a post. Very moving." He pressed a hand to his chest. "'Small towns,' you write. 'Small towns are where feelings go to be buried alive.' Beautiful stuff. Real literary." His grin widened, and Marisol watched him turn it toward the audience like a man showing a knife. "You know what I think? I think our author knows Sandpiper Point a little too well. I think our author has been writing about this town—and certain people in it—for years, under a little pen name, hiding up in her tower." He raised his glass. "Because I don't think A. Wren is a pen name at all. I think A. Wren is—"

"Don't." It came out of Marisol before she decided anything, before strategy or caution or the eleven years of careful silence. Just the word, loud, cracking across the room like a whip.

Every head turned. Brandt blinked at her, delighted. "Well. Something to say, Ms. Vega?"

Her heart was slamming. Her mouth was dry. And underneath all of it, absurdly, she could hear her father's voice: *a Vega pours the wine, mija, but she chooses when the bottle opens.*

"Yes," Marisol said. She set down the bottle. She stepped out from behind the table. "I do."

* * *

Later—much later, lying awake in her father's old house listening to the wind come off the water—Marisol would try to reconstruct exactly what she'd said, and find that memory had blurred it into something more graceful than it was. What she remembered was the heat. What she remembered was that she did not look at Ines once, because looking at Ines would have made it about the two of them, and this was not about the two of them. This was about a drunk man with a ledger full of sins deciding that humiliation was a currency he could still spend.

"What you're about to do," Marisol said, walking forward until she stood between Brandt and the front of the room, "is take a woman's private work and use it as a weapon, because it's the only weapon left that you didn't have to forge a signature to get."

Someone in the back said *ooh*, low, like a wave.

"Careful," Brandt said softly. "You've got debt, Ms. Vega. Big debt. People who live in glass houses—"

"I've read every book A. Wren has written," Marisol said, louder, turning to face the room. "All nine. Twice. Some of them three times." A ripple of laughter somewhere, warm, and she let it carry her. "And I'll tell you what's in them. Not gossip. Not secrets. A keeper who stays when everyone else leaves. A town that takes care of its own. A fisherman who gets cheated by a rich man and comes back anyway." She heard the room shift—heard, specifically, old Tomás Reyes by the window stop pretending to read his program. "If there's sentimentality in these books, Mr. Brandt, it's the kind you wouldn't recognize, because it requires believing people are worth more than their ledgers."

"Pretty speech." Brandt's grin had curdled. "Doesn't change the fact that our humble lighthouse keeper has been—"

"—writing books that four hundred people in this county love?" Marisol turned at last, and looked at Ines directly, and did the bravest thing she had done since coming home, which was to say the true thing in front of everyone. "Yes. Our lighthouse keeper. Who fixed the north beacon after the storm in '09 when the county said it couldn't be done. Who reads to the elementary school every October. Who wrote a book where a woman comes home to a failing vineyard and finds it worth saving." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the last word, and she let it. "I'm grateful to A. Wren. I'm not ashamed of her. And nobody in this room should be either."

Silence. The paper lanterns ticked in the draft from the door.

And then Dot Alvarez, who had run that bookstore through two recessions and one hurricane, set down her spoon and began to clap. Slowly. Deliberately. The way you clap when you want everyone to understand they have a choice.

Tomás Reyes clapped next. Then the schoolteacher. Then the two children on the floor, who had understood none of it and clapped hardest of all. By the time it reached the back of the room, half of Sandpiper Point was on its feet, and Hollis Brandt stood alone in the middle of it like a man caught outdoors in weather he'd been told wouldn't come.

He looked at Marisol. Something moved behind his eyes—not embarrassment. Calculation. The look of a man revising a number.

"Well," he said, draining his glass. "Lovely evening." And he pushed through the crowd and out the door, and the bell above it rang twice, like a warning.

* * *

Afterward, the room dissolved into the happy chaos of a small-town event ending—chairs folding, children being retrieved, Dot pressing a spare copy of *The Salt Almanac* on anyone who stood still. Marisol stayed at the wine table, stacking empty glasses with great concentration, waiting.

She didn't have to wait long.

"You didn't have to do that."

Ines stood beside her, close enough that Marisol could see her hands were shaking. She had them wrapped around a paper cup of coffee she clearly wasn't drinking.

"Yes, I did," Marisol said.

"He could have—"

"He could have what? Told everyone your secret?" Marisol set down the last glass and turned. "Ines. Half this town figured it out years ago. Dot told me the library keeps a waiting list for your books under both names. You weren't hiding. You were just waiting for permission."

Ines stared at her. Outside, the lighthouse beam swept past the front windows, once, twice, steady as a pulse. "That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "Why did you defend me like that?"

Because I've read your dedication page forty times. Because the woman in the road in your second book is me and we both know it. Because watching him aim at you made eleven years collapse into nothing. Because—

"Because it was true," Marisol said. "All of it. Every word."

Ines looked down at her cup. When she spoke again her voice was barely above the sound of the harbor. "Nobody's ever stood in front of me before. Not like that. Not in a room full of people."

"Somebody should have, a long time ago." Marisol heard her own voice go quiet and rough. "I'm sorry it took me eleven years to be somebody."

The beam swept past again. Ines lifted her head, and for a moment—one long, lantern-lit moment—she looked like she was going to say something enormous. Marisol braced for it, wanted it, felt it coming like weather.

"Ines!" Dot called from the register, waving a stack of books. "Sign these before you escape!"

The moment broke, the way moments do, and Ines gave a small helpless laugh and went. But at the door, shrugging into her coat, she paused and looked back across the room—at Marisol, only at Marisol—and said, loud enough to carry:

"Come by the lighthouse this week. I found something in the harbor records. About the consortium." A beat. "About your father's land."

And then she was gone into the dark, and Marisol stood among the folding chairs with her heart going like a gull in a storm, understanding suddenly that whatever Ines had found, it wasn't just history.

It was ammunition.
