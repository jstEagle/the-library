## 15. Tasting Room Confessions

The tasting room at Vega Cellars had been Marisol's father's favorite room in the world, and he had built it like a man who expected to be happy in it: low rafters, a copper bar gone green at the elbows, windows that framed the vineyard rows the way a good frame flatters a painting. It had been closed for three years. The dust had opinions about that.

Marisol spent two days making it ready—sweeping, polishing, coaxing the old lamps to life—and on the third evening, with the first cold snap of the season pressing its nose against the glass, she set out two glasses and called Ines Winterbourne.

Not *called*, exactly. She drove to the lighthouse, stood at the bottom of the tower for a full minute working up her nerve, and then shouted up the spiral stairs like a teenager, "Ines! I need a professional opinion!"

A long pause. Then Ines's voice drifted down, dry as August: "On what? Your life choices?"

"On wine."

"That's worse."

But she came down, pulling on a coat over her sweater, hair escaping from its braid in gray-streaked wisps, and she got into the truck without being asked twice, which Marisol chose to interpret as a good sign.

* * *

The tasting room smelled of oak and dust and lamp oil, and when Ines stepped inside she stopped in the doorway the way people stop at church.

"I haven't been in here since—" She didn't finish it.

"Since the harvest festival," Marisol said. "Eleven years ago. You had cider. You hated it. You told my father it tasted like punishment."

"He laughed."

"He laughed so hard he sat down on a barrel." Marisol lifted the dust cloth off the barrel head at the end of the row—the one her father used to tap samples from, the one with his initials burned into the wood beside a small carved sandpiper. "This is the 2014. Last vintage he finished before—" She stopped too. They were both getting very good at stopping.

She drew the thief, filled both glasses a third of the way, and handed one across. Her hand was not entirely steady. She blamed the cold.

Ines held the glass up to the lamplight without drinking, turning it, watching the wine move. "You're supposed to look for legs," she said. "My mother taught me that. I've never once seen them."

"You're holding it wrong."

"I'm holding it like a woman who's about to be judged by a professional."

"You are." Marisol touched her glass to Ines's—a small clear note in the quiet room—and drank.

The wine was not perfect. The frost scare had cost them; there was a thinness at the finish where richness should have been. But it was alive, and it was theirs, and when she looked up Ines was watching her face rather than her own glass, which was either rude or the most honest thing anyone had done in this room in years.

"Well?" Marisol said.

"It tastes," Ines said slowly, "like somebody fought for it."

Marisol laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of her—and had to put her glass down. "That's the worst review I've ever heard."

"It's the best one I know how to give." Ines turned the glass in her fingers. "I've read enough reviews to know they mostly describe the reviewer. *Tastes like somebody fought for it* describes you."

The room went quiet in a new way after that. Not the awkward quiet of the harbor meeting, all barbs and crossed arms. A listening quiet. Outside, the wind moved through the bare rows with a sound like pages turning.

Marisol topped up both glasses, more out of nerves than ceremony, and leaned back against the barrel. There was a thing in her chest that had been waiting eleven years for this room, this light, this woman, and it was rising now whether she was ready or not.

"Ines. I have to tell you something, and I'd like you to let me get all the way through it before you say anything. Even if what you say is nothing. Especially then."

Ines set her glass down on the copper bar. Carefully, the way she did everything. "All right."

"You remember Hollis Brandt's porch. The night of the festival." Marisol kept her eyes on the wine, because looking at Ines while saying this would be like looking into the sun during an eclipse—something you could do only through a filter. "You remember that I left two days later. No goodbye. No explanation. Just gone, and then gone for good, and you wrote me a letter—I found out later, from my aunt, years after—that I never answered. And you have spent eleven years believing I read it and chose silence."

"I didn't know that's what I believed until recently," Ines said quietly. "But yes."

"I never got it. That's not the confession, though. The confession is why I left." Marisol made herself lift her eyes. "Brandt came to see my father that summer. Three times. I know because I heard the last one. My father owed money—not much, but enough, after the bad year—and Brandt offered to buy the north field. My father refused. And Brandt told him, very pleasantly, the way he says everything, that if the sale didn't happen, certain things would happen instead. The health inspector. The water rights. The bank. He said my father's heart wasn't strong enough for a long fight."

Ines had gone very still. Stillness looked different on her than on other people; on other people stillness was calm, and on Ines it was a held breath, a drawn bow.

"My father didn't tell me any of this directly. But I heard enough. And then Brandt caught me alone at the festival—you remember, I stepped away just before—" Marisol stopped. *Just before we almost kissed under the string lights.* Neither of them needed the sentence completed. "He told me the deal was simple. I leave Sandpiper Point, I stay gone, I don't write to anyone here, especially not you, and my father keeps his farm and his health and his name. If I stayed—if I so much as sent a postcard—he'd take everything my father had left, slowly, and make sure everyone knew the Vegas couldn't be trusted with dirt."

"And you believed him," Ines said. Not a question. Not an accusation either. Something worse: understanding.

"I believed him because I watched him do it to the Alvarados the year before. And because my father sat me down the next morning with his hands shaking and told me that sometimes loving a place means leaving it, and I knew whose words those really were." Marisol's throat tightened. "So I left. I stayed gone. I didn't send a single letter in eleven years, and every year on the anniversary of the festival I told myself it was worth it, and every year I believed it a little less. And the whole time, Ines, the whole time, I thought maybe you hated me for it. I thought silence was the kindest thing I could give you, because anything else would have gotten you hurt too. He mentioned you. Once. He said your lighthouse lease came up for review every five years, and he smiled when he said it, and I understood."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere in the walls the old building settled, the way old buildings do when people finally say true things inside them.

"So that's it," Marisol said. "That's why. Not because I didn't care. Because caring was the weapon he picked up first."

She drained her glass, mostly to have something to do with her hands, and waited. She had rehearsed this speech on the drive over, and again while sweeping, and once at three in the morning staring at her father's initials in the barrelhead, and none of the rehearsals had prepared her for how it would feel to actually say it—to watch Ines absorb it, decade by decade, like weather moving across a field.

Ines was crying. Not dramatically—she was clearly a woman who had practiced not doing things dramatically for a very long time—but tears were standing in her eyes and sliding down without permission, and she wasn't wiping them away.

"I wrote you a letter," Ines said.

"I know. I—"

"No. Let me—" Ines pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes, laughed at herself, wet and unsteady. "You said let you get through it. Fair's fair." She took a breath. "I wrote you a letter the night of the festival. After Brandt pulled you aside. After you came back and wouldn't look at me, and I thought—I thought I'd imagined everything, or that whatever it was, you'd decided against it. And I went home and I sat down at my desk and I wrote you everything. Eleven pages. I told you I loved you. I told you I'd loved you since you threw a green apple at my head when we were sixteen and then apologized to the apple. I asked you to stay. I sealed it, and I addressed it, and I walked it to the post office the next morning, and I paid for postage, and I watched Ruth Ellery take it out of my hands and drop it in the bin behind her."

Marisol's breath stopped somewhere in the middle of her chest.

"And it never arrived," Ines said. "And I spent eleven years believing you read it and chose silence. Which means we spent a decade on opposite sides of the same wall, each of us holding our silence up like a shield to protect the other one, and the whole time the wall was—" She gestured helplessly at nothing. "It was him. It was always him. He took your goodbye and my letter and he built a wall out of them and charged us rent to stand on either side of it."

"How do you know it never arrived?" Marisol whispered. "How do you know he—"

"Because the storm brought it back." Ines said it simply, terribly. "Three weeks ago. A mail sack, washed up on the rocks below the point, decayed through. Inside was my letter. Postmarked November eleventh, eleven years ago. Never delivered. Ruth Ellery was the postmistress, Marisol. Ruth Ellery, who retired comfortable, who bought a boat. I've been sitting with that letter for three weeks trying to decide if it still belongs to the woman who wrote it or the woman I've become, and I think—" Her voice cracked and she let it. "I think it belongs to both of us. I think it always did."

The room was absolutely silent. Marisol became aware, distantly, that she was gripping the edge of the barrel hard enough to hurt her palm, and that she didn't care.

"Read it to me," she said.

"Not tonight." Ines came around the barrel, slow, giving Marisol time to stop her, and Marisol didn't stop her. "Tonight I want to say the part I can say without paper. I'm sorry. For every year I spent angry at you for a crime you didn't commit. For every draft I wrote in my head addressed to a woman who was out there keeping her own kind of vigil. We were both guarding the same person, and it was each other, and neither of us knew."

Marisol reached out and took Ines's hand. It was cold from the walk and the tower and eleven years, and Marisol warmed it between both of hers, deliberately, the way you relight a pilot flame.

"We were idiots," Marisol said.

"Monumental ones."

"Tragic, romantic, monumental idiots."

"That's going on my next book jacket," Ines said, and Marisol laughed through the tears she hadn't noticed starting, and Ines smiled—really smiled, the whole face of it, unguarded—and for a moment the room was warm all the way through, dust and grief and all.

Then Ines's expression shifted. Marisol felt it happen, felt the tenderness in her hand acquire a spine.

"But Marisol. If Ruth took my letter, she was paid. Postmistresses don't intercept mail out of whimsy. And if Brandt paid her to bury my letter, then he wasn't just threatening your father. He was managing us. Both of us. Deliberately. Which means there may be a record. Payments, dates, something." Ines's eyes had gone bright and hard, the keeper's light behind them. "Your ledger. His ledger. Everything you signed with the consortium. Bring it all tomorrow. I've been through the harbor records already and there are numbers in them that don't add up, and I think I know why."

Marisol stared at her. "You want to build a case."

"I want to burn his down," Ines said, mild as milk, "brick by documented brick."

Outside, the wind rose against the windows, and somewhere down the coast the lighthouse swept its beam across the dark water, patient and inexorable, the way truth is supposed to be and rarely manages.

Marisol squeezed her hand once. "Tomorrow, then. Bring the letter."

"I'll bring the letter."

"And Ines—" Marisol hesitated, then committed, because she was done with unsent things. "When you read it to me. Read all eleven pages. Even the part about the apple."

"Especially the part about the apple," Ines said, and squeezed back.
