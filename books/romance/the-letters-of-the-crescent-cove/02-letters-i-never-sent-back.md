## 2. Letters I Never Sent Back

The boxes had been sitting in the corner of her bedroom for eleven days, and Beatrix Eastwick had developed a relationship with them that could only be described as hostile cohabitation.

They'd come from the storage unit in London—the one she paid for quarterly and visited never. When the final invoice arrived with a notice about the facility closing, she'd booked the shipping almost by reflex, the way you swallow a pill without reading the label. Then the boxes arrived, twelve of them, taped and labeled in her own handwriting from five years ago, and she discovered she was not ready to read her own labels.

*WIGMORE — PROGRAMS.* She remembered writing that one. She'd been kneeling on the floor of a flat in Maida Vale at two in the morning, sealing it shut while her hands still shook.

*MOTHER'S THINGS — DO NOT OPEN ALONE.*

She hadn't.

But today was Saturday, there were no lessons until Monday, and the rain had settled over Crescent Cove like a decision already made. The new neighbor's moving van had gone sometime in the night—she'd heard the engine turn over around six and told herself the small lift of relief she felt was nothing, just the return of quiet, just the street going back to being hers. By seven she'd run out of reasons not to open the boxes, which meant by seven-fifteen she was on the floor of her bedroom with a box cutter and the specific dread of a woman about to meet her former self.

The first box held sheet music. Of course it did. She'd known it would, and she opened it anyway, because grief is not logical and neither is the way we arrange our wounds.

Brahms. The E minor sonata, her mother's edition, pencil markings in her mother's slanted hand: *breathe here*, written above a phrase as if the music might otherwise forget how. Beatrix set it on the bed with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might go off.

Then the programs. Wigmore Hall, March four years ago—no. Five. Five years, and she still reached for four first, as if the year of the collapse were a step she could skip on the stairs.

She didn't open the program folder. She put it back in the box and folded the flaps down and sat on the floor for a while listening to the rain.

It was the second box that undid her, but not in the way she expected. Not the reviews—she'd assumed the reviews would be in there, and they were, clipped and folded, one of them with a coffee ring across the word *luminous*, which felt about right, coffee rings being the most honest criticism she'd ever received. Not the photographs either, though those hurt in their ordinary way: her at twenty-six on a stage in Amsterdam, laughing at something out of frame, holding her cello like it was a person she loved.

What undid her was the shoebox inside the box. Unlabeled. She knew what it was before she lifted the lid, the way you know a voice through a wall.

Letters.

*

There were forty-one of them. She'd counted once, years ago, in a bad week, and then made herself stop counting because counting was a form of hoping and hoping was a country she'd renounced citizenship in.

They started three months after Wigmore Hall. The first one arrived at her flat in London forwarded twice, tracking her through the change of address like something with a pulse. No signature. No return address. Just her name on the envelope in handwriting she didn't recognize—small, careful, slightly old-fashioned, the letters leaning forward as if the words themselves were eager—and inside, a single page.

She'd kept every one. She took them out now, one at a time, and laid them on the floorboards in order, and the rain kept on outside, and the room filled up with a decade of someone else's belief in her.

*You don't have to explain the silence,* the first one said. *Everyone will want you to explain it, and you don't owe anyone an explanation. But I hope you know that the person who walked off that stage is not the person I think of when I think of you. I think of the playing. I think of what it did to the air.*

She'd read that letter maybe two hundred times. She could recite it. And still, unfolding it again, she felt the same thing she always felt: that uncanny, disorienting sensation of being *known*—not the public her, not the cellist with the agent and the profile and the quotes in the trade papers, but the actual her, the one who lay awake at 3 a.m. cataloguing every way she'd failed.

How did this person know? That was the question she'd asked for ten years, in the shower, on trains, into her pillow. How did a stranger understand that what she needed wasn't encouragement to return but permission to be exactly where she was? Every other letter she'd gotten after the collapse—from colleagues, from critics, from her agent, from her mother's old friends—had some version of *when will you play again* buried in it like a splinter. This person never asked. Not once in forty-one letters.

*I'm not writing to ask when you're coming back,* said number nine, dated roughly eighteen months after the collapse. *I'm writing because I heard a piece of news—you're teaching now, somewhere on the coast—and I wanted you to know that I think that might be the bravest thing you've done yet. Staying near the instrument when you can't bear the stage. Most people would have burned the house down. You moved into the garden.*

*You moved into the garden.* She'd cried over that line on a bench in a station in Exeter, missing her train, not caring.

Number seventeen, after the year her father died: *I don't know if you'll get this in time or if it will arrive in the middle of everything, but I wanted to say: whatever you're carrying, you're allowed to set it down. Even for a minute. Even just to read this.*

He'd known about her father. How? She'd never announced it publicly; she'd stopped giving interviews years before. She'd searched her memory more than once for anyone who might have known both her and her losses and chosen silence over comfort, and come up with no one. A ghost with beautiful penmanship.

Number twenty-three, after the bad winter when she'd nearly sold the cello—she'd never told a living soul about that, had listed it online at midnight and taken the listing down by dawn, and yet:

*A friend of mine sold her grandmother's piano once and regretted it for the rest of her life. Not because she needed the money back. Because the piano was the last place her grandmother existed. Don't let the hard season make your decisions for you. Hard seasons are terrible landlords; don't sign anything while you live in one.*

She laughed now, reading it, a wet, surprised sound in the empty room. Terrible landlords. Ten years of letters and this stranger had never once been solemn, never once performed sympathy. He—or she; she'd never been able to tell, though the handwriting leaned masculine to her eye, and something in the cadence too, though she'd learned long ago that certainty about anonymous strangers is mostly projection—wrote like someone who understood that the kindest thing you can do for a drowning person is not describe the shore but sit in the water beside them.

The middle years blurred: letters after her students' first recitals (how did he know?), letters on the anniversaries she thought only she remembered, letter thirty-one, which said simply, *Five years ago tonight I heard you play and I have never recovered, and I have decided that recovery is overrated,* which she'd read so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

And then the newest one. Number forty-one. It had arrived eleven days ago—the same day the moving van came, she realized now, though she hadn't connected the two events until this moment, sitting on her floor with a decade spread out around her like fallen leaves.

She'd read it once, standing in her hallway, and then put it in the drawer where she kept her passport, because some letters are too much for ordinary drawers.

She got up and got it now.

The envelope was heavier than usual. Inside, two pages instead of one, and she recognized immediately that something had shifted—the handwriting slightly faster, less composed, as if written late at night by someone who'd finally stopped deliberating.

*Beatrix,* it began. First time he'd ever used her name. Forty letters addressed to *To the one who plays* or *Dear B.E.* or simply nothing at all, and now suddenly her name, in ink, like a hand extended across a table.

*I've been writing to you for ten years without telling you who I am, and I've told myself it was kindness, and mostly it was cowardice. I'm not going to fix all of that in a letter. But I need to tell you one true thing, because I may be running out of chances to say it in person.*

*I live near you now. Or I will soon. I've moved to Crescent Cove—to my grandmother's house, the old Harrow place on Gullwing Lane. I grew up there, in summers. It's the reason I write to you at all, though that's a longer story than a letter can hold, and I promise—I promise—that someday you'll hear all of it from me, standing up, in daylight, like a man and not a shadow.*

*Until then: the cove is lucky to have you. So am I. Forgive me the parts of this that are selfish. Some things you keep close even when keeping them close isn't fair to the person you're keeping.*

*Yours, always,*
*E.*

*E.*

She read it three times. Then she set it down on the floorboards among its forty predecessors, and she looked at the whole constellation of them—the ten years, the forty-one envelopes, the handwriting she knew better than her own—and she felt the ground tilt under the room.

Gullwing Lane was her lane. The Harrow house was next door. The man with the blue tarp and the tired eyes and the way he'd watched her hands—

No. That was coincidence. That was her loneliness arranging facts into constellations, the way she'd once arranged clouds into animals as a child. Strangers moved to coastal towns constantly. People named E existed in their thousands. The letters had come postmarked from everywhere—London, Bristol, once from Edinburgh, once with no legible postmark at all—

Except.

She picked up the earliest letter, number one, and looked at the postmark the way she'd looked at it a hundred times before and never truly seen. Smudged. Partially legible. Two letters visible in the town code.

*C.C.*

Crescent Cove. Or Cheltenham Central, or Colchester Camp, or any of a hundred combinations. She'd never checked. She'd never wanted to know, because knowing would make the letters finite, would give them an author, and an author could die or tire or stop, whereas a mystery could keep her company forever.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking the way you notice weather—as a fact about the world rather than a fact about herself.

Ten years. Someone had watched her life for ten years, from close enough to know about her father, about the cello listing, about the recitals of children whose names had never appeared in any paper. Someone had been near enough all along, and had chosen the long way around, the slow reveal, the letter instead of the doorbell.

And now that someone lived twenty feet from her kitchen window, behind a blue tarp, in a house that smelled of dust and salt, with his grandmother's furniture under sheets and his eyes doing that thing they did whenever she mentioned music—some flicker, some recognition, quickly banked like a covered flame.

*Standing up, in daylight, like a man and not a shadow.*

She gathered the letters up—all forty-one, stacking them with hands that wouldn't quite obey—and put them back in the shoebox, and put the shoebox in the drawer with her passport, and closed the drawer, and stood in the middle of her room listening to the rain and to her own heartbeat, which was behaving disgracefully.

Outside, through the gap in the curtains, she could see the Harrow house. A light was on upstairs. He was there. He was there right now, in the house next door, and he knew—he had to know—that she was the woman in the cottage, and he had said nothing, and she had said nothing, and between the two houses lay twenty feet of wet grass and ten years of unsaid things.

She should march over. She should knock on that door and hold up the letter and say *explain this*. That was what a brave woman would do. That was what the woman in the Amsterdam photograph would have done, the one laughing at something out of frame.

Instead Beatrix Eastwick did what she had gotten very good at doing. She made tea. She washed the teapot nobody had used. She graded a student's scales worksheet with unnecessary thoroughness.

And at some point in the grey afternoon, without deciding to, she found herself at the window again, watching the light in the Harrow house move from one room to another, following it like a tide, wondering—not idly, not safely, but with her whole traitorous heart—who was carrying that light from room to room, and what he would say tomorrow if she stood at his gate and said the one word she'd never sent back in ten years of letters:

*Hello.*

* * *

That night she dreamed of the boathouse.

She hadn't dreamed of it in years—had trained herself out of it, the way you train yourself out of any habit that costs too much. But it came back whole: the summer camp, sixteen years old, the smell of pine tar and low tide, the little stage of warped planks at the water's end where the counselors let the advanced students perform on the last night. She was playing. She couldn't see the audience in the dream, only feel them, a warm dark weight of attention, and somewhere in it one listener who mattered more than the rest, though the dream refused to show his face.

She woke at dawn with the last phrase of the piece still ringing in her ears—the piece, the one from Wigmore, the one from the boathouse, the one she had not played for anyone in five years—and lay still, listening to the gulls, feeling the strange double exposure of the dream: the girl on the plank stage and the woman in the bed, separated by everything and joined by eight bars of music.

The light in the Harrow house was on again. Early. He was an early riser, then. She filed this away and hated herself for filing it away.

She got up. She put the kettle on. And on the counter, next to the kettle, she set letter forty-one, propped against the window where the morning light could reach it, so that while she waited for the water to boil she could look at the word *E.* and the word *Crescent* and the word *Cove*, and admit, in the privacy of her own kitchen, the thing she'd been refusing to admit since the moving van arrived.

Whoever wrote these letters had come home.

And he was close enough now that the not-knowing couldn't last.
