## 4. Borrowed Sugar, Borrowed Time

The storm came in off the water the way storms always did in Crescent Cove—without apology and without warning anyone who mattered.

I'd spent the afternoon pretending I wasn't watching the Harrow house through my kitchen window. There was a legitimate reason to be at the sink: three mugs that needed washing, a kettle that needed filling, a view of the neighbor's roofline that happened to include a man on a ladder in weather no sane person climbed ladders in.

Elias Thorne had been on that ladder for an hour, wrestling the last of the blue tarp over the section of roof the roofer hadn't gotten to before the forecast turned ugly. The wind took the tarp twice. The first time he caught it. The second time it went over the fence like a wounded bird, and he stood in his yard holding the empty ladder rungs and looking, even from this distance, like a man having a private conversation with God about fairness.

I should have stayed at my sink. I want that on the record, somewhere, in some accounting of my life: I knew better.

Instead I was out my back door and across the strip of salt grass between our properties with my hair already whipping loose from its clip, chasing forty feet of blue polyethylene across his yard while he shouted something I couldn't hear over the wind.

"—got it!" I finally understood, pinning the tarp's corner under my knee as it tried to become a sail. "Got—it!"

He came running, and together we wrestled the thing into submission against the side of the house, weighting it with split firewood and a half-bucket of roofing nails and, in the end, me standing on one corner while he secured the other two.

"Thank you," he said, breathless, rain starting to spit sideways. He pushed wet hair off his forehead. Up close, without the polite distance of a borrowed cup of sugar between us, he was taller than I'd registered, and there was sawdust in the creases of his jacket like he'd been wearing it for weeks instead of days. "You didn't have to do that."

"You were going to lose your roof."

"I was going to lose my roof," he agreed. "Now I've only lost my dignity."

Thunder rolled in from the sea, low and long, the kind of sound you feel in your sternum before you hear it properly. Something in my chest answered it—an old flinch, a tightening I knew too well to name out loud.

"Come inside before it opens up," I said, and then heard myself say it, and immediately wanted to take it back. That wasn't the script. The script was: wave politely, retreat behind the hedge, resume a life with clean edges.

But Elias Thorne just looked at me for a second—looked at me the way people look at a door they weren't sure would open—and said, "Only if you let me bring the generator."

"The what?"

"You'll see." He glanced at the sky, which had gone the color of wet slate. "In about twenty minutes, you'll see."

* * *

The power died at 6:14 p.m., which I know because I was watching the clock on the stove when it did, the way I always watch clocks when the light changes. The whole house exhaled into darkness—the refrigerator's hum gone, the lamp in the corner gone, the small electric noises I'd built my evenings around all gone at once.

In the silence, I stood in my kitchen and felt the old familiar creep of panic at the edges. Not the big kind. Not anymore. Just its shadow, its paperwork. Darkness meant losing things. Darkness meant the night my hands stopped working, though I'd never told anyone that part.

Headlights swept across my curtains. Then a knock.

Elias stood on my porch soaked to the elbows, holding a lantern in one hand and what looked like a military surplus generator under his other arm like it weighed nothing, which it clearly did not.

"Before you say anything," he said, "this is not a scheme. My grandmother kept this in the shed for thirty years. It runs. Probably. And your house is on the same grid line as mine, which means if the outage is long, your pipes could freeze if it's cold enough, and mostly I just—" He stopped himself. Rain dripped off his jaw. "Mostly I didn't want you sitting in the dark."

I should have said thank you, but no thank you. I should have said I have candles, I'm fine, goodnight.

What I said was: "Do you know how to start that thing, or are we both going to die of carbon monoxide in my kitchen?"

His grin arrived all at once, like weather. "Fair point. Ventilation first."

* * *

It took us twenty minutes and one minor argument about choke settings, but the generator coughed to life outside my back door, and by seven o'clock I had one lamp burning and a pot of soup on the gas burner, because it seemed insane not to feed the man who had just carried sixty pounds of machinery through a downpour for me.

He sat at my kitchen table with a towel around his neck and his sleeves shoved up, and he looked—wrongly, absurdly—comfortable. Like the chair had been waiting for him.

"This is the best soup I've ever had," he said, "and I'm aware it's lentils."

"It's lentils and whatever was dying in the crisper drawer. Don't romanticize it."

"Too late." He wrapped both hands around the mug of tea I'd given him. I noticed his hands then—broad, nicked, a healing scrape across one knuckle from the ladder. Working hands. But they held the mug carefully, delicately, the way musicians hold things, and something about that snagged in me like a splinter.

"So," I said, to make the snag stop, "the house. Are you actually restoring it, or just slowly demolishing it with extra steps?"

"A bit of both." He blew on his tea. "My grandmother's. She died in March. I grew up here summers—I don't know if I mentioned that."

"You didn't mention anything. You mentioned sugar. Once."

"That's true. I've been very disciplined about mentioning only sugar." He smiled into the mug. "The plan was to fix it up, sell it, done. Clean break. Tidy."

"But?"

"But nothing. That's the plan." He said it a little too fast, the way people say things they've rehearsed until they believe them. Then he looked around my kitchen—at the sheet music stacked on the counter, the metronome, the cello case leaning in the corner like a sleeping animal—and I watched his gaze land on the case and stay there a beat too long.

"You teach," he said. "Music."

"I teach cello. Out of the front room. Twelve students, ages eight to seventy-one. Mrs. Okafor is seventy-one and she's better than most of the eight-year-olds, which keeps everyone humble."

"Do you play?" he asked. "Still? Professionally, I mean."

The question landed exactly where questions like that always landed—in the soft damaged place I kept locked. I reached for the deflection I'd polished over five years.

"I used to."

"What changed?"

"Life." I stood, busied myself at the counter with nothing. "Life changed."

He didn't push. That was the strange thing. Most people pushed—they leaned in with their concerned faces and their *but you were so talented* and their hunger for the story, the collapse, the juicy wound. Elias Thorne just nodded slowly and looked into his tea like it might contain the answer to a different question entirely.

And then, quietly, he said: "Hands are funny things. They remember everything. Even when the rest of us would rather they didn't."

I turned around. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at my hands where they rested on the counter—really looking, with an attention so specific it raised the hair on my arms. Like he'd seen those hands somewhere before. Like he was checking them against a memory.

"How would you know?" I asked.

"My grandmother played piano," he said. "Arthritis took it from her slow, over years. She used to say her hands grieved louder than she did." He lifted his eyes to mine. "I believed her."

The rain hammered the windows. The generator chugged faithfully away outside, keeping one lamp alive, and in that small circle of light I felt something crack—not painfully, not like breaking, but like ice going out on a lake in spring. A shift. A loosening. Terrifyingly pleasant, which was the worst part, because pleasant things were how you got caught.

"I should check the tarp," he said eventually, glancing at the window.

"It's a category-3 storm. You'll check the tarp and the tarp will check you."

"Then I'll lose gracefully." He stood, gathered his jacket, hesitated at the door with the rain roaring beyond it. "Thank you for the soup. And for the tarp. And for—" He gestured vaguely at the kitchen, the lamp, the whole improbable evening. "This."

"Elias." He stopped. I don't know why I said the next thing. Maybe because the dark was close tonight and he'd carried a machine through a storm to hold it off for a stranger. "Why did you really come over? The pipes-frozen explanation was nonsense. It's October. Nothing freezes here until January."

He was quiet for a moment. Rain ran down the porch posts between us.

"When the power goes out," he said, "houses get very quiet. And quiet houses with cellos in them feel lonelier than they should." He pulled the door open onto the wall of noise and wind. "Goodnight, Beatrix."

Then he was gone into the dark, and I stood in my kitchen with my heart doing something complicated and unauthorized, listening to the generator hum like a held note that wouldn't resolve.

* * *

I didn't sleep well. Storms did that—filled the night with sounds that could be footsteps, could be branches, could be the past coming up the walk. Around midnight I gave up, lit the candle he'd left on my porch rail (he'd left candles too; of course he had), and sat by the window with tea going cold in my hands.

Across the grass, the Harrow house was a black shape with one lit window. His. I watched the light move—him walking through rooms that still smelled, presumably, of dust and decades—and I thought about what he'd said. *Quiet houses with cellos in them.*

Nobody knew that. Nobody had ever known that. I'd never said it aloud in my life: that the cello made the silence worse on bad nights, that its case in the corner was a witness, that some nights I covered it with a blanket like putting a sheet over a mirror in a dead man's house.

And this man—who had lived next door for eleven days—had said it like it was obvious. Like he'd read it somewhere. Like he knew.

I told myself it was coincidence. People said perceptive things; it was a whole industry. I told myself the way he'd looked at my hands meant nothing, that plenty of people found hands interesting, that I was a woman starved enough for being seen that any attention felt like prophecy.

The candle guttered. Outside, the storm began, at last, to spend itself, the thunder moving off toward the headland in long reluctant rolls.

And then I remembered—sitting there in the blue-dark with my cooling tea—that I hadn't checked the mail in two days, because of the storm, because of the tarp, because of him. And tomorrow the mail would come, the way it always came, and there might be a letter in it.

There was always a letter in it, sooner or later. Ten years of letters now, unsigned, unerring, arriving like clockwork from nowhere, saying exactly the thing I needed someone to say. *Your hands remember everything,* the last one had said. *That's not a curse. That's proof you're still in there.*

I set the tea down slowly.

*Hands are funny things. They remember everything.*

The words sat side by side in my chest, two sentences from two different mouths, wearing the same coat.

No, I told myself. No. Coincidence. The world was full of coincidences, full of strangers who stumbled near the truth without knowing it, and I would not—I refused to—build a cathedral out of a kind man's turn of phrase.

But I lay awake a long time anyway, listening to the last of the rain, and in the morning, before I made coffee, before I woke Priya's lesson plans or faced the day at all, I walked down my drive in my coat and slippers and opened the mailbox at the end of the walk.

There was one envelope inside.

Cream-colored. No stamp. Hand-delivered, which meant someone had walked up my drive in yesterday's storm—walked up my drive and left it, knowing I hadn't collected the mail, knowing—

My name wasn't on it. There was never a name. But the handwriting on the front, the single word *Beatrix* in that careful, slanted script I'd know anywhere in the world, was smudged at the edge.

Smudged. By rain.

I stood at the end of my driveway with the letter trembling in my hand, and I looked up the road toward the Harrow house, where a light was already on, where a man was probably already awake, and I did not go and ask.

Not yet.

But I carried the letter inside, and I set it on the table beside the mug he'd drunk from, unwashed, and I looked at the two of them together for a long time.
