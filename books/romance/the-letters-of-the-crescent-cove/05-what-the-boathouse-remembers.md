## 5. What the Boathouse Remembers

The solicitor's office smelled of lemon polish and old paper, and Elias Thorne sat in a leather chair too big for him while a man named Mr. Pemberton read his grandmother's will aloud in the tone of someone reciting a grocery list.

Elias had expected the house. The house was the point. He'd flown back from Singapore with a resignation letter drafted on the plane and a plan so clean it practically gleamed: restore Harrow House over six months, sell it to one of the families who'd been circling Crescent Cove real estate for years, pay off the debts that had followed him across three continents, and close the door on this whole stretch of coastline forever. A tidy transaction. Grief converted into liquidity.

"The property," Pemberton said, adjusting his glasses, "includes the boathouse."

Elias glanced up. "It's falling into the water. I've seen it."

"It is," Pemberton agreed, "in poor condition." He turned a page with unnecessary care. "Your grandmother was specific about it, Mr. Thorne. More specific than about anything else, if you want my professional observation. Clause nine. The boathouse may not be demolished, relocated, or substantially altered in its footprint. It must be preserved—restored, if restoration is feasible—or the entire estate passes, in its entirety, to the Aldermoor Coastal Conservation Trust."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when something has just changed shape.

"I'm sorry," Elias said. "Say that again."

Pemberton said it again, slower, with the patience of a man who had delivered unwelcome clauses before. If the boathouse went, everything went—the house, the land, the lot down by the road. All of it to the trust, which would hold it in perpetuity and never sell, because that was what conservation trusts did. And Elias could contest it, of course. Lawyers loved a challenge like that. They also billed like one.

"How much," Elias said, "to fight it?"

"More than you'd want to spend, and no guarantee of winning. Margaret Harrow wrote that clause herself, revised it four times, and initialed every revision. Judges notice things like that." Pemberton closed the folder. "She knew exactly what she was doing."

Elias drove home with the windows down even though it was cold, letting the salt air hit his face, trying to think of his grandmother as a woman instead of an obstacle. It wasn't working. She'd been dead eleven months and she was still steering.

He parked at the top of the drive and got out and walked down through the overgrown garden because he didn't want to look at the boathouse from the house. He wanted to do it properly, the way you approach something you've been avoiding.

The path was choked with bramble. The boards of the little dock were soft underfoot, several of them gone entirely, showing black water beneath. And there it stood at the end of the inlet: the boathouse, gray as driftwood, one wall bowed outward, the roof sagging in the middle like a tired back. Moss climbed the shingles. A heron stood in the shallows beside it, motionless, either guarding it or mourning it.

He'd forgotten how small it was. One room, really, built out over the water on pilings, with a pair of French doors that had once opened onto the deck. His grandmother had kept a rowboat here when he was a boy. He remembered the smell—tar and old wood and lake-brine—and he remembered being told, more than once, *stay out of the boathouse when there are students, Elias, they need quiet.*

Students. He'd forgotten that too, or filed it somewhere he didn't go. Every summer, June through August, his grandmother had rented the boathouse to the music camp up the hill—a practice room, they called it, though it was barely bigger than a shed. Kids came down the path all day carrying instruments in cases, looking serious and sunburned.

One of them had played the cello.

Elias stood at the edge of the rotted dock with his hands in his pockets and let the memory come, because it was going to come anyway and it was easier not to fight it.

He was fourteen. He'd been sent to Crescent Cove for the summer the way difficult boys were sent places—his parents' marriage dissolving in another country, his grandmother's house designated a holding pen. He'd been angry at everything: the weather, the food, the fact of himself. He used to walk down to the inlet at dusk to smoke cigarettes he didn't actually like, and one evening, halfway down the path, he heard the cello.

Not a recording. Not a lesson—lessons stopped and started, corrected themselves. This was continuous, unbroken, a long line of sound coming out of the little building on the water like the building itself had learned to sing. He'd stood in the trees for twenty minutes. Then forty. He missed dinner. He didn't care. Whatever grief he'd carried around all summer—that shapeless, adolescent fury at a world that kept rearranging itself without permission—the cello took it and held it and made it bearable. Made it almost beautiful.

When the playing stopped, he'd run. He never saw who it was. He only knew, from the camp schedule tacked up outside the mess hall, that the practice slot belonged to a girl named B. Eastwick, and that she was fifteen, and that he spent the rest of that summer engineering reasons to walk past the boathouse at dusk, hoping to hear her again, never quite working up the nerve to knock.

Ten years later, standing in front of the same rotting walls, Elias understood two things at once, and both of them landed hard.

The first was that his grandmother had known. Of course she'd known. She'd found him once, that summer, standing in the trees with his cigarette burning down between his fingers, and she hadn't said a word—just touched his shoulder and walked on. She'd known what the boathouse was to him before he did.

The second was that clause nine wasn't a legal technicality. It was a message, addressed to him specifically, sent from eleven months ago: *You don't get to erase this place. You don't get to erase her.*

"Damn it, Gran," he said quietly.

The heron lifted off the shallows and beat away up the inlet, and Elias stayed where he was until the light went, staring at the boathouse like a man reading a letter he'd have sworn he never received.

* * *

Beatrix Eastwick noticed the truck first—a battered pickup parked crooked near the foot of the Harrow drive—and then the man standing at the end of the rotten dock, motionless, for what her watch insisted was nearly an hour.

She hadn't meant to be out. Her rule was simple: the Harrow property was Elias Thorne's business, and she had made a discipline of other people's business staying other people's. But it was Thursday, which meant she walked the inlet loop after her last student left—it was safe, it was scheduled, it was hers—and Thursday's route passed directly along the fence line where the bramble had died back and the water showed through, and there he was.

She should have kept walking. She told herself to keep walking. Her feet did not keep walking.

From thirty yards, through the thinning trees, she watched him stare at the boathouse, and something in the set of his shoulders made her chest ache in a way she resented. He looked like a man at a graveside. She knew the posture. She'd worn it herself, five years ago, standing in an empty green room in London while her career quietly ended behind a closed door.

*Don't,* she thought. *Whatever you're about to feel about a stranger's grief, put it down.*

But then he said something—to the boathouse, to the water, to nobody—and the wind carried the shape of it to her, if not the words, and Beatrix discovered she was already pushing open the gap in the fence, already following the path she'd walked a hundred times at sixteen with a cello case bumping against her leg.

"You're trespassing," she said, when she was close enough, because it was easier than saying anything true.

Elias turned. Up close, in the failing light, he looked wrecked in a quiet way—not the dramatic kind, the kind that gets combed over before anyone sees it. "Technically," he said, "I think I'm the owner."

"Owners can trespass. It's called adverse possession of your own bad decisions." She winced at herself. Small talk had never been her instrument. "Sorry. That was—I don't know what that was."

"No, it was good. I'm keeping it." He looked back at the boathouse. When he spoke again, the flippancy was gone. "Did you know this thing is held together mostly by stubbornness?"

"Some of it," Beatrix said. "The north wall was already bowing when I was seventeen."

The words were out before she could weigh them. She watched them land—watched him go very still, the way people do when a sentence contains more than it appears to—and felt the familiar cold slide of exposure start up her spine.

"You played here," he said. Not a question.

"Everyone at camp played here. It was the practice room. It smelled like tar and spiders and sounded better than any hall I've ever been in." She kept her voice level, factual, a tour guide's voice. "The acoustics are a fluke. Water on three sides, low ceiling. Sound has nowhere to hide."

"And you—" He stopped. Started again. "What did you play?"

Why does it matter? she wanted to ask. Why are you looking at me like that?

"Everything," she said. "It was summer. Nobody assigned repertoire down here. You played whatever you wanted until it got dark." She nodded at the structure, at the bowed wall, at the roof like a tired back. "It won't survive another winter, you know. Maybe not even this one. The pilings on the east side are gone soft. I used to be able to tell the tide by the sound of the boards."

"You still can?" Something moved across his face—too fast for her to name it.

"Habit." She wrapped her arms around herself. The wind off the water had teeth now. "Why are you staring at it like that? If you're planning to tear it down, you should know half the town will write you angry letters. Mrs. Okafor still teaches recorder in there on Saturdays. Under protest. There's a bucket for the leaks."

Something flickered in his expression at the word *letters*—a wince, quickly buried—and Beatrix catalogued it without understanding it, the way she catalogued wrong notes: automatically, and with a small private sting.

"I'm not tearing it down," Elias said. "I can't. Legally." He laughed, but it came out wrong, hollow. "My grandmother put it in her will. Preserve the boathouse or lose the whole estate. House, land, everything goes to a conservation trust." He rubbed a hand over his jaw. "I flew ten thousand miles to sell a house, and it turns out the house comes with a hostage."

Beatrix felt something loosen in her chest and didn't examine it. Relief, maybe. Or its cousin.

"So you're stuck with it," she said.

"I'm stuck with it." He looked at the boathouse again, and this time the look was different—less graveside, more cornered. "Except restoring it properly costs money I don't have, and selling the rest of the property probably requires showing buyers a boathouse that isn't actively dying, and—" He exhaled. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because I asked a question and you're constitutionally incapable of lying?" It came out softer than she intended. Almost fond. She didn't know this man well enough to be fond of him. Three conversations, one storm, one borrowed generator. She recalibrated. "Sorry. Again."

"Stop apologizing. It's disorienting." He glanced sideways at her, and in the last of the light his eyes were the color of the water under the dock. "Do you really think it can be saved? The boathouse."

Beatrix studied the structure she had loved more than any concert hall she ever played—the fluke acoustics, the tar-and-salt smell, the dusk light coming through those French doors while she worked through the Elgar until her fingers gave out and the heron fished the shallows like an usher who'd seen it all before. The place where she had been, briefly, at fifteen, completely unafraid.

"It's wood and water," she said. "Wood and water can always be argued with. It takes money and stubbornness." She paused. "You seem to have access to at least one of those."

"Flattery," Elias said. "From the neighbor. My week is complete."

They stood there a moment longer, the two of them and the dying building, and the silence between them was strange—not awkward, not comfortable, but *occupied*, as though a third person had joined them and neither would acknowledge it. Somewhere out on the inlet, a fish jumped. The heron returned to its post in the shallows.

"I should go," Beatrix said finally. "Dinner. Routine. You'll learn I'm very boring."

"I doubt that," Elias said, and there was something underneath it, some current she couldn't chart, and she decided—firmly, deliberately—not to look for it.

She walked back up the path with her hands in her coat pockets, and at the fence line she stopped, because stopping was safer than what she almost did next, which was turn around and say *I played here the summer I was fifteen, and I was happy, and I've never figured out how to be that again.*

She didn't say it. She went home. She ate standing up at the counter. And when she caught herself, an hour later, standing at her window in the dark, looking at the lit rectangle of the Harrow kitchen and wondering what Elias Thorne had been about to say when he stopped himself twice—she pulled the curtain shut and told herself, as she had told herself for five years, that some doors were better left closed.

Down at the water's edge, unseen, the boathouse settled another millimeter on its rotting pilings, patient as an archive, keeping what it kept.
