## 4. The Carpenter Who Wouldn't Look Up

The Old Mill House announced itself the way old things do — not all at once but in pieces, each one a small argument against time. First the sign: hand-painted, the letters gone soft at their edges, *OLD MILL HOUSE — EST. 1898 — VACANCY*, hanging from two iron brackets that had been repainted so many times the original scrollwork was buried under decades of good intentions. Then the building itself, rising from the riverbank like something that had grown there rather than been built — three stories of weathered clapboard gone silver-gray, a deep porch sagging gently in the middle like a tired back, dormer windows tilted at angles that suggested they had long ago stopped caring what anyone thought of them.

Lena pulled her rental car into the gravel lot and sat with her hands on the wheel.

She had chosen it from her phone in Elias Marsh's parking garage, scrolling through the two lodging options Millbrook offered. The Riverbend Motor Lodge was a strip of beige units by the highway. The other listing had only one photograph: the porch at golden hour, a rocking chair, a bowl of apples on a table between two more chairs. She had booked it without letting herself think about why the photograph made her chest hurt.

Now she let herself think about it.

Eleven years. She did the arithmetic the way she always did when she came back to this town, which was never — eleven years of never — and the number refused to behave like a number. It behaved like a door. It behaved like the space between one heartbeat and the next, which is how long it had taken her, that last morning, to decide not to wake him.

She got out of the car. The river was audible before she saw it, a low constant voice beneath everything else, and the smell hit her second: water and cut wood and something green growing wild along the banks. The air of Millbrook had a taste she had spent eleven years forgetting and recognized in half a second, the way you recognize a language you were once fluent in.

*You're here for work,* she told herself, hauling her suitcase out of the trunk. *Six weeks. A book. A paycheck with enough zeros to make your agent weep.* She had told herself versions of this before, in other towns, standing in front of other strangers' lives with her notebook open and her own life folded up small enough to fit in her pocket. It had always worked. It had always worked because there had never been anything in these towns worth remembering.

The porch steps creaked under her weight, a sound so specific it went through her like a needle finding an old scar. Two rocking chairs. The apple bowl was real; someone had filled it recently, the fruit still cool from a cellar somewhere. And on the wall beside the front door, framed behind glass gone cloudy with age, hung a photograph of the inn in its prime — cars from another century parked along the millrace, women in white dresses on this same porch, the whole building glowing with the smug permanence of things that have no idea what's coming.

Lena looked away from it and opened the door.

Inside, the lobby was exactly as memory insisted it should be, which unsettled her more than if it had changed. The wide plank floors, dark with a century of feet. The front desk built from a single slab of black walnut, its edge worn into a shallow curve where generations of elbows had rested. A staircase climbing the far wall in a slow spiral, its banister a continuous ribbon of carved oak. The air smelled of lemon oil and cold fireplace ash and, faintly, of river.

The desk was empty.

"Hello?" Her voice came out smaller than she intended. She set her suitcase down and waited, listening to the building listen back. Somewhere above her, floorboards shifted under a footstep. Somewhere deeper in the house, water moved through pipes with a knock and a sigh. She stood in the middle of the lobby feeling observed — not by people but by the house itself, by every surface that remembered her, by the staircase she had once climbed at two in the morning on bare feet, laughing, with a boy's hand pulling hers.

A door opened behind the desk, into some back room, and a man stepped through carrying a length of trim molding across his shoulder like a yoke.

He didn't look up. He said, "Be right with you," in a voice roughened by sawdust, and turned to set the molding down against the wall, and Lena had time to register only the hands — forearms flecked with wood dust, a fresh scrape along one knuckle, the particular competence of fingers that knew materials — before he straightened and turned around.

Jonah Reyes.

The recognition was not emotional first. It was physical, a detonation low in her body, her knees going loose, her lungs forgetting their job. Eleven years collapsed to nothing. He was broader through the shoulders than the boy she'd known, his hair shorter, a line or two carved deeper around his mouth, but his eyes — dark brown, nearly black, with that slight downward tilt that had always made him look like he was hearing bad news even when he was happy — his eyes were unchanged, and they found her across the lobby and stopped.

Neither of them moved.

The river spoke through the walls. A clock somewhere ticked with the patience of furniture. Lena became aware of her own body as a collection of betrayals: her hands, which had begun to shake; her throat, working around nothing; her face, which she could feel flushing hot and then draining pale, cycling through every honest reaction while she scrambled for the professional mask she wore like other people wore glasses.

"Checking in," she said. Her voice held. That surprised her. "I have a reservation. Voss."

Something moved behind his eyes — a flinch, or its suppression — and then he nodded, once, and walked to the desk, and picked up the register book, and became, with visible effort, an innkeeper.

"Room's ready." He opened the book, ran his finger down the page. "Voss. Yes." He wrote nothing yet; he slid the pen across the counter toward her. "Three nights?"

"I — " She had booked three nights because she hadn't known how long she'd stay, because she hadn't let herself think about any of it. Now the question hung there, absurdly domestic, loaded with everything. "Actually, I may extend. Through the week. Possibly longer."

"How long?"

"A few weeks. Maybe six."

His pen paused above the paper. Six weeks. The exact span of her assignment, though he couldn't know that, though she would rather have swallowed glass than tell him. But something passed over his face anyway, some calculation he chose not to share, and he wrote *six wks?* lightly in pencil in the margin, a question mark trailing off like smoke.

"Kitchen closes at eight," he said. "There's coffee in the morning, whenever you come down. Heat's finicky in the east rooms, so I put you in the west. Third floor."

The third floor. The room under the eaves with the window that looked out over the millrace, where — 

"You remember the rooms," she said, before she could stop herself.

He didn't look up. He was writing her name in the register now, letter by letter, and she watched his hand do it, watched the familiar slant of his script form the unfamiliar shape of her lie — *Lena Voss*, as though Voss were the truest thing about her. "I restored them," he said. "Hard to forget a room you've rebuilt."

Of course. Of course he had rebuilt them. She looked past him, really looked, for the first time since he'd walked through the door, and saw what she should have seen immediately: the inn was dying and being kept alive at the same time, and both facts were Jonah's. The newel post at the base of the stairs gleamed with fresh oil, but the third baluster from the top was missing entirely, a gap in the banister's sentence. Half the lobby ceiling had been replastered, smooth and white as new snow; the other half still showed water stains spreading in continents of yellow and brown. A stack of sash windows leaned against the wall near the fireplace, each one stripped to bare wood, waiting. A paint can served as a doorstop. Sawdust ghosted the corners of the floorboards no matter how often they'd been swept.

He was saving it alone. She understood that without asking, the way she understood the scrape on his knuckle and the dust in his hair and the careful way he held his left shoulder slightly higher than his right, favoring some old strain. One man, a lease, and a building that needed ten men and a fortune.

"How is it," she asked, and hated how the question came out — soft, unguarded, a hand reaching across a table — "still standing?"

"Stubbornness." He finished writing and closed the register. "Mostly mine."

"And after?" She heard herself say it and couldn't take it back. *After.* After the lease. After whatever deadline was circling this place like weather. She had read the development documents on the plane — she'd read everything, that was her job, she read other people's lives for a living — and somewhere in the appendices of the Millbrook Rising prospectus there had been a parcel map, a riverside footprint, a structure marked in red for removal. She had looked at that map for forty minutes somewhere over Pennsylvania and had not let herself understand it.

Jonah's jaw tightened. She watched the muscle jump under the skin, watched him decide how much truth to spend on a stranger checking in.

"After," he said, "isn't settled."

"It will be soon, won't it? The — " She gestured vaguely, cowardly, toward the east, toward the glass tower upstream where Elias Marsh kept his office above the river. "There's a project. I read about it."

"Everyone read about it." He took a key from a board of cubbyholes behind him — an actual metal key, on a fob of braided leather gone dark with handling — and set it on the counter between them. "Room nine. Top of the stairs, turn right. Breakfast's included if you want it. Checkout's noon."

The transactional litany of an innkeeper, delivered flat as a receipt. But his hand stayed on the key a moment longer than it needed to, and when Lena reached for it their fingers almost touched — almost, separated by a centimeter of charged air — and he pulled his hand back as if the counter had gone hot.

"Thank you," she said.

"Need help with your bag?"

"No." Too fast. "No, thank you. I've got it."

He nodded again, that same controlled single nod, and turned back toward the door behind the desk, toward whatever work was waiting there, and Lena stood alone in the lobby holding a brass key that weighed nothing and everything.

She should have left it at that. She knows that now. There was a version of this story where she signed the book, took the key, climbed the stairs, and got through six weeks on politeness and avoidance, two skills she had mastered. But she was already signing the register — the pen scratchy, the paper soft, the ink blooming slightly where the old paper drank it — and as she wrote her name she saw, just below her line, the previous entries in his handwriting. Guests, weeks of them, names and dates in that same slanted script. And below those, in different ink, older: a column of numbers, measurements, lumber calculations, jotted in the margins like a man who thought in board-feet. And below those, at the bottom of the used page, half-erased by an attempt at erasure that hadn't quite succeeded:

*J.R. — day 4,017.*

Day four thousand and seventeen. She stared at it. Eleven years, counted in days, written in a ledger meant for guests, then scrubbed at with an eraser by a man who apparently counted and was ashamed of counting. Four thousand mornings waking up in this house. Four thousand nights closing it down. And at the end of every day, a mark, a tally, a small stubborn act of arithmetic against loss.

Her signature dried crooked on the page because her hand was shaking too hard to write it straight.

Jonah had stopped in the doorway behind the desk. She felt him there before she looked up — some frequency between them that had never fully shut off, a radio station broadcasting on a band only the two of them could hear. When she raised her eyes he was watching her, finally, fully, for the first time since he'd walked through the door, and his face was doing something complicated and quiet, grief and anger and longing all pressed flat under a carpenter's practical calm, and she understood that he had seen her see the tally, and that neither of them would say one word about it.

"Third stair from the bottom sticks," he said. "Step over it."

"I remember."

The words escaped before she could catch them, and the silence that followed was the loudest sound the old house had ever made. His hand tightened on the doorframe — she saw the tendons stand out — and for one suspended moment the whole eleven-year dam stood at its breaking point, every unsaid thing pressing against it: *where did you go, why didn't you write, I waited, I counted, I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry.*

Then a pipe knocked somewhere in the walls, the house clearing its throat, and the moment broke and drained away.

"Welcome to the Old Mill House," Jonah said, in the voice of a man reading from a script he'd written to protect himself, and disappeared through the door.

Lena stood a long time in the empty lobby. The light through the front windows had gone amber, laying long stripes across the floorboards, catching the dust motes the way light does in places that hold their breath. She picked up her suitcase. At the third stair she stepped over the sticking tread without thinking, her body remembering what her mind had spent a decade burying, and she climbed the staircase under the carved oak banister — his banister, the one he was sanding by hand somewhere in the future she hadn't lived yet — up and up into the dimness of the third floor, where Room Nine waited with its window over the millrace.

She unlocked the door. Inside, the room was clean and spare, a bed, a washstand, a chair, and the window, and through the window the river, moving the way it had always moved, indifferent and eternal, carrying the light downstream toward the sea.

She set her suitcase down and did not unpack it. Unpacking meant staying, and some part of her was still at the threshold, still telling herself the old lie — *just work, just six weeks, just a book* — while her shaking hands betrayed her, while the tally in the register burned behind her eyes like a brand: *day 4,017*, and counting.

Downstairs, faintly, she heard the back door open and close, and the rasp of a saw beginning its patient work.

* * *

She woke at midnight to the sound of hammering.

Not frantic — rhythmic, deliberate, the measured stroke of a man driving nails by feel in the dark. Three taps, a pause, three taps. It came up through the bones of the house, through the floorboards, through the mattress, a pulse in the building's chest. Lena lay in the narrow bed listening, her heart keeping time with it, and understood that Jonah was downstairs working by lamplight, restoring a place that had less than two months to live, nailing the future onto a frame already sold.

She got up. She stood at the window over the millrace. Moonlight lay on the water like spilled milk, and beneath her, through the glow of a workshop window she could see the shadow of him moving — sawing now, the long steady draw of the blade, a sound like breathing.

She pressed her palm flat against the cold glass, over the river, over the roof of the shop where he worked, and whispered the thing she had carried across eleven years and eight hundred miles and had promised herself she'd never say:

"I'm sorry I couldn't—"

The sentence died there, unfinished, the way so many of her sentences had died, and she stood in the dark of Room Nine until the hammering stopped and the light below went out, and even then she did not sleep.
