## 10. Lending Pieces

The morning after the bridge, I did what I have always done with feeling too large to hold: I put it in someone else's mouth.

I sat at the inn's little writing desk with the window cracked to the smell of the river and drafted Elias Marsh's chapter four — "Foundations," he'd titled it, though the title was mine; everything in that book was mine — in which a nineteen-year-old Elias arrives in Millbrook with forty dollars and a borrowed truck and the conviction, as he tells it, that a man is only as good as what he refuses to quit. The prose came out clean. It came out beautiful. It came out like water running over glass, touching nothing.

*He learned early that the world does not reward hesitation,* I wrote. *By twenty-two he had flipped his first house; by twenty-five he could stand on an empty lot and see, whole and finished, the building that would make him.*

None of it was false, exactly. That was the trick of ghostwriting, the thing I'd gotten so good at that I sometimes forgot it was a trick: you take the true pieces of a person and arrange them into a shape more flattering than the truth. You lend them your sentences the way a bank lends money, against collateral they don't know they're putting up. Elias's collateral was his legend. Mine, all these years, had been myself.

I wrote nine thousand words that week. I wrote them with my jaw set and my coffee going cold and my eyes fixed on the middle distance where the river showed silver between the trees. I did not go down to the workshop. I did not linger at the front desk in the evenings hoping Jonah would look up from his ledger. When our paths crossed in the hallway — and the Old Mill House being what it was, narrow and creaking and built for intimacy by people who never imagined strangers — I gave him the nod you give a neighbor. *Morning.* Nothing attached to it.

I felt him feel it. That was the worst part. I felt the way his shoulders changed when he saw me coming, the half-second where hope stood up in him before he remembered to sit it back down. He didn't ask. He didn't press. He just went quieter, the way rooms go when someone stops playing music in them.

Because here is what terror looks like on me, in case you wondered: it looks like productivity. On the bridge, hearing my own nineteen-year-old voice read back to me — *I'm scared, Jonah, I'm so scared, and I can't tell you why yet, but if you knew you'd hate the debt I come with* — something had cracked open in my chest, and through the crack was pouring not relief but volume. Eleven years of grief, plus the baby, plus the leaving, plus the letter's torn-off ending, plus this man standing three feet from me holding proof that I had loved him and never stopped. It was too much. It was a flood, and I am a woman who has spent her whole life building levees out of other people's stories.

So I retreated behind the one wall I trusted: work. I told myself it was discipline. I told myself the Marsh manuscript had a deadline and deadlines were honest in a way feelings weren't. I told myself that once the book was done I would turn around and face whatever was waiting.

But the truth, which I wrote down nowhere, was simpler and uglier: I was afraid that if I stopped moving, I would have to decide whether to stay. And deciding to stay meant risking everything again — the whole heart, not the borrowed piece — and I had already lost one everything in this town. My hands shook when I thought about it. So I made sure I was always thinking about something else.

* * *

Jonah, meanwhile, threw himself into the inn the way I threw myself into the manuscript — which is to say, we were doing the same thing from opposite ends of the same wound.

I could hear him through the floorboards. The scrape of the plane, the sigh of sandpaper, the tap-tap-tap of him testing a board for rot. Some mornings he started at six, and I would lie in bed listening to the old house receive his attention and think: *he is loving this building because I won't let him love me.* He worked on the second-floor banister that week, the original one, black walnut gone gray as driftwood, and each evening another section of it emerged from its shroud, dark and gleaming, the grain rising under his hands like something remembering itself.

One night I passed the top of the stairs and found him kneeling there with a cloth, working oil into the wood by lamplight, and I swear the banister looked better than either of us did. He glanced up. I kept walking. Behind me I heard him exhale, slow, and then the soft rhythm of the cloth resume, patient as tide.

"You missed a spot," I said from the landing, before I could stop myself.

"It'll keep," he said. "Everything here keeps."

I went downstairs faster than dignity allowed.

* * *

On Thursday, the notice went up in the general store window and got photographed and texted around town before noon: **PUBLIC MEETING — MILLBROOK RISING PHASE TWO — OLD MILL HOUSE PARCEL — THURSDAY 7 PM — GRANGE HALL.**

I read it standing in the store doorway with a bag of oranges going cold in my hand. Phase Two. The parcel. Our parcel — the inn's, Jonah's lease, the building whose floors I slept above. Elias hadn't mentioned it in our sessions. He'd talked about vision, legacy, the riverfront reimagined. He had not used the word *demolition* in my hearing, though everyone in town apparently used nothing else.

"You coming tonight?" the store owner asked. Mrs. Okafor, silver braids, a woman who weighed customers with one glance. "You're the writer, aren't you? The one staying out at the Mill House."

"I'm — working on a book," I said. "About Mr. Marsh."

Her glance became a measurement. "Then you should come," she said. "A person ought to hear both sides of a story before she writes it."

* * *

The Grange Hall was full past capacity, folding chairs marching down the aisles, people standing along the walls, a few out the door craning to hear. I took a seat near the back, notebook open, ghostwriter's reflex, and watched the town I'd fled eleven years ago fill a room with its worry.

Elias presided from the front like weather. Charcoal suit, no tie, the easy authority of a man who has never been told no by anything smaller than a recession. Beside him stood two lieutenants with renderings: the riverfront reborn, glass and timber, a "heritage plaza" where — I squinted at the artist's conception — the Old Mill House appeared to have been replaced by a lawn and a historical marker.

"Phase Two represents two hundred construction jobs," Elias said, "and ninety permanent positions. A riverwalk. A market hall. This town has been bleeding young people for thirty years. I'm offering a tourniquet."

Murmurs. Hope is loud even when it whispers.

Then a man stood up in the third row — Ray Delgado, I'd learn later, who ran the hardware store his father opened in 1971. "And the inn? What happens to the inn?"

"The structure is unsound," Elias said smoothly. "Engineering report's available. We're preserving the name — the restaurant in Phase Three will be called the Old Mill House. Same spirit."

"Same spirit," Ray repeated. "My wife waitresses there Fridays. Has for nine years."

"Transitional employment support—"

"Marta Hale baked in that kitchen before your company had a zip code here." A woman near the front, seventy if she was a day, spine straight as a mast. Marta Hale. I wrote the name down without knowing yet why my hand pressed harder on the pen. "That kitchen fed this town through the flood of '96. You want to talk about what's unsound, Mr. Marsh? Talk to the foundation of this place. It isn't the building."

Applause broke out — scattered, then wider. Elias let it run its course with the patience of a man watching rain delay a tee time.

"I understand attachment," he said. "But attachment doesn't pay for a new roof. The leaseholder has known the terms for years. The property changes hands at the end of the month, and sentiment—" he spread his hands, regretful, magnanimous, "—sentiment isn't a deed."

*Sentiment isn't a deed.* I wrote it down verbatim, and beneath it, without meaning to, I wrote: *but whose deed?*

The meeting rolled on. A young couple with a baby spoke about the apartments above the inn — $900 a month, the only place in town they could afford. The fishing guide whose business ran off the inn's dock. The woman who taught piano in the parlor on Saturdays. One by one they stood and named themselves as load-bearing walls of the place Elias called unsound, and I sat in the back with my throat tight, understanding suddenly that this wasn't a building on a parcel. It was a circulatory system. And Elias had drawn up plans to cut it at the neck and call the surgery revitalization.

Near the end, the door opened and Jonah slipped in late, sawdust still on his flannel, and stood along the back wall beside me. Close enough that I could smell cut walnut and linseed oil. Neither of us spoke. On the stage, Elias gestured toward the rendering, toward the future, toward the lawn where the inn used to be, and Jonah watched him with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I could — because it was the expression I'd seen in my own mirror all week. Grief wearing work clothes.

When the meeting broke up, the crowd drained toward the doors in eddies of argument and fear, and Elias worked the room like a tide coming in, shaking hands, absorbing anger, converting it to patience. Then his eyes found me at the back. Found Jonah beside me. Something moved behind his charm — quick, proprietary, recalculating — and he angled toward us.

"Lena." Warm. Loud enough to be overheard. "Working hard, I hope. The book won't write itself." His gaze flicked to Jonah, polite as a customs stamp. "Mr. Reyes. Keeping busy?"

"Keeping the place standing," Jonah said. "Until the end of the month, anyway."

"Well." Elias smiled with all his teeth. "Progress requires clear ground."

He moved on. Jonah and I stood in the emptying hall, and the distance between us — twelve inches of cold air we'd maintained all week — suddenly felt like the most expensive thing in the room.

"He knows about us," Jonah said quietly. "Or thinks he does. He wanted you to hear him say it."

"I know."

"You've been hiding in that manuscript all week. I can hear you typing through the floor, Lena. Nine hours a day. Nobody types nine hours a day unless they're running."

My chest did the thing it does, the closing thing, the levee thing. "It's my job."

"Your job is lending him your voice." He said it without cruelty, which made it worse. "You've been doing it so long I don't think you remember what yours sounds like."

I left without answering. Outside, the night smelled of river and cut grass, and the Grange Hall windows glowed behind me with the last stragglers, and somewhere across town the Old Mill House stood dark and sound and doomed, holding its walls around a letter, a lease, a kitchen that fed a town through a flood.

At my desk I opened the Marsh manuscript and stared at chapter five, the one where Elias conquers his first market crash through sheer force of will. The prose waited for me, smooth and hollow, ready to be filled.

Instead I pulled out a fresh page and wrote, at the top, in my own handwriting, in my own voice, for no one's book but the one forming against my will inside me:

*Sentiment isn't a deed. But somebody check the deed.*

Underneath it, my hand kept moving — names, dates, the timeline Elias had given me in interview one, the acquisition of the Millbrook parcels, 1989. There was a gap in it. I'd noted it weeks ago, professionally, uneasily, and filed it away.

I wasn't filing it anymore.

Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, I heard the scratch of sandpaper start up again — Jonah, awake too, saving what couldn't be saved — and I bent over the county records request forms and began, quietly, to find out what else couldn't.
