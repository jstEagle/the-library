## 13. Daniel Comes Home

The bus from the city came in on the coast road at four in the afternoon, and Eli watched it from the doorway of the packing shed with his arms crossed over his chest like a man holding something shut.

He'd known Daniel was coming for three days — since the terse text message that had arrived while Eli was up a ladder thinning apples: *Festival. Mum wants me there. Staying at hers.* Fourteen words, no punctuation wasted, no warmth in them that wasn't owed to their mother. That was how his brother wrote now. That was how his brother did most things.

The bus sighed to a stop outside the general store, and one passenger got off.

Eli hadn't seen Daniel in person in almost two years. He was broader through the shoulders than he'd been at twenty-eight, or maybe he just carried himself heavier, the way men do when they've stopped expecting things to go their way. He wore a city jacket that didn't belong here anymore, if it ever had, and he stood on the gravel a moment after the bus pulled away, looking down the main street of Bellbird Bay as though checking whether the town still remembered him.

It did. Mrs. Calloway, sweeping her doorstep, gave him a nod that was half greeting, half warning. Old Tom Ferrier lifted two fingers off his beer glass in the window of the pub. Small town. Long memory. The Marchetti name meant something here, and not all of it was kind — not since the expulsion, not since the boy who'd been sent away and the girl who'd been blamed, and the whole rotten business that had split this town down its middle like a lightning-struck trunk.

Daniel shouldered his bag and started walking toward their mother's house without looking left or right, which was how you walked through Bellbird Bay when you didn't want to be seen. Eli knew the walk. He'd invented it himself, years ago, before he'd learned a better trick: to stand very still and let people look.

*You have to tell her.*

The thought arrived on schedule, the way it had been arriving for weeks now, ever since the night Jonah had stayed in the cottage above the packing shed and everything between them had stopped being a performance. It arrived now with the particular weight of a deadline, because Daniel was here, and Daniel could not be hidden, and every hour Eli let pass was an hour the lie grew roots.

He pushed off the doorframe and crossed the yard.

"Daniel."

His brother turned. For a second — just a second — Eli saw the boy underneath, the one who used to climb the fence line with him and steal windfalls and dare each other to eat unripe plums until they were sick behind the tool shed. Then the years closed back over it like water.

"Eli." A nod. "Orchard looks good."

"It is good." He heard how defensive it sounded and hated it. "Best season in a decade. We're running a festival Saturday."

"So I hear." Daniel shifted the bag on his shoulder. His eyes went past Eli, up the slope, to where the rows climbed the hill in their long green ranks, and something moved across his face too fast to name. "Funny place for you to end up. Of all the orchards in the world."

"Funny place for either of us."

They stood there in the road dust while a tractor droned somewhere beyond the hill, and Eli understood that they were both thinking about the same thing and neither of them would say it, because they hadn't said it in fifteen years, and fifteen years is a long time to build a habit out of silence.

"Mum's expecting me," Daniel said finally.

"I know. She told me you were coming." Eli hesitated. "There's a supper Thursday. Here, at the orchard. Harvest workers, some of the town council, the family that owns the land next door. You could come."

Something flickered in Daniel's eyes — suspicion first, then something rawer. "Will she be there?"

Eli's chest went tight. *She.* There was only one she, and they both knew it. Fifteen years, and Jonah Thornbury still occupied the center of his brother's map of the world like a wound that had never been allowed to close.

"Yeah," Eli said. "She'll be there."

"Then I'll come." Daniel smiled, and it was the worst smile Eli had ever seen on him, thin and bright as a blade. "Be good to see what became of her."

He walked on toward their mother's house. Eli stood in the road a long time after he'd gone, watching the dust settle, feeling the festival — his festival, their festival, the one Jonah had built out of nothing to save this orchard — turn in his stomach into something else entirely.

* * *

That evening he found Jonah in the barn, standing on a hay bale with a clipboard, directing two of the seasonal workers in stringing lanterns along the rafters. She'd been at it since dawn. The harvest festival had swallowed her whole — bunting and band bookings and a cider press demonstration and a raffle she'd bullied half the town into donating prizes for — and she wore her exhaustion like other people wore perfume. It suited her. She was lit up from inside.

"There he is," she said when she saw him, and the smile she gave him was the private one, the one that belonged to the version of him who brought her coffee at five in the morning and knew which row of Gravensteins she liked to eat lunch under. "Tell Marta the lanterns go higher. Higher than that. This is a festival, not a funeral."

"You planned a funeral once," Marta said mildly, from her ladder. "For the mayor's wife. Everyone said it was beautiful."

"That was different. Grief has its own aesthetic." Jonah hopped down off the bale, brushed straw from her jeans, and came to him. Up close he could see the ink smudged on her wrist where she'd been writing lists against her forearm again, an old habit he'd learned to find unbearably endearing. "Please tell me you're not here with bad news."

"I'm here with supper," he said. "And a question."

"Supper first. Always supper first."

They ate on the tailgate of his truck, in the last of the light, with the orchard going gold and blue around them and the smell of ripe fruit thick enough to swim in. She talked with her hands, laying out tomorrow's schedule — the band arriving, the cider press, the seating for the elders who'd complain about anything but whom she'd seated with surgical care anyway. He listened and answered where answering was required and felt, the entire time, the question sitting in his throat like a stone he kept trying to swallow.

Finally she stopped, mid-sentence, and looked at him.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The quiet thing. Where you go somewhere else and leave your body here eating potatoes." She set down her fork. "Eli. What is it?"

Here it was, then. The open door. All he had to do was walk through it. *My brother's coming to the festival. My brother is Daniel Marchetti. The boy from your school, the one who was expelled, the one they said you ruined — he's mine. He's my blood, and I've known who you were since the day I found you fighting that tractor in the rain, and I let you walk into this — into me — without telling you.*

He looked at her face, open and tired and happy, happier than he'd ever seen her, and he thought about what the truth would do to it. Not eventually. Immediately. Tonight, three days before the festival she'd poured herself into, with Vivienne already circling with her lawyers and her ledger, with the whole fragile structure of the season balanced on the idea that Jonah Thornbury had finally come home to stay.

If he told her now, she might forgive him. Eventually. But she would spend the festival knowing, and everyone else would see her knowing, and the thing they'd built — the real thing, the terrifyingly real thing — would be dragged out onto the bandstand next to the bunting and examined by the whole town.

Later, he told himself. After the festival. When she isn't carrying everything on her shoulders already. There's no version of this that isn't cruel tonight; there might be a version tomorrow that's only honest.

He knew, even as he assembled the thought, that it was a lie dressed in mercy's clothing. He'd been dressing it that way for weeks.

"The question," Jonah prompted.

"My brother's coming Saturday," he said. "Daniel. He's staying at Mum's."

Her fork paused halfway to her mouth. "Your brother? You've never mentioned a brother."

"No," Eli said, and the word came out flat, and he watched her register the flatness, file it away. "We're not close. He lives in the city. Works in construction management. He's — it's complicated."

"Family usually is." She studied him a moment longer, and he braced for her to push, because Jonah pushed — she'd pushed him about the tractor, about the fences, about every locked door he'd ever shown her. But instead she reached over and squeezed his knee. "Bring him Saturday. Anyone who's yours, I want there."

*Anyone who's yours.* He nearly flinched.

"He's not easy," Eli managed. "Fair warning."

"Neither am I." She picked her fork back up, and the moment passed, and the stone in his throat settled another inch lower, into a place he suspected he'd never dig it out of.

* * *

Thursday's supper went well, which was the cruelest part.

The long table under the barn's open doors, lanterns strung overhead, the workers and the neighbors and old Mrs. Aldous from the council telling the story of the flood of '09 for the eleventh time that summer. Jonah moving down the table like weather, filling glasses, deflecting complaints, laughing at things that weren't funny until they were. And Daniel at the far end, civil, contained, watching.

Eli watched him watch her. Watched him catalog the way the workers deferred to her, the way Marta teased her, the way she'd learned every tree by name. Watched him see, perhaps for the first time, that the girl from fifteen years ago had become a woman who could hold a room — and watched whatever he'd expected to feel about that curdle into something harder.

At one point Eli caught the two of them near the cider barrels, Jonah explaining the pressing schedule, Daniel listening with his arms folded.

"So you run it now," Daniel said. "The orchard."

"I'm learning it," Jonah said. "It's my grandmother's. Was. It's a condition of the will — one full harvest."

"A condition." Daniel's voice was pleasant. Pleasant the way ice is smooth. "That's one way to come home, I suppose. Get paid for it."

Eli arrived at the barrels faster than he'd meant to. "Daniel."

"It's fine," Jonah said lightly, but he saw the flicker in her eyes, the old reflex, the flinch of a woman who'd spent fifteen years hearing variations on that sentence. *One way to come home. Get paid for it.* He saw her decide, deliberately, to let it go — saw her choose grace the way she chose everything, at cost to herself — and something in his chest tore straight down the middle.

"Saturday," Daniel said to her, lifting his glass. "I hear there's a festival. I wouldn't miss it."

* * *

Friday night, Eli couldn't sleep.

He lay in the narrow bed above the packing shed listening to the wind move through the rows, and the ceiling did what ceilings do at two in the morning: it showed him things. Daniel at fourteen, white-faced on the school steps. Their mother weeping into dishwater. The letters — God, the letters, his grandmother's sister's correspondence that he'd found in the Marchetti attic three winters ago, the ones that suggested, that whispered, that made him doubt everything he'd been raised to believe about the Thornbury girl. Doubt he'd carried like a coal in his pocket ever since, warming neither himself nor anyone else.

Doubt he had never once mentioned to Jonah, because to mention it would be to explain why he'd read them, which would be to explain who he was.

And below him, forty feet away, she slept in his bed — had slept in his bed for weeks now, her cold feet against his calves, her hair across the pillow — trusting him with a history she'd never given anyone else. Trusting him with the story of the scandal the way you hand someone the broken pieces of a thing and trust them not to cut themselves, or you.

He got up. He stood at the window looking down at the dark orchard, at the shape of the barn, at the faint glow of the cottage where she slept, and he took out his phone and typed, *Daniel. We need to talk. Before tomorrow.*

He stared at it for a long time. The cursor blinked. Somewhere down the hill an owl called, twice, and quit.

He deleted it.

*After the festival,* he told the dark. *Tomorrow night. When it's over. One more day.*

The dark didn't answer, because the dark had heard him say *one more day* before, and the dark knew what he was by now.

* * *

Festival morning broke clear and gold, the kind of day that seemed complicit in everything.

By nine the orchard was transformed — bunting snapping between the trees, trestle tables down the main aisle, the cider press gleaming, the band tuning up on the flatbed that served as a stage. Cars were parking in the far field. People Eli had known his whole life were walking up the lane in their good clothes, and Jonah was everywhere at once, radiant, commanding, fixing a crooked banner here and a wobbly table there, and every time she passed him she touched him — his arm, his shoulder, the back of his hand — small public claims that made his chest ache with how much he didn't deserve them.

Daniel arrived at eleven with their mother, who kissed Eli's cheek and squeezed his arm and said, "Whatever it is you're not telling that girl, Eli Marchetti, your father is rolling in his grave," and walked off before he could answer, because mothers are like that.

The day gathered speed. The press ran and the first sweet cider came out and children ran shrieking between the rows and the band struck up and the orders started — the grocer from two towns over wanting forty cases, the cidery wanting the whole late crop, Mrs. Aldous announcing from the bandstand steps that the council wished to make the festival annual. Annual. A tradition. Jonah's invention, becoming permanent, becoming real.

Eli stood at the edge of it all with a cider cup going warm in his hand, watching her shine, and felt the lie around him hardening like set concrete. Every hour it held, the truth got heavier. Every laugh she spent on this town, on him, was being spent on false pretenses, and the interest was accruing somewhere he couldn't see, and he understood with terrible clarity that he was not protecting her from anything. He was only choosing the date of the explosion.

He found her at dusk, between the cider barrels and the dance floor, flushed and laughing, and she caught his hands and pulled him in.

"Dance with me," she said. "Everyone's watching. Let's give them something."

"Jonah—"

"One dance. You owe me one dance. You've owed me one since June."

So he danced with her, badly, under the strings of lights with the band playing and the whole town watching, and she laughed up at him with her head tipped back, and across the crowd he saw Daniel standing at the edge of the dance floor with his mother's hand on his sleeve, watching them both with an expression Eli had never seen on his brother's face before.

Not bitterness. Something worse. Something like recognition — like a man watching a door close that he'd always assumed would be there.

Afterward, breathless, Jonah leaned into him and said, near his ear, under the music: "Your brother's been looking at me all day like he's grading a paper I haven't handed in. Are you going to tell me what that's about?"

The music played. The lights swung. Somewhere behind them a child screamed with joy.

"It's nothing," Eli said.

And Jonah went still against him — just slightly, just for a beat, the length of one held breath — and when she drew back her face was exactly as warm as before, and that was the problem. It was exact. Measured. The first cold note in all the music of her, and he heard it, and she knew he heard it, and neither of them said a word as the band swung into the next song and the festival danced on around them, brilliant and doomed, like a lantern burning down to the end of its oil.
