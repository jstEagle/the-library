## 22. The Orchard of Bellbird Bay

The morning of the wedding, Jonah woke before the birds and lay in the dark of the packing-shed cottage listening to the orchard breathe.

That was the only way she could describe it, though she'd tried a dozen times in the past year — to clients, mostly, when they asked her why she'd moved home, why she'd traded city ballrooms for a valley full of apple trees. The orchard breathed. In the hour before dawn the leaves exhaled their cool green breath through the open window, and somewhere down the rows a windfall dropped with a soft thud like a heartbeat, and Jonah lay there and felt the whole valley holding its breath along with her.

One year. She counted it the way Eli counted rings on a stump, from the outside in. One year since the hearing, since she'd stood in a wood-paneled room and read her grandmother's letters aloud until her voice cracked and the truth sat in the middle of the floor where everyone had to look at it. One year since Daniel had come up the orchard lane with his hat in his hands. One year since spring, since the station platform, since Eli standing under the last train's lights saying *I would have waited* and her saying *don't wait anymore.*

And today — today she was going to stand under the oldest tree in Bellbird Bay and marry him.

She got up quietly so as not to wake him — he was still asleep in the loft above the packing shed, because she'd insisted on the old superstition even though neither of them was superstitious, even though they'd spent eleven months planning this wedding together and he knew every detail down to the napkin folds. Some rituals you keep just because they're lovely. That was the thing Jonah had learned about weddings, the thing she'd forgotten for fifteen years and remembered again one row at a time: the rituals weren't lies. They were containers. You poured whatever was true into them, and if what you poured was true enough, they held.

Her dress hung on the back of the cottage door, wrapped in muslin against the dust. She'd made it herself — not sewn it, she wasn't that brave, but designed it, which for Jonah was the same act of faith. Ivory silk, simple lines, sleeves that caught the light like blossom petals. Her mother had cried when she'd seen it. Vivienne had said nothing at all, which from Vivienne was practically a blessing.

Vivienne.

Jonah carried her tea out onto the veranda steps and let herself think about her cousin, because she'd promised herself she wouldn't spend this morning doing it and therefore had to get it over with quickly, like pulling a splinter.

A year ago, after the hearing collapsed — after the lawyer withdrew the challenge with a face like curdled milk, after the letters went into the public record and the town's memory rearranged itself around a new villain, long dead and safely dead — Vivienne had not spoken to her for four months. Then one gray afternoon in late winter Jonah had found her standing at the gate of the orchard, arms crossed, breath steaming, looking like a woman who'd rehearsed something on the drive over and lost all of it between the car and the fence.

"I'm not here to apologize," Vivienne had said.

"Okay," Jonah had said.

"But I managed that farm for nine years. Nine years I kept it alive while everyone pretended it was going to be left to me, and then your grandmother handed it to the girl who ran away, and I—" She'd stopped. Started again, quieter. "I did the math wrong. On who owed whom."

It wasn't an apology. It was better than an apology; it was an accounting, and Jonah understood accountings. She'd opened the gate.

Now Vivienne was coming to the wedding. She'd RSVP'd by postcard, no note, just her name in her narrow handwriting, and when Jonah had asked her — carefully, at Easter, over ham — whether she might consider a small role, Vivienne had looked at her for a long moment and said, "Someone has to make sure the caterer doesn't water down the wine." Which was how Vivienne came to be supervising the bar, and how Jonah came to believe, some mornings, in miracles of a modest and practical kind.

* * *

By seven the orchard was full of people who loved them, which was a sentence Jonah turned over all day like a coin she couldn't quite believe was legal tender.

The chairs went in under the grandmother tree in two curved wings, not straight rows — Jonah had fought for that, had drawn the arc on graph paper until Eli pointed out she could simply have told him and saved three evenings. Two hundred and forty guests, and every single chair accounted for, because Jonah Thornbury did not run weddings with unaccounted-for chairs. The aisle was strewn with petals from the Gravenstein block, blushed pink and honey-scented, gathered by the harvest crew who'd refused payment and worked half the night.

Her mother arrived at eight in a blue dress and immediately began crying, which was expected and budgeted for; Jonah pressed a handkerchief into her hands and said, "Mum. Save it for the vows. They're good ones." Her mother laughed wetly and held her at arm's length and studied her face the way she had when Jonah was small, checking for damage.

"You're happy," her mother said. Not a question. An inventory.

"I'm happy," Jonah said, and found it was true all the way down, no asterisk, no footnote, no condition buried in a will.

Rosalind arrived at eight-fifteen with a hat like a small weather system and opinions about the seating chart, which Jonah absorbed and discarded with the serene authority of a woman who had planned six hundred weddings and finally, finally, was planning one that mattered. Aunt Rosalind had come around slowly, the way a big ship comes around, but she had come around, and last month she'd sent Jonah a photograph — Jonah at fourteen, gap-toothed, standing on a ladder in this very orchard with juice on her chin — with a note that read: *You were always going to come back. Some of us were just too proud to say we hoped so.*

At nine, Daniel Marchetti appeared at the edge of the rows with his toast notes folded in his breast pocket and an expression of a man carrying something fragile across ice.

"How's it looking?" he asked.

"Perfect," Jonah said. "Which means something will go wrong by ten, and I've already decided what it's allowed to be."

Daniel grinned. A year ago his grin had been a wound healing; now it was just a grin. He'd stayed in Bellbird Bay after all — taken over the timber crew, bought the house on Alder Street, started showing up at Sunday dinners with a frequency that suggested he'd decided family was a thing you attended to rather than a thing you survived. He and Eli fought about football and fencing techniques and whose turn it was to bring dessert, and every fight ended the same way, with Daniel clapping his brother on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him, and both of them pretending it hadn't meant anything.

He'd asked her once, in early autumn, sitting on the tailgate of his truck with cider going cold in their hands, whether she forgave him. For fifteen years of believing the worst. For being one more voice in the chorus.

"You were seventeen and someone lied to you expertly," Jonah had said. "I forgive you the way I want to be forgiven. Completely, and without making you earn it first."

He'd cried, a little, and pretended it was the wind.

* * *

At ten o'clock, exactly as predicted, something went wrong: the string quartet's cellist's bridge snapped, and there followed twenty minutes of controlled chaos during which Jonah produced a spare cello from the music shop in town — she'd rented it weeks ago, because she planned for disasters the way other people planned for sunshine — and the quartet played on, and only three people ever knew there'd been a crisis at all.

Eli saw her handle it from across the orchard. She felt him watching, felt the specific weight of his attention the way she'd learned to feel weather coming off the ridge. When she glanced over, he touched two fingers to his heart, and she had to go stand behind the packing shed for a minute and breathe until she could be composed again.

They'd married each other a hundred times in small ways already, she thought. He'd married her the winter he taught her to prune, standing behind her with his hands over hers on the shears, saying *cut to the outward bud, always, give the tree somewhere to grow.* She'd married him the night she'd shown him the ledger for the festival — the real one, the one with the numbers that meant the orchard would live — and he'd read it twice and said, "You built this," in a voice like she'd hung the moon over the valley. They married each other every morning he set her coffee on the veranda rail without being asked, every evening she left the porch light on for him when he walked the fences after dark.

The ceremony was just the part where everyone else got to see it.

* * *

She walked in on her mother's arm, down the petal-strewn aisle under the grandmother tree, and the sight of two hundred and forty faces turned toward her should have been the terror of her girlhood made flesh — all those eyes, all that attention, the whole town watching Jonah Thornbury again.

But these eyes were different. Or maybe she was different. Maybe that was the whole discovery of the year: that visibility didn't have to mean exposure. That you could be seen and held at the same time.

In the second row, Vivienne sat very straight in plum-colored silk, supervising the ushers with her eyes even while her hands rested folded, and when Jonah passed her, Vivienne reached out and straightened the train of the dress with two brisk tugs — fixing it, fixing her, the only language of tenderness she'd ever been fluent in — and Jonah squeezed her hand as she went by and Vivienne pretended it hadn't happened and wiped her eyes when she thought no one was looking.

Eli stood under the tree in a suit he clearly hated and wore anyway, and when he saw her his whole face did the thing it did — the quiet coming apart, the composure going soft at the edges — and Jonah thought, walking toward him through the light falling green-gold through the leaves, *this is the man who fixes fences, and I am never leaving.*

The celebrant spoke. The trees bore fruit overhead — the grandmother's grafts, heavy now with the season's first apples, Blenheim Orange and Ashmead's Kernel and the little russets nobody grew anymore except them, fruit that had been grafted by a woman's hands forty years ago and had outlived her and outlasted every scandal and every silence and every winter, and would go on bearing long after all of them were gone. That was the sermon, really. Nobody needed to say it out loud. The orchard said it all afternoon.

They wrote their own vows. Eli went first, and his voice was steady until it wasn't.

"I'm not good at speeches," he said, and the crowd laughed gently, because they knew him. "So I'll say it short. Fifteen years ago I picked a side without asking any questions, and it took me fifteen years and one stubborn woman to learn that love that doesn't ask questions isn't love, it's just habit wearing love's coat." His voice caught. He looked at her. "You ask questions. You ask all of them. And every answer you've given me has been true, even the ones that cost you. So — I promise to answer yours the same way. All of them. Even the expensive ones. For as long as the trees stand."

Jonah had planned to be composed. She'd written her vows on cards, rehearsed them eleven times, timed them to ninety seconds. She abandoned the cards.

"When I was nineteen I decided weddings were lies," she said. "Big expensive lies people told themselves so they wouldn't have to admit they were afraid alone. I built a whole career on that idea — making beautiful things I didn't believe in, for people braver than me." She heard her voice shake and let it. "Then my grandmother died and trapped me in an orchard, and a man who fixes fences made me learn the names of every tree, and somewhere between the grafting knife and the last train I stopped lying to myself." She took his hands. They were work-roughened and steady and they were hers. "I don't think weddings are lies anymore. I think they're the truest container we've got. And I want to spend my life pouring true things into this one. Eli. Stay past every last train."

"Every one," he said.

And the celebrant said the words, and the orchard held its breath, and then everyone was standing and cheering and the quartet swung into something joyful and the petals came down off the grandmother tree in a slow pink snow, as if the old woman herself had leaned out of the branches and shaken them loose on purpose.

* * *

The reception ran long and golden into the evening, the way the best ones do — lanterns strung through the rows, long tables under the boughs, the harvest crew dancing badly and magnificently, Rosalind's hat migrating from head to head like a benevolent virus.

Daniel gave the toast at sunset.

He stood on the bandstand — the same bandstand where Jonah had kissed Eli in front of the whole town a year ago, the kiss that had been the last easy night before everything broke open — and he raised his glass of Vivienne's strictly-unwatered wine, and the crowd hushed.

"I've known Eli Marchetti my whole life," he said. "He's my brother, and for most of that life I thought I knew everything about him. Steady. Quiet. A man who fixes things instead of talking about them." He looked at Eli, and Eli looked back, and something passed between them that had taken twenty years and one terrible lie to build. "Turns out the steadiest thing about him is how he loves. He loved me when I didn't deserve it. He loved this orchard when it was just a job. And he loved Jonah Thornbury from almost the moment she stepped off that train with a wreath in her arms — loved her carefully, and stupidly, and eventually honestly, which is the only way worth doing it."

He turned to Jonah. "Fifteen years ago, a lie cost this town two families. Today, the truth gives them back. Jonah — welcome home. Officially. With paperwork." Laughter, tears, the glasses rising. "To Jonah and Eli. To the orchard. May it out-bear us all."

"To the orchard!" the crowd roared, and Jonah laughed and cried and let her mother crush her, and over her mother's shoulder she saw Vivienne raise her glass alone, slightly apart, and mouth something Jonah couldn't hear but understood perfectly.

Later, when the lanterns burned low and the band played something slow, Eli found her at the edge of the dance floor and spun her out under the trees, and they danced among the rows like teenagers, her silk hem dragging through fallen petals, his hand splayed warm against her back.

"Mrs. Marchetti," he murmured.

"Thornbury-Marchetti," she corrected. "I hyphenated. It's in the program."

"Of course it is. You planned this wedding."

"Down to the spare cello."

He laughed, low, and pulled her close, his chin resting on her hair, and for a while they just swayed while the music and the crickets and the breathing orchard wrapped around them.

Above them, the grandmother tree stood heavy with fruit, its grafted branches — two kinds of apple joined into one body, growing together or not at all — silvered in the lantern light. Jonah tipped her head back to look at it, at the year's crop hanging ripe and patient, waiting to be picked.

"First thing tomorrow," she said, "we start the harvest."

Eli smiled against her hair. "First thing tomorrow," he agreed, "we sleep in. The apples can wait one day."

And for once — for the first time in her life — Jonah Thornbury believed him.
