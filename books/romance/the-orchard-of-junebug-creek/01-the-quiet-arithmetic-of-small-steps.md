## 1. The Quiet Arithmetic of Small Steps

The mail came at eleven, as it always did, and Bram Dravenmoor watched it come the way he watched everything now—from a slight remove, through glass, with his hands occupied.

He was pruning the apple trees when the postal truck ground up the county road. February work, technically wrong for it, but wrongness was a luxury he'd stopped affording himself years ago. You pruned when you could prune. You lived when you could live. The shears made their small wet clicks in the cold, and he counted them without meaning to—click, click, click—the way he counted most things these days. Steps to the woodpile: forty-one. Boards in the porch rail: twenty-six. Years since anyone had printed his name on anything that mattered: fifteen.

Fifteen was a good number for counting. It divided cleanly. It was also, he had learned, roughly how long it took for the world to forget a man completely, if the man cooperated.

Bram cooperated beautifully.

The truck paused at his box—a dented thing on a leaning post, DRAVENMOOR stenciled on the side in paint so old it had gone the color of bone—and moved on. He set down the shears, knocked the snow off his knees, and went to see what the world wanted from him today. Usually: nothing. A seed catalog. The electric bill. Once a year, a letter from his accountant in Ithaca that read like a weather report from another planet.

Today there were two envelopes. One was the electric bill. The other was heavy cream stock, no window, no meter stamp, addressed in handwriting so controlled it looked typeset.

*Mr. B. Dravenmoor.*

Not Bram. Not the name from the newspapers. Just the initial, the surname, the rural route. Whoever had written it knew where he lived and chose, deliberately, not to know him. That took money. Money or manners, and this envelope had both.

He stood in the orchard row with the snow coming down fine as sifted flour and opened it with his thumbnail, because a man who'd spent fifteen years being careful didn't tear things open in front of witnesses, even when the only witness was an apple tree.

*Dear Mr. Dravenmoor,* it began. *Your name was given to me by a party who values discretion above all else. I am seeking a ghostwriter for a memoir of considerable length and consequence. The fee is one hundred fifty thousand dollars against completion. The client's identity will be disclosed only upon your acceptance of terms. All correspondence prior to that disclosure will be conducted through this office.*

There was an address in Manhattan. There was a telephone number. There was a single line at the bottom, unsigned:

*We understand you do not do interviews. This is precisely why we are writing to you.*

Bram read it three times. Then he folded it along its original creases—he noticed himself doing that, the small compulsive tidiness of a man who kept his life folded along its original creases—and put it in his coat pocket, and went back to the trees.

One hundred fifty thousand dollars. His last ghostwriting job, a general's memoir of a war nobody remembered fondly, had paid sixty. The one before that, a tech founder's book about disruption, had paid eighty and cost him four months of listening to a man describe himself as a visionary while eating soup with his mouth open. Bram was good at this work. That was the joke of it, the private, airless joke he carried around like a stone in his boot: he had become, by every measure except the one that had once mattered to him, a better writer than he'd ever been famous for being. Fame had made him lazy. Invisibility had made him exact.

He finished the row. He went inside. He did not call the number.

That was the arithmetic he lived by, and he was honest enough with himself to name it, at least in the privacy of his own skull. Small steps. Small sums. Nothing that drew attention. A man who had been dragged through the literary press like something pulled from a drain did not accept contracts from cream-stock envelopes with Manhattan addresses, because Manhattan addresses had teeth, and he had been bitten before.

He made coffee. He sat at the kitchen table with its view of the creek, frozen now, holding its breath under ice, and he told himself the story he always told himself, which was that this was enough. The farmhouse, bought cheap because it needed everything. The ghostwriting income, laundered through an agency in Rochester into something resembling ordinary freelance earnings. The town eleven miles away where he was known, if at all, as the fellow who fixed his own roof and paid cash at the hardware store and never said much. Junebug Creek wasn't even the town's real name—it was the creek's name, and the creek had gotten it from some extinct insect, and the town itself was called Harrow Falls, but the post office had given up correcting people decades ago. Bram liked that. A place that had stopped correcting people. A place that let you be called what you were called.

He was halfway through the coffee when he caught himself doing the other thing, the dangerous thing, which was wondering.

Who gave my name? Nobody gives my name. My name is a dead language; even the people who used to speak it have forgotten the grammar.

And underneath the wondering, quieter, older, in a voice he hadn't permitted to speak aloud in years: *A memoir of considerable length and consequence.* Every ghostwriter said no to the first offer. It was practically liturgy. And every ghostwriter, somewhere beneath the liturgy, already knew whether he was going to say yes.

Bram washed his cup, dried it, hung it on its hook. Outside, the snow thickened. The forecast on the radio—he still had a radio, a battery model, a habit from a childhood house that lost power every winter—mentioned a system moving down from the lakes later in the week. Early blizzard, maybe. Rare for February's end, rarer still this far east of the lake effect belt. *Stock up,* the man said cheerfully, as though stocking up were a pleasure rather than a discipline. For Bram it was both. He liked having reasons to nail shut the hatches. He had built his whole life into a hatch, more or less.

* * *

The agency in Rochester ran on a woman named Odile Fenn, who was seventy-three, chain-smoked on her fire escape in all weathers, and had never once asked Bram a personal question in nine years of sending him work. She was, as far as Bram could determine, the closest thing he had to a friend, which said less about Odile than it did about the shape of his life.

"You got a letter," she said, when he called her that evening. Not a question. Odile's calls began in medias res; she considered small talk a form of embezzlement.

"How do you know I got a letter?"

"Because they called me first. Tuesday. Wanted references." A drag, audible, the paper rasping. "I gave them the general. I gave them the soup man. I did not give them your address, before you ask—they already had it. Which bothered me, frankly."

"It should bother you."

"It bothered me right up until they mentioned the number." He could hear her shrug through four hundred miles of fiber optic. "Hundred and fifty, Bram. That's not memoir money. That's apology money. Somebody wants to say sorry in hardcover."

"Or buy something," Bram said. "Confessions aren't the only things that get bound."

"Mm." Odile exhaled smoke at New York State. "You want my professional opinion?"

"No."

"They're going to fly you in regardless. They said Thursday. Car service, first class, the works. If you don't show, they'll take it as an answer, and if you do show—" She let it hang. Odile was a great believer in letting things hang; she said suspense was the only honest wage left in publishing.

"If I do show?"

"Then you'll want to wear the coat that doesn't look like it lost an argument with a woodstove."

He looked down at the coat in question. It had, in fact, lost such an argument, several, over many winters, and had won others, and on balance Bram considered the record respectable.

"I'm not saying yes," he said.

"You're saying no in a very slow voice," Odile said. "I've heard it before. It's how you said yes to the general."

* * *

Thursday came gray and windless, the kind of day that felt held. The car service arrived at seven-forty, a black sedan idling in his dooryard like a large patient animal, and Bram locked the farmhouse door behind him and stood a moment on the step with the key in his fist, performing the ritual he performed whenever he left: cataloguing what the house contained that would ruin him if found. The drafts in the barn office, filed under false titles. The notebooks. The photograph he kept in the desk drawer, face down, of three young people on a dock at a writers' colony fifteen years ago, squinting into a summer that hadn't yet learned what it was going to do to them.

He put the key in his pocket. The house would keep.

The drive south took most of the day, and Bram spent it the way he spent all travel now—invisible, watchful, reading the landscape like a manuscript he was fact-checking. Somewhere past Binghamton the terrain turned urban and the sedan joined a river of taillights, and Bram felt the city arrive in him the way weather arrives in a joint that broke long ago. Fifteen years, and Manhattan still knew exactly where the fracture was.

They put him up—or rather, the firm put him up, the firm being a nameless entity whose letterhead said only *V—* and a suite number—in a hotel off Gramercy Park, and the meeting was set for ten the next morning, and Bram slept badly in sheets too smooth, dreaming in fragments: a dock, a typewriter ribbon gone red, a woman's laugh cut short by a door closing.

At nine-fifty he stood in a corridor on the thirty-first floor of a tower he declined to look up at, outside a door with no name on it, wearing the coat that had won its arguments, and he experienced the hairline crack.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing that unsettled him most—not drama but precision. He had built his life out of small steps, each one measured, each one safe, and standing in that corridor he understood suddenly, the way you understand a floor is rotten only after it gives, that the steps had not been leading anywhere. They had been leading here. Fifteen years of careful arithmetic, and the sum had been waiting for him on the thirty-first floor the entire time, patient as debt.

The door opened before he could knock.

"Mr. Dravenmoor." A young man, immaculate, relieved-looking, as though Bram were a package long delayed. "Please. He's ready for you."

*He.* Not *they*. Not *the client*. The pronoun dropped into Bram's chest and sank.

The office beyond was wide and quiet and faced north, all glass and gray light, and at the far end of it, framed against the window with the whole city laid out below him like something he owned the negative rights to, sat a man in a wheelchair turned toward the door, hands folded on a blanket across his knees, waiting.

Bram knew the silhouette before the light resolved the face. You don't forget a silhouette you spent five years competing against—you learn it the way you learn the outline of a knife in a drawer.

But it couldn't be. Because the man in the chair was thin in a way that silhouettes shouldn't be, and his hands, folded so carefully, trembled at rest like leaves deciding whether to fall, and Julian Voss—Julian Ashford Voss, publisher, titan, the man who had taken everything Bram had and printed it under his own imprint—Julian Voss was dying. You could see it from thirty feet. It came off him like heat off a road.

"Close the door," Julian said. His voice was the same voice. That was the cruelest part. Fifteen years had stripped the flesh off him and left the voice untouched, warm and dry and faintly amused, a voice that had talked judges into prizes and women into silence. "And sit down, Bram. We have a great deal of arithmetic to do, you and I. I understand you're fond of arithmetic."

Bram did not close the door. Not yet. He stood on the threshold of the office and the threshold of the life he'd built, one hand on each, and understood that whichever way he stepped next, the quiet arithmetic of small steps was over.
