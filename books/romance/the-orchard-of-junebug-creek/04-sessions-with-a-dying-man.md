## 4. Sessions with a Dying Man

The recording equipment arrived before Julian did.

A crate from a Manhattan audio house, packed in foam, with a note in Julian's secretary's hand: *Mr. Voss prefers the room to sound like a room. No studio treatment.* I set the microphone on the kitchen table of the townhouse he'd lent me for the sessions—a borrowed life inside a borrowed life—and ran a test, and my own voice came back through the headphones sounding like a stranger's, which was fitting. I had been a stranger's voice for fifteen years. It was the one thing I was reliably good at.

Julian arrived at nine each morning, always nine, never a minute early, because punctuality was a form of power and Julian Voss had never once surrendered power voluntarily, not even to time. He wore charcoal or oyster gray. He carried his own bag, refusing the doorman's help, and I understood later that this was not humility but arithmetic—he was calculating how much strength each gesture cost and spending accordingly.

The first week, I thought the sessions were going well. That was the trap of Julian: he was so fluent that fluency read as health. He'd settle into the chair across from me with his legs crossed and his cufflinks catching the light, and he'd talk about the founding of Voss & Halloway, the first list, the famous auction for *The Winter Orchard*—my auction, though he didn't know I knew that, or he knew it perfectly and enjoyed it—and his voice had the polished warmth of a man telling stories he'd told at a hundred dinners.

But I was being paid, in part, to listen beneath the story. That's the ghostwriter's actual trade. Not the sentences. The seams.

I noticed the missed words first. Small ones, function words, the connective tissue of speech. He'd say, "The board met in March, and we—the decision was made to acquire Halloway," and slide past the gap as if it had never been there. A younger Julian would never have lost a preposition. A younger Julian would have died before losing a preposition. I started keeping a private tally in the margin of my notes, a column of small tick marks, and by the end of the second week the column had begun to look like grass growing through pavement.

Then the hands.

He held them folded when he talked, courtly, deliberate, and I took it at first for an old man's posture. But one morning he reached for the water glass and the reach was wrong—not unsteady, exactly, but *budgeted*, the way you reach for something when you've already priced the movement. The glass chattered against his teeth. He set it down with exaggerated care, smiled, and said, "The building's heating is a disgrace," as though the tremor were the radiator's fault.

I said nothing. That was the first cowardice, and I want it on the record, in whatever ledger this book turns out to be: I noticed, and I said nothing, and part of why I said nothing was that some cold cellar of my heart had begun, without my permission, to hope.

* * *

It was on the ninth session that I saw the bracelet.

He'd taken off his jacket—unprecedented, a concession to the radiator he'd blamed—and his shirt cuff rode back two inches, and there it was: a hospital band, the cheap paper-plastic kind, printed in that cheerful institutional font. Patient name. Patient date of birth. A barcode, as if a man could be scanned and shelved.

The name on it was Julian Voss. The date of birth was right. I knew it was right because I had spent one long, humiliating night of my twenty-ninth year memorizing it, along with everything else about him, back when knowing your rival was a form of devotion. The band was from Sloan Kettering. I didn't need to look up what Sloan Kettering was. Everyone knew what Sloan Kettering was.

He saw me see it. I'll give him that—he didn't perform surprise, didn't yank his cuff down and laugh it off. He simply looked at the band, then at me, with an expression I had never seen on him before and have never been able to name since. It was the look of a man watching a door close that he had personally left unlocked.

"Memorial Sloan Kettering," I said. Not a question. I don't know why I said the whole name, except that saying it whole made it realer and I have always been a coward in the direction of real things.

"An old friend of the family," Julian said. "A consultation. Nothing dramatic." He drew the cuff down with two fingers, smooth as a magician, and the bracelet vanished, and we both pretended the trick had worked.

I let the tape run. That was the other cowardice—the recorder's red eye watching him lie to me, and me letting it lie there in the file like evidence, though evidence of what, I couldn't yet have said.

That night I transcribed three hours of his voice and found, in the middle of an anecdote about the Halloway acquisition, a sentence he didn't remember saying: *"You don't get to decide what a life was worth. Other people do that. That's the joke of it. That's the whole joke."* He'd said it quietly, half to himself, in the pause after the anecdote, and my hand had stopped on the pedal because I recognized the cadence, and the cadence was mine. Not the words—the weariness. He'd caught my weariness the way you catch a cold.

I sat in the borrowed townhouse until two in the morning with the transcript glowing on my laptop, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do. The missed words. The budgeted hands. The bracelet from the hospital whose name is a synonym, in New York, for the end of the story. And underneath it, older and less forgivable: the memory of Julian at thirty-four, at the colony at Junebug, holding my manuscript the night before he took it, and how his hands hadn't trembled then. How steady they had been. How steady they had needed to be, to do what he did.

I should have felt satisfaction. That's what I'd promised myself on the train down from Junebug, when I took the contract: whatever this book was, whatever he was paying for, I would be the one holding the pen over *his* life for once, and there would be a justice in that, a small cold justice like a coin you keep in a drawer.

Instead I felt something I had no drawer for.

* * *

The tenth session, he was late.

Julian Voss, late. His secretary called at 9:04, then 9:20, and at 9:41 the townhouse door opened and he came in without the bag, without the coat, in a sweater I had never seen him wear—cashmere gone soft and shapeless, the sweater of a man at home in a life I'd never been shown. His face had the gray undertone of someone who has recently been sick in a bathroom and decided to continue.

"Traffic," he said.

"There's no traffic," I said. "It's a residential street."

He looked at me for a long moment, and something in him seemed to settle, the way a house settles at night, creaking into a shape it will hold until morning. "No," he agreed. "There's no traffic." He sat down across the microphone from me and folded his hands, and I watched him do it—watched him make the fold deliberate, watched him spend the strength—and he said, "Before we begin. I want to tell you what this book is."

"It's a memoir," I said. "The early chapters are—"

"It's a settlement of accounts."

The phrase went into the room and stayed there. Outside, February was doing its gray work on the windows. I remember the microphone's red light and how patient it looked, how much better at waiting than either of us.

"Publishers keep ledgers," Julian went on, in the voice he used for board meetings, which was also, I realized with a slow cold turn, the voice he used for confessions—there had never been a difference. "Debits and credits. For forty years I have been very good at moving numbers between columns. And there are—" he paused, and I watched him lose a word, watched him reach for it and miss, watched him cover it with a sip of water, "—there are entries that have never been posted. Entries I intend to post. In this book. Under my name."

"What kind of entries?"

His eyes came up to mine, and for a moment—not a trick, not a performance, I would stake what's left of my own name on it—for a moment he looked *afraid*, and the fear made him young, made him the man from the colony, made him the man who had sat across from me in a kitchen in Junebug Creek with my manuscript between us and said, *You'll thank me someday, Bram. The world isn't ready for you. I'm doing you a kindness.*

"Names," he said. "Dates. Amounts." He smiled, and the smile was the old smile, and the old smile had always been a wall with a door in it. "Enough to make your lawyers earn their retainer, if I had lawyers, which I've dismissed, because lawyers are for men who intend to survive the book."

"Julian." I said his name the way you set something down carefully. "Why now?"

He looked at his hands. Then, before he could answer—before he could decide, I think, whether to lie—the cuff shifted again, and the bracelet was there, and this time he didn't hide it. This time he turned his wrist deliberately, the way you show a scar, and let me read it in the gray light: the barcode, the dates, the cheerful institutional font.

"Because," he said, "the account is about to be closed. And I find I am not willing to let the closing entry be written by anyone but me." He straightened, and the publisher came back into his spine like a man returning from a trip. "Now. Chapter six. The Halloway acquisition. Where were we?"

* * *

I walked him out at noon. At the door he stopped, one hand on the frame, and stood with his back to me, and I watched the shoulders under the shapeless sweater and understood that the sweater was not a concession. It was a uniform. He had already resigned from the life of charcoal and cufflinks. He was dressing, these mornings, for the country he was about to move to.

"Bram." He didn't turn around. "Do you remember Junebug Creek?"

The question was so far off the map of the morning that it took me a second to answer, and in that second I was twenty-nine again, and the orchard was full of late light, and Wren was laughing at something Julian had said, and I was holding a manuscript that had three weeks left of being mine.

"I remember it," I said.

"There's a session I want to schedule," he said, still not turning. "Not here. Upstate. There's a farmhouse I've been told about, near the creek. I want to record the middle chapters where they happened. I find—" and here his voice did something I had never heard it do, something small and cracked and almost humble, "—I find I can't say some of it in this city. The rooms are too full of people I've paid."

He turned then, and he was smiling, and the smile had the door in it, and behind the door, I understood at last, was not a confession.

It was an invitation. Or a trap. With Julian, at the end, I no longer believed there was a difference—and the terrible thing, the thing I have not told anyone, is that standing in that doorway, watching the man who ruined me ask to be driven into my exile, I felt my heart do the one thing it had sworn off doing for fifteen years.

It moved toward him.
