## 14. The Last Train Doesn't Stop Here

The storm broke sometime before dawn, the way a fever breaks—suddenly, and with no promise it meant anything good.

I woke to a silence so complete I thought at first I'd gone deaf. Fifteen years of living alone had taught me every sound this house made: the tick of the stove cooling, the groan of the barn boards in wind, the creek muttering under its ice. All of it was gone. The blizzard had spent itself against the ridge in the night, and what remained was that post-storm hush, the world scoured and held still, as if the sky were embarrassed by what it had done.

I lay in bed and listened to the house breathe around two other people's sleep. That was the strangeness. For fifteen years the silence of this place had been mine alone, a thing I'd built plank by plank like the farmhouse itself. Now it had strangers in it. One of them was dying down the hall. The other was Wren Calloway, asleep—or not sleeping—in my guest room, twenty feet from where I lay staring at the ceiling like a man waiting for a verdict.

The light through the curtains was blue and merciless. I got up, dressed in the dark out of habit, and went to the window.

The orchard looked like something excavated. The old Baldwin apples stood hip-deep in drifts, their black branches furred with white, and beyond them Junebug Creek ran silver under a sky scrubbed clean of everything except cold. It was beautiful. It was always beautiful after storms, which I had privately always considered the landscape's cruelest trick—the way it apologized in light for what it did in dark.

Down the hill, maybe four miles as the crow flew, the rail line curved along the creek toward town. In ordinary winters the morning freight came through at 6:40, and the passenger run—the last one anyone could call reliable—left the junction at dawn. I had checked the timetable three times yesterday, folding and unfolding the paper until it went soft as cloth. Three quiet days, I'd told myself. Three days of snow, two guests, one deadline, and then the train would come and carry them both back to the lives they'd ruined without me, and I would stand in my doorway and watch them go and feel nothing but relief.

That was the plan. I want to be honest about how much I believed in it, because belief is the thing I'm worst at, and I had invested heavily.

I put the kettle on and dug out the battery radio from the drawer under the phone shelf. The landline had died with the power flickers two nights ago; the radio was my last thread to the world outside, and I turned its dial with the care of a man defusing something.

Static. Then, faintly, a voice reading emergency notices in the flat tone of someone who has read forty of them already: *Route 9 closed. Route 12 closed. County plows beginning operations on primary roads only. Rail service suspended indefinitely pending line inspection between Harlow Junction and—*

I turned it off.

*Indefinitely.* There was a word I knew intimately. I had lived indefinitely for fifteen years. Indefinitely was the country I'd emigrated to, and I knew its customs: you never unpack all the way, you never say anything out loud that you can't take back, you measure your life in small steps because small steps can't fall far.

The kettle screamed. I took it off the heat and stood there holding it, and through the window I saw Wren come out onto the porch.

She was wearing my coat—the wool one I kept on the peg by the door, too big for her, sleeves folded back twice—and she stood with her arms crossed against the cold, looking at the orchard the way I'd just been looking at it, like a woman taking inventory of a prison that had unexpectedly been redecorated. Her breath hung in the air. She didn't move for a long time.

I should have let her have the morning. Instead I poured two cups of coffee and carried them out, because apparently fifteen years of discipline could be undone by one coat on one woman, and I wasn't ready to examine what that meant.

"They canceled the trains," I said, when I reached her. No preamble. We had never been people who needed preambles, even now, even broken the way we were.

She took the cup. Our fingers didn't touch, and I noticed that we had arranged it that way, deliberately, the way you step around a hole in the floor.

"I heard the radio through the wall," she said. "Indefinitely."

"Indefinitely."

She looked down the hill toward where the tracks ran, invisible under the snow, and I watched her do the arithmetic. I watched her arrive at the number.

"There'll be a plow convoy," she said. "Once they clear Route 12, they'll run convoys through to Harlow. They always do."

"They will."

"So I could be in the city by tomorrow night."

"You could."

She drank her coffee. The steam rose off it and bent sideways in the wind coming down off the ridge. Somewhere in the orchard a branch gave up its load of snow with a soft whump, and we both flinched, and pretended we hadn't.

"Bram." She said my name carefully, like setting down something breakable. "Julian is dying."

It wasn't a question. She'd seen it too—the way he'd gripped the banister last night, the gray under his skin, the way he'd laughed at his own joke and then forgotten the end of it halfway through. He was worse this morning; I'd heard him moving at four, heard the bathroom door, heard the long silence after that I'd learned to recognize over months of sessions in Manhattan: the sound of a man negotiating with his own body and losing ground.

"Yes," I said.

"And if I leave on the convoy—"

"He'll die here, or he'll die in a hospital in Harlow, and either way it'll be within the week. The doctor on the phone said—" I stopped. Julian's diagnosis was his to tell, or it had been, once. Lately it seemed to belong to everyone in the house. "He doesn't have much time, and he knows it, and he came here anyway. He followed you into a blizzard to force a session. Whatever else Julian Voss is, he's a man finishing something."

Wren set her cup on the porch rail. Snow had started sliding off the roof behind us in slow sheets, the house shedding its burden, and she turned and looked at me with an expression I remembered from another life—from a colony summer when we were young and she used to look at me across a bonfire like I was a language she was learning.

"And you?" she said. "What are you going to do?"

"That depends on what you do."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you make your decision look like mine so you don't have to own it. You've been doing it since I met you. You hid behind Julian's name for fifteen years, Bram. Don't hide behind mine now."

The words landed exactly where she'd aimed them, because Wren Calloway had always known where my soft places were—that was the gift she'd had as an agent, and the weapon she'd become under pressure, and the wound she'd left me with. I felt my jaw tighten. I felt the old anger rise, and beneath the anger, worse, the old tenderness, undimmed, absurd, still there after everything like a pilot light nobody remembered lighting.

"The convoy comes through tomorrow," I said. "Maybe the day after. When it does, you can flag it down. Anyone can. That's your choice, and it's a real one, and I'm not going to make it for you."

"And if I stay?"

I hadn't expected her to say it. I had expected her to circle it, to negotiate with it, to make me say it first—that was our choreography, the dance we'd perfected in the years before the fall, each of us waiting the other out. But she was fifty-three years old now and tired, and the snow had taken away all the rooms we used to hide in.

"If you stay," I said, and my voice did something I didn't authorize, roughened, dropped, "then you stay. Not indefinitely. Not as a guest passing through. You stay, and we figure out what that means, and it won't be easy, and I will probably ruin it, because I ruin things, it's the one talent Julian never managed to steal from me."

Something moved through her face—grief and laughter fighting for the same ground.

"That's the worst invitation I've ever received," she said.

"It's the first one I've extended in fifteen years. Cut me some slack."

* * *

Julian didn't come down until nearly noon. I heard him on the stairs before I saw him—each step taken with a pause after it, a man paying tolls—and when he appeared in the kitchen doorway, leaning on the frame, I saw that the night had taken more from him than the day before had. His skin had gone the color of the snow-shadow under the apple trees. He'd dressed fully, suit and tie, armor for a battlefield, and the effort of it showed in the sweat at his temples.

"Well," he said, surveying the kitchen, the coffee pot, Wren at the table with the galleys, me at the stove making eggs because cooking was the only prayer I knew. "Domesticity. How revolting. How I've missed it."

"Sit down, Julian."

"In a moment. First, business." He lowered himself into the chair across from Wren with the control of a man descending into cold water, and laid both hands flat on the table, and I saw the tremor he couldn't suppress anymore, the fingers drumming a private morse code against the wood. "The convoy. I assume we're discussing the convoy."

"Route 12 opens tomorrow, maybe," Wren said. "They're running plows now."

"Then I want a session tonight. Full session. The bribery chapter—we haven't touched the judges, and Bram keeps softening me, and I won't be softened. If I'm going to be on that convoy tomorrow, the manuscript leaves this house finished."

"You're not getting on any convoy," I said, before I could stop myself.

The kitchen went very quiet. Julian looked at me, and for a moment the charm fell away entirely, the way a mask slips when the face behind it forgets to hold it up, and what was underneath was just a sick old man with wet eyes, furious at being seen.

"Pardon me?" he said softly.

"You heard me. You're not well enough to ride four hours in an unheated convoy truck over unplowed roads. And before you argue—yes, I know you'll argue, arguing is your cardio—but I've watched you die by inches for six months, Julian, and I'm telling you as your ghostwriter and your host and the man whose career you destroyed: you are not getting on that truck."

Wren had gone still. Julian stared at me, and something passed over his face that I had never seen on it before in thirty years of knowing him, in rivalry and betrayal and the strange forced intimacy of these snowbound days. It looked almost like gratitude. It looked almost like terror. With Julian they had always been the same expression.

"You'd keep me here," he said. "In your house. Against my stated wishes."

"I'd keep you alive long enough to finish your confession. After that, do whatever you want. Die on the convoy. Die in the ditch. It's a free country and you've never once respected either fact."

He laughed then—a real laugh, cracked down the middle, and it turned into coughing, and Wren rose to get him water, and I stood at the stove with my spatula in my hand and understood that I had just done the most frightening thing of my life, which was not publishing under my own name, which was not confronting Julian, which was not even opening my door to them in the storm.

It was this. Saying *stay*. Meaning it. Building, out of eggs and coffee and stubbornness, a case for why the people who broke me should remain inside the walls I'd built to keep the world out.

Because here is what I knew, standing there while the two of them settled at my table like it was a table they'd always eaten at: the train might come back next week. The road might open. And when it did, part of me—the part that had measured out fifteen years in small safe steps—wanted to be on it. Wanted to hand them the house and the orchard and the creek and walk away into whatever anonymity was left, the way I always had, the way I always would, unless somebody stopped me.

Unless I stopped me.

* * *

The convoy notice came over the radio at dusk, read in that same flat voice: plows had breached Route 12 as far as Miller's Crossing, eight miles south. A civilian convoy would stage at the junction tomorrow morning, weather permitting, bound for Harlow. Anyone stranded was advised to assemble at the firehouse by seven a.m.

Weather permitting. In this county those two words were doing heroic work.

I carried the news upstairs, because Julian had gone up after lunch and not come down, and I found him sitting upright in the guest bed with the blankets pooled around him and the recorder on his knees, turning it over and over like a man handling a relic. The window behind him framed the darkening orchard, the last violet light failing in the west.

"You heard?" I said.

"I heard. Seven a.m. at the firehouse. Very civic." He set the recorder down. "Bram. A question, and I want the honest answer, which I realize is asking a lot given our history."

"Ask."

"If I don't make that convoy—if I'm still here next week, or the week after—will you finish the book? Not for the money. Not for the archive. Will you finish it because it deserves to be finished?"

I stood in the doorway with the hallway cold at my back and the lamplight yellow between us, and I thought about all the answers available to me, and how many of them were true.

"Yes," I said.

He nodded slowly, and lay back against the pillows, and closed his eyes, and for a moment he looked so much like the young Julian from the colony—the brilliant, hungry, terrified Julian, before the hunger won—that I had to grip the doorframe.

"Tell Wren," he said, without opening his eyes, "that the convoy will wait for no one. And tell yourself, Bram, while you're at it, that neither does anything else."

I went downstairs. Wren was at the window in the front room, watching the road, watching the dark come down over the fields, and she didn't turn around when I told her about the convoy, the firehouse, the seven a.m. assembly.

"I know," she said.

"Wren. If you're going, you need to decide tonight. They won't hold the trucks."

"I know that too." She was quiet a moment. Her breath fogged the glass, bloomed and faded, bloomed and faded. "Bram, when I was twenty-six, I got on a train out of this county and I told myself I'd never look back at it. I built a whole life on not looking back. And it worked—it worked beautifully, right up until it didn't."

She turned then. The lamp caught her face, and I braced myself for the answer, whichever one it was, the way you brace for cold water.

"Set another plate," she said. "I'm staying."

And upstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, I heard Julian begin to cough—the deep, wet, terrible cough of a man who would not see the spring—and I stood in my own front room, in the house I'd built to be empty, and felt the terror and the relief arrive together, indistinguishable, like twins.

Tomorrow the convoy would leave without us. Tonight there were plates to set, and a confession to record, and a dying man's last chapter waiting in my handwriting—and somewhere down the valley, the last train that didn't stop here anyway rolled on through the dark without any of us aboard.
