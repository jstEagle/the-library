## 1. The Last Train Schedule

The lighthouse had not spoken to a ship in eleven years, but Beatrix Nystrom still climbed its stairs every morning as if it might.

She went up the ninety-one steps with a thermos of coffee and the day's first light coming in sideways off the water, and she stood in the lantern room where the great lens sat dark under its glass hood like a sleeping animal, and she looked out at the gray-green sweep of the Atlantic until her chest unclenched. That was the ritual. Not maintenance — the Coast Guard had stripped the lamp long before she inherited the keys — but something closer to prayer. *I am here. The light is out. I am here anyway.*

From the gallery she could see all of Maple Hollow laid out below her: the crooked main street with its salt-bleached storefronts, the church steeple, the orchard climbing the hillside in neat green rows that would go gold at the edges by September. And past the orchard, the train station — small, shingled, stubbornly operational — where the morning train from the city arrived at 7:42 and the last train back departed at 9:15 in the evening. She knew the schedule the way other people knew psalms. She had never once been on either train.

Five years, and she had never once gotten on.

She told herself it was a choice. That was the word she used, *choice*, polished smooth from handling. She had chosen the lighthouse cottage with its drafty windows and its view of nothing but water and sky. She had chosen the part-time work cataloguing the historical society's papers, chosen the Thursday shifts at Maren's kitchen table learning to put up preserves, chosen the small life the way you choose a coat that fits — because after what happened in the city, anything larger felt like it might swallow her whole.

But some mornings, standing in the lantern room with the wind worrying at her hair, she felt less like a woman who had chosen a small life and more like a bird that had decided, on excellent evidence, never to fly again.

* * *

Back down in the cottage, the novel waited for her on the desk by the window, as it did every day, patient and accusatory.

It lived in a blue binder now, three hundred pages of it, printed single-spaced because paper felt more real than a screen. *The Saltwife's Daughter*, she was calling it, though the title changed monthly. It was about a woman who came home to a coastal town to mend herself and found instead that the town needed mending too — which was, Beatrix admitted privately, barely fiction at all. She had written it the way you write anything true: by dressing your own life in someone else's clothes and hoping no one recognized the seams.

Three hundred pages. One chapter left.

The final chapter had been blank since March.

It wasn't that she didn't know how it ended. She knew exactly how it ended; she'd known since the second month, when the story stopped being hers and started telling itself. The problem was that finishing it meant sending it somewhere. An agent. A contest. A drawer in an editor's office in New York, where people who remembered her name might see it and remember why they knew it, and say things about it, and say things about her, and —

Beatrix set down her coffee harder than she meant to.

No. Today was not a thinking-about-the-novel day. Today was a sorting-the-historical-society-papers day, and she was already late, and the fog was burning off into one of those hard blue September mornings that made even Maple Hollow look like a place worth arriving at rather than merely hiding in.

She was buttoning her cardigan when the phone rang.

Her mother's calls always announced themselves the same way: two rings, a pause, three more, as if Margaret Nystrom could never quite commit to the conversation. Beatrix let it do its full pattern before answering.

"Mom."

"Beatrix! Good, you're alive. I can never tell with you. You've gone so quiet down there." Her mother's voice had the bright, carrying cheer of a woman who'd spent thirty years talking over church suppers. "Are you sitting down?"

"I'm standing by the door."

"Well, sit anyway. The reunion is happening. October twelfth, right here in the Hollow, Aunt Viv's idea, God help us all. Everyone's coming. Your brother, the cousins, Uncle Ray even though he swears he hates everyone —" A breathless pause, the kind her mother deployed like punctuation. "And Delia."

The name landed the way cold water lands: not painful at first, just shocking, and then everywhere at once.

Delia. Cousin Delia, who had shared her bunk at every childhood summer, who had held her hair back at nineteen outside a bar in Portland, who had — five years ago, in a conference room Beatrix still sometimes dreamed about — forwarded the emails. All of them. The private ones, the stupid hopeful ones, the ones where Beatrix had confessed that she'd fudged a citation, one citation, in a report that had made her briefly famous and then, within nine days, famously finished. Delia had sent them to Beatrix's firm with a note that said only: *You should know who you're celebrating.*

Beatrix had left the city eleven days later. She had not been alone in a room with Delia since.

"Bea? Are you there? You've gone quiet, which for you is saying something."

"I'm here." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "That's — a lot of people."

"It's a *reunion*, sweetheart, lots of people is the entire point." A rustle; her mother had switched the phone to her other ear, settling in. "Now. There's a matter we should discuss. You'll need a date."

"I don't need a date."

"You'll be thirty-one in November, you live alone in a lighthouse, and the last man you mentioned to this family was a barista you described, and I quote, as 'having kind eyes.' Delia is bringing Marcus. Marcus has a boat. You cannot arrive alone, Bea, I'm begging you, I will not survive watching you stand at the punch bowl like a widow."

"I like the punch bowl. The punch bowl and I have an understanding."

Her mother laughed, then didn't. "Beatrix. Please. Just — think about it. Bring someone. Borrow someone. I don't care. Come with a warm body and a smile and let me have one afternoon where nobody looks at my daughter like she's the sad branch of the family tree."

After she hung up, Beatrix stood in the middle of her kitchen for a long time, listening to the house tick and settle around her.

A date. As if the problem were arithmetic. As if the wound were loneliness rather than exposure — the bone-deep knowledge that the last time she'd let herself be seen, fully seen, someone she loved had sold the view.

She thought about the trains again. 7:42 in, 9:15 out. She thought about the blue binder on the desk, its final chapter blank as an unsent letter. She thought about Delia's face across a Thanksgiving table, twenty years of shared summers curdling into something she'd have to smile through while holding a casserole.

"No," she said aloud to the empty kitchen, and the kitchen, being a kitchen, agreed with her.

* * *

The historical society occupied the old cannery office on the harbor road, a low building that smelled of dust and vinegar and the particular sweetness of very old paper. Beatrix liked it there. Dead documents asked nothing of you except attention, and attention was the one thing she had never run short of.

She was halfway through a box of 1950s tax assessments when the door opened and a stranger walked in.

She noticed him the way you notice weather — a shift in the air before you understand it. He was tall, rumpled in a way that seemed deliberate, with dark hair going silver early at the temples and a canvas jacket that had seen more cities than this one. He carried himself like a man accustomed to rooms deciding whether to trust him.

"Afternoon," he said. "I'm looking for records. Land records, mostly. Deeds, easements, transfers — anything touching the old Hollis orchard property."

"The orchard?" Beatrix set down her pencil. "That's odd timing. Most people ask about the lighthouse or the whaling logs."

"Do they?" He smiled, and it was a good smile, easy and a little self-deprecating, the kind that made you feel like the two of you were already sharing a joke at the world's expense. "I'll confess I don't know much about the lighthouse. I'm new in town. Elias Ward." He offered his hand. His grip was warm and brief. "I'm doing some research on the property. The new owner — Pemberton, I think his name is?"

"Aldous Pemberton," Beatrix said. "He bought the Hollis parcel in the spring. Nobody's met him more than twice. He keeps to the city and sends lawyers."

Something flickered behind his eyes — quick, professional, gone before she could name it. "Lawyers," he repeated mildly. "Of course he does."

She showed him to the deed cabinets, and he thanked her, and for an hour they worked in companionable silence broken by questions he asked with just enough charm that answering them felt like flirting even when it wasn't. Where did the orchard boundaries run. Who had held the neighboring parcels. How long had the older families been here. *Maren Nystrom,* Beatrix heard herself say at one point. *My grandmother. Her land touches the orchard on the north side.* And Elias Ward wrote the name down carefully, the way a man writes down something that matters.

When he left, the bell over the door chimed, and Beatrix stood among the dead papers feeling something she hadn't felt in five years.

Not attraction, exactly — or not only. It was more alarming than that. It was the feeling of a window opening in a house she'd sealed shut. Here was a stranger who had stepped off the 7:42 train into her small, safe, suffocating life, asking questions about the world beyond the harbor, looking at her like she was a person rather than a cautionary tale. And underneath the warmth of it ran a colder current she couldn't yet read: *nobody comes to Maple Hollow in September to look at deeds.*

She walked home along the harbor road with her collar up against the evening wind, and the lighthouse rose ahead of her white against the darkening water, and the thought that had been circling her all day finally landed.

Delia. October twelfth. Marcus and his boat.

And a stranger with kind eyes and a liar's easy smile, who was probably gone by morning — but who, for one strange hour, had made her feel like a woman in the first chapter of a story rather than the last.

Beatrix stopped at her gate and looked back toward the village lights.

"Borrow someone," her mother had said.

She laughed once, out loud, at herself — at the sheer recklessness of the idea forming, the first reckless thing she'd felt in five years — and went inside to make a list of everything she knew about Elias Ward.

It was a very short list. She intended to fix that.
