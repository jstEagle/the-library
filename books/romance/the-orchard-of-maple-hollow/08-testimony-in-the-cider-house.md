## 8. Testimony in the Cider House

The knock came at seven in the morning, and Beatrix knew before she opened the door who it would be.

She had spent the night the way she spent her worst nights — at the kitchen table with the lamp burning low, the manuscript of her novel stacked beside a cold cup of tea. She had read his report three times. *Prepared for Halloway Development Group.* Addressed to a man named Corwin Halloway, whom she had never met and already despised. Every name in Maple Hollow was in it: Maren Nystrom, the Ostergaards, old Tom Voss up on the ridge, their deeds itemized like specimens pinned to a board. Elias's handwriting was neat. His conclusions were neater. *Subject landholders show declining capacity; recommend accelerated acquisition before estate transfers complicate title.*

He had written them down like weather.

She opened the door. Elias stood on the step with the gray light behind him, hat in his hands, and he looked — she noted this with a kind of cold clarity that surprised her — terrible. Unshaven. Eyes rimmed red as though he hadn't slept, or had slept badly, or had spent the night rehearsing whatever he'd come to say.

"Beatrix."

"You have some nerve," she said quietly. The harbor was calm behind him; somewhere a gull complained. "You have an actual, measurable amount of nerve, standing on my grandmother's porch after I read what you wrote about her."

"That report was never sent." He said it fast, like a man stepping onto thin ice at a run. "I want you to know that first. It was drafted because it was my job to draft it, and then I burned the job instead of sending it. I told Halloway's office last night I was done. There is no version of me that hands Maren Nystrom to a developer, Beatrix. There never was going to be."

"There's a version of you that wrote it." Her voice didn't shake, which felt like a small victory. "That's the version I hired. That's the version I—" She stopped herself at the edge of the word. *Kissed. Trusted. Let into the orchard at night.* "That's the version I let hold my hand in front of my whole family."

"I know." He looked down at his hat, turned it once. "And I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking for something smaller. An hour. In the cider house. This morning."

"Why the cider house?"

"Because Maren asked for it." He lifted his eyes then, and there was something in them she hadn't seen before — not the investigator's flat competence, not the boyfriend's easy warmth, but something rawer. Fear, maybe. Or hope wearing fear's coat. "She called me at six o'clock this morning. She said, *Tell my granddaughter to bring the deeds and tell her not to decide anything until she's heard everyone.* Everyone, she said. I don't know what she's done, Beatrix. But she did it while you were still asleep, and I think you should see it."

Beatrix stood in the doorway a long moment. The wind came off the water and moved the loose strand of hair at her temple, and she thought of all the doors she had closed in five years — the apartment in the city, the magazine job, the friendships that had gone soft and strange with distance — and how carefully she had taught herself that closed doors were safety, that smallness was survival.

Maren had signed away part of the orchard to keep a debt off Beatrix's shoulders. Quiet sacrifice, again, the family inheritance no one had ever put in writing.

*Don't decide anything until you've heard everyone.*

"All right," she said. "One hour. And Elias — you walk behind me. You don't stand next to me. Not today."

He nodded like a man accepting a sentence. "Behind you," he said. "Wherever you want me."

* * *

The cider house sat at the lowest fold of the orchard, a long stone building older than anyone living, with a slate roof greened by moss and windows fogged with a century of pressing. Beatrix had expected — if she had let herself expect anything — a handful of people. Maren, perhaps. Maybe the Ostergaard brothers, who were loyal to her grandmother out of forty years of shared harvests.

What she found was nearly the whole town.

They filled the benches along the walls and stood in clusters between the presses, breath steaming in the cold stone air. Tom Voss from the ridge, in his good coat. The Halvorsens. Widow Astrid Lund with her cane and her fox-fur collar. Young Ruthie Ostergaard, who couldn't have been more than twenty-two and whose family farm had been in Pemberton's sights since spring. Even Pastor Eklund, hatless, looking solemn as a Sunday. Forty people. Fifty. Faces Beatrix had known her whole life, faces that had watched her leave and watched her come home and never once asked why either time.

Maren stood at the head of the room beside the great wooden press, and when she saw Beatrix in the doorway she raised one hand — not a wave, exactly. A summons. A benediction. Both.

"I called them at dawn," Maren said, without preamble, in the voice she used for weather reports and hard truths. "Every household in Hollow. I told them there was something they needed to hear before the Harvest Festival, and that it concerned every deed in this valley except the church's." Her eyes went past Beatrix, found Elias standing two steps back, and softened by a degree only Beatrix could have measured. "And I told them the young man who found out the truth would be here to answer for it."

"Maren—" Elias began.

"You'll get your turn." She said it the way she said *the apples are ready*, and something in the room shifted — not hostility, exactly, but the weight of attention settling on him like snow. "First, my granddaughter reads. Beatrix. Come here."

Beatrix crossed the room feeling every eye. Maren pressed a folder into her hands — the copied deeds, the ones Elias had photographed in the study, now printed and annotated in Maren's own careful hand. Dates in the margins. Names of witnesses. *Signed under advisement of Pemberton's counsel, alone, no family present.*

"They'll listen to you," Maren murmured, so only Beatrix could hear. "Not to me. I'm just an old woman they're fond of. You're the one who left and came back, and that means they believe you know what's worth staying for."

Beatrix opened the folder. Her hands were steady. Later she would wonder at that; in the moment it simply seemed that the paper demanded steadiness and gave it back.

"Astrid Lund," she read. "Parcel fourteen, north slope. Easement granted March of this year, granting Aldous Pemberton permanent access rights and first refusal on sale, in exchange for consideration of four thousand dollars against a debt of thirty-one thousand incurred through loan restructuring advised by the same office."

A sound went through the room, low and collective, like wind through the trees before a storm decides.

"Widow Lund," Beatrix said, looking up, finding the old woman's eyes, "did anyone explain to you that the debt could be contested? That the restructuring terms weren't legal?"

"No one explained anything, dear." Astrid's voice was thin but clear. "There was a man with a briefcase and a cup of coffee, and he talked very fast, and by the time I understood the papers he'd already poured me a second cup." A pause. "I was lonely. He knew it."

Beatrix read the next deed. And the next. Tom Voss. The Halvorsens. Parcel after parcel, the same architecture of predation — isolation, urgency, complexity, a signature extracted from tired hands. When she reached her grandmother's name her voice caught, and Maren, standing beside her, took the page gently from her fingers and finished reading it herself, in a level voice, as though reciting a psalm.

When it was done, the silence in the cider house was total. Then Tom Voss said, "I took mine to a lawyer in Ashford. Cost me eight hundred dollars to be told it was probably enforceable. *Probably.*" He spat the word. "That's what the law gave us last time too. Remember the mill? Eleven years of court and we got nothing but legal bills and a parking lot."

"We remember," said Pastor Eklund quietly.

"So what do we do?" Ruthie Ostergaard, young and fierce. "We can't afford another eleven years. Most of us won't live eleven years."

It was Maren who answered, but she answered looking at Beatrix.

"The law failed us because it works slowly and we work alone," she said. "So we stop being alone, and we stop waiting. Saturday is the Harvest Festival. The whole county comes through that fairground — the press from Ashford, the radio, half the state's politicians eating fried dough and kissing babies." She let that sit. "We read the deeds aloud. All of them. On the bandstand, at noon, where no lawyer can call it a deposition and no judge can seal it. We make it public in the oldest way there is. We tell the truth in front of witnesses, and we keep telling it until it can't be buried."

"And Pemberton?" someone asked. "His lawyers?"

"Pemberton built all of this on our silence," Maren said. "Silence is the only thing he owns outright. So we take it from him."

The room broke open — voices rising, overlapping, Astrid Lund laughing a small disbelieving laugh, Tom Voss pounding the bench, Ruthie already saying *I'll make the copies, I'll make fifty copies* — and in the middle of it Beatrix turned and found Elias standing where she had made him stand, behind her, apart, watching her with an expression she had no defenses against. Not calculation. Not charm. Just a man watching the woman he loved do something brave, and knowing he had no right to be proud of her and being proud anyway.

She crossed to him through the noise.

"You knew this would happen," she said. "When Maren called you. You knew what she was planning."

"I hoped." He didn't flinch from her. "I came anyway, knowing you might shut the door. Because you deserved to hear it from the town, not from a file. And because—" He stopped. Started again, plainer. "Because I'd rather stand in front of you and be refused than stay away and be safe. That's the whole truth. It's the only thing I've got that isn't in a report."

Beatrix looked at him for a long moment. Around them the cider house roared with plans — the bandstand, noon, the reporter from the *Ashford Courier* whom Pastor Eklund swore he could reach, Maren organizing committees with the efficiency of a general. Five years ago she had learned that trust was a door best kept locked. But doors, she was beginning to understand, were not walls. A door could be opened. A door could even be held open, trembling, for a man who had lied to her and then chosen her over everything else.

"Saturday," she said. "You'll stand with me on the bandstand. Not behind me. Beside me — where everyone in this valley can see exactly who I chose, lies and all."

Elias exhaled, and it seemed to take the last five days with it. "Beside you," he said.

Outside, the light was climbing over the orchard, gilding the rows, and the apples hung heavy and ready, and somewhere down the coast road a car was turning in — a dark sedan moving too slowly for a delivery, pausing too long at the gate.

Ruthie saw it first. "Is that—"

"Yes," Beatrix said, folding the folder closed. "That's Pemberton's car."

He had heard, somehow. And he was coming.
