## 10. After the Last Train Leaves

The morning after the Harvest Festival, Maple Hollow woke up changed.

Beatrix felt it before she understood it — a lightness in the air, the way the gulls wheeled differently over the harbor, the way Mrs. Okafor waved from her porch with both arms instead of the guarded half-lift she'd used all autumn. The easements were voided. Pemberton's lawyer had left on the noon ferry yesterday, clutching his briefcase like a wounded animal, and Pemberton himself had driven out before dawn, his silver car kicking up gravel all the way to the county road. By evening, the town had already begun telling the story back to itself, embellishing it, softening it, making it theirs.

But Beatrix had not gone down to the harbor to hear it retold. She had stayed in the lighthouse all day, and through the night, and when the sun came up gray and gold over the water, she was still at her desk — writing the last chapter of her novel.

She had been afraid of that chapter for five years. It had waited for her like a locked room, and every night she had circled it, drafting everything but the ending, because endings required her to decide what she believed: whether people could be trusted again, whether love was worth the cost of being seen, whether a life could be rebuilt after it had been publicly dismantled. She had thought she'd need answers before she could write the words.

She didn't. She only needed to write them.

Her pen moved until the light went gold again, and then it stopped, and there it was — *The End* — two small words that looked absurdly ordinary on the page, as if they hadn't taken half a decade and a whole town to earn. Beatrix sat back and read the final paragraph twice, and then she laughed, once, wetly, alone in the round stone room at the top of the world, because the heroine of her novel ran toward something in the last lines. She hadn't planned that. Her hands had decided before she did.

She checked the clock. Twenty past four.

The last train of the season left Maple Hollow station at six-fifteen. Elias had told her this morning, standing in Maren's kitchen with his duffel bag already packed by the door, his voice carefully level in the way she now knew meant he was holding himself together with both hands. "My work here is finished," he'd said. "It's time I went home."

*Home.* He'd said it like a place he could still find on a map.

She had let him go. She had stood in her grandmother's kitchen and watched him shoulder the bag and she had said, "Safe travels," as if he were a guest and not the man she loved, because some old scar tissue in her had whispered *let him leave first, it hurts less if you don't ask him to stay.* Five years ago she had learned that reaching for people gave them something to take away from you.

But she had written a novel about a woman who runs toward something. And Beatrix Nystrom had never once in her life written anything she didn't mean.

She was out the door before she fully decided to go, taking the cliff path two steps at a time, her coat flying open, the manuscript clutched against her chest in a leather folder — because if she was going to do this, she was going to do it completely, the way you did everything in Maple Hollow: in front of witnesses, with your whole heart showing.

* * *

The station was a small wooden thing, more shed than building, with a platform of weathered planks and a single bench where generations of departing souls had sat waiting for the world beyond the hollow. The train tracks ran along the edge of the orchard, and in October the trees were bare-limbed and patient, their fallen apples sweetening into the grass.

Elias was there.

He stood near the end of the platform with his duffel at his feet, looking down the empty tracks toward the bend where the train would appear, and even from thirty yards Beatrix could see that he wasn't waiting the way people wait when they're eager to leave. He was waiting the way men wait outside courtrooms. Like a verdict was coming.

Her breath caught. For one cowardly second she considered turning back — and then she thought of Maren signing that easement in silence, sacrificing herself quietly so no one would have to watch her do it, and Beatrix set her jaw and walked onto the platform with her head high.

He heard her footsteps and turned. Something crossed his face — hope, quickly buried, the way he buried everything.

"Beatrix." Her name in his mouth still did something to her spine. "I would have come by to say a proper goodbye."

"No, you wouldn't," she said. "You'd have left a note. You're a note-leaver. I found your report, remember."

A ghost of a wince. "Fair."

"The train doesn't come for another fifty minutes," she said, walking closer, close enough now to see the tiredness around his eyes, the stubble he hadn't bothered with, the way his hand flexed at his side like it wanted to reach for hers and had talked itself out of it. "Which is good. Because I've spent five years learning how to say things carefully, and carefully takes time."

"Beatrix—"

"I finished my book." She held up the folder. "Last night. All of it. The ending too. Do you know what happens? The heroine stops waiting to be worthy of her own life and walks straight into it. Very unsubtle. My editor will hate it. I don't care." Her voice was shaking and she let it shake. "I wrote that before I knew what I was going to say to you, and then I realized I'd already said it. On the page. To a man who was you wearing a different name. So now I'm saying it to your face, Elias Ward, investigator, liar, note-leaver, man who burned down his entire career rather than hand over an old woman's testimony to a rival developer—"

"You know about that?"

"Maren told me. You called her from the ferry dock Tuesday night. She tells me everything now. It's terrifying. I've created a monster." She was crying a little and laughing a little and she didn't stop either one. "You gave up everything. Your employer, your reputation, probably your references. For us. For a town you'd known three weeks. And then you packed your bag anyway, because you decided we'd be better off without the complication of you, and Elias, that is the single stupidest thing a smart man has ever done in Maple Hollow, and this town once elected a lighthouse keeper who couldn't swim."

His mouth twitched. "That's not in the historical record."

"It's in *my* record." She stepped right up to him, close enough to feel the warmth coming off him, close enough that the folder pressed between them. "Here's what I came to say. I'm not asking you to stay. Asking is what you do when you're prepared for the answer to be no. I'm telling you the door is open. It's been open since the cider house. Maybe since the orchard. Maybe — God help me — since the ferry dock, when I hired a stranger off a bench because he smiled like he meant it. If you get on that train, I will survive it. I survived worse. But I'll know, for the rest of my life, that I stood on this platform and told you the truth, and whatever you do with it is yours."

The wind came off the water and moved through the bare orchard, and somewhere far away a dog barked twice and went quiet.

Elias looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something she didn't expect. He crouched, opened his duffel, and pulled out a folded piece of paper — creased, handled, soft at the edges.

"What's that?"

"My ticket situation." He handed it to her.

She unfolded it. It was a printout of the seasonal rail schedule — the same schedule she'd pinned above her desk in Chapter One of her real life, the schedule that had started all of it, the last train marked in fading ink. Except someone had drawn a careful line through the six-fifteen departure. And beneath it, in Elias's small, precise handwriting:

*Not traveling today.*

"That's dated three days ago," she said slowly.

"I bought nothing. I booked nothing. I stood in Maren's kitchen this morning with my bag packed because I wanted — I needed — to give you the choice clean. No pressure, no performance. If you'd never come, I'd have sat on this bench until the train passed, and then I'd have walked back up the hill and knocked on your door and started over like a man with no train to catch." He exhaled, long and unsteady. "I was never leaving, Beatrix. I just needed to know you'd want me to stay as something other than a role. Not the fake boyfriend. Not the investigator. Just — me. Elias. Who is bad at goodbyes and worse at beginnings and has never once in his life run toward anything until this town taught him how."

"So you drew a line through the train."

"I drew a line through the train."

Behind them, far down the track, the six-fifteen sounded its horn at the crossing — a long, mournful note rolling in across the orchard, announcing its approach to a station where no one waited to board. They both turned to watch it come around the bend, iron and diesel and bound for cities neither of them belonged to anymore.

And as it slowed — as it actually began braking, doors hissing open to the cold October air, the conductor leaning out to scan an empty platform — Elias Ward took Beatrix's hand, laced his fingers through hers in front of God and the conductor and the whole listening orchard, and did not move.

The conductor waited. Waited longer than conductors wait. Then he tipped his cap at the two of them standing hand in hand among the fallen apples, shook his head with something that might have been a smile, and pulled the door shut.

The train pulled away, carrying everyone's else's departures with it, growing smaller and smaller down the line until it was a smudge, then a sound, then nothing at all.

"Well," Beatrix said, when her voice worked again. "Now you're stuck with us. There isn't another one until spring."

"I know." He turned to face her fully, both hands around hers now, the manuscript folder tucked between their chests like a treaty. "I counted on it."

"You counted on it."

"Beatrix." His voice dropped low, the way it did only for her, only ever for her. "Ask me why I really stayed."

She searched his face — this man who had arrived with questions and secrets and a smile like a lit window, who had lied to her beautifully and then told her the truth ugly, who had chosen a town full of strangers over a career built on leaving. Behind him the orchard stood bare and waiting, the first buds of next year's fruit already sleeping in its branches, and above it all the decommissioned lighthouse kept its white watch over the water, needing no keeper, keeping faith anyway.

"Why did you stay?" she asked.

He smiled — not the charming stranger's smile from the ferry dock, but the real one, the one that belonged to no performance at all.

"Because," he said, "you haven't shown me the last chapter yet. And I intend to find out how it ends."

She laughed, and pulled him close, and kissed him there on the empty platform while the rails ticked softly, cooling, and the tide came in below, and somewhere up the hill a lamp was being lit in a kitchen that had room for one more chair.

Hand in hand, they walked home.
