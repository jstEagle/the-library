## 1. The Ghost in the Manuscript

The ferry to Port Alder ran twice a day in October, and Jonah Halloran took the morning one because it gave him four hours of legitimate idleness before anyone could ask him what he did for a living.

He sat at the stern with a thermos of coffee going lukewarm and watched the mainland shrink to a gray pencil line. The water was the color of unpolished pewter. Gulls hung above the wake like punctuation marks nobody had bothered to place. He had been on ferries like this one maybe thirty times in his career — the approach to some coastal town where a man with money wanted his life made legible — and he knew the ritual by heart. There would be a driver waiting at the dock. There would be a house too large for its owner's actual personality. There would be a study, a decanter, a stack of photographs sorted into piles labeled *usable* and *not*, though no one ever said those words aloud.

And there would be a voice. Not the subject's real voice — that was the first thing they always got wrong about ghostwriting, that the ghost was supposed to capture the man. No. The ghost captured the man *as he wished to be remembered*. That was the job. That was the whole job.

Jonah had gotten good at it. That was the problem, if he was honest, which he tried not to be, professionally speaking. Honesty was a material he worked with the way a mason worked with limestone: something to be cut, shaped, load-bearing only where you needed it to be.

His phone buzzed. A text from Charles Vane's assistant, a brisk woman named Dolores whom he'd spoken to exactly twice:

*Mr. Vane asks that you come straight to Harbor House on arrival. He says the tide is right this afternoon and he wants to walk you through the wharf story while the light holds.*

*The wharf story.* Jonah almost smiled. Every subject had one — the founding myth, the scene they'd rehearsed so many times it had worn smooth as sea glass. For Vane it was apparently the day in 1979 when he'd bought out his partners' shares at the harbor, the transaction that had made Vane Shipping what it was and, according to everything Jonah had read in the pre-interview transcripts, unmade a few other things along the way.

He typed back: *On my way.*

Then he put the phone away and let himself do the thing he did on every crossing, the private liturgy: he thought about who he would have been if he hadn't become this.

Not much of an exercise anymore. The muscle had atrophied. Ten years ago the question had kept him up at night; five years ago it visited on anniversaries; now it arrived mostly on ferries, in the space between two shores, when he belonged to neither.

*Jonah Halloran, forty-one, of nowhere in particular.* He'd stopped putting a city on his tax forms beyond "current." His mail went to a service in Boston. His books — eleven of them, two of which had been bestsellers under other men's names — lined shelves in houses he'd never see again after the final invoice cleared. Once, in a bookstore in Portland, he'd found a memoir he'd written displayed face-out on a table, the subject's bearded grin filling the jacket, and he'd stood there for a full minute reading his own sentences in a stranger's mouth until a clerk asked if he needed help.

*I wrote this,* he'd almost said. What he'd actually said was, "No, thank you," which was, he reflected now, more or less the sentence his entire life was built on.

The ferry horn sounded. Port Alder came up out of the water the way old towns do — all at once, without apology. A crooked wooden dock. A hill of salt-bleached houses climbing toward a white church steeple. Behind the town, half-hidden in the gray air, the dark green suggestion of hills, orchards maybe, though October had stripped most of them bare. A crane stood idle over the shipping yard like a heron that had given up.

It was beautiful in the way places are beautiful when they've stopped trying. Jonah felt the familiar small ache of arrival, the sense of walking into a story already in progress — and the familiar relief of knowing he wouldn't have to write his own part in it. Someone else's manuscript would absorb him. It always did.

* * *

Harbor House sat at the top of the town's one steep street, a shingled Victorian with widow's walks and a view of everything Vane had conquered. The driver — a silent young man who introduced himself as Teague and nothing else — took Jonah's bag and led him through a front hall paneled in dark wood, past a staircase, into a study that smelled of pipe smoke and old varnish.

Charles Vane stood at the window with his back to the door, and Jonah took the moment — ghosts get few of these, moments when the subject doesn't know they're being observed — to look at him freely. Seventy-eight years old, broad through the shoulders still, the kind of big man whose bulk had gone from power to ballast over the decades. White hair cut short and practical. Hands clasped behind his back like a ship's figurehead turned to face inland.

"You're punctual," Vane said, without turning. "That's something. My last biographer kept me waiting twice, and I fired him before he'd written a word."

"I read the transcripts on the crossing," Jonah said. "The 1987 expansion chapter is strong."

Vane turned then, and his eyes were quick and pale and appraising, the eyes of a man who had spent sixty years pricing things, including people. "You don't waste time either. Good." He crossed the room and shook Jonah's hand with a grip calibrated to communicate something. "You'll want to know why I chose you."

"Everyone does."

A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Because you wrote Ellery Crane's book, and Crane was a liar of the first order, and you made him sound like scripture. If you can do that for Crane—" he gestured at the window, the harbor below, the fleet of three rusting freighters at anchor, "—you can do it for a man telling the truth."

*Telling the truth.* Jonah filed the phrase where he filed all such phrases, in the drawer marked *material.*

"The wharf story," he said. "Dolores said the light was right."

"The light is always right in October. It forgives nothing and flatters everything. Come on." Vane took a walking stick from the stand by the door — not because he needed it, Jonah suspected, but because a man like Vane understood props. "I'll show you the harbor from the point. You can't write a harbor from a window."

They walked down through the garden, past apple trees gone wild and leggy, their fruit unpicked and dropping into the grass with soft thuds. At the point, where a bench faced the water, Vane stopped and planted the stick.

"There," he said, and pointed with his chin at the long wooden finger of the main wharf. "Nineteen seventy-nine. I was thirty-six years old. My father was dead eight months, the company was bleeding, and my two partners — the Aldous brothers — wanted to sell the wharf rights to a mainland conglomerate and retire to golf courses. I bought them out. Every share. In one afternoon."

"In one afternoon," Jonah repeated, making a note he didn't need to make. He knew this scene from the transcripts. He knew it better than Vane did, probably, because the transcripts contained the variations — the three different tellings, each with the details shifted slightly, the way a shoreline shifts with the tide.

"One afternoon," Vane confirmed. "And I'll tell you what your predecessors never understood. Everyone wants to make it about the money. It wasn't about the money. It was about the *town*. Those brothers would have sold the wharf to men who'd have automated it inside five years, torn down the cannery, moved the fleet. Port Alder would be a marina and a gift shop now. I kept it alive. Whatever else anyone says about me —" here his jaw tightened around something unsaid, a door closing on a room, "— I kept this town alive."

Jonah looked at the wharf. It was true that the town existed, that the boats came in, that the cannery — half of it shuttered, but half of it running — still employed two hundred people. It was also true, per the documents Jonah had spent three weeks reading in a law office in Boston, that the Aldous brothers had been pressured into selling, that the price had been a fraction of fair value, that one of them, Robert Aldous, had died four years later with the lawsuit he'd filed still pending and his family scattered to the mainland. It was true that *kept alive* and *took captive* could describe the same act, depending on where you stood.

But Jonah's contract didn't require him to stand anywhere. That was the beauty of it. The ghost has no body; the ghost has no position.

"It's a strong scene," he said. "The single afternoon. We open on the weather — I'm thinking fog, if the records support it — and we build to the signature. The pen coming down. Everything before that day is prologue; everything after is consequence."

Vane studied him. "You make it sound simple."

"It's not simple. It's just clear. Clarity is the hard part. Most lives are weather; a memoir has to be climate."

Something flickered across the old man's face — approval, yes, but underneath it something lonelier, the look of a man realizing he was about to be fixed in place forever, like a specimen in resin. Then it passed, and Vane clapped him once on the shoulder with a hand like a mooring cleat.

"Dinner's at seven. Dolores will set you up in the east cottage — you'll work there, not in the house. I find writers underfoot intolerable." He turned back toward the house, then paused. "Halloran. The chapters I've outlined for you — the early ones, the company ones — you'll draft them fast. But there's a section coming. Later. Family matters." The stick tapped once against the stone. "When we get there, you'll write what I tell you to write. Understood?"

"Understood," Jonah said, and heard how easily it came out of him, smooth as water finding a channel it had run down ten thousand times.

* * *

The east cottage was a converted boathouse fifty yards down the lawn, all cedar and glass, with a desk facing the water and a bed he'd barely use. Jonah unpacked with the efficiency of a man who had learned never to fully arrive anywhere: laptop, chargers, the box of transcripts, the legal pad on which he kept — in a shorthand no one else could read — the gap between what his subjects remembered and what had happened.

He worked until the light went. The opening chapter came quickly, the way it always did when the material was good and the conscience was quiet. *Fog on the morning of October 14th, 1979...* He gave Vane the weather he wanted. He gave the Aldous brothers dignity in defeat, which was generous of them both. He wrote the pen coming down on the signature like a keel breaking water, and when he read the paragraph back he felt the small cold satisfaction of craft well executed, and beneath the satisfaction, as always, the faintest hum of something he refused to name, the way you can refuse to hear a dripping tap until three in the morning.

Around nine he made tea and carried it out onto the cottage's little deck. The town below had gone to a scatter of yellow windows. The harbor lights doubled themselves in the black water. Somewhere a buoy bell knocked against its mooring, patient as a heartbeat.

This was the hour Jonah liked best and trusted least. The work was done for the day, the borrowed voices were hung up like coats, and what remained was just a man standing on a deck with tea going cold, in a town where no one knew his name, about a life that wasn't his.

Ten years ago tonight — he did the arithmetic automatically, the way other people checked the time — he had been in a restaurant in Boston watching Claire sign the last of the papers, her handwriting smaller than he remembered it, and he had thought: *this is the last thing I will ever write that's truly mine.* He hadn't meant it as a joke. It had turned out to be one anyway.

After Claire there had been the letters. He didn't let himself think about the letters during working hours — they were the one file he kept off the legal pad, the one account he never audited. Anonymous notes that had started arriving six months after the engagement ended, forwarded through his agent with no return address, signed only with a single initial. *M.* Someone who had read a magazine profile of him, someone who wrote that she was sorry, that betrayal by the people who know us best is its own specific weather, that he should keep writing even if only for himself. Eleven letters over ten years. He had answered none of them. Answering would have made it real, and real things could end, and he had lost enough real things to last —

Well. To last, evidently, a lifetime of other people's endings.

The last letter had come fourteen months ago. He'd read it standing in his kitchen in Boston and then put it in the drawer with the others and told himself he'd answer it on the weekend, and the weekends had stacked up like unread newspapers, and then the Vane contract had come and swallowed the year whole.

He finished the tea. Below him, at the edge of the property where the lawn met the lane, he noticed a light he hadn't seen earlier — not at Harbor House, but beyond it, in the small cottage that sat on the neighboring lot, half-hidden behind a windbreak of pines. A warm square of lamplight in a downstairs window. As he watched, a shape crossed behind the glass, unhurried, carrying something — a box, maybe, or an armful of firewood — and the light held steady, domestic, awake.

New neighbor, probably. Or an old one he'd have no reason to meet. Port Alder was full of people living their own unfinished stories, and Jonah Halloran had made a career of never asking about them.

He went inside, closed the deck door against the cold coming off the water, and opened the laptop. The cursor blinked in the dark of the screen, patient, indifferent, waiting for him to give it someone else's life.

*Chapter One,* he typed. *In the beginning, there was the harbor.*

Outside, far down the lane, the light in the neighboring cottage burned on.
