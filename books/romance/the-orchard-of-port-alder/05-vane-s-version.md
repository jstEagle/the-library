## 5. Vane's Version

The rain came in off the water around noon, and by two o'clock the Harbor House smelled of woodsmoke and old money and something faintly medicinal that I suspected was Charles Vane himself — a man preserved in tinctures of his own importance.

He received me in the study the way a king receives an ambassador: seated, already mid-thought, as though my arrival were merely the next item on an agenda he'd been running since dawn. The room was all dark wood and darker portraits. His wife hung above the fireplace in oils, young forever, her smile painted by someone who had clearly been paid to flatter.

"Sit," he said, without looking up from the ledger open on his desk. "We'll do the chapter today. The one about the family."

I set my recorder on the leather blotter, then thought better of it and took out my notebook instead. There was something about this room that made machines feel obscene. Or maybe it was me. Maybe I was the obscene thing — a man paid to turn a life into a version of itself.

"You've read my notes," Vane said.

"I have."

"Then you know what happened." He closed the ledger with a soft, final sound, like a lid going down on something alive. "My daughter will tell you otherwise, if she ever speaks to you, which she won't. She has her mother's talent for revision. But I have the documents. I have the dates. I have the truth, Halloran, which is more than most men can claim at seventy-three."

"Mr. Vane—"

"Charles." He looked at me for the first time. His eyes were pale gray, the color of the harbor in February, and they had the particular patience of a man who had bought and sold people larger than me. "You're being paid to write what I tell you. Not to editorialize. Not to balance. Balance is for accountants."

I wrote *Charles* at the top of a fresh page and felt the small, familiar death of it — the moment where I stopped being a person and became a conduit. Ten years of practice. I could do it standing on my head.

"Begin wherever you like," I said.

He began, as it turned out, with the weather.

* * *

"It was the worst spring in forty years," he said. "Fog for six weeks straight. You couldn't see the channel buoys from the point. And in the middle of it, my wife got sick — really sick, not the tiredness she'd been complaining about since Christmas, but the thing that would kill her, though we didn't have the name for it yet. And my daughter—" He paused, and I braced myself for grief, because good liars always pause before the load-bearing lie. "My daughter chose that spring to empty the accounts."

I kept writing. My hand did what hands do when the mind objects: it transcribed.

"Explain 'empty,'" I said, because even a ghost gets to ask questions. It's one of the few dignities left to us.

"She moved the operating funds. Two hundred thousand dollars, give or take — into accounts only she controlled. She was twenty-six years old. She had no authority to do it, but she'd been handling her mother's affairs, and her mother trusted her, and she used that trust like a crowbar." He turned his chair toward the window, toward the rain silvering the glass. "I found out from the bank manager. A man I'd known thirty years, calling me at home, embarrassed, apologetic, careful. That's how you know you've been robbed, Halloran. When your friends start talking to you carefully."

"And her reason? As you understood it?"

"As I understood it?" He laughed — a short, hard sound with nothing in it. "She said she was protecting the company from me. From *me*. Said I'd made decisions she disagreed with. Said the Vane line was going to be sold out from under the town." He shook his head slowly, a performance of sorrow so practiced it had worn smooth as sea glass. "She was twenty-six. She'd read some articles. She thought she understood shipping because she'd grown up around it the way a child grows up around church — hearing the words without knowing the doctrine."

"What did you do?"

"What any father does." Now he turned back, and his face arranged itself into something wounded and noble, and I understood that he had rehearsed this too — maybe in front of the mirror, maybe just in front of the dark, over and over across ten years until the wound and the mask had fused. "I gave her a choice. Put the money back, come home, and we'd never speak of it again. Or keep it, and lose everything else."

"And she chose?"

"She chose to leave." He said it flatly, letting the flatness do the work. "She flew out that night. Didn't say goodbye to her mother, who was dying, Halloran. Dying. Her mother asked for her at the end — asked every day for three weeks — and Mara Ellison was in another state building herself a new life on stolen money."

There it was. The load-bearing lie, set neatly into place, mortared with a dying woman's name.

I want to tell you that I stopped him there. That I set down my pen and said, *Mr. Vane, I can't write this, because I've met your daughter's handwriting, and it doesn't look like a thief's.* I want to tell you that. But the truth is that my pen moved, and my voice said, "And after the funeral?" and my whole self stood aside like a servant while the master of the house walked through the rooms of his own myth, turning on lights.

"After the funeral," he said, "she sent a lawyer's letter. Wanted her mother's papers. Personal correspondence, photographs, the family records. I refused, naturally. Those things belong here, with the family." He gestured at the shelves, the ledgers, the locked drawers — the archive of a life curated by its sole surviving author. "She sued. Lost. Sent the letter twice more over the years. I still get one, some years. Like a tide table. Very regular."

He almost smiled at that, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was real. Whatever else was false in the room, his pleasure in denying her was genuine. Grief curdles differently in different men. In Charles Vane it had curdled into a hobby.

"The chapter should end there," he said. "With the letters. Let the reader understand: I never closed the door. She's the one who walked away, and she's the one who keeps knocking on it with lawyers instead of her fists. A father forgives anything except being made the villain in his own house."

I wrote it all down. Every word. My shorthand is fast and ugly and honest — it catches the pauses, the little wet sounds a man makes when he weeps on schedule. Somewhere in those pages was also, folded tight, the record of my own disgrace: a man taking dictation from power, converting a woman into a cautionary tale, and calling it work.

"There's one more thing," Vane said, as I flipped to a clean page. "The orchard."

"The orchard?"

"Her grandmother planted it. On the property behind the cottages — you'll have seen it, the old fenced lot with the apple trees gone wild. My mother-in-law's pride. When Mara left, the orchard went to ruin. Nobody's touched it in ten years." He said this lightly, tossing it off, but I'd learned his rhythms by then, and lightness was where he hid the sharpest things. "If it comes up — if anyone asks — the orchard is a symbol. Use it that way. Everything she abandoned. Everything she let die. Write it however you like, but make sure the reader feels it."

*I'll make sure the reader feels whatever's true,* I did not say.

"Understood," I said.

* * *

I stayed for coffee afterward, because declining would have been a statement, and statements weren't in my contract. We sat in the study while the rain worked at the windows, and Vane talked about the early days — the first boat, the first loan, the winter he ate herring and ambition — and this part was good, this part was true, and I wrote it gladly, because a man's real story is usually better than the one he's selling, and my job, at its best, is to trick people into telling it.

But underneath the good hours ran the other transcript, the one no recorder would catch:

*Two hundred thousand dollars,* he said. But whose ledger said so? His.

*She emptied the accounts.* Or she moved them somewhere he couldn't reach them. There's a difference, and men like Charles Vane have never once acknowledged it in their lives.

*A father forgives anything except being made the villain.* And what does a daughter forgive, Mr. Vane, when the villain writes the only book?

I thought about the woman next door — the new tenant in the Calloway cottage, the one with the boxes and the late lamp, whom I'd seen exactly once across the market crowd, feeling something I hadn't let myself name. I thought about how towns like Port Alder keep their stories the way the sea keeps wrecks: submerged, shifting, dangerous to dive. And I thought: *if she ever reads this chapter, she will know exactly what it cost to write it, because she will feel the ghost's fingerprints on every page — the absence where a man's conscience used to be.*

"Halloran." Vane's voice pulled me back. "You've gone somewhere."

"Just listening," I said. Which was true, in the way my sentences had learned to be true — technically, hollowly, safe.

"Good. Listening is the job." He refilled his coffee from the silver pot, his hand steady, his eyes on the fire. "My editor wants the family chapters first. Says scandal sells a memoir faster than ships do. So you'll have the draft to me by Friday." He glanced over, mild as milk. "Any problems with that?"

"No problems," I said.

And there it was — the crack. Small as a hairline fracture in a hull, invisible from the deck, running cold and straight through the middle of me. Because I heard my own voice say *no problems* and recognized, with a clarity I hadn't managed in ten years, the exact tone of a man lying to himself in someone else's interest.

I packed my notebook. At the door I paused, the way you pause at the edge of deep water.

"The orchard," I said. "Your mother-in-law's trees. Are any of them still alive?"

Vane looked surprised by the question, then faintly contemptuous, as though I'd asked after the health of his enemies.

"Crabapples, probably, by now," he said. "Things go back to wild when nobody tends them. That's nature's whole argument, isn't it? Why we need men to keep order."

I let myself out into the rain.

* * *

The walk home took twelve minutes along the cliff road, and I spent all of them arguing with a man who wasn't there — myself, ten years younger, the one who'd once believed that words were sacred things you handled carefully. *You're a writer,* that younger man kept saying. *Writers don't launder reputations.* And the man I'd become kept answering, reasonably, wearily: *writers also eat.*

By the time I reached the cottages the rain had thinned to mist, and the lane was that particular blue-gray of a coastal dusk, lamps coming on one by one behind salt-fogged glass. Next door, the Calloway cottage glowed. Her lamp was lit again — third night running — and through the thin curtains I could see the shadow of movement, someone pacing, someone working, someone building a life out of boxes in a town that remembered her, whoever she was.

I stood at my gate longer than a man should stand at his own gate.

Then I noticed the fence.

It ran between our properties, old cedar, leaning where the winters had leaned on it, and along its length — I saw it now, in the lamplight spilling from her yard — someone had been working. A section near the corner stood straightened, its broken pickets replaced with fresh-cut wood, pale and new against the gray. Sawdust still clung to the grass. Someone had been repairing the boundary between us, quietly, without asking, the way you tend a thing you intend to stay beside.

I looked at the cottage window. The shadow had stopped moving. For a moment — I'd swear this later, under oath, to myself — the curtain shifted, as if someone on the other side had been watching me watch the fence.

I went inside. I sat down at my desk. I opened the notebook to the day's pages, all that fluent ugliness, and I read what I had written about a woman I'd never met, in a dead man's voice, in a living liar's service.

Then I picked up my pen to make it beautiful.

That was the part I'd never admitted before, not even to myself: the crime wasn't the taking of dictation. Anyone can be bullied. The crime was the craft — the way I could take a cruel, crooked, self-serving account and sand it smooth, give it rhythm, make it *move*, until a reader finished the chapter believing a woman had abandoned her dying mother, and believing it because the prose was good. My talent was the weapon. I had spent ten years sharpening it on other people's lives.

I worked until midnight. The chapter came out clean. It came out devastating. It came out exactly as Charles Vane dreamed it, and better, because dreams don't know how to structure a reveal and I do.

At half past midnight I carried my tea dregs to the kitchen, and through the window above the sink I saw that her light was still burning next door. And below it, at the fence line, in the yellow wash of the porch lamp, the fresh-cut pickets gleamed like something newly forgiven.

Friday, Vane wanted the draft. Friday was four days away.

Four days to decide which kind of ghost I was going to be.
