## 20. Ferry Day

**JONAH**

The horn woke him before the alarm did.

One long note rolling across the harbor, the way it had every morning for three weeks now — the ferry testing its voice, reminding Port Alder that the season was ending, that the water would soon close like a book and the town would be left alone with itself until spring.

Jonah stood at the window of his cottage with a duffel bag at his feet and a manuscript box under his arm. Four hundred and twelve pages. He'd slept maybe two hours, and not because of nerves. Because of the strange calm that had settled over him somewhere around four in the morning, when he'd finished rereading the whole thing one last time — Vane's lies, his own complicity, every sentence he'd ever written in another man's voice — and understood that whatever happened today, he was done hiding behind other people's words.

He'd made copies. Three sets. One in the box, one mailed to a lawyer in Portland on Thursday, one in the trunk of his car under a tarp, which was ridiculous, because no one in Port Alder stole from cars. But old habits died hard. A man who'd spent ten years as a ghost learned to keep backups of himself.

Mara hadn't answered his letter.

Three days since he'd slid it under her door. Three days of watching her kitchen light come on at six, go off at midnight, of seeing her silhouette move past the window with the deliberate pace of someone who had decided something but hadn't told him what. He'd rehearsed a dozen speeches on the walk down Harbor Road. All of them collapsed into the same simple truth: *I wrote you back. It took me ten years. I'm sorry.*

The town was already gathering at the dock.

That was the thing about Port Alder he'd never gotten used to — how a town of nine hundred people could produce a crowd of four hundred for anything: a christening, a funeral, a ferry. The last boat of the season was all three at once. People brought coffee in thermoses and children on their shoulders. Old Mrs. Trelawney had set up near the gangway with a folding chair and the expression of a woman who attended disasters professionally.

And at the center of it, standing too straight in a coat that cost more than the ferry earned in a season, was Charles Vane.

Jonah's stomach dropped. Vane wasn't supposed to be here. The plan — Jonah's plan, fragile as eggshell — had been to board quietly, publish independently from Portland, let the lawyers do the shouting. But Vane had called him last night at eleven, voice smooth as varnish: *Meet me at the dock before the boat, Mr. Halloran. We should conclude our business like gentlemen.* And Jonah, who had spent a decade concluding other people's business, had said yes, because refusing felt like handing Vane a weapon.

Now he saw the weapon. Vane hadn't come alone. Beside him stood a lawyer in a gray suit — mainland cut, city shoes wrong on salted planks — carrying a leather portfolio the way soldiers carry ordnance.

*He's going to do it here,* Jonah realized. *In front of everyone. He's going to make it a spectacle so the town chooses sides before I can say a word.*

Vane saw him coming and smiled. It was a good smile. Jonah had watched him deploy it in interviews for twenty years of press clippings. It was the smile of a man who had never once lost an argument he'd chosen to have in public.

"Mr. Halloran." Vane's voice carried. He wanted it to carry. "Right on time. You always were."

"Charles—"

"I understand you've taken certain materials from my house." Vane said it loudly, pleasantly, the way you'd discuss weather. "Pages from my manuscript. My property. My *life*, Mr. Halloran, reduced to stolen paper in your little box there."

A murmur went through the crowd. Jonah felt four hundred pairs of eyes settle on him like gulls on a fish head.

"The pages are copies," Jonah said. "You know they're copies. And you know what's in them isn't true."

"What's in them is *mine* to say or not say." Vane stepped closer, and up close Jonah could see the strain under the polish — the sleepless nights, the fury banked down to coals. "I paid you to write a book. Instead you've been writing your own little revolution. Did you think I wouldn't find out? Did you think a man who built an empire out of other people's silence wouldn't recognize a thief?"

"There it is," someone said in the crowd, low. Jonah didn't look. He kept his eyes on Vane, because looking away now would be the old habit, the ghost's habit, the flinch.

"You're right," Jonah said, and the crowd quieted, because nobody expected the accused to agree. "I did take your pages. I took them because they're lies, Charles. I wrote half those lies myself, and I'm not going to apologize for stealing them back. That book erases your daughter. It turns her mother into a footnote and Mara into a villain, and you did it because she saw you clearly once and you've never forgiven her for it."

The murmur became a sound. Not approval — Port Alder didn't hand out approval at dockside — but attention, sharp and total, the whole town leaning in.

Vane's lawyer opened the portfolio. "Mr. Halloran," he began, "you are in violation of a signed confidentiality agreement, and we are prepared to pursue—"

"Mara saw through him," Jonah said over him, louder now, his own voice strange and full in his ears, a voice he barely recognized because it was finally, entirely his. "Ten years ago she saw through him and he threw her out of his life and out of this story, and I helped him do it again on paper. So yes. Call it theft. Call it breach. Sue me. But read the pages first. Read them and ask yourselves why a man needs a ghostwriter to tell the truth about his own family."

* * *

**MARA**

She heard his voice before she saw him.

She'd been standing at the edge of the crowd for ten minutes, the tin box heavy against her ribs where she'd strapped it inside her coat like a heart transplant, listening to her father perform outrage the way he'd performed success her entire childhood — big gestures, bigger grievances, the whole theater of Charles Vane designed so that no one ever looked backstage.

Then Jonah's voice came over the crowd, and it was nothing like the careful, agreeable murmur she'd heard at dinner, nothing like the biographer's polite deflections. It was raw and certain and it said her name — *your daughter* — and something in Mara's chest unlocked that had been locked so long she'd forgotten it was a lock.

Her father's face did something she had never seen it do.

It faltered.

Just for a second. Just a flicker, like a lamp in wind. But Mara knew that face better than anyone alive, and she saw it: the moment the performance met something it couldn't script. Her father had built his life on the assumption that no one would ever contradict him in public, because he'd made contradiction expensive. And here was a ghostwriter — a hired pen, a man paid to be invisible — spending his career, his livelihood, his good name, on her.

She moved before she decided to move.

"Mara—" her father started, when he saw her coming, and for the first time in her life she heard hesitation in his voice. "This doesn't concern you."

"It's the only thing that concerns me." She stopped between them, between her father and the man she loved, and unzipped her coat, and pulled out the tin box, and held it up so the whole dock could see it — rusted, salt-stained, ordinary. "Do you know what this is? Anyone? Edie Marsh, you ran the pharmacy thirty years — you might remember. Ruth Calloway, you cleaned our house every Thursday for a decade. This box sat under the floorboards of my grandmother's orchard shed for twenty-five years. It's my mother's letters."

The crowd had gone absolutely still. Even the gulls seemed to be listening.

"My mother wrote letters," Mara said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her; grief and resolve had apparently reached some final settlement overnight. "Hundreds of them. To her sister, to friends, to her lawyer. And in them she tells a very different story than the one my father has been paying people to write about her." She turned to face him fully. "She tells the story of a husband who forged her signature on the loan documents in 1989. Who used her family's land as collateral without her knowledge. Who let her take the blame when the auditors came, because her name was smaller than his and easier to bury."

"That is a lie," Vane said, but it came out wrong — too fast, too high, a man reaching for a rung that wasn't there.

"I have her handwriting," Mara said. "I have dates. I have a letter to her attorney dated March 4th, 1990, describing exactly what you did, in her own words, asking him what her options were — and his reply, telling her that fighting you would cost more than she had. She wasn't silent because she agreed with you, Dad. She was silent because you made silence cheaper than truth. And then you spent thirty years telling everyone — telling *me* — that she abandoned us. That she was unstable. That she walked away." Her voice finally cracked, just once, and she let it. "She didn't walk away. You erased her. The same way you tried to erase me. The same way you tried to erase the truth in that manuscript."

"She's disturbed," Vane said to the crowd, gesturing at his daughter, and Mara watched him reach for the oldest tool in his kit — the one he'd used on her mother, the one he'd used on her. *Unstable. Difficult. Emotional.* "Surely you can all see—"

But the town of Port Alder looked at her, and then looked at him, and Mara saw the arithmetic happen across four hundred faces. Edie Marsh lowered her eyes. Ruth Calloway folded her arms. Old Mrs. Trelawney, professional attendant of disasters, said clearly and without drama:

"I always wondered why Eleanor stopped coming to church."

It was such a small sentence. It shouldn't have been the one that broke him. But Mara watched her father look around at the faces of the town he'd bought and charmed and managed for forty years, and saw him understand, all at once, that authority was not a possession but a loan, and the whole town was calling it due.

"You need a ghostwriter," Mara said quietly, just for him now, stepping close enough that only he could hear, "because you never learned to tell the truth in your own voice. I used to pity you for that. I don't anymore. But I'm done being a character in your book."

The ferry sounded its horn again — longer this time, the boarding call — and the sound rolled through the crowd like a verdict.

* * *

**JONAH**

Later, Jonah would remember the strangest detail: the gulls lifting off the pilings all at once when the horn sounded, a white confetti against gray sky, as if the harbor itself had exhaled.

Vane's lawyer was murmuring in his ear. Vane wasn't listening. He was looking at his daughter with an expression Jonah had no name for — something between hatred and hunger and, buried deepest of all, the naked astonishment of a man watching himself be seen accurately for the first time. It was, Jonah thought, the closest thing to honesty Charles Vane had produced in decades, and it was happening entirely on his face, wordless, in front of everyone.

"I'll see you in court," Vane said finally. His voice had gone flat, the varnish stripped off. "Both of you. Everything you have is worthless without context. I'll bury you in context."

"Then you'll be burying my mother twice," Mara said. "Go ahead. See how it reads."

Vane looked at her a moment longer. Then he turned, coat swinging, and walked up the ramp toward Harbor House, the lawyer scrambling after him with the useless portfolio, and the crowd parted for him the way crowds part for storms — not out of respect, but because no one yet knew what else to do.

And then it was quiet, and the ferry horn faded, and Jonah found Mara standing beside him with the tin box still clutched to her chest, both of them breathing hard, surrounded by four hundred people pretending very hard not to stare.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi." His voice came out wrecked. "I had a speech."

"I heard most of it. It needed work."

"It really did." He laughed — a cracked, disbelieving sound — and then the crowd was moving toward the gangway, and the morning was continuing, and there were perhaps ninety seconds left before the ferry doors closed and the season ended and everything either began or didn't.

"I mailed copies to a lawyer in Portland," he said quickly. "Thursday. Whatever happens, the pages exist outside this town now. Your mother's letters, my draft notes, everything. You don't have to decide anything today. You don't owe me anything. I just need you to know that I chose — yesterday, last night, this morning, every hour — I chose you and the truth over the contract, and I'd choose it again with worse odds."

"I know," Mara said.

"You know?"

"Jonah." She shifted the tin box to one arm and took his hand with the other, right there, in front of the whole town, and Jonah Halloran — ghost, coward, man fluent in every voice but his own — felt his knees actually weaken. "I read your letter. Three times. I stayed up all night reading it, and then I sat with my mother's letters until dawn, and I realized something. Ten years ago you couldn't answer a letter from a stranger because you thought you had nothing worth saying. And this morning you stood up in front of this entire town and said the hardest thing you've ever said, in your own voice, for me." Her eyes were bright and fierce and wet. "That's the answer. That's the answer to a letter I wrote when I was twenty-six years old and didn't even sign my full name. You just gave it. In public. With witnesses."

The deckhand called the last boarding. Somewhere behind them, Mrs. Trelawney said, "Well, are you two getting on or aren't you," in the tone of a woman who had bet money on the outcome.

"Come with me," Jonah said. "Not to run. Just — Portland has decent coffee, and a lawyer, and I want to watch you read the manuscript and mark it up in red pen until it tells the truth. Your story, your edits. I'll be the reader. Just the reader. That's the only job I want."

Mara looked at the ferry. At the water. At the town that remembered her, and the cottage next to his, and the orchard with its first bare sapling waiting for spring.

"On one condition," she said.

"Anything."

"We come back before the trees bloom. And we plant the rest of the orchard together. Every tree. My mother's rows, restored."

The horn sounded a third time — impatient now, final — and Jonah picked up the manuscript box, and Mara tucked the tin of letters inside her coat, and hand in hand they walked up the gangway while the town of Port Alder watched them go, some in judgment, some in wonder, and Mrs. Trelawney, who had indeed bet money, collected quietly from Ruth Calloway as the ropes came off and the last ferry of the season pulled away from the dock, carrying two people who had finally, after ten years and four hundred pages, begun to tell the truth.
