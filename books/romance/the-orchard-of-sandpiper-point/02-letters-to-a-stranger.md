## 2. Letters to a Stranger

The train still stopped at Sandpiper Point twice a day, which was more than the town deserved, in Victor Marsh's estimation, and less than it needed to survive, in everyone else's. Julian Marsh stepped off the evening one with a leather bag and a lie already rehearsed: *I'm here to help.*

Eleven years since he'd last stood on this platform. The paint on the station sign had faded from green to the color of old celery. Someone had planted geraniums in a barrel by the ticket window anyway — defiant, doomed, blooming. He remembered that about this place. Everything here grew out of spite or stubbornness, and no one could tell the difference.

His father's voice came through the phone before Julian had even cleared the platform.

"You're there?"

"I'm here."

"Good. The Hollis property closes Friday. After that we need the orchard signatures moving. That town loves nothing more than being told what it can't have, so don't tell them. Charm them. Buy them dinner. Be the reasonable Marsh for once."

*The reasonable Marsh.* As if reason were a family trait and not a costume Julian put on each morning like a coat he couldn't take off in public.

"Dad, the orchard—"

"Is eleven acres of dead trees and sentiment. I've seen the appraisals. Don't make me regret sending you instead of doing it myself."

He hung up, because Victor Marsh never ended conversations; he simply finished with them. Julian stood a moment longer, watching the train pull away toward the city, carrying with it every version of himself that wasn't useful to his father. Then he picked up his bag and walked into the town he'd been sent to dismantle.

* * *

Sandpiper Point in October smelled of woodsmoke and low tide. Main Street ran four blocks from the station to the water, and Julian walked all four slowly, cataloguing what had died and what hadn't. The hardware store lived. The diner lived — the Marigold, its neon coffee cup flickering like a heartbeat with arrhythmia. The bait shop had become a candle shop, which felt like a metaphor someone else should write.

And at the corner of Harbor and Second, wedged between a shuttered pharmacy and a real estate office with his father's name in the window, stood Crane & Daughter, Books.

He knew the name before he read it. He'd spent three weeks memorizing the file on the flight west: *Kestrel Crane, 34. Widow. One child. Lease expires March. Property under option, Parcel 14-B.* His father's researchers reduced human beings to parcels so efficiently that Julian sometimes wondered if they slept well, and then remembered that people who did this work didn't wonder anything at all.

The bookshop's windows were lit warm against the gray afternoon. Inside, he could see shelves crowded past any sane capacity, a ladder on brass rails, and a woman with dark hair pulled back reading aloud to a small girl curled in the window seat. He couldn't hear the words through the glass. He only saw the girl laugh, and the woman pause as if the laugh were something she wanted to keep.

Julian looked away first. It felt like losing something.

He found his rental cottage at the north end of town — his father had booked it for a month, "long enough to get the signatures," a sentence that contained an entire philosophy of other people's lives. The cottage smelled of cedar and disuse. On the kitchen table, next to a welcome basket of apples he suspected were ornamental, sat a cream-colored envelope.

*TOWN OF SANDPIPER POINT COMMUNITY FUNDRAISER,* said the printed label. *You've been matched!*

He almost threw it out. Then he thought of his father's voice — *charm them* — and opened it, because even a pen-pal assignment could be reconnaissance. Know the town one letter at a time. That was the kind of arithmetic Victor would approve of.

Inside: a single sheet, typed by someone who clearly believed in exclamation points.

*Dear Neighbor! Your pen pal has requested correspondence "on any subject except weather." Please write to Box 47, care of the Sandpiper Point Library. Sign only your assigned number. Your number is: 12.*

Assigned number twelve. He was a number now. It occurred to him, standing in a borrowed kitchen in a town he'd been sent to hollow out, that this was the most honest identity anyone had given him in years.

* * *

He wrote the first letter that night, at the kitchen table, with the apples going soft beside him and the harbor fog pressing at the windows like something trying to get in.

Three drafts. He kept all of them, which was itself a confession.

The first draft was charming. *What a pleasure to be part of this project* — his father's voice wearing his handwriting. He burned it in the sink without ceremony.

The second draft was honest, which is to say it was a weapon pointed inward, and he wasn't ready to hand a stranger a knife just because they'd asked nicely.

The third draft went like this:

---

*Dear Number Seven,*

*I'm told you asked for any subject except weather, which suggests you've already had too much weather, and I respect that. I'll offer you a subject instead: names.*

*I've been thinking about what it costs to carry one. Mine fits me badly. It's a good name, technically — heavy, expensive, engraved on buildings I've never entered. People react to it before they react to me, which means I've spent my whole life arriving places second, behind my own reputation. You learn to stand slightly to the left of yourself. You get very good at doors.*

*I won't tell you my name yet. Not out of caution — out of shame, maybe, though I haven't decided what the shame attaches to. The name, or what I've done under it.*

*A question, since letters require them: what's the bravest thing you've ever done that no one noticed?*

*I ask because I suspect the answer matters more than anything anyone's ever applauded you for. My own answer is embarrassing and I'll trade it for yours.*

*Yours in anonymity,*
*Number Twelve*

---

He read it over twice. It was guarded and unguarded at once, which was more than he'd managed in person for a decade. He sealed it, addressed it to Box 47, and set it by the door, where it lay in the morning light like something small and alive he'd decided not to step on.

* * *

Monday he began the work.

Victor had arranged a suite at the Sandpiper Inn — the good room, the one with the view of the orchard, because Victor believed in making men look at what they were about to lose. Julian ate breakfast there alone while a folder waited beside the orange juice like a guest who'd arrived early.

*Parcel 14-B. Eleven acres. Former commercial orchard, non-operational since 2009. Current holders: estate of Thomas Crane (deceased), Kestrel Crane (surviving spouse), minor child. Appraised value: $180,000. Offer pending: $210,000 — "generous."*

He read the appraisal twice, looking for the thing his father's people always buried somewhere, the little kindness of a number massaged until it lied. He didn't find it. Not yet. But he'd grown up learning to read his father's documents the way sailors read weather, and the air pressure in this one felt wrong.

At ten he walked Main Street with a smile calibrated to be disarming but not slick, and shook hands. The hardware store owner, a woman named Dot with forearms like dock rope, took his hand and held it a half-second too long, the way you hold a fish you're deciding whether to throw back.

"Marsh," she said. "Like the developer Marsh."

"The same family."

"Mm." She released him. "We had Marshes here once. Good ones. They're all dead or gone, I suppose."

"My grandfather grew up two streets over."

"Then you know how this ends," she said, and sold him a flashlight he didn't need, because refusing to sell to him would have been a courtesy she couldn't afford.

At noon he ate at the Marigold and listened. This was the actual job, the part Victor never understood: towns don't surrender to charm, they surrender to exhaustion, and Julian's task was to measure how much was left. He heard about the school roof. The ferry schedule. The doctor who drove in twice a week from Ashford. A town running on fumes and pride, both nearly empty.

And underneath it all, threaded through every conversation like wire through cable: *the orchard.* The council meeting Thursday. The buyout. Voices dropped when they mentioned it, the way voices drop near a grave.

"Crane woman's fighting it," the waitress said, refilling his coffee unasked. "Bookshop lady. Her husband planted half those trees, rest his soul. She says selling the orchard is selling the town's spine." She looked at him with the frank assessment of someone who fed strangers for a living. "You're not here for the buyout, are you?"

"I'm here on business," Julian said, and discovered that the lie had gotten heavier since Monday. It gained weight daily, like wet sand.

* * *

Thursday night he attended the council meeting, sitting in the back row as instructed, saying nothing, watching his father's lawyer present the offer with slides. And he watched Kestrel Crane stand up in a room full of tired people and refuse to be tired.

He'd study her later, in memory, the way you study a page you'll be tested on. But that night he only registered fragments: her voice steady in a room that wanted her to sit down. The word *spine*, used without apology. The way the whole room angled toward her like plants toward light.

She lost the vote, 4–2. Procedure, not verdict. The sale wasn't final; the signatures weren't signed. Walking out into the cold after, he passed her on the steps — close enough to see she'd been crying and had stopped, the way you stop a clock when the time doesn't matter anymore.

Their eyes met for one second. She looked at him the way Dot had shaken his hand: measuring, weighing, deciding. Then she walked down the steps and away, and Julian stood in the dark feeling identified, accused, and — though he'd never have used the word — *seen.*

That night he checked the mailbox at the library, because Box 47 was real and the town's little machine had actually worked. An envelope waited, thin, addressed in handwriting that slanted forward like someone walking into wind.

Number Seven.

He didn't open it on the street. He carried it home like contraband, sat down at the kitchen table with the fog coming in off the water, and read:

---

*Dear Number Twelve,*

*You asked what brave thing I've done that nobody noticed. Here's mine: I stayed. Everyone in my life expected me to leave — after the funeral, after the lease got hard, after the winter that took the heat out of half this street. Leaving would have been the smart thing and everyone would have understood it and no one would have blamed me. Staying was stupid and difficult and invisible, and I did it anyway, every single day, and no one gave me a medal because staying doesn't photograph well.*

*Your turn. You promised a trade.*

*Also: I know what it's like to arrive places second, behind your own reputation. Mine is smaller than yours sounds — "the widow who runs the bookshop" — but it fits the same way yours does. Badly. Like a coat cut for someone else's shoulders.*

*Tell me about the doors. You mentioned them like a man who's been locked out of something.*

*Number Seven*

---

Julian read it three times. Then he sat very still, listening to the foghorn out past the point, counting the intervals between blasts the way he had as a boy, when summers here meant his grandfather's boat and a version of the Marsh name that still fit.

*Tell me about the doors.*

He got out fresh paper. Outside, the fog swallowed the harbor whole, and inside, a man whose name was worth millions wrote, carefully, in a hand no one would ever trace back to him:

*Dear Number Seven — you asked about the doors. All right. Here is one...*

And below the foghorn, in a town scheduled for demolition, the war over his own name quietly began.
