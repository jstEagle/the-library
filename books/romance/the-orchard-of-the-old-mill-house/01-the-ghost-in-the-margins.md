## 1. The Ghost in the Margins

At three o'clock in the morning, the city outside Ulyana Ashgrove's window was the color of a bruise healing—deep violet at the edges, a thin gold seam where the harbor lights bled into the sky. Her apartment smelled of cold coffee and the lemon oil she rubbed into her desk when she couldn't sleep, which was most nights, and her laptop screen held the last paragraph of a life that did not belong to her.

*And that,* the paragraph ended, *is how I learned that a woman can survive almost anything if she is willing to be underestimated first.*

Ulyana read it four times. It was good. It was, she thought with a bitterness that had long since mellowed into something like companionship, the best sentence anyone would say all year.

She deleted it.

Not because it was wrong. Because Marguerite Voss would never say *almost anything*. Marguerite said *anything at all*, with a flourish, with certainty, with the breezy invincibility that had made her memoirs sell by the half-million and made dinner parties fall silent when she lifted her glass. Marguerite did not hedge. Marguerite did not know what it cost to be underestimated, because Marguerite had been adored since birth, or at least since her first novel won the Larchmont Prize at twenty-six.

Ulyana knew exactly what it cost. She had an itemized list.

She retyped the line: *And that is how I learned that a woman can survive anything at all—if she is willing to be underestimated first.*

Better. Truer to the voice. The voice that was hers and not hers, the way a hand-me-down coat is yours after enough winters. She saved the file as MV_Memoir_FINAL_v14.docx, because there had been thirteen other finals, and because the number fourteen felt like a private joke only she would ever get.

The cursor blinked. Outside, a taxi hissed through wet streets somewhere below, carrying someone home from someone else's arms.

"Congratulations," Ulyana told the empty room. "You've done it again."

Her cat, a gray ruin of a creature named Dostoevsky for reasons she could no longer defend, opened one eye from his post on the radiator, assessed her tone, and went back to sleep. Even the cat had stopped pretending to listen.

***

The email from her editor arrived at 9:47 the next morning, which meant Diana had read through the night too, which meant—Ulyana allowed herself one full second of hope—that it was good.

*Ulyana,* Diana wrote. *I don't know what happened in v13 to v14 but SOMETHING did. The orchard chapter. The grandmother. "A woman can survive anything at all"—I got chills. This is the most honest thing Marguerite has ever written. Whatever you're doing, keep doing it.*

Ulyana set down her phone.

The most honest thing Marguerite had ever written. Of course it was. It was about the summer Ulyana turned nine, when her grandmother had hoisted her onto the cider press at the Old Mill House and said, *Pressing apples is like grief, little one—you get out only what you're willing to put your whole weight on.* Ulyana had written that scene eleven years ago for a novel no one wanted, then cannibalized it for a magazine piece no one read, and now here it was, reborn in the mouth of Marguerite Voss, where it would finally be loved by strangers.

There was a version of this that was theft. There was also a version that was simply what happened to women who were good with their hands: they built things, and other people hung their names on them.

Diana's second email came forty minutes later, subject line: *Lunch?*

They met at the café on Perry Street where the tables were too small and the coffee was served in cups the size of thimbles, and Diana arrived glowing the way she only glowed over manuscripts or gossip, and today it appeared to be both.

"I cannot stop thinking about the grandmother," Diana said, shrugging off her coat. "Edith? Edith Ashgrove?"

"Mm." Ulyana stirred her thimble of coffee though it needed nothing.

"The way she says so little and means so much. And the mill house itself—it's practically a character. Is any of that real? Marguerite swears up and down it's all invented."

"It's real," Ulyana said, before she could stop herself. Then, smoothly, because she was very good at this: "Marguerite borrows. You know how she works."

"How she *works*," Diana repeated, laughing. "God, I'd love to meet whoever taught her to write like that. Some ghost out there deserves a medal. Or a lawsuit." She said it lightly, a coin tossed in the air, and Ulyana watched it spin and come down heads, tails, heads, forever.

"Some ghost," Ulyana agreed, and drank her coffee, and smiled with the particular smile of people who have gotten excellent at being nobody.

***

That evening, the letter was waiting in her mailbox. Not an email—a letter, on paper the weight of good linen, addressed in handwriting that slanted forward like a woman leaning into wind.

*Ulyana,*

*One hundred years ago my mother planted the first trees at the Old Mill House, and this October the orchard turns a century old, which is more than I can say for several members of this family's judgment.*

*We will gather the last weekend of September. All of us. Yes, even your brother, and yes, even his wife, whose name I am choosing not to write down as an act of mercy.*

*You will come Friday and stay through Sunday. Bring the man you are seeing. Your mother tells me there isn't one, which she considers a tragedy and I consider a mystery, and I intend to solve it.*

*The central tree is dying. Come see it before it goes.*

*Grandmother Edith*

Ulyana stood in the hallway of her building, holding the letter, while Mrs. Okafor from 4B maneuvered past her with a bag of groceries and a look of concern.

"You all right, honey?"

"My grandmother wants me to bring a boyfriend to a family reunion," Ulyana said.

Mrs. Okafor nodded sagely. "Don't we all."

The trouble was not the reunion. Ulyana could survive a reunion; she had survived thirty-four of them, developing over the years the precise skills required—laughing at her brother Daniel's jokes before he finished them, complimenting her sister-in-law Petra's centerpieces with the sincerity of a hostage video, answering *and what is it you do again, Ulyana?* with *freelance writing* and letting them fill in the rest with whatever smallness suited them.

The trouble was the sentence: *Bring the man you are seeing.*

Because there wasn't one. There hadn't been one in six years, not since Marcus, who had explained to her over a dinner she'd paid for that loving her felt, to him, like "an investment with unclear returns," and who had then married someone else within the year and sent a wedding invitation anyway, which Ulyana still kept in a drawer as evidence, like a pressed flower of a bad idea.

Her grandmother did not know about Marcus. Her grandmother knew everything else. That was the problem with Edith Ashgrove—she read people the way Ulyana read manuscripts, finding the true story underneath the presentable one, and Ulyana had spent her whole adult life constructing a presentable one just sturdy enough to pass inspection from across a dinner table. Up close, her grandmother would find the rot. Up close, she would ask questions. Up close, *there isn't anyone* would become a conversation, and conversations with Edith had a way of ending with Ulyana admitting things she'd sworn to take to her grave.

So the options were these: arrive alone and endure the interrogation, invent a boyfriend out of thin air and pray no one asked follow-up questions (they would; Petra alone would conduct a full background check between the salad course and dessert), or—

Or.

Ulyana climbed her stairs slowly, turning the third option over like a stone found on a beach.

She knew a man. Well—she knew *of* a man. Theo Lindqvist, the arborist who had been restoring the community garden behind her building since spring, a tall, quiet Swede—or possibly Minnesotan; his accent was the kind that could be either—who spoke to trees in a low, patient voice and to people almost never. He had once spent forty minutes explaining to her why the courtyard maple was dying—not the dramatic reasons, the boring ones, the soil compaction, the girdling root—and Ulyana had listened the whole time because it was the first conversation in months where nobody wanted anything from her except, apparently, to save a tree.

He was, as far as she could tell, single. He was, as far as she could tell, uninterested in her, which was precisely the qualification she required. A man who wanted nothing from her could be paid to want nothing convincingly. It was clean. It was transactional. It was, she told herself, the same skill set she used every day: give people the story they need, collect the fee, walk away before anyone gets attached to the words.

It was a terrible idea. She could already hear her grandmother's voice: *A lie in a family is a seed, Ulyana. It grows whether you water it or not.*

But the alternative was standing in the orchard alone, under the dying central tree, being looked at by Edith Ashgrove until the truth fell out of her like ripe fruit.

Ulyana set the letter on her desk, next to the laptop where Marguerite's finished memoir glowed, fourteen drafts deep, wearing another woman's name.

Then she took out a legal pad—the good kind, the kind she used for plotting—and wrote at the top, in her smallest, most dangerous handwriting:

*Terms of engagement.*

Below it: *One weekend. One story. No questions.*

She underlined it twice, and somewhere in the building a pipe knocked, and Dostoevsky woke to investigate, and Ulyana Ashgrove sat down to plan the second-greatest fiction of her career—the first being, though she did not yet know it, her own life.
