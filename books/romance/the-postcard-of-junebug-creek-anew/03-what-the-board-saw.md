## 3. What the Board Saw

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which Marisol would later decide was the universe's idea of a joke. Nothing good had ever come to Junebug Creek on a Tuesday — not the flood of '09, not the rezoning vote, and certainly not the manila packet that sat on her desk like a coiled snake wearing a bow tie.

She knew before she opened it. She knew by the way Gerald Pruitt stood in her doorway with his hands folded over his vest, wearing the expression of a man about to deliver terrible news he had personally arranged.

"Ms. Vega," he said. "You'll want to sit down."

"I've been standing since six a.m.," Marisol said. "I'm directing a festival, Gerald. Sitting is a rumor I've heard about other people's jobs."

"Then you'll want to read standing." He set the packet down. "It's been distributed to every board member. And to the *Gazette*."

The Gazette. Her stomach performed a maneuver she hadn't attempted since high school gymnastics.

Inside were photocopies. Letters. Her letters — Number 14, written in her own slanted hand on festival letterhead she'd grabbed absentmindedly from the supply closet, because apparently even her subterfuge couldn't resist a little institutional branding. And across from them, Number 7. Edmund's handwriting, all sharp descenders and impatient loops, like a man perpetually running slightly ahead of his own sentences.

She read her own words back to herself in the fluorescent quiet of her office:

*I was twenty-six years old and they told me the festival would die without my testimony. They told me a lot of things at twenty-six. I believed all of them.*

And beneath it, his:

*Everyone assumes the collapse was drink, or drugs, or some scandalous rot. It wasn't. It was panic. My hands simply stopped believing in me, and I have never forgiven them for it.*

"Oh," Marisol said softly. "Oh no."

"The board," Gerald said, "has questions. About your judgment. About whether an ongoing... correspondence of this nature with Mr. Quintana — conducted under the anonymity the program promised, which you then, it appears, exploited—" He paused, savoring it. "—represents a conflict of interest. There will be a vote Friday. Before the closing concert."

"Gerald." She looked up. "Did you intercept these?"

"I received them," he said, with the serene precision of a man who had learned long ago that technically true statements were the load-bearing walls of a respectable life. "From an anonymous source."

"An anonymous source who had access to the pen-pal coordinator's filing cabinet."

"What a suspicious mind you have, Ms. Vega. It's exactly this sort of paranoia that makes the board wonder about your fitness."

He left. Marisol sat down after all, because her knees had filed their own motion of no confidence.

* * *

By noon, the whole town knew. Of course it did. Junebug Creek processed information the way a corn maze processes lost tourists: slowly, thoroughly, and with everyone eventually ending up somewhere they hadn't planned.

Dolores at the bakery had already renamed the cinnamon rolls "Pen-Pal Pastries." Two teenagers asked Marisol, with the merciless frankness of the young, whether Number 7 was "the sad cello guy." And when she walked past the rehearsal barn, conversation died so completely she could hear the cicadas recalibrating.

Edmund was inside, tuning. Or pretending to tune. The same four notes, over and over, the way a person pokes at a bruise.

Marisol pushed through the door. "We need to talk."

"Do we?" He didn't look up. "I'd have thought we'd covered everything. In writing."

"You saw the paper."

"I saw the paper." He set the bow down with exaggerated care, the way he did everything when he was furious — as if rage were a fragile instrument requiring white gloves. "Number 14. Very poetic. 'They told me a lot of things at twenty-six.' You know what they told me at thirty-four? That the woman testifying against me had tears in her eyes while she did it. Turns out those were crocodile tears with excellent penmanship."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "What's not fair is that I told a stranger things I've never told my own sister. Things I haven't told my *therapist*, and I pay her by the hour. And now the whole town knows my hands quit on me in front of two thousand people, because the woman who ended my career wanted someone to confess to." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd ever heard from him. "Was that the plan? Get me comfortable, get me talking? A little restorative justice, Vega-style?"

"If you believe that," Marisol said, "then you didn't read a single word I wrote."

"I read every word." His voice cracked on it, just barely. "That's the problem."

She left before he could see what his words did to her face. Professional habit. Three years of practice.

* * *

Thursday night, she found him packing.

Not tidying — packing. The cello case open on the bed of his rented room above the hardware store, clothes folded with the grim efficiency of a man rehearsing an exit. A bus schedule lay on the nightstand. The 6:40 to Asheville.

"You're leaving." She stood in the doorway. "Before the concert. Before the vote."

"There's no vote that concerns me anymore. They're not voting on me. They're voting on *you*." He zipped the garment bag. "Which is somehow worse."

"So run. Again."

He flinched. Good, she thought, and immediately hated herself for thinking it.

"You don't understand what it's like," he said quietly. "Standing in front of people waiting for you to fail again. I did it once. I won't—"

"You did it once and everyone let you believe it was the end of your story." Marisol stepped into the room. "Edmund. Listen to me. Gerald Pruitt is going to stand up Friday night and tell the board that my judgment is compromised, that I ran a healing program and used it as cover for — what does the memo say — 'an inappropriate personal entanglement.' And every person in that room is going to nod, because nodding is free. And if you're on a bus to Asheville, there will be no one in that room who knows the truth. Not the board's truth. *The* truth. That I testified because I was twenty-six and scared and told the festival would die otherwise. That you collapsed because your body betrayed you, not your character. That two people wrote letters they meant."

"And what am I supposed to do? I don't have a contract here. I don't have a stage. I don't have permission from anyone to say anything."

"No," Marisol said. "You don't. Welcome to justice outside the law. It's terribly furnished and nobody claps."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he must have looked at her letters, hunting for the lie and finding only the tired, stubborn woman underneath. "Why do you care if I stay? After what I said yesterday."

"Because three years ago, people who knew the truth stayed silent when it mattered. I was one of them. I know exactly what silence costs, Edmund. I've been paying it off in installments ever since." She picked up the bus schedule, considered it, and tore it neatly in half. "Stay or go. But if you go, go knowing that staying silent would make you the same as everyone who watched you fall and said nothing."

She put the halves on his nightstand and walked out before her hands could shake where he could see them.

* * *

Friday morning, Edmund Quintana came to her office carrying his cello case and an expression she couldn't classify — until she realized she'd seen it before, in his letters, in the line about his hands: a man deciding whether to trust himself again.

"I need a favor," he said.

"We're both about to be unemployed. Name it."

"The closing concert. Your program has a gap after intermission. Fifteen minutes, unallocated, because Dolores insisted the pie auction needed breathing room."

Marisol stared at him. "Edmund. What are you planning?"

"Something I should have done three years ago." He lifted the case. "I don't have a stage. So I'm asking you for yours. And I can't promise my hands will cooperate. I can't promise anything except that I'll be telling the truth, out loud, in front of everyone — including Gerald Pruitt and his vest."

"It could end your career for good," she said. "Whatever's left of it."

"It ended three years ago, Marisol. Everything since has been the encore." He almost smiled. "Besides — you once wrote me that courage isn't the absence of fear, it's fear with its shoes on, showing up anyway. Number 14, page two. I checked."

Outside, the church bell rang ten o'clock. Down the street, the Gazette's press was already rolling with a special edition — GERALD PRUITT's face on the front page beside the headline the whole town would be reading by lunch:

**FESTIVAL DIRECTOR'S SECRET LETTERS SPARK BOARD VOTE TONIGHT.**

Marisol looked at the man standing in her office holding a cello and half a torn bus schedule taped carefully back together, and understood that whatever happened tonight, nothing would survive it intact — and something might survive it whole.

"Fifteen minutes," she said. "Don't be late."
