## 1. Debts Instead of Grapes

The foreclosure notice was the only thing on the property that hadn't frozen over.

I found it at seven in the morning, thumbtacked to the cellar house door with a precision that suggested the bank's courier had better aim than the bank's compassion. *NOTICE OF INTENT TO FORECLOSE*, it said, in a font designed by someone who believed urgency should look like a funeral. The paper flapped in a wind that came down off the ridge smelling of iron and old snow, and I stood there reading it twice, as though the second pass might reveal a typo. It didn't. The bank was very good at arithmetic.

"Good morning to you too," I told it, and went inside to put the kettle on, because grief is easier to carry with both hands full.

Here is what I inherited from my uncle Walter Underhill, rest his stubborn soul: forty acres of vineyard on the north slope of Maple Hollow, a cellar house built in 1911 and repaired approximately never since, a tractor that had not started since the Clinton administration, and — this was the crown jewel, the heirloom, the thing he'd wrapped in a bow and left me in a will that read like an apology with a signature — two hundred and thirty thousand dollars of debt secured against all of it.

People say you can't inherit debt. People are wrong. You can inherit anything if the paperwork is creative enough, and Walter's lawyer was an artist.

* * *

I walked the vines after coffee, because that's what you do, because ritual is just a way of telling time when you don't want to know what time it is. The rows ran up the slope in long gray lines, bare canes lashed to wires, and under the frost they looked less like a vineyard than like the skeleton of one — something that had been alive once and was now mostly an argument about whether it could be again.

January is honest in wine country. There's no canopy to hide behind, no fruit to flatter you. A vineyard in January tells you exactly what it is, and Maple Hollow was telling me: tired. Half these vines were older than my father. Cabernet franc on the high rows, seyval blanc down where the cold pooled like water in a sink, and everywhere the telltale gaps where winterkill or neglect or simple exhaustion had taken a vine and nobody had replanted. You could read the history of the last fifteen years in those gaps. Every missing vine was a year Walter couldn't afford help, or a year he could afford it but not the argument with himself about whether it was worth it.

He'd stopped believing around 2016, I think. That was the year he sold off the south block to the co-op farmers to cover a loan payment, and after that the vineyard became less a business than a habit. He kept pruning because pruning was what Underhills did in February, the same way he kept going to church because church was what Underhills did on Sundays — not out of faith, exactly, but out of a reluctance to break a streak.

And then last March his heart did what hearts do when they've been asked to keep a streak going for eighty-one years, and I got a phone call, and a will, and a foreclosure notice with a three-month fuse that has now burned down to about six weeks.

Second chances, I have learned, always arrive too late for people like us. They arrive the way the bus arrives in Maple Hollow — technically scheduled, practically mythical, and when it finally does come, it's already full or going somewhere else.

* * *

Let me back up, because if I'm going to narrate my own ruin I should at least do it in order.

Ten years ago I was somebody. Not famous-somebody, but the promising kind of somebody — the young enologist with the fellowship, the clean lab coat, the future people described in the conditional tense. I had an internship at Calloway & Voss in the Willamette Valley, a mentor who said I had "an unusual palate," which at twenty-four is the nicest thing anyone can say to you, and two friends from college who were going to be in my wedding someday, whichever of them lost the coin toss.

One of them was Delia Marsh, who made wine the way some people pray — seriously, constantly, with a private intensity she pretended wasn't there. The other was Theo Vance, who made friends the way other people make money: easily, in volume, and always with someone else's labor doing the actual work.

Then, in the autumn of my twenty-fifth year, there was a scandal. Falsified lab reports, they said. Doctored fermentation records, they said. An intern — me — cooking the numbers on a flagship reserve to hide a stuck batch, because ambition, they said, makes people do terrible things. There was a hearing. There was testimony. There was Theo's testimony, delivered with that wounded, reluctant honesty he could turn on like a tap, saying he'd seen the altered spreadsheets, saying he'd tried to give me the benefit of the doubt, saying — and this was the knife, this was the part I still heard at 3 a.m. — *"I don't think Edmund's a bad person. I think he's a person who got in over his head."*

Sympathetic. Reasonable. Devastating. He testified me out of a career in four minutes flat and cried a little doing it, and everyone agreed afterward how brave he'd been, how hard it must have been, turning in a friend.

I never touched another commercial ferment. I drifted — cellar hand, harvest crew, a season in New Zealand picking fruit for people who didn't ask questions, a stretch managing a tasting room in a town so small the wine list was a formality. I got very good at being around wine without being trusted with it. It's a skill. Like juggling. Nobody needs it, but it keeps your hands busy.

Delia, for her part, simply vanished from my life, which I took — reasonably, I thought — as a verdict. She'd been there that autumn. She'd known the evidence. And she'd said nothing, or nothing enough, and then she was gone, and I decided that silence was its own kind of testimony.

So when I say I inherited a vineyard, understand that what I actually inherited was a chance to fail at the only thing I ever wanted, in front of the only town that remembered why I'd stopped wanting it.

* * *

By noon the light had gone flat and white, the kind of sky that means the weather is thinking about something. I stood at the top of the north slope with my hands in my pockets and did the math again, because I do it every morning, the way other men check their phones.

Two hundred thirty thousand, principal and accrued. Six weeks to the foreclosure date. The vineyard, assessed honestly — which the bank would not do — might bring ninety. Maybe a hundred ten if the buyer romanticized the view. The gap between those numbers was the rest of my life, expressed in digits.

There were options. There are always options; that's what the brochures say. I could sell the equipment piecemeal. I could subdivide — except the land wouldn't perk for building and everyone knew it. I could write to the bank and request forbearance, which is a word banks use to mean *we will consider allowing you to continue suffering on a schedule*. I had written that letter twice already. Both times a person with a pleasant voice had explained that my file was "under review," and I had understood, in the way you understand things without wanting to, that my file was being reviewed by a system that had already decided and was merely waiting for the calendar to catch up.

Or — and here was the option I turned over every night like a stone looking for the warm side — I could walk away. Sign it over, take whatever scrap the bankruptcy left me, and go be nobody somewhere warmer. There was a winery in the Finger Lakes that hired seasonal crews and didn't Google their pickers. I could prune other people's vines until my hands gave out and never again stand on ground that was supposed to be mine.

Some mornings that option felt like mercy. Most mornings it felt like the second betrayal — the one I'd be doing to myself, with my own signature, which at least would be honest handwriting for once in this story.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't end. It just changes address. Ten years ago it moved into my résumé, and now it lived in the way the woman at the feed store paused half a beat before saying my name, in the way old Mr. Aldous at the co-op still called me "Walter's nephew," as though the family name were a loan I hadn't finished repaying. Maple Hollow remembered everything. Small towns are elephants with post offices.

And yet.

I crouched at the end of row twelve and brushed the frost off a cane with my thumb. Under the gray bark, if you scored it with a thumbnail, there was green. Of course there was green. These vines had survived worse than neglect — they'd survived the '04 freeze that took out half the county, they'd survived Walter's budget, they'd survived me, arguably, by continuing to exist without any particular help. Dormant isn't dead. Any first-year viticulture student knows that. Prune them right, and even a vineyard this far gone will push new growth by May, because that is the entire obscene optimism of a grapevine: it assumes, every single spring, that this year will be different.

I wished, not for the first time, that I could borrow the trick.

* * *

In the afternoon I went through the cellar house, because the assessor was coming — the bank was sending someone to value the property, they'd said, a consultant, arriving Thursday — and I wanted to know what they'd see before they saw it.

What they'd see: a long stone building sunk into the hillside, half earth-sheltered, the way cellars used to be built when people expected buildings to outlast them. Oak barrels stacked two high along the east wall, most of them empty, a few holding vinegar that had given up pretending. A press older than my uncle. A crushing pad cracked into a mosaic by thirty winters. Everything swept, because I'd swept it — I couldn't help myself; the place deserved tidiness even in defeat — but swept the way you tidy a room where someone died.

My uncle's office was at the far end, a closet with a desk and a window fogged with age. I sat in his chair, which still held the shape of him the way a glove holds a hand, and looked at the wall where he'd pinned forty years of harvest notes. *Sept 19th — picked block 2, brix 21.4, good day.* His whole life, reduced to dates and sugar readings, and somehow that was more moving than any eulogy. A man is his vintages. Walter had made thirty-eight of them, and every one had been honest, and the last dozen had been losing money, and he'd made them anyway, because the alternative was to stop being a winemaker and he didn't know how to be anything else.

I knew the feeling. I was living in it.

On the desk, under a stone paperweight shaped like nothing in particular, I found the letter he'd never sent. I recognized his blocky capitals. It was addressed to the bank, dated last November, and it said, in its entirety:

*I am asking for one more season. I am eighty-one years old and I am asking for one more season.*

They had denied it in nine days. I knew because the denial was in the same drawer, folded once, neat as a surgical scar.

I put both letters back where I'd found them and sat there while the light went blue in the window, and I want to tell you I felt angry, because anger is useful, anger prunes things. But what I felt was closer to recognition. Walter had spent his last year writing polite letters to a machine. I was going to spend my next six weeks doing the same. Inheritance, it turns out, is mostly repetition.

* * *

Dusk came early, the way it does in January, sliding down the ridge like something poured. I lit the wood stove in the cellar house kitchen — the farmhouse itself had a roof leak I couldn't afford to fix, so I'd been living in the cellar house, which sounds picturesque and is actually just cold with better acoustics — and I ate eggs and toast standing up, and I reviewed the day.

Assets: forty acres, mostly dormant, some dead. Equipment, antique. Building, historic, which is a real-estate word for *drafty*. A reputation, damaged, non-transferable. A foreclosure date, fixed. And a green line under the bark of every living vine, which the bank's appraiser would not be able to see, because you need a thumbnail, and patience, and a reason to look.

Liabilities: everything else, including, increasingly, hope.

I washed my plate. I banked the stove. And I stood at the window watching snow start — fat, unhurried flakes, the kind that mean the storm is still deciding — and I let myself have the thought I'd been refusing all week, the one that sits under all the others like groundwater:

That I didn't actually want to save the vineyard. What I wanted was to be the kind of man who could. The difference matters. One is a business plan; the other is a wound. And wounds, unlike vineyards, respond to attention.

Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and quit. The snow thickened. Tomorrow the consultant would arrive on the afternoon bus, some bank hire with a clipboard and a formula, and would walk these rows and write down a number, and the number would be smaller than the debt, and that would be that. I'd shake hands, sign papers, and Maple Hollow would pass out of Underhill hands after a hundred and nine years, and the town would nod sympathetically and say *shame about Walter's place,* and in five years someone with money would plant pinot noir on the north slope and get a feature in a magazine I used to read.

Unless.

I didn't finish the thought. Finishing thoughts like that is how people end up disappointed, and I had a strict personal policy about disappointment, which was to schedule it rather than improvise.

But I'll admit this much: when I finally lay down on the cot in Walter's old office, listening to the wind work the chimney, I slept better than I had in weeks. Maybe it was the snow, muffling the world down to something bearable. Maybe it was the green under the bark, still in me somewhere, doing its slow dumb optimistic work.

The bus from the city comes Thursdays, one departure, arriving at 3:40 if the driver feels generous.

I checked the clock. It was Wednesday night.

Thursday, then. Whoever they sent — some stranger with the bank's arithmetic in their briefcase — would find me in the rows, probably, or sweeping the crush pad, performing competence for an audience of one. I'd be polite. I'd be cooperative. I'd show them the property and keep my hands steady and my mouth shut about green lines and second seasons, because that's not information a foreclosure consultant can use.

That was the plan, anyway. I had it all arranged, the way you arrange a funeral: dignified, efficient, final.

I just didn't yet know whose.
