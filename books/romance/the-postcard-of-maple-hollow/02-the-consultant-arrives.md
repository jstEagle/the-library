## 2. The Consultant Arrives

The bus to Maple Hollow runs twice a day, and both times it smells of diesel and old upholstery and the particular despair of small-town transit. I had eleven years since my last ride on it, and apparently the seats remembered me, because they creaked in exactly the same accusatory way.

I stepped down onto Main Street with a garment bag over one shoulder, a laptop case over the other, and a leather folio containing a foreclosure assessment that would decide whether a vineyard lived or died. The bank had sent me because I was thorough, un sentimental, and cheap by regional-director standards. What the bank did not know—what I had carefully not mentioned on my disclosure forms—was that I knew Maple Hollow the way you know a scar. Not because it hurt anymore, but because you can't look at your own hand without seeing it.

The town hadn't changed so much as settled, the way snow settles: everything a little lower, a little grayer, edges softened into something that looked gentle from a distance. Hutch's Hardware still had the same hand-painted sign, though HUTCH'S now read HUTCH & SON, which meant old man Hutch was either dead or finally retired, and I genuinely couldn't guess which. The diner was still called the Blue Plate even though the plates were white and always had been. Someone had strung lights across the street between the pharmacy and the feed store, out of season, sagging like they'd given up halfway through the job.

I stood on the curb and let the cold find me. October cold in the hollow has teeth. It comes off the ridge and goes straight through wool, straight through skin, straight to whatever you've been keeping warm and hidden.

"Delia Marsh." A voice behind me, delighted and unhurried. "As I live and breathe."

Ruth Calloway. Of course. Ruth had run the diner counter since before any of us were born, or possibly since before the town was born; there are people like that in towns like this, load-bearing people, and Ruth was one. She'd also been the first person in Maple Hollow to tell me, at nineteen, that I had a good palate and a bad attitude, and she'd never once revised either assessment.

"Ruth." I let her hug me, because refusing would take more energy than enduring. "You look exactly the same."

"Liar. I look better. You look tired." She held me at arm's length and studied me with the frank appraisal of a woman who has spent forty years reading customers. "Business?"

"Business."

"For the bank."

"That's the idea."

Her eyes narrowed a fraction—not suspicion exactly, more like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to. "Which property?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dreading since the file landed on my desk three weeks ago, except I hadn't known I was dreading it until this exact moment, standing on a curb in a town I'd sworn off, being asked it by a woman who missed nothing.

"The Underhill property," I said. "Maple Hollow Vineyard."

Something moved across Ruth's face. Not surprise. Something older and sadder than surprise.

"You didn't know," she said. It wasn't a question either.

"Didn't know what?"

She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture I recognized as her version of deciding how much truth a person could carry. "Edmund's uncle passed in the spring. Left him the place. Vines, cellar house, the whole mess." She paused. "And the debts. Mostly the debts."

My stomach performed a slow, professional drop, the kind I usually only felt when an appraisal came back worse than the loan officer's optimism. Edmund. Edmund Underhill owned the property I'd been hired to assess for foreclosure. Edmund Underhill, who I had not spoken to in eleven years, not since the night everything ended, not since I stood in a hearing room and defended him and lost, and he walked out of my life without looking back, and I told myself for a decade that I was fine with that, that I'd made my peace, that some doors close for good reasons.

Eleven years of peace. It had taken Ruth Calloway four seconds to demolish it.

"He's running it?" I asked, and hated how careful my voice came out, how neutral, how much it cost to sound like a consultant instead of a woman whose chest had just tightened around something sharp.

"Barely," Ruth said. "He's trying. That boy works like a mule and wins like a donkey, if you follow me. Everything he touches turns to work." She shook her head. "First frost came early this year. Took half the block before he could get nets up. He'll be at the property now, I expect. Always is."

"Right." I adjusted the folio under my arm. "Well. Professional courtesy. I'll introduce myself properly when I do the walkthrough."

"You'll do more than that." Ruth's voice went flat and certain, the voice she used when she refilled your coffee without asking. "You'll be fair, Delia Marsh. Whatever else happened back then—and I heard plenty, and believed maybe half of it—you'll be fair."

"I'm always fair. It's my entire personality. Ask anyone at Meridian Trust."

"They'd say that about a shark too." She patted my arm, which somehow stung worse than the slap it replaced. "Come get dinner tonight. You can tell me all the things you're allowed to tell me, and I'll pretend to believe them."

* * *

I should explain what happened back then, because Ruth said *whatever else happened* the way people say it when they know the shape of a story but not its contents, and I have spent eleven years being the only person who knows the contents, and keeping them has made me strange.

In college, the three of us were inseparable in the way that only happens once, if it happens at all. Edmund, Theo Vance, and me. Edmund was the serious one—all soil chemistry and patience, the kind of student who took notes in two colors and lent them out gladly. Theo was charm wearing a blazer; he could make a professor laugh his way past a deadline and make you feel chosen while he did it. And I was the one who loved Edmund and never said so, because we were twenty and I had a plan and the plan didn't include wanting anything that badly.

Then came the internship scandal. Falsified lab reports at the Harrow Creek co-op, where Edmund had the summer placement everyone wanted. The numbers were wrong in a way that cost three small growers their contracts. There was a hearing. Theo testified—he'd been at the co-op too, part-time, filing paperwork—and his testimony was clean, reasonable, devastating. He cried a little. Everyone believed him. Everyone believed the paper trail too, and the paper trail pointed at Edmund.

I spoke in his defense. I said what I knew—that Edmund checked his work three times, that he'd flagged discrepancies himself weeks earlier, that the timeline didn't hold. It didn't matter. He was expelled from the program. His career died at twenty-two, and mine shouldered on without him, and I carried the specific guilt of the survivor: I got to keep going.

He never blamed me. That was the worst part. At the end, in the parking lot, he said, "You tried, Delia. Nobody else did." And then he left, and I let him, because I was twenty-two and cowardly and certain that chasing him would only confirm what everyone already believed about us both.

I built a career on the rubble. Vineyard consulting, then banking-side assessment, then a reputation for being unsentimental, which is what people call you when they learn you've had your sentiment surgically removed after a bad outcome. Two years ago my own career nearly collapsed—a client dispute, an accusation of bias, ugly months of suspension—until the bank reinstated me and I told myself the lesson was to stop defending lost causes.

And now the bank had handed me a lost cause with Edmund Underhill's name on the deed.

* * *

I rented a car from the only agency within forty miles, which turned out to be a man named Denny operating out of the back of his auto shop, and drove the ridge road with the windows down because I needed the cold on my face like a person needs a splash of water.

The turnoff to Maple Hollow Vineyard is marked by a post with a faded sign, MAPLE HOLLOW ESTATE WINES, the ESTATE painted over sometime in the nineties. I almost missed it. I had driven this road a hundred times in another life—to harvest parties, to the night we pressed our first experimental batch in Edmund's uncle's cellar and Theo spilled most of it and blamed the cat, though there was no cat.

The drive wound down through bare maples. And then the vineyard opened up below me, and I pulled over, because you have to, because anyone would.

It was beautiful the way ruin is beautiful. Rows of vines marching down the slope toward the creek, most of them dormant and correct, but here and there a gap like a missing tooth where dead vines had been pulled. The cellar house sat at the bottom—stone foundation, cedar siding gone silver, the cupola leaning slightly eastward like a man listening hard. Smoke rose thin from its chimney. Someone was home. Someone was fighting for this place with a woodstove and whatever stubbornness he'd inherited along with the debt.

I sat in Denny's rental Corolla and did not move for a long time.

Here is what I refused to name, sitting there: that my heart was going like I was twenty again. That the dread in my stomach wasn't about the assessment at all. That some traitorous part of me had spent eleven years composing sentences I'd never send, and now every one of them was rising at once, and I was going to have to walk down that hill and say *Mr. Underhill, I'm from Meridian Trust* to a man who once taught me to taste tannin by holding my palm against a barrel stave.

Professional distance. That was the job. Assess the asset, document the liabilities, recommend disposition. The bank wanted a number. Numbers were safe. Numbers had never once looked at me across a college library table and made me forget what page I was on.

I got out. I walked down.

* * *

He was in the vineyard, of course, not the house. Ruth had said he would be. He was pruning—or attempting to—with a pair of shears that had seen better decades, working down a row of Cabernet Franc with the focused clumsiness of a man who learned viticulture from books and grief rather than seasons.

He hadn't seen me yet. Good. It bought me ten seconds to look at him honestly, which is a thing I hadn't permitted myself in years.

He was thinner. Gray had gotten into his brown hair early, unfairly well. He moved like someone carrying weight in places weight shouldn't go—shoulders, jaw. But his hands, when he reached into the canopy, were still sure. Some things survive everything. His hands had survived.

"Mr. Underhill," I said.

He turned. And I watched recognition arrive in stages—the polite blankness of a stranger, then the flicker of *do I know her*, then the full catastrophe landing, his face going through surprise and something rawer underneath before discipline clamped down over all of it.

"Delia." My name in his mouth after eleven years. It sounded the same. That felt obscene. "Delia Marsh."

"Meridian Trust sent me." I held up the folio like a shield, like credentials, like a reason. "Regional asset review. I'm the assigned assessor for the Underhill loan portfolio."

For a moment he just looked at me. The wind came down the slope and moved between us, rattling the last leaves on the trellis wires.

"Of course it's you," he said finally, and there was no anger in it, which was somehow worse. Just a tired, dark humor, the laugh you give the universe when it outdoes itself. "Eleven years, and the bank sends Delia Marsh to foreclose on me."

"Assess," I corrected. "Foreclosure is a recommendation, not a foregone conclusion."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"No. It's supposed to be accurate. Comfort isn't in my scope."

Something almost like a smile touched his mouth and died. He looked past me at the rows, at the ridge, anywhere but at me, and I understood—I understood completely—because I was doing the same thing.

"When do you start?" he asked.

"Tomorrow morning. Full walkthrough. Cellar house, equipment, inventory, books." I paused, and professionalism carried me the rest of the way like a raft. "I'll need access to the ledgers."

That landed harder than I expected it to. Something crossed his face—quick, unreadable, gone. "The ledgers," he repeated.

"Standard procedure."

"Sure." He set the shears down on the row post with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he wants very badly to throw them. "Standard procedure. Well. Welcome to Maple Hollow, Ms. Marsh. I'd offer you the grand tour, but—" he gestured at the frost-burned rows, the sagging trellis wire, the cupola leaning eastward, "—the grand tour is mostly a list of things that don't work."

"I've assessed worse."

"I don't doubt it." He picked up a canvas sack of cuttings and slung it over his shoulder. For a second, just a second, his eyes found mine and held, and eleven years fell away so fast I actually swayed. Then he said, quietly, "You look well, Delia," and walked up the row toward the house, leaving me standing among the frozen vines with a folio full of numbers and absolutely none of the answers I'd come for.

I watched him go. The smoke curled from the chimney. Somewhere down the valley, the light was already failing, early and gray, the way it does in October when winter means business.

Tomorrow I would be thorough. Tomorrow I would be unsentimental. Tomorrow I would count barrels and depreciate equipment and put a number on a man's whole life, and I would be excellent at it, because excellence was the only thing I'd ever managed to build out of that parking lot eleven years ago.

But tonight, standing in his vineyard with my heart misbehaving like a girl's, I admitted the thing I'd been refusing to name since the bus:

I had hoped, against every professional instinct I owned, that the file was wrong. That the property belonged to anyone—anyone at all—but Edmund Underhill.

And I had dreaded, in equal measure, that it might not.

The porch light came on. He paused at the door, silhouetted against it, and I could have sworn he looked back once before going inside.

I stood in the cold a while longer, telling myself it meant nothing.

I have never once believed myself, and I didn't start then.
