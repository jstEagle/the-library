## 16. Allies in the Hollow

The morning after the storm, Maple Hollow woke up shoveling.

I stood on the porch of the cellar house with a cup of coffee that had been reheated three times on the wood stove and watched Ruth Calloway's plow blade scrape up my drive like a slow orange dawn. Somewhere under that snow were the vines I was supposed to be losing, and somewhere in my coat pocket—wrapped in a freezer bag because I am not entirely without sense—were eleven years of ledgers that said the man taking them from me had built his career on arithmetic he'd invented himself.

Delia came out behind me, hair still damp, wearing my uncle's old barn coat because hers had frozen solid overnight. On anyone else it would have looked like a costume. On her it looked like a decision.

"The accountant," she said. "Walt Ferro. He did the co-op's books until '09. If Theo doctored shipment records, Walt would have kept copies. Men like him always keep copies."

"You've met him?"

"Once. At a trade conference. He corrected a slide I hadn't finished presenting yet." She blew into her hands. "He'll want to see the originals."

"He'll want to correct my handwriting too."

"Probably." And then, quieter, in the voice she used when she wasn't hiding behind her credentials: "Edmund. Once you show people this, it stops being ours. It becomes everyone's. There's no walking it back."

I looked out at the vineyard, white and silent and technically not mine for much longer. "Delia, I've spent ten years being the only one who knows what happened to me. It turns out that's just another word for alone. I'm done with it."

She nodded once, and something in her shoulders let go—or maybe locked in; with Delia it was hard to tell the difference between surrender and commitment.

* * *

Ruth Calloway runs the feed store and, unofficially, everything else within six miles of the crossroads. When I walked in at nine, she was arguing with a delivery driver about salt prices and didn't look up until I set the ledger on her counter.

"Edmund Underhill." She wiped her hands on her apron. "You're either here to buy fence posts or to say goodbye. Which is it?"

"Neither. I need to borrow your back room and about eight people who hate Theo Vance."

That got her attention. Ruth has a face built for poker but her eyes have never learned the game. She looked at the ledger, at me, back at the ledger. "Eight might be ambitious before noon. Give me till supper."

By supper there were fourteen.

They filled the back room of the feed store the way water fills a cellar—finding every crack, settling everywhere. The Kessler brothers from the co-op, both of them big men gone soft around the middle, sitting on upturned crates because there weren't enough chairs. Marta Okafor, who ran the nursery and had once, I remembered now, lost a contract to a regional distributor whose paperwork had mysteriously beaten everyone else's. Old Pete Lindqvist, who farmed forty acres of apples and hadn't spoken to me since my uncle's funeral, on the theory—apparently—that grief was contagious. And in the corner by the space heater, Walt Ferro: seventy-three years old, cardigan buttoned wrong, a canvas satchel on his lap held shut with both hands like it contained a sleeping animal.

Which, in a way, it did.

"I'm going to show you some numbers," I said, "and I need you to understand that I'm not asking you to believe me. Believing me is how this town got burned last time. Check everything. That's the whole point."

Marta crossed her arms. "Last time we checked nothing, and Theo Vance told us what to think. You're saying don't do that again even for you?"

"I'm saying especially not for me."

I opened the ledger to the winter of 2013—the shipments my uncle logged the same season Theo, then a junior analyst at the bank, had produced records showing Maple Hollow had sold product it never declared, the discrepancy that became the fraud finding, the finding that became my disgrace, the disgrace that became this room full of people who'd spent a decade politely pretending I was a cautionary tale.

I laid the pages flat on Ruth's folding table and stepped back.

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Pete Lindqvist leaned forward, squinting, and read aloud in the voice of a man reading scripture or an invoice, which for Pete were the same genre: "'Nov. 14 — 22 cases reserve Chardonnay to Hallowell Distributing, freight paid.' Twenty-two cases." He looked up. "Theo's report said eighteen."

"It said eighteen," I agreed.

"And your uncle's handwriting—" Marta was already bent over the page, nose almost touching it. "This is the same hand all the way down. Same ink, looks like. Nobody writes twenty-two and calls it eighteen unless they're bad at math or lying."

"Walt," Ruth said quietly.

The old man had not touched the ledger yet. He sat with his satchel and looked at me for a long time, and I understood that whatever happened next would happen on his schedule, not mine.

"In 2013," he said finally, "I kept the books for the co-op. Every member farm, every shared shipment, every freight bill. In March of 2014, a young man from the bank came to my house. Very polite. Very sorry to trouble me. Asked to see my copies of the Maple Hollow shipping records." His hands tightened on the satchel. "I gave him coffee and showed him nothing. My wife thought I was being paranoid. I told her: when a bank sends a polite young man instead of a letter, they already know what the letter would say."

He opened the satchel.

Inside were notebooks—actual paper notebooks, ruled, dated, their spines cracked with age—and beneath them, folders. Carbon copies. Triplicate freight manifests. A decade of the co-op's numbers kept by a man who trusted no institution further than he could carry it.

"Hallowell Distributing took twenty-two cases from Maple Hollow in November of 2013," Walt said. "I know because I booked the freight share. Here—" he slid a carbon across the table, yellowed but legible, "—twenty-two. And here is the bank's exhibit from the hearing, which Mr. Vance personally provided to the co-op board so we could, quote, understand our exposure." He produced a photocopied page, and the two documents lay side by side under the fluorescent light like a confession and its alibi.

Eighteen. The bank's copy said eighteen.

The room made a sound—not loud, more like a building settling, fourteen people exhaling ten years at once.

"They changed the number," Pete said slowly. "But why eighteen? Why change it at all?"

"Because eighteen fit the pattern," Delia said from beside me. She'd been silent through all of it, letting the town speak to its own, but now she stepped forward and the room made room, because Delia Marsh explaining numbers is a force of nature and everyone could feel the barometric pressure drop. "If you alter one record, it's a mistake. If you alter every record to match a consistent narrative, it's evidence. Theo needed the discrepancies to look systematic—Maple Hollow systematically underreporting sales. So he adjusted each figure to support the story. But he couldn't adjust the freight bills, the distributor's own invoices, the receipts on the other end. He counted on nobody ever checking both sides of the transaction." She paused. "He counted on this town not checking. On writing you off as people who wouldn't notice."

"Oh, he noticed we'd notice," Ruth said dryly. "That's why he sent the polite young man to Walt's kitchen. He just guessed wrong about Walt."

"I keep duplicates," Walt said, with the mild pride of a man describing a lifetime hobby that had just turned out to be a loaded weapon. "Always have. My father kept duplicates through the Depression. He said paper is the only witness that doesn't die, get drunk, or move to Florida."

There was actual laughter then—short, startled, the kind that comes out of people when grief changes shape so fast they don't know what to do with their faces. I felt it hit me sideways too, and I had to look at the ledger for a second because my eyes were doing something I didn't authorize.

* * *

We worked until midnight.

It shouldn't have been moving, a dozen people at folding tables with a space heater ticking and Ruth's coffeemaker dying heroically in the corner, but it was. Marta organized the documents by season because she organizes everything, including, I suspect, her dreams. The Kessler brothers cross-referenced freight carbons against Walt's notebooks, calling out matches in voices that got louder as the matches piled up. Pete, who hadn't spoken to me in four years, put his hand on my shoulder on his way past and said, "Your uncle was a better bookkeeper than the bank deserved," and kept walking, and that was somehow the worst moment of the night, emotionally speaking.

Delia and I built the timeline on a sheet of butcher paper taped to the wall. Every altered figure. Every date where Theo's "records" diverged from physical receipts. And in the margins, in her small precise hand, she noted the thing that made my stomach turn over: the handwriting habits. The way the altered figures sat slightly high on the line. The open-topped fours. The way he crossed his sevens twice when he was being careful.

"I've seen this hand before," she said quietly, marker capped, staring at the wall. "In college. He used to take notes for the whole study group because his printing was neater than anyone's. Ten years later and he still crosses his sevens the same way." She laughed, and it wasn't a funny laugh. "You can forge a document. You can't forge yourself into a different person."

Around one in the morning, Walt closed his notebooks and laced his fingers on top of them and delivered the evening's closing argument to the room at large.

"Now hear me. What we have here proves the numbers were changed. It does not prove who changed them. A good lawyer will say Edmund's uncle altered his own books, or that records got garbled, or that Walt's carbons are misfiled. The bank will say all three before lunch." He looked at me over his glasses. "What we need is testimony. People willing to stand up in front of the review board and say: I shipped this, I received that, the numbers don't lie and neither do I. That's harder than photocopies. Photocopies can't lose their livelihood."

Silence. The space heater ticked. I braced myself for the sound of chairs scraping backward, for the polite retreat of sensible people from another man's war.

Pete Lindqvist stood up first.

"I'm seventy-one years old," he said. "My orchard's paid off, my wife's gone, and my knees are shot. What exactly do they think they can take from me?" He shrugged into his coat. "I'll testify."

"So will I," said Marta. "They blacklisted me from the regional contracts in '14. I always figured it was because I asked questions about the Maple Hollow findings. Turns out asking questions was the crime." She snapped her folder shut. "I've got questions for the board now too."

One after another, like lights coming on down a dark road. The Kesslers. Ruth, who pointed out that she owned the building and therefore testified wherever she pleased. Even the youngest Kessler brother, who must have been thirty-five and had been quiet all night, said, "My dad signed the co-op petition against your family back then. He's been sick about it for years. Let me be the one who fixes it."

And Walt Ferro gathered his satchel, buttoned his cardigan correctly this time, and said, "I'll bring the duplicates. All of them. And if the bank's lawyers want to impugn my bookkeeping, they can explain to the board why a retired man's carbon copies match every distributor receipt in three counties while their director's records match nothing but his own ambition."

People filed out into the cold, breath steaming under the yard light, voices low and quick with the particular energy of people who have stopped feeling powerless and haven't yet found out what it costs. Ruth locked the back door behind the last of them and looked at me.

"My husband banked with that outfit for thirty years," she said. "When the foreclosure notice went up on your gate, half this town drove past it and felt relieved it wasn't theirs. That's what fear does—it makes other people's funerals feel like weather reports." She patted my arm with a hand like a work glove. "Not tonight. Tonight it felt like weather coming in that we could actually do something about."

* * *

Delia and I walked back to the cellar house through snow that squeaked underfoot, the sky scraped clean and brilliant with stars, the whole valley lit blue-white and silent. She carried the butcher-paper timeline rolled up under her arm like a diploma. Neither of us talked for the first quarter mile, and it was the good kind of silence, the kind with furniture in it.

"Ten years," she said finally. "Ten years, and all it took was a blizzard and a retired bookkeeper with trust issues."

"Every revolution has unglamorous logistics."

She laughed—a real one this time—and then sobered, stopping in the middle of the road. "Edmund. Tomorrow Theo gets wind of this. He'll accelerate the foreclosure, and he'll come after me—I told you, he's already hinted my objectivity is compromised. By tomorrow night he may have us both finished professionally. Are you ready for how fast this moves once it starts?"

I thought about the room at Ruth's. Fourteen people who had spent a decade treating my family's ruin as background noise, and who had spent one night turning it into a case file. I thought about Pete's hand on my shoulder. About Walt's carbons, patient as seeds in a drawer for eleven years.

"No," I said honestly. "But I was ready to lose alone, and I'm apparently not allowed to anymore. That's a better problem."

Ahead of us, the cellar house windows glowed orange against the dark, and beyond it the vineyard lay under its blanket, the dead-looking vines holding whatever they hold under snow, waiting out the cold the way they always have—not gone, just biding.

My phone buzzed in my pocket. A text from an unknown number, and my stomach dropped before I even read it, because I already knew.

*Heard you had quite the meeting at the feed store,* it said. *We should talk before the board does. Tomorrow, 8 a.m., my office. Bring the ledgers. Or don't—and find out what else I can make disappear.*

Delia read it over my arm, and I felt her go very still.

"He knows," she said.

"He knew before we did," I said, pocketing the phone. "The question is whether he knows about Walt."

Above us the stars kept their own ledger, indifferent and complete, and somewhere down the valley a plow scraped toward morning. Eight o'clock was seven hours away, and for the first time in ten years, I wasn't dreading a meeting with Theo Vance.

I was looking forward to watching him find out what this town keeps duplicates of.
