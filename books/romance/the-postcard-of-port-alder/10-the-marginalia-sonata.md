## 10. The Marginalia Sonata

The house had a way of rearranging itself around Jude when they weren't looking. Not literally—though after four days among Odile's things, Jude would no longer have sworn to that—but in the sense that every morning it offered up something new, as if the rooms were dealing cards from a deck Jude had only begun to count.

It was the third night of playing through the annotated scores that the pattern finally broke open.

Jude sat in the parlor with the lamp turned low and the rain doing its patient work against the windows, the cello borrowed from the church basement propped between their knees like an old friend who'd changed too much to recognize. They had been working through the stack in chronological order—or what they guessed was chronological order, because Odile had never dated anything, as if time were a courtesy she extended to others but declined for herself. The margins were the thing. Every score, every piece, carried her handwriting in the spaces where composers left room for breath: *slower here*, *let it ache*, *W. loved this passage*.

That last one appeared eleven times before Jude started keeping count.

Tonight they reached a score of Debussy—the Cello Sonata, water-damaged at one corner—and found the margins so dense with annotation that the printed music seemed almost incidental, a fence post for Odile's climbing vines of ink. And there, wedged into the space above the final movement's opening bar, a phrase that stopped Jude's bow mid-stroke:

*For W., entirely — O.*

Not *W. loved this passage*. Not an observation. A dedication.

Jude set the bow down on the music stand with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might go off. The lamp hummed. The rain continued. Somewhere under the floorboards of this house, or in its walls, or in whatever drawer Odile had deemed worthy, there might be more.

They turned back to the first page and began again, but this time not playing—reading. Reading the margins as text instead of instruction. And once you read them that way, once you stopped hearing them as a cellist's notes-to-self and started hearing them as a woman talking to someone across decades of silence, the whole architecture revealed itself.

*Begin where we began.* Written above the first bar.

*She always rushed the second phrase. I let her.* Above bar nine.

*This is where she laughed at me.* Bar fourteen.

*Don't look for the melody yet. It hides, like she did.* Bar twenty-two.

Jude's mouth went dry. These weren't performance notes. They were a narrative—a sequence, a map laid over the sonata's skeleton, each annotation a waypoint in a story that began somewhere and, if you followed it, ended somewhere else. Odile hadn't been annotating Debussy. She'd been using Debussy the way a spy uses a newspaper: as cover for a message hidden in plain sight, legible only to someone who knew to read the white space.

*Where the ferries sleep.*

The phrase surfaced from three nights ago, from the margin of the Fauré Élégie, written in Odile's hand but somehow different—pressed harder, urgent, the pen nearly tearing the paper: *The rest is where the ferries sleep. She'll know. If not her, then whoever loved us enough to look.*

Jude sat very still for a long moment, listening to the house listen back.

Then they got up, found a pencil, and began to transcribe.

* * *

It took until nearly three in the morning. Jude worked the way they'd once worked orchestral parts—not inspiration, but method. They copied every marginal note from every score onto a single legal pad, arranged them by what they now suspected was narrative order rather than composer order, and spread the pages across the parlor floor like evidence at a trial. The lamp made islands of light among the papers. The cello watched from its corner with the mute reproach of an instrument going unplayed.

There were forty-one annotations across nine scores. Read in sequence, they told a story in fragments:

*Begin where we began.* (The breakwater? The tide organs Sable had mentioned, half-laughing, on that first afternoon under the hood of the rental car?)

*She always rushed the second phrase. I let her.* (A lesson. Wren had been a musician too—or at least a student.)

*This is where she laughed at me.* 

*Don't look for the melody yet. It hides, like she did.*

*Eleven years I wrote to her in every key. This is the twelfth.* 

And then, near the end, the annotations changed character. The fondness drained out of them and something harder came in:

*I composed it for her. All of it. Every note is a sentence I couldn't say out loud.*

*The manuscript is finished. It has been finished for years.*

*But a letter can be returned unopened. Music cannot. You have to be in the room.*

*So I will play it for her. When I am ready. When I am brave enough.*

*The rest is where the ferries sleep.*

Jude knelt on the floorboards amid the scattered pages, and the grief that moved through them was strange because it wasn't theirs—it belonged to a woman dead eight months, reaching through ink to say *I made something for you and I never found the courage*. Odile Lockridge, who had raised Jude on discipline the way other grandparents raised children on sugar. Odile, who had taught them that the heart was a muscle and muscles obeyed. Odile, who had apparently spent some portion of her life unable to obey her own doctrine, sitting on this same floor maybe, composing a confession in D minor and hiding it from herself.

*You have to be in the room.*

Jude looked up at the dark windows, at the town beyond them invisible under rain, and thought about the boathouse. Because that was the thing about Port Alder—everyone knew where the ferries slept. There was only one place. The derelict municipal boathouse at the east end of the harbor, condemned since before Jude was born, visible from the road as a black tooth of rotting timber that only showed itself fully at low tide, when the water pulled back and exposed the pilings like the ribs of something drowned.

Sable had pointed it out once, in passing, without passing comment. Jude remembered because Sable's voice had done something odd when she said it—gone flat, careful, the way a person steps around a hole in the ice.

Jude gathered the pages, squared their edges, and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning came gray and rinsed-clean, the storm blown out overnight, the harbor lying flat and bright as hammered tin. Low tide was at ten past nine; Jude checked the tide table tacked inside the kitchen cupboard—Odile had kept tide tables the way other people kept calendars—and saw that the next comparable low tide wouldn't come for two weeks, and after that the winter equinoxal lows, and after that nothing until spring. The season was closing like a book.

Which meant today.

Jude drove the rental car—which no longer shuddered, which ran with the suspicious smoothness of a machine that had been properly loved—down the hill into town, and found the garage just off Front Street with its roll door up and radio going, some station out of Astoria playing standards through a speaker that crackled on the high notes.

Sable was under a truck. Just her boots showing, and one arm, and the occasional profanity delivered in a tone of professional affection.

"I need your help," Jude said, before courage could reconsider.

The wrenching stopped. Silence, then the slow scrape of a creeper rolling backward into daylight. Sable sat up, wiping her hands on a rag that had surrendered its original color sometime last decade, and looked at Jude with an expression carefully sanded down to neutral. Since the night at the breakwater—since the bad cello and the laughter and the moment on the walk home when something had almost happened and Jude had flinched away from it like a hand from a hot stove—there had been this new surface between them. Polite. Watchable. Ice over moving water.

"That's a first," Sable said. "People usually need my help with cars."

"It's not about the car."

"Shocking." But she got up, tossed the rag on the bench, and gave Jude her full attention, arms crossed, head tilted—the posture of a woman who fixed things for a living and recognized a broken thing when she saw one. "You look like you didn't sleep."

"I decoded it." Jude heard their own voice come out too fast, too tight, the old performance anxiety threading through it. "The margins. In the scores. They're not practice notes—they're a map. A sequence. Odile wrote—" They stopped, breathed, made themselves start over slower. "She composed a piece for Wren. A whole piece. She says so in the margins. She finished the manuscript and hid it, and the margins tell you where. Or they end by telling you where. The last line is the same phrase from the Fauré."

Something moved behind Sable's eyes—quick, submerged, gone. "What phrase?"

"*Where the ferries sleep.*"

The garage went quiet except for the radio, which chose that moment to finish its song and dissolve into static before finding the next one. Sable stood very still. Her gaze went past Jude, out the roll door, toward the harbor—toward the east end of the harbor—and Jude watched her arrive at the same conclusion Jude had arrived at at three in the morning, and watched her flinch from it.

"The boathouse," Sable said quietly.

"You knew."

"Everyone knows the boathouse." She said it too evenly. "I didn't know that."

"No. But you knew something." Jude took a step closer. The ice under their own feet was cracking now too, and they let it. "Every time this comes near Wren Halloway, you go somewhere I can't follow. The day I showed you the postcard. The night at the breakwater when I asked why nobody in this town will say her name. You know things, Sable. About Odile. About Wren. Maybe about that postcard. And I'm not asking you to explain yourself. I'm asking you to help me find what Odile hid, because I think—" Their voice caught, and they hated it, and kept going anyway. "I think it's the thing she owed her. The thing the postcard promised. Eleven years late is still better than never."

Sable looked at her hands. The grease in the creases. When she spoke again, the flat carefulness had thinned enough to show the current underneath.

"My aunt used to take me to that boathouse when I was small. Before she left." A muscle worked in her jaw. "We'd sit on the catwalk at low tide and she'd tell me the boats were sleeping and we had to whisper. I was six. I believed boats dreamed." She laughed, short and airless. "I haven't been inside since she left. Nobody has. The town pretends it isn't there."

"Then we'll be the first," Jude said. "In eleven years."

Sable was quiet long enough that the radio filled it again—strings this time, some adagio swelling through the crackle. She stared out at the bright hard water of the harbor, and Jude watched her weigh something, the scales of it visible in her face: loyalty against curiosity, grief against hope, the safe answer against the true one.

Then she reached for her jacket on the hook by the door.

"Tide's going out," she said. "We've got maybe two hours before it comes back and takes the ground floor with it. And Jude—" She paused, jacket half on, and for a moment the guardedness dropped entirely and what was underneath was younger than her face, almost a child's expression, the six-year-old asked to whisper where the boats were dreaming. "If it's really there. If she really wrote something. My aunt waited eleven years thinking Odile forgot her. Thinking she was easy to forget." Her voice roughened. "Don't let it be nothing."

"It won't be nothing," Jude said. "Odile didn't do anything halfway. Not even hiding."

* * *

The boathouse revealed itself the way secrets do: gradually, then all at once. From the road it was a suggestion, a dark geometry against the far mudflats. But as they picked their way down the gravel path and out onto the exposed flats—the tide withdrawn a quarter mile, the harbor bottom steaming faintly in the thin sun, gulls stitching patterns overhead—it rose up whole and enormous, a cathedral of blackened timber on stilts, its roofline sagging like a tired shoulder, its great sliding doors hanging open at drunken angles to show a throat of darkness within.

"Mind the pilings," Sable said. "And don't step anywhere I don't step. The mud lies."

They crossed together, jumping the channels of brackish water, and the smell of the flats rose around them—iodine and rot and ancient salt—and Jude felt the strangest lift in their chest, a feeling with no name except perhaps *approach*, the sensation of walking toward something that had been waiting.

Inside, the boathouse smelled of creosote and bird droppings and time. Light fell through gaps in the plank walls in long dusty blades. The slipways ran down into empty mud. Ropes hung from beams like hanged men. Along one wall stood the skeletal remains of a workbench, and above it, nailed to the joists, a row of rusted hooks where tools had once lived, and beneath the hooks, in the shadow, a sea chest.

Or what had been a sea chest. It was swollen with damp, its iron fittings bloomed orange, its lid fused shut by decades of weather. But it was closed. It had been *kept* closed. Someone had put it here deliberately, up off the floor on two rotting planks, tucked beneath the tool hooks where a casual eye would see only clutter.

Sable made a sound low in her throat. "That wasn't there when I was six," she whispered. "I'd remember. I remember everything about this place."

"Odile must have hidden it later. After Wren left." Jude knelt beside it, heart hammering with a rhythm entirely unauthorized, entirely undisciplined. "Help me. It'll take both of us."

The lid fought them. Salt had welded it, and the wood groaned like something waking, and then—with a crack that echoed through the whole hollow building—it gave. Dust and the smell of cedar and oilcloth rolled out.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, lay a bundle. Jude lifted it with both hands, the way they'd once been taught to lift a newborn instrument, and unwound the cloth layer by layer while Sable held the lantern they'd brought, her breath audible, her stillness total.

Sheet music. A manuscript in Odile's hand—every note, every dynamic marking, every tempo indication drawn in that upright, disciplined script Jude knew from a lifetime of birthday cards. The title page read:

*Sonata for Wren*
*for cello and tide organ*
*— O.L., Port Alder*

And beneath the title, smaller, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*To be played where the ferries sleep, at the turning of the tide, so she can hear it coming in.*

Beneath the manuscript, at the bottom of the chest, lay one more thing. An envelope, addressed in the same hand, stamped, postmarked—sent, this time, not unsent. The address was a nursery in a town two hours south. The stamp bore a cancellation mark and across the envelope's face, in red rubber letters faded to pink:

*RETURN TO SENDER — ADDRESSEE UNKNOWN.*

Sable made no sound at all. That was the worst part—the total absence of sound from a woman Jude had heard laugh, curse, sing badly to the radio. She stared at the returned envelope as though it were a body.

"She tried," Jude said softly. "At the end. She tried to send it."

"She sent it to the wrong address." Sable's voice came out scraped raw. "Or the right address and Wren was already gone. Moved. Running." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "God. Eleven years. She was *here*, Jude. The answer was sitting in the dark thirty feet from the water the whole time, and my aunt was two towns over thinking she'd been forgotten."

Jude looked down at the title page—at *so she can hear it coming in*—and felt the last of their trained-heart philosophy go quiet, the way a radio goes quiet, not broken, just finally listening.

"We know where Wren is now," they said. "The postmistress's cousin. Two towns south. The nursery."

Sable lowered her hand. Her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath the fury, terrified with hope. "Two towns south," she repeated. "Fifteen minutes' drive."

"Fifteen minutes," Jude agreed. "And a letter eleven years late."

They climbed out of the boathouse into the returning light, the manuscript wrapped again in its oilcloth and carried against Jude's chest like a pulse, and neither of them noticed, until they were halfway across the flats, how closely they were walking—shoulder to shoulder, matching steps, two people moving toward the same door.
