## 19. Last Ferry of the Season

The contract sat on the table like a held breath.

Jude had read it twice in the green room after the audition—the second time only because their hands were shaking too badly to stand up yet, and reading was the only thing they could do that didn't require standing. *Principal Cello. Port Alder Philharmonic. Five-year term.* The same orchestra, the same hall, the same stage where eleven months ago their hands had simply stopped mid-phrase and left them standing in front of two thousand people like a mannequin someone had forgotten to wind.

They had played flawlessly today. That was the strange part. Every note had arrived exactly when summoned, obedient as a soldier, and Maestro Voss had closed his eyes during the Elgar and let the silence after the final cadence stretch long enough that Jude knew, before he opened his mouth, what he was going to say.

*Welcome home,* he'd said. And meant it.

And Jude had walked out of the building with the contract still folded in its envelope, unsigned, because somewhere between the last note and the handshake, a sentence had surfaced from somewhere below thought, from somewhere below training:

*Hearts aren't trained. They're trusted.*

It was Odile's handwriting they'd heard it in. Of course it was. Eleven years of marginalia in the scores upstairs, all those small penciled instructions—*breathe here, don't guard this passage, let it hurt a little*—and Jude finally understood that she hadn't been writing performance notes at all. She'd been writing love letters in advance, to whoever came looking.

The taxi to the coast had cost more than the flight would have. There were no flights that made the ferry. There was only the coastal road, two hundred kilometers of gray water on the right and logging trucks on the left, and a driver named Petros who kept glancing at the cello case wedged between them and asking questions Jude answered in monosyllables until he gave up and turned up the radio instead.

The schedule lived in Jude's head now, printed there in the same font as every ferry timetable posted in every window in Port Alder: *Last sailing of the season, 4:40 p.m., weather permitting.*

It was 3:52 when they passed the sign for Halloway Narrows. It was 4:09 when the road dipped toward the sound and Jude saw the water for the first time—that particular pewter color, the fog sitting on it like a lid—and something in their chest unclenched so violently it felt like injury.

4:31. The town appeared in pieces: the cannery roof, the church spire, the breakwater with its tide organs standing black against the light. The dock. The dock where—

*Please,* Jude said, not to God, not to Petros, but to the ferry itself, visible now at the end of the road, moored and loading, its ramp down, its diesel idle rumbling across the water like a large animal deciding whether to wake.

Petros took the last corner on two wheels, which Jude chose not to comment on.

* * *

Sable Ives had been arguing with the ferry schedule since dawn.

Not out loud. Out loud she'd done everything correctly—checked the bilge pumps, bled the fuel line, signed off on the safety inspection with the harbor master, and stood at the gangway in her orange vest watching passengers board with the flat professional calm of a woman whose whole job was making departures look routine. Inside, she had been arguing since six a.m., and the argument went like this:

*She's not coming back. She got what she came for. Voss doesn't offer principal chairs twice; even I know that, and I fix engines. She'll sign it tonight over dinner in some restaurant with cloth napkins, and by spring she'll be playing Elgar to two thousand people who never have to know her hands ever stopped working, and that's the right ending. That's the ending she rehearsed her whole life for.*

Then the other voice, quieter, the one that sounded annoyingly like Odile: *You've never once seen her do the thing she rehearsed her whole life for without changing her mind halfway through.*

At 4:33, boarding complete, Sable gave the deckhand the nod. The ramp began to rise.

That was when she saw the car.

It came down Harbor Road too fast, a rented sedan with salt spray crusted up its doors, and it braked at the head of the dock at an angle that suggested the driver had stopped thinking about parking approximately forty meters before actually stopping. The door opened before the car fully settled. And then Jude Quintana was running—actually running, coat open, no luggage except the cello case banging against their hip like a heartbeat they couldn't keep up with—and Sable's hand was already moving, already gripping the ramp control, before her brain caught up and asked what exactly she thought she was doing.

Thirty seconds. That was the argument she lost. Thirty seconds of holding a loaded ramp against company policy against the tide schedule against four months of telling herself she knew how this story ended.

The deckhand looked at her. She looked back. He shrugged and went to help the passenger aboard, because deckhands, unlike mechanics, did not have reputations built on refusing to move things.

Jude reached the foot of the ramp with nine seconds left and stopped, chest heaving, fog beading in their hair, and looked up at Sable standing above them on the gangway with her hand still on the lever.

"You're leaving," Sable said. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"No." Jude climbed. Halfway up they had to stop, breath gone, and finished the sentence from the middle of the ramp, which meant they said it to Sable's knees, and then corrected course and said it again to her face. "I'm bringing it home."

"Bringing what home?"

"The contract." Jude pulled the envelope from inside their coat, where it had ridden against their ribs for three hours like a second heartbeat, and held it out. Unsigned. Uncreased. Sable could see the blank line where a signature belonged, and above it the letterhead she'd seen framed on the wall of the concert hall in a photograph Odile kept in the attic, back when the attic was full of photographs and not just boxes.

Sable didn't take it. Her hand stayed on the ramp lever. "Why are you showing me?"

"Because if I put it in my pocket again I'll find a reason to sign it, and I wanted—" Jude's voice did something complicated and recovered. "I wanted one person to see it unsigned. So it counts."

The deckhand cleared his throat. The engine note changed beneath them, deepening, committing. Sable released the lever and the ramp rose behind Jude like a door closing on the mainland, on the road, on the version of their life that fit inside an envelope.

"Last ferry of the season," Sable said. "You understand you're stuck with us now. Until April."

"I'm counting on it."

* * *

The crossing took forty minutes, and for thirty-eight of them neither of them spoke.

They stood at the stern rail because it was cold enough that no one else wanted it, and the town fell away behind them in the fog, and ahead the water opened into that gray immensity that had frightened Jude the first week and now felt less like emptiness than like room. The cello case stood upright between them, leaning against the rail, riding the swell.

"You played well," Sable said eventually. "I can tell. You have the walk."

"The walk?"

"People who just played well walk different than people who just played okay. You're walking like the ground owes you money." She glanced sideways. "Voss say anything?"

"He said welcome home."

"And what'd you say?"

"I said thank you. Then I asked him for a week to decide, and he said the offer stands until Friday, and then I ran for a taxi." Jude turned the envelope over in their hands. "I didn't decide on the drive. I want to be honest about that. I decided about a hundred meters from the dock, when I saw the breakwater. Before that I was just—driving very fast in your general direction and calling it instinct."

"That's how most decisions get made here. The fog doesn't leave you room for the fancy kind."

Jude laughed—a real one, startled out of them—and Sable watched it happen with an expression she probably believed was neutral.

"There's something else," Jude said. "About the contract. If I sign it, it's five years. Principal chair. I'd have to be in the city for rehearsals eight months of the year."

The word *city* landed between them and sat there.

"But," Jude continued, and their voice found the register it used for the hard passages, the one that didn't waver by deciding not to, "Voss also mentioned they're funding a community outreach position. Teaching. Island schools, coastal towns. It pays badly and nobody wants it. I told him I knew someone perfect." They looked at Sable. "I told him I'd take it myself if I could do both. He laughed. He said nobody does both. I said he clearly hadn't met anyone from Port Alder."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. A gull kept pace with the boat, riding the wake, indifferent to everything human happening beneath it.

"You'd give up the chair," she said. "The actual chair. For a teaching post and a ferry commute."

"I spent eleven months learning that the chair isn't the music. I learned it the worst possible way, in front of everyone, and then I learned it again slowly, in a house full of her handwriting." Jude's hand found the cello case, rested on the handle. "Odile turned down Juilliard. Did you know that? It's in the letters. Wren never knew why she stayed. Now we do. She stayed because she was dying and she loved one person more than she feared leaving her, and she couldn't figure out how to say it, so she said nothing for eleven years." Jude shook their head slowly. "I'm not doing that. Whatever else I get wrong—I am not doing that."

The ferry rounded the point. Ahead, through the thinning fog, the lights of the harbor came on one by one, and the breakwater emerged from the gray like the spine of something surfacing.

"Sable." Jude turned to face her fully, the rail at their back, the wind pushing their hair sideways. "There's a grave up on the hill that I need to visit tomorrow morning. Early. And there's a letter I need to read aloud there, and I can't do it alone. I've tried to imagine doing it alone about forty times and every version ends with me standing there unable to make my mouth work." They swallowed. "Wren should hear it from family. But I need—"

"Yes," Sable said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were going to ask me to be there. The answer's yes." She looked out at the approaching harbor, jaw tight, eyes bright in a way she'd deny later. "Eleven years I thought Odile abandoned her aunt because the music mattered more. One letter fixed that in an afternoon. Somebody should've read that letter aloud a decade ago. I should've. I had it in my hands." She exhaled. "So yes. Tomorrow. Early. I'll bring coffee and I'll stand next to you and if your mouth stops working, mine won't."

The ferry slowed. Beneath them the engine dropped to that idling growl, and the dock lights grew close enough to count, and figures moved along the pier preparing to receive the season's last arrival.

One of the figures was tall, silver-haired, wearing a coat too good for the weather, standing apart from the others near the top of the ramp.

Jude saw her first. Felt Sable go still beside her a half-second later.

"She said she wasn't coming," Sable said quietly. "She said the dock was sentimental and she had seedlings to pot."

"Wren's waiting," Jude said. And the words settled over them with a weight that had nothing heavy in it at all—the weight of a thing finally being carried the way it was always meant to be carried, by hand, the last distance, on foot.

The ramp began to lower.
