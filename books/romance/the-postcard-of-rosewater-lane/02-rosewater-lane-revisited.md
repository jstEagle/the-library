## 2. Rosewater Lane, Revisited

The train climbed out of the valley the way memory climbs out of sleep—slowly, reluctantly, with long pauses where it seemed to forget its own purpose. Vesper watched the vineyards give way to orchards, the orchards to bare winter fields stitched with frost, and told herself that the tightness in her chest was only the altitude.

She had brought two bags: one for clothes, one for work. The second held Julian Ashford's memoir notes in three leather binders, his handwriting slanting across hundreds of pages like a man racing something. She had read them twice on the journey north and had learned nothing she trusted, because every page was a door painted on a wall. *The year I turned twenty-four, I understood that love is a debt you can incur without signing anything.* Sentences like that. Beautiful, evasive sentences. He had always been able to make surrender sound like philosophy.

*From the draft notes of J. Ashford, undated:*

*I have been asked what I remember most about that winter, and the honest answer is the light—the way it came through the east window at seven in the morning and laid itself across the floorboards like a confession no one had asked for. She was asleep with her manuscript under her cheek. I remember thinking: whatever happens next, this room will always belong to her.*

Vesper closed the binder. Outside, the mountains had arrived all at once, blue-white and enormous, wearing their snow like old money wears age.

The taxi let her off at the foot of the drive just before four. Ashford Vineyards rose out of the dusk in stages—first the terraced vines, pruned back to their winter skeletons, then the great stone house itself, three stories of honey-colored masonry with new glass doors where she remembered shutters. Someone had strung lights along the eaves. Someone had decided the past should be festive.

Julian was waiting in the courtyard, and the sight of him performed its old violence efficiently. Ten years had silvered his temples and put lines around his mouth, but he stood the same way he always had—one shoulder slightly lower, as if permanently braced against a wind only he could feel.

"Vesper." Her name in his mouth sounded like an apology rehearsed too many times.

"Mr. Ashford." She let the formality land between them like a dropped tool. "You've kept the place."

"Kept parts of it. Lost others." He reached for her bag; she shifted it out of range with a smoothness she'd practiced for a decade. Something flickered across his face—not surprise. Recognition. "The storm's supposed to come tomorrow night. We should get your interviews done while the roads are still ours."

"How efficient."

"I'm told efficiency is my best quality," he said, and led her inside.

* * *

He gave her the room at the top of the stairs—the good room, the one with the view over the valley—and she understood immediately that this was either courtesy or cruelty, and that with Julian the two had never been distinguishable. From the window she could see the whole eastern slope, and beyond it, half-hidden by bare plane trees, the roofline of the house on Rosewater Lane.

She had not known, until this moment, that Rosewater Lane still existed as anything but a street name in her head. They had found that house together at twenty-three, two rooms above a shuttered bakery, rent they could barely afford, a stove that lit on the third try or not at all. She had written her first published story at its kitchen table. He had read every draft aloud in a voice he invented for her narrator, low and wry, until she believed the words were worth the air it took to say them.

Now, after dinner she did not taste, she stood in the converted tasting room that had been the ground floor of that house, and felt the vertigo rise through the soles of her feet.

They had kept the bones. That was the unbearable part. The original beams still crossed the ceiling, darkened now with a lacquer meant to look accidental. The wall where her bookshelves had stood was a floor-to-ceiling rack of wine bottles, each label angled toward the light like a congregation facing forward. Where the kitchen table had been—her table, scarred with candle wax and one memorable ring from a pan left too long—there was now a long bar of polished walnut, and behind it a young woman decanting a red the color of late afternoon.

"You're standing where the icebox used to be," Julian said from the doorway.

She didn't turn around. "You turned our house into a shop."

"We turned a ruin into a livelihood. There's a difference, though I grant it's thin." He came to stand beside her, close enough that she caught the smell of cold air and crushed grapes that clung to him like a second skin. "The bakery below closed the year after we left. The roof went in the spring. My father bought it for the cellar space—the soil under this street runs cool all summer. It seemed better to use it than to watch it fall down."

"It wasn't yours to renovate."

"No," he said quietly. "It wasn't."

And there it was again, that habit of his, that immediate capitulation that somehow conceded nothing. She remembered arguing with him the same way ten years ago—he would agree with her so completely, so gracefully, that the argument would collapse and leave her holding her own anger like a bouquet nobody wanted.

"The east window," she said. "Is it still—"

"Behind the barrel display. We couldn't take it out. Structural." He hesitated. "Do you want to see it?"

She did not want to see it. She wanted it more than she had wanted anything in years.

* * *

*From the draft notes of J. Ashford, page 112:*

*People assume renovation is an act of erasure. It isn't. Renovation is an act of guilt—you keep the beams because you cannot bear to lose the last proof that something stood here before you failed it. Every bottle we racked against that wall was a small apology to a woman who will never accept it. I built the tasting room around the east window because I knew, someday, she would come back and stand exactly where she is standing now.*

Vesper read that passage that night in her room, by lamplight, while the first flakes of the coming storm ticked against the glass like fingers testing a lock. She read it four times. Then she wrote in the margin, in her sharpest hand:

*If you knew she'd come back, why did you leave?*

The question sat there on the page, ink drying, unanswered—as she suspected it would remain for days yet. Below her window, the vineyard lay white and silent, the pruned vines row upon row like sentences waiting to be finished.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would sit him down with a recorder and a list of questions, and she would be professional, and thorough, and immune. Tomorrow she would extract the truth the way you extract a splinter—cleanly, quickly, without flinching.

She fell asleep with the lamp still burning and dreamed of a kitchen table with wax scars, and a voice reading her words aloud in the dark, getting them almost right.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless, the hush before snow. She found Julian already in the tasting room, arranging glasses with the concentration of a man defusing something. Through the windows behind him, the sky over the ridge had gone the color of pewter.

"Before we start," he said, not looking up, "there's something I should show you. For context. Chapter material."

"Context," she repeated.

"The cellars. Where the story actually begins, if we're being honest about beginnings." He set down the last glass with a faint click. "My father's papers are down there too. The real ones. If you're going to write this book, Vesper, you'll need to understand what was happening in this family the winter I—" He stopped. Started again. "The winter everything ended."

Outside, far up the valley road, she saw the weather moving in—a gray wall of it, unhurried, certain, swallowing the ridgeline peak by peak. The last train south ran at six. She did the arithmetic without wanting to: interviews today, perhaps tomorrow morning, departure well ahead of the storm if she pushed.

Or she stayed, and the mountain decided for her.

"Show me the cellars," she said, and picked up her recorder, and followed him down into the dark where the truth—or some version of it—was waiting in barrels older than their grief.
