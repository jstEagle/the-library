## 3. What the Storm Decides

The snow began at noon, the way mountain storms do—without announcement, without mercy, as if it had been waiting all morning for permission.

Julian Ashford stood in the tasting room of Ashford Vine and watched it come down over the terraced rows of dormant vines, each one pruned back to its winter skeleton, and he told himself for the hundredth time that what he had done was not unforgivable. Requesting a ghostwriter by name through a publisher's office three hundred miles away was not a crime. It was not even, strictly speaking, a deception. Publishers matched writers to projects all the time; it was practically an algorithm. He had simply—nudged the algorithm. He had said *Vesper Thornbury or no one*, and when his editor had raised an eyebrow and asked if he was certain, given the—history—he had said yes with such flat finality that she had stopped asking questions.

He had not told Vesper any of this. He had let her believe her publisher had assigned her, that the coincidence of their names colliding again after ten years was merely the industry's small world turning as small worlds do. And now she was upstairs in the east guest room, unpacking with the brisk efficiency of a woman who intended to treat this house like a hotel and him like a source, and the snow was falling harder, and the last train out of the valley left at 6:40.

He checked his watch. 4:15. The road down the mountain would close within the hour—the county always closed it when accumulation passed six inches, and the forecast had promised fourteen by midnight. He had known that when he scheduled these interviews for late February. He had known it the way a man knows the tide tables before he invites someone sailing.

*Guilty hope*, his mother used to call it, though she'd meant something gentler: the hope you feel when you want a thing so badly you can't look at it directly, the way you can't look at the sun. He wanted three days of snow. He wanted the trains cancelled and the phones down and the world reduced to this house and its cellars and the woman in the east room who had once slept in his bed on Rosewater Lane and woken with ink on her cheekbone from falling asleep over her notebooks.

He wanted it, and he had arranged it, and he could not say so.

* * *

She came down at five, dressed in a charcoal sweater and jeans, her hair pinned up with whatever she'd found in her bag—a pencil, he noticed, an actual pencil pushed through the knot of it—and the sight of her in his kitchen doorway did something to his chest that he had spent a decade training himself not to feel.

"Your housekeeper says the road's already closed," she said. "Above the second switchback."

"It closes early this time of year." He kept his voice level, professional, the voice of a man discussing logistics. "The train—"

"I called. Cancelled. Something about ice on the rails at Miller's Crossing." She set her phone face-down on the counter with a small decisive click, as though putting the day itself away. "So I'm stranded here until tomorrow at the earliest. I hope your guest room doesn't have ghosts."

The irony of it landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall a long way before it hit anything.

"No ghosts," he said. "Only wine."

"Then we may as well work." She pulled out her recorder, set it on the oak table between them, and looked at him with those grey eyes that had once read him like a first draft—finding every place he'd overwritten, every place he'd lied. "Chapter one. The night you left Rosewater Lane. Start wherever you like."

Outside, the wind rose against the north windows, and the snow came down thicker, erasing the vineyard row by row, until the whole estate seemed to float unmoored in white.

* * *

They worked for two hours by the big hearth in the library, because the power flickered twice and Julian lit the fire partly for warmth and partly because he could not bear the fluorescent honesty of overhead lights while they did this. Vesper sat across from him with her legs folded under her, notebook balanced on her knee, writing in that fast slanted hand he remembered from a thousand mornings—grocery lists, margin notes, the beginnings of stories she never finished because she gave everything to other people's words.

That was the thing about Vesper Thornbury that the world didn't know. The celebrated ghostwriter, the woman whose sentences sold memoirs for politicians and actresses and CEOs—they were all his sentences too, in a way, or hers, but signed by strangers. She had built a career out of disappearing into other people's truths. He had built a life out of the same skill. They had been two ghosts haunting each other for ten years, and now the storm had blown them into the same room and demanded they take form.

"The night you left," she said again, when his answers had gone thin. "You're giving me weather reports, Julian. I need the night itself."

"There isn't much to tell."

"Then tell me there isn't much to tell in a way I can print." Her pencil stilled. "You wrote me a note. Eleven lines. I still have it—I could recite it. You said you'd be gone before I woke, that it was better this way, that you'd explain someday. Ten years, Julian. Someday is taking its time."

The fire popped. Snow ticked against the glass like fingers testing a lock.

"Some explanations don't improve with age," he said quietly.

"Try me."

And God, he wanted to. That was the trap he'd built for himself, brick by brick: he had brought her here to finally say it—to put the truth of his father's ruin, the creditors circling like gulls, the way he'd signed papers in a lawyer's office at two in the morning promising to vanish cleanly so her name would never be dragged into the Ashford collapse—and instead he sat there feeding her the same evasions he'd fed everyone else, because saying it aloud would mean admitting why he'd really requested her. Not for the memoir. The memoir was the letter; she was the address. He had engineered a blizzard around a confession he still couldn't make.

"Tell me about the note instead," he said. "What you remember of it."

Her expression changed—something closing, something hardening, the way ice forms on a pond from the edges inward. "You're interviewing me now? In my own interview?"

"It's context."

"It's deflection." She snapped the notebook shut. "You know what the worst part is, Julian? It's not that you left. People leave. It's that you made me spend ten years thinking I imagined how much it mattered. You let me build a whole version of that night—you, standing in the kitchen with your coat already on, not even able to look at me—and you've never once told me my version was wrong. Or right. Nothing. You just let me live inside it."

"That's not—" He stood, and the movement was too fast, too honest, betraying him. "Vesper."

"Don't." She was standing too now, gathering her recorder, her notebook, her terrible composure. "We'll pick this up tomorrow. If the trains run."

"Vesper, wait—"

But she was already at the door, and she paused there with her hand on the frame, not turning around, and said, so quietly he almost missed it beneath the wind:

"You know what I keep thinking? That you asked for me. Your publisher let that slip last week—that you requested me by name. So either you wanted the best writer in the business, or you wanted me. And I've been sitting here all evening trying to decide which possibility I hate more."

The door closed behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

He did not sleep. He moved through the darkened house like a man revisiting a crime scene, touching the things she had touched—the doorframe, the kettle she'd used for tea, the chair pushed back from the table at a fleeing angle. Outside, the storm had buried the road entirely; he could see it from the study window, a white river where the drive had been, and beyond it the vineyard rows dissolving into the general whiteness of the world.

Tomorrow the trains might run. Tomorrow she would sit across from him again with her pencil in her hair and her loyalty to her own wounds, and he would have to choose—again, as he had chosen every day for ten years—between the truth and the coward's comfort of protecting her from it.

His father had taught him that choice. His father, who had run the vineyard into the ground on borrowed money and borrowed time, who had taught Julian that love was a liability an auditor could seize. *Never sign your name to anything you can't walk away from.* Julian had taken the lesson further than the old man ever intended: he had walked away from the only person he couldn't bear to lose, believing distance was a kind of shelter.

It had been a lie then. It was a lie now, standing in this cold beautiful house with her sleeping one floor above him.

In the morning, he told himself. In the morning he would find the words.

But when dawn came, grey and silent and deep in snow, it was not his courage that changed anything. It was a knock at his study door—early, urgent—and Vesper standing in the hallway with a shoebox in her arms and an expression he had never seen on her face before, not in ten years, not in all their years before that.

"Julian," she said. "I was looking for spare blankets in the linen closet upstairs. There are boxes in there. Old ones." She held the shoebox out toward him with both hands, the way one holds something fragile, or damning. "One of them has your handwriting on it. And my name."
