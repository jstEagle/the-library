## 6. Pruning Season

The vines slept, and Julian had always found a strange mercy in that. Dormancy was not death; it was the vine holding its breath, gathering itself for the violence of spring. His father used to say you could tell everything about a man by how he pruned—whether he cut out of fear or out of faith. Julian had spent ten years cutting out of fear. He was beginning to suspect it showed.

He found Vesper at the edge of the east block just after eight, breath fogging, her city coat traded for one of his mother's old wool jackets that swallowed her to the knees. She'd pulled her hair back with a pencil through the knot—a habit he remembered from a decade ago, from mornings when she wrote before dawn and forgot she owned a mirror. The sight of it landed somewhere beneath his ribs and stayed there.

"You're late," she said, without looking up.

"I'm the owner."

"Then I'm docking your advance." She held up the pruning shears he'd left on the porch rail for her, testing their weight like a duelist. "Show me again which canes stay."

He stepped close enough to take her hand—only her hand, only the hand—and turned it palm-up, laying the shears across it. Her skin was cold from the morning air. He did not let go as long as instruction strictly required.

"Everything that grew this year comes off," he said. "All of it. Even the canes that look promising. Especially those." He guided the blade to a slender shoot still holding a shriveled grape like a garnet earring. "The vine will spend all winter feeding this if you let it. It looks like hope. It's actually a debt."

"And what stays?"

"The old wood. The structure. What survived last year's weather." He released her hand and stepped back before the moment could become something else. "You keep the frame. You trust it to carry fruit you'll never see coming."

Vesper looked down the rows, three hundred meters of bare, twisted arms against the white field, and Julian watched her do the thing she always did: translate. He could nearly hear the sentence forming behind her eyes—the vine as metaphor, the pruning as grief, some elegant line about how we all cut away our best growth because we don't believe we deserve the weight of it. She'd built a career on sentences like that. He'd built a life on avoiding them.

"Start here," he said. "Cut at an angle, away from the bud. Water runs off; the wood doesn't rot."

"Like a roof over a wound," she murmured, and made her first cut.

* * *

By noon they had worked half the row, and the rhythm had returned to them the way water returns to an old channel—finding the grooves a decade of drought hadn't managed to erase. She reached high; he caught the fallen brush. She asked questions that were interviews wearing work clothes; he answered more honestly than he'd answered anyone in years, because it was easier to be honest with his hands full.

She told him about the years between, in the sideways way writers tell things—not the facts but the textures. A flat above a bakery in Lyon where the smell of yeast got into her notebooks. An editor who taught her that sentimentality was fear wearing perfume. A stretch of eighteen months when she'd written under three different names and couldn't remember which one paid the rent.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now I'm a ghost with good posture." She snapped a cane free and tossed it onto the growing pile. "People pay me to disappear into their lives. I'm very good at leaving no fingerprints."

"That's a terrible way to live."

"It's an excellent way to live," she corrected, and for a moment her voice had an edge he recognized—the old Vesper, the one who argued with him about Rilke at two in the morning and never once conceded a point she didn't want to concede. "No one can hurt a person who isn't there. You should try it sometime."

The words hung between them in the cold air, and Julian understood that she wasn't talking about her profession anymore. Neither of them pretended otherwise. He picked up the shears.

"There's a cane here that needs taking out," he said quietly. "It grew toward the house instead of the sun. Wrong direction entirely. But it's the healthiest wood on the vine."

"Then it stays."

"No. It drains everything from the rest. You can love a branch and still have to cut it." He positioned the blade. "That's the whole job, Vesper. Choosing which losses feed the fruit."

He cut it. The sound was small and clean, and neither of them spoke for a long time after.

* * *

It happened because of the wind. That was the truth he would cling to later—that the storm door of the equipment shed blew open, and the papers inside went skittering across the frozen ground like startled birds, and Vesper chased them down before he could stop her.

They were his father's. He kept them in the shed because he could not keep them in the house and could not burn them either: the bankruptcy filings, dated eleven years ago that March. Ashford Vineyards, Ltd., liabilities exceeding assets by a figure that had once made Julian's hands shake to read. Creditor letters. A notice of asset seizure with a list attached—the estate, the cellar stock, the equipment, everything bolted down and much that wasn't. And clipped to the front page, in his father's slanted hand, a list of names. People who owed the family money. People the creditors would pursue next, associates, guarantors—

Her name was third from the top. *V. Thornbury.* His father had taken a personal loan against Julian's future earnings once, early, stupidly, and Vesper had co-signed it at twenty-four because she believed in the vineyard, believed in Julian, believed in the whole sunlit fiction of the life they were building. Eleven years of interest had accrued on a signature she'd probably forgotten she ever gave.

Julian saw her read it. Saw her lips move slightly over the figures. Saw the color leave her face in the particular way it left it when she was working hard not to feel something—the same look she'd worn the night he left, standing in the doorway of the house on Rosewater Lane while he lied to her with a calmness he had practiced for weeks in front of a mirror.

"This is why," she said. Not a question. The paper trembled in her grip. "This is why you—"

"The wind," he said, and took the pages from her, too roughly, and folded them away. "It's nothing. Old business. Finished business."

"Finished." She laughed, and the laugh was awful, bright and brittle as ice on a puddle. "Your father owed money in my name, Julian. My name is on a creditor's list from a decade ago and you're calling it finished business?"

"They never came for you. I made sure they never came for you."

The sentence escaped before he could catch it, and he saw immediately what it had cost him—saw her go very still, the way she went still before a kill in her prose, zeroing in on the wounded thing.

"*Made sure,*" she repeated softly. "What does that mean, Julian? What exactly does that mean?"

And God help him, he wanted to tell her. The whole truth sat in his chest like a stone he'd been carrying so long he'd grown around it, and here she was, snow in her hair, her hands still smelling of cut vine, asking him plainly. He opened his mouth—

Headlights swept the yard.

A car he knew came crunching up the gravel drive, and Julian felt the moment close like a door. Camille's Audi, chains on the tires, arriving the way Camille arrived everywhere: as though the world had been holding a space for her and she'd simply decided to fill it.

* * *

Camille Duval stepped out in a camel coat worth more than the tractor, and Julian watched Vesper perform the appraisal in a single glance—the coat, the confidence, the familiarity of the woman's stride on this ground—and file her conclusions where writers file such things.

"You're pruning?" Camille said, kissing the air near both his cheeks. "In February. You hate February." Then, turning, extending a gloved hand: "Camille Duval. Business partner. And you must be the ghost."

"The biographer," Vesper said, shaking it. Her voice was pleasant. Her eyes were not.

"Of course. Forgive me." Camille smiled the smile she used in negotiations, warm as a heat lamp and exactly as impersonal. "Julian speaks of your work. Though I confess I was surprised he wanted a memoirist at all. He's always been so—" she searched delicately, "—*private*. About certain years especially."

"Camille."

"We're expanding into the Burgundy market in the spring," Camille continued, sailing over him. "It requires capital. It requires commitment. It requires—" she glanced at Vesper, then back, and let the pause do its work, "—a partner who intends to remain. In every sense. Julian has a tendency toward dramatic departures, Ms. Thornbury. I'm sure your research has uncovered that much."

The wind moved through the bare rows with a sound like a long exhale. Vesper looked at Julian, and he could not read her face at all, which frightened him more than anything she might have said.

"Well," she said finally, lightly, terribly. "I'm only here until the manuscript's done. Ghosts don't sign leases."

She walked back toward the house alone, her borrowed coat trailing almost in the snow, and Julian stood in the wreckage of the moment knowing—with the sick certainty of a man watching his second harvest rot on the vine—that he had perhaps thirty seconds to follow her, and that if he let them pass, whatever had been rekindling between those dormant rows would freeze solid by morning.

He took one step. Camille's hand closed on his sleeve.

"She deserves the truth," Camille said, low, urgent, not unkind. "But understand what it costs. If the creditors' list surfaces—if it comes out that you left her to protect her, that your father endangered her—the estate's liability reopens. My investors will walk, Julian. Everything we've rebuilt walks with them." Her grip tightened. "I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you the price of the truth, so you choose it with open eyes. Like pruning. Isn't that what you're always saying? Choose which losses feed the fruit."

He stared at her. "How do you know what I say in my own vineyard?"

"Because I've been listening for six years," Camille said simply, "waiting for you to stop pruning yourself." She released his sleeve. "Go after her. Or don't. But know that whichever you choose, someone pays."

Julian stood alone among the sleeping vines, the bankruptcy papers burning in his pocket like contraband, and looked at the house where the light had just come on in the guest room window—her window—and understood that tomorrow, when she sorted his papers for Chapter Three, she would find the rest of it.

Or he could give it to her first.

The snow began again, soft and indifferent, erasing all footprints equally.
