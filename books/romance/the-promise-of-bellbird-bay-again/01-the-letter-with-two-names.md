## 1. The Letter with Two Names

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which Cyrus Hartwell would later decide was the universe's idea of a joke. Nothing important ever happened on Tuesdays. Tuesdays were for grocery runs and reheated soup and the small, gray maintenance of a life built entirely out of caution.

She almost threw it away.

It sat on her counter for two hours among the junk mail—a water bill, a flyer for a gutter-cleaning service—before the weight of it bothered her. Not the paper weight. The other kind. The envelope was heavy the way a held breath is heavy, and the return address was printed, not handwritten, in the crisp serif of a law firm: *Whitcombe & Parr, Attorneys at Law, Bellbird Bay.*

Bellbird Bay. She hadn't seen those two words together in three years, and seeing them now was like hearing an old song from a room away—recognizable, unwelcome, tugging at something she'd stitched shut.

She opened it with a paring knife because tearing it with her hands felt too much like surrendering to urgency.

*Dear Ms. Hartwell,* the letter began, in the bloodless prose lawyers use when they know what they're delivering is a live grenade. *We write regarding the estate of the late Margaret Ashford Thorne, deceased, and matters arising from the probate of her holdings...*

Cyrus read the first paragraph standing up. She read the second one sitting down, the kitchen chair scraping against the linoleum like something protesting.

*...records indicate that the parcel known as the Ashford Orchard, comprising eleven acres and associated structures, is held in joint tenancy between Elias James Thorne and one Cyrus Wren Hartwell. As you are named on the deed...*

Named on the deed.

She read it four times. The words didn't rearrange themselves into sense. *Joint tenancy. Cyrus Wren Hartwell. Eleven acres.* Her name, sitting there in legalese, attached to a piece of earth she hadn't stood on in six years, beside a name she hadn't spoken aloud since a courtroom made it into something that tasted like ash.

Elias Thorne.

The letter went on to explain, with lawyerly delicacy, that the late Mrs. Thorne—Elias's grandmother, though Cyrus had known her as Miss Margie, the woman who'd let a teenage Cyrus practice cello in her parlor while the whole valley smelled of apple blossom—had amended her will in the final months of her life. The amendment had apparently never been properly recorded, a clerical oversight, and now the probate court required all parties to either confirm or relinquish their interest in the property.

*Should you wish to divest yourself of your share, arrangements can be made promptly.*

Divest. Such a clean word for it. As though her name were a stain that could be laundered out of the record, the way everyone had spent three years trying to launder it out of everything else.

Cyrus set the letter down. Her hands were not shaking, she noted with a kind of clinical pride. Three years of practice had taught her hands not to shake. That had been the whole project, really—the rebuilding of Cyrus Hartwell into a woman whose hands stayed steady, whose voice stayed level, who lived in a rented cottage two towns over from Bellbird Bay and taught beginning piano to children who had no idea their teacher had once played the cello for two thousand people, most of whom now knew her name for entirely different reasons.

The phone sat on the counter. She did not call the number at the bottom of the letter. She did not call anyone. What she did, after a long time standing at the window watching the gray sea-light move across the harbor, was go to her bedroom and take a suitcase down from the top of the closet.

She told herself it was a simple errand. Drive to Bellbird Bay, find the lawyer's office, sign whatever needed signing, and be home by dark with her name scrubbed off the last piece of her old life. Thirty minutes of paperwork. A signature, maybe two. She'd been signing away pieces of herself for three years now—her career first, then her apartment, then the instrument itself, sold to a conservatory student who'd looked at her with such naked awe that Cyrus had left the shop before she could say something unforgivable.

One more signature. That was all.

She packed the suitcase anyway.

* * *

The road into Bellbird Bay had not changed, which felt like an ambush. Cyrus had prepared herself for change—for new storefronts, a repaved highway, some evidence that the world had continued without her. Instead the road unspooled exactly as it always had: the climb through the pines where the light came down in green coins, the first glimpse of water through the trees, the long curve past the shuttered cannery with its rusted sign. Her body knew this road the way it knew a piece of music, and it played itself without her permission, every turn arriving a half-second before she remembered it.

The orchard was at the end of it, past the last farmhouse, where the paved road gave up and became gravel. She saw the trees before she saw anything else—rows of them marching up the hillside, apples and pears and the old quince at the boundary, and beyond them the blue slab of the bay. It was late March. The trees were still bare, or nearly—there was a haze of green at the tips of the branches, the first tentative grammar of blossom, and the whole hillside looked like a held note.

She had expected the orchard to be dead. That was what the lawyer's letter had implied, what she'd built the whole trip around: a dying property, a signature, an exit. She had rehearsed the transaction in the car the way she used to rehearse difficult passages—*I'd like to relinquish my interest, here are the documents, thank you, no, I won't be staying.*

What she had not rehearsed was smoke.

There was smoke rising from the chimney of the little stone house at the orchard's heart—the house where Miss Margie had lived, where Cyrus had drunk cocoa at that kitchen table at fifteen, her fingers still stiff from cold practice rooms, while Margie told her she had fire in her hands and shouldn't waste it on being careful.

Someone was living in Margie's house.

Cyrus pulled the car onto the gravel shoulder and sat with the engine ticking down and her heart doing something arrhythmic and unwelcome. She knew, in the way you know a chord's final note before it sounds, who it would be. The deed had told her. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it are different disciplines entirely, and she sat in the car for a long moment, hands on the wheel at ten and two, breathing the way her old teacher had taught her to breathe before walking onstage.

Then she got out, because the alternative was sitting there until dark, and she had not driven ninety minutes to be defeated by a parked car.

The gate was new—she noticed that. The old one had sagged and groaned; this one swung smooth on oiled hinges. The path to the house was raked. The trees nearest the lane had been pruned with a competence she recognized and resented, because she had spent one long, disastrous summer learning to prune beside Elias Thorne, and she knew what his cuts looked like: clean, angled, a little too exact, as if the trees were being graded.

She was almost to the door when she heard it.

A cello.

Not a recording—she could tell the difference the way she could tell the difference between rain and a recording of rain. Someone was playing, badly, in the room just beyond the door. The intonation wandered. The bow arm was all wrong, locked at the elbow, and the piece was—she stopped on the path, the gravel crunching under her boot the only sound louder than her own pulse—it was the Swan. The Saint-Saëns. Played slowly, laboriously, by someone who loved it and could not yet do it justice.

The door was open. Not wide—ajar, letting out woodsmoke and the smell of coffee and that specific, unmistakable sound. And through the gap she could see the back of a man sitting in Margie's old chair by the window, a cello—*her* old student cello, she realized, the one with the chip in the scroll, the one that had been in the attic of this house for twenty years—braced between his knees, his dark head bent over it in concentration.

Elias Thorne had broader shoulders than he'd had at twenty-four. His hair was longer. But she would have known the set of those shoulders anywhere, the way they leaned into the instrument like a man leaning into a wind.

Cyrus stood on the path in the cold March air and watched, through a door he didn't know was open, the man she had spent three years hating play the cello like a beginner.

He stopped. Started the phrase again. Got it slightly less wrong. She heard him mutter something—she caught the shape of it, *slower, you idiot*—and something happened in her chest that she refused to name. It was not warmth. It was absolutely not warmth. It was probably indigestion.

She knocked. Two raps on the doorframe, crisp, official, the knock of a woman on business.

The music stopped. The silence that followed had a texture to it, dense and ringing, like the air after a final chord.

Then footsteps, and the door swung wider, and there he was—Elias Thorne in the flesh, taller than her memory, with lines around his eyes that hadn't been there before and a bow still held loosely in one hand like a man who'd forgotten to put down a tool. He looked at her, and she watched recognition move through him in stages: confusion, disbelief, and then something raw and unguarded that he tried to close off a half-second too late.

"Cyrus," he said. Her name in his mouth sounded exactly the way it had in the courtroom. Like a verdict.

"Thorne." She kept her voice level, kept her hands at her sides, kept every one of her three years of careful construction intact. "I got a letter. It seems your grandmother left me a piece of your orchard."

"She wasn't only my grandmother." He said it quietly, without accusation, and it landed harder than a shout would have.

"I'm here to sign it away." She lifted her chin. "Whatever the process is, I'll do it today, and then you won't have to see me again."

Something crossed his face—too fast to name, gone before she could read it. He stepped back from the doorway, and the light from inside fell across the threshold between them, and she saw, over his shoulder, the room she had once known so well: Margie's kitchen table, Margie's blue curtains, and on the wall where Margie's family photographs had hung, a single framed program. She couldn't read it from the path. But she knew its shape. She knew the shape of a concert program the way she knew the shape of her own hands.

"Come in," Elias said, and his voice was careful, and his eyes were not. "There's coffee. And there are things you should probably know before you sign anything."

"I don't want to know things."

"I know," he said. "You never did. But Margie didn't leave you eleven acres by accident, Cyrus. And I don't think she left them to the two of us by accident, either."

The wind came down off the hill then, through the bare trees, carrying the faint green smell of the first buds, and somewhere up the slope a bellbird started its ragged, beautiful song. Cyrus stood on the threshold of the house where she had learned to play, hating him, hating the letter, hating the way her heart had picked up tempo like a metronome no one had asked for.

"Thirty minutes," she said finally, stepping inside. "I'm signing, and I'm leaving."

Elias set the cello down in its case with the gentleness of a man handling something breakable, and closed the lid, and said, "We'll see."
