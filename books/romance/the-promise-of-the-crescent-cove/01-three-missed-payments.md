## 1. Three Missed Payments

The oven timer had stopped meaning anything hours ago. Mirabel Delacroix worked by rhythm now, by the muscle memory of his hands and the low amber glow of the café's kitchen lights, folding butter into flour the way his grandmother had taught him before the family decided he was a stranger. Outside the window, Crescent Cove lay black and breathing, the tide coming in with that soft, patient sound it made, like someone waiting to be let in.

Three envelopes sat on the counter where he could see them. He'd propped them against the sugar canister on purpose—punishment, motivation, he wasn't sure which. The landlord's letterhead was a particular shade of cream that he'd come to dread the way other people dreaded the smell of hospitals. *Final notice.* The words had stopped frightening him somewhere around the second envelope and had settled instead into a kind of dull arithmetic: eleven days, four thousand two hundred dollars, and a café that took in less on a good Saturday than the rent demanded in a week.

So he baked. It was the only prayer he knew.

At two in the morning the kitchen smelled like brown butter and cardamom, like the kouign-amann his grandmother used to slip him under the table at family dinners, back when the Delacroix name still meant something warm. He'd named the café *Sel & Sucre* after her recipe book, and sometimes he wondered if that had been an act of love or an act of defiance—carrying her into exile with him, one laminated dough at a time.

He pulled a tray of canelés, their dark ridges glistening, and set them to cool, and that was when the phone buzzed against the flour-dusted counter. His mother's name on the screen. He almost didn't answer. He almost always almost didn't answer.

"Mirabel." Her voice was smooth as poured cream, the voice she used for boardrooms and for him, though he could never decide which category he fell into. "You're coming to the reunion. Crescent Cove, the estate, the last weekend of August. Your grandmother's eightieth."

"It's a bit early to be calling about August."

"It's a bit early for you to still be pretending you have a choice." A pause, the sound of ice shifting in a glass. "The whole family will be there. Your aunts. Your cousins. Julian and his wife." Julian. His brother, who had inherited the family's approval along with everything else. "You'll want to bring someone."

Mirabel closed his eyes. Flour clung to his lashes. "Mother—"

"Everyone asks, Mirabel. Everyone will ask. A man your age, alone at every gathering, looking like—" she exhaled, delicately, the way she did when she was choosing between cruelties, "—like something the tide left behind. Bring someone. Anyone. Make it easier on yourself."

The line went soft with static, or maybe with the ocean, and he said, "I'll think about it," which in their family's private language meant *no* and they both knew it, and then she said, "Wear something that fits," which meant *I love you, badly*, and hung up.

He stood there a long moment with the phone against his chest. Through the window, the first grey suggestion of dawn was touching the water. Somewhere down the beach, a gull complained about the state of the world, and Mirabel found he agreed with it entirely.

* * *

The regular came in at seven-fifteen, the way he always did, when the light came slanting through the front windows and turned the dust motes into something almost holy. Theo. That was all Mirabel knew of him—first name only, offered once with a smile that had made Mirabel forget, for a full three seconds, what he was doing with his hands.

Theo ordered alone. He always ordered alone. Black coffee and whatever Mirabel had made that morning, eaten slowly at the corner table by the window, sometimes with a book, sometimes with nothing but the view of the cove and an expression Mirabel had never been able to read—like a man memorizing a room he intended to leave.

"The usual," Theo said, settling onto the stool at the counter because the corner table was still stacked with chairs from last night's cleaning. His eyes went to the trays of pastry, still warm, still impossible. "You were baking all night again."

"What makes you say that?"

"You've got flour on your jaw. Right here." He touched his own face, an inch below his ear, and Mirabel's hand went up before he could stop it, brushing at skin that was, of course, clean. Theo's mouth curved. "There wasn't any. But now I know you were up all night."

"That's entrapment."

"That's observation." Theo accepted the coffee, wrapped both hands around the cup, and looked at Mirabel with that unhurried attention of his, the attention that made the whole café feel smaller and warmer than it was. "You look exhausted, Mirabel."

"Thank you. Flattery will get you everywhere."

"I mean it." And there it was—that perceptiveness again, gentle and relentless, like a tide working at a seawall. "You look like a man carrying something heavy and pretending it's a tray of croissants."

Mirabel laughed despite himself, a short, cracked sound, and turned to the espresso machine for something to do with his hands. The three envelopes were still propped against the sugar canister. Theo was looking directly at them. Mirabel shifted his body to block the view, too late, too obviously, and the silence between them filled up with everything he wasn't saying.

"Rough month?" Theo asked quietly.

"Rough year." He said it before he could dress it up. That was the trouble with exhaustion—it pried the lids off things. "Rough—" He stopped. Wiped the counter that was already clean.

The café was empty except for them. The ocean breathed against the seawall. And Mirabel heard himself say, in a voice that didn't sound like his own, half-drunk on thirty-one hours without sleep: "How would you feel about being my boyfriend?"

Theo's eyebrows rose, but he didn't laugh. That was the first miracle. He turned on the stool, fully, giving Mirabel the whole of his attention, and waited.

"Not—" Mirabel's ears were burning. He gripped the counter. "Not really. Fake. Fictional. A weekend engagement, so to speak. There's a family thing. A reunion, at my grandmother's estate, and my mother has made it very clear that arriving alone is a form of surrender, and I—" He exhaled. "I have spent six years not surrendering to these people. I'm not going to start over a seating chart."

"And you'd trust me to play the part?"

"I trust that you show up every morning at seven-fifteen and you're kind to Mrs. Okafor even when she takes forty minutes to choose a scone. I trust that you listen more than you talk, which in my experience means you're either very wise or very dangerous, and frankly at this point I can't afford to be picky." He was babbling. He could hear himself babbling and couldn't stop. "I'd pay you. I can't pay you, actually, but I'd—there's free pastry. There's unlimited pastry. There's a room at the estate that doesn't have a view but has a very dramatic fireplace."

"Mirabel." Theo said his name like it was worth saying slowly, and the babbling stopped. "What's the family like?"

"Beautiful," Mirabel said. "The way a riptide is beautiful."

Something moved behind Theo's eyes then—quick, unreadable, gone before Mirabel could name it. Theo lifted his coffee, and over the rim his gaze was steady and warm and entirely too knowing.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"I'll be your boyfriend." A pause, delicate as spun sugar. "The fictional kind. For now."

Mirabel should have felt relief. Instead he felt the strange vertigo of a man who has just opened a door he can't explain—and through it, faintly, the sound of the sea.

* * *

That night, after close, Mirabel found a note tucked under the sugar canister, in handwriting so elegant it looked rehearsed:

*Terms to be negotiated. Starting with: you tell me why a Delacroix bakes alone in a cove everyone else abandoned. —T*

He read it three times. Then he laughed—really laughed, alone in the dark kitchen, flour in his hair and eviction letters on the counter and, for the first time in years, something that felt dangerously like hope rising in him like dough, warm and alive and impossible to press back down.
