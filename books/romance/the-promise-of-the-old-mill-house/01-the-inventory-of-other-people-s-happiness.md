## 1. The Inventory of Other People's Happiness

The rain began, as catastrophes at Galloway & Finch weddings always did, at the precise moment it could do the most damage.

Astrid stood beneath the white tent on the lawn of the Cabot estate in Back Bay, watching the first fat drops strike the silk canopy overhead like a drummer testing his timing. Inside, two hundred guests were finishing their champagne and preparing to be moved—by the string quartet, by the candlelight, by the speech he had written for the groom that contained exactly one joke about law school and one devastating line about how Marcus Whitfield had waited thirty-four years for a woman worth being early for.

It was, by every measurable standard, perfect.

Astrid had built perfection the way other men built houses: stud by stud, invoice by invoice. He knew the weight of a napkin folded just so. He knew which cousins required seating adjustments after the third divorce and which grandmothers would weep if seated too near the band. He knew that the bride's mother would need a glass of prosecco at 4:15 precisely, or the entire western wing of the family would curdle like milk left in sun.

"Rain," said Priya, his associate, materializing at his elbow with a tablet clutched like a shield. "Forty percent chance, they said. Forty."

"The forecast is a suggestion," Astrid said. "The tent is a fact."

He had ordered the tent doubled anyway. He always did. Somewhere in the ledger of his professional life there was a column labeled *paranoia* and it was, he liked to tell clients, the only thing standing between their wedding and a story their grandchildren would tell with wincing.

The rain intensified. The quartet switched, without missing a beat, from Vivaldi to something more forgiving. The guests oohed—the good kind of ooh, the *isn't this romantic* ooh—and Astrid felt the familiar machinery engage inside him: the part that converted chaos into choreography, disaster into anecdote.

*What a story,* they'd say later. *Remember when it poured? Remember how the candles flickered and everyone huddled together under the tents?*

People didn't remember weather. They remembered how it felt. That was the whole business, really. Astrid sold feelings with a start time and an open bar.

* * *

The ceremony itself went off without a hitch, which should have been enough. It usually was.

Marcus cried at the right moment—not the ugly cry, but the single shining tear that photographs beautifully. Elena's father walked her down the aisle at exactly the tempo Astrid had rehearsed with him twice in the hotel ballroom, once with a coat rack standing in for the bride. The vows were the ones Astrid had helped them draft over three sessions of wine and nervous laughter, and when Marcus said *I choose you, not once but daily*, there was an audible catch in the crowd, a collective inhale, the sound of two hundred people briefly believing in something.

Astrid stood at the back with his headset on and his clipboard against his chest, and he felt nothing.

Not bitterness. Not envy, exactly. Something quieter and more alarming than either: recognition. He knew these vows the way a locksmith knows locks—from the outside, mechanically, without ever having been on the wrong side of one himself.

*I choose you, not once but daily.*

Eleven years ago, a boy had made a promise under a broken mill wheel in Millbrook, Vermont, population four thousand, and then gotten into a car the next morning and driven south until the map ran out of reasons to stop. The boy hadn't chosen daily. The boy hadn't chosen at all. The boy had done arithmetic instead—his family's debts against Wren Halloway's future—and decided she was better off subtracted.

Astrid Galloway, thirty-three, celebrated planner of other people's happiness, had built an entire career on the premise that love, properly managed, could be made safe from weather. And here was the secret he carried to every event, pressed flat beneath his tuxedo like a fold in the lining: he no longer believed a word of it.

"Dance floor opens in six minutes," Priya said. "And the mother of the bride is asking whether the candles are soy."

"They're soy."

"She wants to confirm."

"Tell her I personally blessed each one."

Priya smiled and went. She was very good. They were all very good. That was the trouble with excellence—it kept you moving fast enough that you never had to stand still and look at what you'd built.

* * *

The recriminations arrived at 9:40 p.m., as recriminations do, wearing the face of the mother of the groom.

"It's coming down sideways," Mrs. Whitfield announced, appearing beside him at the bar with the expression of a woman who had paid a great deal of money for a garden ceremony and received, instead, a nautical experience. "The photos. The *photos*, Astrid. We were promised golden hour."

"We were promised a contingency plan," Astrid said pleasantly, "and you have one. Look at your left."

To the left, under a second tent he had insisted upon and been called extravagant for, the photographer had assembled a backdrop of amber lanterns and trailing greenery. The bride was laughing in it. The light was, frankly, better than golden hour would have been.

Mrs. Whitfield looked. Her shoulders descended half an inch. "Well," she said. "Well." And then, because mothers of grooms are constitutionally incapable of pure surrender: "It's not what we planned."

"No," Astrid agreed. "It's better. Give it until the album comes back."

She swept off, mollified, and Astrid remained at the bar holding a glass of champagne he had no intention of drinking, and thought: *eleven years.* Eleven years of golden hours manufactured under tarps. Eleven years of first dances timed to the second and toasts edited for length. Eleven years of standing at the edge of other people's joy with a radio in his ear, making sure the sparklers ignited on cue.

He was the best in Boston. Three magazines had said so. Clients booked him eighteen months out. His calendar was a fortress, and inside the fortress lived a man who had not returned to his hometown since the summer he was twenty-two, who had not answered so much as a Christmas card from anyone in Millbrook, and who could not hear the phrase *I promise* spoken aloud—even in rehearsal, even by strangers—even now, eleven years later, without something in his chest turning over like a stone in a streambed.

"Astrid." Marcus Whitfield appeared, glowing, damp-cuffed, radiant with the specific happiness of a man who has just married up. "Man. The speeches. My mother's already crying and it's not even the father-daughter dance. How do you *do* it?"

"I sell weather insurance," Astrid said, "and hope."

Marcus laughed, clapped him on the shoulder, and went back to his wife, and Astrid watched him go the way you watch a train leave a station you're not on.

* * *

His hotel room at midnight smelled of lilies, because the hotel had lilies everywhere and Astrid had long ago stopped requesting otherwise. He sat on the edge of the bed still in his dress shirt, tie undone, shoes off, and opened his laptop out of habit rather than necessity. The inbox filled. Vendor confirmations. A seating chart dispute for a September wedding in Newport. An invoice from a florist with the emotional range of a hedge trimmer.

And then, third from the top, an email from an address he did not know: *Hollis & Prue, Solicitors, Millbrook, Vermont.*

He almost deleted it. Some instinct—call it superstition, call it the animal brain that knows a smell before it knows why—made his thumb hover.

*Mr. Astrid Galloway:*

*We write regarding the restoration of the property known as the old Aldous Mill House, Route 9, Millbrook, currently underway under the direction of Halloway Design Studio. During structural work on the east wall, workmen discovered several items sealed within the wall cavity, among them correspondence addressed to you. As the named party, we are obligated to forward notice of its existence pending your instruction.*

*The letter bears a postmark dated August 14th of this year—*

Astrid stopped reading. Read it again. The date swam.

*—of 2014.*

Eleven years ago. The week he left. The week everything ended. He had written a letter that night—he remembered the pen, the cheap paper, the lamplight in his ruined kitchen—but he had never mailed it. He was certain. Certain the way you are certain of your own hands. He had sealed it and slipped it—

Slipped it where?

The memory moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice: present, unreachable, refusing to come up.

*Please advise at your earliest convenience whether you wish the item forwarded to the above address, held for collection, or otherwise disposed of.*

*Yours faithfully,*
*R. Prue*

Astrid set the laptop on the bed beside him and looked at it for a long time. The rain had followed him from Boston; it ticked against the window like a patient finger.

Wren Halloway's studio was restoring the mill house. Wren Halloway, whose name he had not spoken aloud in eleven years and had thought, conservatively, ten thousand times. Workers on her site had pulled a letter out of a wall—a letter in his handwriting, postmarked the week he vanished from her life without goodbye—and the universe, with its usual flair for timing, had decided that eleven years was quite long enough.

He thought about replying in the morning. *Dispose of it,* he could write. *Burn it. I have no claim on that house or anything in it.* It would be easy. He was excellent at easy; he had a waiting list.

Instead, before he could talk himself out of it, Astrid typed:

*Hold it. I'll collect it myself.*

He stared at the sent message, heart going like a fist on a door, and understood that he had just done the one thing his entire carefully constructed life was designed to prevent.

He was going back to Millbrook.
