## 1. The Ghost in the Margins

The sentence Kestrel Galloway was deleting had taken him forty minutes to write and would take four seconds to destroy.

*And then I understood, for the first time, that ambition was a kind of love.*

He read it twice. It was good. It was the best line in Chapter Seven. It was also, unmistakably, his — the cadence of it, the way it turned on itself like a wave deciding to come back. Marcus Vane did not think in waves. Marcus Vane thought in ladders: rung, rung, summit, foreword by someone famous.

Kestrel selected the sentence and pressed delete.

"That's the third one this week," said Priya's voice from his phone, propped against the salt shaker at his elbow. She was on speaker because she'd refused to hang up until he agreed to something, and they both knew it. "You keep writing beautiful things into other people's books and then murdering them."

"They weren't right for Marcus."

"Nothing's right for Marcus. Marcus is a man who describes sunsets as 'a strong visual asset.' You're ghostwriting a memoir for someone who has never once been haunted by anything." Priya's face on the screen was doing the thing her face did — the tilted head, the patient eyes of a woman who had spent thirty-one years loving a brother who treated love like a subscription service he kept forgetting to renew. "Which is why you're coming to the reunion."

"I'm not."

"You are. Gran's turning eighty. The whole family's descending on the Saltline Inn like a very polite invasion. There will be cousins you've never met and an aunt who thinks Wi-Fi is a government program. It'll be charming."

"It'll be *Hollowmere*," Kestrel said, and heard his own voice go flat as slate.

Priya was quiet for a moment. She knew, of course. Everyone knew, in the way families know things — not the details, but the shape of the hole. Ten years since he'd last stood in that town. Ten years since the cliffs, the rain, the sound the world made when it came apart. "Kes—"

"Don't." He softened it with a smile he'd practiced in mirrors until it looked load-bearing. "I have a deadline. Marcus needs me."

"Marcus needs a typist. You need a life. And you need a plus-one, because if you show up alone Aunt Devi will spend the entire weekend introducing you to her dentist's son, and I cannot protect you from that. Nobody can. He does magic tricks, Kes. At dinner. Unprompted."

Kestrel laughed despite himself — a real one, brief and startled out of him like a bird off a wire. Then he caught sight of his own reflection in the dark window of the café: a man in a good coat, alone at a table set for two, laughing at a phone. The laugh died politely.

"Priya. I love you. I'm not going back there."

"You're going back there," she said, "and here's the thing — I already told Gran you'd bring someone. So now you either invent a boyfriend or you let Aunt Devi work her magic." A pause, weaponized. "Your move, ghostwriter."

She hung up before he could argue, which was her superpower.

Kestrel sat looking at the deleted sentence, at the cursor blinking in the space where something true used to be. That was the job, really. That was the whole job. He made his living being the voice in other people's mouths, the hand behind other people's names. Twelve books under pseudonyms, three memoirs under other men's names, one novel — his only novel — published a decade ago under a name he no longer answered to, remaindered within the year, and never mentioned since.

Invisibility wasn't loneliness, he told himself, gathering his laptop. Invisibility was *safety*. Ghosts don't drown. They just drift.

He almost believed it. He'd had ten years of practice.

* * *

The bookshop on Wexford Lane smelled of dust and bergamot, and Kestrel went there the way other people went to church — irregularly, guiltily, and always feeling better afterward.

He was in the poetry section, not buying anything, just standing near the Neruda with his hands in his pockets, when a voice beside him said, "You've been reading that same spine for six minutes. Either it's the most fascinating spine in London or you're hiding from someone."

Kestrel turned.

The man was leaning against the shelf with the ease of someone who had never once worried about taking up space. Dark curls pushed back like he'd lost an argument with them that morning and made peace with it. A canvas tote slung across his body, stuffed with paperbacks, a paperback of which was actively escaping. He wore a cardigan the color of strong tea and an expression of frank, cheerful curiosity.

"Hiding," Kestrel admitted, before his professionalism could catch up with his mouth.

"From?"

"My sister. On the phone. She wants me to attend a family reunion and bring a boyfriend I don't have."

The man winced in sympathy. "Ah. The invented-boyfriend gambit. Classic. My sister once told our mother she was engaged to get out of moving home. The wedding had to be *cancelled*. There was a venue deposit. There were canapés."

"There were *canapés*?"

"Salmon. Tragic waste." He stuck out a hand. "Wren Ashby."

Kestrel hesitated half a heartbeat — long enough to consider giving a fake name, which was his instinct, his native tongue — and then, absurdly, didn't. "Kestrel Galloway."

"Kestrel," Wren repeated, tasting it. "Like the bird. The one that hovers in the wind without flapping."

"That's the one."

"Suspends itself over open ground. Waiting." Wren smiled, and it was a dangerous smile, warm and unhurried, the kind that made a person feel *seen*, which Kestrel had built an entire career around avoiding. "That tracks."

They ended up in the café next door because Wren declared that anyone fleeing familial pressure deserved tea, and Kestrel — who had planned to go home to a silent flat and a chapter of Marcus Vane's fake soul — found himself sitting across from a stranger while the afternoon went golden outside the window.

"So let me get this straight," Wren said, stirring honey into his cup with the focus of a chemist. "One weekend. Coastal town. Family reunion. You need a boyfriend so convincing that your aunt retires from matchmaking on the spot."

"Essentially."

"And your actual objection to attending is...?" Wren tilted his head. "Because most people don't need a decoy boyfriend to skip their gran's birthday. Most people just say no."

Kestrel looked at his tea. The truth rose in him like groundwater — *the town, the cliffs, the boy who fell, the sentence I rehearsed for ten years and never said* — and he capped it, the way he always did, with something professional and smooth.

"Complicated history," he said. "It's easier with company."

"Mm," said Wren, in the tone of a man filing *complicated history* away in a drawer marked *later*. And then, casually, catastrophically: "I'll do it."

Kestrel blinked. "Do what."

"The weekend. Your boyfriend. I'm between jobs — I teach, mostly, and it's half-term — and I've always wondered what I'd be like as someone else for a few days." He counted on his fingers. "Terms: you cover travel and lodging, we agree on a meet-cute story that holds up under cross-examination by at least two aunties, and no lying about anything that matters. Fake boyfriend, real honesty. Those are my rates."

"That's—" Kestrel searched for the flaw, the trap, the reason sensible men didn't hire strangers from poetry sections. "That's a terrible business model."

"It's a *wonderful* business model. I get a seaside holiday and a story; you get freedom from the magician dentist's son." Wren leaned forward, chin on his hand, and his eyes were quick and kind and far too perceptive. "Unless, of course, you'd rather go alone and hover over open ground, waiting."

The words landed somewhere Kestrel hadn't authorized access to.

"One weekend," Kestrel said finally. "Strictly transactional."

"Strictly," Wren agreed, and shook his hand, and his grip was warm and certain, and Kestrel noticed — with distant professional alarm — that it felt less like closing a deal than opening a door.

* * *

They settled the details over the following week: the story (met at a bookshop, six months ago, first date at a curry house — "specificity sells," Wren said, "aunties interrogate like customs officers"), the hand-holding protocol ("initiate freely, withdraw instantly, consent is the whole act"), the sleeping arrangements ("two beds, wall between, I snore like a haunted accordion").

Kestrel told himself the flutter in his chest on the train north was dread. Dread of Hollowmere, of the cliffs, of the past waiting exactly where he'd left it. That was a clean, familiar fear, and he knew how to carry it.

He did not examine the other flutter — the smaller, warmer one — that arrived whenever Wren pointed out the window and said, *look, the sea*, as though Kestrel might otherwise have missed it.

The Saltline Inn appeared at the end of the lane just as the light went amber, all weathered shingles and lit windows, and there on the lawn was Priya, waving both arms like a woman signaling rescue aircraft, surrounded by a crowd of Galloways already craning their necks toward the arriving couple.

"Ready?" Wren murmured, sliding his hand into Kestrel's as naturally as breathing.

"No," Kestrel said honestly.

"Perfect. Neither am I. Best way to start a lie." Wren squeezed his fingers once, then raised his free hand in greeting, beaming. "Hello, family! We're hopelessly in love!"

Laughter rippled across the lawn. Priya's eyebrows shot up with naked delight. And Kestrel, smiling his practiced smile, stepped onto the grass of the town he'd sworn never to see again —

— and across the lawn, half-shadowed by an old apple tree, a tall figure in a grey jacket turned toward the laughter.

Kestrel's heart stopped, restarted wrong.

Ten years. A scarred jaw catching the last of the light. Eyes he would know anywhere, even changed, even older, even *impossible*.

Theo.

Alive.

The ground tilted. Wren's hand tightened around his — "Kestrel? You've gone white" — but Kestrel couldn't answer, couldn't breathe, could only stand there on the lawn of the Saltline Inn while every carefully buried year clawed its way to the surface, and Theo Ashcombe began, slowly, to walk toward him.
