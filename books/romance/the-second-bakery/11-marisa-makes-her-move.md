## 11. Marisa Makes Her Move

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Ivo would later decide was fitting. Tuesdays were the days Marisa Duarte did her costings — he'd learned the rhythm of her weeks by then, three months into the interviews, the way you learn the tides of a coastline you're beginning to suspect is not entirely honest with you. On costing days she sat in the small office above the flagship bakery with a ledger open and her reading glasses pushed up into her hair like a crown she kept forgetting to wear, and she was, in those hours, at her most unguarded. Numbers didn't flatter. She liked that about them.

So when her assistant called and said, *Ms. Duarte would like to see you this afternoon, just the two of you, no recorder*, Ivo knew it wasn't about chapter nine.

He took the stairs rather than wait for the ancient elevator, and came out into the corridor that smelled of yeast and caramelized sugar and, beneath it, the faint industrial tang of the proofing room's steam. Through the glass wall of the office he could see Marisa already seated, but not behind the desk. She'd arranged the two armchairs by the window instead, angled toward each other over a low table where a coffee pot waited beside two cups and a plate of the brioche her kitchen made only for staff. It was a staging. He recognized it as one because he had spent his career recognizing them — the novelist's instinct for scene-setting never quite left a man who now wrote other people's lives for a living. She wanted this to feel intimate. Informal. Like two friends talking rather than an employer and a hired pen.

That was the first thing that made his stomach tighten.

"Ivo." She rose to pour the coffee herself, waving off the offer of help. "Sit. You take it black, yes? I've watched you drain four cups in an interview and never once ask for sugar."

"You noticed that?"

"I notice everything in my bakery." She handed him the cup and settled into the opposite chair, folding her legs beneath her, which was something he'd never seen her do in their formal sessions. There, she sat like a portrait of herself: spine straight, hands arranged, the legend of Marisa Duarte occupying her body like a tenant with a long lease. Here, by the window, with the afternoon light catching the flour dust suspended in the air between them, she looked briefly like a woman in her sixties who was tired. Then the look passed, or was put away, and the legend returned. "How are you finding chapter nine? The Paris years."

"They're coming along."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have today."

She laughed — a real laugh, short and surprised out of her, and for a moment he almost forgot what he'd come to suspect. Almost. But the honey cake sat in his memory like a stone dropped in still water, its ripples reaching every conversation they'd had since. *My mother's recipe,* Beatriz had said, and Ivo had tasted his own childhood in the crumb, and neither fact could be reconciled with the polished legend Marisa told him every Tuesday and Thursday afternoon: that she alone, at nineteen, working a single summer in a rented kitchen, had invented the signature loaf that built an empire.

"Let me ask you something," Marisa said, leaning forward, elbows on her knees. "And I want you to answer honestly, because I'll know if you don't. Are you happy with this book?"

Ivo turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "Define happy."

"The book I'm going to be proud of. The book that makes my granddaughter cry in a good way. The book that—" she waved a hand at the window, at the bakery floor below where customers moved through the warm light like fish through sunlit water, "—explains all this. Where it came from. What it cost."

"What it cost," Ivo repeated carefully.

"Everything costs." Her eyes were sharp suddenly, the tiredness gone as if it had been a trick of the light, and maybe it had been; maybe the tiredness was itself a performance, another angle of the legend, the wise matriarch allowing herself one human moment before returning to business. "You know that better than anyone. Your novel—"

"We're not here to talk about my novel."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "We're here to talk about yours later, maybe. If things go well."

* * *

She let the sentence sit there while she poured herself more coffee she didn't drink, and Ivo understood that the meeting had properly begun. Everything before had been the overture.

"Here's what I've been thinking," Marisa said. "The manuscript is good. Better than good — you write me like someone I'd want to be, which is a rarer gift than writing someone as they are. But it's taking time. And time—" she tapped the ledger on the desk behind her without looking at it, "—time is the one ingredient I can't buy more of. My doctor wants me to slow down. My board wants a succession plan. My granddaughter keeps asking when the book will be ready so she can read about the day I invented the Duarte loaf." A pause, delicate as a pulled thread. "I'd like it delivered early. Six weeks early. And in exchange—"

Here she reached into the drawer of the side table — she had planned even this, the drawer stocked in advance — and laid a folder on the low table between them. Not a contract. Just papers. He could see figures through the thinness of the top sheet.

"—I'm prepared to make you a partner in the enterprise. Not literally, of course, not in the legal sense. But a stake. A percentage of the memoir's earnings, in perpetuity, plus a development fund for your own work. Enough that you'd never have to ghost again unless you *wanted* to. Enough that the novel—" and there it was, the word landing soft as a knife going in, "—gets written on your own clock instead of nobody's."

Ivo did not touch the folder. His hands stayed wrapped around the coffee cup, and he was grateful for the warmth of it, because his fingers had gone cold.

"There's a condition," he said.

"One condition." She smiled, and it was the smile from the book jacket, the one that had sold a million loaves and a million breakfast-table fantasies of the woman who baked them. "Keep the family stuff vague. The early years. Before the flagship, before any of it. Write it beautifully — you always do — but write it in soft focus. Founding myths aren't meant to be audited, Ivo. Every family has a version of events. Mine happens to be the one with a bakery attached."

"And if the version has holes in it?"

"Then patch them with light." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't. "You're a writer. You know better than anyone that memory is a draft, and drafts get revised. What I'm asking for isn't a lie. It's a final edit."

* * *

He walked home the long way, which was stupid, because the long way passed the shuttered building — the Almeida building, his grandmother's building, its windows papered over now for thirty years except where teenagers had peeled back a corner to let the streetlight in. He stood across the street in the dark and looked at that lit sliver and thought about the folder sitting in Marisa's office, the numbers on the first page, the zeros marching in rows like well-behaved soldiers.

The debts were not dramatic. That was the humiliating part. No gambling, no disasters, no single catastrophic mistake he could point to and say *there, that's where it went wrong*. Just the slow arithmetic of a decade: the advance that hadn't covered the years the novel took, the rent on an apartment sized for a life he'd assumed he'd have by forty, the credit card that had become a second income, the loan from his sister that he repaid in installments and shame. The number was not enormous. It was merely larger than anything his integrity could earn in the same amount of time.

And Marisa's offer would erase it in a stroke. Not reduce it. Erase it. Plus the fund. Plus the freedom. Plus the novel, finally, written in daylight instead of stolen hours, the book he'd been carrying around inside him like a swallowed stone for ten years.

All it cost was soft focus.

He thought about his grandmother's hands. She was eighty-six now, and her hands had the tremor of great age, but she still kneaded bread on Sundays when she was strong enough, still worked the dough the way her mother had taught her in the kitchen that the microfilm photograph showed — both grandmothers, young and laughing, flour on their forearms, standing side by side in one kitchen before there were two bakeries and a silence between families that had outlasted the people who started it. He thought about how his grandmother never spoke of it, how the subject simply wasn't raised at Almeida tables, how the absence had a shape, the way a missing tooth has a shape you keep touching with your tongue.

He thought about Beatriz's face when she'd found the clipping. He hadn't been there — she'd described it to him afterward, in the uneasy alliance they'd built over cold coffee and shared purpose — but he could see it anyway, the way you can see a place you've never visited if someone describes it with enough grief. Her mother's handwriting beside a newspaper story about two sisters and a betrayal. Thirty years of a lie, folded into a recipe box like a letter never sent.

*Founding myths aren't meant to be audited.*

He said it aloud to the empty street, testing it, and heard it for what it was: the sound of corruption knocking politely. Not breaking down the door. Not holding a gun. Just knocking, gently, wearing a kind face, offering tea.

The worst part — the part he would carry longest — was how reasonable it sounded. Because it *was* reasonable, in the way most betrayals are. Nobody writes a memoir to destroy themselves. Marisa had built something real out of whatever she'd taken; thousands of people ate her bread every morning and were glad of it; did the provenance of a recipe matter more than the livelihoods of two hundred employees, the joy of a town's breakfasts, the pride of a granddaughter? Soft focus was practically a literary tradition. Half the memoirs on his own shelf were soft focus. He could write it so beautifully that no one would ever think to look past the blur.

He could almost make himself believe it. That was the terrifying thing. He stood there turning the argument over like a coin, checking both faces, and the coin kept landing favor-side up, and some cold quiet part of him understood that this was exactly how it worked — not the dramatic fall, but the slow persuasion, the excellent reasons, the debt schedule doing the moral arguing for you.

His phone buzzed. Beatriz.

*Did the interview go okay today? You sounded weird on the phone last night.*

He looked at the message for a long time. The lit sliver of window in the dead building threw a thin blade of light across the pavement, almost reaching his shoes.

*I'll tell you tomorrow,* he typed. Then deleted it. Typed again: *It went fine.*

Three words. He stared at them and understood that he had already begun — that the soft focus wasn't waiting for the manuscript, it had started here, tonight, on a dark street outside his family's sealed history, in a text message to the one person who deserved the truth most.

He hit send anyway.

* * *

The next morning he signed.

Marisa had the papers drawn up within a day — of course she did; she had probably had them drawn up before she ever offered, the way she had the folder stocked in the drawer — and her lawyer walked Ivo through them in a conference room that smelled of nothing, which struck him as obscene after months in a bakery. Sign here, initial there, the percentage in perpetuity, the development fund disbursing quarterly, delivery date moved up six weeks with penalties for delay that were, he noted, roughly triple the penalties in his original contract. She had built the trap with a craftsman's attention to detail. He respected it the way you respect a beautiful machine designed for a terrible purpose.

When the lawyer left, Marisa came around the desk and shook his hand with both of hers, warm and firm, and said, "Thank you, Ivo. You won't regret this."

He knew, standing there with her gratitude in his grip, that she believed it. That was somehow worse. In her version of events she was doing him a kindness — rescuing a talented writer from penury, protecting a family legacy from an audit it couldn't survive, keeping two old women's peace intact by leaving the past decently blurred. She might even have been right, in some accounting he couldn't afford to do.

He walked out past the ovens. The morning shift was in full swing, the bakers moving with the wordless choreography of people who had done this ten thousand times, and the smell hit him the way it always did — warm, forgiving, like being a child in a kitchen where someone loved you. He had loved this place once, in the first weeks, before he knew what it was built on. He had stood right here watching the loaves come out and thought: *this is what making something means.* Now he watched the same loaves and saw the folder, the drawer, the numbers, the knock at the door answered.

Outside, the city went on being morning. He checked his bank app because he hated himself and needed the evidence: the development fund's first disbursement had landed already. The number sat there glowing, and beneath it, his outstanding balances, and he watched the app recalculate, watched the debts dissolve like sugar in warm water, and felt absolutely nothing that resembled relief.

What he felt was the specific nausea of a man who has just discovered the exact price of himself, and learned it was affordable.

He texted Beatriz: *Can I see you tonight?*

She replied in under a minute, a thumbs-up and a question mark, and he pocketed the phone and stood on the corner outside the flagship bakery, under the green awning with the gold lettering he'd once photographed because it was beautiful, and began composing the truth in his head — *I signed something, I took the money, I need you to hear it from me before you find it anywhere else* — rehearsing the confession like lines in a play he desperately didn't want to open.

Across the street, a courier in a brown uniform climbed off a motorbike with a document envelope in hand, checked an address on his scanner, frowned at it, and started toward the residential side of the square — toward the narrow street where Beatriz's apartment sat above the closed tailor shop, where she lived and baked at midnight and had stopped, three weeks ago, answering to the name Duarte.

Ivo's stomach dropped before his mind caught up, before he could even say why, before the courier had crossed half the square with the envelope tucked under his arm like a verdict looking for its courtroom.

He was already moving.
