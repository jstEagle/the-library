## 13. Deadline Pressure

The manuscript had a smell now. That was the first thing Ivo noticed when he opened the file each morning — not on the page itself, which was only light and glass and cursor-blink, but in the apartment around it. Three weeks of drafting had soaked into the curtains, the couch cushions, the inside of his own mouth: burnt coffee, cold takeout, and something faintly sour he eventually identified as his own reluctance, given physical form.

He worked at the kitchen table because the desk felt too much like an altar. The laptop sat open to Chapter Four — *The Founding* — the chapter Marisa loved most, the one she'd read aloud to him twice with her hand pressed flat over her heart as though steadying a bird. *I stood alone in that kitchen at nineteen years old with nothing but flour on my hands and a dream in my chest.* It was beautiful. It was a lie. And every morning Ivo sat down and made it more beautiful, sentence by sentence, the way you might braid rope for your own hanging.

*I stood alone.* Except she hadn't. There was a photograph on microfilm at the county library that said otherwise — two young women shoulder to shoulder behind a counter, sleeves rolled, both of them laughing at someone off-camera. One of them was Marisa's mother. The other was his grandmother.

He typed: *I stood alone in that kitchen.*

Deleted *alone*. Typed it again.

The cursor pulsed like a small patient heart.

Outside, October was doing its best work — the plane trees along the street going copper, the air sharp enough to bite through a sweater. People passed his window carrying paper bags from Duarte's flagship, warm loaves tucked against their coats like infants. He could see the bakery's awning from here if he leaned sideways, that deep imperial green with the gold lettering, and some mornings he watched customers queue in the cold and thought: they don't know either. They're buying a story with their crust. We're all eating the legend.

His phone buzzed against the table. Beatriz.

He looked at it for four full rings. Then he picked up.

"Tell me you've eaten something that isn't coffee," she said, by way of hello. Her voice had that morning roughness, flour-dusted even through the line.

"I had toast."

"Toast is a vehicle, not a meal."

"It had butter."

"Barely counts." A pause, and he heard her turn away from the phone, heard the clatter of her little kitchen — the sheet pan going into the rack, the particular knock of her rolling pin against the counter edge that he'd started to recognize the way you recognize a friend's footsteps on stairs. "I'm testing the rye again tonight. Come by at nine. Bring your appetite and your guilt."

"My guilt?"

"You have a guilt face. You think you hide it but you don't. It's very loud for such a quiet face."

He laughed, and hated how easily it came, how warm she'd made herself in the cold architecture of his weeks. "Nine o'clock," he said. "I'll bring the butter."

He hung up and looked at the screen. *I stood alone in that kitchen.* Two hundred and forty pages to go. Eleven days until delivery.

He didn't tell her about the contract. That was the arithmetic he'd settled on, the ugly little ledger he kept folded in the back of his mind: what she didn't know couldn't curdle between them. He'd signed Marisa's deal six days ago — partnership stake, accelerated deadline, *keep the family material vague* — and every hour since, the signature had been growing teeth. He could feel them when he swallowed.

* * *

The safety-deposit box lived in the basement of First Meridian Bank, down a corridor that smelled of floor wax and old money, behind a door that required two keys and a disapproving glance from Mr. Okafor, who had guarded the boxes since before Ivo was born and treated every visitor as a prospective felon.

Ivo came on a Tuesday afternoon, telling himself it was prudence. Telling himself a lot of things lately; the telling-himself had become a full-time occupation layered over the ghostwriting, which was itself a lie layered over a theft layered over thirty years of silence. He carried the folder flat against his ribs like contraband, which, in a sense, it was.

Inside the box: the microfilm printout of the two grandmothers in one kitchen, 1989, the *Gazette*'s caption reading *SISTERS-IN-FLOUR: THE DUARTE AND ALMEIDA PARTNERSHIP THAT BUILT A TOWN'S APPETITE*. The county register page showing the bakery lease held jointly by Helena Almeida and Rosa Duarte, terminated under circumstances the clerk had annotated with a single dry word: *disputed*. The retired columnist's letter — Mrs. Ferreira, ninety-one years old and sharp as a paring knife, who remembered everything and owed no one her silence: *Rosa took the card out of Helena's box with her own hands. I saw it happen. I was young and I said nothing, and I have regretted it my whole life.* And beneath all of it, the photograph Beatriz had found tucked in her mother's recipe box, the one that had made her go quiet in a way he'd never seen her go quiet — the honey cake on a windowsill, and behind it, half-turned toward the camera, a young woman with Ivo's grandmother's exact stubborn chin.

He laid the folder in the steel drawer. His hands were steady, which felt like a betrayal of something.

Here was the thing he could not say aloud, not to Beatriz, not to his grandmother, not to the mirror: he hadn't taken the deal because he believed Marisa's version. He'd taken it because the number cleared his debts — the advance he'd burned through waiting for a novel that wouldn't come, the loan against the nothing he owned — and because some cowardly cellar of himself whispered that the truth would survive without him carrying it, that someone else would dig it up someday, that he could be a decent man later, after he was solvent, after the book shipped, after.

*After* was where integrity went to die. He knew this. He typed it into his notes app once, at 3 a.m., then deleted it before dawn like a man burying evidence of his own conscience.

Mr. Okafor slid the long drawer back into its slot and turned the key. "Anything else today?"

"No," Ivo said. "That's everything."

It wasn't everything. It was the opposite of everything. It was everything he owned of the truth, locked in a bank basement while he walked back upstairs into the daylight and the lie.

* * *

The drafting got worse before it got better, and then it got worse again, which he learned was simply the shape of complicity — not a slope but a tide, coming in twice daily whether you watched it or not.

Chapter Seven: *The Rival Bakeries.* In Marisa's telling, the Almeida family had closed their shop out of simple economics, a sad but blameless fading, and the Duarte rise afterward was pure merit. He wrote it in her voice, which was the cruelest part — he'd gotten so good at her voice, at the warm gravel of it, the way she turned nouns into promises. *We didn't take anything from anyone. We just stayed standing when others sat down.* He polished that line until it gleamed. Then he sat back and looked at it gleaming and had to get up and stand at the sink and run water over his wrists until his pulse came down.

Sleep became a negotiation he kept losing. He'd draft until two, lie down, and find his grandmother's hands waiting behind his eyelids — those knuckled, flour-ghosted hands that had kneaded bread on a formica table in a kitchen the size of a closet, teaching him that dough tells the truth about whoever handles it. *You can't rush proofing,* she used to say. *And you can't fake a good crumb. The inside always shows.* He was faking a crumb. Two hundred and forty pages of it. The inside was going to show someday, and everyone he loved would be standing in the blast radius.

At 4 a.m. on Thursday he got up and wrote, in a separate document, the true chapter — the one that would never ship. Helena and Rosa, side by side. The recipe card moving between their kitchens like a shared heartbeat. The day Rosa's daughter walked out with it and never brought it back. He wrote eleven hundred words with his chest aching, and when he finished he saved it to a drive he kept in his coat pocket, next to his keys, so that it was literally on his person at all times like an organ.

Then he opened the false manuscript and kept typing, because the deadline was real and the money was realer, and Marisa had moved the delivery date up twice already, each time with a smile that showed exactly the right amount of teeth.

* * *

Friday, she summoned him to the flagship for a review session. The bakery at mid-morning was theater: the bread wall rising in golden tiers, the queue snaking past the window display, the smell of the signature loaf — *her* loaf, the legend said, invented alone at nineteen — thick enough to lean on.

Marisa received him in the office above the ovens, a room that managed to be both luxurious and austere, all pale wood and one framed photograph of her mother. She wore white like armor. She looked tired in a way she'd hate to hear described.

"Read me the founding chapter," she said.

He read it. She closed her eyes during the good lines. When he finished, there was a silence with bread in it, warm and suffocating.

"You've got the gift," she said finally. "You make me sound like the woman I decided to be." Something flickered across her face then — quick, gone — that might have been grief or might have been indigestion. "Keep the family section vague. Vaguer than this. The reunion business, the sister, any of it. My memoir is not a court transcript."

"No," Ivo agreed. "It's a monument."

She smiled at that, pleased, and pushed an envelope across the desk — the second bonus, cash-heavy, smelling faintly of the drawers it had lived in. He took it. His hand did not shake, and he catalogued that too, added it to the file of evidence against himself.

Walking out, he passed the ovens on the lower level, the great deck ovens he'd admired his first week, when this place was still a cathedral to him. A baker he'd come to know, a kid named Tomás with forearms like bread loaves themselves, waved a peel at him. "Book almost done, senhor Ivo? My mother pre-ordered."

"Almost done," Ivo said, and heard how it sounded: like a confession wearing a coat.

* * *

Saturday night he went to Beatriz's apartment with butter and a bottle of vinho verde and the guilt face she'd allegedly diagnosed, and for three hours the tide went out. They baked the rye together — her testing, him fetching, the tiny kitchen generating heat like a small affectionate furnace — and when the loaf came out she broke it open while it was still too hot, the way her mother supposedly did, the way his grandmother definitely had, and steam rose between their faces and she said, "Look at that crumb," with such naked joy that something in his chest tore along a seam he hadn't known was load-bearing.

"This is the best part," she said, mouth full. "This exact minute. Before anyone decides what the bread means. It's just bread."

"Just bread," he repeated, and kissed flour off her cheekbone, and she let him, and for one entire evening he forgot which side of the ledger he was standing on.

Then, near midnight, half-asleep against her shoulder with her radio murmuring low, he heard her say, drowsy, "You'll tell me, won't you? If Marisa tries something. If the book starts becoming a weapon instead of a story."

The guilt arrived instantly, punctual as a train.

"Of course," he said.

She was already asleep. He lay awake in the dark of her narrow bedroom listening to her breathe and understood, with the clarity that only 1 a.m. provides, that he had just lied to her in the softest possible voice, and that soft lies were still lies, and that the deposit box downtown was getting heavier every day he left it there, and that the manuscript was due in five days, and that he was going to deliver it anyway, and that somewhere in the future there was a version of this night in which Beatriz found out — couriers misdeliver contracts, secrets have terrible aim — and he would deserve whatever her face did when it happened.

He got up quietly, dressed in the dark, and let himself out into the cold.

* * *

Sunday he didn't write. Instead he walked to the bank, which was closed, and stood outside it looking at the brass letters FIRST MERIDIAN like a man visiting a grave he'd dug himself. Then he walked to the station platform where he'd once asked a woman to pretend to be his girlfriend, and then to the festival grounds, where workers were raising the big tent for the centennial bake-off, hammering stakes into ground that had fed both his family and hers.

Beatriz's entry form was public knowledge now — posted on the festival board under the joint name, ALMEIDA-DUARTE, in her careful handwriting. Anyone could see it. Marisa could see it. Marisa, who had told him *keep the family vague*, whose lawyers were on retainer, whose deadline he was meeting in five days with a beautiful, hollow book.

He stood at the board a long time. The wind smelled like canvas and cut grass and, faintly, from somewhere across town, baking bread.

Five days, he thought. Five days to finish the lie, collect the money, and decide whether the truth stays in a steel drawer or comes out into the light where it belongs — and whether it comes out because he chooses it, or because someone else digs it up first and finds his fingerprints all over the shovel.

His phone buzzed. An email. From Marisa's assistant.

Subject line: **Final Manuscript — Delivery Moved Up.**

Three days.

Ivo read it twice, standing in the wind, and felt the last clean exit close behind him like a door in a house he'd set fire to.
