## 14. Caught Between Courses

The courier came at nine in the morning, which was the first wrong thing.

Beatriz Duarte had lived above the old tailor's shop on Rua das Flores for six years, and in all that time no one had ever delivered anything to her door before ten. The morning deliveries went to the businesses below — bolts of fabric once, now mostly nothing, since Senhor Almada had retired and left his windows dressed in mannequins nobody updated. Her building got mail through a slot that stuck, and packages only when she ordered flour in bulk, and those always came by freight van with a driver who honked twice because the buzzer didn't work.

This man was in a pressed uniform with a clipboard and an expression of professional indifference. He held out a stiff envelope, cream-colored, heavy as a wedding invitation.

"Signature required," he said.

"I think you have the wrong address."

He turned the clipboard toward her. Her own name was printed there — *B. Duarte* — in a font chosen by someone who thought elegance meant serifs. Below it, the address was correct. Below that, the sender's name made her stomach drop before her mind caught up to it: *Duarte Bakery Group, Attn: M. Duarte, Office of the President.*

"Sign here," the courier said.

She signed. Her hand did it without consulting her, the way hands do when they've spent a lifetime signing things under duress — school forms, lease agreements, the resignation letter from Marisa's company seven years ago that she'd written at three in the morning and delivered still smelling of proofing dough.

She carried the envelope upstairs like it might go off.

Inside were two documents. The first was a cover letter on Marisa's letterhead, typed by an assistant who spelled Beatriz's name right but got everything else wrong in subtler ways. *Dear Ms. Duarte,* it began, *enclosed please find a courtesy copy of the agreement executed between Ms. Marisa Duarte and Mr. Ivo Almeida, provided for your awareness given your recent association with both parties.* Courtesy copy. For her awareness. As if she were a shareholder. As if she were being notified of a merger.

The second document was the contract itself.

She read it standing up, because sitting down felt like surrender. She read it twice, then a third time slowly, the way you reread a diagnosis, looking for the loophole, the misprint, the sentence that would make it not true.

It was true. Every clause of it.

Ivo Almeida had agreed to deliver the completed manuscript of *Rising: The Marisa Duarte Story* within three weeks — three weeks, when the deadline had been two months out; he'd told her so himself over burnt toast in her kitchen, complaining about the pace, promising there was time. He had accepted a bonus whose figure had a comma in it that made her eyes sting. And in Section 7, subsection C, in language so clean it was almost beautiful, he had agreed — *the Author shall exercise discretion regarding matters pertaining to pre-founding family history, presenting such material, if at all, in terms agreeable to the Client.*

Family stuff vague. Those had been Marisa's words, or words like them. Beatriz knew because Ivo had told her about the offer weeks ago, back when it was still a thing he could refuse, back when they'd sat across from each other at her tiny table with the wobbly leg and he'd said, *I'm going to say no, obviously,* and she'd believed him. She had believed him the way you believe in gravity — completely, without evidence, just because everything else in your life depended on it.

And then, apparently, he hadn't said no. He'd said yes, and signed, and dated it eleven days ago, and told no one. Told *her* no one. Eleven days of phone calls and festival planning and one evening spent hand-copying their grandmother's recipe card onto fresh cardstock for the bake-off entry, and he had been carrying this folded in his life the whole time like a stone in his shoe, walking beside her, limping invisibly.

She set the contract down on the counter. Then she picked it up again because she couldn't stop touching the evidence, running her thumb along the signature line where his name sat in blue ink — *Ivo Almeida*, the tail of the *A* sweeping back under the whole word like something trying to hide.

Betrayed twice over. That was the phrase that arrived, uninvited, and refused to leave. Once by Marisa, thirty years ago by proxy and seven years ago in person, when her sister had looked at her across the office desk and said, *You were never serious about any of this, Bia.* And now by Ivo, who had held her mother's recipe box open with her and read the newspaper clipping aloud in a voice that shook, who had stood in the county archive microfilm room and pointed at the grainy photograph of two young women in one kitchen and said, *That's them. That's our grandmothers,* as if the word *our* cost him something and he paid it gladly.

She had let herself believe they were on the same side. Worse — she had built a festival entry around it. *Almeida-Duarte*, printed on the form in her own handwriting, submitted to the centennial committee with provenance documentation she'd assembled like a case for a court that didn't exist yet. She had put both names on that entry card thinking names meant something. Thinking a name was a promise.

Her phone buzzed against the counter. His name on the screen. Of course. She watched it buzz, and buzz again, and go dark, and she did not move.

* * *

The morning passed the way mornings pass after bad news — in pieces, badly. She tried to work. There was an order for a christening cake due Saturday, fondant roses to roll, and she stood at her counter rolling them and found that her hands, traitorous things, kept making them smaller and smaller until they looked like wounds rather than flowers, and she scraped the whole batch off the marble into the compost and started over.

It was the honey loaf that finally undid her. Not making it — she wasn't making it, not today — but seeing the recipe card propped against the tile backsplash where she'd left it after the practice run. Their grandmother's handwriting, faded brown ink, *bolo de mel da avó* at the top in a looping hand, and beneath it, in different ink added later, annotations in her own mother's writing: *ovens run hot, watch the last 10 min.* Two generations of women correcting each other lovingly across decades. And now a third generation, hers, supposed to carry it into a festival tent under a joint name while the man who helped her revive it sold its silence for a number with a comma in it.

She thought about the night they'd baked together. Flour to her elbows, flour in his hair, the timer shrieking and him lunging for it and knocking the salt bowl into the sink, both of them laughing so hard the neighbors banged on the wall. It had felt like the first honest hour of her adult life. Baking *with* someone instead of against them — she'd written that in her head like a line from a book, because it was too tender to say aloud. And the whole time, somewhere in his coat pocket or his inbox or the locked drawer of whatever hotel room he worked from, the contract had already existed. Already signed. Already dated.

He hadn't even had the decency to betray her recently. He'd done it early, efficiently, and then spent eleven days letting her build cathedrals on top of the lie.

The phone buzzed again. Third call. She turned it face-down on the counter, and the screen lit against the marble like something drowning, and she watched it until it stopped.

* * *

By late afternoon she had composed herself into something functional, which is why it was so unbearable when the knock came at four o'clock and she opened the door without checking the peephole and there he was.

Ivo Almeida stood in her doorway holding a paper bag — she could smell the pastéis de nata through the bag, warm, from the place near the station they both pretended wasn't better than anything either family ever made — and wearing the particular expression of a man who has rehearsed a speech in the stairwell and lost his nerve at the threshold.

"You're not answering," he said.

"No," she said.

"Beatriz." He shifted the bag from one hand to the other. "Can I—"

"How did you get my address?" she asked, though she knew, and the knowing was its own small cruelty. "No. Stupid question. You've been here a hundred times." She stepped back from the door, not to invite him in but because standing that close to him made her chest hurt, and distance was the only analgesic she had. "Did you sign it?"

He went very still. She watched him decide whether to lie — watched the whole deliberation happen behind his eyes like weather moving across a valley — and then watched him fail to do it, because he was, whatever else he was, constitutionally incapable of lying to her face for more than four seconds.

"Yes," he said. "Eleven days ago. Before—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Before the archives. Before we found the photo. Before I understood what—"

"Before what?" Her voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Before you understood what you were selling?"

"It's not that simple."

"Read me Section Seven." She crossed her arms. "Go on. You've clearly memorized it. 'The author shall exercise discretion regarding matters pertaining to pre-founding family history.' Discretion, Ivo. My mother's whole life, reduced to a discretion clause. And you signed it with a pen I probably bought."

"The money would have cleared my debts." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage, and she gave him credit for the honesty even as it curdled. "All of them. In one stroke. And I told myself I could sign it and still find another way to get the truth out — that the contract bound the memoir, not me, not us, not the festival—"

"That's not how contracts work. That's not how *anything* works." She heard her voice rise and hated it, hated that she sounded like Marisa, precise and cutting, the family instrument tuned for exactly this. "You sat at my table. You copied my grandmother's card in your own handwriting. You said—" She stopped, because what he'd said was too much to repeat, and repeating it would only prove how much of it she'd believed.

"I know what I said."

"Do you? Because the paper says otherwise. The paper says you took her money and promised to keep the family stuff vague, and the paper has a date on it, and the date says you'd already decided before you ever let me think we were—" She couldn't finish. The sentence had a shape she refused to give it in a doorway with a bag of custard tarts going cold between them.

"I was going to tell you."

"When? After delivery? After the memoir shipped?" She laughed, and it was an ugly sound. "Or were you waiting to see if the truth survived on its own, so you wouldn't have to risk anything? So you could keep the money *and* the clean hands *and* me?"

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. None of this is fair. My mother died with her name attached to a theft she didn't commit. Your grandmother spent fifty years being called the liar. And you — you had the receipts, Ivo. We *found* them together. And you signed them away anyway." She stepped back inside the doorway, putting the frame between them like a border. "I withdrew from the festival this afternoon. Called the committee an hour ago. They were very understanding. Everyone's always very understanding when a Duarte quits something."

"Beatriz, don't—"

"Don't what? Don't quit? Don't feel?" She shook her head. "I put your name next to mine on an entry card. Do you understand what that cost me? Do you understand what it means, for me, to attach my name to anyone's? I walked away from the family business rather than have my name stand for something I didn't choose. And then I chose yours. Freely. On paper." Her throat closed. She pushed through it, because if she didn't finish now she never would. "So take your tarts, and your comma, and your discretion clause, and go write her book. I hope it wins prizes."

She closed the door. Gently. That was the worst part, she thought, leaning against it, listening to him not knock — that she did it gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child, the way her mother used to close the bakery's front gate at night, careful with the hinges, because some things you expect to need again tomorrow.

His footsteps went down the stairs. Paused on the landing. Went on.

The phone started buzzing in her hand before he'd even reached the street. She looked down at the screen glowing in the dimming kitchen — *Ivo* — and then past it, at the recipe card still propped against the tile, her mother's annotation watching her: *watch the last 10 min.*

Ten minutes. Everything, apparently, happened in the last ten minutes.

She declined the call. Then, because declining wasn't enough, because the buzzing would start again in ninety seconds and she knew herself, she powered the phone off entirely and set it face-down in the drawer where she kept the spare piping tips, among things she wasn't using.

Through the window, far below, she watched him come out onto Rua das Flores and stop under the tailor's dead window display, the mannequin in its forty-year-old dress looming over him like a chaperone. He stood there a long moment, the paper bag hanging forgotten from his fist, and then he took out his own phone and she saw him look at it, and she knew — the way you know bread is done by smell alone, without opening the oven — that he was calling her, and that the call was going nowhere, ringing in a drawer full of piping tips.

He turned and walked toward the station. Toward, presumably, Marisa's flagship, where the ovens ran all night and the founding legend hung framed above the register in gold letters, and where a manuscript waited to be finished on schedule by a man who had, at last count, eleven days of silence left to spend.

Beatriz stayed at the window until the street emptied. Then she untied her apron, hung it on its hook — muscle memory, ritual, the closing sequence of every day of her working life — and stood in the middle of her small kitchen surrounded by everything she'd rebuilt alone: the christening cake order, the fondant dust, the recipe card, the drawer with the dead phone in it.

On the counter, the courier's envelope lay where she'd left it, cream-colored and final. She picked it up, meaning to throw it away, and found she couldn't. Evidence, Ivo would have called it. He liked that word. He'd taught her to think in it — receipts, records, provenance, the paper trail that outlives everyone's good intentions.

Fine. She would keep the receipts.

She slid the contract into the recipe box, on top of the newspaper clipping, beneath her mother's handwriting — betrayal filed with its own antidote — and closed the lid, and stood there in the quiet with her hand flat on the wood, listening to the building settle around her, wondering, with a coldness that frightened her more than anger ever had, what else in the last eleven days had been signed, sealed, or decided without her —

and whether the woman who'd withdrawn from the festival was finished, or only getting started.
