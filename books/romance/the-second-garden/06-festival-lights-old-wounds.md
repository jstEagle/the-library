## 6. Festival Lights, Old Wounds

### Mara

The lanterns went up at dusk, the way they did every year, strung from the eaves of the feed store to the bell tower like a rope of small captive suns. Mara had watched this festival for seven years from the same spot—the doorway of his shop, one hand on his daughter's shoulder—and every year he had told himself the same lie: that he was content here, in the shallow water of a life that asked nothing of him.

Tonight Sol had gone off with her friends before he'd finished buttoning his coat. "You're slow, Papá," she'd said, already running, her braid swinging like a metronome counting down his excuses.

So he walked alone through the plaza, past the cider press and the fiddlers tuning their strings, past tables of candied apples and the old men arguing about rainfall as if they could argue it into being. And then he saw Ines.

She stood near the orchard booth—*their* booth now, God help him—wearing a dark green dress that made him think, absurdly, of leaves just before they turn. Her hair was down. He had never seen it down. In three weeks of shared ledgers and contested pruning schedules, she had kept it tied back like everything else about her: contained, defended, out of reach.

She was laughing at something the widow Okafor said, and the sound carried across the plaza and landed somewhere under his ribs that he had boarded over years ago.

He should have turned around. He was holding two cups of cider he didn't remember buying, which meant some part of him had already decided not to turn around.

"You came," she said when he reached her. Not an accusation. Worse—an observation, mild as weather.

"Sol wanted to come." He handed her a cup. "I was escorting."

"Where is she?"

"Gone. Evaporated. Children do that here." He drank. The cider was too sweet and too warm and he was grateful for both, because they gave his mouth something to do besides say what it wanted to say, which was: *you look like someone I used to dream about before I knew your name.*

The fiddlers began. An old waltz, the kind the town played every year because the town believed some things should never change, even when everything else had.

Ines looked at the dancers. Then at him. Then away, toward the lanterns, as if the answer to whatever question hung between them might be written up there among the paper lights.

"One dance," she said. "For appearances. Widow Okafor is watching, and if we stand here glaring at each other all night, the whole town will decide we hate each other, and then the orchard vote goes badly."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," Mara said, and set down his cup.

She took his hand. Her palm was rough from three weeks of work among the trees—he knew those calluses now, had catalogued them without meaning to—and her fingers closed around his with a decision in them that startled him. She was not reluctant. That was the thing he would remember later, lying awake: she was not reluctant at all.

They stepped into the current of dancers. The waltz turned them past the cider press, past the bell tower, past the faces of neighbors who had known Lucia, who had mourned with him, who had watched him fold himself shut like a letter no one was allowed to open.

"You're stiff," Ines said quietly.

"I'm careful."

"Those aren't the same thing."

"No," he admitted. "They're not."

Her hand moved from his shoulder to the back of his neck—not a scandalous distance, barely an inch, but he felt it the way you feel a door opening in a house you thought was empty. The music swelled. A lantern above them guttered and brightened, guttered and brightened, like a heart making up its mind.

"Ines." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Why did you really come back? The funeral was a reason. It wasn't *the* reason."

Her eyes found his and held there, and for a moment the guardedness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was so naked and so old that his breath stopped in his chest.

"I don't know yet," she said. "Ask me again when I do."

The song ended. They did not step apart. Around them the dancers clapped, the fiddlers retuned, the town flowed on—but the two of them stood in the middle of the plaza holding on to a dance that was over, and neither of them called it accidental, and neither of them let go first.

It was Marta Reyes who finally broke the spell, calling out cheerfully across the crowd—"Mara Villalobos! Dancing! Write down the date, somebody!"—and the laughter that followed was mostly kind and entirely observant, and by morning it would be everywhere: the widower and the sister who came back, spinning in the lantern light like people who had forgotten to be careful.

Mara felt the heat climb his neck. But Ines only lifted her chin, looked straight at Marta, and smiled—a real smile, unarmored—and said, "Yes. Somebody write it down."

And Mara thought, with a vertigo that had nothing to do with dancing: *we are already in trouble.*

### Ines

She could still feel his hand on her waist an hour later, which was ridiculous, because hands were hands, and she had been held before, married once, buried once—no. That was Lucia's arithmetic, not hers. Ines had loved and left and lost in her own smaller ways, and she had learned to keep the ledger closed.

But the ledger wouldn't stay closed tonight.

She walked the edge of the plaza where the lanterns thinned out and the dark began, past the last food stall, to the low stone wall that bordered the orchard road. From here she could see the trees—her trees, his trees, the grammar of that still unresolved—standing black against the star-scattered sky, patient as witnesses.

"He dances better than he lets on."

Marta Reyes materialized beside her with the stealth of a woman who had spent forty years knowing everyone's business. Ines didn't startle. You couldn't grow up in this town and be startled by Marta.

"We were maintaining appearances," Ines said.

"Mm." Marta sipped her cider. "For twenty minutes? Appearances usually take four."

Ines said nothing. Below them, the plaza glowed; the fiddlers had moved to a faster tune, and Sol Villalobos spun through the crowd with her friends, laughing with her whole body the way children do and adults forget. Ines watched her and felt the old ache shift shape again—the ache that had brought her back, though she still couldn't say its full name aloud.

"She looks like Lucia," Marta said.

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone's right." Marta turned, and her voice lost its gossip's lilt and became something older, almost gentle. "I liked your sister. I grieved her. And I'll tell you what I told half the town tonight already, since you'll hear it anyway: it does my heart good to see that man hold his head up. Seven years he's been walking around like a man serving a sentence nobody named." She paused. "Whatever you're doing to him, Ines—keep doing it."

Then she was gone, swallowed by the light and noise, leaving Ines alone with a sentence she didn't know how to carry.

*Keep doing it.* As if Ines were doing anything but standing in the wreckage of her own decisions. As if the flutter in her chest during that waltz were a gift she'd chosen rather than a door she'd failed to lock.

Because here was the truth she circled every night in the room above the bakery: she had come back for reasons she refused to finish thinking. The deed was the excuse. The funeral was the excuse. Even the orchard—Lucia's trees, Lucia's stubborn legacy—was only the stage. What she had actually come home for was standing in the plaza in a good coat, holding two cups of cider he hadn't remembered buying, looking at her like a man reading a letter he wasn't sure he was allowed to open.

And she had danced with him anyway. Had put her hand on his neck. Had said *ask me again when I do*, which was either honesty or cruelty, and she genuinely could not tell which.

The trouble was the letters. The tin box sat in her rented room like a coal she couldn't put down—Lucia's handwriting, Lucia's confessions, pages Ines had read twice and then hidden under her spare sweater as if fabric could make them innocent. *Tell him or don't tell him,* Lucia had written, *but if you stay away because of me, then I've ruined two lives instead of one, and I can't bear the arithmetic.*

Two lives. Lucia had always been careless with other people's sums.

"Ines."

She turned. Mara stood a few feet away, lantern light catching one side of his face, the other side in shadow, and behind him the plaza roared softly on—music, laughter, Sol's bright spinning figure—all of it suddenly far away, like a party in another country.

"Sol's asking for you," he said. "She wants to show you the wish lanterns. Apparently you release one for the dead and one for the living, and she wants—" He stopped. Started again. "She wants us to do it together. All three."

*All three.* A family-shaped sentence, thrown out carelessly, landing like a stone in still water.

Ines looked at him—at the careful man who was trying very hard not to hope, and failing, and not knowing he was failing—and felt her denial crack straight down the middle.

"All right," she said. "Show me how it's done."

But as they walked back toward the light together, side by side, close enough that their sleeves touched, Ines saw, at the edge of the plaza, a car she recognized—a developer's sedan, parked where no festival-goer would park, its driver smoking and watching the crowd with the patience of a man being paid to wait.

And she understood, with cold certainty, that tomorrow was going to ask more of them than a dance ever could.
