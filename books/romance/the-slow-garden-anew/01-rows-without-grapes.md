## 1. Rows Without Grapes

The frost came early that year, and Isandro Fairweather was grateful for it.

Cold meant the vines went dormant, and dormant vines asked nothing of him. No water, no sulfur, no 4 a.m. panic when a storm cell parked itself over the eastern ridge. He could walk the rows with his hands in his pockets and pretend, for an hour, that he was a man inspecting an estate instead of a man defending one.

He started, as he always did, at the gate.

The gate had been his father's idea — wrought iron, the letter F worked into the scrollwork by some blacksmith in Valdivia who had charged the Fairweathers a scandalous sum and delivered it late. His father had loved it. Elias Roque had laughed at it, once, standing right where Isandro stood now, saying: *You put your name on a gate, Isandro, you'd better be sure of what's behind it.*

Ten years ago this month, Elias had driven off the old coast road in the rain, and everything behind the gate had become Isandro's problem.

He walked.

Row one through row six were Cabernet, planted the year Isandro turned nine, and they were the closest thing the vineyard had to a pension. Solid. Boring. They would outlive him if he let them. Row seven was the experiment — Tempranillo, against every expert's advice about the microclimate — and it had cost him two seasons of near-ruin before it decided to behave. Rows eight through twelve were still bare earth, terraced and waiting, because waiting was free and planting was not.

He catalogued as he walked. It was a habit now, older than any of his farming habits: *what can I afford to lose.* The tractor, no — the tractor was load-bearing, financially speaking; sell it and the whole operation tipped over. The second truck, yes. The pump house roof could go another winter if he tarped it. The barrels in the cellar — he counted them without looking, thirty-one good, four questionable — those were the difference between a vintage and a rumor of a vintage.

And the land itself, obviously. The land was the thing everyone wanted, which meant it was the thing he could least afford to lose, which meant it was the thing he thought about losing most.

That was the arithmetic of his life. Everything he owned was also something someone else was patient enough to wait for.

* * *

The bank's letter had come on Tuesday. He'd read it twice at the kitchen table, then folded it into thirds and put it under the fruit bowl, which was where bad news lived in that house until it either resolved or rotted. The short version: the restructuring he'd begged for was approved, barely, contingent on nothing going wrong. Contingent, though the loan officer hadn't used these words, on Aurelio Fairweather continuing to find Isandro's survival more useful than his collapse.

Isandro had sat with that for a long time after reading it. Not the debt — debt was weather, you dressed for it. The other part. The fact that his entire livelihood rested on the goodwill of a man who smiled like a judge, because he was one, and who had never once, in ten years, given Isandro a reason to feel safe.

Safety wasn't a state you achieved in the Fairweather family. It was a crop. You planted it in spring, you weeded it all summer, you harvested it carefully and stored it in a cool dark place, and even then a single hard frost could take the whole field overnight. Isandro farmed safety the way other men farmed grapes — daily, obsessively, with an eye always on the sky.

His routine was simple. He answered Aurelio's calls within three rings. He sent the quarterly summaries unasked, clean and boring, numbers that said *nothing to see here* in the fluent language of accounting. He attended the family dinners he couldn't refuse and drank exactly what was poured. And once a year — this was the expensive part — he let Aurelio be right about something small, publicly, so the judge could enjoy the particular pleasure of magnanimity. Last year it had been the pruning method. The year before, the label design. Small surrenders, cheap at the price. You fed the bear a chicken so it wouldn't come looking for the whole herd.

It had worked for ten years. He repeated that to himself sometimes, like a prayer with a crack down the middle of it: *it has worked, it is working.*

* * *

The second letter came on Thursday, and it did not come from the bank.

He found it in the mailbox at the road — the actual mailbox, rusted, on a leaning post, because Isandro had never gotten around to replacing it and replacing it felt, obscurely, like admitting he planned to stay. The envelope was heavy cream stock, the kind of paper that arrived pre-impressed with expectations. His name and address in fountain pen, in handwriting he recognized before he saw the signature.

*Aurelio.*

Isandro stood at the mailbox in the cold and did not open it. This too was practice. There had been years when a cream envelope from Aurelio had preceded a phone call within the hour, and the call had been pleasant, and the pleasantness had been the worst part. So he made himself walk back up the drive first, make coffee, sit down, and only then break the seal — a ritual of small defiance that changed nothing and made him feel, briefly, like a man with options.

The letter was one page. Aurelio wrote the way he spoke: warm, unhurried, every sentence carrying a passenger it didn't announce.

*Dear Isandro —*

*It has been too long since the family gathered properly, and your aunt insists — rightly — that another year cannot pass without one. The reunion will be held at the house on the last Saturday of the month. Your presence is expected, and I will confess, anticipated. The family takes pride in what you have made of the old place, and it would mean a great deal to us all to hear of it firsthand.*

*One matter more, mentioned with affection: your cousin Beatriz has taken it upon herself to worry about your solitude. She tells me you have not brought anyone to a family occasion in three years, and she has begun, as Beatriz does, to arrange introductions. I mention this only so you may prepare yourself. Or better — so you may spare yourself. A man who arrives with a companion answers no questions at all.*

*With love and respect,*
*Uncle Aurelio*

Isandro read it twice.

The first time, he heard the voice — that measured baritone, the warmth laid over the steel like frost over a plowed field. *Your presence is expected.* Not requested. Expected. *So you may spare yourself.* An offer shaped exactly like a threat, or a threat shaped exactly like an offer; with Aurelio the two had long ago fused into a single alloy.

The second time, he read it as a document, because that was how you survived documents from Aurelio. What was actually being said? That the reunion was mandatory. That his solitude had been noticed, catalogued, and deemed a vulnerability. That Beatriz's matchmaking was real but secondary — the primary message being that the family watched him, discussed him, and had opinions about the shape of his life. That a man alone was a man whose story nobody else could confirm.

*A man who arrives with a companion answers no questions at all.*

Isandro set the letter down and looked out the kitchen window at the bare rows, gray cane against gray sky, and felt the familiar low hum start up behind his sternum — the vigilance, the crop-tending instinct, the sense of a hand moving through his fields that wasn't his.

Because here was the thing about questions. Isandro had spent ten years making sure no one asked the big one. *What happened the night Elias Roque died?* Nobody asked it anymore, and that silence was the most valuable asset he owned — worth more than the Cabernet, worth more than the land, purchased at a price he paid for in installments every single day. But a reunion was a room full of people with wine in them and history between them, and rooms like that loosened things. Rooms like that produced questions from cousins who'd been children ten years ago and didn't know what they weren't supposed to know.

And Aurelio knew that. Which meant the invitation wasn't only an invitation. It might be a test. It might be a trapdoor. It might be nothing at all — Aurelio was capable of genuine sentiment, probably, in the way a riverbed was capable of holding water.

Either way, refusing was not on the menu. Refusing would be the first unpaid installment in a decade.

So: he would go. He would go, and he would smile, and he would tell the story of the vineyard's recovery in the version that had been agreed upon years ago — the version where a grieving young man had clawed his father's land back from bankruptcy through sheer stubbornness, end of story, nothing underneath.

But the companion question. That was real. Beatriz's introductions meant dinners with women selected for their harmlessness, meant conversations he'd have to steer, meant a spotlight he could not afford. And worse — a weekend of being the family's sad bachelor gave every aunt and uncle license to ask him anything, kindly, over cake. *Are you happy, Isandro? Are you sleeping? Do you ever think about those days, after the accident?*

A companion would close that door. A companion would give him someone to steer conversation toward, someone whose presence was itself an answer: *I'm fine, look, I'm loved, move along.*

The trouble was that Isandro knew approximately four women well enough to ask, and all four were either married, related to him, or employed by the bank.

He finished his coffee. He got out the ledger book — the real one, the farm one, columns in his own cramped hand — and opened a fresh page, because that was how he thought, in columns. On the left he wrote *Costs of going alone.* On the right, *Costs of finding someone.* Both columns filled fast. Neither column contained a number he liked.

At the bottom of the page, almost without deciding to, he wrote a third line:

*There are ads. People do this now.*

He stared at it. It was absurd. It was slightly humiliating. It was also, he noticed, the first entry in either column that didn't make his chest tighten — because a hired companion was a transaction, and transactions were the one language Isandro had spoken fluently for ten years. Terms. Boundaries. Payment. Nothing real at risk, nothing true exposed. A person who understood, without it being said, that some stories were for telling and some were for keeping.

He closed the ledger. Outside, the light was going blue over the eastern rows, and somewhere past the tree line the coast road curved along the cliff where the guardrail still ended twenty meters short of where it should — he'd checked, once, at night, unable to stop himself — a gap the county had never fixed and no one ever mentioned.

Ten years of rehearsal, and the sentence still lived in him like a stone swallowed whole: seven words he had never once said aloud, not to a priest, not to a pillow, not to the dark.

He locked the gate behind him, out of habit, though there was nothing left out there to steal but dirt and patience.

In the morning, he told himself, he would look at the ads.

In the morning, he would begin rehearsing a different story — the meet-cute, the courtship, the plausible lie — while the true sentence stayed buried where he kept it, deeper than the vines, colder than the frost, waiting for a spring he prayed would never come.
