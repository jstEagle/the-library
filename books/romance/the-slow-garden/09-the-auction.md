## 9. The Auction

*Jonah*

The grange hall smelled of floor wax and fear, and I recognized the second one as my own.

I had dressed for battle in my only dress that didn't have orchard soil ground into the hem — a blue thing my mother once called "funeral-adjacent," which felt appropriate. Elias stood beside me at the back of the hall, his hair combed into submission and failing, wearing a jacket I'd never seen before. It fit him badly, which meant it was old. Which meant he'd bought it before he left. Which meant I was cataloguing the biography of a man's clothing instead of focusing on the fact that we were about to lose everything.

"Stop doing that," he murmured.

"Doing what?"

"Reading me like a manuscript."

"You're an easy read. Terrible pacing. The hero disappears for twelve years and nobody forgives him."

"The hero is standing right here," he said, "about to watch a man in a lavender tie take his grandmother's trees."

The man in the lavender tie was Gerald Voss, representing Meridian Development, and he sat in the front row with the serene entitlement of someone who had already won. Beside him, arms crossed, face carefully blank: Marisol Thorne. She hadn't looked at her brother once since we arrived. He hadn't looked away from her. It was like watching two people conduct an argument entirely through the backs of their necks.

The auctioneer — Dale Pruitt, who also sold tractors and once a canoe of disputed provenance — rapped his gavel and called the lot: *Fairweather Orchard, forty-two acres, dwelling and outbuildings, sold to satisfy outstanding debt.*

My stomach did something acrobatic and unlicensed.

I had planned, on the drive over, to be dignified. To sit with my hands folded and accept whatever came with grace. But then Voss opened bidding at four hundred thousand — a number so low it was practically a sneer — and something in me stood up before the rest of me agreed to it.

"Excuse me," I said, and walked to the front of the room.

Elias made a sound behind me like a man watching someone step off a cliff. I understood. I'd spent twelve years perfecting the art of not being seen wanting things. But there is a difference between privacy and cowardice, and I had finally learned which one I'd been practicing.

Dale lowered his gavel uncertainly. "Ma'am, this isn't —"

"I'm not bidding." My voice carried better than I expected; lighthouse blood, maybe. All those years shouting over wind. "I'm going to read you something first. Then you can bid."

Voss sighed the sigh of a man inconvenienced by weather. Marisol uncrossed her arms.

I took out the pages I'd carried in my handbag all morning, soft at the edges from handling. My unfinished novel. Chapter one. The scene where a girl stands in a frost-killed orchard and watches a boy walk down the lane without turning around.

"This is fiction," I told the room. "Mostly. I write romance novels, which some of you buy from the drugstore and hide inside grocery bags, and I'm not judging you, because I hid mine too. For twelve years I wrote about love happening to other people because it seemed safer that way. Love, in my books, could be revised. Deleted. Killed off in chapter nine if the reviews were bad."

Someone in the third row — Mrs. Okonkwo, I think, from the bakery — made a small sound of recognition.

"But this book I couldn't finish. Because the hero left, and I couldn't imagine why anyone would come back, so the story just stopped. And then last spring a solicitor's letter arrived and informed me that the man who left had inherited half my grandmother's orchard alongside me, and I learned that real life doesn't do revisions either. It just keeps growing whether you tend it or not."

I looked down at my pages. My hands were shaking. I let them.

"There's a line in here I wrote when I was twenty-three and furious. *He took the road out of town and my whole heart with it, and the worst part is the heart kept writing to him anyway.*" I looked up. "That's what this orchard is. It's a letter we kept writing without knowing if anyone would answer. Our grandparents planted these trees across a feud they weren't supposed to cross. There are initials carved in the oldest apple tree — E.F. and J.F., cut by two people who chose each other over the story everyone else had written for them. Forty-two acres of somebody else's stubborn love, still bearing fruit."

Voss shifted. Somewhere near the windows, old Tom Beckett set down his coffee.

"A developer will tell you this land is worth more as patios," I said. "And maybe it is. Maybe patios are worth more than apples. But I don't think a town runs on patios. I think it runs on the bakery that lets kids run tabs, and the hardware store that trusts you till Friday, and yes — an orchard that has fed this coast for eighty years and asked almost nothing back except that somebody, eventually, stay."

I folded the pages. My voice, when it came again, was smaller.

"He stayed," I said. "Twelve years late, but he stayed. That's the ending I couldn't write. So I'm asking you to help me keep it."

Silence. The bad kind, the kind with weight.

Then Mrs. Okonkwo stood up and said, "Well. Somebody bid against the patio man," and the room broke open like a seed case.

*Elias*

Here is what nobody tells you about watching the woman you love stand in front of a hundred people and tell them the truth: it undoes you faster than any accusation ever managed. She'd thrown pots at me — well, one pot, and it missed — she'd given me the silent treatment for weeks measured in shared kettles and disputed ladders, and none of it had cracked me open like hearing her read my own leaving aloud in her novel voice, steady and merciless and forgiving all at once.

Bidding started moving. Five-fifty. Six. The fisherman's co-op pooled something. Dale Pruitt's gavel worked up a sweat.

Then Voss raised a finger and said, "Eight hundred thousand," and the room went quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes.

Meridian wasn't bidding to win anymore. They were bidding to end it.

Marisol leaned toward him and said something low. He didn't flinch. I knew that profile — our father's jaw set against the world. I had burned her contract in my stove three weeks ago rather than take her exit, and I had told Jonah nothing, and I had told myself it was mercy. Watching Marisol now, watching her watch me, I understood it had been cowardice with good posture.

Dale called for further bids. Once. Twice.

I stood up.

"I pledge everything I own," I said. My voice came out rough. "The boat. The savings. The —" I gestured vaguely at myself, which got a nervous laugh, which I deserved. "Whatever's left after that, I'll owe in labor. I can graft. I can prune. I can fix the packing shed roof, which, frankly, needs it. Eight-fifty."

Voss turned fully around for the first time. "Mr. Thorne. You don't have eight hundred and fifty thousand dollars."

"No," I agreed. "But I have forty-two acres' worth of reasons, and apparently I've learned to say them out loud. Nine hundred."

"Nine-twenty-five," Voss said, bored now, cruel now.

"Nine-fifty," said a voice from the doorway, "on behalf of the Fairweather Coast Community Trust."

Every head turned. Standing there, slightly damp from sea fog, holding a folder like a shield: Ruth Calloway, retired schoolteacher, the woman who had failed me in eleventh-grade English and apparently never stopped grading. Behind her crowded half the town — the co-op men, the bakery, Tom Beckett, the pastor, my own landlord, people whose names I knew and faces I'd spent twelve years avoiding.

Ruth opened the folder. "Nine hundred seventy-five thousand. Raised over six weeks. In cash pledges, produce futures, and one very generous bequest from Edith Fairweather's estate that nobody thought to mention because Edith wanted to see who'd fight for it first." She looked directly at me, and her eyes were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "She always said the orchard would keep whoever deserved keeping. We just had to wait and see."

Jonah's hand found mine. Her fingers were cold and shaking and gripping hard enough to hurt.

"One million," Voss said, but the word had gone hollow, and everyone heard it.

Nobody followed him. Dale looked at Ruth. Ruth nodded once, slow, like a judge.

"The fair warning," Dale said. "Going once. Going twice —"

Marisol stood up.

The whole hall held its breath. She walked past her brother — past me — to the clerk's table, and I felt the floor tilt under everything. Then she signed something, slid it across, and turned to face the room.

"Meridian withdraws," she said. Her voice didn't shake, but her hands did, and I recognized that too. "Effective immediately. We're — the company is satisfied with its coastal portfolio." A beat. "I'm satisfied," she added, quieter, looking at no one and everyone, "that some things shouldn't be paved."

Voss stared at her. She stared back with our father's jaw, and for the first time in my life I saw her win an argument with him without saying a word.

Dale Pruitt raised his gavel like a man raising a toast. "Sold. To the Fairweather Coast Community Trust — and to whoever those two intend to inflict themselves upon for the next fifty years."

The hall erupted. Jonah made a sound beside me that was half laugh, half sob, and I caught her as the crowd closed in, shaking hands, clapping backs, Mrs. Okonkwo already announcing pies for a harvest festival whether anyone had asked or not.

Over the noise, Marisol appeared in front of us. She looked at Jonah first. "Your chapter one needs work," she said. "The hero should explain himself sooner. Readers hate that."

"I'm aware," Jonah said.

Marisol looked at me last. Twelve years of silence passed between us in about four seconds, and then she hugged me, brief and fierce, the way we used to as children when the house got loud.

"You burned my contract," she said into my shoulder.

"I know."

"Good." She pulled back, eyes bright and dangerous. "But you're going to tell her about the ledgers. Tonight. Or I will."

She walked out into the fog before I could answer, and Jonah turned to me with a question already forming, and I realized the auction hadn't ended anything at all.

It had just rung the opening bell.
