## 16. Rescue in Whiteout

The door of the inn banged open at four in the afternoon, and the wind came in with it like something that had been waiting all day for the invitation.

I was at the long table in the great room, pencil in hand, the unfinished score spread out before me — Theo's piece, our piece, still nameless, still full of holes where my hand couldn't go and his pride wouldn't bend. The pages lifted. A pencil rolled off the table and I let it go, because Julian was standing in the doorway with snow on his shoulders and a look on his face that stopped my heart before he said a single word.

"He's not back," Julian said.

"Back from where?"

"The north trail. He went up this morning. He said—" Julian's jaw worked. "He said he needed to walk. After yesterday."

After yesterday. After the frozen rehearsal, after the recriminations, after Theo had stood in the middle of the great room with his hands shaking — not the way mine shook, but with fury — and said, *I can't do this here, I can't breathe in this house,* and walked out into a morning that had been merely gray.

And then the storm had come down off the ridge like a lid closing.

"How long has it been snowing like this?" I asked, though I knew. I'd been watching it since noon, telling myself it would pass, telling myself Theo Vance had grown up in these mountains too, telling myself anything rather than feel what I felt now, which was cold starting at the base of my skull and spreading downward.

"Three hours. Maybe more." Julian was already pulling his coat from the peg by the door. "Visibility is nothing. Mrs. Aldous says the trail markers will be buried past the second switchback."

"Then we wait for it to break—"

"It's not going to break tonight. You know how these storms sit on the mountain when they mean it." He looked at me across the room, and there was no conductor in his face, no critic, none of that terrible precision he wore like armor. There was just a man, fifty-some years old, afraid. "Kestrel. He went up there angry. Angry people don't turn around at the first sign of weather. They push through because turning around means admitting they were wrong to come out at all."

I knew that. God help me, I knew that better than anyone; I had spent ten years pushing through storms of my own manufacture because turning around meant admitting what I'd sacrificed had been real.

I took my coat off the peg.

"You can't come," Julian said. "Your hand—"

"My hand is fine for walking." I pulled the glove on over it, the left one, the one I wore even indoors now, as if the glove could hold me together at the seams. "And you're not going up that trail alone. If he's hurt, you'll need someone to stay with him while you run back. If you're hurt, someone needs to bring help. That's arithmetic, Julian, not sentiment."

For a moment he looked like he might argue. Then something in my face must have ended the argument, because he nodded once, sharp, and threw me the coil of rope from the hall closet.

Mrs. Aldous wanted to come. We told her no — someone had to keep the fire high and the lights in the windows, someone had to be the fixed point we navigated home by. She pressed a thermos of coffee into my good hand and said, "Bring him back, dear. Both of you," and I didn't ask which *both* she meant, because I was afraid the answer mattered more than I could bear.

* * *

The world outside the inn had been erased.

I had seen storms before — everyone who lives near mountains has — but never one like this, never a whiteout, where the sky and the ground surrender their border and become one seething gray-white nothing. Ten feet from the door, the inn vanished. Twenty feet, and I had only the pull of the rope in my left hand to tell me Julian existed.

We moved tied together, ten feet apart, because if one of us went into a drift or off the trail edge, the other might feel it and stop. The rope bit into my palm through the glove. The wind came in gusts that shoved like hands, that found every gap in my collar and filled it with needles. My eyes streamed and froze at the corners. Every breath was a small violence.

"Left fork!" Julian shouted, and his voice arrived shredded, half of it eaten by the wind. "Stay against the bank!"

I couldn't see the bank. I trusted him. That was the astonishing thing — that I trusted him, Julian Marsh, the man whose review had ended my life in nine hundred words, whose voice I had heard in my head for a decade every time I picked up a bow. But here was the truth the storm stripped me down to: the man on the other end of the rope had walked beside me for two weeks, had argued with me about dynamics until midnight, had sat with me in silence while I relearned three measures of Theo's piece forty times. Whatever Julian Marsh had been at thirty-five, the man holding my rope was someone I had, without deciding to, begun to believe.

"Theo!" I shouted it into the white, again and again, until the word lost meaning and became pure sound. "THEO!"

The mountain gave nothing back. Wind, and more wind, and the squeak of snow under boots, and somewhere below us the muffled roar of the creek under its ice.

At the second switchback we found his footprints — or what had been his footprints, half-filled already, a ghost-line of dimples heading up-trail. My heart lurched so hard it hurt.

"He went up," Julian called. "Past the markers."

"Why would he go *up?*"

"Because the view from the overlook is where he used to go when we were young!" Julian's laugh was terrible, breathless. "He always thought altitude solved things!"

We climbed. The trail steepened and the wind doubled its hatred and my legs began to burn in a way that reminded me, absurdly, of the last movement of the Shostakovich, of playing until the muscles failed and the music carried you anyway. I put my head down and climbed. I did not think about my hand inside its glove. I did not think about the letter I had written two nights ago and hidden in the score, the letter that said everything, that waited for courage I wasn't sure I owned. I thought only: *up, up, he is up there, and I have not yet said one true thing to his face, and I will not let the mountain take that from me.*

* * *

We found him at the overlook, or nearly — the rope went taut, Julian stumbled, swore, and then his voice cracked open across the wind in a register I had never heard from him:

"THEO! THEO, HERE!"

A shape. A dark shape against the white, ten yards off the trail, half-buried at the base of a wind-carved fir. I was moving before I decided to move, dropping to my knees in the drift, digging with my good hand, my bad one, both of them, gloves and all.

He was conscious. That was the first mercy. His eyes found mine and focused slowly, and his lips were blue, and he said, muffled and slurred, "Kestrel. You're — hallucinating. Good. Always liked your — taste in ghosts."

"Shut up," I said, and started laughing or crying or both, some sound I didn't recognize coming out of me. "Shut up and stand up, you idiot, you beautiful idiot—"

Julian was there, hauling, and between us we got Theo upright, got his arm over Julian's shoulders and mine, got the rope re-rigged so all three of us were bound in a line. He could walk, barely. His right leg had gone through ice somewhere; the boot was frozen solid; every step made him hiss.

"Talk to him," Julian ordered me. "Keep him talking. People stop walking when they stop talking."

So I talked. Into Theo's ear, shouting over the wind, I talked the whole way down — about the festival, about the piece, about the cello section of the youth orchestra I'd once coached, about anything, everything, and he answered in fragments, and each fragment was a proof of life.

"You wrote — eight bars — for the left hand alone," he slurred at one point. "In the slow movement. Did you notice?"

"I noticed."

"I wrote it before I knew — about your hand. Before any of it. Years ago. It was always — going to be yours."

Something broke open in my chest and I kept walking, kept talking, because stopping was death and feeling it fully was also, somehow, death, and I could do neither.

* * *

The lights of the inn appeared out of the gray like a promise kept. Mrs. Aldous had kept them burning in every window, and they were the most beautiful thing I have ever seen, before or since.

We got Theo to the fire. We got the frozen boot off with warm water and patience and Mrs. Aldous's iron nerve. We got blankets and coffee and brandy into him, and color came back to his face by degrees, and Julian sat on the floor beside the couch like a man who had forgotten chairs existed, and I stood with my back to the hearth and watched them both and understood that something had permanently changed in the architecture of the three of us.

Theo caught my eye. "You came up the mountain," he said. His voice was still rough. "Both of you. In that."

"You'd have done it for us," I said.

"No," Theo said simply. "I'd have wanted to. I don't know that I'd have managed it." He looked from me to Julian and back. "Ten years I spent hating you both. And you climb through a whiteout to drag me home." His eyes were wet. He didn't hide it. "I don't know what to do with that."

"Nothing," Julian said. "Tonight you do nothing but live. Tomorrow—" He stopped. Looked at me.

Tomorrow. The concert. The board. The evidence in Julian's satchel, the scandal we had agreed to detonate in front of the whole valley, the truth about who had paid for my destruction and why I had let them believe it was mine.

"Tomorrow," I said, "we finish the piece."

But Theo was still looking at me, and there was something new in his face — not the old wound, not gratitude exactly, something rawer. "Kestrel. When I was up there, freezing, I kept thinking — I kept thinking about the night of the hearing. What you said to the board. Word for word, I've remembered it for ten years." His voice dropped. "And today, on that trail, it finally sounded wrong. Not cruel. *Rehearsed.* Like a speech someone taught you."

The fire popped. Julian had gone very still.

"Tell me I'm wrong," Theo said quietly. "Or tell me the truth. But tonight, Kestrel — after today — I think we're owed one or the other."

I looked at him, at the man I had dragged down a mountain, at the man whose future I had traded away in a windowless room when I was twenty-six, believing love meant leaving him unburdened. The letter was upstairs, folded into the score, patient as snowfall.

I opened my mouth.
