## 2. A Man Off the Ferry

The ferry was called the *Maren B*, and it smelled of diesel and herring and forty years of small regrets, which Theo Vasquez decided was as good a place as any to become someone else.

He had practiced the name on the drive up the coast, saying it under his breath at red lights like a man rehearsing a toast. Tom Vail. Two syllables, forgettable, the kind of name a bartender would repeat back without interest. *Vail.* He'd found it in a phone book in a bus station in Astoria, running his finger down the column of V's with the peculiar giddiness of a man choosing his own witness protection. There was something almost funny about it—*veil*, for God's sake, he'd actually picked a name that was a synonym for hiding—and he'd laughed once, alone in the parking lot, and the laugh had come out wrong, like a cough.

The ferry deck pitched. Gulls hung motionless over the wake, riding the wind without effort, and Theo envied them their one talent: they never pretended to be anything but what they were.

Thirty-one years he'd been exactly what he was. Valedictorian. Law review. Deputy prosecutor at thirty-one, youngest in the office's history, a man whose face had been in the newspaper often enough that his mother kept a scrapbook. And then eleven months ago a federal indictment with his name on it, and the scrapbook went into a box, and the box went into his sister's garage, and Theo had learned the first lesson of disgrace: people don't look at you differently so much as they stop looking at you at all. You become a hole in the shape of a man.

He hadn't done it. That was the thing no one wanted to hear anymore—the thing that had stopped being interesting somewhere around the second week of the scandal. He hadn't taken the money, hadn't buried the evidence, hadn't traded leniency for favors in the Kessler case. But he also couldn't prove he hadn't, because proving it meant implicating men above him, and the men above him had spent twenty years making sure the architecture of the office could bear any weight except theirs. Every document that might have cleared him had a signature missing or a date smudged or a witness who suddenly couldn't remember. The truth, Theo had discovered, was not a defense. It was just another theory of the case, and a poorly funded one.

So he'd resigned. Not fled—resigned, with a letter his lawyer had called "dignified" in the tone people use for coffins. The trial was set for March. He had four months. Four months to decide whether he'd walk into that courtroom and tell a version of the truth that saved himself by burning everyone else, or a version that burned him and changed nothing, or—and this was the option he told himself he'd come to the island to consider—a version that simply never got told, because the defendant failed to appear and became a story other people told about him until even the story wore out.

Invisibility. He was rehearsing it now, standing at the rail while the island assembled itself out of the fog ahead: a dark green shoulder of land, a white lighthouse like an exclamation point, a scatter of rooftops around a harbor the color of hammered tin. He practiced being nobody. Shoulders down. Eyes soft, unfocused, the gaze of a man looking at nothing in particular because nothing in particular was his business. He'd read once—you read a lot when you can't sleep—that fugitives give themselves away by walking too purposefully. So he made himself amble. He made himself a man who ambled.

The ferry bumped the dock. A deckhand threw lines to two men waiting on the pier, and the whole operation had the bored choreography of something done ten thousand times, and Theo stepped onto the island with a duffel bag, a rented room arranged by mail, and a heart going like a fist on a door.

Nobody looked at him. He catalogued this the way he used to catalogue jury reactions—noting, filing, adjusting. An old woman with a cart of seed potatoes glanced up and away. A boy on a bicycle rang past without slowing. The harbor smelled of salt and creosote and woodsmoke, and somewhere behind the fog line a dog was barking at something only dogs could see, and Theo stood on the dock of Harrow Island feeling the strangest sensation of his life, which was relief.

No one knew him here. That was the entire plan, and it was working, and the relief was so violent it frightened him. Because underneath the relief was another thing, quieter and colder, and it said: *this is what it will be like forever. No one knowing you. No one looking. You came here to disappear and you're already good at it.*

He walked up from the dock along a road lined with blackberry bramble gone feral, following directions he'd memorized from a county map. The rental was a cottage above the cove, the listing had said—furnished, month to month, no questions asked. He liked that last part. He'd paid three months upfront in cash and the landlady, a Mrs. Okafor with reading glasses on a beaded chain, had looked at him exactly long enough to say, "You'll want the good blanket; nights come cold early here," and nothing else. He could have kissed her.

But the cottage wasn't available until tomorrow. Some mix-up with the cleaning. Mrs. Okafor had been genuinely sorry, pressing the key to nothing into his hand like a promise. So Theo had a day to fill and a duffel bag getting heavier by the mile, and that was how he came to be walking the fence line of an orchard in the late afternoon light, telling himself he was only stretching his legs.

He'd known the orchard was here. Of course he had. He'd grown up with the knowledge the way you grow up knowing where your own scars are. His grandfather had worked these trees—had pruned and grafted and cursed at them through forty summers, until the fire took everything, and after the fire there had been a settlement, and after the settlement there had been silence, and the silence between the Vasquez family and the Marchetti family had lasted longer than most marriages do.

Theo had not come to the island for the orchard. He wanted that on the record, before whatever court existed inside him. He'd come because it was far, and small, and off-season, and because a man awaiting trial needed a place where his face meant nothing. The orchard was incidental. The orchard was geography.

Except that now he was standing in front of it, and the trees rose up out of the ground like a congregation, old apples gone half-wild, their branches knuckled and gray, holding on to a few late leaves the way old men hold on to arguments. The grass between the rows was waist-high and going to seed. Nobody had tended this ground properly in years, and yet the trees stood on, stubborn, producing fruit no one picked that fell and rotted sweet in the grass, feeding the wasps and the deer and nothing else.

There was a gate. Weathered cedar, sagging on its hinges, and on it a padlock—brass, older style, polished at the edges from decades of hands. Even from outside the fence Theo could read the initials stamped into its face, worn but legible, the way certain names wear into a family's history:

**E.M.**

Elias Marchetti.

Theo put his hand on the fence rail. The wood was silvered and soft. Somewhere beyond the third row of trees, he knew, stood the house Elias Marchetti had built, and the lighthouse beyond that, and in the lighthouse lived whoever was left of the Marchettis. He didn't know who. He'd stopped asking his mother questions about the island years ago, when he was sixteen and she'd gone quiet in a way that ended conversations rather than pausing them.

*You could turn around,* he told himself. *This isn't your errand.*

But his feet didn't move, and Theo recognized the paralysis for what it was, because he'd spent eleven months becoming an expert in his own evasions. He wasn't looking at an orchard. He was looking at the last unopened file of his childhood—the fire, the settlement, the grandfather who came home from the mainland one final time smelling of smoke and lawyer's cologne and never spoke the family name with pride again. Theo had been nine years old. He remembered his grandfather standing at this same fence, maybe, or one like it, gripping the rail hard enough to whiten his knuckles, saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing, all the way home.

The padlock hung there like a period at the end of a sentence.

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor that runs to irony, Theo heard footsteps on the road behind him.

He turned with the smoothness of a man who had nothing to hide and therefore everything, and saw a figure coming up the lane carrying a canvas feed sack over one shoulder. Tall. Dark hair tied back. Moving with the easy stride of someone who knew every stone in the road personally. As the figure drew closer, details resolved: work jacket, rubber boots, a woman, mid-thirties, with a face that—

Theo's stomach dropped through the boards of the world.

He knew that face. He had known that face across a decade of summers, across a picnic table at a church social, across the aisle of a school bus that ran the island loop, across the specific and terrible distance of two families who used to be friends and then were not. Ulyana Marchetti. Little Ulyana Marchetti, who at eleven had thrown a crab apple at his head with sniper accuracy, who at thirteen had defended him in a fistfight he hadn't started, who at fifteen had stopped speaking to him entirely, and who was now—older, sharper, her jaw set like a keel—walking toward him with the unhurried confidence of a person on her own island.

She hadn't seen him yet. Twenty yards. Fifteen. Theo did the arithmetic of escape and found no exit that didn't make things worse. Running would confirm guilt. Hiding was impossible. Introducing himself as Tom Vail to a woman whose family history was braided with his own was either brilliant or insane, and he had approximately ten seconds to decide which.

Ten seconds is a long time. In ten seconds a man can rehearse invisibility and watch it fail.

He settled on the only strategy that had ever served him well in a courtroom and never once in his life: he would say very little, and let the other party build the case against themselves.

Ulyana Marchetti slowed. Her eyes went from his face to the duffel bag to the padlock, and something moved behind them—recognition, or its careful suppression, he couldn't tell. She stopped six feet away, close enough that he could smell cut grass and diesel fuel and, faintly, woodsmoke, though there was no fire anywhere.

"You're standing at my gate," she said. Not hostile. Worse: neutral. The voice of a woman taking inventory.

"Just passing through," Theo said. "Admiring the trees."

"They're not much to admire in October."

"I disagree." He gestured at the orchard with a hand he kept steady through sheer professional discipline. "Old stock. Somebody grafted half these rows by hand—look at the union scars. Whoever did it cared more than the maintenance suggests."

Something flickered across her face at that—surprise, quickly filed away. "You know fruit trees."

"My grandfather did. I absorbed some by proximity." All true. All useless. All safe.

She shifted the feed sack on her shoulder. Her eyes were gray-green, the exact color of the sea before weather, and they stayed on him with a patience that felt like a searchlight sweeping water. "Passing through to where? There's nowhere *through*. The road ends at the cove."

"The cottage above the cove. I'm renting it." He extended the lie like a man laying down a card he hoped wasn't marked. "Tom Vail."

A pause. The wind moved through the top of the orchard, and a single yellow leaf let go and spiraled down between them, landing in the grass like a small surrender.

"Tom Vail," she repeated, without inflection, and Theo watched her weigh the name and find it wanting. "Well, Mr. Vail. This orchard is private property. The gate stays locked."

"Understood." He nodded, already turning, already composing his retreat, and then—because some part of him that had nothing to do with strategy and everything to do with nine-year-old boys and fires and grandfathers made him stop—he looked back at her and said, "Whoever Elias Marchetti was, he grafted those trees like a man writing letters to the future. Someone should read them."

Ulyana went very still.

It lasted perhaps two seconds. Then her expression closed like a drawer, and she said, "Enjoy your stay on the island, Mr. Vail," and walked past him up the lane without another word, her boots striking the packed earth in a rhythm that sounded, to Theo's overtrained ears, exactly like a verdict being reserved.

He watched her go until the fog took her, and then he stood alone at the locked gate with his duffel bag and his borrowed name, and the padlock caught the last of the light and held it, brass and patient, like a small sun that answered to no one.

*E.M.*, it said. *Elias Marchetti.*

Theo turned and walked back down toward the cove, rehearsing invisibility step by step, and discovering—as the fog thickened around him and the first drops of evening rain touched his face—that the rehearsal had a flaw in it he should have foreseen.

He had come to an island where everyone knew everyone, wearing a name that fit him like a coat two sizes too small, on land his own family had once bled for, and the first person he'd met was the one woman alive who could look at him and see straight through both the alias and the years.

Tomorrow the cottage would be ready. Tomorrow he would begin the quiet life, the small life, the invisible one.

Tonight, walking down through the fog with the rain coming harder and the lighthouse beam sweeping overhead in long white strokes—*once, twice, pause; once, twice, pause*, like something breathing, like something keeping count—Theo Vasquez understood that he had not escaped his trial at all.

He had only changed venues.
