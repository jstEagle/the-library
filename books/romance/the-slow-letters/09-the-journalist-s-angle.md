## 9. The Journalist's Angle

The journalist arrived on the Tuesday mail boat wearing a coat too fine for the island and a smile calibrated for people who wanted something.

Ulyana saw him first from the lighthouse gallery, where she'd gone to check the lamp's rotation before the fog rolled in off the strait. He stood at the end of the dock in a charcoal overcoat, holding a leather satchel the way other men held fishing rods—like an instrument of patience. Even at that distance she could read him. Cities taught a particular posture: shoulders squared toward opportunity, weight on the balls of the feet, always half-ready to move toward whoever mattered most. The islanders around him moved like driftwood. He moved like a hook.

She told herself it was none of her business. She had gotten good at telling herself things.

By noon he had introduced himself at the general store as Daniel Reyes, *freelance*, and bought coffee he clearly didn't drink, and by two o'clock Marisol at the post office was already on the party line with the shape of the story: mainland man, nice shoes, asking questions about the Vasquez trial. Asking questions about a man named Theo Brandt who had rented the old Kowalski cottage in June and hadn't been seen since except with his sleeves rolled in somebody's orchard.

Ulyana heard it third-hand, standing in her own kitchen with a jar of plum preserves going warm in her hands, and she did not drop the jar. That was the thing about her, she thought later. She never dropped anything. She just carried it until the weight changed her posture permanently.

*Theo Brandt.* The alias sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Not because she hadn't known—I've known since the rope, she might have said, if anyone had asked, though no one had—but because hearing it spoken aloud in Marisol's bright gossiping voice made it real in a way her own private knowledge never was. She had known and said nothing. She had pruned trees beside him and split kindling beside him and let him teach her grandfather's grafting cuts back to her as if he were learning them, and all of it under a name that wasn't his, and she had called that coexistence.

Now a man in a charcoal coat was going to call it something else.

He found her at four o'clock, at the orchard gate, which was where everyone eventually found her this season. He came up the path with his satchel and his smile and stopped a polite six feet away, close enough to talk, far enough to claim he never trespassed.

"Ms. Marchetti." He pronounced it correctly, three syllables, Italianate. "Daniel Reyes. I'm writing a piece on Theodore Vasquez."

"I don't know anyone by that name."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You know Theo Brandt. Rents the Kowalski cottage. Six-two, bad left knee, terrible taste in jokes. Co-manages this orchard with you under a deed error the county still hasn't corrected." He opened the satchel—not reaching for anything, just letting her see the folder inside, the printed edge of a photograph. "I've been on the island nine hours. You'd be amazed what people tell a man who buys their coffee."

The wind came down through the apple rows and moved the leaves in that long silver ripple they made before rain. Ulyana set her hand flat on the gatepost. Her grandfather had planed that post smooth forty years ago; she knew its grain better than her own signature.

"You should leave," she said.

"In a minute. I want to give you something first, free of charge, because I think you're the only person on this rock who'll understand what it means." He drew out a folded page and held it up without offering it. "Arraignment date. November fourth. Federal courthouse, Seattle. Obstruction, conspiracy, eleven counts attached to the Alderman case. His counsel filed for dismissal in September; the motion was denied October second. He has until the fourth to appear or a warrant issues." Reyes folded the page away again, unhurried. "That's nineteen days from today."

The arithmetic happened in her body before her mind consented to it. Nineteen days. First frost usually came the last week of October. He had promised her the truth by first frost. He had shaken her hand on it in the shed with cider on his knuckles, and she had believed him—or worse, she had believed she was being patient, generous, magnanimous, when really she had simply given him a longer leash than she'd have given anyone else and called the length love's preliminary sketch.

"And you're going to print all this," she said. "His name, his face, the date. So every reporter in Seattle knows exactly which ferry to meet."

Reyes shrugged, and the shrug was honest, which somehow made it worse. "That's the job. But here's the part I actually came to say." He stepped closer, and up close his eyes were tired in a way that didn't match the coat. "I've read the case file twice. The indictment is garbage. It's built on a chain of custody so rotten it shouldn't hold a screen door, and everyone involved knows it, and Vasquez is going down anyway because someone senior needed a body and he was convenient. I'm not writing a takedown, Ms. Marchetti. I'm writing the story of how the machine eats its own. And there's a version of that story where a woman on an island spent a summer teaching a fugitive prosecutor how to prune apples, and it's a beautiful version, and it makes him human instead of a headline." He paused. "But I can't write it unless someone talks to me. And right now the only person on this island who matters to him won't even confirm he exists."

"He doesn't," Ulyana said. "Exist. As far as I'm concerned."

Reyes looked at her for a long moment, and then he nodded slowly, almost respectfully, and closed the satchel. "My grandmother was from a place like this," he said. "Sardinia. She used to say the sea keeps everything you throw in it and gives it back when you least want it." He turned to go, then stopped. "Nineteen days. Whatever he hasn't told you yet—it's the thing that matters most. It always is."

She watched him walk back down the path, coat too fine, step too certain, and she stood at the gate until the light went long and copper across the water, and then she went to find Theo.

* * *

He was in the east rows with the pruning saw, working a Gravenstein that had grown wild past any sensible shape, and he heard her coming—he always heard her coming, some old habit from a life of courtrooms—and he didn't turn around. That was the first answer.

"Theo Brandt," she said.

The saw stopped mid-stroke.

"Or should I do it properly? Theodore Elias Vasquez. Former assistant United States attorney. Eleven counts. Arraignment November fourth, which is nineteen days away, which means first frost comes before your deadline does, which means—" Her voice did something she hated, caught on something, and she pushed through it. "Which means you shook my hand knowing you'd already broken the deal."

He set the saw down on the grass with exaggerated care, the way men handle objects when they're afraid of what their hands will do otherwise. When he turned, his face had the gray look of a man who has been waiting months for a specific knock at the door.

"How much do you know?"

"Everything that fits on one page of a federal indictment. A very charming man shared it with me at my gate." She kept six feet between them—the same polite distance, and she registered bitterly that she'd learned it from the same species. "What I don't know is why you're standing in my grandfather's orchard under a fake name instead of answering a subpoena."

"Because the subpoena is the trap." He said it quietly, without drama, and that quietness was somehow the most frightening thing he'd offered her all summer. "If I show up on the fourth, I testify, and the only way the testimony helps me is if I give them what they want. Which is my mentor. Marcus Hale. Twenty-six years on the bench-adjacent side of the building, gave me my first job, spoke at my swearing-in. They want me to say he ordered the evidence buried in the Alderman case. And here's the problem, Ulyana." He took a breath. "He may have. I genuinely don't know. What I know is that the file I was handed had already been cleaned before it reached me, and either he cleaned it or someone above him did, and if I testify to what I suspect, I destroy a man on a guess. If I testify to what I know, I perjure myself. There is no honest version of that courtroom for me. So I resigned, and I ran, and I picked the most remote place I could find on a map, and then the county—" he laughed, short and awful, "—the county put me in an orchard with you. I've stopped believing in coincidence since June."

She stood very still among the apple trees. Somewhere behind her a crow was working the fence line, making its dry wooden sound.

"So that's it," she said. "That's the truth. Delivered nineteen days early, technically. Congratulations. You met the deadline."

"Ulyana—"

"No. Let's do the math, since you're so fond of clean ledgers." She heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used for editors, for contracts, for anyone she didn't trust with the mess underneath. "In July you learned my grandfather's grafting notation in one afternoon. Nobody learns that notation in one afternoon. I showed you the notebook and you read it like a man reading his own handwriting, and I told myself, *he's quick, he's careful, he respects the work.* In August you asked me three separate times whether Grandpa ever wrote anything else. Letters. Accounts. Anything about the fire year. Three times, casual as weather. And I answered you, Theo. I answered you honestly, every time, because I didn't know there was a question underneath the question."

Something moved through his face—she would remember it afterward as the exact moment the ground shifted under him, though at the time she read it only as guilt, plain and sufficient.

"What fire?" he said.

And there it was. The lie. Small, practiced, delivered with the same steady calm he'd used on everything else all summer, and she watched him deploy it and felt something in her chest perform a cold little calculation, itemized and total.

"You just told me you don't believe in coincidence," she said softly. "But you asked me about a fire I never mentioned."

The crow lifted off the fence. The leaves rippled. Theo stood in the row with the saw at his feet and the whole green world tilting around him, and she watched him understand, watch him arrive at the edge of whatever he knew and refuse to fall over it.

"Ulyana." His voice cracked on the second syllable, and for one unguarded second he looked less like a prosecutor than like the boy who'd once sat on her grandfather's dock eating plums with juice to his elbows, the boy from before whatever happened between their families, before the silence that had lasted twenty years until a clerical error ended it. "There are things I can't tell you yet. Not because I'm lying to you. Because I'm still finding out whether they're true, and if I say them wrong, they become true in the way that destroys people. Words are load-bearing. You of all people know that. You write fiction—you know what a sentence does once it's out of your mouth."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't you dare make this literary. This isn't craft, Theo. This is a woman who let you into the one place she keeps, and you paid rent in installments." Her eyes were burning and she refused to blink, refused to give him the mercy of looking away. "I gave you pieces. All summer. Pieces of the orchard, pieces of my grandfather, pieces of—" she stopped, because the sentence had a cliff at the end of it and she wasn't going over it in front of him. "And you gave me a résumé with the interesting parts redacted. That's the arithmetic. That's what we are. An exchange rate."

"That's not—"

"Nineteen days." She picked up the fallen saw and held it out to him, handle first, because she was her grandfather's granddaughter and she would be courteous if it killed her. "You have nineteen days and a promise older than this one. First frost. Everything. If there's a single syllable after that that I learn from a stranger in a good coat, then you can take your half of the harvest and your half of the deed and explain to the county why the co-management failed."

She walked out of the row. She did not run, though every nerve in her legs voted for it. At the gate she stopped once, hand on the planed-smooth post, and looked back down the long aisle of apples, and he was still standing where she'd left him, small now between the trees, a man rehearsing invisibility who had just been seen completely.

* * *

That night she climbed the tower and lit the lamp and watched the beam swing its slow white arm across the black water, and she tried to write and couldn't, and tried not to think and couldn't do that either.

Because here was the thing she circled all night and never landed on: Reyes had said *whatever he hasn't told you yet, it's the thing that matters most,* and Reyes was right, and she knew it, because she was keeping one herself.

Downstairs, in the wall behind the pantry shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, her grandfather's letters waited—the ones she'd found in June, the ones about the fire, the ones she had hidden from Theo in July when he asked his first casual question, the ones that could burn down everything either of them was pretending to build.

Two liars in one orchard, she thought, turning the lamp's brass key. And only one of us getting paid for it.

The beam swept the water, swept the dark, swept the orchard where he slept, and Ulyana Marchetti stood in the light she kept for strangers and understood, with perfect clarity, that frost was coming early this year.
