## 17. The Last Ferry Before Frost

The radio said the ferry would run at noon and not again until spring.

Ulyana heard it twice—once at six, when the announcer's voice crackled through the static like a man reading his own eulogy, and once at nine, when she was already standing in the orchard with pruning shears in her hand and no memory of having picked them up. The trees had gone bare-boned overnight. Frost had come in the small hours and taken the last leaves without ceremony, leaving them lying in the grass like letters no one had bothered to open.

She had been avoiding the orchard for eleven days. She had been avoiding Theo for eleven days too, which was harder, because he lived four hundred yards away and because the island had a way of folding people into the same small rooms whether they consented or not. They had passed each other at the store, on the cliff road, once in the churchyard where neither of them had any business being. Each time they had performed the same careful pantomime: a nod, a half-smile with nothing behind it, eyes that touched and slid away like wet stones.

The pages were still in the tin box under her bed. She had taken them out every night since the storm, read them until the words wore grooves in her, and put them back. Her grandfather's handwriting, cramped and apologetic even on the page where he confessed to letting the Vasquez orchard burn and saying nothing. *I told myself it was protection,* he had written. *It was fear wearing protection's coat.* She knew the coat well. She had been wearing one herself all autumn.

Today the coat came off, or it didn't, and either way the ferry was coming at noon.

She made the preserves first. It was absurd—the whole gesture was absurd, a jar of apples cooked down to amber in a kitchen that smelled of cinnamon and sugar while the man they were for packed his bags four hundred yards away—but her hands wanted work, and work was the only language she trusted before noon. She used the Gravensteins from the first-fruit tree, the ones they'd pressed for cider back in September when the whole town had crowded into the yard and Theo had told a joke so terrible that old Marta Kowalski had snorted cider through her nose, and Ulyana had laughed—really laughed, head back, helpless—and hated herself for three days afterward.

The wax sealed clean. She held the jar up to the window light and watched the sun come through it like a lamp through stained glass. On the lid, in her neatest hand, she wrote: *First fruit. Marchetti–Vasquez orchard.* Both names. She hadn't planned to write both names. Her pen had decided on its own.

* * *

At eleven she walked down to the dock with the jar in one hand and the tin box under her arm, and the weight of both was nothing compared to the weight of what she intended to say, which was, as far as she could tell, almost nothing at all.

The island had turned out to see him off—or to see him go, which was not quite the same thing. Half the town stood along the seawall in coats and wool caps, and Ulyana understood, watching them, that everyone knew. Of course they knew. The journalist had seen to that; the trial date had been in the mainland papers; Mrs. Okonkwo at the post office read the mainland papers the way other people read scripture. But knowing had not hardened them. That was the thing about the island that mainlanders never believed: it collected its outcasts the way driftwood collects at the high-tide line, and after a season nobody remembered which pieces had washed in and which had always been there. Marta pressed a loaf of bread into Theo's hands. The Halvorsen boys, who had spent October following him around the orchard asking questions about grafting with the solemnity of junior surgeons, stood at attention like an honor guard. Even Father Brennan, who disapproved of nearly everything on principle, shook his hand and said something low that made Theo's jaw tighten in the way it did when he was keeping himself from feeling a thing.

Ulyana stayed at the edge of it, by the fuel shed, and watched.

He looked different from the man who had stepped off the ferry in September with a thin alias and thinner luggage. Not better, exactly. Lighter in some places and hollowed in others, the way a house looks after a fire has been put out—still standing, still itself, but with the smoke smell never quite leaving the walls. He wore the canvas jacket she'd told him was useless against island wind, and he was wearing it anyway, collar up, and she thought: *he'll be cold in the city, and he won't buy another one, because that's who he is now, a man who keeps wearing the jacket someone scolded him about.*

The ferry horn sounded once, far off past the point, muffled by fog that hadn't learned yet that it was supposed to be a winter fog, thin and mean, instead of this soft gray blanket that made the whole sound look like a painting of itself.

Theo turned from the Halvorsen boys and saw her.

Something crossed his face—not surprise, because he had known she would come, and not relief either, because he had known she might not. It was the look of a man standing at a door he has knocked on many times without answer. He crossed the dock toward her, weaving between crates and coil rope, and stopped a polite distance away, the distance of colleagues, of co-signatories, of two people bound by paper and nothing else.

"You came," he said.

"It's my dock."

"I know." A ghost of a smile. "I checked the deed."

And there it was—their old language, the contract-speak they'd built in September to keep from saying anything real, and she felt how flimsy it had become, how it barely covered them anymore, like a tarp blown loose in the gale. Eleven days of silence and he opened with the deed. She almost loved him for it. She almost walked away for it.

"The ferry's early," she said. "Fog or no fog, Captain Reyes doesn't wait. He says winter starts at the waterline."

"I know. I've been standing here since ten."

"Martians could have invaded. You'd have missed it."

"Then the island would've been yours again." He looked out at the water, where the ferry was resolving out of the fog, white and blunt and indifferent. "You'd have survived. You did fine for thirty years before I showed up with my paperwork."

"That's not—" She stopped. Breathed. The tin box under her arm suddenly weighed what it actually weighed. "Theo."

He turned back to her fully then, and up close she could see he hadn't slept, or had slept badly, the kind of sleep that is just lying down with your eyes closed while your whole life files past in the dark. His hands were steady. She noticed that. Whatever else the city had done to him, whatever the trial and the disgrace and the mentor's poisoned gifts had done, his hands were steady, and she thought of him on the stand with those hands flat on the rail, telling the truth with the same steadiness, and her chest did something complicated and painful.

"I need to say some things," she said, "and you're going to let me say them without fixing them, because fixing things is what got us here."

"All right."

"You lied to me. About who you were, at the start, and I understand why, and understanding hasn't made it hurt less, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. You found those pages and you waited for me to give them to you instead of asking, and I understand that too, and it also hurt. And I—" Her voice caught on the next part and she made herself push through, because she had promised herself on the walk down that she would not flinch, not here, not at the dock, not with the whole town pretending not to listen. "I hid them because hiding is what my family does. My grandfather burned an orchard and buried the truth in a wall for fifty years and called it protection, and I took those pages and I put them under my bed and I called it *deciding*, but it wasn't deciding. It was the same coat. Fear wearing protection's name. And I have spent my whole life being proud that I'm not a coward, and it turns out I'm just a coward with better lighting."

Theo's breath went out of him slowly. "Ulyana—"

"You said you'd let me finish."

He closed his mouth. Behind them the ferry horn sounded again, closer, and somewhere in the crowd Mrs. Okonkwo said something to Father Brennan that made them both look over and then quickly away.

"So here's what I know," Ulyana said. "You could have taken the deal. Your mentor offered you everything back—your name, your career, your whole shining life—if you destroyed those pages. I know because you told me, in the cider house, when you confessed everything else. And you stood alone in the orchard at night with the offer in your pocket, and you didn't take it. Nobody made you not take it. There was no witness, no jury, no one to see. You just didn't. And then you went to the city and you testified, and you implicated yourself when you didn't have to, and you're going back now for sentencing knowing exactly what it might cost you, and you're boarding that boat anyway." She lifted the tin box and held it between them. "A coward wouldn't do that. So maybe we're both liars about what we are. Maybe that's the one thing we've been honest about all along."

* * *

He took the box the way people take things that are heavier than they look—with both hands, carefully, as if it might go off.

Inside, wrapped in the dish towel from her kitchen because she hadn't owned anything more ceremonial, were the torn pages. Her grandfather's account of the fire. The truth that had ruined one family and sheltered another, written out in a dead man's cramped hand, offered up at last to the light.

Theo unwrapped it right there on the dock. He didn't ask if he could. He read the first page standing, then sat down slowly on a mooring bollard and read the rest, and the crowd behind them went quiet in the way crowds do when they sense they are witnessing something that will be retold for years. Ulyana stood and watched him read her grandfather's shame, and she felt strangely light, strangely cold, the way she imagined a tree feels after the last leaf goes—exposed, but finally able to see the whole shape of itself against the sky.

When he finished, he didn't speak for a long moment. Then: "Why now?"

"Because you asked me once what I was afraid of, and I said nothing, and that was a lie too." She shrugged, and the shrug cost more than the speech had. "Because the ferry comes at noon and not again till spring, and I couldn't spend the winter being my grandfather."

Theo folded the pages along their old creases, gently, and put them inside his jacket, over his heart, which she pretended not to notice and failed completely. "I'll bring them back," he said. "After. However it goes—I'll bring them back."

"Don't bring them back."

He looked up sharply.

"Keep them. They're yours. They were always yours; my family just kept them in escrow for fifty years." She heard her own voice go dry and steady, the novelist's voice, the one that could narrate anything, even this. "Consider it a transfer of title. No clerical errors this time. Both parties present and consenting."

A laugh broke out of him—short, startled, real—and it did something to the air between them, cracked it open the way ice cracks on the pond in late November, all at once and irreversibly. For a second she thought he might reach for her, and she braced for it, wanting it and dreading it in equal measure, and he didn't, and she was grateful, and she was bereft, and both feelings sat in her chest like passengers who refused to disembark.

Instead he stood, and she held out the jar.

He took it and turned it in his hands, reading the label, and when he reached the second name his thumb stopped on it and stayed there, pressing the ink like a man testing whether a wound has healed. "Marchetti–Vasquez," he read.

"The orchard's name now. Everyone on the island already calls it that. Marta started it. I gave up correcting her in October."

"It's good cider," he said quietly. "Best I've ever had. And I grew up in apple country, so I'm qualified."

"You're qualified for very little, Vasquez."

"No," he agreed. "But I'm getting better."

The ferry slid alongside the dock with a groan of tires against pilings, and the deckhands threw lines, and the crowd stirred like a field in wind. Time collapsed. There was no more speech left in her, no more confession, no more cleverness; there was only the fact of him about to step onto a boat and leave, and the winter after that, long and white and full of a silence she had chosen and would now have to live inside.

He shouldered his bag—one bag, he had arrived with one bag and he was leaving with one bag, the alias abandoned somewhere, the old life declined—and paused at the gangway.

"Ulyana."

"Don't make a speech. You're terrible at speeches. You filibuster."

That startled another laugh out of him, and then his face went serious, and he said, simply, "Thank you. For the pages. For—" he gestured vaguely at the dock, the town, the whole gray island, "—all of it. I came here to disappear and instead I—" He stopped, shook his head, gave up on the sentence. Some things, she thought, don't fit in testimony either.

"Go," she said. "Before Reyes leaves without you. He'll do it. He's done it to Father Brennan twice."

Theo boarded. She watched him find a place at the rail, the jar cradled in one arm like something living, the pages flat against his chest. The deckhands cast off. The engine turned over with a sound like a clearing throat.

And then, because the moment demanded it and because she had promised herself she would not flinch, she stepped to the edge of the dock and called out over the widening water, loud enough for the whole town to hear, loud enough for the fog itself to carry:

"Come back for the harvest!"

He turned at the rail. The fog took his face for a moment and gave it back. He raised the jar—raised it high, the way you raise a glass, the way you raise a standard, the way you raise anything you intend to keep—and though she couldn't hear his answer over the engine and the water, she saw his mouth shape it, and she read it easily, the way you read a letter you've been waiting all your life to receive.

*Every harvest.*

The ferry pulled out past the point. The crowd dispersed slowly, reluctantly, the way audiences leave a play they aren't ready to be finished with. Marta squeezed Ulyana's arm without a word. Mrs. Okonkwo was already composing whatever she would say at the post office tomorrow, and Ulyana found she didn't mind. Let them talk. Let the story belong to the island. Stories kept things alive; she had built a career on that.

She stayed on the dock after everyone had gone, long after the ferry's wake had flattened into ordinary water, long after the fog closed the channel like a book.

The winter would be long. The orchard would sleep under snow, and she would keep the light burning every night, and she would write—under her own name, God help her—and she would prune in February and graft in April and watch the buds break in May, counting the days the way her grandfather must once have counted them, except that her grandfather had counted toward nothing, toward safety, toward the grave, and she was counting toward a boat in spring with a man on it who carried her family's worst truth over his heart like a passport.

Faith, she thought, walking back up the hill with her hands in her pockets and the frost crunching under her boots, was just letting go and believing the thing would return. Nobody warned you it felt exactly like loss. Nobody warned you that you do it anyway.

Behind her, the lighthouse turned its beam out over the empty water, patient as a promise, sweeping the fog for a shape that wasn't there yet—

—and in her coat pocket, her fingers found the postcard she'd bought that morning at the store and never mailed, blank except for the address she'd written in a hand steadier than she felt: a city courthouse, a detention wing, a man waiting on a verdict.

She had written it before the dock. Before the pages. Before she knew she'd have the courage.

Now all she had to decide was what to say.
