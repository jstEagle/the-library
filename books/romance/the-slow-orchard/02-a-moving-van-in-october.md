## 2. A Moving Van in October

The van arrived on a Thursday, which felt like an ambush dressed as an ordinary day.

Orsen had been in the back of the shop, elbow-deep in a box of remaindered paperbacks that smelled of damp and other people's abandoned ambitions, when he heard the engine—the particular diesel grumble of something too large for Halloway Lane. He registered it the way one registers weather: distantly, without alarm. Then Ivy called from the front window, her voice pitched with the specific excitement children reserve for spectacle.

"Daddy! There's a *truck* next door."

Next door meant the cottage. The cottage meant nothing, because the cottage had meant nothing for eleven years—had stood empty since old Mrs. Pettibone died, its windows gone the grey-green of pond water, its garden surrendered to bramble and bindweed until the whole lot looked less like a house than a memory of one. Orsen had grown so accustomed to its decay that he'd stopped seeing it, the way you stop seeing your own hands. Someone had bought it in the summer, he remembered; there had been a solicitor's letter, a surveyor poking about with a clipboard, a brief flurry of speculation at the bakery that had died away when no one appeared to claim the place. He had assumed, comfortably, that whoever it was would never actually come. That was how derelict things worked. They stayed derelict. They were safe.

He wiped his hands on his apron—a habit, since the apron was already beyond saving—and went to the window.

The moving van sat angled across the lane like a beached whale, hazard lights blinking their idiot orange pulse against the wet grey morning. Two men in matching fleeces were wrestling a sofa up the cottage path, and behind them came a woman carrying a box marked KITCHEN in black marker, holding it against her hip the way one holds a shield.

Orsen knew her before he saw her face.

He knew her by the way she moved—quick, economical, slightly ahead of her own body, as though she were always arriving a half-second before herself. He knew her by the dark hair escaping its knot at the nape of her neck. He knew her, God help him, from a photograph he had kept for ten years in a drawer beneath his socks, where no one would ever think to look: a woman of perhaps twenty-five, caught mid-laugh at some beach or lakeside, squinting into sun, her arm slung around the shoulders of a girl whose face the camera had blurred beyond recognition.

Wren Halloway.

The name arrived in his mind with the weight of a stone dropped down a well. He counted—he couldn't help counting—one, two, three seconds before she turned, before the box shifted, before her face came around into the grey light of the lane and confirmed what his body had already known and his mind was still refusing to ratify.

It was her. It was *her*. The stranger who had pulled his daughter out of the river ten years ago this November, who had vanished before the ambulance came, before anyone thought to ask her name, before Orsen could do anything but stand in the shallows holding his dripping, sobbing, living child and watch a figure in a soaked green coat walk away up the towpath without looking back.

She was forty feet away, carrying a kitchen into the cottage beside his bookshop.

"Daddy?" Ivy tugged his sleeve. "Do we know them?"

"No," Orsen said, and his voice came out steady, which struck him afterward as a small miracle, a sleight of hand performed by his own throat without his permission. "No, sweetheart. New neighbours, I expect."

"Can I take them something? Mrs. Ferro said when people move in you take them something."

"That was very kind of Mrs. Ferro." His heart was doing something arrhythmic and shameful behind his ribs. "We'll see."

*We'll see* was what he said when he meant *never*, and Ivy, aged eight and fluent in paternal evasion, rolled her eyes and returned to the window to supervise the furniture.

Orsen stayed where he was, half-hidden behind the doorframe of the stockroom, and watched Wren Halloway set down her box on the cottage step, straighten, and look up at the facade of the building she had just bought. She looked at it the way people look at decisions they have already made and are only now beginning to feel. Then her gaze travelled along the row—past the rusted gate, past the leaning fence—and stopped on the bookshop. On the window. On, he realized with a lurch, the exact square of glass behind which he was standing.

He stepped back. It was not graceful. It was the movement of a man who has put his hand on a stove.

*She didn't see you,* he told himself. *Even if she did, she doesn't know you. She's never seen your face. You made sure of that.*

Ten years of making sure of that. Ten years of PO Box 114, of letters written in a deliberately flattened hand, of signing himself only ever as *O.*—a single letter, anonymous as a watermark. Ten years of learning everything he could about her from a distance that would have alarmed her if she'd known its dimensions: that she'd trained as an archivist, that she'd worked in London, that she'd left a job abruptly two years ago for reasons the internet declined to specify, that she had an aunt in this town she'd apparently never visited, dead now eight months, leaving her a cottage she had let rot another season before finally, in October, in the rain, arriving.

He had told himself, all these years, that the letters were enough. That gratitude could live honestly on paper even if its author could not. That someday—someday being a country he visited often and never settled—she might write back, might want to know him, might give him permission to step out from behind the postmark. She never wrote back. He kept writing anyway. Letter one hundred and twenty had gone into the slot last Tuesday, rain-spotted and trembling slightly at the corners because his hands had been unsteady that day for reasons he hadn't examined.

And now here she was. Not at the end of a postal route but at the end of his street. Close enough to touch. Close enough to *suspect*.

Because that was the thing—the thing he had never written down, not even in the burned drafts, not even in the privacy of his own skull at three in the morning. The letters weren't only gratitude. Gratitude was the respectable word he'd built around the truth the way you build a fence around something wild. But the truth was this: somewhere in the decade of writing to her, in inventing a voice for himself that was kinder and braver than his own, in telling her about the bookshop and Ivy and the orchard and the small weather of his days, he had fallen in love with a woman he had met for ninety seconds in the water and never once spoken to.

Ivy asked him once, recently, why he wrote to someone he'd never met. He'd given her a story about gratitude. Children accept those stories. Adults, he suspected, would not.

* * *

By noon the rain had thickened from drizzle to something with intent, and the movers were sheltering under the van's rear awning, drinking tea from a thermos, while Wren Halloway stood in her new doorway surveying boxes with the expression of a general regarding a battlefield she had personally chosen.

Orsen spent the afternoon failing to work. He shelved the same section of poetry twice. He mispriced a first edition by a factor of ten and caught it only because the error was absurd enough to snag even his distracted eye. At four o'clock Ivy announced that Mrs. Ferro had been right, they absolutely had to take the new neighbour something, and produced—from where, he never learned—a Tupperware of ginger biscuits that had been in the shop's back room long enough to qualify as archaeology.

"They're fine," Ivy said, reading his face. "They're the hard kind. Hard ones keep."

There was no arguing with that logic, and besides, some treacherous part of him wanted to go. Wanted it with a hunger that frightened him. So they went—Ivy leading with the biscuits held out before her like an offering, Orsen following with his collar up and his heart conducting itself disgracefully—and crossed the strip of mud and dead bindweed that separated his life from hers.

She opened the door before they knocked. She must have seen them coming through the bare windows; there was no glass in the door yet, or curtains, or anything between the cottage's cold interior and the world.

"Oh," she said. And then, recovering, with a smile that arrived a beat late, like a train after a delay: "Hello. Sorry—you caught me mid-catastrophe."

Up close she was both exactly the photograph and nothing like it. The same quick eyes, grey-green, set deep; the same hair, darker than he'd imagined it in winter light. But the laugh-lines were deeper, and there was a tiredness around her mouth that the photograph hadn't carried, the tiredness of someone who had been carrying something heavy for a long time and had learned to hold it without letting it show.

"We're from next door," Ivy announced, thrusting the Tupperware forward. "These are biscuits. They're the hard kind but they keep."

Wren Halloway laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of her—and Orsen felt the sound land somewhere beneath his sternum and stay there, glowing, like a coal.

"The hard kind are the best kind," she said gravely, accepting the container. "Thank you. I'm Wren. Wren Halloway."

"Ivy," said Ivy. "This is my dad. He owns the bookshop. He writes letters."

Something flickered across Wren's face—nothing, probably nothing, a muscle twitching against the wind—and Orsen heard himself say, "Occasionally," in a voice that sounded, to his own ears, like a man reading lines he'd been handed backstage.

"He writes lots of letters," Ivy clarified, merciless. "Every week. To a secret person."

"Ivy."

"What? It's true."

Wren looked between them, amused, curious, and Orsen watched the curiosity settle into her face and thought: *there it is, already, the sharpness, the thing that will undo me.* Because he had read enough of her over the years—her aunt's letters, the few mutual acquaintances who mentioned her—to know that Wren Halloway did not let questions go. Questions stuck to her. She was, he had once written to her, in letter sixty-something, *the sort of person who pulls threads, and I mean that as the highest compliment.*

"Well," Wren said, "secret persons are lucky people, getting weekly letters. Nobody writes letters anymore." She shifted the Tupperware to her other hip and glanced past them at the bookshop, its warm lit windows bleeding amber into the wet afternoon. "That's yours? The shop?"

"It is. Blackwood's. Hasn't been a Blackwood running it for a while, but the sign's expensive to change."

"You're welcome to come browse," Ivy added. "Dad gives good recommendations. He gave Mrs. Ferro a book about a whale and she cried for two days and loved it."

"That is a strong endorsement." Wren's eyes came back to his, and held, and Orsen experienced the peculiar vertigo of being looked at by someone who did not know she was looking at the most important person in her correspondence. "Actually—do you sell secondhand? My aunt's house is full of books and I can't tell what's worth keeping. I wouldn't know a first edition if it bit me."

"I could help with that," Orsen said. Too quickly. He heard the eagerness in it and hated himself, and course-corrected into something more nearly casual: "If you wanted. No obligation. Some of it's just old, not valuable. There's a difference."

"There usually is," she said, and something in the way she said it suggested she wasn't talking only about books, and then the moment passed, or seemed to, and the rain filled the silence, drumming on the empty porch, running off the broken guttering in bright ropes.

A mover called something from inside—*lady, where d'you want the dresser*—and Wren excused herself with an apologetic half-smile, and the interview was over, and Orsen walked the twelve feet back across the mud to his own door feeling as though he'd crossed a border.

* * *

That night he took the photograph out of the drawer.

He hadn't planned to. He told himself he was checking something—comparing, verifying, as if there were any doubt—but the truth was simpler and worse: he needed to look at her, now that looking was possible, now that she was forty feet away instead of a hundred miles and a decade. He sat at the kitchen table with a single lamp burning and studied the image he knew better than his own reflection: Wren Halloway, twenty-five, laughing, sunlit, her hand on the shoulder of a blurred girl in a red swimsuit.

The girl in the red swimsuit was Ivy. Eight years old then, eighteen now—or no, eight then, and now she was upstairs asleep with a book face-down on her chest, breathing, alive, because of the woman laughing in this photograph. Because on a cold bright afternoon in November ten years ago, Wren Halloway had gone into the river after a child she had never seen before, without hesitating, without calculating, and had brought her back.

Orsen had been thirty yards upstream, talking to a colleague on his phone, his back to the water. Thirty yards. The distance, he had calculated many times since, that a man's attention travels in the time it takes his daughter to slip on wet stones. By the time he'd turned, by the time he'd understood the screaming, Wren was already in—no coat off, no phone down, no thought for herself—and the current had them both, and for nine seconds that he would spend the rest of his life reliving, neither of them surfaced.

Then they had. Ivy first, coughing, and then Wren, hauling her toward the bank, and Orsen had run and waded and taken his daughter's weight, and when he'd turned to thank—

Green coat. Towpath. Gone.

He'd found out her name within a month, through means he was not proud of and never explained to anyone: a witness statement, a description, a woman named Halloway visiting an aunt nobody much talked about. He'd written the first letter that winter. He'd told himself it would be the only one.

One hundred and twenty letters ago.

He put the photograph away, gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping. Then he sat in the dark kitchen and faced the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since morning.

She was here. Not passing through—she'd bought the cottage, she'd hired movers, she'd accepted biscuits. She was going to be his neighbour. She was going to come into his shop, browse his shelves, ask his advice about her aunt's books. She was going to be *present*, in the flesh, in the daily grain of his life, and every day she was there he would be lying to her—not with words, he needn't say a word, but with the whole architecture of his silence. She would thank him for recommendations. She would chat with him over the paperbacks. And all the while he would know what he knew, and she would not, and the letters would keep arriving at PO Box 114 like love notes smuggled into a prison.

Unless she found out. Unless she worked it out. And she would—God, she might—because the town was small, because Ivy talked, because Wren Halloway pulled threads.

Or unless he told her. And telling her meant telling her everything, including the part that had nothing to do with rescue and everything to do with shame: why he'd hidden, why he'd never simply knocked on a door, why a man who owed a woman his daughter's life had spent ten years paying the debt in anonymous instalments rather than walking up to her and saying *it was me, it was always me, and I'm sorry, and also—*

Also. That word again. That dangerous, tender, ruinous word.

Outside, the rain eased. Through the gap in the curtains he could see the cottage next door, one window lit now, a lamp glowing weakly behind plastic sheeting. She was in there, unpacking her kitchen, eating his stale biscuits perhaps, not knowing that the man in the bookshop had written her a hundred and twenty letters and burned a dozen more.

Orsen pressed his forehead against the cool glass and breathed, and made himself a promise he already knew he wouldn't keep: *not yet. Give it a week. Let her settle. Then decide.*

Behind him, on the table, the lamp threw his shadow long and doubled against the wall—one shadow for the man, one for the letter-writer—and for the first time in ten years, the two of them were standing in the same room.
