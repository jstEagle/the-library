## 9. Letter One Hundred Twenty-One

The shop closed at six on Thursdays, and by half past the rain had settled into that steady, patient October rhythm that made the whole street sound like it was breathing. Orsen counted the till without counting it, his fingers moving through the familiar arithmetic while his mind stayed upstairs, in the flat, where a sheet of cream-laid paper had been waiting on his desk since morning.

One hundred and twenty letters in ten years. He knew the number the way other men knew their golf handicaps or their fathers' war stories—by heart, by ache, by the small shameful pride of it. Fourteen letters a year, more or less. One every five weeks, give or take the seasons. He had never missed one. Not when Ivy was born—not Ivy's birth, but the fevered week after, when he'd sat all night in a plastic chair outside the pediatric ward with a pen in his pocket like a splinter. Not when his mother died and he'd found, going through her things, the photograph of his father shaking hands with men whose faces he had spent a decade trying not to recognize. Not even the night last spring when the boiler burst and flooded the shop's back room and he'd stood ankle-deep in cold water thinking, absurdly, *the drafts*, before remembering they were safe in the tin box on the high shelf.

One hundred and twenty letters, and not one of them signed.

He locked the till drawer, turned the sign, and climbed the stairs to where the paper waited.

* * *

The desk lamp threw its circle of yellow onto the page, and Orsen sat with his hands flat on either side of it, the way you'd sit with something wild and wounded that might still bolt. Outside, the rain worked at the gutters. Downstairs, the floorboards ticked as the shop cooled, each small sound arriving through the soles of his feet like a message sent up from the building itself—from the years soaked into it, from the shelf where the tin box sat, from the wastepaper basket where his failures went to die.

He had started the letter four times already today. In the margins of an invoice. On the back of a bookmark. Twice on proper paper, both abandoned. The problem was not finding words—he had never once, in ten years, run out of words for her. The problem was that tonight the words wanted to be true in a way they had never been allowed to be.

Because she was here now. Three walls away, behind the hedge, in her aunt's cottage with its new curtains and its windows lit gold against the dark. Wren Halloway, whose name he had written at the top of one hundred and twenty envelopes without ever once saying aloud. He said it now, quietly, testing it against the room. *Wren.* It sounded different spoken than written. Written, it had always been a kind of prayer. Spoken, it was a woman's name, and it belonged to someone who bought milk at the corner shop and argued cheerfully with Mrs. Prewitt about chrysanthemums and had looked at him across the counter three days ago with an expression he couldn't file anywhere—something between recognition and appetite, as if she were trying to place him in a story she'd half-remembered.

Dear Wren, he wrote, and stopped.

That was the trouble. *Dear.* For ten years the salutation had been a fiction he could hide inside—an epistolary affection, a correspondence with a stranger, deniable, gentle. But she wasn't a stranger anymore. She was the woman who had laughed at his terrible pun about Dostoevsky and then, catching herself, looked almost angry about having laughed. She was the woman who held books the way some people held birds, careful and close. She was the woman who had pulled his daughter out of the river before Orsen even understood what the screaming meant.

He put down the pen. Pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until the dark bloomed orange.

* * *

It came the way it always came—not as memory but as weather. Fragments, pressure, the past pressing up through the floorboards of the present like water through a cellar wall.

A Tuesday in June, ten years ago. Heat like a hand on the back of the neck. The river running low and brown-green over its stones, willows dragging their fingers along the banks. He had taken Ivy—six years old, sunhat, sandals, the whole inventory of a small life—to the water because she'd begged and because he wanted, God help him, twenty minutes of not being needed. He could admit that now. He had sat on the bank with a paperback he would never finish and let his attention drift to the shape of clouds, and in the space of that drift—

The scream. Not Ivy's. A woman's voice downstream, raw and enormous, cutting the afternoon open.

Then: the hat. The sunhat, floating. Then: nothing where his daughter should have been.

He did not remember running. That was the mercy of it—the body taking over, the mind refusing the footage. What remained were shards, and the shards were these:

Cold water closing over his wrists. His own voice saying her name in a register he had never heard himself use. Stones turning underfoot, treacherous, the current stronger than it looked, stronger than it had any right to be in June. And then—a splash upstream, ahead of him, someone faster or braver or simply closer—and a figure in the water, a woman, arms around his daughter, hauling her toward the shallows with a competence that looked almost furious.

He reached them as the woman knelt in two inches of water, turning Ivy, checking her breathing with quick practiced hands. Ivy coughed. Cried. The world resumed.

And the woman looked up at him—mud-streaked, gasping, hair plastered to her face—and there was a moment, one long silver moment, when everything could have happened. When he could have said *thank you* properly, could have asked her name, could have fallen to his knees in the river mud and meant it. Her eyes were gray-green, river-colored, and they held his for exactly as long as it took him to understand that he was not going to speak.

Because he saw, in that moment, what looking at her cost. Because behind her, on the path, two men had stopped to watch—one of them wearing the face Orsen had spent his childhood learning to fear, his father's oldest friend, a man whose family name was stitched through this town like wire through concrete. Because the woman who had saved his daughter was a stranger, and strangers who waded into rivers in this town had a way of becoming stories, and stories had a way of being buried, and Orsen Blackwood knew better than anyone alive what this town did with things it wanted buried.

So he had said thank you. Just that. Two words, mangled, barely audible. And she had nodded, wrung out her shirt, and walked away up the bank, and he had watched her go with his daughter shivering against his chest and his father's name lying across his tongue like a stone.

He learned her name later. Of course he did. Wren Halloway, passing through, staying with relatives, gone within the month. By the time he'd worked up the courage—or the cowardice, the two felt identical from the inside—she was a hundred miles away, and a letter was the only shape his gratitude could take.

The first letter had been simple. *You saved my daughter's life and I never learned how to say so.* No name. A post office box for a return address, because even his gratitude felt like something that could be traced back and used. He had told himself the anonymity protected her. He had almost believed it.

But the letters hadn't stopped, and that was the truth he circled and never landed on: somewhere in the second year, maybe the third, the letters stopped being thanks and became something else. Something with no safer name than the one he refused to write. He had loved her since before he knew her face. He had built a decade of devotion out of paper and postage, and now she lived next door, and the architecture was coming down.

* * *

Orsen opened his eyes. The lamp hummed. The letter waited, one line long.

He picked up the pen and wrote, and this time he did not stop, and what came out was not the careful, curated tenderness of one hundred and twenty previous letters but something rawer, something with the skin torn off it:

*Dear Wren. I have been writing to you for ten years and I have never told you the truest thing, which is that I was there. I was in the river. I was the man who arrived too late, who watched you pull my daughter from the water and could not even manage your name. I am the man next door. I am the bookshop. I am the tin box on the high shelf. Every letter you ever received came from these hands, from this desk, from a man who loved you first as a stranger in a river and then as a stranger on paper and who has now run out of ways to remain a stranger at all. My name is—*

His pen stopped.

The nib rested on the paper, and a small bloom of ink spread beneath it, black and patient, like a hole opening.

*My name is.*

Three words. Ten years. He could not do it. Not on paper—paper could be found, paper could be read, paper could end up in the wrong hands, and the wrong hands in this town had a family crest. If he wrote it, it existed. If it existed, it could hurt her—the way his name had hurt people before, the way the Blackwood name lay over this town like a tarpaulin over something everyone agreed not to look at.

He set down the pen and read the letter back. It was the most honest thing he had ever written, and it ended in a lie of omission, and he hated it, and he could not send it. Sending it would be a confession delivered like a grenade through a mail slot. She deserved the sentence spoken, not posted. She deserved his face when she heard it.

She deserved, he thought with a bleak laugh, to hear it from a man who wasn't hiding behind a post office box.

He carried the letter to the sink in the little kitchenette, struck a match, and held the corner of the page to the flame. The fire took it greedily, climbing the lines of ink, eating his confession sentence by sentence. He held it over the basin until the flame reached his fingers, then dropped the last burning scrap and watched it curl into ash. The smoke smelled of ink and scorch. He ran the tap and washed the ashes down, and stood a moment with his hands braced on the sink, watching the gray swirl vanish.

One hundred and twenty-one letters written. One hundred and twenty mailed. The arithmetic of his cowardice, balanced perfectly.

Behind him, on the desk, the blank sheet waited. He would write another tomorrow. Letter one hundred twenty-two. He was, he understood suddenly, a man who could do this forever—write and burn, write and burn, a lifetime of honest letters addressed to no one, ash in the basin, love in the dark. The thought did not feel like devotion. It felt like drowning, slowly, in warm water, in a room with no one watching.

Downstairs, a floorboard creaked though there was no one there. Or just the shop settling. Or just the past, pressing up again through the old wood, patient as water.

He turned off the lamp. And in the sudden dark, through the window, he saw that the light in the cottage next door was still on—and that a silhouette moved behind the curtain, pausing, it seemed, at the glass. As if she were looking out. As if, across ten yards of wet garden, Wren Halloway were looking directly at his window, at the exact moment a man inside it burned something and could not explain why the smoke smelled like her name.
