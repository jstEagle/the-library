## 14. Ivy Runs Away (Two Streets)

The afternoon it happened began like any other Tuesday in October, which is to say it began with rain threatening and not yet falling, the sky the color of old pewter, and Orsen Blackwood standing behind the counter of Thorn & Page pretending to inventory a box of remaindered poetry while actually watching the clock crawl toward three o'clock, when Ivy would come clattering through the door with her backpack half-unzipped and her laces trailing.

He had learned, over ten years, to measure time in small arrivals. The mail truck at eleven. The school bell at three. Wren Halloway's footsteps on the boardwalk at odd hours now, unpredictable as weather, each one landing somewhere between dread and longing in his chest. She had been coming into the shop nearly every day that week—buying books she didn't seem to read, asking questions about the town that seemed casual and weren't—and every visit left him feeling like a man walking on a frozen river, listening for the crack beneath his boots.

At three-ten, no Ivy.

At three-twenty, he told himself the crossing guard had lingered, that Mrs. Prewitt had kept the class late for some celebration, that children were chronically distracted creatures who stopped to examine every interesting stone.

At three-forty, he called the school, and the secretary's voice went careful and flat in the way voices do when they are about to ruin your life: *She signed out at dismissal, Mr. Blackwood. She walked out the front doors with the others.*

He did not remember leaving the shop. He remembered the bell above the door chiming wildly behind him, remembered the cold slap of air, remembered calling her name into the street where the first drops of rain were beginning to stipple the pavement like dark freckles. He ran two streets toward the school, then cut back through the alley behind the bakery, then stood in the middle of Mill Road turning in a slow circle like a compass needle with no north.

*Think,* he told himself. *Think the way she thinks.*

Ivy was eight. Ivy was methodical in the way of children who have learned too early that the world requires managing. If she had run away—and the word arrived in his mind unbidden, ugly, certain—she would have gone somewhere with purpose. Not random. Somewhere that meant something.

And there was only one place in this town that meant what it meant.

His blood went to ice water.

* * *

He would replay the next forty minutes for the rest of his life, he was sure of it—the way memory keeps its own cruel archive. The rain arriving in earnest, sheeting down, turning the world gray and silver. His voice hoarse from shouting her name at doorways. Mrs. Abernathy on her porch saying she'd seen a little girl heading toward the river path, yes, maybe an hour ago, alone, walking fast, *determined*, that was the word she used, and the word went through him like a nail.

Because he knew why a child would walk determinedly to the river in October.

She wanted to see it. After all these years of the story—Daddy's story, the story he had built like a dam around the truth, telling her about a brave lady who pulled a little girl from the water, a guardian angel with kind eyes—he should have known that eventually the story would grow legs. That eventually his daughter would want to stand on the bank of the place where the brave lady had been brave, and look at the water, and try to make the story fit the geography.

He ran. Forty years old, bad knee, ten years of sedentary bookshop hours, and he ran like the river itself was chasing him, because in every nightmare version of this day, it was.

The path down to the Alder River was slick with wet leaves. The water was high with autumn rain, brown-green and muscular, pushing against its banks with the low constant roar of something alive and indifferent. And there—at the bend, where the willows hung their exhausted hair over the current—two figures.

One was small. One was not.

Orsen's heart performed some terrible gymnastic maneuver, relief and terror colliding mid-air, and he stumbled down the last stretch of path, mud sucking at his shoes, rain running into his eyes.

Wren Halloway stood with her coat soaked through, her dark hair plastered to her face, holding Ivy's hand. Not gripping it. Holding it—the way you hold something you're afraid might bolt. They were both looking at the water.

"Ivy," he said, and his voice came out broken into pieces.

She turned. Her face was blotchy from crying or rain or both, but her chin was set in the particular way it set when she had decided something and dared the world to argue. "Hi, Daddy."

"Hi, Daddy." He crossed the distance and crouched, gathering her in, checking her with his hands—arms, shoulders, face—as if the river might have taken pieces of her already. She was soaked to the knees; her shoes were ruined; she was whole. "You don't—you do not ever—" His voice failed him entirely. He pressed his forehead to hers and just breathed, ragged and loud, while the river roared its indifference ten feet away.

"She wasn't running away," Wren said quietly. "Not really."

He looked up at her. Rain dripped from her nose. There was something in her expression he couldn't read—or could read too well, something tender and stricken at once, as if she were watching a wound open in real time.

"She asked me to bring her here," Wren said. "I found her on the bridge. She was standing at the rail looking at the water, and when I asked her what she was doing, she said—" Wren's voice caught, just slightly. "She said she was looking for the spot. Where it happened."

The rain filled the silence.

"Daddy tells a story," Ivy said into his shoulder, muffled. Her small fists had closed in his shirt. "About a lady who saved a girl from this river. Ten years ago. Before I was born." She pulled back to look at him, and her eyes—Mara's eyes, God help him, Mara's gray eyes—were fierce and wet. "But I think it wasn't before I was born, Daddy. I think it was me."

The world went very quiet, the way it does after a detonation.

"I found my baby book," Ivy went on, relentless, the way only an eight-year-old can be. "It doesn't say anything about a river. But Aunt Colleen's letter to Grandma does. It says, *thank God the Blackwood girl is all right after that awful business at the Alder.* And I'm the Blackwood girl. And Daddy always gets quiet when the story gets to the part where the lady carries the little girl out of the water. He always stops right there." Her breath hitched. "So I came to see if the water still looked like a place where somebody could drown. And Miss Halloway came, and she knows the story too, Daddy. She knows it exactly. Every single part. Even the parts you never told me."

Orsen rose slowly, his knee screaming, rain hammering his shoulders. Wren was watching him across the space of three feet and ten years, and her face was no longer unreadable at all. It was the face of a woman assembling something, piece by piece, and watching the picture resolve.

"The parts I never told anyone," he said carefully.

"The lady in the story has green eyes," Ivy said. "Daddy says so. He says the lady had green eyes like new leaves, and she didn't even hesitate, she just went straight into the water." Ivy looked up at Wren, then back at him, then at Wren again, and Orsen watched the arithmetic complete itself in his daughter's face with a clarity that made him want to kneel in the mud. "Miss Halloway has green eyes like new leaves."

"Wren," he started, and had nothing. No sentence followed. Ten years of rehearsed words, one perfect sentence polished smooth as river glass, and not one syllable of it would come.

Wren held up a hand—not sharp, just steady. "Not here," she said softly. "Not in front of her. Not in the rain." She looked down at Ivy, and her expression gentled into something that hurt to witness. "Come on, sprout. Let's get you dry. Your dad can walk us back, and we'll all have hot chocolate, and nobody has to explain anything until everyone's warm."

Ivy took Wren's hand without hesitation, without ceremony, as if she had been taking it all her life, and the sight of it undid Orsen more thoroughly than any accusation could have.

They walked back up the path together, three abreast, the river roaring behind them like something that knew it had almost won.

* * *

By the time they reached the shop, the rain had settled into a fine persistent drizzle, and the windows glowed amber against the gray. Orsen lit the lamp behind the counter with hands that would not quite stop shaking. Wren toweled Ivy's hair in the reading nook with a dish towel she'd found without asking, moving through his shop with the ease of someone who had already memorized it, and Ivy sat wrapped in the afghan from the armchair, pink-cheeked, chastened by proximity rather than punishment, drinking the cocoa Orsen made with too much sugar because today deserved too much sugar.

"You're not mad," Ivy said. It wasn't a question. It was a test, and she watched him over the rim of her mug the way she watched him over the rim of everything lately.

"I'm terrified," Orsen said honestly. "Madness would be easier. You walked to the river alone, Ivy. In the rain. Anything could have—I couldn't find you, and for"—he checked himself, swallowed—"for a long time, I didn't know where you were."

"I knew where I was going." She said it simply, without defiance. "That's different."

"It isn't."

"It's a little different." A ghost of her usual stubbornness. Then it faded, and she looked into her cocoa. "I'm sorry, Daddy. But I needed to know. You tell the story like it's a fairy tale, but fairy tales don't make your hands shake." She glanced up. "Yours are shaking right now."

Wren, folding the towel, went very still.

Orsen set down the kettle. He sat on the edge of the nook's bench, elbows on his knees, and looked at his daughter—this child who had been pulled from a river before she could form memories of it, who carried the event in her body the way trees carry rings, invisible and structural.

"You're right," he said. "It isn't a fairy tale. It happened. A long time ago, a little girl fell into the Alder River, and a woman went in after her, and she brought her out, and that woman was braver than anyone I've ever known." His voice held. He was surprised by that. "And I've never told you the whole story because the whole story belongs partly to someone else, and it wasn't mine to give away. Do you understand?"

Ivy considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "Is it Miss Halloway's?"

The drizzle ticked against the window. Wren did not move.

"Some of it is," Orsen said.

Ivy nodded slowly, satisfied, as if this confirmed a theory she'd been developing for weeks. Then she yawned—a great jaw-cracking yawn, the adrenaline finally presenting its bill—and burrowed deeper into the afghan. "Can Miss Halloway read to me? Just one chapter. Since I scared everybody."

Orsen looked at Wren. Wren looked at Orsen. Something passed between them over the head of his daughter, dense and wordless, a message in a bottle thrown across ten years of water.

"One chapter," Wren agreed, and picked up the battered copy of *The Secret Garden* from the nook shelf as if she knew exactly where it lived. Maybe she did. She'd been in this shop enough times.

Ivy was asleep before the third page. Children are like that—they detonate and then sleep, spending their catastrophes like coins. Her breathing went slow and deep, one hand curled around the afghan's fringe, and the rain kept up its soft percussion on the awning outside, and the lamp made a small gold room of the world.

Orsen and Wren sat on opposite ends of the bench, the sleeping child between them like a border neither dared cross.

"She knew," Wren said at last, barely above the rain. "Before today. She's been piecing it together for a while, hasn't she. The story. The way you stop at the same place every time."

"She's observant." He rubbed his face. "She gets it from her mother."

"Mara." Wren said the name gently. She'd learned it from the town, of course—from the ledger of small-town griefs that everyone keeps whether they mean to or not. "I'm sorry. I didn't know her."

"Few people did, really. She was—" He stopped. Started again. "She died when Ivy was two. And a year before that, I stood on the bank of that river and watched a stranger dive into the water after a drowning child, and I couldn't move. I couldn't move, Wren. I screamed and I couldn't move, and this woman—this stranger—went under twice and came up with my daughter, and I have spent ten years writing letters to her because I couldn't say any of that out loud to her face."

The words were out before he could weigh them, before he could catch them at the border of his teeth, and he heard them land in the quiet room like stones dropped into still water—one, two, three ripples spreading outward.

Wren turned to look at him fully. The lamplight caught the green of her eyes, those new-leaf eyes, and they were wide and wet and burning with a question she had clearly been carrying since the riverbank, since the wastepaper basket, since longer than he knew.

"Your letters," she said. "The ones to the PO box. To me."

"Wren—"

"Say it." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated that, he could see she hated it, this proud careful woman undone in a bookshop by a child's bedtime and a man's slip of the tongue. "Orsen. Say it plainly. Once. Without the story stopping in the middle."

He opened his mouth.

And Ivy stirred in her sleep, murmuring something soft and unintelligible, and the moment folded itself shut like a fan.

Wren exhaled slowly. She rose, tucked the afghan higher around Ivy with hands that trembled only slightly, and looked down at the sleeping girl with an expression Orsen would carry for the rest of his life—tenderness and heartbreak braided so tightly together they were indistinguishable.

"Not tonight," she said quietly. "But soon, Orsen. Because I've found things in this town—old things, ugly things—and I need to know if you're a man who hides the truth to protect people, or a man who hides it to protect himself. And I need to know which one I've been—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. "Goodnight. Lock the door behind me."

The bell chimed. The rain received her.

Orsen sat in the lamplight with his sleeping daughter and the ruins of his silence around him, and understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only after the dam has already cracked, that there were perhaps two days left in which he could still choose how the water came through.
