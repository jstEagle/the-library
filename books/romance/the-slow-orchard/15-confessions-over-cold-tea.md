## 15. Confessions Over Cold Tea

Wren did not sleep. She had meant to—she had even gone through the motions of it, the lamp switched off, the quilt pulled to her chin, the cottage settling around her in its familiar creaks and sighs—but every time she closed her eyes she saw the paper again, that single discarded page she had fished from the wastepaper basket behind the counter of Thornbury & Daughters Books, and the handwriting on it, looping and slanted and unmistakable.

*Dear Wren,* it began. Not *Dear Ms. Halloway.* Not *To whom it may concern.* *Dear Wren*, written in Orsen Blackwood's careful hand, addressed to an address she had not lived at in eleven years—the flat above the bakery on Corben Street, where she had been twenty-two and broke and certain the world owed her something better than what it was giving.

She had sat on the back step of the shop with the letter in her lap for a long time, long enough for her tea to go cold in the mug beside her, long enough for the light to change from gold to grey to the bruised violet of early evening. The streetlamp outside the shop buzzed on. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and gave up.

One hundred nineteen letters. She had counted them last night, laid out in order on her aunt's old dining table, the ones she'd kept and the ones she'd found again in the box marked *MISC — KEEP* in her aunt's attic. One hundred nineteen envelopes, postmarked over ten years, all bearing the same careful hand, all addressed to a woman who had never once written back because she had never known who to write back *to*.

And now one hundred twenty lay crumpled in a bin behind a bookshop, unfinished, unsigned, and the man who wrote them sold novels three doors down from her kitchen window and made cocoa for his daughter and said *good morning, Wren* in a voice she had begun, God help her, to wait for.

She got up before dawn. She dressed in the dark. She put the draft letter in her coat pocket, where it sat against her ribs like a stone, and she walked the two streets to the bookshop while the town was still the colour of unpolished pewter, mist lying in the hollows of the lane like spilled milk.

The shop didn't open until ten. But there was a light on upstairs—she could see it from the lane, a warm rectangle in the blue dark—and so she knocked. Not the polite knock of a customer. The knock of a woman who has been awake all night and intends to be answered.

Footsteps. A pause. The door opened on the chain, and Orsen's face appeared in the gap, sleep-creased and wary, and then—she watched it happen—watched the recognition move through him like weather. He knew why she was there. She saw him know it.

"Wren." Her name came out carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "It's half six."

"I know." She held up the letter. "I found this in your bin."

The chain slid free. He opened the door the rest of the way, and the smell of the shop came out to meet her—paper and dust and the ghost of yesterday's coffee—and beyond him the stairs went up into shadow where Ivy slept, and Wren thought: *he has a child asleep one floor above us, and I am about to burn his life down, and I cannot make myself care.*

"Not here," he said quietly. "Not with her upstairs."

"The orchard shed," Wren said. "Nobody's there before eight."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, went back inside, and came out five minutes later in a coat and boots, without waking his daughter, and they walked through the sleeping town together like conspirators, ten feet apart, saying nothing, their breath hanging in the cold air between them.

* * *

The orchard shed smelled of apples going soft and the creosote-dark wood of old shelves. Wren lit the paraffin lamp on the workbench because her hands needed something to do, and the flame steadied, and the small room filled with amber light and long shadows, and still neither of them spoke.

Orsen stood by the door as if he'd considered leaving and couldn't quite commit to it. His hands were in his pockets. He looked older than he had at the festival meetings, older than he had across the harvest table with apple pulp on his knuckles. He looked like a man standing in front of a judge.

"Say it," he said finally. "Whatever you came to say. Say it, and I'll answer what I can."

Wren took the letter from her pocket. She smoothed it flat on the workbench, under the lamp, and turned it to face him. *Dear Wren.* His own handwriting, staring up at him.

"One hundred twenty," she said. "That makes this number one hundred twenty-one, doesn't it? Except this one never got posted. This one ended up in the rubbish behind your own shop, which tells me either you've stopped, or you got scared, or—" her voice cracked and she hated it, hated how much it betrayed, "—or you never intended for me to find any of them at all."

Orsen looked at the letter the way a man looks at an old wound. "How long have you known?"

"I found the draft two days ago. I've had the others for—" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Years. Ten years, Orsen. My aunt kept every one. I thought they were from a madman, at first. Then I thought they were from someone kind. Then I stopped thinking about them at all, because that's what I do, isn't it? I put things in boxes marked MISC and I don't open them." She pressed her palms flat to the bench. "But you. You were *here*. Selling me books. Recommending me a novel about confession, of all the—did you think that was funny? Did you sit there watching me read it and—"

"No." It came out rough. "No, Wren, I swear to you, it wasn't like that."

"Then tell me what it was like." She came around the bench. The lamp threw her shadow huge against the wall of hanging tools. "Because here is what I know. For ten years, someone wrote to me. Someone knew things about my life—knew when I left Corben Street, knew when my mother was ill, knew when I lost the job in the city. Knew things I told no one. And that someone watched me walk into his shop and shake his hand and buy his recommendations and let his daughter fall asleep against my shoulder in the orchard, and he said *nothing*. Nothing, Orsen. Do you understand what that does to a person? Every kindness you ever showed me is suddenly a lie with a receipt attached."

"They weren't lies." His voice was low but it had iron under it. "None of them."

"Then prove it. Tell me why. Tell me about the first letter—letter one. Tell me what happened that made a stranger start writing to a woman he'd never met, because I have read those letters a hundred times and there is always something underneath them, some debt being paid, some—" she stopped. Breathed. "There's a night in them. There's a night in every one of them, like a stone at the bottom of a well. And I want to know what happened on that night."

The silence stretched. Outside, the mist was lifting off the orchard rows; she could see the pale shapes of the trees through the grimy window, row after row after row, patient as gravestones.

Orsen took his hands out of his pockets. He crossed to the workbench and looked down at the letter for a long time, and when he spoke, his voice was almost gentle, which was somehow worse than anger.

"Yes," he said. "I wrote them. All of them. Every letter you've ever received—I wrote it. There's no one else. There never was."

The admission landed in her chest like a dropped weight. Part of her had held out, all night, for some other explanation—a brother, a friend, a cruel joke, anything. But hearing him say it plainly, without flinching, undid something, and she had to turn away and look at the tool wall until her eyes stopped stinging.

"Why?" she managed.

"Because I owed you something I couldn't pay in person."

"What thing?"

He was quiet for so long she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, each word came out like a coin counted out of a nearly empty purse.

"A life. You saved a life, Wren. Ten years ago. And the person who should have thanked you couldn't, and so I did it instead, badly, for a decade, in ink."

Wren turned slowly. The lamp flickered between them. "Saved a life," she repeated. "What life?"

"You know what life."

"I don't." And then, because she did—because some cold current had been moving under her skin since the night Ivy asked her, in the lane by the river, *were you the lady from the story Daddy tells*—she whispered it anyway. "Ivy."

Orsen closed his eyes.

"That's why you moved next door to me," she said. The words were coming faster now, tumbling. "That's why you were at the cottage that day in October—I saw your face when the van arrived, I thought you were just being neighbourly, but you *recognised* me. You knew who I was before I ever told you my name. You've known for ten years and you moved your whole life next door to mine and you never—you let me—"

"I didn't move next door to you." His eyes opened, and there was something raw in them. "You inherited a cottage beside my shop. That's not the same thing, however it feels."

"It feels the same."

"I know it does." He dragged a hand down his face. "God, I know it does."

"So explain it." She stepped closer. The smell of him—woodsmoke and old paper—was suddenly unbearable, familiar, a thing she had been breathing for months without knowing it belonged to her letters too. "Explain the night. Explain what happened at the river. Because I remember pieces, Orsen, I remember cold and screaming and a small body, and then eleven years of nothing, and then your daughter looks at me like I'm a ghost from a bedtime story, and I need you to say it out loud. All of it. Now."

He looked at her then—really looked, the way he had maybe never let himself look before—and she saw the sentence rise in him. She saw it come up his throat like water coming up in a drowning man. His mouth opened.

And closed.

"Not like this," he said.

The words fell into the room and lay there.

Wren stared at him. "Not like—what does that mean?"

"It means not in a shed at seven in the morning because you cornered me with a piece of paper." His voice shook, and he mastered it. "It means that what I have to tell you is the worst thing I know, and if I say it here, in this light, with you looking at me like that, it will be the only true thing you ever believe about me. Everything else—the shop, the festivals, the apples, Ivy falling asleep on your shoulder—all of it will just be scaffolding around that one night. I can't have you learn me backwards, Wren. I can't have the night be the beginning."

"So it gets to stay a secret?" Her voice rose despite herself. "You get to decide when I'm allowed to know the truth about my own life?"

"Yes." He said it simply, terribly. "For one more week. That's all I'm asking."

"A week." She laughed, and there was nothing soft in it. "You've had ten years."

"And I've spent nine of them rehearsing, and one of them ruining everything by staying silent when I should have spoken, and I will not add a botched confession in a freezing shed to the list." He picked up the letter from the bench—his own handwriting, his own cowardice—and folded it once, precisely, and held it out to her. "Keep it. It's yours. They're all yours. But the night itself—not like this. Please."

She didn't take the letter. She stood there with her arms wrapped around herself, cold to the bone in a way the paraffin lamp couldn't touch, and she understood—with a clarity that felt like grief—that he was not refusing her out of cruelty or even cowardice alone. He was protecting something. The shape of it eluded her, but it was large, and it was old, and it had teeth.

"There's more than the river," she said quietly. "Isn't there. Something you're not telling me even now."

Orsen said nothing. But his silence had a texture, and the texture was fear.

"The cannery," she said. "Your father's name is in those records somewhere. I haven't proven it yet, but I will."

Something moved behind his eyes—too fast to name, gone before it finished arriving. "Then you'll understand," he said, "why some truths take longer than others."

"I don't want understanding." Her voice cracked clean through. "I wanted honesty. I wanted the man from the letters, and instead I got a bookseller who lies beautifully."

She pushed past him toward the door. At the threshold she stopped, one hand on the frame, the mist and the pale orchard rows waiting beyond her.

"For what it's worth," she said, without turning around, "the letters were the truest thing anyone ever gave me. Whoever he was."

She heard him breathe in behind her, sharp, as if she'd struck him.

"Wren—"

"Don't." She stepped out into the cold. "You have your week. Use it."

* * *

She walked home through the lifting fog with her arms tight around herself and the letter still folded in her pocket, because in the end she had taken it after all—had snatched it off the bench on her way past him, unable to leave it, unable to leave any part of him behind, which was the whole problem, wasn't it, the whole rotten heart of it.

The cottage was cold. She built up the fire with hands that wouldn't quite steady, and when it caught she sat on the hearthstone and unfolded the letter and read it by firelight, properly this time, all of it, to the end.

It wasn't finished. It broke off mid-sentence, halfway down the second page, in a line that stopped her breath:

*I have loved you since before I ever saw your face, and I am tired, Wren. I am so tired of loving you in secret. If you are reading this, then I have finally—*

And there it ended. No signature. No night. No name.

Just the ash of a sentence, and a week, and a town full of records waiting to be opened.

Wren fed the corner of the page to the fire, watched the flame take it, and pulled it back out singed, because she could not bring herself to let it go entirely.

Outside, the mist burned off the orchard, row by row, and somewhere across town a man was teaching his daughter long division, or watering the shop plants, or standing at a window watching smoke rise from her chimney, counting days.

Seven, she thought. He thinks he has seven.

She looked at the ledger notes spread across her aunt's table—the cannery, the dates, the name half-erased in the clerk's hand—and understood, with a cold settling certainty, that he might not get even that.

Because she had an appointment with the postmaster at nine.

And the postmaster, she was fairly sure, had been keeping secrets a great deal longer than Orsen Blackwood had.
