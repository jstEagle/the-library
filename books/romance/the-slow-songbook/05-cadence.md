## 5. Cadence

The voice note arrived at 6:14 on a Sunday morning, which meant she had been awake since before dawn, and which meant—though I had no right to know this yet—that the house was at its worst for her in those hours, when the light came up grey through tall windows and there was nothing to do but be a person alone in it.

I played it twice before I made coffee.

*"He wants to know about the songbook,"* she said, and I could hear her moving as she spoke, the small percussion of a life being lived around a microphone: a drawer opening, a chair pushed back, the particular hush of a room with high ceilings. *"Fine. The songbook. My mother's. Green cloth boards, gold lettering gone to brass shadow, spine cracked like a knuckle. She wrote in it from 1958 until—" * A pause, long enough that I checked whether the recording had cut out. It hadn't. She was choosing. *"Until she stopped."*

Then: *"There's a melody on the last page. Unfinished. Eight bars and then nothing, just her handwriting trailing off into the margin like a sentence she lost interest in finishing, or couldn't. I used to play it for her when she was dying. I got it wrong every time. She never corrected me. That's either love or indifference and I've spent forty years deciding which."*

A breath.

*"Your question was whether the songbook should open the inheritance chapter or close it. Open it. Obviously. You close a door with a songbook; you open one too. Ask me better questions. This one was lazy."*

The recording ended. I sat in my kitchen with my hands flat on the table, the way you do when the ground has shifted under a building you thought you knew.

Because I knew that songbook.

Not hers specifically—I had never seen hers—but I knew *of* it, the way you know of a painting only one photograph of which has ever been published. Eleven years ago, early in my career, back when I still read criticism for pleasure instead of reconnaissance, I had read a profile in a now-defunct literary quarterly. The subject was Vivienne Ashford, then riding the success of her second biography, the one about the composer's forgotten mistress that had made her name. The profile was mostly the usual furniture—childhood, ambition, the marriage that ended, the reputation for surgical precision—and I remembered almost none of it.

What I remembered was a single paragraph near the end. The interviewer had asked her about objects, about the things writers keep, and she had said—she must have been thirty-six or so, younger than I am now—

*"My mother kept a songbook. Green cloth, gold lettering worn to nothing. She composed in it, badly, beautifully, for thirty years. The last page is an unfinished melody, eight bars, and then her hand just gives up mid-phrase. When I can't write, I look at that page. Not for inspiration—for permission. She stopped. The world went on. There's a kind of mercy in that I've never found anywhere else."*

I had underlined it. I still had the magazine. I knew exactly which box it was in.

* * *

I want to be precise here about what happened next, because precision is the only honesty available to a man in my position, and because what happened next was not dramatic. There was no lightning. I did not stand up and pace. I simply sat at my kitchen table while the coffee went cold and felt the particular vertigo of a locked door discovering it had been unlocked all along.

I knew her work. Not the way a reader knows a writer—not the way anyone knows anyone—but the way a craftsman knows another craftsman's hands. For six years I had ghostwritten in adjacent territory: memoirs of musicians, biographies-as-told-to, the whole grey economy of other people's lives. And before that, in the years when I still signed things, I had reviewed two of her books. I had written four thousand words on her syntax once, in a journal nobody read, arguing that her sentences did their real work in the pauses, that she built meaning the way a rest builds music—by the shape of what she refused to say.

I had admired her. That was the humiliating part, if humiliation required an audience, and with her it always had. I had admired her the way you admire weather: from inside it, helplessly, taking notes.

And she did not know me at all.

Except—the thought arrived slowly, the way cold comes up through floorboards—except that she might. Because three years before the festival, before the panel where she dismantled me in front of four hundred people and a livestream that would not die, I had written that review. Unsigned, as most of my work was even then, but the byline said *Gideon Eriksen*, because journals insisted, and because in those days I still occasionally pretended I had a name worth printing.

She had never mentioned it. But Vivienne Ashford struck me—even across the encrypted portal, even voice-only, even now—as a woman who forgot nothing she chose to keep.

The festival. I have tried, over the years, to file it away as anecdote, the way you're supposed to, the way everyone tells you to. It doesn't file. Here is what happened, one more time, because this chapter of my own life is the one I have never been allowed to ghostwrite:

There was a panel called something forgettable—*The Ethics of the Borrowed Voice*, or some such—and I was on it because my first credited book, a musician's memoir that was really ninety percent mine, had done well enough to make me a specimen. I said something careful and moderately intelligent about consent and collaboration. And Vivienne Ashford, seated to my left with the relaxed posture of a duelist, turned to the moderator and said, pleasantly, audibly, into every microphone in the hall:

*"With respect, we're hearing a great deal about ethics from a man whose entire career consists of not existing."*

Laughter. Not cruel laughter, at first—laughter with air in it, laughter that hadn't decided yet. I attempted a reply. I remember beginning a sentence about anonymity as a discipline, and I remember her interrupting—not rudely, that was the genius of it, she interrupted the way an editor does, with the confidence of someone improving your work—saying, *"But don't you see, that's precisely the confession? Discipline is what you call it when you're proud of the constraint. What's the constraint here, Mr. Eriksen? Talent?"*

Four hundred people. The laugh that time had no air in it at all.

I finished the panel. I answered two audience questions. I shook hands afterward with people whose eyes were already sliding past me toward the bar, toward her, toward the bright center of the room where Vivienne Ashford stood holding a glass of wine she barely touched, radiant, victorious, entirely unaware—or so I told myself for eleven years—of what she'd done.

I went home and took the ghostwriting contract that was waiting in my inbox, and I never signed my name to anything again.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I performed that Sunday morning, and have re-performed every day since, arriving each time at the same unbearable sum:

She knows my cadence. Or rather—no. Let me be exact, because she would demand exactness, because exactness is the only gift I can give her that she cannot buy.

*I* know *her* cadence. Intimately. I have read everything she has published, most of it twice, some of it aloud in empty rooms to hear how the rests land. I know that she begins chapters with objects rather than people. I know she distrusts the word "love" and spends it like a miser, so that when it appears it detonates. I know her mother appears in her work exactly four times, always obliquely, always via the songbook or the garden or the man who never claimed her, and never by name. I know that her humor arrives in the third clause of a sentence, disguised as an afterthought, so that you laugh a half-second late and feel ambushed by your own affection.

And she knows—what? An anonymous contractor designated by initials. A voice on a portal. A set of follow-up questions she has already pronounced lazy.

The asymmetry was obscene. It was also, I understood with a clarity that felt like fever, dangerous. Because correspondence does something to people. Four weeks of voice notes and manuscript pages had already done it to me: I argued with her drafts in the margins the way you argue with a lover, delightedly, losing on purpose. I had caught myself last Tuesday writing a sentence solely because I predicted it would make her exhale that short, reluctant breath she gave instead of laughing—a breath I had begun, God help me, to compose for.

Affection, arriving under false pretenses, compounds daily. And the interest is paid in the currency of the eventual reveal.

* * *

I opened the manuscript.

Her inheritance chapter existed now in my draft—my draft, though every word of substance was hers, filtered through dictation and my structuring—and the songbook sat at its opening as she had commanded. I had written the transition myself. I reread it:

*The green songbook weighed less than a bird. Her mother had carried it from Lagos to London in 1961, in a suitcase with a broken latch held shut by a stocking, and in forty years of English weather it had never once been opened in company.*

It was a good paragraph. I will permit myself that. It was a good paragraph and it was built entirely out of her voice notes, out of the way she had said *broken latch held shut by a stocking* with a tenderness she immediately disowned—*"God, that's sentimental, cut it"—*and which I had kept, because it was true, and because cutting it would have been a kind of theft.

I sat with my cursor blinking at the end of the chapter and drafted my reply to her Sunday note. The professional version first, naturally:

*Noted on placement. On the unfinished melody: I think the eight bars should appear on the page itself—set as verse, broken off exactly where she broke off. Readers should collide with the silence physically. One question, and take your time: when you say she "stopped," do you mean she chose to, or that the stopping chose her? The chapter changes depending on your answer.*

Clean. Useful. A better question than the lazy one, as instructed. I would send it and think no more about it until Tuesday.

Except.

At the bottom of the draft, unbidden, my hands typed:

*P.S. I read the profile once—the quarterly, the year your second book came out. The paragraph about the songbook. "Permission," you called it. I've thought about that word for eleven years.*

I stared at it.

Eleven years. The number sat there confessing everything: the timeline, the review, possibly the festival, possibly the face. Delete, delete, delete. My finger hovered. And then—this is the part I have trouble explaining, even to myself, especially to myself—I didn't delete it. I saved the draft. I closed the laptop. I made a second cup of coffee I did not drink, and I carried the saved draft around my flat like a coal in cupped hands, telling myself I was merely considering it, weighing it, performing due diligence, when what I was actually doing was standing at the edge of the one thing I had sworn off at thirty-four: being known.

Because here is what the P.S. actually offered her, stripped of its cleverness: *I have been paying attention to you for longer than this assignment. I am not a stranger. I may be the opposite of a stranger.* And here is what it risked: everything. The portal, the contract, the delicate anonymous architecture we had both agreed to pretend was neutral ground. If she traced the timeline—if she was half as forensic as her books suggested—she would find the review, and through the review the name, and through the name the face from the festival, and then every gentle question I had asked her would retroactively become surveillance, every kindness strategy, every page of her dying memory that I had gathered into my hands evidence of something I had taken rather than something I had been given.

Or she would not trace it. Or she would trace it and not care. I had no data. That was the torment of it: I knew her sentences better than I knew her, and her sentences were silent on the subject of forgiveness.

* * *

Near dusk I found the magazine.

It was in the third box from the left in the closet I never open, under a stack of contracts with redacted names, wrapped against damp in a plastic sleeve I don't remember buying. The quarterly's cover had faded to the color of weak tea. I turned to the profile. There it was, the paragraph, my old pencil line still underlining *permission*, the graphite slightly smudged as if I had touched it recently, which perhaps I had, in some year I've forgotten, which is its own small terror.

And beneath the paragraph, in the interviewer's framing, a detail I had not remembered, or had buried: *Ashford keeps the songbook on the desk she writes at, facing outward, "so it can watch me work."*

So it could watch her work.

I looked around my own flat—at the desk facing the wall, at the drawers of unsigned drafts, at the life I had built with the thoroughness of a man constructing his own absence—and I understood, with the mild surprise of someone reading their own diagnosis, that I had arranged my entire working existence so that nothing could watch me. No photographs. No bylines. No window behind me on video calls. I had made myself the negative space in every room I entered, and I had called it discipline, and a woman on a stage had once asked, in front of four hundred people, what the constraint actually was.

Talent, she'd suggested. The laugh had said otherwise.

I sat down at the desk with the songbook paragraph glowing on my screen and I opened the saved draft, and the P.S. was still there, patient as a lit fuse.

*Take your time,* my professional question said to her. *The chapter changes depending on your answer.*

So did mine. So did everything.

I read the P.S. once more. Then I selected it—all of it, eleven years of attention compressed into three sentences—and my thumb rested on the trackpad, and outside my window the city went on making its indifferent evening noise, and somewhere across that same city, in a house too large for one woman, a green songbook sat facing outward on a desk, watching her work, waiting to see what I would sign.

I pressed delete.

The draft went clean. Professional. Safe. A stranger's voice asking a stranger's question. I hit send, and the portal swallowed it with its soft mechanical click, and I told myself the relief I felt was relief.

It wasn't. It was the other thing. It was the sound a door makes when you lock it from the inside—and the knowledge, already forming, unwelcome as a headache, that I had just taught myself exactly how much it would cost to unlock it again.

Her reply came the next morning, faster than usual, as if she had been waiting by the portal the way I had been pretending not to.

*"Better question,"* she said. *"You're getting less lazy. I'll answer it Thursday. But first—"* and here her voice changed, dropped into a register I hadn't heard from her before, quieter, almost shy, which from Vivienne Ashford was more alarming than any tremor—*"first, tell me something. Anything. About you. You know an obscene amount about my mother's suitcase latch. I don't even know if 'the ghostwriter' is a man. Give me one true thing, or I'm going to start inventing you, and I promise you won't like my version."*

I sat very still in my kitchen, listening to the recording end, listening to the flat fill up with the silence after it.

She wanted a true thing. From me. And I had just deleted the truest thing I owned.
