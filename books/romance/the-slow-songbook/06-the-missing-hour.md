## 6. The Missing Hour

The hour began at 4:17 and ended, as far as the world was concerned, at 4:17.

Vivienne knew this because she had checked her watch in the car park of Sainsbury's on Holloway Road, standing beside her open boot with a bag of blood oranges in one hand and her keys pressed into her palm hard enough to leave their teeth printed there, and the watch had said 4:17. Then she had driven home through the last grey light, carried the oranges into the kitchen, set them in the blue bowl her mother had owned, and looked at the clock above the stove.

It said 5:23.

Sixty-six minutes. Gone. Not lost the way an evening is lost, dissolved pleasantly into wine and conversation, but *removed*—lifted out of her like a page torn from a book she was holding. She stood in the kitchen with her coat still on and tried to find the seam of it. She remembered the car park. She remembered the oranges—she could feel their weight still, that dense citrus gravity, and when she opened one later over the sink its smell would be so violent and sweet it made her eyes sting, as if the fruit were trying to tell her something. She remembered choosing them, turning each one over in her hand, judging its heaviness the way her mother had taught her to judge everything: by weight, by what it held.

And then nothing. A white space. The drive home existed only as an assumption, a conclusion drawn from evidence—the car in the street, the keys in the bowl by the door, the faint ache in her right shoulder from hauling the shopping bag—and not as memory. She had no recollection of driving. Somewhere between Holloway Road and this kitchen, an hour had closed over her head like water, and she had come up on the other side of it without drowning, without even getting her hair wet, and that was somehow worse than drowning would have been.

She hung up her coat. She made tea she did not drink. She sat at the kitchen table with her hands flat on the wood and performed, for an audience of one, the exercise she prescribed herself the way a doctor prescribes pills: *retrace*. Where had she parked? Third row, near the trolley bay—she could see the trolley bay, rust bleeding down its metal leg. Had she gone back into the shop for anything? No. Had she spoken to anyone? There had been a man loading his boot two spaces down; she thought she might have said something about the weather, or he had, one of those small transactions strangers perform. Or perhaps that was another day entirely, some other car park, some other year. That was the treachery of it now: her past had stopped being a fixed country and become a composite, stitched together from decades of similar afternoons, and she could no longer swear which afternoon any single thread belonged to.

The kettle clicked as it cooled. Outside, a blackbird was doing its late, liquid arithmetic in the hedge.

*It's tiredness,* she told herself. *You've been working too hard. You're sixty-one, not dead.* And then, quieter, in the voice she used for facts she refused to look at directly: *You know what this is.*

She did know. Of course she knew. She had known since the word—since February, when "casserole" had walked out of her vocabulary mid-sentence at dinner with Nell and left her gesturing at empty air while Nell watched her with an expression she had catalogued and filed under *concern, sisterly, manageable*. She had known since the mornings when she stood in her own hallway and felt the house tilt slightly, familiar and foreign at once, like a photograph of a place you'd lived. Knowing was not the same as admitting. Admitting was a door, and doors, once opened, let in everything.

So she did what Ashford women did. She tidied the evidence.

* * *

Nell rang at seven, as she always did on Thursdays, and Vivienne answered on the second ring with her voice arranged into its public setting—warm, wry, faintly impatient, the voice of a woman whose only problem was a stubborn third chapter.

"You sound odd," Nell said, before hello had properly finished.

"I sound like a woman who's been fighting a ghostwriter all afternoon."

"Is he dreadful?"

"He's infuriating." This much was true, and truth was the best camouflage; it always had been. "He asked me how I *felt* about my mother's songbook. Felt. As though I were a patient describing a rash."

"And what did you say?"

"That I'd hired a biographer, not a therapist." Vivienne turned the phone against her ear and watched the dark window, where her own reflection kept her company, faint and silvered. "He sent back three questions anyway. All gentle. All devastating. He has this way of asking—" She stopped. She had been about to say *he asks like someone who already knows the answer*, which was true and also dangerously close to the softness she was not permitted to feel yet, and she rerouted. "He asks like a barrister who's read your diary."

Nell laughed. Then, in the pause, in the smaller voice Nell saved for real things: "Viv. Are you eating? You sounded breathless on Sunday."

"I'm eating. I'm sleeping. I'm dictating my life story to a stranger who won't tell me his name, which is either the most modern thing I've ever done or the least." She smiled at the window, at the woman smiling there. "I'm fine, Nell."

Three words. She counted them out like coins across a counter. *I'm fine*—the oldest lie in the family ledger, the one their mother had signed daily for thirty years while their father's absence sat at the table like an uninvited guest. Vivienne had sworn, at fifteen, watching her mother pour tea for a man who never came, that she would never inherit that sentence. And here she was, fluent in it, passing it down the line like a recipe.

"Good," said Nell, unconvinced, and moved on to the parish council, and the roof, and whether Vivienne had remembered the neurologist's number was—

"There is no neurologist," Vivienne said, lightly, instantly, the reflex faster than thought. "Where did you get neurologist?"

"The brochure. In your study. When I was looking for the—"

"It's research." Smooth. Seamless. *For the book,* she almost added, and didn't, because the book was becoming a country she couldn't govern; it kept annexing new territory. "I'm writing about memory, Nell. It's called due diligence."

A silence on the line, long enough to have furniture in it.

"All right," Nell said finally, in the tone of a woman putting something on a high shelf to deal with later. "Thursday next week, then. Bring the oranges."

After she rang off, Vivienne sat in the dark kitchen for a long time, the phone cooling in her hand, and understood with terrible clarity what she had just done. Not the lie itself—lies were easy, lies were load-bearing, the family architecture was built of them—but the *speed* of it. No hesitation. No cost. The brochure lie had come out of her fully formed, polished, wearing its little hat, and some part of her had stood aside and watched it go with professional admiration.

That was the part that frightened her. Not the forgetting. The fluency.

* * *

On Friday morning she sat down to dictate.

The recorder was a small black thing Nell had bought her years ago for conference talks, and it had a red eye that glowed when it listened, and Vivienne had developed an irrational tenderness toward it—the one listener in her house who never interrupted, never worried, never looked at her sideways over a teacup. She pressed the button. The eye lit. She began where she had left off, in the spring of 1979, her mother in the garden with the songbook open on her knee, humming.

She gave him the good material. She knew she was doing it—she could hear herself curating even as she spoke, the old biographer's instinct running in reverse, editing her own life on the way out of her mouth. She described the garden. She described the melody her mother hummed while pinning sheets on the line, the one that went up at the end like a question. She described the smell of Sunlight soap and cut grass and the particular green shade of shade beneath the apple tree, and she heard her own voice do what it always did, lift and shape and land, and she thought: *this is why they pay me. Even ruined, I can make a sentence stand up straight.*

She did not mention the car park.

It wasn't a decision, exactly—or rather, it was a decision made in the way most of her important decisions were made now, swiftly and below the waterline, by the part of her that had taken over governance without announcing a coup. The missing hour simply did not belong in the manuscript. The manuscript was the record. The manuscript was the thing that would outlast her, the book she was building against the dark like a lamp in a window, and you did not put the cracks in the lamp. You put the light in the lamp. Let some future reader—let *him*, the nameless man with the gentle, devastating questions—meet her at her best, in the garden, in 1979, with the sheets snapping on the line. The hour in the car park belonged to no book. It belonged to the white space, and the white space was hers.

Except, of course, that it wasn't. That was the whole horror of it. The white space was growing, annexing rooms, and she was the only witness, and she was busy perjuring herself.

She stopped the recording. Started it again.

"One more thing," she said, and heard her voice go bright, over-bright, the register she used for signing books. "My sister thinks I should see a doctor. I've told her I'm fine. Note for the record: I am *fine*. If my memoir contains any gaps, let the record show they are editorial."

She laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and then heard how it sounded, alone in a big house, laughing at a joke whose punchline was her own erasure, and the laugh died in the room like a bird hitting glass.

She sent the file. She watched the progress bar fill, the voice note travelling out through the wires to whatever anonymous desk received it, and she felt the specific relief of a smuggler who has gotten the goods across the border—followed immediately by the specific shame of knowing what she'd smuggled, and to whom, and why.

* * *

That night she dreamed of the car park.

In the dream it was full of water, shallow and silver, and every trolley was floating, and she was walking through it looking for her mother, who was sitting in the driver's seat of a car that may or may not have been theirs, singing the song that went up at the end like a question. In the dream Vivienne knew all the words. She leaned against the wet glass and listened and thought: *when I wake up I will write this down, I will remember, I will keep it*—and even inside the dream, some clerk in the back office of her mind stamped the request *denied*, and she woke at three in the morning with her heart going and the melody already dissolving, note by note, like sugar in warm water.

She lay in the dark and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do. Sixty-six minutes. One hour, six minutes. How many more hours were currently missing that she didn't know about? That was the question that made her sit up. An hour you noticed was an accounting problem. An hour you didn't notice was a hole in the floor of the world, and she was walking around her own life in the dark, and somewhere ahead of her there was a staircase that wasn't there.

Her mother had hidden things too. Not memory—money, mostly, and men, and the enormous fact of Vivienne's father, unclaimed and undiscussed, folded away in drawers the way other families folded linen. Vivienne had grown up watching a woman make dignity out of concealment, and she had told herself for sixty years that she was different, that she was the honest one, the one who wrote other people's secrets down. But the hands were the same hands. The instinct was the same instinct. *We don't say it. We don't show it. We carry it beautifully.*

She got up, went downstairs in the dark—she knew the house by touch, thirteen steps, the banister's cold brass, the doorframe her hip found without instruction—and stood in the kitchen in the blue light of the garden. The oranges sat in the bowl, glowing faintly, six small suns. She picked one up and held it to her nose and the scent went through her like a bell through bronze, and she thought, with sudden ferocity: *I will write it down. Tomorrow. I'll tell him about the car park. I'll tell him everything. He's writing my life; he can have all of it, even the white space, especially the white space—*

And then morning came, and with it the light, and with the light the great softening machinery of pride, which smoothed everything over the way frost melts off a windscreen, revealing not the road but your own reflection in the glass.

She did not mention the car park.

She dictated instead a chapter about her mother's hands—how they were always moving, mending, kneading, turning pages—and she made it beautiful, and it was beautiful, and somewhere in the middle of it she heard herself say, "She taught me that a thing carried well looks almost like a thing forgiven," and stopped, appalled, because she hadn't meant to say it aloud, and because she knew, with the clarity that only came to her now in flashes, like lightning through a shuttered window, that the sentence was not about her mother at all.

She deleted nothing. She sent it. Some sentences, once escaped, you had to let live.

But that night, before bed, she took the neurologist's brochure out of the drawer where she'd hidden it from Nell—from herself, really; Nell was just the pretext—and read it again by lamplight. *Memory concerns. Early assessment. Confidential.* The word *confidential* was printed in a serif font, reassuring as a handshake.

There was a phone number. There was, God help her, an online booking form.

Vivienne Ashford, who had spent her career making other people confess on the page, sat with the brochure in her lap and understood that she was approaching the one appointment she could not ghostwrite her way through. Whatever she decided, the white space was keeping its own records now. It did not need her permission. It did not need her voice notes. It was already writing the chapter she refused to dictate—and the only question, the question that sat in the dark kitchen with her like a second guest, was whether she would get her version down first, or whether the missing hours would finish the book without her.
