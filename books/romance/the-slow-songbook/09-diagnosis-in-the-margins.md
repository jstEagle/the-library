## 9. Diagnosis in the Margins

The transcripts arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing and everything, because Tuesdays were when Vivienne Ashford dictated.

Gideon had learned her rhythms the way a sailor learns weather—not by study but by necessity, by the slow accumulation of small observations that eventually became instinct. She dictated in the mornings, he'd discovered, before the light in whatever room she occupied grew too honest. Her voice notes came through the encrypted portal between eight and eleven, always prefaced by that small clearing of the throat, that half-second of silence where she was deciding whether to begin at all. In the evenings she sent corrections—terse, exacting, occasionally cruel in the way that only precision can be cruel. *No, not "gilded." I said "gilt." There's a difference. Gilding is something done to a thing. Gilt is what it is.*

He had begun to live inside her voice. This was the danger of ghostwriting that no one warned you about: you did not borrow a life, you were colonized by it. He woke with her cadences in his mouth. He caught himself narrating his own morning—the kettle, the grey light over the canal, the gull that had taken up residence on his neighbor's roof—in her clipped, ironic register, as though his days were drafts she might one day request to see.

And now, on this Tuesday, he sat at his desk with the new transcript open on one screen and the previous months' material spread across two others, and he was doing something he had never done with any client's material: he was reading it like an editor reads a manuscript, yes, but also like a doctor reads a chart.

It started, as these things often do, with a repetition.

* * *

The anecdote about the dog was the first flag. In March, she had told him about a terrier named Bisto who belonged to a neighbor in County Wicklow, how the animal would wait at the gate every afternoon for a school bus that no longer ran that route, waiting for children who had grown up and scattered decades ago. It was a good anecdote—she knew it was a good anecdote; she had built her career on knowing such things—and she told it well, with the particular tenderness she reserved for stories about loyalty that outlasts its object.

In June, she told it again.

Not word for word. That was the thing—he checked, he went back through every transcript twice, three times, because he wanted to be wrong. The second telling was looser, the details shifted slightly: the bus became a van, the village unnamed, the breed uncertain (*some kind of terrier, or maybe not, some scruffy thing*). But the bones of it were identical. The gate. The waiting. The children gone. And the tell, the one that made his chest go tight, was that the second time, she laughed at the end of it—a small, startled laugh, as though the story were new to her, as though she were discovering its shape even as she gave it to him.

People repeat themselves. Everyone knows this. Grandmothers repeat themselves, uncles at weddings repeat themselves, and the polite world has developed an entire choreography for pretending not to notice. But Gideon had spent twenty years inside other people's memories. He had transcribed ten thousand hours of recollection, and he knew the difference between a story someone loves and a story someone has lost the ability to know they've told.

A loved story returns with embellishment. Each retelling adds a detail, polishes a phrase, adjusts the emphasis the way a cook adjusts seasoning. The teller is present in every version, curating.

This was different. The second Bisto was thinner than the first. The details hadn't been added; they'd been *subtracted*. And she didn't know.

He should have let it go. He wants that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps accounts of men's cowardice and grace: in that first hour, sitting in his cold studio with the canal light going silver outside, he genuinely tried to let it go. One repeated anecdote is weather, not climate. People circle back. Manuscripts have loops.

But then there was the name.

* * *

Her editor at her first publishing house—a man who had championed her early biographies, who appeared in four separate anecdotes across the spring transcripts as a figure of generosity and mischief—was called Marcus Webb. She had spoken of him with real warmth: the lunches at a trattoria near Soho Square, the way he read manuscripts in the bath and delivered notes smelling faintly of sandalwood soap, the night he defended her against a hostile review at some long-ago party, standing on a chair.

In the July dictation, describing the same era, she referred to him as *Martin*. Then, mid-sentence, she stopped.

There was a pause on the recording—four seconds, five—and then her voice came back altered, smoothed into something performative. *"Marcus,"* she said, correcting herself with a brightness that fooled no one, least of all him. *"Marcus Webb. Funny, the names that stick."*

Funny, the names that stick. As though it were nothing. As though he hadn't just heard a woman reach into her own past and find a door suddenly locked from the inside.

Gideon sat very still for a long time after that. The gull on the roof made its harsh complaint. Somewhere down the canal a barge engine turned over, coughed, settled into its low workaday rhythm. His tea went cold beside him, untouched, which for Gideon Eriksen constituted a genuine emergency under normal circumstances.

Because here was the thing about being a professional rememberer: you learned the architecture of memory the way a carpenter learns wood. You learned which gaps are ordinary—the tip-of-the-tongue fog around proper nouns, the way everyone's childhood compresses after forty—and which gaps are structural. Which cracks are settlement and which are foundation.

He had heard the difference once before, years ago, helping a former client's widow assemble letters for a memorial volume. The widow's recollections had looped and thinned in exactly this way, and six months later came the diagnosis, and eighteen months after that, the funeral. He remembered sitting in her sitting room while she told him, for the third time in an hour, about her husband's proposal at Kew Gardens, and he remembered the specific quality of her confusion—not embarrassment, not yet, but a kind of gentle bewilderment, as if the story were a room she kept entering and finding the furniture rearranged.

Vivienne's pauses had that quality now. Not often. Not yet. But growing.

He began keeping a private log—which felt, immediately, like a betrayal, and which he did anyway. A document on his hard drive, unencrypted, titled only *Timeline Notes*. Dates. Anecdotes. Cross-references. The Bisto repetition. The Marcus/Martin slippage. A Tuesday in late July when she'd described her mother's songbook as blue and then, three weeks later, insisted it had always been green, and when he'd gently quoted her earlier description she'd gone quiet and then said, with terrible lightness, *"Well. One of us is inventing things, and it isn't me."*

That one had stung, and he'd deserved the sting, because he had handled it badly—too quick, too precise, the correction of a man who edits rather than a man who listens. He'd lain awake that night composing better versions of the exchange, gentler versions, versions in which he simply asked her to describe the book again without reference to color at all.

Because that was the other thing he understood, lying awake: she was managing him. Every ghostwriter learns to read a subject's management strategies—the deflections, the charm offensives, the strategic vulnerability deployed to keep the professional at a safe distance. Vivienne had been doing all of it since March. But lately the management had acquired a new texture. She was not deflecting to control the narrative. She was deflecting to conceal deterioration. There was a difference, and the difference had a smell, and the smell was fear.

* * *

He almost wrote to her a dozen times that week. Drafts accumulated in his folder like sediment: *I noticed the repetition of the terrier story—I wonder if we could—* deleted. *When you spoke of your mother's songbook in April you said—* deleted. *Is everything all right?* deleted before the sentence finished forming, because it was presumptuous, because they had rules, because Rule Two was *no identities, no past*, and a question like that cracked the whole covenant open.

The rules. God, the rules. He had set them himself, back in those first careful weeks—no names, no histories, no contact outside the portal—as much to protect himself from her as anything else, though he'd dressed it up as professionalism. And now the rules he'd built to keep his own ghosts out had become walls between him and a woman who might be losing the thread of her own story, alone in a house too large, dictating to a stranger because a stranger couldn't watch her hands shake or hear the word she couldn't find.

He thought about Nell, the sister, who surfaced in the transcripts like a lighthouse—fierce, funny, exasperated, clearly suspicious of the entire arrangement. Nell would notice. Nell was presumably already noticing. But Nell was family, and family was the last person a proud woman tells, and so Vivienne had apparently decided that the only witness safe enough for her disappearing act was a man with no face.

Which made him, he realized, not her ghostwriter but her confessor. And confessors, he thought grimly, do not get the luxury of pretending they haven't heard what they've heard.

On Thursday night he poured a whiskey he didn't want and took out the festival clip again—the one he'd found weeks ago, the one he'd watched too many times already. Seven years younger, Vivienne Ashford in a red jacket, luminous and merciless, dismantling him in front of four hundred people while the audience laughed. *Ghostwriting is where writers go to die,* she'd said, *and Mr.—what was it—Eriksen—is clearly comfortable at funerals.* He had memorized her cruelty the way you memorize a wound. What he was only now admitting was that he had also memorized everything else: the exact angle of her chin when she was certain, the way her hand moved when she talked about books, the fact that she'd worn a ring on her right hand and none on her left.

He watched the clip now and felt something he had no clean name for. Not the old humiliation—that had calcified into something more like scar tissue. Something closer to grief, pre-emptive, absurd: grieving a woman who was still alive, still sharp on the screen, still capable of destroying him, while the woman in the transcripts was quietly losing the war her younger self would never have surrendered.

*"You're clearly comfortable at funerals,"* she'd said.

Well. Someone had to be.

* * *

He composed the question for two days.

That was the discipline of it, the craft of it—the part of ghostwriting no one sees, the invisible architecture beneath a single line. He drafted and discarded perhaps thirty versions. Too clinical and she'd freeze. Too warm and she'd bolt. Too vague and she'd dismiss it; too precise and she'd know he'd been keeping a file on her mind like evidence.

What he finally settled on, at nearly midnight on Saturday, was this:

*"Vivienne—I've been assembling the timeline for the middle chapters, and a few threads seem to double back on themselves. The terrier at the gate appears twice, in March and June, in slightly different clothes. And I may be misreading my own notes, but the name of your first editor seems to shift between tellings. It's probably nothing—timelines tangle when a life is told out of order, and yours is being told very much out of order. But I'd rather ask than guess. Take your time. No corrections needed tonight. Just, when you're ready: tell me about the trattoria again. Start anywhere you like."*

He read it eleven times. He added the last line—"Start anywhere you like"—because it was true, because it gave her a door instead of a mirror, because if she was frightened then the kindest thing he could offer was not accuracy but permission.

Then he sat with his finger over the send key for a long moment, feeling the full weight of what he was doing: using the only intimacy they had—her voice, his attention—to ask a question that might end everything. If she was merely tired, he'd insulted her. If she was ill, he'd cornered her. Either way, the portal would never feel the same after tonight. He understood that. He pressed send anyway, because the alternative was to keep taking dictation from a woman coming apart and call it professionalism, and he had been comfortable at funerals long enough.

* * *

Sunday passed. Nothing.

Monday. Nothing.

He worked on other projects—there was always another project, some CEO's ascent or athlete's redemption, lives laundered through his sentences—and found himself unable to hold any of it. He reread her March transcripts like a man rereading letters. He walked the canal towpath in the rain without a coat and got properly soaked and barely noticed. On Monday night he opened the portal just to look at it, that grey interface with its grey confirmation messages, and felt the full absurdity of his position: a man in love, if that's what it was, with a voice; a man terrified, if that's what it was, for a woman who had once destroyed him and would be within her rights to destroy him again.

Tuesday evening. Wednesday. The silence developed texture, weight, a physical presence in the flat. He caught himself leaving his phone face-up on the desk, checking the portal between paragraphs, telling himself each empty refresh meant nothing—agencies delayed things, servers failed, people took holidays.

Thursday morning, day four, he broke his own rule and typed a second message, short, stripped of craft:

*"Take all the time you need. I'm here."*

He stared at it, hating its nakedness, and sent it anyway.

And then, that evening, as the canal went dark and the gull settled into its uneasy sleep on the ridge tiles opposite, the portal chimed.

Not a voice note. A text message, typed, which she never did—her medium was voice, always voice, and the typing itself was a kind of retreat, a woman choosing the medium where she could edit before committing. Four words:

*"We need to talk."*

Gideon read them standing up, though there was nowhere to go, nothing to do but stand in his cold bright studio with his heart going like a fist on a door, understanding that the question had landed, that the silence had been a woman deciding whether to run or speak, and that tomorrow—or whenever she chose—she would either end the assignment or tell him the truth she had been hiding since March.

He sat down slowly at his desk, opened a blank page, and waited for her voice.
