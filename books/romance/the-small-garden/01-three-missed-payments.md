## 1. Three Missed Payments

The oven timer went off at 3:47 a.m., and Emrys Reyes was already awake to hear it.

She'd been awake since three, actually, lying on the cot in the back office she refused to call a bedroom, staring at the water stain on the ceiling that shaped — if you squinted, if you were generous, if you were as tired as she was — a lopsided heart. Her mother had painted over it twice. It always came back. Some things just insisted on themselves.

She swung her feet onto the cold tile and stood up into the day.

The kitchen of Sugarwreath Café was four steps wide and six steps long, and Emrys could navigate it blind, which was good, because at this hour it might as well have been. She lit the deck ovens, pulled the butter from the walk-in where it had been tempering since midnight, and let her hands start the work her brain hadn't agreed to yet: laminating dough for croissants, folding and rolling, folding and rolling, the rhythm of it steadier than any heartbeat she owned.

Three missed payments. That was the number. She'd counted them again before getting up, because apparently she enjoyed pain the way other people enjoyed crossword puzzles. The flour supplier. The dairy distributor. And the big one — the one with the red stamp and the phrase *final notice* printed in a font designed by someone who hated joy — the landlord of the building itself.

*Final notice.* As if anything about Sugarwreath's situation were final. Final meant decided. Final meant over. Emrys had been living in the space between *overdue* and *over* for eleven months now, and she intended to keep living there indefinitely, thank you very much, like a woman squatting in the gap between two cliffs.

The first batch of croissants went in at 4:15. She wiped her hands on her apron — her mother's apron, actually, faded navy with a small embroidered sprig of rosemary at the hip, because her mother had believed every recipe needed an herb and every apron needed a blessing — and walked out front to face the invoice box.

The invoice box was a wooden crate that had once held clementines. Now it held her ruin, organized alphabetically by vendor, which was either admirable or pathetic depending on how much sleep she'd gotten. She carried it to Table Six — the best table, the window table, the one with the view of Marigold Street and, past the laundromat, the chain-link fence and raised beds of the Fenwick Street Community Garden — and she sat down with a pencil behind her ear and a calculator app she never trusted and began, for the nine-hundredth time, to do the math.

The math did not work.

It had not worked in some time. She knew this the way you know a song is sad even in another language. But there was a particular ritual to counting money you don't have, and Emrys performed it faithfully, because the alternative was admitting that the ritual was pointless, and if the ritual was pointless then the café was dying, and if the café was dying then—

No. She flipped to the next invoice.

Sugarwreath had been her mother's dream before it was ever hers. Lucia Reyes had scrimped and saved and catered a hundred weddings and one truly legendary quinceañera to open these doors when Emrys was nineteen, and for six years the place had hummed — cinnamon and cardamom, regulars who ordered "the usual," Sunday afternoons so crowded you couldn't hear yourself think. Then her mother got sick, and the humming slowed, and then her mother died, and the humming stopped altogether for a while, and by the time Emrys worked up the nerve to come back — really come back, not just stand outside looking through the glass like a ghost auditioning for her own life — the regulars had found other cafés and the equipment had found other problems and the debt had found its way, comfortably, permanently, home.

That was four years ago. Four years of fourteen-hour days. Four years of being her own accountant, plumber, marketing department, and occasionally, when the toilet backed up during a rush, her own hazmat team. Four years of telling herself *next quarter*. Next quarter the foot traffic would pick up. Next quarter the new pastry case would pay for itself. Next quarter she'd finally get ahead.

Next quarter had the survival instincts of a mayfly.

"Okay." She said it out loud, to the empty café, because saying things out loud made them real and real things could be fought. "Okay. Numbers."

Numbers: she owed $14,207 across all accounts. She had $3,880 in the business account and $612 in the personal one, and the personal one was already spoken for — rent on the studio apartment she kept mostly to store books and shower, since she slept here more often than not. The weekend harvest festival was coming in six weeks, which historically brought maybe $2,000 in extra sales if the weather cooperated, and the weather in this town cooperated the way cats cooperate: on its own schedule, for its own reasons.

Total projected shortfall by December, if nothing changed: somewhere north of nine thousand dollars.

Emrys put down the pencil and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations.

There was an option. There was always an option; options circled failing businesses like gulls circling a dropped fry. Marcus Tran at the credit union had mentioned a consolidation loan, which was banker for *I will lend you enough rope to hang yourself more efficiently*. There was a franchise outfit that wanted to buy the corner lot — she'd tossed their letter unopened, but she remembered the number on the envelope's return address, and numbers like that didn't knock politely. And there was Dev, her ex, who had texted her last month — *hey, weird question, are you seeing anyone?* — with the unmistakable energy of a man who had recently gotten promoted and was feeling charitable toward his past.

She had not answered Dev. She would not answer Dev. But the fact that she'd even hesitated — three full seconds, thumb hovering over the keyboard, three seconds in which she'd imagined what it would be like to hand the whole mess to someone else, someone with a salary and a 401(k) and a jawline she used to find comforting — that fact sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the thing Emrys Reyes had figured out, at cost, the hard way, with interest: romance was a luxury good. Like saffron. Like vanilla bean from Madagascar. Like the imported French butter she bought exactly once a year, at Christmas, and wept over while using.

Love required surplus. Love required slack in the system — time to linger, money to waste, emotional bandwidth left over after the bills were paid. And Emrys had no slack. Every hour she had went into the ovens or the ledger. Every dollar had a job and a name and a due date. If she let someone in — really in, past the counter, past the charming barista patter — they would see the cot in the back office and the invoice crate and the water-stain heart, and they would look at her the way her cousin Priya had looked at her at Thanksgiving two years ago, that quick flicker of pity quickly hidden, and Emrys would rather eat her own laminated dough raw than be pitied again.

So: no. Sworn off. Not temporarily, not *until things settle down*, but structurally, architecturally, the way you board up a window in a storm. Romance was a luxury, and Emrys Reyes was not currently in the market for luxuries. She was in the market for eleven hundred dollars by Friday and a miracle by Christmas, and she intended to acquire both through sheer bloody-minded competence.

The oven chimed. Croissants. She got up, pulled them — golden, shattering, perfect, the one thing in her life that did exactly what it was supposed to do — and set them on the rack to cool, and the smell filled the little kitchen like an argument she was winning.

* * *

At 5:50, Rosa Delgado knocked on the front glass with her cane, ten minutes early, as always.

Rosa was eighty-one and had been coming to Sugarwreath since before it was Sugarwreath, back when the space had been a nail salon and Lucia Reyes had still been dreaming out loud at the garden, waving her trowel at the empty storefront across the street. Rosa took her coffee black with one sugar and sat at Table Six and read the obituaries first, "to know who I've outlived," she said, cheerfully, every single morning, and Emrys loved her with the fierce uncomplicated love you reserve for people who ask nothing of you except your continued existence.

"You look terrible, mija," Rosa announced, settling in.

"Thank you. I cultivated it."

"No sleep?"

"Some sleep. Between the third and fourth invoice."

Rosa snorted and unfolded her paper. Emrys brought her coffee and the heel end of yesterday's brioche, toasted, which Rosa pretended not to know was free and Emrys pretended not to know Rosa knew.

Outside the window, Marigold Street was doing its slow gray wake-up. The laundromat's sign buzzed on. Mr. Okafor swept his sidewalk next door at the hardware store. And down at the end of the block, visible through the fence slats, the community garden lay dark and dew-heavy, her mother's old plot a tangle of gone-to-seed kale and the rosemary bush that refused, year after year, despite neglect, drought, and one memorable incident involving a delivery truck, to die.

Lucia Reyes had helped found that garden twenty-two years ago. Had poured her Sundays into it, her Saturdays, her spare cash, her considerable fury whenever the city threatened it — and there had been threats, over the years, developers sniffing around the lot like dogs around a butcher's door, and every time, Lucia had been there at the zoning meetings with her clipboard and her voice that could strip paint. Emrys had grown up thinking her mother could save anything she set her mind to.

Then her mother had gotten sick, and it turned out there were things even Lucia Reyes couldn't out-organize, and Emrys had learned the difference between a dream you can fight for and one you can only sit beside.

"You're doing the face," Rosa said, without lowering her newspaper.

"What face?"

"The face your mother used to make. When the numbers were bad but she wasn't ready to say so."

"I don't make faces. I'm a professional."

"Mm." Rosa turned a page. "She'd have figured it out by now, you know. Your mother. She'd have found the angle."

"The angle," Emrys said, "is that I'm broke, Rosa."

"The angle," Rosa said, "is that you're the best baker in five neighborhoods and everybody knows it and nobody can afford you, and your mother would have said that's not a bakery problem, that's a story problem. You're telling the wrong story." She folded the paper at last and fixed Emrys with eyes that had watched this street for eight decades and missed approximately nothing. "Also, your cousin called the house phone looking for you. Tía Marisol. Something about a letter."

Emrys's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for aircraft.

"A letter."

"From the family. About the reunion." Rosa picked her paper back up, merciless. "The harvest one. At the hacienda. Where everyone shows off."

Everyone showed off. Everyone measured. Priya with her tech salary and her Bay Area townhouse, Priya whose wedding had cost more than Sugarwreath's annual revenue, Priya who was kind about it, which was worse, Priya who asked *and how's the little shop doing?* in a voice like a hand reaching gently toward a wound.

Every two years, the Reyes clan gathered for the harvest reunion, and every two years Emrys attended and was weighed, and found — not wanting, exactly, but *lighter* than expected. Less than the sum of her ambition. A woman running a failing café in a neighborhood everyone else had driven away from, sleeping above her own ovens, single at thirty-four, serving coffee to octogenarians.

And this year, this year of all years, with three missed payments and a final notice and nine thousand dollars of shortfall, she was supposed to stand in her aunt's backyard holding a paper plate and say, brightly, *things are great, really, we're expanding.*

"I'm not going," Emrys said.

"You say that every time."

"And I mean it every time."

"You went last time."

"That was a tactical error."

Rosa smiled into her coffee, the smile of a woman who had outlived everyone in the obituaries and intended to outlive Emrys's excuses too. "Tía Marisol says if you don't RSVP by Friday, she's sending your uncle to collect you. And mija—" she patted Emrys's hand where it rested on the table, next to the invoice crate, next to the math that didn't work, "—you remember what your Uncle Berto is like in a car. Four hours. About his prostate."

Emrys laughed despite herself, a short, cracked sound, and outside the window the sky over the garden was turning the color of peach flesh, and the first croissant customers of the day were pushing through the door smelling of cold air and expectation, and none of it — not one bit of it — solved anything.

But it was 6 a.m., and the doors were open, and the ovens were warm, and Emrys Reyes had survived worse mornings than this one. Probably. She'd have to check the ledger.

She tied her apron tighter, pinned on her smile, and stepped out from behind Table Six to greet the day — while in the back, unopened among the invoices, sat the cream-colored envelope with the familiar looping handwriting, the one she'd been avoiding for a week, the one that smelled faintly of her aunt's lavender drawer and contained, she was certain, a summons she could not afford to answer and could not, somehow, bring herself to refuse.
