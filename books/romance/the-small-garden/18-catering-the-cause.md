## 18. Catering the Cause

I baked until my hands ached and my kitchen smelled like a bribe.

That was the plan, anyway. You couldn't argue people into saving a garden, but you could feed them, and fed people stayed longer, listened harder, opened their wallets with less resentment. My mother had known that. Every meeting she'd ever run had ended around a table with something warm in the middle of it. I'd spent three years pretending I didn't remember her tricks. Now I was using all of them at once, and it felt like both a betrayal and an inheritance.

Two hundred mini galettes. Six dozen lemon bars dusted with powdered sugar so fine it looked like frost. A honey-lavender cake shaped like nothing in particular because I'd run out of ambition around midnight and decided rustic was a brand. And forty of the tarts — the signature ones, the ones Auntie Rosa had sneered at before Julian Ashford told a room full of Reyes women that pastry was architecture, that the latticework alone took years to learn, that he'd eaten tarts on three continents and mine could hold its own against any of them.

He hadn't looked at me when he said it. That was the worst part. He'd said it to the table, easy as weather, like defending me cost him nothing, and then he'd gone back to his wine while my cousin's fiancé laughed too loudly and my aunt studied me over her glasses with an expression I still couldn't decode.

That was eleven days ago. Eleven days since the walk-in freezer, since the cold got into both our voices and stayed there. Since he stood among the sheet pans with steam coming off his breath and said, *I came here to stop it*, and I said, *You came here to scout it*, and neither of us had been entirely wrong.

Eleven days of not answering two texts. The first one: *The fundraiser is Saturday. I'll be there.* No question mark. The second, sent six hours later, apparently after some internal struggle I chose not to imagine: *Unloading starts at seven.*

I answered neither. But I also didn't change the time on the flyer, which felt, in the economy of grudges, like a concession the size of a country.

* * *

Saturday came up gray and cold, the kind of October morning that made the garden look less like a cause and more like a casualty. Frost had hit the basil hard earlier in the week; Priya's tomato cages stood empty like little wire ghosts. The notice from Ashford Development was still stapled to the fence post by the gate, rain-warped now, the corporate blue logo bleeding into the wood grain. Someone had drawn a small angry face on it in Sharpie. I chose to believe that was Dee, age nine, who watered the plot next to her grandmother's and took the demolition threat more personally than any adult on the block.

By seven-fifteen the folding tables were stacked against the shed and nobody strong enough to move them had arrived yet. Priya was arguing with a folding chair that had opinions. Marcus from the hardware store stood holding a coffee and offering commentary. I was arranging galettes on a tray with the focus of a surgeon, because if my hands were busy they couldn't shake.

"You know he's going to show," Priya said, not looking up from her war with the chair.

"I know no such thing."

"Emrys."

"He texted twice. I didn't answer. That's a whole conversation in grudge language."

"It's a whole conversation in coward language," she said, and then the chair surrendered and she sat down on it, victorious, and looked at me with the mild mercilessness of a woman who has known you for nine years. "You froze him out in a walk-in. Fair. He earned it. But you've been baking like someone trying to sweat out a fever, and those aren't fundraiser quantities, those are *feelings* quantities."

"They're forty tarts."

"They're a cry for help in puff pastry."

I put a lemon bar down with slightly more force than necessary. "He lied to me. Not by commission — by omission, which is worse, because omission is a choice you make every single day and he made it for months. Every croissant. Every walk down this street. Every time he asked me about the garden and wrote things in that notebook, he knew—"

"He knew," Priya agreed. "And then he quit. Publicly. Got cut off. Is currently living above the laundromat on Delancey, which, by the way, is not a building his family owns, I checked, because I check things now, we all check things now." She folded her arms. "You don't have to forgive him today. But if he shows up and hauls tables, let him haul tables. People are allowed to be more than their worst omission."

"That's very generous for someone whose community garden is being stolen by his family."

"My community garden is being saved by forty tarts and a woman who can't stop making them. We're all compromised."

* * *

He showed up at 7:40, on foot, in a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled and work gloves hanging out of his back pocket like he'd planned the whole outfit as an argument.

I saw him come up the block and had exactly enough time to arrange my face into something neutral, which lasted approximately four seconds, because the first thing he did was walk past me without a word, go straight to the stack of tables, and start hauling them across the grass two at a time like a man serving a sentence he'd assigned himself.

No hello. No apology speech. No attempt to catch my eye. Just tables, moved with the grim efficiency of someone who understood that the only currency he had left was labor, and he intended to spend all of it.

"Morning," Marcus said to him, cautiously.

"Ashford," Marcus added, when Julian didn't answer fast enough, testing the name like a loose tooth.

"Yeah." Julian set a table down and unfolded its legs with a snap. "You can say it. Everybody does."

"I was gonna say, we're short on the canopy poles. You know anything about canopies?"

Julian blinked. Whatever script he'd prepared for hostility, helpfulness had clearly not been in it. "I — yes. Actually. If you've got them."

"Shed. Emrys, key?"

I handed over the key without letting our fingers touch, which required a small feat of choreography I refused to be embarrassed about. He took it, nodded once — at me, not quite at my eyes — and went.

"Well," said Priya.

"Don't," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said 'well' in a tone."

"The tone was 'well, he's been here ten minutes and already fixed our canopy problem, interesting how some people apologize in furniture.'" She picked up a tray of lemon bars and headed for the sign-in table. "I'm just saying, watch what he does, not what he says. It's the only honest data left."

* * *

He worked for four hours without stopping.

I know because I watched more than I meant to, between batches and refills and the slow accumulation of neighbors. He assembled the canopy with Marcus, argued gently with a stubborn pole, lost, won. He strung lights along the fence even though the event wasn't until evening, because Dee informed him the lights were essential and Dee's word was apparently law now. He carried chairs. He dug out the fire pit that had silted over since summer. At one point I looked up from the tart table and caught him kneeling in the dirt by the herb spiral, righting a row of markers my mother had painted twenty years ago — *THYME, SAGE, THE MINT THAT BETRAYS YOU* — setting each one back in the earth with both hands, careful, like he was handling evidence of something.

My mother's handwriting. His hands. I turned away and burned my thumb on a tray edge and decided the two events were unrelated.

The crowd built through the afternoon. Half the neighborhood, then more than half. People who'd never set foot in the garden came for the food and stayed for the speeches. Councilwoman Okafor arrived at five and ate two galettes and asked intelligent questions about zoning. The local paper sent a photographer who kept pointing his camera at the Ashford Development notice, and I kept positioning myself in front of it, and we developed a silent antagonistic relationship that ended with him getting a shot of me laughing at something Dee said, which was fine, actually. Let them print that. Let them see what they were threatening.

I sold out of everything by seven. Everything except the tarts. I'd held those back deliberately — forty of them, arranged on my good stand under a hand-lettered card reading *BAKED BY EMRYS REYES, OF REYES & DAUGHTERS* — and I watched people circle them all evening, drawn and hesitant, the way people circle anything beautiful that reminds them of a bill.

At quarter past seven, Julian appeared at the tart table.

Up close he looked tired in a way sleep wouldn't fix. There was dirt on his knees from the herb spiral and a scratch along one forearm from the canopy pole, and he stood there with his hands at his sides like a man presenting himself for inspection.

"These are the last thing left," he said.

"They're not for sale."

"No?"

"They're for the volunteers. People who worked." I lifted my chin. "You qualify. Barely. You were late."

"Forty minutes."

"I counted."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile used to live. "Fair. Can I—" He gestured at the stand.

"Take one. One. They're portioned for gratitude, not appetite."

He picked up a tart and held it a moment, turning it, studying the lattice. "You fixed the crimp," he said quietly. "It used to pinch on one side. Left side. You'd rush it at the end."

My chest did something complicated. "You noticed that?"

"I notice everything about your pastry. It's the only thing about you I ever got to look at honestly." He said it without drama, without leaning on it, and then he took a bite, standing there in the cold with dirt on his knees, and closed his eyes for half a second longer than a man should over a tart. "Yeah," he said, mostly to himself. "Still the best thing in this city."

And then — because he was who he was, because he'd learned something in the wreckage or maybe always known it and only now could afford it — he didn't push. He set down the rest of the tart, wiped his hands on his jeans, and said, "They'll want you to speak tonight. When they announce the petition count. You should know your mother's name is on the founding paperwork. Priya found it. First signature."

He walked away before I could answer, back toward the lights, back toward whatever needed hauling, and I stood at my tart stand with my heart going like a mixer on high and thought: *that's not fair, that's cheating, you can't just — hand me my own mother like a gift and walk off.*

* * *

The announcement came at eight, under the string lights, with the whole neighborhood pressed shoulder to shoulder and the frost-breath rising off the crowd like the garden itself was exhaling. Priya read the numbers — eleven hundred signatures, four hundred over target — and the sound that went up wasn't a cheer so much as a roar with crying in it. Councilwoman Okafor said the right words about historic community assets. Somebody started singing, badly, and everybody joined.

And I spoke. Three minutes, maybe. About my mother, and the mint that betrays you, and how she believed a garden was a promise a neighborhood makes to its future self. My voice cracked exactly once, on the word *kept*, and eleven hundred strangers pretended not to notice, which is the kindest thing a crowd can do.

Afterward, in the dismantling, in the clatter of chairs and the dying of the lights, I found myself walking toward the gate past the shed, and Julian was there stacking the last of the tables alone, and I stopped, because pride is heavy and I was tired of carrying it uphill.

"The left side of the crimp," I said.

He looked up.

"I fixed it because somebody told me once it pinched. Years ago. I never asked how he knew." I wrapped my arms around myself against the cold. "You paid attention even when you were lying. That's the part I can't figure out how to hold."

"I'm not asking you to hold it yet." He set down the table. "I'm asking you to let me keep showing up. Tables, poles, whatever's heavy. Until the weight of it means something."

"That's not how forgiveness works."

"No," he agreed. "But it's how it starts. Same as gardens. You don't get harvest first."

We stood there in the dark with the smell of frost and cut grass and cooling sugar, and I wanted — God, I wanted — to say something true and large. Instead I heard myself say:

"There's a zoning hearing in three weeks. The council votes after. If you're going to keep showing up, that's where it counts. In the room. On the record. Against them."

Something went very still in his face. Then he nodded, slow, like a man accepting terms he'd been dreading and choosing anyway.

"I'll be there," he said. "On the record."

I turned to go, and made it four steps before his voice caught me.

"Emrys. The tarts." A pause. "Thank you. For the one. Even though I barely qualified."

I didn't turn around. "Next time," I said, "do the dishes."

Behind me, quiet enough that I almost missed it, he laughed — and it was the first time in eleven days the sound of him hadn't hurt.

I was halfway home, hands still smelling of lavender and sugar, when my phone buzzed. A number I didn't know. A photo message loading slowly over the weak signal at the top of my street.

When it finally came through, it was a picture of a document. A permit application, stamped APPROVED, dated tomorrow — and the applicant line didn't say Ashford Development at all.

It said: *Meridian Group.*

Someone else was moving on the garden.
