## 7. Marguerite's Version

Marguerite Voss had eaten at some of the finest restaurants in Europe. She had dined at three-Michelin-star temples in Lyon, at private chefs' tables in Vienna, at a rooftop in Lisbon where the bill had made her laugh out loud. None of it had prepared her for the particular violence of recognizing a man by his hands.

She was standing in the doorway of the vineyard's kitchen, having come down from the tasting room in search of coffee and possibly a second blanket, and the cook had his back to her. He was working bread dough on the scarred oak counter, and his hands moved with a fluency that was beautiful and wrong at the same time—beautiful because they were sure, wrong because she knew those hands. She had spent fifteen years watching them from the second chair, then from the first chair, then from concert halls he would never enter.

Dominic Montrose was making bread at a failing vineyard in the Hudson Valley, and he had not turned around.

She should have said something. She was, by every professional measure, an excellent improviser; she had once covered a snapped string mid-Shostakovich so smoothly that the critics wrote about it for a week. But she stood in that doorway like a woman who had walked into the wrong funeral, and she said nothing, and the snow ticked against the window, and Theo's voice drifted down the hall complaining about the wifi as though the universe had not just rearranged itself around a loaf of sourdough.

*Of course it's him,* she thought. *Of course it's this vineyard. The universe has never once let me have a simple weekend.*

She had, in fact, chosen Vireo Vineyard herself. That was the bitter joke of it. She'd picked it from a list of forty venues because the photographs showed good acoustics in the tasting room—barrel-vaulted ceiling, stone floor, the kind of space where a cello could bloom—and because it was cheap, because the vineyard was failing, and because it was two hundred miles from anywhere she was known. She had wanted a weekend of logistics and bad coffee and no one asking her to sign anything.

She had not checked who cooked here. Why would she? Cooks were anonymous. Cooks were the people who existed in the margins of her life, delivering trays to green rooms.

Dominic Montrose had never been in the margins of anything. That had been the whole problem.

* * *

The storm had made the decision for all of them, which Marguerite privately appreciated. She was terrible at leaving. She always had been. Give her a closed road and a legitimate excuse to stay, and she would take it with both hands and a small, guilty prayer of thanks.

So now there were three of them in a farmhouse kitchen with the power flickering like a nervous candle, and the cook—Dominic, *Dominic*, she had to stop flinching at the name in her own head—moved through the room feeding them by lantern light with the calm of a man who had done this a thousand times.

"Soup's on the stove, bread's in twenty minutes," he said, not looking at her. "Theo, there's more wood on the porch if the fire gets low. Marguerite—" a pause, so brief she might have imagined it, "—there's a blanket chest at the top of the stairs."

"You know my name," she said.

"You're the reason the roads are full of people looking for a gala venue," he said. "It was in the email thread."

It was a lie. It was a competent, well-shaped lie, and she respected it the way she respected good phrasing, and she decided not to call it. Not yet. There was a thing she had to do first, and it required him not to bolt.

She sat down at the kitchen table.

Here is what Marguerite knew, and had known for fifteen years, and had carried the way you carry a stone in your shoe—not so heavy you can't walk, but heavy enough that you never forget it's there:

The week of Dominic Montrose's final concert, she had seen a man in the musicians' lounge who should not have been there.

She had been twenty-six, hungry in the way that young cellists are hungry, second chair and clawing at the first. She had come back to the hall at eleven at night for a practice room she'd booked, and the building had been mostly dark, and in the lounge—the small, carpeted lounge with the bad couch and the coffee machine that only worked if you kicked it—there had been a man crouched by the counter, doing something with a small brown bottle.

He had looked up. She had looked at him.

It had been Anton Reiner.

The conductor. *The* conductor. The man whose name on a program could sell out a hall in four cities. The man who had built both her career and Dominic's, who had plucked Dominic out of a youth orchestra at nineteen and called him "my left hand" in interviews, who had taken her on at twenty-three and told her, in his soft accented English, that talent was a debt and he was the collector.

Anton Reiner had stood up in the empty lounge, put the small brown bottle in his coat pocket, and said, "Miss Voss. You practice late."

And she—young, ambitious, terrified, and clever enough to understand in one cold flash exactly what she was looking at—had said, "I do."

He had smiled. He had wished her a good night. And she had gone up to her practice room and played Bach for two hours with her heart slamming, telling herself she had seen nothing, that there were a hundred explanations, that the great Anton Reiner would never—

Except that three days later, Dominic Montrose's hands had failed him in the middle of the Elgar, in front of two thousand people, and the diagnosis had been some vague neurological nonsense that no specialist could quite confirm, and the narrative had written itself: the golden boy, the conductor's favorite, cracked under the weight of his own legend. And the chair had opened. And she had sat in it.

She had sat in it and she had never once, in fifteen years, let herself finish the thought.

That was the discipline of it. That was the art. She had built a career the size of a cathedral on a foundation she refused to look at, and she had gotten very, very good at not looking. She had told herself stories: that it was a coincidence, that the bottle was nothing, that even if it was something, she hadn't done it, she had only failed to stop it, and there was a difference, wasn't there, wasn't there—

There was not a difference. She had known that for a long time too.

* * *

The bread came out of the oven while the storm screamed against the windows, and it filled the kitchen with a smell so aggressively domestic that Marguerite nearly laughed. Dominic set the loaf on a board and cut it, and she watched his hands do it—steady, sure, the knife moving in long even strokes—and something in her chest ached in a way she had no instrument for.

"Good bread," she said, because she had to say something, and because it was true.

"It's the one thing I never get wrong," he said.

"You used to say that about the Elgar."

The words were out before she could weigh them. She saw him go still—not dramatically, not like a man struck, but like a man listening to a distant sound and deciding whether it's thunder. Theo, at the far end of the table with his laptop and his gala spreadsheets, looked up sharply.

"Did I," Dominic said.

"You said it once. In Salzburg. You said, 'The Elgar and I have an understanding.'" She turned her coffee cup in her hands. "I thought it was arrogant. I was twenty-five and I thought everything you said was arrogant."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was true," she said, "and that's worse."

He looked at her then, for the first time all evening, fully, across the lantern light and the steam off the soup, and his eyes were the same—dark, level, with that unnerving quality of a man who is always hearing a second line under whatever you're saying. Fifteen years ago that look had made her furious. It still did. That was almost a comfort. Some things survived anything.

"You're a long way from the concert halls, Miss Voss," he said.

"So are you."

"I live here."

"You *hide* here," she said, and immediately regretted it, because it was cruel, and because it was true, and because the moment she said it she understood that she had no right to it—that of the two of them, she was the one with blood on her hands and he was the one with ruined ones, and cruelty from her position was not boldness, it was theft.

But he didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing. He just looked at her with something almost like gentleness and said, "Somebody has to feed the harvest crews," and went back to his soup, and Marguerite sat there with her secret shifting in her mouth like a loose tooth.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when Theo had finally surrendered to the storm and gone up to bed with his laptop under his arm like a shield, and the fire had burned down to a red murmur—Marguerite stood at the kitchen window and watched the snow erase the world.

The vineyard was beautiful under it. She could admit that. The rows of dormant vines ran down the hillside in long dark lines, half-buried, patient, the whole field looking like a page of music waiting for someone brave enough to play it. She thought about what this place must have been to him. A refuge. A place where nobody knew his name and his hands were just hands, good for bread, good for nothing else, and that was enough.

She had spent fifteen years in the world he'd fled. She knew its exact weight now, in a way she hadn't at twenty-six. She knew the airports and the managers and the critics who wrote about her "flawless technique" in reviews that never once mentioned whether the music had meant anything. She knew the particular loneliness of being excellent. And she knew—she had known for years, in the small honest hours—that the chair she'd taken was haunted. That every great night had a faint wrong note under it that no audience could hear but she could, always, a single sustained tone that was the sound of a man's career snapping in half so hers could begin.

And now she was snowed into his kitchen, and he was ten feet away sleeping in the loft above the tasting room, and the secret was in her chest like a swallowed stone, and the storm had closed every road out.

Here was the thing about Marguerite Voss that the profiles never got right: they called her cold. Ice queen, the London papers had said, after a review she hadn't deserved and a quote she had. But cold was not the truth of her. The truth was that she felt things at a volume most people couldn't survive, and so she had learned to keep the lid on, and the lid read as cold, and the lid had worked for fifteen years.

The lid was not working tonight.

She thought about telling him. She actually stood there, at the window, in the blue dark, and rehearsed it. *Dominic. In October of that year, the week of the concert, I saw Anton in the musicians' lounge at eleven at night with a bottle in his hand, and I said nothing, and I took your chair, and I have been paying for it ever since in a currency you can't see.*

She got as far as *Dominic* in her head.

And then she thought about what it would do. It would not just wound him—it would unmake him. Because she had read the interviews, everyone had read the interviews, the ones where he'd said, in that careful, dignified way of his, that his body had failed him, that it was illness, that he bore no one any ill will. He had built a whole identity on that story. *The man whose hands betrayed him.* It was a tragedy, but it was a *clean* tragedy. It was nobody's fault. It was the kind of thing a man could carry into a kitchen in the Hudson Valley and set down at the end of the day.

What she knew would take that from him. It would tell him that his body hadn't betrayed him at all. That a man he had loved and trusted—she knew he'd loved Anton, everyone knew, Anton had been the closest thing to a father the golden boy had—had done it deliberately. That the fifteen years of silence, the small life, the bread, the vineyard, all of it had been built on a lie someone else had written.

And that she had known. That she had stood in a dark lounge at twenty-six and looked the truth in the face and walked upstairs and played Bach.

She pressed her forehead against the cold glass.

There was also, she admitted, in the last honest hour of the night, the smaller and uglier truth: telling would end her. Not metaphorically. Anton Reiner was still alive, still revered, still conducting, still a man whose word against hers would be a landslide. A young second chair's testimony, fifteen years stale, against the great Anton Reiner? She would be finished. Sued, smeared, discredited, and worse—remembered not as the woman who told the truth but as the bitter rival who'd tried to tear down a dying legend. She had a gala season booked through June. She had a manager asleep down the hall who had given her his whole career. She had a life that, whatever its haunted foundation, was *hers*.

So she stood at the window and she did not tell him, and she told herself it was mercy, and the words fit so poorly that she could hear them rattling.

*One more day,* she thought. *The roads will open, and we'll all go back to our lives, and I'll carry it a little longer. I'm good at carrying it. I've had practice.*

The snow kept falling. Down the hall, a floorboard creaked—Theo, sleepless too, pacing like a man rehearsing something. And from the loft above the tasting room, silence, which she chose to believe was sleep.

She went up to bed with the stone still in her chest, and in the morning she would make conversation about acoustics and catering contracts and the gala in June, and she would be flawless, because she was always flawless, and flawless was the whole problem.

But as she climbed the stairs, one thought followed her up like a cat that had decided to adopt her:

*Anton is coming to this gala. Anton always comes where I am. And in June, in this tasting room, with these stone floors and this barrel-vaulted ceiling, Dominic Montrose is going to be standing in this kitchen when that man walks through the door—and I will have to watch what fifteen years of silence does when it finally meets the man it was built on.*

She closed her door on the thought. It came in anyway, under it, like snow.

Down the hill, the storm went on burying the vines, and Marguerite lay awake listening to the house tick and settle, and somewhere below her, in the dark kitchen, the bread she hadn't eaten cooled on its board, and the secret sat in her chest and quietly grew its teeth.
