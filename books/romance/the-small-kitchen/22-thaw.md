## 22. Thaw

The sound woke Marguerite before the light did: a long, wet drip from the eaves outside the tasting room window, then another, then a whole stuttering sentence of them, as if the roof had finally found its voice after three days of silence.

She lay on the cot they'd made up for her between the wine barrels and listened to the storm die.

Snowmelt. That was the sound. That was the end of it.

For three days the world had been white and sealed, and she had been—she searched for the word and didn't like any of the candidates—*happy* was too small and too large by half. *Safe* was a lie. *Trapped* was technically accurate and emotionally fraudulent. She had been held, the way dough is held under a cloth while it rises: warm, enclosed, unable to escape, changing all the while into something else.

And now the roads were opening. She could hear it in the drip. She could hear her exit being carved out of the mountainside one meltwater bead at a time.

Marguerite Voss had spent twenty years making entrances. She was very good at them—the coat over the arm, the timing of the doorway, the way she could make a green room feel like she'd brought the weather with her. Exits, too. She'd left orchestras, managers, lovers, continents, each departure polished until it gleamed. What she had never once practiced, in all those years, was staying past the moment when leaving became possible.

She got up, dressed in yesterday's sweater because her suitcase had run dry two days ago, and went looking for coffee.

* * *

Dominic was already in the kitchen, of course. He was always already in the kitchen; she suspected he slept standing at that counter the way soldiers slept standing at their posts. The room smelled of yeast and woodsmoke and the particular scorched-sugar sweetness of the failed red he'd been reducing into something for breakfast that had no right to work as well as it did.

"Roads are open," he said without turning around. "Theo's been on the phone since six. The plow came through at five."

"I heard." She poured herself coffee from the dented pot. Her hands were steady. She noticed this the way she always noticed it now—with a small, superstitious gratitude, as if steadiness were weather that might turn. "I'll need to get back to the city. There's the recording session Thursday. And the gala contracts won't negotiate themselves."

"Mm," said Dominic, which was not agreement and not disagreement but simply the sound of a man feeding a sourdough starter that had survived three days of no electricity through sheer stubbornness, much like its owner.

She wrapped both hands around the mug and watched him work. His hands moved over the dough with a fluency that still caught at something behind her ribs—not envy anymore, or not only envy, but a kind of grief-adjacent awe. Those hands. She had sat second chair and watched those hands across fifteen years of rehearsals, and then she had watched them fail, and then she had watched the world decide what their failure meant, and she had said nothing, nothing, nothing, for fifteen years, and now—

Now the roads were open.

"Your bread's better than mine," she said, because she couldn't say any of the rest of it yet.

"My bread is the only thing I've let myself be good at," Dominic said mildly. "You'd be better if you stopped trying to conduct the dough."

"I do not conduct the dough."

"You shape it like you're cueing a section. Left hand first, imperious little gesture. I've been watching you for three days."

"You've been watching me sabotage your loaves, you mean. Which is rich, coming from—" She stopped. The joke had been aimed at the old wound, the old war, the thing they'd spent three days carefully dismantling brick by brick, and it landed wrong, landed on the fresh mortar, and she saw him feel it.

They stood in the kitchen with the drip-drip-drip of the thaw coming through the walls, and Marguerite understood with terrible clarity that this was the last morning. After today there would be no more flour on his forearms, no more of Theo pretending to read his phone while actually watching them both, no more of the strange, provisional peace of the snowbound house where the truth had come out in pieces, like ice cracking off a branch.

Unless she did something about it.

* * *

Theo appeared at seven-thirty with his coat already on, which was a message in itself. Theo Voss communicated primarily through wardrobe and logistics; Marguerite had managed him as much as he'd managed her for eleven years, and she knew his vocabulary cold. Coat on at seven-thirty meant *we leave by nine*. Coat on, plus the folder of gala paperwork squared against his chest, meant *the world has resumed and we must return to it immediately*. Coat on, plus the way his eyes kept flicking toward the kitchen door every four seconds—

That meant something else entirely.

"The plow cleared Route 9," he announced to the room at large. "The county says the pass is open by noon. Marguerite, we should aim for the city by dark if we want you rested for Thursday."

"Of course," Marguerite said.

"We should pack."

"We should," she agreed.

Neither of them moved. Dominic, at the counter, kneaded dough with the serene concentration of a man who had decided that if the other two wanted to have an entire conversation in subtext, they were welcome to it, and he would be here with the bread.

Theo looked at her. She looked at him. Fifteen years ago she had watched this man choose a career over the person he loved, and she had thought, even then, even in the middle of her own hunger for his chair—no, for *a* chair, any chair, the chair—that she was watching something shameful. She had told herself it was ambition. Everyone told themselves it was ambition. Ambition was the great absolution of their world; you could commit nearly any sin under its banner and be forgiven by morning.

But she had watched Theo these three days—watched him find the cello in the barn and carry it halfway to the kitchen door and then stand there like a man holding a live grenade; watched him sit up past midnight with old tour documents spread across the tasting room table, his face going grayer page by page; watched him look at Dominic's scarred hands and go white to the lips—and she had understood that what she'd witnessed fifteen years ago hadn't been ambition at all. It had been fear, wearing ambition's coat.

And fear, she had learned this week, was contagious. She had caught it herself, backstage, the week of the final concert. She had seen what she'd seen and said nothing, and the silence had fit so comfortably that she'd worn it for fifteen years.

Well. The roads were open. The coat was coming off.

"Theo," she said. "Could you give us a moment?"

He blinked. "We're on a schedule, Marguerite."

"We're on a schedule," she agreed pleasantly, "that will survive ten minutes. Go check whether the barn cello needs a case for travel. Or don't come back for ten minutes. Either activity suits me."

Something passed over his face—relief and dread in equal measure, which she was beginning to understand was simply his face now, whenever the truth was nearby. He went.

* * *

Dominic had put the loaves in the oven and was scrubbing the counter with the thoroughness of a man avoiding eye contact. Marguerite hopped up onto the stool at the end of the island, the one that had become hers over three days without anyone assigning it, and folded her hands.

"When I leave today," she said, "I'm going to call a journalist."

The scrubbing stopped.

"Not a tabloid. Elena Marsh, at the *Tribune*. She's done investigative work on orchestral labor practices; she's fair; she's careful. And I'm going to tell her about the week of your final concert. About what I saw. About who I saw."

Dominic set down the sponge with exaggerated care, the way he did everything now, the way she supposed a man learned to do everything when his hands had betrayed him once and might again. "Marguerite."

"Let me finish. Please. You've let me finish things this week; extend the courtesy to its natural conclusion." She took a breath. The oven ticked as it heated. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked, thawing. "You know what I know now. You know someone tampered with your medication. You know I saw the person doing it, and you know why I stayed silent—the chair was open, my career was right there, and I was twenty-nine years old and hungry in a way I didn't even recognize as hunger. I told myself it was probably nothing. A mix-up. People mislabel medications. I told myself that story so many times I almost believed it, and then I believed it, and then I built a life on top of it the way you build on a fault line, and the whole time, the ground was exactly as rotten as I'd always known."

"Why are you telling me this now? We talked about this. Two nights ago. You told me everything—or you told me everything except the name."

"And I'm not going to say the name to you." She held up a hand before he could speak. "Not because I'm protecting him. Because it shouldn't come from me sideways, in a kitchen, in anger. It should come from testimony. From documents. From Theo's ledger and my affidavit and whatever lawyers can assemble. It should come in a form that can't be dismissed as a rival's spite, because God knows the world will try. They'll say I wanted your chair. They'll say I wanted your ruin. Both true, once, and both irrelevant, and both reasons the story has to be armored in fact before it goes anywhere near the public."

Dominic was very still. Outside the window, the snow on the vine rows was slumping, settling, giving up its grip on the wires. "So you're going to burn your own career down. Over something that wasn't your fault."

"It was my fault. That's rather the point. Not the sabotage—his crime, his alone—but the silence. The silence was mine, all fifteen years of it, and I've carried it like a stone in my shoe, and I am so tired, Dominic. I am fifty-one years old and I play beautifully and I am so tired." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it, because she had decided somewhere around four this morning, listening to the eaves drip, that she was done performing composure in this house. This house had seen her drunk on bad red wine confessing complicity; it had seen her shape lopsided loaves; it had seen her hands shake. Let it see the rest.

"So here is what I'm asking," she said. "Not for forgiveness. For company. I will testify. I will go public. I will burn it all down—the gala, the conductor's patronage, the recording contract, all of it, gladly, because none of it was ever built on anything solid. But I will not do it alone, and I will not do it in a way that leaves you here, safe and silent, while I take the fire. Because I know you, Dominic Montrose. Three days of bread and blizzards, and I know you. You'll tell me to stay out of it. You'll tell me your small life is enough, that the kitchen is enough, that you never want the stage again—and maybe that's even true, maybe the stage means nothing to you now. But the *lie* isn't nothing. The lie is why you're here instead of wherever you'd have gone freely. The lie made this refuge necessary. And I refuse—I refuse—to let the truth die in this house just because the roads opened."

The oven ticked. The bread began, faintly, to smell like itself.

Dominic looked at her for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet and rough. "Do you know what I was doing when you came to scout this place? Before the storm?"

"Hiding in the kitchen. Theo told me. He thought it was funny."

"He would." A ghost of a smile. "I hid because I recognized your walk from thirty feet away, through a window, in the dark. Fifteen years, and I still knew your walk. And my first feeling wasn't anger. It was—" He shook his head. "It doesn't matter what it was. What matters is that I've spent three days learning that the woman I hated for half my life is braver than I am. That's a hard thing to learn about an enemy. It's an impossible thing to forgive yourself for."

"I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm asking for an ally."

"And if I say no? If I ask you to leave it buried?" He turned to face her fully, and she saw how much it cost him, the question. "If I tell you I don't want the wreckage? Marguerite, he's dying. The man is *dying*, and beloved, and there are students and foundations and a whole edifice, and you'd torch it, and for what? I'm not coming back. My hands—" He lifted them, palms out, the scars pale in the morning light. "These don't do what they used to. Vindication doesn't change the instrument."

"No," she said. "But it changes the story. And you of all people know that the story is what killed you. Not the tremor. The tremor was survivable—people play with worse. What killed you was the world deciding the tremor meant cowardice, weakness, fraud. The lie didn't just take your career, Dominic. It took your *explanation*. You've lived fifteen years inside a story written by the man who broke you, and I watched you do it, and I helped, and I am telling you that I cannot watch one more day of it." She slid off the stool. Her legs felt unsteady and her voice felt steady, and she'd take that trade. "So. Here is my offer, and it is not generous, and I don't pretend it is. I delay my departure. I miss Thursday's session—I'll reschedule, they'll survive, I'm the draw anyway. I stay until we've called Elena together, or until you've told me plainly, to my face, that you forbid it and mean it. And if you forbid it—if you truly choose the lie over the truth, knowing everything—I'll go, and I'll keep your secret, and I'll hate myself quietly for the rest of my life, which is apparently my signature move."

"That's a terrible offer."

"It's the only one I have."

From the hallway came the unmistakable sound of a man who had absolutely not gone to check on a cello case, attempting to become very interested in a wall.

Dominic closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked older and younger at once, the way people do when something heavy shifts. "The bread needs twelve more minutes," he said. "Sit down, Marguerite. If we're going to plan an arson, we're going to do it properly, with breakfast."

* * *

They worked through the morning the way conspirators do—quietly, in pieces, so that no single act felt irreversible until all of them were. Marguerite drafted the outline of her account on the back of a gala program, dating everything, pinning the week of the concert to rehearsal schedules she remembered with the pathological precision of a performer. Theo sat across the tasting room table with his ledger open and his pen moving, and twice Marguerite caught him looking at Dominic with an expression she refused to name, because naming it would require admitting she'd spent eleven years managing a man who was in love with someone else, and she had a reputation to maintain.

At noon the sun came out, stupidly cheerful, and the driveway emerged from the snow in glistening brown ribbons. Theo's car sat under a foot of white like a sleeping animal. Someone—it was Theo, she saw through the window, though he pretended otherwise—went out and swept it clear, and then stood beside it for a long moment, looking back at the farmhouse, before coming inside without having started the engine.

By two o'clock, the outline was three pages long. By three, Theo had located the shipping manifests he'd flagged weeks ago, the ones with the pharmacist's initials that matched nothing legitimate. By four, Marguerite had Elena Marsh's number written on her palm in pen, smudged but legible.

Nobody dialed it.

"There's no rush," Theo said, for perhaps the sixth time, in the tone of a man soothing horses.

"There's every rush," Marguerite said. "The conductor is dying, which means his allies are circling to protect the legacy, which means the window is now. Once he's dead, this becomes revisionist gossip. While he's alive, it's testimony." She looked at Dominic, who was staring at the three pages as though they might bite. "Tomorrow," she said gently. "We call tomorrow. Tonight we sleep, and tomorrow we call. Agreed?"

Dominic was quiet for so long that the clock in the hall ticked eleven times, audibly, like a witness.

"Agreed," he said at last. And then, quieter, almost to himself, looking out the window at the ruined, dripping, hopeful vines: "There's something I have to do first. Tonight. Before any of it."

"What?" Theo asked.

Dominic looked at the barn.

Marguerite followed his gaze, and understood, and felt something in her chest turn over—the same thing that had turned over when she'd first heard him play, twenty years ago, from the back of a rehearsal hall, before she'd known his name, before either of them had known what they'd cost each other.

"The cello," she said.

"The cello," Dominic agreed. "If I'm going to tell the truth about my hands, I should probably find out what they can still do."

Outside, the thaw ran loud off the eaves, and the road stood open, and nobody packed.
