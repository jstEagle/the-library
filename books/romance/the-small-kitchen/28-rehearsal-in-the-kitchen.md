## 28. Rehearsal in the Kitchen

### Dominic

The trick to feeding thirty people and then playing Bach was, apparently, the same as the trick to everything: you did it badly at first.

It was Marguerite who said that, on the first night we tried it, standing in my kitchen with her coat still on and a cello case at her feet like a dog she'd brought home without asking. She'd stayed past thaw, past the gala contract, past every reasonable excuse, renting the cottage by the road from Marta under the fiction of "overseeing venue logistics." Nobody believed the fiction. Marguerite least of all.

"You'll play terribly," she'd said, unzipping the case with the reverence other people reserved for reliquaries. "For a week. Maybe two. And then you'll play less terribly, and one day you'll look up and realize you're playing."

"I've been through this before," I told her, wiping down the board. "I know how it ends."

"No," she said, lifting the barn cello out of its crate—my cello, the one Theo had carried to my door and couldn't knock on, the one I'd finally let him carry all the way in. "You've been through how it *ended*. This is different. This time there's bread."

She wasn't wrong about the terrible part. The first night, after service, with the kitchen still smelling of braised short rib and the dish rack dripping, I put bow to string and produced something that made her flinch. Not dramatically. Marguerite Voss does not flinch dramatically; she flinches the way a tuning fork flinches, a small precise shudder of wrongness. But I saw it.

"That was," I said, "the opening of the First Suite."

"That," she said, "was a threat you made against the First Suite. It's considering its options."

I laughed. That surprised me more than anything—the laugh coming up out of me easy, in this kitchen where for fifteen years I had not so much as hummed while I worked. I had rules. No music in the kitchen. No humming, no radio with words, nothing that might remind my hands what they used to be. Marguerite had broken that rule the way she broke most of mine: cheerfully, and without asking permission, and then standing there holding the pieces until I took them back.

"Again," she said. "Slower. And stop apologizing to the instrument with your shoulders. It can't hear your guilt. It can only hear your arms."

* * *

### Marguerite

Here is what nobody tells you about teaching: it is humiliating in exactly the opposite direction from being taught. When you learn, you fail in public. When you teach, you discover in public how much of what you do is instinct you cannot name, and how much of your own technique is superstition wearing a lab coat.

Dominic Montrose's hands were a crime scene and a miracle in the same pair of wrists. When they seized—and they seized, the second night, halfway through the Courante, his fingers curling inward like leaves in frost—I watched him go somewhere else entirely. His face didn't crumple. It *closed*, the way a shop closes, shutters down, sign flipped, everyone inside pretending not to be home. Fifteen years of practice at that particular closing. He could do it between one note and the next.

"Stop," I said.

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you pretend your body is a stranger who wandered in off the street." I set my tea down. We had a system now: he played, I drank tea I didn't want, and when his hands went I talked him back down instead of letting him white-knuckle through it, which was the old way, the way that had built the wall between his mind and his hands so high neither of them could see each other anymore. "Put the bow down. Shake them out. Tell me where it starts."

"It starts—" He flexed his fingers, scowling at them like employees who'd called in sick again. "Left hand. Fourth finger. It's always the fourth finger."

"Because you don't trust it. You trust the first three because they're strong. The fourth is the little one, the weak one, the one that has to ask for help from the wrist. You've been protecting yourself from it for fifteen years, and it's gotten lonely and dramatic."

"That is the most unhinged diagnosis I've ever heard."

"I'm a cellist, not a doctor. Unhinged diagnoses are our whole tradition." I came around the counter—he insisted on keeping the kitchen island between us during lessons, some last fortification—and took his left hand in both of mine. His fingers were warm from the oven and trembling faintly, and scarred across the knuckles from years of knife work, cook's scars, honest ones. "Feel that? No judgment. Just weight. Your fourth finger weighs exactly as much as the others. It always did. The only thing that ever failed you wasn't in your hands at all, and we both know it now, and your hands are allowed to know it too."

He looked at me then—not the wary look, not the rival look, but the look of a man being handed something fragile and trying to figure out if it's a gift or a trap. I knew that look. I'd worn it in mirrors for fifteen years.

"Play the Courante again," I said, releasing him. "From the fourth bar. Just four bars. If the finger seizes, it seizes, and we have wine."

It seized. We had wine. But the third time, it held for six bars, and the sixth time it held for twelve, and by the end of the week Dominic played the entire Courante in my kitchen while a pot of stock simmered behind him, and when he finished he stood very still, listening to the last of the sound come back off the tile walls, and said, quietly, "It sounded like a kitchen."

"It sounded like a cello in a kitchen."

"No." He shook his head, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen since the old footage, the young footage, the competitions. Wonder. "It sounded like the room was part of the music. Concert halls never did that. They just took the sound and kept it. This gives it back."

That was the night I stopped thinking of myself as his coach and started thinking of myself as whatever the word is for two people rebuilding the same stolen thing from opposite ends.

* * *

### Dominic

We developed a liturgy. Service ended at nine. Dishes by ten. She arrived at half past with her case and her thermos and her opinions, and we worked until midnight or until my hands quit, whichever came first, and some nights both.

Hour by hour, that's how she put it once, and she was right—it wasn't practice the way I'd practiced at Juilliard, the monastic eight-hour siege. It was smaller and stranger than that. An hour of scales while the stock reduced. Twenty minutes of shifting exercises while the bread proofed, because she claimed—correctly, damn her—that watching the dough rise calmed my breathing and calm breathing calmed my fourth finger. I learned the Sarabande of the Fifth Suite standing at my own counter, and when I play it now I still smell rosemary.

"You're associating the music with safety," she said, when I pointed this out. "Good. Music should live somewhere. Yours lived in terror for fifteen years. Evict it."

Some nights were bad. There was an evening in late April—I remember the rain starting, that soft early rain that means the vines are waking—when I got through the Gigue of the First Suite clean, actually clean, and instead of joy I felt a grief so sudden and total I had to put the cello down and grip the counter. Because the Gigue was the piece. The encore, fifteen years ago, the one I never got to play, the one the tremor stole mid-phrase in front of eleven hundred people. My body remembered finishing it before my mind caught up, and then it remembered everything else.

Marguerite didn't say anything clever. She turned off the burner under the stock that didn't need turning off, an act of pure ceremony, and sat down on the flour bin, and waited. Rain on the roof. The cello ticking softly as it cooled.

"I keep waiting for it to be taken back," I finally said. "The good nights. Like someone's going to audit the books and find out I haven't suffered enough to deserve them."

"The person who would take them back is dead," she said. "Or dying. Which is nearly the same thing, in his case." She meant Aurel Kovacs, though she wouldn't say the name either; we had developed a whole grammar of avoidance around him, *he* and *him* and *the man in June*, because the gala was coming and with it the conductor himself, standing in this tasting room, and none of us had figured out yet what we'd do when he walked in. "And even if he weren't—you've got witnesses now. Me. Theo. A vineyard full of people who've watched you rebuild this note by note. Nobody audits a harvest, Dominic. You either brought it in or you didn't."

"And did I?"

She looked at the cello, leaning against my counter next to the olive oil like it had always lived there. "You're looking at the ledger."

* * *

### Marguerite

I will admit something I will deny under oath: I look forward to these nights more than I have looked forward to anything in my career. Galas, premieres, the Kennedy Center—all of it applause-shaped and hollow at the center. But this—a dented kitchen, bad coffee, a man rebuilding his hands one hour at a time while I drink tea I don't want—this has a center.

Tonight I brought the metronome, which he hates.

"Absolutely not."

"It's a tool, not a punishment."

"It's a small mechanical conscience, and it ticks at me."

"Then let it tick. Tempo is the first thing fear eats." I set it on the counter beside the salt cellar, which struck me as symbolically correct. "You rush. Every time the passage gets hard, you run from it. Sixteen-year-olds rush. Masters breathe. Pick one."

"I pick the wine."

"Later. Adagio movement first. Metronome at fifty-four." I clicked it on, and he gave me a look of profound betrayal, and began.

And here is the thing I could never have learned on any stage in the world: watching him work was teaching me my own instrument over again. I had spent thirty years treating difficulty as an enemy to be conquered before it conquered me—faster, harder, flawless, the Kovacs method, the whole glacial edifice of perfection he'd built us inside of. Dominic couldn't do flawless. Flawless was gone; the seizure had burned that bridge. So he did something else instead. When a passage fought him, he slowed down and *listened to it*, asked it questions, found the shape of it the way he found the shape of a sauce—by attention, by patience, by treating the thing in front of him as an ingredient rather than an obstacle.

"Stop," I said, halfway through the evening. "Do that again."

"Do what?"

"That. What you just did with the shift in bar nine. You missed it and then you—sang through the miss. Made it part of the line."

He shrugged, embarrassed. "In the kitchen we call that fixing it in the pan. You burn the garlic, you don't start the dish over, you adjust. Deglaze."

"Deglaze," I repeated. Thirty years of conservatory training, and the man deglazed his way through Bach. "Say that again slower. I'm writing it down."

"You are not writing it down."

"I am absolutely—" I reached for my bag, and he threw a dish towel at me, and I laughed, actually laughed, in a kitchen in the Hudson Valley at eleven at night, and somewhere in Budapest an old man was dying with our careers in his filing cabinet, and for one whole minute I forgot to be afraid of him.

Then the phone buzzed on the counter. Theo. Dominic glanced at it, and I watched the evening change temperature.

"You should answer," I said.

"It's late."

"He doesn't call late unless it matters."

Dominic picked it up. Listened. I watched his face do three things in five seconds—concern, then confusion, then a stillness I recognized, because I'd worn it myself the night I decided to tell him the truth about the concert.

"Yes," he said into the phone. "Yes, read it to me."

* * *

### Dominic

Theo's voice on the line was careful in the way he was careful when the news was bad and he hadn't finished deciding how bad.

"There's a letter," he said. "Came to the office this afternoon. Handwritten. From Kovacs."

Marguerite went very still by the flour bin. Outside, the April rain kept up its soft work on the vines, indifferent, patient, the way spring is.

"He's heard," Theo said. "About Marguerite's interview, about the testimony, all of it. And he writes—" a pause, paper rustling, and I could see him in his office in the city, glasses pushed up, reading aloud the way he read everything, like each word might be wired. "He writes that he wants to visit. Before the gala. Privately. He says—" Theo's breath caught, just barely. "He says he wants to make things right. Those are his words. *Make things right.*"

In the kitchen, the stock ticked as it cooled. The metronome sat silent beside the salt. Marguerite's eyes found mine across the counter, and in them I saw what I felt in my own chest: that no letter from Aurel Kovacs had ever in sixty years contained a generous impulse that wasn't also a hook.

"When?" I asked.

"June," Theo said. "Two weeks before the gala. He says he's coming to the vineyard."

I looked down at my hands—steady tonight, steady for hours, steady through a whole Gigue—and thought about everything they'd learned in this room, and everything they were about to be tested against.

"Tell him the kitchen's open," I said.
