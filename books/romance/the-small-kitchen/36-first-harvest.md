## 36. First Harvest

The alarm went off at four-thirty, and for the first time in fifteen years, Dominic woke before it.

He lay in the dark of his little room above the kitchen and listened to the vineyard breathe. There was no wind. The frost had come and gone weeks ago, and what remained was that particular September stillness, the kind that felt held, like a note a singer was saving. Somewhere down the slope, a tractor ticked as its engine cooled from yesterday's test run. And beneath all of it—so faint he might have imagined it—the smell of grapes coming ripe, which was less a smell than a promise: sugar waiting in the dark, patient, ready.

He got up, dressed in the dark out of habit, and padded down the stairs into his kitchen.

His kitchen. He still caught himself saying it that way sometimes, with a small private astonishment, the way you might say *my house* about a place you'd rented for years before realizing you'd never once thought of leaving. The room was exactly as he'd left it last night: dough proofing under cloth on the counter, the big stockpot hanging from its hook, Marguerite's postcard from Lyon pinned to the corkboard between the crew schedule and a child's crayon drawing of a grape cluster that someone's daughter had left as payment for a cinnamon roll. The cello stood by the window now, not crated, not hidden—standing upright in its stand like a guest who had finally been offered a chair.

Dominic filled the kettle, lit the burner, and stood with both hands wrapped around the counter's edge while the blue flame caught.

His hands were steady. That was the miracle he no longer bothered to call a miracle. They trembled some mornings, a fine shiver like water deciding whether to boil, and then they settled. He had learned—Marguerite had taught him, hour after hour in this kitchen after service, her voice patient and merciless—that the trembling was not the enemy. The enemy was the flinch. The flinch was what had cost him fifteen years. You could play through a tremor; you could not play through terror of the tremor.

"Four-thirty," he said aloud to the empty room, and heard how pleased he sounded, and laughed at himself for it.

There was bread to bake—six loaves, because harvest day meant forty people who would work from first light until their backs gave out, and forty people deserved real food—and there were eggs to scramble in batches, and there was the sausage he'd started curing three weeks ago specifically against this morning, and there was coffee, gallons of it, because Lena the vineyard owner believed with religious fervor that no one should be asked to distinguish Pinot Noir from Syrah before caffeine.

He tied on his apron. He turned the dough out onto the floured wood. And he began.

* * *

By five-forty the kitchen smelled like the inside of a promise, and the first headlights came swinging up the gravel drive.

They arrived in ones and twos at first—the early crew, the faithful, the ones who'd been with Vireo since the years when "vineyard" was a generous word for "hillside with ambitions." Old Marta, seventy-one and faster on a picking row than anyone half her age, came in stamping the dew off her boots and announced, "I smelled your bread from the road, Dominic, don't insult us both by pretending that's an accident."

"It's entirely strategic," Dominic said, handing her coffee. "Bread as morale warfare."

"And the sausage? Tell me the sausage is happening."

"The sausage is happening."

Marta closed her eyes briefly, as if in prayer. "Then we will bring in every grape on this hill if I have to carry them in my teeth."

The rest came in a flood after that—the seasonal crew, the neighbors who showed up every year for the free labor lunch and stayed for the feeling of it, the two teenagers from down the valley who mostly carried crates and flirted with each other and were, everyone agreed, better entertainment than useful. By six-fifteen there were thirty-eight people crammed into and around the small kitchen, and Dominic moved through them like a conductor—which was a thought that would have gutted him once, and now just made him smile into the steam.

Scrambled eggs in shifts. Sausage sliced off the curing rack while people actually applauded, which embarrassed him so thoroughly he threatened to withhold the second batch. Bread torn, not cut, because tearing was honest. Coffee refilled without being asked. Lena stood on a chair at six-thirty and gave the speech she gave every year—short, because she was a woman who respected hunger—"The fruit is ready. It waited for us. Let's go get it before it changes its mind," and the crowd roared, and the door banged open, and the whole bright noisy tide of them poured out toward the rows where the grapes hung heavy and silvered with dawn.

And then the kitchen went quiet, and Dominic stood in the sudden stillness with a dish towel over his shoulder and realized he was grinning like a fool at nobody.

"You look ridiculous," said Marguerite from the doorway.

She was dressed for the field—boots, borrowed gloves, her hair shoved under a cap that said VIREO in faded letters—and she looked, Dominic thought, like a woman who had spent her life on concert stages deciding, deliberately, to be gloriously wrong-dressed. She had a crate already slung on one hip.

"You're supposed to be resting your hands," he said. "You have the gala in—"

"In ten days, yes, and my hands are fine, and if I spend one more morning doing scales while other people pick grapes I'm going to lose my mind. Move. I want a row."

"Marta will destroy you."

"Marta can try."

She took her coffee, drained half of it standing up, and paused at the door. For a moment neither of them said anything. Fifteen months ago she had walked into his tasting room as a stranger scouting a venue, and he had hidden in this kitchen with his heart hammering like a boy's. Now she stood in his doorway in a borrowed cap, and the thing between them was not friendship exactly—it was something older and stranger than that, forged in confession and bread dough and four A.M. conversations—but it was solid, and it was theirs, and neither of them wasted words decorating it.

"Eight bars," she said. "That's all I'm asking for tonight. You promised."

"I know what I promised."

"Then I'll be back before dark, and you'll owe me eight bars, and if they're good I'll consider nine." She banged out the door after the crew, and Dominic shook his head, and got back to work.

* * *

Theo appeared at seven, which meant he had driven through the dark from the city, which meant he had probably slept three hours, which meant—as far as Dominic could determine—that Theo had decided sleep was a luxury for people with less to do.

He came in carrying two more crates of supplies for the festival planning and a paper bag that he set on the counter with the exaggerated casualness of a man delivering contraband.

"Lemon tarts," he said. "From that place in Rhinebeck. For the afternoon break. Don't make it a thing."

"I'm making it a thing."

"Don't."

"They're going to sing your praises, Theo. Marta alone—"

"Dominic."

"—is going to propose marriage—"

"*Dominic.*"

He was laughing. Dominic realized he was laughing too, easily, the way you laugh with someone whose moods you've learned to read across a crowded room. Fifteen months ago this man had stood in his kitchen doorway holding a cello case he couldn't make himself put down, white-faced, unable to knock. Now he set down his keys next to the salt jar like he knew where it lived. Because he did know where it lived. That was the part that undid Dominic a little, when he let himself think about it: not the grand gestures, but the accumulated evidence. The man knew where the salt lived. The man had opinions about where the cast-iron should hang. The man had, three weeks ago, quietly fixed the screen door and then pretended for eleven days that he hadn't.

"Coffee?" Dominic asked.

"Please. Black. I've been up since four driving past fields thinking very serious thoughts about porta-john placement."

"That's the romance they warned me about."

Theo took the mug and leaned against the counter beside him, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and for a moment they just watched the light come up over the vines through the window. Down the rows, tiny figures moved with their shears and crates, and someone—Marta, unmistakably—was already shouting instructions at the teenagers.

"They look good," Theo said. "The vines. After the ice."

"Lena pruned them hard. I thought she'd killed half of them." Dominic remembered that February morning, the two of them walking the frozen rows, her shears flashing, cutting away everything that would never bear fruit so the rest could live. He'd hated it. Every cut had felt like a small violence. "She told me that's the whole job, really. Deciding what doesn't get to stay."

Theo was quiet a moment. When he spoke, his voice had that careful quality it got when he was approaching something sideways. "You ever think about how much of your life was spent growing things that couldn't bear?"

Dominic turned the dish towel over in his hands. "Every day for about a year," he said. "Less lately."

"And now?"

"Now I think—" He stopped. Down the hill, Marguerite had reached her row, and even from here he could see her attacking it with the competitive fury of a woman who intended to beat a seventy-one-year-old at manual labor. He smiled. "Now I think the pruning worked. On the vines. On me. Ask me again after tonight."

"After tonight," Theo repeated softly, and didn't push, and that—his willingness to stand beside a silence instead of filling it—was one of the thousand small reasons Dominic had already made his decision, though he hadn't told him yet. Tonight. Eight bars, or maybe nine. Maybe, if his hands held and his nerve held, the whole piece. The program was printed; Theo had shown him the proof two days ago with the final line reading *Cello Solo — Performer to Be Announced*, and had looked at him with such naked hope and such disciplined restraint that Dominic had nearly told him right there over the proofs.

Tonight, he'd told himself. Let them bring in the harvest first. Some things you earned in order.

"Eat something," he said instead, and pushed a heel of warm bread into Theo's hands, and Theo ate it standing up, looking out the window at the rows, and if his free hand found Dominic's elbow and rested there—briefly, lightly, like a man testing whether a bridge would hold—neither of them mentioned it, and neither of them moved away.

* * *

The morning became the kind of morning you don't narrate so much as surrender to.

Dominic cooked in waves. Mid-morning: cider and the lemon tarts, carried out to the end of the rows on a wagon, greeted with a cheer that startled crows out of the tree line. Noon: the full meal—roast chickens he'd started at five, potato salad, greens from the garden, six loaves of bread demolished down to crumbs, and a barrel of the previous year's rosé that Lena broached with a mallet to general delight. People sat on tailgates and overturned crates in the dappled shade between the rows, and Marta told the story of the hailstorm of '09 for the benefit of the teenagers, improving it slightly each year, and the two young lovers fell asleep sitting up against each other and were photographed mercilessly.

Dominic ate last, standing, as was his custom, and found Marguerite beside him with a plate piled like she'd earned it.

"Eleven crates," she announced. "Marta did thirteen, but she cheated. She started early."

"She started early because she's smarter than you."

"She started early because she has no inner monologue telling her to pace herself." Marguerite tore a chicken leg in half with her teeth and gestured with it at the hillside. "Look at it, though."

He looked. The rows ran down the slope in long green lines, and where the crew had passed, the vines stood lighter, relieved, their fruit gone into lugs and bins stacked at the row ends in purple-dark abundance. The air smelled of crushed skins and sun-warm leaves and, faintly, of someone's cigarette smuggled far from the equipment. It was not beautiful in any way a photograph would capture. It was beautiful the way work is beautiful: evidence of bodies and hours agreeing on something together.

"Fifteen years ago," Marguerite said, quieter, "I played Carnegie Hall. Sold out. Standing ovation." She chewed, considered. "I don't remember a single minute of it. Not one. I remember I was terrified the whole time and my agent got drunk and I found out later the review called me 'flawless,' which I remember being furious about, because flawless is what you say about a thing that didn't move you." She shrugged. "This I'll remember. The tarts. Marta cheating. That idiot asleep on his boyfriend's shoulder. Go figure."

Dominic said nothing, because there was nothing to add, and because she hadn't said it for him anyway. She'd said it the way people say things in the middle of harvest—out loud, to the air, to whoever's standing there—because the day is too full to hold it all inside.

* * *

The afternoon went long and golden. The last rows came in as the light went low and amber, and the whole crew converged on the crush pad, where the bins were weighed—cheers at each number, groans at the total, which was, Lena announced with tears she didn't bother hiding, the largest harvest in Vireo's history, "which, granted, is a low bar, but we cleared it *magnificently*."

Someone had brought a fiddle. This was, Dominic suspected, Marta's doing, and Marta's nephew's doing, and within twenty minutes there was music on the crush pad—fiddle, a guitar produced from a truck cab, someone drumming on an empty bin—and people dancing badly and joyfully in their boots among the grape bins while the sky went pink behind the ridge.

Dominic stood at the edge of it with Theo, both of them holding cups of the new-press juice, watching.

"You could be down there," Theo said. "They'd love it. One song."

"My dancing frightens livestock."

"One song, Dominic."

"Ask me again after tonight."

Theo went still, the way he always did when that word surfaced between them. *Tonight.* He didn't ask. He had become, over these months, extraordinarily good at not asking—had learned it, Dominic suspected, the hard way, in a green room fifteen years ago, where asking the wrong question had cost him everything. Instead he just nodded, and clinked his cup against Dominic's, and said, "After tonight, then," and drank his grape juice like it was champagne.

Across the pad, Marguerite was teaching the teenagers to waltz to a fiddle tune that was in no way a waltz, correcting their elbows with the same ferocity she brought to bowing technique, laughing—actually laughing—in a way Dominic had never once seen her laugh on a stage. Marta caught Dominic's eye across the crowd and raised her cup to him, and mouthed something he chose to interpret as *good bread* rather than what it actually was, which was, judging by her expression, *kiss him, you fool.*

The sun went down. The lanterns came on along the crush pad, the same style of lanterns that had lit his kitchen during the whiteout, and the party softened into singing, and the singing softened into people drifting home in ones and twos with leftovers wrapped in foil.

And Dominic slipped away, up the path to the barn, where the cello waited in the lamplight.

* * *

He carried it back through the dark to the kitchen, and set it by the window, and stood there a long moment with his hand flat on the warm varnish.

Down the hill, the last notes of the fiddle faded. In the oven, tomorrow's bread rose under cloth. On the counter, Theo's festival programs sat in a neat stack—he'd left them there, one copy face-up on top, and Dominic picked it up and read the final line again by lantern light:

*Cello Solo — Performer to Be Announced.*

Through the window, he saw Theo coming up the path from the crush pad, lantern swinging, tired and happy, and Marguerite a few steps behind him arguing cheerfully about whether the encore should exist.

Dominic set the program down. He picked up the bow.

His hands, when he lifted them, were trembling—just barely, just the old familiar shiver, water deciding whether to boil.

He smiled, and drew the first note out into the quiet kitchen, steady as bread rising, and began to play.
