## 37. The Festival Program

Theo Voss had managed crises on four continents. He had talked a soprano out of jumping off a hotel balcony in Vienna (metaphorically; she was actually just standing on the railing to see the snow). He had smoothed over a double-booking that put a string quartet and a death-metal band in the same festival tent in Oslo. He had once spent six hours convincing a sponsor that "artistic differences" did not require a press release.

None of it had prepared him for printing two hundred programs and finding a blank line where the performer's name should be.

It was the morning of the harvest festival, and the kitchen of Vireo Vineyard smelled like a small miracle: bread, apples, brown butter, woodsmoke from the oven Dominic had insisted on firing at five a.m. Theo stood at the long table with a stack of freshly printed programs, a cup of coffee going cold beside him, and a feeling in his chest like an elevator with a fraying cable.

The program itself was beautiful. Marguerite had insisted on letterpress, and the vineyard owner's niece ran a print shop in Cold Spring, so the thing looked like an heirloom. *First Harvest Festival, Vireo Vineyard.* A list of events: the grape-stomp demonstration (Dominic had argued against it; the crew had overruled him), the children's pie contest, the tasting flights, the lantern walk at dusk. And then, at the bottom, set apart in italics:

*Closing piece: Cello solo. Performer unannounced.*

"Unannounced," Theo said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud was how he kept from screaming. "Unannounced is not a word you put in a program. Unannounced is what you say when the performer has *not yet agreed to perform*."

He flipped through the stack as if another copy might contain different information. It did not. Two hundred copies. All identical. All ending in a dare.

He knew whose handwriting it was. That was the worst part. The program had gone through three drafts, all of them Theo's, all of them sent to Dominic for approval, and all three times the final line had come back with the performer listed as *TBD*. Theo had assumed TBD meant TBD. Apparently TBD meant *trust me*, which was a phrase Dominic Montrose used approximately once per decade, and never about anything with this many witnesses.

Theo put down the programs. Picked them up again. Put them down.

"You're going to wear a groove in the table," said Marisol, the vineyard's foreman, passing through with a crate of apples. She was sixty, built like a fence post, and had been watching Theo hover around this kitchen for ten months with the patient amusement of someone who had seen every kind of weather.

"The closing piece," Theo said. "There's no name."

"There's a cello."

"There's a cello and a blank line and two hundred people coming."

Marisol set down her crate and looked at him the way she looked at vines that needed pruning—assessing, unsentimental, fond. "You want my opinion?"

"No," Theo said, "but I'm going to get it anyway."

"He's ready." She picked the crate back up. "Been ready since August. You're the one still deciding whether to be scared on his behalf." She left before he could construct a rebuttal, which was her habit and, he suspected, her pleasure.

* * *

The panic, when he let himself feel it properly, arrived in its usual organized fashion. Theo's anxieties were always well-dressed. They wore blazers.

Item one: Dominic's hands. Theo had watched the rehearsals in the kitchen after service—had deliberately *not* watched, most nights, sitting in the tasting room with paperwork he wasn't reading while the sound came through the wall. Some nights the music was whole. Some nights it seized halfway through a phrase and stopped, and there would be silence, and then Marguerite's voice, low and even: *Again. From the top. Your hands aren't the problem, your breath is.* And then again. Theo had learned to tell, by the length of the silences, what kind of night it was.

Item two: the crowd. Two hundred people, outdoors, at dusk, including half the Hudson Valley wine community, three food writers who had RSVP'd because the vineyard's kitchen had developed a reputation, and—one item Theo had personally failed to prevent—a stringer from a national paper doing a feature on post-pandemic agritourism. Anyone with a phone could film it. Anything filmed could be found. If Dominic played and his hands betrayed him in front of two hundred witnesses—

But that was the old arithmetic, wasn't it. That was the conductor's arithmetic, the green-room arithmetic, the arithmetic that had cost fifteen years. *What if he fails.* As if failure were the unforgivable thing. As if the unforgivable thing weren't what you did to avoid the possibility of it.

Item three, the item under all the items: *he didn't tell me.* Ten months of rebuilding something between them—slow, careful, two steps forward and one step into the pantry—and Dominic had printed a program with a blank line and let Theo find out from a piece of paper. Which meant either Dominic didn't trust him, or Dominic trusted him completely and knew exactly what Theo would do with the information, which was try to manage it, and manage it, and manage it until there was nothing left to manage but the memory of it.

Theo recognized the shape of that fear because he'd been carrying its twin since March. Every time he offered evidence, lawyers, a comeback tour—the whole glittering machine of vindication—he'd watched Dominic flinch, and Theo had told himself the flinch was about the stage. Lately, in the four a.m. kitchen, over coffee strong enough to strip varnish, he'd begun to suspect the flinch was about him. About being managed. About loving a man whose profession was turning people into deliverables.

So. The program. The blank line. The leap.

Theo carried the stack of programs out to the tasting room, set them on the bar, and went looking for the man who'd written them.

* * *

He found Dominic behind the barn, which was where he went when the kitchen got ahead of him—which today it emphatically had, since the pie contest entries were due at noon and someone (Marisol) had signed Dominic up to judge them. He was splitting kindling, of all things, sleeves rolled, using the axe with the same economy he used a knife. Fifteen years of cooking had rebuilt those hands into something new. Theo watched them move—steady, certain, unremarkable—and felt the old grief and the newer hope sit down together inside him like two guests who'd finally agreed to share a table.

"You've seen the program," Dominic said, without turning around. He'd gotten frighteningly good at knowing Theo's footsteps.

"I've seen the program." Theo leaned against the barn doorframe. "I have notes."

"I bet you do."

"The closing piece lists a performer as unannounced. In print. In letterpress. There are two hundred copies."

"There are two hundred copies," Dominic agreed. He split another piece of kindling. The axe rang against the block, a clean bright note.

"Dominic." Theo made himself say it plainly, no manager in his voice, just the man. "Are you playing?"

The axe stopped. Dominic rested his hands on the handle and looked out past the vine rows, where the grapes hung heavy and dusty-blue in the October light, ready or nearly ready, the whole year's argument with the weather about to be settled.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the honest answer. I've been trying to decide all week, and I couldn't decide, and then I realized the deciding was the problem. So I printed the program without my name on it and decided I'd find out at seven o'clock tonight whether I'm the kind of man who walks out there."

"That's insane."

"It's the sanest thing I've done in fifteen years." He turned around then, and his face was calm in a way Theo had rarely seen on it—not the calm of hiding, which he knew intimately, had catalogued across a decade of tours, but the calm of a man standing on ground he'd chosen. "If I write my name down, I spend all day performing the decision instead of making it. I'll rehearse my courage until it's dead. This way—" he shrugged, "—seven o'clock comes, and either my hands take me out there or they don't. No performance. Just the truth, late."

"And if they don't?"

"Then two hundred people hear me say the piece is withdrawn, and we eat pie, and the world turns." He said it lightly, but Theo saw the cost of it in his eyes, the price tag he'd already accepted. "Theo. I need you to hear this next part."

"I'm listening."

"If I play, I don't want you backstage. I don't want you watching my hands. I don't want you counting the mistakes." Dominic set the axe against the barn wall and crossed to him, close enough that Theo could smell woodsmoke and bread and the rosemary soap he bought in bulk. "I know why you'd do it. I know you'd be keeping score to protect me. But I can't leap with you holding the back of my shirt."

Theo's throat worked. "That's—" He laughed, mostly air. "That's the most terrifying thing anyone's ever asked me. Do you understand what I do for a living? I hold shirtbacks professionally."

"I know." Dominic's mouth curved. "It's why you're good at everything except this."

"And what exactly am I supposed to do at seven o'clock? Stand in the crowd like a civilian?"

"Yes."

"Hold your hand?"

"After. Not before." Dominic said it simply, and it undid Theo more than any grand declaration could have—the *after* in it, the assumption of an after, the future tense arriving without fanfare after fifteen years of conditional clauses. "Before, I do it alone. After, I'd very much like to not be alone. Those are different jobs and you're only qualified for one of them right now."

Theo looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way you nod when you sign something.

"Okay," he said. "After."

* * *

The day went by the way festival days do—too fast and too slow at once, a blur of crates and extension cords and children with pie filling on their chins. Theo worked. It was what he had; he threw himself into logistics like a man bailing a boat, directing parking, troubleshooting the sound system, talking the health inspector into a generous interpretation of the word *tent*. Twice he caught himself reaching for his phone to check on Dominic and physically stopped his own hand, which Marguerite observed from across the yard with an expression of profound satisfaction.

She cornered him at four, behind the tasting room, where he was alphabetizing nothing in particular.

"You look terrible," she said. She was radiant, frankly unfair in a charcoal dress, her cello case open at her feet because she'd been asked to play something during the lantern walk and had agreed with suspicious ease.

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

"How is he?"

"Calm. Genuinely calm. It's horrifying."

Marguerite smiled and looked out at the vines. "You know what he told me last week, during rehearsal? His hands seized—it happens less now, but it happens—and he sat there breathing, waiting for it to pass, and then he said, *you know what's strange? Even when they stop, I don't want to stop.*" She turned back to Theo. "Fifteen years ago he wanted to stop more than anything in the world. Now the wanting has reversed. Whatever happens tonight, that already happened. The rest is just weather."

"When did you get wise?" Theo asked.

"I was always wise. You were both too busy being dramatic to notice." She snapped her case shut. "Go fold chairs or whatever it is you do to keep from feeling things."

* * *

At six-forty-five, the light went gold and then amber and then began its slow slide toward violet, and the crowd gathered in the clearing between the tasting room and the first vine rows—two hundred and change, folding chairs and blankets and kids on shoulders, cider in paper cups. Theo stood where Dominic had told him to stand: in the crowd, among them, no clipboard, no headset, no role. It felt like being asked to attend his own surgery as a spectator.

Marguerite opened the evening with the Bach—of course the Bach, the Sarabande, played simply, without ornament, her cold-hands problem solved by twenty minutes of Marisol's heating pad and sheer professional will. The crowd hushed. Somewhere near the back a baby fussed and was carried away, and the music held the space like water holds a stone.

Then she finished, bowed, and stepped to the microphone.

"For our closing piece," she said, "the program is honest. The performer is unannounced. He'll introduce himself—or he won't. Either way, we thank you for a beautiful first harvest."

And she walked off the little stage, past the front row, and stopped directly in front of Theo, and took both his hands in hers, and squeezed once, hard, and did not let go.

The silence stretched. Five seconds. Ten. Theo's heart performed maneuvers it had not attempted since his twenties. At the edge of the clearing, by the barn door, a figure stood in the last of the light with a cello case in one hand—Dominic, in his good jacket, the one with the frayed cuff he refused to replace. He didn't move. The crowd murmured, puzzled, beginning to shift.

Then Dominic set the case down, opened it, took out the cello, and walked.

He walked slowly, unhurried, the way he moved through his kitchen, and the crowd quieted by degrees as understanding rippled outward—he's the cook, someone whispered, the cook who feeds us, the cook from the vineyard dinners—and Theo realized, with a jolt, that these people knew him. Not as the disgraced prodigy, not as the cautionary tale. As the man whose bread they fought over. He was not walking onto a stage. He was standing up at his own table.

Dominic reached the microphone, adjusted it down, and spoke without preamble, his voice carrying clear across the clearing.

"My name is Dominic Montrose. Fifteen years ago I stopped playing. Tonight I'm starting again—not because my hands are cured, but because I finally understood that they were never the whole story." He paused, and found Theo's eyes across the crowd, and something passed between them that Theo would spend the rest of his life failing to describe. "This is a sarabande too. Different Bach. Same idea: slow, and honest, and impossible to rush."

He sat. He tuned. He placed his hands.

And Theo—who had promised not to watch, who had promised not to count, who had sworn off scorekeeping forever—watched, and counted, and could do nothing else, because the first note came out trembling, audibly trembling, and the whole clearing heard it, and Theo's stomach dropped through the earth—

—and then Dominic breathed, the way Marguerite had taught him, and the second note landed true, and the third, and by the fourth bar the tremor was gone, absorbed, folded into the vibrato like a river absorbing rain, and the music opened up over the vineyard the way the sky opens after a storm, and nobody in that field was thinking about hands anymore. They were just listening. Theo was just listening. Marguerite's grip on his hands tightened, and he felt wetness on his own face and did not bother to hide it, because everyone around him was doing the same, and the sun went down over Vireo Vineyard while a man who had spent fifteen years believing he was the thing his hands could do discovered, publicly, at last, that he had been wrong.

The piece ended. The silence afterward lasted three full seconds—an eternity, a gift—and then the applause came up like weather of its own, and Dominic stood, and bowed, and looked straight at Theo.

*After,* he'd said. *Not before.*

Theo let go of Marguerite's hands and started forward through the crowd—and stopped.

Because at the edge of the clearing, standing apart from the applause, hat pulled low despite the dark, a car parked crookedly behind him on the gravel, stood an old man leaning on a cane. Thin as a winter branch. Familiar in the way a scar is familiar.

Maestro Aurel Brandt had come to hear the ending himself.

Theo's blood went cold. Beside him, Marguerite had gone rigid, her hand flying to her mouth—and Dominic, still holding the cello, had seen him too.
