## 7. Eleven Vintages

The tractor woke him before the alarm did, which was how Tomás Ferreira knew it was going to be one of those days—the kind that began with something already broken and ended with him too tired to remember what he'd been angry about.

He lay for a moment in the grey light coming through the shutters, listening to the engine cough and catch, cough and catch, the way old men cough when they're deciding whether to keep going. The tractor had been his father's. So had the coughing rhythm of it, so had the stubbornness required to love a machine that wanted to die.

Eleven years ago this valley had smelled different to him. He couldn't say exactly how—sweeter, maybe, or sharper—but he knew that some part of every September since had been spent trying to smell that other September through this one, the way you press your ear to a wall to hear a conversation on the far side.

Her name still lived in his mouth like a stone at the bottom of a well. He didn't go down for it anymore. He just knew it was there.

* * *

By seven he was in the east rows, walking the Tinta Roriz with Marta's clipboard under his arm because she'd thrust it at him on her way to the office and said, *count the clusters, don't estimate, we can't afford estimates this year.* His sister had a gift for making hope sound like an accusation.

The vineyard was beautiful in the way that dying things are sometimes beautiful—late summer gold in the leaves, the fruit hanging heavy and dark as secrets, the whole slope of Quinta Ferreira running down toward the river like a green staircase someone had abandoned halfway to heaven. Tourists would have called it picturesque. Tomás saw the arithmetic underneath: two hundred fewer hectares than his grandfather had worked, forty fewer than his father, a mortgage held by a bank in Porto whose manager smiled too much on the phone, and a harvest that would either buy them another year or become the elegant obituary of five generations.

His father had nearly lost it all twice. Once in 2008, when the co-op collapsed and took half the valley's contracts with it, and once in 2013—the year Tomás preferred not to think about, though thinking about it had become, like everything else in his life, a routine. That was the year of the hospital in Viseu, the bad winter, the ledgers with their soft pencil columns, the year his father sat in the kitchen with his hands flat on the table like a man pinning down a map that kept trying to fly away, and said: *We'll sell the south field first. Then the cellar equipment. Then, if God is crueler than I think, the house.*

They hadn't sold the house. They'd sold the south field, most of the equipment, and eleven years of Tomás's life, which he'd handed over without ever quite signing anything. There was no contract for becoming the keeper of a family's decline. You just woke up one morning and the keys were in your pocket and your father's voice was in your head saying *check the hydrometer* and you were thirty-one and then thirty-five and then thirty-eight, and the girl who was supposed to have been beside you was a photograph in a drawer and a silence at the dinner table that no one ever mentioned because mentioning it would have made it a thing with edges instead of the fog it actually was.

Paloma.

Even now, in the privacy of his own skull, the name arrived whole, uninvited, wearing the same expression it had worn on the platform: apologetic, resolute, already leaving.

She'd been twenty-three. He'd been twenty-four. She'd come to Valdemora for a summer that turned into two, working at the pension by the station, writing things in notebooks she wouldn't let him read, laughing at his terrible Portuguese jokes in a way that made him funnier than he was. Her Portuguese had been careful and slightly formal, like a dress worn for the first time; her silences had been fluent. He'd loved both languages of her.

And they'd made the rule. Of course they'd made the rule—it had seemed romantic then, the kind of thing people in songs did. The last train of the day left Valdemora at 21:47, rattling north toward the junction at Pampilhosa, and whoever was still standing on the platform after it passed had chosen, deliberately, irrevocably, to stay. To stay for good. For the vines, for the mud, for the smallness, for each other. It had been his rule, mostly—he'd grown up inside the romance of the place and didn't know yet how heavy its soil could be—and she had agreed to it with such seriousness that he'd believed agreement was the same as intention.

On the last night of that August, she'd kissed him at the gate and said she needed to think, and he'd walked her partway to the station because that was what you did, and at the corner where the road bent she'd told him not to come any further, and he'd obeyed her, because love at twenty-four is largely obedience dressed up as trust.

The train left at 21:47. He knew because he'd checked the clock in the kitchen, and then again, and then gone out to the gate anyway, telling himself he wasn't checking whether she'd stayed, only getting air. The road to the station was dark. He hadn't gone. He'd told himself that going would be pressure, that if she chose to stay she should do it without him watching.

In the morning she was gone. A note under the door of the pension, delivered to him secondhand by Senhora Alves, who had the tact of a fire alarm: *I'm sorry. I'll explain someday.* No address. No explanation came. Someday turned out to be a country with no train service.

He'd spent the first year furious. The second year writing letters he burned. The third year pretending the fury was finished. By the fifth year he'd achieved the great adult achievement of turning grief into routine: he no longer looked up when the 21:47 passed the crossing and sounded its horn over the valley; he no longer flinched at the word Lisbon; he could even hear fado in a restaurant without leaving the table, though he still chose the seat facing away from the door.

That was the trick nobody warned you about. Grief doesn't leave. It just learns your schedule.

* * *

At noon he found Marta in the office, hunched over the laptop with the bank's spreadsheet open and her face arranged into the particular blankness she wore when the numbers were bad.

"Well?" he asked.

"We'll make it," she said, which from Marta meant *we won't, but I've found a way to delay finding out.*

"Marta."

"The Vinho Verde contract renewed at the same rate, which is to say an insulting rate. The tasting room made less than last year. And the insurance—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Their father had died believing Marta was the practical one and Tomás the dreamer, and she had spent nine years defending that classification like a border she'd drawn herself. "The insurance premium went up. Again."

Tomás sat down on the stool by the filing cabinet, the one with the wobbly leg that had been wobbling since 2009, and looked at the wall where their grandfather's photograph hung above the certificates nobody read anymore. In the photo, Avô Duarte stood in front of the cellar door with his arms crossed, squinting against the sun, a man so certain of the future he hadn't bothered to smile in advance of it.

"Did you call back about the agronomist?" Marta asked.

"I called. He comes Thursday." Tomás rubbed his eyes. "Marta, do you ever think about 2013?"

Her hands went still on the keyboard. It was a small stillness, but he'd known her forty years and small stillnesses were her native language.

"What about it?"

"Everything about it. The hospital. Dad. The ledgers." He heard himself going somewhere he hadn't planned to go, the way water finds a crack. "It's strange. Lately I keep—there's something I can't place. About that autumn."

"Autumns are all the same here," Marta said lightly. Too lightly. "Grapes, rain, debt. Repeat until dead."

But Tomás wasn't listening anymore, because the crack had opened and something was coming through it.

An envelope.

* * *

It surfaced the way those things do—not as a picture but as a sensation first: the feel of paper between fingers, the specific weight of a letter that has traveled. He was standing in the doorway of the old sorting room off the kitchen, the room where the mail used to pile up during the worst months, when their father was in Viseu and the household ran on Marta's schedules and everyone's handwriting blurred together from exhaustion.

He remembered the table in that room. Remembered the stacks of bills, the co-op circulars, the get-well cards from cousins. And underneath them, or among them, or passing through them—

An envelope. White. Foreign stamps. His name on it in handwriting he knew better than his own, because he'd watched it form across a café table for two summers, the little upward flick she gave to her capitals, the way her *r*s leaned forward like they were in a hurry to reach the next letter.

He had never received that letter. He was sure of it the way you're sure of a load-bearing wall. He'd have remembered. A letter from Paloma, after she left, after the note that explained nothing—he'd have remembered it the way you remember a bone breaking.

And yet.

The memory had texture. Not a dream's texture—dreams are smooth, they slide—but real texture: the envelope lying on the sorting-room table, face-up, near the lamp. Evening. The smell of the room, paper and dust and the ghost of the disinfectant they'd used all that winter. And himself seeing it, or almost seeing it, or—

Or being told about it. Was that it? Had someone said *there's a letter for you* and then—what? He reached for the next frame and found the hole where the next frame should be. The memory ended like a page torn out.

"Tomasz." Marta was standing behind him. When had she gotten up? "You've gone white. Sit down."

"There was a letter," he said. "That autumn. After she left. I think there was a letter."

The silence that followed lasted three seconds and contained an entire childhood.

"Don't be ridiculous," Marta said. "If there'd been a letter, you'd have gotten it."

"Then why am I holding a piece of it?" He turned around slowly. "Why do I remember the table, the lamp, the stamps? Marta, I remember the *stamps*. Two of them, overlapping, and one had a tower on it. Who remembers the stamps on a letter they never saw?"

"You're tired. Harvest is coming, you're not sleeping—"

"Marta." He said her name the way their father used to say it when the accounts didn't add up—not angry, just immovable. "Look at me and tell me."

And here was the thing he would turn over for nights afterward, the splinter he'd swallowed without noticing: she looked at him. She met his eyes the way she always did, steady, dry, unafraid—and then she blinked, once, a fraction late, and reached past him to switch off the sorting room's lamp, though it was noon and the lamp wasn't on.

"You're imagining things," she said. "There was no letter. And even if there had been, what difference would it make now? She's eleven years gone. She's somebody else's life entirely."

Somebody else's life. The phrase landed strangely, and he couldn't have said why until later, walking the rows in the long gold afternoon with the clusters counting themselves under his hands: because that morning, driving back from the co-op, he'd seen a woman at the bus stop by the Valdemora sign—a stranger, city shoes, city suitcase, standing very straight the way certain people stand when they're rehearsing—and something in his chest had lurched sideways, absurdly, uselessly, before his mind caught up and said *not her, obviously not her, she's eleven years and a whole ocean away.*

But the woman had turned, as the bus pulled away, and looked up the road toward the quinta, and even at that distance there had been something in the set of her shoulders that his body recognized before his brain objected. Something that stood like a person deciding.

He'd told himself it was nothing. He was good at telling himself things; he'd had eleven years of practice.

Now, walking the east rows with the sun going amber and the grapes hanging dark and patient, Tomás let himself hold both facts side by side without resolving them, the way you hold two stones in one hand: a letter he'd never received but somehow remembered, and a stranger at a bus stop with familiar shoulders.

The 21:47 sounded its horn at the crossing, far off, thinning into the hills.

For the first time in years, he listened until it was gone.
