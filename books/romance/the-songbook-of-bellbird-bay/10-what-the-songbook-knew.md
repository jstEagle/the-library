## 10. What the Songbook Knew

The rain had been coming down for three days straight, which meant the roofers wouldn't come, which meant the east wing stayed tarped, which meant Jonah Reyes was out of things to sand, paint, hammer, or argue about. He'd run out of arguments with Tamsin two days ago, actually—a personal record—and the silence she'd left behind in the ballroom had a texture to it. Not peace. More like a held breath.

So he did what he always did when he couldn't work with his hands. He worked with his eyes.

The songbook sat where Tamsin had left it, on the piano in the front parlor, weighted down with a piece of sea glass to keep the wind from turning its pages. Edith Marsh's handwriting covered every page in faded violet ink—the same looping, impatient script that appeared on every note she'd ever left taped to the inn's doors: *BACK IN TEN MINUTES. IF THE KETTLE WHISTLES, TAKE IT OFF. THIS MEANS YOU, DENNIS.*

Jonah had known Edith for exactly one summer of his boyhood and one week of his adulthood, and both times she had struck him as a woman who said exactly what she meant. Which was why the songbook bothered him. Because the songbook was full of things she hadn't said.

He'd flipped through it a dozen times since Tamsin found it. Setlists, mostly. Decades of them. *The Hollis-Brandt wedding, June 1974. The Okonkwo-Feeney wedding, September 1981. The Vasquez wedding, twice, because the first attempt got rained out and Edith declared the second one "the real one, weather's just rehearsal."* Each page had the processional, the recessional, the first dance, the toasts. Standard stuff. The kind of thing Jonah could have written in his sleep, and had, more or less, for the last fifteen years.

But there were annotations. Little marks in the margins he'd taken for doodles. A small circle beside certain songs. A dash beside others. A tiny star—no, not a star. He'd assumed a star. Tonight, with the rain drumming the tarps and nothing else to do, he looked properly, and saw it wasn't a star at all.

It was a bell. A tiny hand-drawn bell, five petals of ink flaring out from a clapper, drawn quick and sure, the way you draw something you've drawn a thousand times.

And beside some songs, instead of a bell, there was a bird. A small, fat bird with a beak open mid-song.

Jonah sat down on the piano bench and pulled the book into his lap.

* * *

It took him two hours and most of a pot of coffee to see the pattern, and when he saw it, he had to put the book down and walk to the window and stand there like a man who'd found a stranger's tooth in his soup.

The bells and the birds weren't decorations. They were a second setlist.

He went through the book again, page by page, decade by decade, writing down every song marked with a bird. *Moon River, 1972. Both Sides Now, 1975. Songbird, 1979. The Water Is Wide, 1983.* Thirty-one songs in all, scattered across forty years of other people's weddings, marked in a hand that never wavered, never explained itself.

And every single one of them was marked with a bell, too.

The birds and the bells always traveled together. Never a bird without a bell. But there were bells without birds—songs marked with a bell alone, dozens of them, the ordinary machinery of weddings. The bird only appeared on certain ones. And the bird-marked songs, Jonah noticed, were never the ones Edith had written in the main columns. They lived in the margins. Squeezed in sideways at the bottom of the page. Written in the spaces left over, the way you write something you want to remember but don't want to be the point.

*Songbird* was written sideways in the margin of the Okonkwo-Feeney page, small enough that you'd miss it if you weren't looking. Beside it, in even smaller letters, almost an afterthought, almost a whisper: *she liked the line about the wire.*

Jonah read that four times.

*She.*

He turned back through the book with new eyes. The marginal notes were sparse—Edith had been a woman of economy—but they were there, a handful across forty years. *For the porch in June.* *She hummed this one wrong, on purpose, to make me laugh.* *Last one before she went.* And once, on a page dated 1968, in ink so faded it was nearly a ghost: *M.*

Just the letter. Just *M.*

Jonah sat very still. The rain kept on. Somewhere under the tarps, the inn creaked the way old houses do when they're thinking.

He knew this handwriting. Not Edith's—the other one. The marginal hand was different. Rounder. Slower. The *M* had a little flourish on its tail, a small flourish, like a bird lifting off.

He'd seen that flourish before. On the framed certificate in the hallway downstairs—the one he'd walked past a hundred times without reading. He went and got a flashlight and stood in front of it, dripping rain from his shoulders onto the floorboards.

*MARGARET QUILL, ORGANIST, BELLBIRD BAY INN, 1961–1987.*

The same round hand. The same flourish on the *g*, the *Q*, the tail of every letter, like birds taking off all down the page.

* * *

He didn't sleep much that night. In the morning he went to the one person in Bellbird Bay who'd been alive long enough to know things and stubborn enough to tell them: Ruth Okonkwo, who ran the hardware store and who had, in the two months since they'd arrived, sold Jonah forty dollars of sandpaper and exactly zero pleasantries.

She was restocking screws when he came in. She looked at him over her glasses and said, "You're the one who broke the porch step."

"I fixed the porch step."

"You broke it first. That's the part I remember." She set down her box. "What do you want, Reyes?"

"The inn's organist," Jonah said. "Margaret Quill. What happened to her?"

Ruth's face did something complicated. It was quick, but Jonah had spent fifteen years reading faces across negotiation tables, and he caught it—the small flinch, the way her eyes went to the window as if the answer might be standing outside.

"Where'd you hear that name?"

"Edith's songbook."

Ruth was quiet for a long moment. Then she came out from behind the counter, flipped the sign on the door to CLOSED, and pointed him to a stool.

"Margaret Quill played every wedding at that inn for twenty-six years," she said. "And Edith Marsh planned every one of them. And the two of them were—" Ruth stopped, and Jonah watched a woman who'd never in her life been at a loss for a word decide, deliberately, to find one. "—close. Very close. Margaret had a room on the third floor, the one with the round window. She lived there winters. Summers too, mostly."

"What happened in '87?"

"She left." Ruth said it flatly, but her hands were busy, straightening screws that didn't need straightening. "Her family was in Vermont. Her sister got sick. She went to take care of her, and she didn't come back. Edith waited—well. Edith didn't wait. Edith *ran the inn* and *planned weddings* and *never once*—" Ruth's jaw tightened. "Edith never left Bellbird Bay again. Not for a wedding, not for a funeral, not for anything. Forty years, she stayed in that house. And every June she'd put flowers in the third-floor room. Fresh ones. I know because I delivered them. Peonies. She'd never say why, and I never asked, because in a town like this you learn which questions are furniture and which ones are landmines."

Jonah thought of the third-floor room. He and Tamsin had catalogued it in week one: *Round window, good light, water damage on north wall, one iron bedframe.* He thought of Edith's will. *To the two who ran.* He'd assumed it meant running away—from the bay, from each other, from whatever happened in the snow ten years ago.

But maybe it also meant the opposite. Maybe it meant the two people who knew what it cost to run, and what it cost to stay.

"Did the town know?" Jonah asked. "About them?"

Ruth looked at him for a long time. Outside, the rain kept falling on a town that had spent a century perfecting the art of not seeing what was in front of it.

"Everybody knew," she said. "Nobody knew. That was the arrangement. Edith planned the weddings, Margaret played them, and the two of them lived in that inn like a fact nobody wrote down. And then Margaret's sister got sick, and Margaret went, and Edith—" Ruth stopped again. "You know what Edith said at the memorial service? Not Margaret's—Edith's, twenty years later. The minister asked if anyone wanted to speak, and Edith Marsh, who planned two hundred weddings and never once got to plan her own, stood up and said, 'She had perfect pitch.' Sat right back down. That was the whole eulogy. Half the town thought it was a joke about the organ."

Jonah's throat was tight. "It wasn't."

"No," Ruth said. "It wasn't."

* * *

He walked back along the seawall in the rain without hurrying, because the songbook was in the waterproof bag against his chest and some part of him was afraid of it now, the way you're afraid of a letter you haven't opened.

In the parlor, he laid the book on the piano again and went through the bird-marked songs one final time, and this time he read the margins as what they were: a correspondence. Forty years of a conversation conducted entirely in other people's weddings. *Moon River* for the porch in June. *Both Sides Now* because Margaret had argued with Edith about it once, in 1975, and Edith had marked it with a bell and a bird and the word *she wins.* *The Water Is Wide*—and beside it, in Edith's hand, so small it was nearly nothing: *she played this at our last one. I never told her it was mine too.*

*Our last one.*

Not their wedding. There had been no wedding. There could be no wedding—not in 1987, not in this town, not for two women who lived like a fact nobody wrote down. So they'd had something else instead. They'd had setlists. They'd had margins. They'd had a language built out of other people's joy, a whole marriage conducted in the white space of two hundred strangers' ceremonies, and when it ended, there was no paperwork, no hearing, no deed. There was a room on the third floor with fresh peonies every June, and a eulogy four words long, and a songbook full of bells and birds that nobody was ever meant to read.

Jonah sat down at the piano bench, and the bench was still warm from where Tamsin had sat that morning, and he thought: *she never told her it was mine too.*

Ten years ago, in the snow, he had a sentence. He'd carried it out of that inn at dawn like a stolen thing, and he'd never said it, and he'd told himself a hundred reasons why—timing, pride, the fact that Tamsin Delacroix was the competition and you don't hand the competition ammunition. But sitting here with Edith's ghosts, he knew those reasons were scaffolding around a simpler truth: he'd been afraid that saying it would make it real, and if it was real, he could lose it. Edith Marsh had never been afraid of losing it. She'd just been born into a town and a time that made saying it impossible. So she'd done the next best thing. She'd written it in the margins and waited forty years for someone to find it.

And then she'd written a will that put the songbook in the hands of two people who were also living in the margins of their own story, also fluent in a language of things unsaid, also running.

*The two who ran.*

Jonah understood now that the will had never been about the inn. The inn was just the paper the sentence was written on. Edith had spent her whole life proving that belonging didn't need the law's permission—that a love with no certificate could still fill a house for forty years. And she'd left the house to whoever could prove the same thing. Not with paperwork. The council could keep their paperwork. With the thing the paperwork was supposed to stand in for.

Justice outside the law had a lineage here. It ran through this house like a joist under the floorboards—invisible, load-bearing, holding everything up.

He turned to the last page of the songbook. It was blank except for one line at the top, in Edith's violet ink, written in a steadier hand than the rest, as if she'd known someone would arrive here eventually:

*The setlist isn't finished. It never is. That's the whole point of a house like this.*

Below it, a space. Below the space, a smaller line: *There's room in the margins.*

Jonah sat there a long time. The rain was easing. Through the parlor window he could see the ballroom's dark windows across the hall, and beyond them the gray sea doing its gray work, and he thought about the hearing that was coming, and the wedding they still had to pull off, and the woman upstairs who was currently not speaking to him for reasons that were entirely his fault and entirely about the thing he couldn't say.

There's room in the margins.

He got a pencil. He didn't write anything—not yet. But he left the pencil on the songbook, on the last page, under Edith's line, and he closed the cover on it gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping room.

Then he heard the floorboard on the stairs—the third step, the one that sang—and Tamsin came down into the parlor with her arms full of paint swatches and her eyes red-rimmed and her jaw set in the particular way that meant she'd decided to be professional about something that was tearing her apart, and Jonah stood up from the piano bench with Edith's songbook in his hands and forty years of unsaid things in his chest, and said, before he could stop himself:

"Tamsin. Do you know who Margaret Quill was?"

She stopped on the last step. Rain-light moved across her face.

"No," she said slowly. "But I have a feeling you're about to tell me why that matters."

He held up the songbook. "Because I think Edith didn't leave us an inn," he said. "I think she left us instructions."

And upstairs, faint through the floorboards, the third step sang again—though no one had stepped on it.
