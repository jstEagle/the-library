## 16. Edith's Letters

The attic stairs were the kind that announced you.

Jonah had been in the inn for six weeks now, and he'd learned its vocabulary—the groan of the third porch board, the way the kitchen door needed a hip-check, the particular sigh the boiler made at two in the morning like an old man settling into a chair. But he'd never climbed the narrow staircase behind the linen closet, because Edith Marsh had kept it locked her whole life, and some habits outlive the person who taught them.

The lock had finally surrendered to a flathead screwdriver and forty minutes of Jonah talking to it the way he talked to difficult vendors. Now it hung open, and the stairs went up into a darkness that smelled of cedar and dust and, faintly, impossibly, of roses.

"Okay, Edith," he said to nobody. "I'm coming up. Don't haunt me. I've had enough haunting for one decade."

The attic was bigger than the inn had any right to contain. A single round window at the far end let in the gray afternoon light, and it fell across decades of a life packed away with terrible precision: hatboxes labeled by year, steamer trunks with brass fittings gone green, a dressmaker's mannequin wearing a wedding gown so old its lace had gone the color of weak tea. Everything was dusted. That was the detail that stopped him. Someone had come up here—recently, regularly—and dusted every surface of a room no one was supposed to enter.

Edith had kept her own museum open after hours.

He found the letters in the fourth trunk he opened, under a quilt stitched with initials that weren't hers: *E.M. and R.C.*, intertwined, the thread faded from rose to ghost. The tin was a biscuit tin, the kind that never held biscuits again once a family decided it was important. On the lid, in Edith's upright, unhesitating hand—the same hand that had filled the songbook—someone had written: *Letters I did not send. Do not read unless you are the one who needs them.*

Jonah sat down on the floorboards with the tin in his lap and felt the weight of that instruction settle over him like the quilt itself.

He was, he was fairly sure, the one who needed them.

* * *

There were forty-one letters. He counted twice, because counting was something to do instead of feeling.

They spanned forty years. The earliest was dated June 1963, written on paper so thin he could see the ghost of the next page through it. The latest was dated eight months before Edith died, written on the cream stationery from the front desk, the kind Jonah had used himself to leave notes for Tamsin about paint samples.

He read them in order, because order mattered, because Edith had clearly believed in sequence the way she'd believed in setlists—that a story told out of order tells a different story entirely.

The first letter began: *Dear Ruth. I have started this letter nine times and burned eight of them in the kitchen stove, which Marion thinks is because I am careless with paper.*

Ruth. Ruth Calloway. The R.C. on the quilt. Jonah sat very still while the attic rearranged itself around him.

He knew the name, vaguely, the way you know names from the back pages of local history—a Calloway girl who'd left Bellbird Bay in the sixties, taught school in Portland, never came back except to be buried. There was a Calloway plot in the cemetery on the hill. He'd walked past it a hundred times as a boy without knowing he was walking past the reason Edith Marsh had locked her attic for fifty years.

The letters told him anyway.

They told him about two women who met at a dance in 1962 and spent one summer being so happy that the happiness itself became dangerous. They told him about a town that noticed, and a mother who wept, and a Ruth who chose Portland because Portland was far enough away to survive in. They told him about Edith staying—not because she was braver, but because someone had to keep the inn, and because leaving felt like agreeing with everyone who said she was wrong.

*I stayed,* she wrote in 1971, *and everyone calls it loyalty. Some mornings I call it cowardice with good posture.*

Jonah laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, because he recognized the sentence the way you recognize your own handwriting in someone else's letter.

He read until the light through the round window went from gray to gold to the bruised purple of early evening, and he didn't turn on the lamp he'd brought, because some things you should read by dying light. The letters got shorter over the decades. The middle ones were mostly news—storms, renovations, the slow decline of the town, the council's various schemes to pave or sell or "revitalize" things. But every letter, all forty-one, ended the same way. With the same three lines, repeated like a chorus, like a vow, like a woman practicing:

*I loved you then. I love you now. I will say it properly someday, when there is time.*

Forty years. Forty-one unsent letters. And at the end of every single one, a sentence she rehearsed and never delivered, because the person it belonged to was four hundred miles away living a life Edith had refused to ruin, and because *someday* is the most comfortable lie ever invented.

The last letter was different. It was short. It was dated last spring, when Edith must have already known what the doctors knew. It said:

*Ruth died in March. I found out from a newsletter. A newsletter, after fifty-eight years. I am writing this not to send it but to finish it, because I promised myself I would say it properly someday, and I am running out of somedays. So here it is, the whole thing, no rehearsal:*

*I loved you then. I love you still. I am sorry I waited for time that was never going to be given.*

*If anyone reads this—if anyone ever finds this tin—I hope they are smarter than I was. I hope they say it while the person is standing in front of them, close enough to touch. I hope they don't wait for a snowstorm or a funeral or a lawyer's letter to make them brave. Belonging isn't proven by papers. It's proven by staying, and by saying, and I only managed one of those.*

*That is why the will says what it says. Let whoever reads this understand: the inn was never the test. I just wanted to give two stubborn people a building big enough to hide in, and ninety days to run out of hiding places.*

Jonah put the letter down very carefully, the way you put down something sleeping.

* * *

He didn't cry, exactly. He did something worse. He sat in a darkening attic doing math.

Ninety days. They were on day sixty-something—he'd lost exact count somewhere around the hearing filings, which was its own kind of telling. Sixty days of sharing a roof with Tamsin Delacroix. Sixty days of notes left in the songbook because speaking had become too expensive. Sixty days since the newspaper article, since she'd looked at him across the ballroom with ten years of confirmation in her eyes—*there it is again, the leaving, the silence, the proof*—and he had let her look, because explaining meant confessing, and confessing meant the snowstorm, and the snowstorm meant everything.

Harrow had offered him the inn, sole and entire, if he'd swear Tamsin was a stranger to the bay. He'd refused. He'd refused in under a minute, actually, which should have counted for something, and instead he'd tucked the refusal away like a letter in a biscuit tin, unsent, unrevealed, and let it fester in the dark where secrets go to grow teeth.

And now Edith, dead eight months, had seen through all of it from inside a locked attic.

*Two stubborn people,* she'd written. *A building big enough to hide in.*

He thought about the songbook downstairs, the note he'd found weeks ago that wasn't his—Tamsin's handwriting, unfinished, abandoned mid-sentence: *the thing I should have said in the snow was*

He'd read it eleven times. He'd stood in the empty ballroom holding that page like it was a live wire, and he had not answered it, because answering required his own half of the sentence, and his half had been stuck in his throat for ten years like a fishbone.

*I loved you then.* Four words. He'd watched Tamsin plan two hundred weddings. He'd heard her deliver vows secondhand until she could recite the great ones from memory. Four words, and he couldn't manage them, and a woman who'd failed at the same thing for forty years had looked down—or up, or however the dead look—from her locked attic and arranged an entire inheritance around the problem.

The will was never paperwork. Harrow understood paperwork; that was why he was going to lose. The will was a sentence. One sentence per heir, rehearsed for a combined fifty years, due before the deadline.

Jonah took out his notebook—the cheap spiral one he used for site measurements—and copied the last letter out by hand, all of it, because Edith had earned a witness and because his own handwriting might make it real in a way reading hadn't. His pen slowed at the final lines. He copied them twice.

Then he sat on the floor of Edith Marsh's attic, in the dark, holding a piece of paper that was essentially instructions, and admitted the thing he'd been circling for sixty days:

He was going to lose her. Not to Harrow, not to the council, not to the hearing scheduled the day after Priya and Dev's wedding. To silence. The same silence, the same exit, the same dawn walkout, performed one more time with feeling. Unless he said it. Unless he stood in front of her, close enough to touch, and said it while saying was still possible.

*I loved you then. I love you now. I'm done waiting for time that was never going to be given.*

It sounded simple written down. Everything true does.

He folded the copy into his breast pocket, over the left side, where it sat like a splint on a break. Then he climbed down the announcing stairs, each step creaking his arrival to the whole house, and paused at the bottom with his hand on the linen closet door.

Downstairs, faintly, he could hear Tamsin in the ballroom, testing string lights, humming along to whatever was playing on her phone—something old, something with a horn section, something Edith would have approved of. Day sixty-three. The wedding was in five days. The hearing was in six.

Five days to learn what a woman had failed to learn in forty years.

Jonah pressed his hand flat against the paper in his pocket and went down to find her, rehearsing under his breath the whole way, like a man memorizing a setlist.
