## 18. The Wedding at Bellbird Bay

### Tamsin

The morning of the wedding, Tamsin woke at five and lay in the dark of her room above the kitchen, listening to the sea work at the rocks below the bluff, and made a list. She always made a list on wedding days. It was how she kept herself from feeling too much: if every fear could be named, itemized, and assigned a contingency, then no fear could ambush her.

*Item one:* The florist's van was due at six, and the peonies had been sitting in the cold room since Thursday, which meant they would open all at once, gloriously, catastrophically, sometime around three o'clock — precisely when Priya was meant to walk down an aisle lined with them.

*Item two:* The string quartet had never played in a room with acoustics like the ballroom's, where sound pooled and lingered the way it used to when Edith Marsh filled this floor with dancers.

*Item three:* Jonah Reyes would be standing at the far end of that ballroom in approximately nine hours, wearing whatever he wore when he wanted to look like he hadn't tried, and she would have to stand opposite him for the length of an entire ceremony without looking at him more than was professionally necessary.

She got up. She put the kettle on. She wrote *peonies — open early, rotate to sun* on the back of an envelope, because some problems could be solved with logistics, and she loved those problems best.

The inn had never looked like this. Ten weeks ago it had been a ruin wearing a coat of salt air — shutters hanging, ballroom floor buckled, the great hearth cold since before either of them was born. Now the shutters stood straight, painted the blue-green of harbor water; the floor gleamed under two months of their combined stubbornness, plank by sanded plank; and the hearth — God, the hearth. They'd relit it last night, just to test the draft, and the whole room had gone gold, and Tamsin had stood there with soot on her cheekbones thinking: *this is what it was for. This room was built to hold light.*

It would hold light today. It would hold Priya in saffron silk and Dev weeping openly, shamelessly, into his pocket square. It would hold two families who had flown across an ocean to disapprove and who were going to walk out of here having failed, magnificently, to do so.

And it would hold her and Jonah, on opposite sides of the aisle, mouthing along to vows that weren't theirs.

That was the item she couldn't assign a contingency to. That was the item she'd been avoiding since the seating chart.

Because here was the thing about planning weddings for ten years: you learned the liturgy by heart. You knew every variation — the traditional, the modern, the interfaith, the hand-written, the borrowed-from-a-poem. And somewhere in the last decade, without meaning to, Tamsin had composed her own vows. Not on paper. In the private theater of her skull, during other people's ceremonies, while she stood at the back checking sightlines. They surfaced unbidden, the way a song you hate can colonize your brain: *I choose you in the snow and out of it. I choose the version of you that stayed and the version that ran.*

Ten years of rehearsal. Zero performances.

She heard Jonah's boots on the stairs — she could identify his tread now, which was its own indictment — and went out to the landing with her clipboard held like a shield.

"Florist's early," he said, looking up at her. "Peonies?"

"Rotating them to the sun."

"I already did. Six-fifteen." He almost smiled. "You're welcome."

"You're insufferable."

"And yet the floor looks incredible." He said it plainly, no armor on it, and for a second the morning tilted — the way it had over that shared plank, the way it had by candlelight when he'd leaned in and she'd said *words first, Jonah, I need words first,* and he'd looked at her like a man being asked to defuse something with his teeth.

Then he'd deflected, and the candle had burned down, and they'd gone back to notes passed through Edith's songbook like teenagers too shy to speak.

"Ballroom walkthrough at eight," she said. "Chuppah weights are in the hall closet. If Harrow comes—"

"He RSVP'd," Jonah said. "Can you believe it?"

Actually, she could. Councilman Harrow, who had spent three months trying to prove neither of them belonged to Bellbird Bay, had sent back his card with a single word — *Attending* — in fountain pen. Tamsin had stared at it for a long time, trying to decide whether it was surveillance or surrender.

"Everyone belongs somewhere," she said finally. "Even to the people trying to evict them."

Jonah looked at her a moment too long. "Yeah," he said. "Something like that."

* * *

### Jonah

By noon, the ballroom smelled like peonies and beeswax, and Jonah had stopped pretending he wasn't nervous.

Not about the wedding. The wedding was bulletproof. He'd watched Tamsin work these ten weeks and understood, finally, why her reputation preceded her into rooms he hadn't even entered yet: she planned like someone building a lifeboat. Every detail load-bearing. Every contingency stitched in. When the quartet's cellist called at eleven to say her bridge had collapsed on Route One, Tamsin had a replacement cellist — a retired music teacher from the Methodist church, delighted to be asked — before Jonah finished pouring the coffee.

No. What made his hands unsteady as he hung the last of the lanterns was simpler and worse: today was a rehearsal for something.

He'd figured it out around week seven, sanding the floor, watching her laugh at something he'd said — really laugh, head back, unguarded — and thought with a kind of horror: *this is what it would be like.* Not the fantasy version. The Tuesday version. Coffee at six, peonies rotated, arguments about chair counts, her handwriting on the backs of envelopes. He'd spent ten years telling himself the snowstorm was a fluke of circumstance — trapped travelers, too much wine, lowered defenses. But the truth was that the storm hadn't created anything. It had only revealed what was already true, and then he'd spent a decade pretending the reveal hadn't happened.

Now he stood in a restored ballroom, in a town that had started saying hello to him by name at the hardware store, holding a lantern hook, waiting for vows he could recite from memory because he'd written his own versions years ago, late at night, in hotel rooms after other people's weddings.

*I should have stayed in the snow. I choose the man I was too afraid to be at twenty-six. I'm done running ahead of the good thing.*

Edith had rehearsed hers for forty years. He'd read the letters now — all of them, up in the attic, by flashlight, his throat closing around each page. *My darling Ruth, if I ever find the courage, the sentence is this:* and then the sentence, and then forty more letters that ended unsigned, unsent, unspoken until she was dead and the sentence became a legal condition disguised as a will.

*Say it before time runs out.* That was the whole inheritance. That was the test.

And tomorrow — tomorrow the hearing. Tomorrow he would stand in front of the council and tell the whole town about the snowstorm, about Harrow's offer, about everything, because belonging couldn't be proven with paperwork and he was done letting silence do his talking for him.

But that was tomorrow. Today there was a wedding.

The guests began arriving at four. Jonah stood at the door with Tamsin — professionally necessary, they told themselves — greeting people, and the people kept coming, and coming, and coming. The fisherman whose boat had hauled their lumber. The woman from the bakery who'd fed them cinnamon rolls through the worst of the restoration. Priya's aunt, who had arrived as opposition and been converted somewhere between the rehearsal dinner and this afternoon, now wearing a smile like a woman who'd lost a war and discovered she preferred the winning side.

And then Harrow. Gray suit, gray expression, gray little nod. He paused in front of Jonah longer than courtesy required.

"Reyes."

"Councilman. Glad you could make it."

"I came to see whether the property was being maintained." A beat. His eyes went past Jonah's shoulder, to the ballroom glowing gold behind him, the hearth lit, the floor shining, the whole impossible thing alive. Something moved across his face — quickly, but not quickly enough. "It appears to be," he said, and went inside, and sat down, and did not leave.

Tamsin appeared at Jonah's elbow. "Did he just—"

"Yeah."

"Huh," she said softly. And then the quartet tuned up, and there was no more time for anything but the wedding.

* * *

### Tamsin

Priya walked in on her brother's arm, and the ballroom did the thing Tamsin had dreamed it doing for ten weeks: it held its breath.

Saffron silk. Marigolds in her hair. Her chin up, her eyes wet and furious and joyful all at once, walking past the family members who had told her no — past her grandmother, who sat rigid in the third row with her hands folded like a closed door — down an aisle of peonies that had, against all odds, opened exactly on schedule, because Tamsin had rotated them at dawn and would accept the credit silently and forever.

Dev was crying before she reached the fourth row. Big, unhidden tears, the kind men were taught to ration, spent here like a man who had decided rationing was over.

The officiant — the Methodist music teacher's husband, a gentle giant of a man — began to speak, and Tamsin took her place at the end of the front row, where planners stood: close enough to intervene, far enough to disappear.

Across the aisle, mirrored, stood Jonah.

She didn't look at him. She looked at Priya and Dev, at their joined hands, at Dev saying *I do* like a man planting a flag. But she felt Jonah the way you feel weather through a wall — a pressure, a presence, a change in the air — and when the officiant said, "Dev, repeat after me," and Dev repeated, and then it was Priya's turn, and Priya turned to face the room and said her vows aloud, clear as a bell over water—

*"I choose you,"* Priya said. *"In front of everyone who said I shouldn't. I choose you in the easy seasons and the storms. I am done hiding the truest thing I know."*

— Tamsin's lips moved.

She didn't decide to. The words were simply there, ten years deep, worn smooth as river stones from handling: *I choose you in the snow and out of it. I choose the version of you that stayed and the version that ran. I am done training my heart to be quiet.*

She caught herself halfway through. Pressed her lips together. Felt her eyes sting and blinked it back down, professional, contained, the muscle doing what the muscle had been trained to do.

And then, because she was weak, because it was a wedding and weddings were licensed anarchy, she let her eyes slide across the aisle.

Jonah was mouthing words too.

His jaw moved slightly, his gaze fixed on Priya and Dev but focused somewhere thirty feet behind them and ten years ago, and though she couldn't hear him, she knew — she *knew*, the way you know a song from its first two notes — that he was speaking his own rehearsed sentences into the air between them. That somewhere in that stubborn, armored chest lived a set of vows with her name on them, performed for a decade to empty rooms.

Their eyes met across the aisle, over the heads of two families becoming one, and neither of them looked away, and neither of them spoke, and the joy in the room sharpened into something with an edge — because this was what Edith must have felt, Tamsin thought suddenly. Standing at other people's weddings for forty years, next to Ruth, mouthing the truest thing she knew, while the town applauded love it refused to permit.

The rings were exchanged. The officiant pronounced them. Priya and Dev kissed to a sound like the roof lifting off — applause, cheering, someone's whistle, old Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery sobbing into her napkin — and the ballroom blazed, and the hearth threw gold on the walls, and Tamsin stood in the middle of the most beautiful wedding of her career feeling like a woman standing on a platform watching a train she wasn't on pull away.

Jonah crossed to her through the crowd. Up close, his eyes were wet, and he didn't pretend otherwise.

"That," he said, "was the best one I've ever seen."

"It was," she agreed. Her voice came out rough. "We're good at this."

"We're good at this," he echoed, and something in the way he said it — *we*, weighted, deliberate — made her heart do the thing hearts do when they've been trained too long and the training starts to fail.

* * *

### Jonah

The dancing started at seven and did not stop.

Edith's songbook had given them the setlist — Jonah had transcribed her annotations into a running order, decade by decade, and the band played all of it, and the older half of the town danced to songs they'd danced to at this very inn fifty years ago, and cried about it happily, and told anyone who'd listen that Edith Marsh was surely watching from somewhere and *approving*.

Even Harrow danced. Once. A stiff, dignified turn around the floor with his wife during "Moon River," during which his face attempted several expressions and settled, briefly, on something dangerously close to wonder. Jonah watched from the edge of the floor and thought: *there it is. The man under the councilman. He remembers what this room was.* It wouldn't save them tomorrow. But it meant the fight wasn't against a wall. It was against a man who remembered.

At ten o'clock, Priya found Jonah by the hearth. Her flowers were wilted, her shoes were off, and she looked incandescently happy.

"You're staring at her again," she said.

"I'm inventorying the candle inventory."

"You're staring at her," Priya repeated, with the serene authority of a woman who had gotten everything she wanted that day by refusing to pretend. "You know what my grandmother said to me tonight? At the cake? She said, 'I opposed you because I was afraid for you.' And I said, 'Grandmother, I know. Fear always dresses up as disapproval.'" She patted his arm. "Just a thought. Free of charge. Congratulations on the wedding — it was flawless. Both of yours will be too, presumably, whenever you two stop conducting your entire relationship through sticky notes."

She sailed off before he could answer, which was probably wise, because he had no answer, only the sentence — *the* sentence — rising in his throat like water finding its level.

He found Tamsin at midnight, blowing out candles along the windows, the guests gone, the ballroom littered with petals and joy. Outside, the sea kept its own time. Tomorrow there would be a hearing, and he would stand up in front of the council and say everything — the snowstorm, the offer, the letters, all of it — and after tomorrow there would be no more secrets left in him except one.

"Tamsin." His voice cracked on the second syllable. She turned, candle snuffer in hand, firelight on her face.

"Yes?"

"The hearing's at nine," he said. "I need to tell you what I'm going to do tomorrow. All of it. Before you hear it from anyone else."

Her eyes narrowed — not with suspicion, he realized, but with hope, which was worse. "Jonah. What did you do?"

He took a breath, and reached into his jacket for the copied pages of Edith's letters, and began: "Harrow offered me the inn. Three weeks ago. Sole ownership — if I testified that you were a stranger to this bay."
