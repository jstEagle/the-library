## 1. The Keeper's Hours

The lighthouse had not been lit in thirty-one years, but Cyrus Vane still climbed it every morning, as if the habit itself were a kind of fuel.

She went up at six, before the fog had finished deciding what kind of day it wanted to be. One hundred and twelve steps, spiraling inside a stone throat that smelled of salt and old iron and, faintly, of her grandfather's pipe tobacco, though he had been gone ten years now and the tobacco smell was surely only her imagination, worn into the walls like a groove worn into a stair. She counted the steps anyway. She always counted. One hundred and twelve was a number you could hold in your hand, and Cyrus had learned early that a person survives on the things she can hold.

At the top, the lamp room. The great Fresnel lens sat in its cradle like a sleeping animal, brass and crystal, prisms stacked in a ring that caught the gray morning and broke it into small rainbows that wandered the walls as the sun moved. She wiped the glass with a cloth she kept folded in her cardigan pocket. She oiled the door hinge. She checked the weather seals her grandfather had installed the summer before he disappeared, and she wrote the date and the conditions in the maintenance log in her careful, upright hand—*June 3. Fog to 8 a.m., clearing. Glass sound. Hinge oiled.*—because a log was a promise you made to whoever came after you, even if no one ever would.

The Coast Guard had decommissioned the light when she was seven. Automation, they said, though there was nothing automated about the dark rock shoal a half mile out, the one the old charts called the Junebug's Tooth, the one that had bitten more hulls than anyone liked to count aloud. The town had fought the decommissioning the way small towns fight everything: with petitions and potlucks and a float in the harvest parade, and then, when the petitions failed, with silence. The light went dark. The keeper's cottage went to the family. And the Vane name went, slowly, from *the people who keep us safe* to *the people whose grandfather—* well. People in Junebug Creek did not finish that sentence in front of Cyrus. They just let it hang in the air like weather.

She didn't mind, mostly. Invisibility was a craft, and she had gotten good at it.

* * *

By seven she was down the hill and in the cottage kitchen, which was the same kitchen her grandmother had kept for fifty years, right down to the blue enamel kettle and the calendar from the feed store that no one had ever thrown away. Her grandmother, Odessa, was still asleep; she kept the hours of a woman who had spent her life married to a lighthouse, awake at odd tides, dozing at odd noons. Cyrus made coffee in the percolator, which took nine minutes and could not be hurried, and while it grumbled she sat at the table with the tin.

The tin had once held shortbread. Now it held ten years of letters.

She did not open it every morning. That was the discipline of it—her own small scripture, the liturgy she had built out of the wreckage of being nineteen. On Mondays she read the newest letter, if there was one. On Thursdays she answered it, or tried to. And on the first Sunday of every month, she allowed herself the whole archive, beginning to end, in order, because the letters were a story and stories lost their meaning when you read the middle first.

But this was not Sunday, and there was no new letter—there had been no new letter in two years—and still her hand went to the tin the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth.

*You don't have to,* she told herself. *You know them all by heart.*

She opened it anyway.

The letters were unsigned, every one. The first had arrived eleven days after the funeral, in an envelope with no return address, postmarked from a town she'd had to look up—Harlow Bend, three counties east, a place she'd never been. The handwriting had been careful, a young man's handwriting trying to be an old man's, all the letters standing very straight as if at attention, except the *g*s, which looped low and backward like little hooks, like hands reaching down into water.

*I don't know you,* that first letter said, *and you don't know me, and I'm not going to pretend I have the right words, because I've read every condolence card in the stationery store and none of them are true. But I know what it is to lose someone in the water, and I know what it is to have the whole town watching to see how you fall apart. So I'm not writing to tell you it gets better. I'm writing to tell you that tonight, at 8:47, the tide will turn, and it will turn tomorrow too, and the day after, whether or not you get out of bed. The sea keeps its appointments. You can hate it for that or you can lean on it. I lean on it.*

She had been nineteen, sitting in this same kitchen, three days past the point where she had stopped being able to imagine a future, and the letter had arrived like a hand out of dark water. Not a rescue. A hand. An offer. *Take it or don't.*

She had taken it.

For eight years the letters had come, roughly monthly, sometimes more, always unsigned, always postmarked Harlow Bend. They were not romantic. She wanted to be clear about that, even in the privacy of her own head. They were better than romantic. They were the correspondence of two people who had agreed, without ever saying so, to be honest about the one thing the rest of the world wanted them to perform their way through. He told her about his brother once—only once—a brother who had loved boats and had died near the water too, though he never said how, and she had understood that this was the door he was showing her and also the door he was keeping shut, and she had never pushed. You didn't push a hand that was holding you up.

And she had told him things she had never told anyone. That she blamed herself for not answering the phone the night her grandfather's boat, the *Sweet June*, went down on the Tooth. That the last thing she'd said to him was *I'm busy, Grampa, I'll call you tomorrow.* That she had started a novel at seventeen and could not finish it, could not finish anything, because finishing meant ending and endings were where people left you. That she sometimes stood at the top of the lighthouse and imagined the light lit again, one hundred and twelve steps below her grandfather's hands on the rail, and that the imagining was the closest thing to prayer she had.

He had written back: *Finish the novel. Not for the ending. For the middle. The middle is where the living happens.*

So she had tried. For ten years she had tried.

* * *

The novel was called *The Salt Road*, and it lived in a green notebook that had been green in 2014 and was now more of a gray, its spine held together with archival tape she ordered specially from a shop in Portland. It concerned a lighthouse keeper's daughter in a fictional town, and it had stalled, at last count, four thousand words from the end, at the exact point where the daughter had to forgive somebody, because Cyrus had discovered—four thousand words from the end, after six years of work—that she did not actually know how forgiveness worked. She could describe it from the outside. She could not render it. It was like being asked to write about a country she'd never entered.

She had tried to explain this to her correspondent, two years ago, in the last letter she'd sent. She'd written four drafts. In the final one she had come closer to the truth than she ever had:

*I think I'm stuck because I think forgiveness is a door and I don't know what's behind it. I've been standing in front of it so long I've furnished the hallway. I have a chair in the hallway. I have a whole life in the hallway. What if I open the door and there's nothing there and I've wasted the hallway?*

The reply had never come. She'd told herself the post office had lost it. She'd told herself he was ill, or traveling, or that life had simply gotten loud the way life does in your thirties. She'd told herself a great many things, and she had kept writing to him anyway—every Thursday, sealed, stamped, addressed to a post office box in Harlow Bend that had never once answered her—and if that made her a woman who talked to an empty room, well. She had built her whole life out of rooms like that. One more was not going to kill her.

What she did not do—what she had never once done in ten years—was try to find out who he was.

This was the deepest article of her scripture, the one the others hung from. She knew why, and the why was simple and terrible: because wanting to know was the first step toward losing it. The letters worked because he was a voice and not a face, a hand and not a history. If she found him, she would find a man with a body and a bad day and probably a wife, and the hallway she'd furnished would collapse, and she would be nineteen again, standing at the edge of the water with the phone still ringing in her memory. The not-knowing was the only safe place she had. So she kept it. She guarded it the way her grandfather had guarded the light: daily, without drama, because that was the job.

She was a woman who took small safe steps, and she had made her peace with that, and if her peace had a draft in it—well. All the old houses did.

* * *

At nine, her grandmother came down in her quilted robe, and the day became a day with two people in it, which was the most people it usually held.

"You're up early," Odessa said, though Cyrus was always up early, and Odessa always said it, and this too was liturgy.

"Fog burned off fast."

"Mm." Her grandmother filled her own cup, then stood at the window looking down the hill toward the creek, toward the water, toward the place where the town began. She was eighty-one and stood like a mast. "Committee's coming by today. About the centennial. I told them you'd help with the booklet."

"Grandmother."

"They're bringing pie."

"That's bribery."

"Obviously. It's effective bribery. You'll do it." Odessa sipped her coffee and did not look at her granddaughter, which was how she delivered most of her orders. "A hundred years since the town was incorporated. It's good for you. You've been up in that tower writing about people who don't exist. Write about people who do. They're just as strange, and you can't invent them, which will be a discipline."

Cyrus almost smiled. "And what will you be doing while I'm being disciplined?"

"Supervising." Her grandmother turned from the window. For a moment her face did something Cyrus couldn't read—some weather moving behind it, out over deep water. "The past is going to come calling this summer, Cyrus. A hundredth birthday is when a town opens all its drawers. I'd rather we be the ones holding things when they come out."

"That's ominous."

"That's pie," said Odessa, as a car crunched up the gravel drive, and the morning swept them both away into casseroles and clipboards.

* * *

The committee took three hours and ate an entire lemon meringue, and by the time they left, Cyrus had been drafted into writing the commemorative booklet—*a short history, nothing fancy, you're the only one in this town who finishes sentences*—and had agreed, because agreeing was easier than being seen to resist, and invisibility was a craft.

It was late afternoon when she finally walked back up to the lighthouse for her evening round, the second of her two daily ascents, because a keeper kept hours whether or not anyone was keeping score. The light was going long and gold across the water. The creek glittered. Down at the harbor, boats knocked gently against their slips, and the smell of someone's dinner—fried something, onions—drifted up the hill.

She was halfway up the steps, at step sixty, when she heard the truck.

It came along the coast road from the east, from the direction of Harlow Bend, though she didn't know that yet and wouldn't have cared if she had. It was a big moving truck, the kind that carried whole lives, and it rattled and groaned up the hill past the lighthouse gate and did not stop, and Cyrus stood on the stairs with her hand on the cold rail and watched it go, and thought, with the small cold clarity of a woman whose world is built of small safe things: *that's the boathouse.*

The old Calloway boathouse. Empty since before she was born, since the family that built it left town in a hurry that nobody ever explained, the way families in small towns sometimes leave. The property had changed hands twice in the winter, quietly, through a lawyer in Portland. She'd heard Odessa and the postmistress speculating about it over the mail slots in February and had tuned it out, the way she tuned out most talk of other people's comings and goings. Other people's comings and goings were not her business. Her business was one hundred and twelve steps, twice a day, and a green notebook, and a tin of letters from a man who had stopped answering, and a hallway she had furnished very carefully over ten long years.

The truck backed down toward the boathouse with a beeping she could hear even from inside the lamp room, where she had finished her rounds without knowing she'd finished them. From the height of it she could see the whole property: the sagging boathouse with its peeling green paint, the dock pilings like broken teeth, and a man getting out of the truck's cab.

He stood looking up at the water for a moment, the way you do when you've come a long way. Then he turned, and even at this distance she could see that he was tall, and that his hair was dark and wind-moved, and that he was looking up the hill now, at the lighthouse, at her window, with an expression she could not read at all.

Cyrus stepped back from the glass before he could see her looking, which was the first small safe step of many she would take that summer, and not nearly enough.

Down the hill, the truck door slammed, and the sound carried across the water like a starting gun.
