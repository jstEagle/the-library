## 10. Moths at the Porch Light

*Cyrus*

The porch light had been her grandmother's idea, back when the lighthouse still worked and the porch light was a redundancy nobody questioned. Now it was the only light on that stretch of the creek road that wasn't the moon, and every June it collected moths the way the town collected rumors—quietly, in numbers, until the whole thing hummed.

Cyrus sat on the top step with a mason jar of sweet tea going warm in her hands, watching the moths throw themselves at the glass bulb with a devotion that looked, from a distance, a lot like love and a lot like self-destruction, and she could not for the life of her decide which.

Wren was supposed to be here at seven.

It was 7:04, and she had counted every one of those minutes with the discipline of a woman who had spent ten years learning not to wait for anything. She'd rehearsed the evening on her walk down from the lighthouse: they would talk about the festival booklet, about the stage he'd been rebuilding down by the water, about the centennial committee's ongoing war over whether the fireworks should go off before or after the lighthouse relighting. Ordinary things. Safe things. The kind of conversation you could have with a neighbor.

The kind of conversation she was having increasingly less of, because somewhere in the last three weeks the conversations had stopped being about stages and booklets and started being about the shape of each other's days, and Cyrus had noticed—with the quiet alarm of a woman watching a tide come in farther than it ever had before—that she was telling him things. Real things. Things she hadn't written down even in the letters she still mailed every month to a correspondent who hadn't written back in two years.

She heard the truck before she saw it, the old diesel rattle carrying up the creek road, and her heart did something embarrassing that she chose not to examine.

He parked crooked, the way he always did, and came up the walk carrying a paper sack and a look on his face she couldn't read in the porch light—some weather moving behind his eyes that he hadn't let land yet.

"Peace offering," he said, holding up the sack. "Mrs. Odell was closing the bakery and gave me the day-old cinnamon rolls because I fixed her screen door on my lunch break. I feel like a man who's been paid in contraband."

"You fixed her screen door."

"It was hanging by one hinge. It was a crime against carpentry. I couldn't walk past it."

"You couldn't *walk past it*," Cyrus repeated, and shook her head, and moved her feet off the step so he could sit, and the space where her feet had been felt suddenly like a decision.

He sat. Their shoulders didn't touch, but the air between them did something that shoulders would have been proud of.

The moths threw themselves at the light. The creek talked to itself down in the dark. Wren opened the sack and split a cinnamon roll with the seriousness of a surgeon, and for a while they just ate, and it was so easy, so dangerously, catastrophically easy, that Cyrus kept waiting for the part where it got hard.

"You've got sugar—" he started, at the same moment she said, "The stage looks—"

They stopped. Laughed. The laugh did more damage than the almost-touch.

"Stage looks good," she finished. "The town's going to lose its mind. Half of them have never seen the boathouse look like anything but a ruin, and now you've gone and made it beautiful."

"It was beautiful. It was just sleeping." He brushed cinnamon off his fingers, and she watched his hands do it—the ink stain still living in the crease of his right thumb, the one she'd noticed the first week and never mentioned. "That's most of what I do, honestly. People think carpentry's about building. It's mostly about finding what's already there and convincing it to stand up straight."

"That's what you said about the stage."

"I say a lot of things twice. You should hear my opinions on cedar."

"I've heard your opinions on cedar. Twice."

"Then you know they're correct both times."

She was smiling. She could feel it happening, the full unguarded version, the one she kept for the lighthouse and the letters and nobody else, and she thought: *stop*. And then she thought: *why?* And the second thought was winning, had been winning for weeks, when Wren went quiet in a way that changed the temperature of the whole porch.

"Cyrus." He said her name carefully, like it was a load-bearing wall. "Can I tell you something that isn't about cedar?"

The moths hit the glass. The creek kept talking. Somewhere in her chest, a decade of Tuesdays sat up and paid attention.

"You can tell me anything," she said, which was true, which was the problem, which was the sentence she'd been building toward in her own letters for ten years without ever once saying it out loud to the right person.

He turned on the step to face her. In the porch light his eyes had gone the color of the water past the sandbar, that deep uncertain green, and she saw that his hands had gone still in his lap, that he was gathering himself the way she'd seen him gather himself before lifting a beam alone.

"I came to Junebug Creek for a reason," he said. "Not just the boathouse. I—" He exhaled. Started again. "I've spent a long time being someone's friend from a distance. And I got tired of it. I got tired of—"

His phone rang.

The sound cut through the porch like a gaff through water. Wren flinched—actually flinched, the whole line of him jumping—and when he pulled the phone out of his pocket and looked at the screen, Cyrus watched every ounce of the man who'd been about to say something important get packed away, folded up, stored, in the space of one ring.

"I'm sorry. I have to—" He was already standing. "It's my mother."

"Take it," Cyrus said. "Of course. Take it."

He stepped off the porch, down onto the walk, turned his back to her, and answered. And Cyrus, who had spent her whole life learning to read silences the way sailors read water, sat very still on her step and listened to half a conversation in the dark.

*"...Yes. I'm still in Junebug Creek."*

A pause. The creek filled it.

*"No. No, I know what the doctor said."*

Another pause, longer. His free hand came up and pressed against the back of his neck, and she saw his shoulders climb toward his ears and stay there.

*"I can be there tomorrow. First ferry. I'll be on the first ferry."*

His voice had changed. That was the thing she couldn't unhear. It hadn't gone loud or gone cold—it had gone *small*, the way a voice goes when it's talking to someone who used to be bigger than the whole world and is now, suddenly, mortal. She knew that voice. She'd used it herself, at nineteen, into a hospital phone, in a hallway that smelled like antiseptic and salt.

He hung up. He stood on her walkway with his back to her for three full breaths—she counted them, the way she counted everything—and when he turned around, the weather behind his eyes had landed, and he'd built a wall in front of it so fast and so smoothly that a stranger would never have known.

But she wasn't a stranger. She'd spent ten years loving a stranger through paper, and paper taught you everything about the architecture of what people hide.

"Is she okay?" Cyrus asked.

"Yeah." He said it too quickly. "Yeah, she's—she wants to see me. Family thing. It's nothing urgent, but she's in—" the smallest hitch, sanded smooth almost before it happened, "—in Asheville. It's a few hours. I should probably head up tonight so I'm not driving at dawn."

"Tonight," Cyrus said. "It's nearly nine."

"I know. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I was—" He gestured vaguely at the porch, at the cinnamon rolls, at the whole evening, the whole almost. "Can I take a rain check? On the thing I was going to say?"

"Sure," Cyrus said, and smiled, and her smile was steady and warm and cost her everything she had. "Family's family. Go."

He hesitated at the bottom of the steps. For one second—one long, humming second, a moth's whole lifetime against the glass—she thought he was going to come back up. She thought the wall was going to come down and the important thing was going to come out and whatever it was, whatever it cost, at least it would be the truth, at least it would be *them*.

"I'll be back before the festival," he said. "I promise."

"I know," she said.

He got in the truck. The diesel caught on the second try. She stood on her porch with the light humming behind her and watched his taillights shrink down the creek road until the dark took them, and then she stood there a while longer, because the moths were still throwing themselves at the bulb and she didn't want to go inside and be alone with what she'd heard.

*It's nothing urgent,* he'd said. But he'd packed the important thing away like a man packing for a long trip. And he'd said *Asheville*, and he'd said it the way you say a word you've prepared, and Cyrus Vane had spent ten years learning the difference between a sentence that was true and a sentence that was merely built to stand up.

She went inside. She didn't turn on the lamp. She sat at the kitchen table in the dark and pulled the shoebox of letters toward her—not to read, just to touch, the way you touch a railing—and she thought about the way his voice had gone small, and the way he'd flinched at the phone, and the way he'd said *I've spent a long time being someone's friend from a distance* right before the world had interrupted him, as if the world itself couldn't bear to let him say it.

*Nothing urgent,* she thought. *Then why does it feel like the tide going out?*

* * *

*Wren*

The first hour of the drive he didn't think at all. He let the road do the thinking, the white lines ticking past in the high beams, the windows down, the salt air slowly giving way to pine, and he drove the way he'd learned to drive in the worst years—fast enough to outrun the sentence he'd almost finished on her porch.

*I've spent a long time being someone's friend from a distance. And I got tired of it.*

He'd been going to say it. He'd been going to say all of it, or the beginning of all of it, the true part at least: that he'd read her letters like scripture for ten years, that he'd come to Junebug Creek because the distance had finally become unbearable, that the boathouse was an excuse the way a stage is an excuse for a play. He'd had the words loaded and ready. Ten years of drafts in a box in his living room, and every one of them had been rehearsal for that porch.

And then the phone had rung, and the universe—laughing, presumably—had said *not yet*, and then, when he'd seen the screen, had said *not ever, maybe*.

The hospice number had called at 8:40. Not his mother. Not yet. The nurse, with that gentle flat kindness they trained into them, saying his mother had asked for him, saying it was time to come if he was coming, saying it in the way people say things when they're not allowed to say *hurry*.

He'd looked back at the porch. He'd looked at Cyrus sitting in the moth-light with her jar of tea and her careful open face, and he'd thought: *if I tell her now, if I tell her any of it now, I'll never get through what's coming.* Because he knew himself. He knew that if he said the true thing and she took his hand, he would fold, he would stay, he would sit on that porch until the festival and the truth and the sea bag under the floorboards all came due at once, and she would learn everything in the worst possible order, from the worst possible angle.

So he'd lied.

*Asheville.* His mother was in Asheville, that much was true. But he'd said *family thing* and *nothing urgent*, and he'd watched her smile and believe him, and the lie had gone down like a nail driven through his own hand—clean, deliberate, and screaming the whole way.

By the second hour the road stopped working and the thinking started, and the thinking was merciless.

She'd smiled. That was the part that would keep him company the whole drive. She'd smiled and said *family's family, go*, with such easy grace, such trust, and he had driven away from it with a pocket full of falsehoods, and somewhere around the county line he'd realized that this was the shape of his whole life: every good thing arriving with a bill attached, every kindness he offered costing someone else the truth.

His brother had taught him that. His father had taught him that. His mother had taught it best of all, lying in her narrow bed with her confession sitting in a drawer three feet from her hands for eleven years, protecting a dead man's name at the cost of a living girl's.

*You're your father's son,* he thought, passing a sign for a town he didn't read. *You lie with a kind face.*

He turned the radio on and off twice. He stopped at a gas station and bought coffee he didn't drink. And somewhere past the last ridge, with the hospice lights coming up through the trees, he did the thing he'd been avoiding since the porch: he let himself imagine telling her everything.

Not the porch version. All of it. The wreck, the radio, the torn page, the sea bag, the ten years of Tuesdays, the letters in his own handwriting sitting in a shoebox on her kitchen table. He imagined her face doing what faces do. He imagined her counting backward through every warm evening they'd spent, looking for the lie in each one, and finding it, because it was in all of them, because he'd built the whole summer out of it.

And underneath the horror of that imagining, quiet as a keel, was the other thing. The thing he'd almost said.

*I got tired of the distance.*

He gripped the wheel. The hospice sign slid past. He parked in the visitor lot between a minivan and a hearse, and sat with his hands on the wheel, and let himself have exactly sixty seconds of the truth before he went inside to lie to his dying mother about being fine.

Then he went in.

* * *

*Cyrus*

She didn't sleep. She told herself she would and then she didn't, and around midnight she gave up the pretense and carried the shoebox out to the porch, because the porch was where the truth lived tonight, and the moths were still at it—faithful, doomed, magnificent—and she sat down on the top step with ten years of Tuesdays in her lap.

She didn't read them. She'd told Wren once that she reread them like scripture, and that was true, but tonight she just held them, one hand flat on the box lid, feeling the weight of a decade of kindness from someone she'd never seen.

*Family's family. Go.* She kept hearing her own voice, easy and warm. She kept seeing his face when he turned back at the bottom of the steps, that one suspended second where she'd been so sure.

*Nothing urgent.*

She thought about the way he'd flinched at the phone. Not startled—flinched, the way you flinch from a thing you've been dreading, a thing with a name you already know. She thought about *first ferry*, said with the flat certainty of a man reading a schedule he'd memorized long ago. She thought about the hitch in his voice, sanded smooth, and how good he was at sanding things smooth, how that was literally his trade.

And then, arriving quietly, the way the worst thoughts arrive—not like a wave but like a tide, everywhere at once, undeniable:

*He lies well. He lies like a man who's had practice. He lies like a man protecting something.*

She sat with that until the moon moved. She didn't let it become suspicion yet. She was too honest with herself for that—suspicion would come when it had evidence, and until then it was just fear wearing suspicion's clothes, fear that the first real thing in ten years was already slipping back out with the tide.

But she did one thing. She got up, went inside, and found the work order from the hardware store, the one from the week of the storm, the one with his handwriting on it, and she laid it on the kitchen table beside the shoebox.

She didn't compare them. Not tonight. She just put them side by side, the way you put two photographs of the same suspect on a corkboard, and she turned off the lamp, and she went to bed with the two papers touching each other in the dark.

Down the creek road, the boathouse stood empty, its new boards pale as bone in the moonlight, holding whatever it was holding under its floor.

And three hours north, a truck sat in a hospice lot while a man who had spent ten years being kind from a distance sat inside a small bright room, holding his mother's hand, lying with a kind face, while she looked up at him with eyes already halfway to forgiveness and said, "Did you tell her yet, baby? Did you tell the girl?"

And Wren Calloway, who had lied all night, could not lie to her.

"Not yet," he said.

His mother closed her eyes. "Then you'd better hurry," she said. "Kindness keeps, but guilt doesn't. And that girl's been waiting ten years for the truth—and she doesn't even know it yet."
