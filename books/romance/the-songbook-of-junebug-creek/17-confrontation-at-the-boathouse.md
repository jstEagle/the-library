## 17. Confrontation at the Boathouse

Cyrus had rehearsed a hundred openings on the walk down the creek road, and every one of them dissolved the moment she saw him.

Wren was in the boathouse doorway, sanding a length of trim, sleeves rolled to the elbow, sawdust catching the late light like pollen. He looked up when her boots hit the boards, and his whole face did something complicated—brightened, then braced, as if he'd been waiting for this moment for ten years and hoped it would never come.

She held out the sea bag's contents in both hands. The torn logbook page. The radio schematic with its circled wire. Her grandfather's name written in someone else's hand: *Vane — radio dead before she left the dock.*

"I found it," she said. "Under your floorboards."

The sander went silent. Somewhere out on the creek, a heron lifted off, wings slapping the water, and the sound seemed to take all the air in Junebug Creek with it.

"Cyrus." He set down the trim piece with exaggerated care, the way a man does when his hands are shaking and he doesn't want to admit it. "Let me explain."

"No." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You don't get to arrange this. You don't get to choose what I learn and when, the way you chose it for ten years. You're going to stand there and give me everything. All of it. In order. Starting with the letters."

* * *

Wren had imagined this conversation so many times that reality felt like a stranger wearing his imagination's clothes. In his head, he'd always been calmer. Braver. In his head, he'd led with the truth instead of being cornered by it.

"The first letter wasn't to you," he said. "Not exactly. It was to my brother."

He made himself keep going, because if he stopped now he would never start again.

"Daniel. My older brother. He crewed on boats out of Harrow Point—three towns over, where we grew up. The night your grandfather's boat went down, Daniel was on the water too. Fishing the same stretch. He heard the Mayday go out and got no answer from the Vane boat. Not static. Nothing. Like the radio had never been turned on." Wren's throat worked. "He went in after them anyway. His boat went down trying. They never found him. They never found your grandfather either."

Cyrus hadn't moved. She stood in the doorway with the evidence cradled against her chest like something wounded, and her eyes were the color of deep water in winter.

"My father," Wren said, "had serviced that radio two weeks earlier. There were records. Receipts. And when the inquiry started asking questions, my mother—" His voice cracked and he hated himself for it. "My mother found the schematic. Found proof that my father had signed off on work he never did. That the radio was dead before your grandfather ever left the dock. She burned what she could and hid the rest, and she told me it was to protect us. To protect *me*. I was sixteen. I believed her because believing her was easier than hating her."

"And the letters." Cyrus's voice was very quiet. "Where do the letters come in?"

"Daniel used to write. Letters home, mostly jokes, mostly complaints about the coffee. After he died, I couldn't stop writing to him. Letters with nowhere to go." Wren laughed, and it was an awful sound. "Then one day I wrote one to you instead. I'd seen you at the memorial—you were nineteen, standing at the rail of that lighthouse like you were going to step off it. I knew that look. I wore it myself. So I wrote to you, unsigned, and I told you things I couldn't say to anyone in Harrow Point, and somehow—" he gestured helplessly at the space between them, at ten years of Tuesdays "—somehow you wrote back."

"You knew." She took one step into the boathouse, and the floorboards announced her like a verdict. "All those years. You knew what happened to my grandfather while you were telling me to eat, to sleep, to write another page. You sat there with the truth under your tongue and fed me comfort instead."

"Yes."

"You let me grieve a man who abandoned ship. Who ran. That's what everyone said—that Elias Vane left his crew and drowned running. And you knew he stayed aboard. You knew he died trying to fix a radio your father broke, and you let me believe the lie because the truth was inconvenient for *you*."

"It was never convenient," Wren said, and his voice finally broke open. "Do you think I slept? Do you think there was one Tuesday in ten years I didn't sit down to write to you and see his name? I came here to find the courage to tell you. I bought the boathouse because it was the last place my brother's boat was seen. I tore up the floorboards looking for what my mother hid, because I thought—if I could put the truth in your hands, maybe the words would come easier. Maybe I could be forgiven by accident."

"That's not how forgiveness works."

"No," he agreed. "I know that now."

* * *

Cyrus looked at him—at the ink-stained fingers she had loved across a decade of paper, at the face she had learned like a second language—and felt the two halves of him grind against each other inside her chest until she couldn't tell which one was bleeding.

Her correspondent had pulled her back from the edge. Had written to her every week through the worst years of her life, patient and funny and fierce, had told her once: *Grief is just love with nowhere to land. Give it somewhere to land.* She had built her whole self on those letters. She had finished nothing, risked nothing, loved nothing outside their pages—and she had called that safety, but standing here now she understood it had been something else. It had been a room with one window, and he had been the view.

And the man who gave her that window was the son of the man who killed her grandfather.

"Coward," she said.

He flinched as if she'd struck him.

"Not because you lied." She set the papers down on his workbench, one by one, each page landing like a nail driven home. "Because you built the lies out of kindness. Every single one. You lied gently. You lied beautifully. You made lying look like love, and I lived in it for ten years and called it the best thing that ever happened to me." Her voice shook and she let it. "A coward built from kindness is still a coward, Wren. Maybe worse. Because I can hate a cruel man cleanly. How am I supposed to hate you?"

"You shouldn't," he said. "Hate my father. Hate my mother for the cover-up, if it helps. But Cyrus—the letters were the only true thing I ever had. Everything else in my life was managed and hidden and half-burned. The letters were the one place I didn't lie. Ask me anything I ever wrote you. Any line. I meant all of it."

"I know you meant it." The tears came then, hot and furious, and she wiped them away with the back of her wrist like a child. "That's what makes it unbearable. If you'd been lying, I could throw it all away. But you weren't. You were true and false in the same breath for ten years, and I can't separate them, and neither can you."

"Cyrus—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't say my name right now. You haven't earned it today."

The silence that followed was the longest of her life. Out on the water, the tide was turning; she could hear it, that soft vast exhale beneath everything, the sound she'd fallen asleep to since childhood. It sounded, tonight, like something ending.

"What happens now?" Wren asked. His voice was wrecked. He looked, suddenly, like the boy who must have been sixteen and drowning on dry land while his family burned the evidence around him, and she hated that she still saw it, still ached for it, even now.

"Now?" She picked up her grandfather's torn logbook page and folded it into her pocket with hands that wouldn't quite obey. "Now I'm going to walk up that road, and you are not going to follow me. Now I'm going to figure out who I am without the letters, because apparently I've never done that. And Wren—" she stopped at the door, not turning, because if she turned she would stay "—my grandmother has been asking me why I look like a woman carrying two funerals. Tomorrow I'm going to tell her the truth. All of it. Whatever it costs."

"She already knows more than you think," Wren said quietly.

Cyrus went still. "What did you say?"

But he only shook his head, eyes wet, jaw set—the same stubborn gentleness that had written to her through ten years of Tuesdays. "Go home, Cyrus. Talk to your grandmother. And when you decide what I am to you—" his voice dropped to almost nothing "—the door will be open. It's always been open. That's the one thing I never lied about."

She walked out into the blue dusk and up the creek road, and behind her the boathouse light burned in the window like a letter with no address, and she did not look back, because looking back was the one thing she could not survive.

Behind her, Wren Calloway sat down among his sawdust and his salvaged truths and put his head in his hands, and the tide kept turning, indifferent, taking the day out with it.

Up the hill, in a kitchen that smelled of cedar and old radio parts, a lamp was already lit. Her grandmother was waiting.
