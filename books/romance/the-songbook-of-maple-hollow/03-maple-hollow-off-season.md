## 3. Maple Hollow, Off Season

The last hour of the drive, Alden Frost turned off the radio and let Vermont do the talking.

It was mostly silence out there in late October—the kind of silence Nashville never managed, where the quiet itself seemed to have a pulse. The hills had gone the color of old brass. Farmhouses slumped behind stone walls older than the state itself, and every so often a church steeple rose up white against the gray sky like a held breath. He drove with the windows cracked even though the air coming through had teeth in it, because he wanted to smell it: woodsmoke and wet leaves and cold dirt, the exact chemistry of a place he'd spent fifteen years pretending hadn't rearranged his life.

His manager had thought the trip was a good idea. *Oral history material*, Marcus had said, tapping the contract with a manicured finger. *The publisher wants texture. Roots. You grew up two counties over—give them the landscape, give them the origin story.* And Alden had nodded along, because nodding along was most of what he did these days, a man in an expensive jacket agreeing to be himself on schedule.

What Marcus didn't know—and what Alden had not put in any email, any phone call, any of the careful summaries that kept his life legible to other people—was that the Hollow Inn sat at the end of this road, and that he had not been back since he was twenty-three years old.

He'd told himself the avoidance was practical. Who needed a B&B in a town of four hundred people? But honesty had a way of ambushing him in cars, alone, with nothing but the road ahead. The truth was simpler and uglier: he'd been afraid of the parlor. Of the piano that still lived there, presumably, under whatever dust fifteen years could lay down. Of the guest book. Of the staircase with the fourth step that sang.

Of her.

*Vesper Underhill.*

He said the name in his head the way you'd test a bruise—not because it felt good, but because some part of him needed to confirm it still hurt. It did. Fifteen years, three albums, one Grammy nomination, a fiancée whose smile photographed beautifully, and it still hurt, which struck him as either romantic or pathetic depending on how much whiskey was involved.

He came over the rise where the valley opened up and there it was: Maple Hollow, laid out along its creek like something spilled and never cleaned up. A general store with a porch. A white-steepled church. The sugarhouse with its tin cups still nailed to the maples, waiting for a spring that was six months away. And at the far end of the green, half-hidden by the elms that had gone bare as wire, the Hollow Inn—three stories of weathered clapboard, wraparound porch, windows catching the last flat light of the afternoon.

Alden pulled over before the bridge and just looked at it for a while.

He was twenty-three again in about four seconds flat. That was the thing about memory nobody warned you about—it wasn't archival, it wasn't fair, it hit you whole, unedited, with all the original sound.

* * *

They'd come in June, both of them broke, both of them twenty-three, riding up from New York in a borrowed station wagon that overheated twice on the Taconic. Vesper had navigated with a paper map folded into a square the size of her palm, and she'd read the roads aloud like lyrics, like the route itself was a song they were learning by ear. She'd had a habit then of narrating the world in melody—*that truck horn's a minor third, listen*—and he'd pretended to find it exhausting while secretly building his whole future on it.

The inn had been nearly empty that week. Off season, Ruthie had said, though June hardly seemed off anything; she'd explained that after foliage, the hollow went quiet, and after mud season, quieter still, and the inn survived on writers and fishermen and people running from things. She'd looked at the two of them—at their shared duffel bag, at the way Vesper's hand rested on Alden's arm like it had always lived there—and she'd given them the room at the top of the stairs for less than the posted rate, saying only, *You'll want the quiet.*

They'd wanted the quiet. They'd gotten everything else.

Three songs in seven days. He could still name them the way other men named their children. *Hollow Bell,* which started as a joke about the church bells being a quarter-tone flat and ended up being the truest thing either of them had ever written. *Paper Season,* which Vesper had begun humming while drying dishes in Ruthie's kitchen and which he'd finished at the piano at two in the morning while she slept upstairs, and which he'd never once performed without feeling like a thief. And the third one—the third one didn't have a real title yet when she left, just a working name scrawled in pencil above the staff: *Stay (unfinished)*, which in retrospect was less a title than a plea neither of them had the nerve to say out loud.

That parlor. God, that parlor. The upright piano with the dead key in the middle register—E above middle C, they'd called it the ghost note, and Vesper had insisted the song should keep it, should build around the absence, *because the missing part is part of the music, Alden, don't you get that yet?* He hadn't gotten it yet. He'd spent fifteen years getting it, one verse at a time.

She used to sit cross-legged on the floor with her back against the piano bench, writing in that notebook of hers, the one with the cover soft as cloth from handling. Her handwriting slanted forward like it was in a hurry to get somewhere. When she laughed she covered her mouth with the back of her wrist, and when she played she closed her eyes, and when she listened—to him, to the creek, to the rain on the porch roof—she did it with her whole body turned toward the sound like a plant toward light.

*A girl who could hear what songs wanted to be.* He'd dictated that line into his memoir session three weeks ago, into the little microphone on his laptop, to a ghostwriter he'd never met—a professional, Marcus promised, discreet, invisible, someone who would take the raw tape and make it literature. He'd said the line and felt clever, and then he'd sat alone in his Nashville kitchen feeling like a man who'd sold something he didn't own.

Because here was the arithmetic he'd never managed to balance: every good thing in his catalog traced back to that week. Not just the three songs—those were never recorded, never released, buried in a notebook he still kept in a fireproof box like evidence—but the *method*. The way he wrote now, leaving space in the arrangement, building around absences, trusting the listener to hear the missing E—that was hers. That was Maple Hollow. He'd taken the lesson and left the teacher, and the industry had handed him trophies for it.

And she had let him go. That was the part that curdled, the part nostalgia couldn't sweeten no matter how long he simmered it. Because he'd woken up on the eighth morning to an empty bed and a made-up side, and Ruthie had handed him a note in that hurrying handwriting—*Don't wait. You know why. —V*—and he had known why, or thought he had, and he'd been angry enough to spend a decade calling it betrayal instead of what it probably was.

She'd left because he'd gotten the call. That was the truth underneath everything. Three days before, his phone had rung in the parlor and it had been Hal Brennerman from Meridian Records, offering him the deal—the deal, the one every kid with a guitar dreamed about—and Alden had said yes before he'd finished hearing the terms. And Vesper had smiled and kissed him and said *of course, of course, this is your life starting,* and something in her voice had already begun packing.

He'd asked her to come with him. He was almost sure he'd asked her to come with him. Nashville, the apartment he'd find, the co-writing—he'd sketched it all out on a napkin at the general store, drawn her a whole future between the coffee ring and the ketchup stain. And she'd said, gently, in the voice she used for sad songs, *Alden. I'm nobody. You're about to be somebody. Those two things can't ride in the same car.*

He'd told her she was wrong. He'd told her love didn't have a billing order. And she'd smiled that smile and touched his face and said, *Ask me again when you believe it yourself.*

And then she was gone before he could ask her anything, ever again.

Fifteen years of telling that story had worn grooves in it, and the grooves all ran one direction: she left, she chose his career over him, she decided his future for him. It was a comfortable story. It fit on a talk show. But lately—in the small hours, in the space between the second bourbon and sleep—an uglier draft kept surfacing, one where he'd heard exactly what she said and believed exactly what she feared, and had gotten on the plane anyway, telling himself she'd change her mind, telling himself ambition was temporary and love was permanent and never once testing either theory until both had expired.

The letter. There had been a letter. He'd written it the night after she left, sitting right there in that parlor, four pages in pen because he wanted it to be permanent, wanting to say everything the napkin hadn't held. And then he'd folded it into an envelope, addressed it to her apartment in Brooklyn—the walk-up above the laundromat, he still knew the address, could still write it blind—and he had not sent it. He'd told himself he'd mail it from the city. Then he'd told himself the moment had passed. Then he'd stopped telling himself anything and just carried it, through five moves and two record deals and one engagement, a letter sealed and yellowing in a box with the notebook, addressed to a woman who had become, year by year, more myth than memory.

Some mornings he woke up furious at her. Some mornings, worse, he woke up understanding her completely.

* * *

The inn's parking lot held three cars: Ruthie's ancient Volvo with a *HOLLOW NOT HOLLOW-EYED* bumper sticker, a pickup with out-of-state plates, and—Alden slowed, frowned—a sedan with New York tags parked at an angle, like it had arrived in a hurry or a storm of indecision.

He killed the engine and sat for a moment with his hands on the wheel. Through the windshield the inn stood waiting, porch boards silvered, a wreath of dried bittersweet already hung on the door too early for the holidays, the way Ruthie always did things slightly ahead of the calendar, as if time were a guest who might otherwise arrive unannounced.

Time had arrived unannounced plenty of times since. It was arriving now.

He got out. The cold came up through the gravel and into his shoes, honest cold, clean cold, and somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and quit, and the church bell rang the quarter hour a quarter-tone flat, and Alden Frost stood in the road with his bag over his shoulder and laughed once, short and disbelieving, because of course. Of course it was still flat. Some things refused to be fixed, and he had built an entire career on the ones that wouldn't.

Ruthie opened the door before he knocked. She was seventy-something now, smaller than he remembered, her hair gone fully white and cut short as a hedge, but her eyes were the same—quick, amused, missing nothing, the eyes of a woman who had spent fifty years watching guests fall in and out of love in her parlor and had opinions about all of them.

"Well," she said. "Look what the road dragged in."

"Hello, Ruthie."

"You look tired," she said, stepping back to let him in. "Successful, but tired. Like a cake that got frosted too soon." She took his coat before he could object. "You're in the blue room. Top of the stairs. Mind the fourth step, she still sings."

"I remember."

"I expect you remember most things," Ruthie said, and there it was, that flicker of knowing, that terrible innkeeper's X-ray vision, before she turned and led him into the hall. "Kitchen's open till nine. Parlor's yours if you want it—I hear you're here to work. Recording voices, Marcus's girl said on the phone. Oral histories." She snorted softly. "Town'll talk. Half of them think a memoir is a medical procedure."

The hallway smelled the same—beeswax and woodsmoke and something floral and old, lavender maybe, baked into the walls themselves. The fourth step sang under his boot, a low wooden note, and he felt it in his chest like a hand pressed flat against him.

"Ruthie," he said, and she paused on the stairs without turning around. "Is the piano still—"

"Parlor's exactly as you left it," she said. "I don't move things that matter." And then she climbed on ahead of him, and he followed, and the house received him the way water receives a stone.

He meant to unpack first. He meant to call Marcus, review the recording equipment, be a professional about the whole enterprise. Instead his feet took him down the hall to the parlor door, and he stood there with his hand on the knob for a long moment, listening to the silence inside, the particular furnished silence of a room holding its breath.

The piano sat under the window exactly where it had always sat, dust-sheeted, patient. Late sun came through the glass and lay across the floorboards in long amber planks. On the side table by the fireplace, stacked with a decade of other guests' leavings, sat the guest book—cloth-bound, fat, its spine broken soft.

Alden crossed the room and lifted the dust sheet off the piano. The keys were yellowed ivory, the dead E exactly where he'd left it. He pressed it. No sound. Just the give of the mechanism, the absence, the missing part that was part of the music.

*Don't you get that yet?*

"Yeah," he said quietly, to the room, to the key, to the girl who wasn't in it. "I get it now."

Behind him, out in the hall, the front door opened—cold air, the jingle of the bell Ruthie had hung there sometime in the previous century—and a voice he did not recognize asked the innkeeper, politely, wearily, whether the road from the pass had reopened.

And Alden, standing in the amber light with his hand still on the silent key, felt the strangest premonition move through him, cold and certain as the draft from the door: that this trip was not going to be about oral history at all. That the past he'd come to record had plans of its own. That somewhere in this house, in this town, in this season of his life he'd assumed was safely archived—

Something was about to sing.
