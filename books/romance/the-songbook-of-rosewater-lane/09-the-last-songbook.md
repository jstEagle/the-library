## 9. The Last Songbook

**Paloma**

The songbook weighed almost nothing. That was the thing that kept undoing her—how light it was, this water-stained notebook with a cracked spine and a rose pressed flat between its final pages, when everything it might still hold felt enormous.

She'd carried it down from the attic at dawn, after Theo's truck finally made it up the lane through the melting slush, after Dana left with a look on her face that Paloma couldn't yet name. Now the kitchen table was buried under paper: her grandmother's will, three years of loan statements, the county's notice of delinquent penalties, and Hale's contract with its red flag of a clause about ephemera.

"Read it again," Theo said. He stood at the counter with his sleeves rolled, hair still damp from the walk through the wet vines, looking like a man who had slept badly and chosen to be here anyway.

Paloma opened the notebook to the first page. Her grandmother's handwriting looped across the top in faded violet ink: *For the girl who comes after me. Sing it all before you sell it.*

"I thought it was just sentiment," Paloma said. "A guilt trip from beyond the grave."

"Your grandmother didn't write guilt trips." Theo pulled out the chair beside her—not across, beside—and she noticed that too, the way he kept choosing proximity over distance now, as if he were making up for ten years of it one small decision at a time. "She wrote songs that made grown men cry at harvest dinners. Read me the marginalia."

The margins were crowded. Little notes in the same violet ink, some dated, some not: chord changes, a line crossed out and resurrected, a recipe for plum wine tucked against a verse about winter. And then, halfway through, a phrase underlined twice:

*The land keeps what the voice keeps.*

Paloma ran her thumb beneath it. "That's not a lyric."

"No," Theo said quietly. "It isn't."

---

**Theo**

He had spent the drive up Rosewater Lane rehearsing an apology and arrived instead with a theory. It was better than an apology. Apologies were currency he'd already spent; theories you could work with your hands.

"Look at the dates." He turned the notebook toward her, careful of the brittle pages. "Every entry with that phrase—the land keeps what the voice keeps—is dated within weeks of a performance. Harvest dinner, the church social, that festival they used to run on the lane. Your grandmother tied the songs to the property. Deliberately."

Paloma's breath went shallow. "You think it's legal language."

"I think your grandmother was smarter than half the lawyers I've met." He flipped toward the back, where the handwriting grew larger, more deliberate, as if she'd known someone would need to read it in bad light by candlelight or desperation. "And I think if she meant it as a condition—if keeping the songs meant keeping the land—then there has to be a matching clause somewhere official. Wills don't sing, Paloma. But they do reference."

She was already pulling the will across the table, scattering penalty notices. He watched her read, her lips moving slightly, and something in his chest turned over. Ten years ago he'd loved her like a firework—all noise and color and no staying power. This was different. This was wanting to sit at a kitchen table with someone until the light changed, decoding dead women's handwriting, because her fight had become his.

"Here." Her finger stopped on a paragraph near the end. She read aloud, slowly. "*To my granddaughter, the vineyard entire, with this charge: that the music shall not cease upon the land. Should my family keep singing—should the heritage of this place remain living and performed—the property shall carry a conservation easement in perpetuity, protecting it from development and forgiving all penalties accrued in its keeping.*"

The kitchen went very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside, meltwater dripped off the eaves in a slow rhythm, almost musical.

"A heritage designation," Theo said. "If the family keeps singing."

"If we keep singing." Paloma looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious and lit from inside. "Theo. The festival. The festival I've been fighting for—it's not just a fundraiser. It's the *condition*. If we hold it, if we prove the music is alive here—"

"The easement activates. Hale can't touch the land. The county forgives the penalties." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "She built you an escape hatch out of sheet music."

"She built it out of trust." Paloma's voice cracked. "She trusted me to keep singing even when I was selling her songbooks one by one to keep the lights on. God, Theo, I sold—" She stopped, pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"You didn't know."

"I sold pieces of her to buy time, and the whole time the answer was sitting in the attic waiting for me to read the margins."

Theo reached across the table and took her hand. She let him. That small surrender undid him more than the kiss had.

"Then we read every margin," he said. "Today. Right now. Because the festival filing deadline is Friday, and today is Wednesday."

---

**Paloma**

They worked like they used to work in the college library—knees nearly touching, passing pages back and forth, finishing each other's sentences without meaning to. By noon the kitchen table looked like a cartographer's nightmare: songbooks open to flagged pages, sticky notes bleeding color across the will, Theo's laptop glowing with the county code he'd pulled up, reading sections aloud in a voice gone hoarse.

"Section 12.4," he said. "'Heritage designations require documented continuous cultural practice on the property, evidenced by public performance within the preceding calendar year, together with a filed intent to establish an annual tradition.'" He looked up. "We don't have a public performance in the preceding year."

"We have recordings." Paloma was already digging through the cedar chest in the corner, the one she'd tripped over a hundred times and never opened properly. "Grandmother recorded everything. Harvest dinners, the solstice nights—there are tapes, Theo, dozens of them. Dates stamped on the labels."

"And the annual tradition?"

"The festival application. I filed the intent last month—it's why Hale got so aggressive, why he wanted the signing fast." The realization landed cold and clean. "He knew. Dana knew. They knew the festival could do this."

Theo went still. "Dana told me the deal was contingent on my endorsement. She never said anything about a heritage provision."

"Would you have believed me if she had? A week ago?" Paloma held his gaze. "I wouldn't have believed you."

"Fair," he admitted, and there was no bitterness in it, only the tired honesty of two people who had finally stopped pretending their history hadn't happened. "So we submit the tapes, the will, the songbooks, the festival intent. All of it. Before five o'clock Friday."

"Before five o'clock Friday," she echoed, and then, because hope was a dangerous vintage and she'd already drunk deep: "And if the county says the easement needs a court to interpret it? If Hale's lawyers stall us past the deadline?"

"Then we stall them right back." Theo pulled his phone from his pocket, scrolled, and set it on the table between them: a contact card. *Elena Ruiz-Whitaker, Land Use Attorney.* "She handled Marchetti Cellars' water rights. She owes me a favor, and she hates Marcus Hale personally. I'm calling her now."

Paloma stared at him. "You're staking your firm's attorney on my vineyard. After everything."

"After everything," he agreed, "I'm done hedging my investments." But his eyes said what his mouth was too careful to say outright, and she let herself hear it.

---

**Theo**

Elena called back at four o'clock Thursday with a voice like a drawn blade. "The provision is unusual but enforceable. Grandmother drafted it with a notary and a witness—both named in the codicil appendix. You'll want those signatures verified. And Theo? File everything simultaneously. County clerk, recorder's office, and a certified copy to Hale's counsel so they can't claim surprise later. Surprise is how developers win."

By Thursday night the farmhouse looked like a war room. Paloma digitized tape after tape, her grandmother's voice rising out of forty-year-old static—*Stand in the rows and sing to the fruit, mija, the vines remember*—while Theo drafted the petition, cross-referencing parcel numbers and penalty ledgers. At midnight they ate cold pizza standing up. At two a.m., Paloma fell asleep sitting upright, her cheek against the cedar chest, one hand still resting on the last songbook.

Theo covered her with the quilt from the attic—the one they'd found folded beside the notebooks—and worked another hour alone, reading her grandmother's marginalia by lamplight. Near the end of the book, in fresher ink, a single line he hadn't seen before:

*If the girl is reading this with a boy beside her, tell him the second verse was always his.*

His throat closed. He looked at her sleeping face, at the ink smudge on her cheekbone, and understood that Rosewater Lane had been deciding this long before either of them walked back into it.

---

**Paloma**

Friday came down to minutes.

The clerk's office closed at five. At 4:41 they stood at the counter while a woman named Doris—reading glasses on a chain, expression carved from bureaucratic granite—flipped through their submission: the will, the codicil, fourteen digitized performances spanning thirty years, the festival intent filing, the last songbook itself, which Paloma had refused to leave behind.

"Cultural practice documentation looks sufficient," Doris said. "Codicil witnesses check out. Conservation easement contingent on heritage designation, pending review." She stamped the first page. "You understand the developer's counsel may contest."

"They may," Theo said. "But they'll be contesting a stamped filing."

At 4:52, Doris stamped the last page and slid the receipt across the counter. "Congratulations. Rosewater Lane is now a protected heritage property, pending final review. Penalties suspended effective immediately."

Paloma took the receipt with both hands, the way you take communion.

Outside, the afternoon sun had broken through the week's clouds, and the lane glittered where the snowmelt ran in the ditches. Theo's hand found hers on the courthouse steps, and she laced her fingers through his without looking, without hesitating, the way her grandmother must have once laced hers around a guitar neck.

"It's not over," she said. "Hale will fight it. There'll be hearings, lawyers, months of—"

"There will," Theo agreed. "And we'll be there for every minute of it. Together."

Her phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, and her stomach dropped before she even read it:

*Ms. Dravenmoor. Marcus Hale. We should talk before my attorneys make this unpleasant. Tonight. I'm already on my way to the valley — and I've brought someone who insists on seeing you.*

Below it, a photo attachment loaded slowly over the weak signal.

Paloma stared at the screen, the receipt trembling in her other hand.

"Theo," she whispered. "It's my mother."
