## 2. The Deed With Two Names

The county records office smelled like toner and old rain, the way government buildings always do—like they've been quietly absorbing other people's problems for decades and have given up on ventilation. I signed in at the front desk with a pen chained to a clipboard, which felt about right. Everything in my life lately came with a chain attached.

"Voss," I told the clerk. "I'm here about an estate. Orchard property, out past the point."

She looked me up and down with the particular weariness of a woman who has seen every possible version of a man arriving to receive bad news, and she said, "Halloran's office is down the hall. He's expecting you."

*Expecting.* That word did a small, unpleasant dance in my chest. Grandmother Ruth had been dead eleven months, and in all that time her lawyer had never once been *expecting* me anywhere. Grief, it turns out, comes with paperwork, and paperwork comes with delays, and delays come with interest. I'd spent the better part of a year assuming the orchard was simply mine now—a sad, overgrown inheritance I'd get around to dealing with the way you get around to cleaning out a gutter: eventually, resentfully, with a ladder.

I was wrong about the ladder. I want that on the record.

* * *

Marcus Halloran was a compact man with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair and a handshake like he was testing fruit for ripeness. His office had one window overlooking the parking lot, and on his desk sat a folder so thick it could have stopped a bullet. I chose to believe that was a coincidence.

"Quentin Voss." He said my name like he was confirming a reservation. "Sit down. Can I get you coffee?"

"I own a café," I said. "I'm contractually obligated to be disappointed by other people's coffee."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way you sometimes see light move through a house when nobody's home. He didn't offer the coffee again.

"So," I said. "The orchard."

"The orchard." He opened the folder, and I braced myself for what I assumed was coming: back taxes, probably. A lien. Some bureaucratic tangle that would cost me money I didn't have, which was the standard currency of my life these days. Three missed payments on the café loan. I'd started keeping the bank's letters in a drawer under the register, unopened, the way you keep a hornet's nest in a shoebox and tell yourself it's fine.

Instead of a bill, Halloran slid a single sheet across the desk. A copy of a deed. Heavy paper, formal type, my grandmother's signature at the bottom in handwriting that still made something in my ribs ache—she'd written her grocery lists in that same slanted, certain hand, *butter, yeast, the good apples for Quentin.*

I read the deed. Then I read it again, because the first time clearly hadn't happened.

Two names.

**Ruth Voss-Halloran Estate, conveying to: Quentin James Voss and Marisol Elena Reyes, as joint tenants.**

The room did something strange. It got very quiet and very loud at the same time—the way it does when your ears are ringing. I could hear the clock on Halloran's wall, a cheap thing ticking like it was counting down to something, and I could hear nothing at all.

"That's a mistake," I said.

"It's not."

"My grandmother never met—" I stopped. Started again. "Marisol Reyes and I haven't spoken in twelve years. My grandmother knew that. She was there for most of it. She held my head, more or less, while I did it."

Halloran took off his glasses, folded them, set them on the folder with the care of a man who'd done this before and knew exactly where the landmine was buried. "Your grandmother amended her will fourteen months before she died. She was, by all accounts, entirely lucid. Her doctor signed off. So did I, for what it's worth—I notarized the amendment myself."

"Why?" It came out rougher than I intended. "Why would she do that?"

He looked at me over the folder with an expression I can only describe as *lawyerly compassion*, which is compassion with all the warmth professionally removed. "Mr. Voss, I represent the estate, not its motives. But if you're asking me to speculate—and clients always are, so I'll save you the trouble—your grandmother left a letter. It's addressed to both of you. You'll receive your copy when the estate formally transfers, or ninety days from today, whichever comes first."

"Ninety days."

"There's a standing offer on the property. Cormorant Development Group. They've been circling this parcel since before your grandmother passed—coastal acreage, water access, the kind of thing they turn into condos with names like *The Landing at Sandpiper Point.*" He said the name with the flat disgust of a man who'd grown up swimming off that same coast. "Their current offer is substantial. It would cover the back taxes, the estate's administrative costs, and leave a considerable remainder split between the two named parties."

"And if we don't sell?"

"Then you have ninety days to satisfy the tax obligation yourselves, or the county forces the sale at auction. Open bidding. Cormorant will be there, and they will outbid two private individuals without breaking stride." He leaned back. "There's also a clause. Either party can petition to buy out the other's share. Standard provision. One of you could walk away whole."

One of us could walk away whole. I turned that phrase over and found it familiar in a way that made my stomach drop, because walking away whole was my one genuine talent. Ask anyone. Ask Marisol Reyes, who could tell you all about the mechanics of it: how a man does it in the dark, without a note, how he lets a phone ring out, how he lets twelve years pile up on top of a silence until the silence becomes load-bearing architecture.

"When does the ninety days start?" I asked, because someone had to ask practical questions, and apparently it was going to be me, in this office, with my hands not entirely steady on my knees.

"From formal notification. Which requires both parties present." Halloran slid a second paper across the desk—an address, a phone number, printed in the same bland font as everything else that had ever ruined my day. "Ms. Reyes currently resides in Watsonville. She runs a small farm operation. Organic, I believe."

Of course she did. Of course she'd gone and become exactly the person she'd always said she wanted to be, while I'd stayed in town and shrunk, year by year, into a man who counted coins at two in the morning and called it a business plan.

"She doesn't know yet," I said.

"No."

"She's going to think I engineered this."

Halloran put his glasses back on, which I understood to mean our time was ending. "Mr. Voss, may I offer some unsolicited advice? Free of charge, which in this office makes it rare currency."

I nodded.

"Don't open with an explanation. Whatever you say first, she'll spend the rest of the meeting hearing it as a defense." He closed the folder. "Lead with the deed. Let the document do the talking. Documents are harder to argue with than men are."

* * *

I drove out to the point afterward instead of going back to the café, which was closed anyway on Tuesdays, which was a lie I told people—it wasn't closed, it just sold almost nothing on Tuesdays, and staying open meant watching the door not open. The orchard sat where it had always sat, behind the rusted gate with the sandpiper weathervane, and from the road I could see how far it had gone feral. Grass waist-high between the rows. Branches reaching out of the canopy at wrong angles, like hands forgotten mid-gesture. My grandmother's trees, untended for a year and a half, doing their stubborn best to die with dignity.

I didn't go in. I stood at the gate with my forearms on the cold metal and looked at the farmhouse roofline and thought about the last summer any of it had been simple.

I was twenty-three. Marisol was twenty-two, and she used to come out to the orchard after her shifts at the cannery, and we'd eat whatever fruit was ripe sitting on the tailgate of her truck with our legs swinging like we were kids, and she'd talk about the farm she was going to have someday—*someday* doing a lot of heavy lifting in her sentences—and I'd listen and think, with the arrogant certainty of a young man in love, that I had time. Time to tell my father no. Time to figure out how to love her without it costing anything.

Then I found the ledger in my father's desk drawer. The second ledger, the real one. And I learned that love costs exactly what it costs, and the price was due immediately, and the only currency I had was my own disappearance.

So I disappeared. Cleanly, even. No note, no fight, no scene. I let her believe what was easiest to believe—that I'd gotten bored, gotten ambitious, gotten offered something bigger down the coast. People believed it. People are generous that way; they'd rather invent a villain with a motive than accept a coward with a reason.

And now my dead grandmother, who had held my hand at my father's funeral and never once asked what I'd traded to pay for the flowers, had reached out from beyond the grave with a piece of heavy cream paper and written two names on it. Mine. Hers.

It was either the cruelest thing Ruth Voss ever did, or the kindest, and I suspected—with the dread sharpening in me now, settling into inevitability like weather—that it was neither. It was the most *her* thing she ever did. She'd spent eighty-one years believing that people were like orchards: that you could graft a broken branch back onto living wood if you were patient enough, and brutal enough, about the cutting.

My phone buzzed in my pocket. The bank, probably. Or a supplier. Or the universe, calling collect.

I didn't answer it. I looked at the address on the slip of paper in my hand—Watsonville, an hour south, a woman who ran a small organic farm and hadn't heard my voice in twelve years—and I understood, with terrible clarity, that the ninety days had already started somewhere inside me. That the deadline wasn't the county's. It had never been the county's.

Somewhere down the coast, Marisol Reyes was living her life, and in roughly forty-eight hours I was going to knock on her door holding a deed with her name on it, and she was going to look at me the way you look at a tide that brought something back that you'd already mourned and buried.

I got in the truck. I sat in the truck. Through the windshield, my grandmother's weathervane creaked on its rusted pin, turning toward the sea, pointing at nothing, pointing at everything.

"Okay, Grandma," I said to nobody. "You win. You always won."

I started the engine, and the orchard watched me go.
