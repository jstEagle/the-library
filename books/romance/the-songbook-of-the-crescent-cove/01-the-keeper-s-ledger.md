## 1. The Keeper's Ledger

The ferry from the mainland smelled of diesel and old rope, and Wren Voss spent the forty-minute crossing with her forehead against the salt-streaked glass, watching Crescent Cove assemble itself out of fog the way it always had—first the white scar of the lighthouse on its promontory, then the crooked line of cottages along the harbor road, then the water itself, that impossible green-gold she had tried and failed to describe in a hundred letters.

Ten years of letters. None of them signed.

She hadn't planned to come back. She'd planned to send a lawyer, sign whatever needed signing, and let the house on Gullwing Lane go to whoever wanted the trouble of a roof that leaked in three places. But then the call had come at two in the morning—her editor's voice careful and gentle in a way that made Wren think of hospitals—and she had found herself booking the first ferry north with nothing but a suitcase and the manuscript of a novel that was due in eleven weeks and still missing its ending.

Her grandfather had been dead for six weeks before anyone told her. That was what estrangement did; it turned grief into an administrative task. *Your grandfather passed. The estate needs settling. Whenever you can manage it.* As if he were a library book she'd checked out and forgotten to return.

The ferry bumped the dock. She stepped onto the planks of the harbor and the wind hit her like a hand on the chest—the same wind, the same cold slap of it, ten years gone and unchanged. The gulls were arguing over something near the fish market. Somewhere behind the shuttered ice cream stand, a radio played something tinny and old.

Wren stood very still and let the place recognize her.

*You came back,* said the cove, in the voice of every wave against every piling. *I knew you would.*

She walked up the hill to Gullwing Lane with her suitcase wheels rattling over cobblestones, past the cottage next door where a moving van sat with its back doors open, crates stacked on the gravel drive. She didn't look closely. She was saving all her courage for the house.

The key was where it had always been, under the third flowerpot on the left, though the pot had cracked and someone—her grandfather, presumably, in his last stubborn months—had wedged a folded square of duct tape beneath it. Inside, the house exhaled the smell of cedar and cold ash and time. Dust hung in the light through the east window like something suspended mid-decision.

"Hello," Wren said aloud, absurdly, to no one. Her voice sounded younger than she felt.

She set down her suitcase. She did not go upstairs to the bedroom where she'd spent every summer until she was twenty-two. She went instead to the room at the back of the house that they had never named—the small room with the desk facing the sea, where her grandfather had kept his accounts, his charts, his tide tables, and, she had assumed, nothing else.

The desk was oak, older than both of them, its surface worn pale where his forearms had rested for fifty years. On top of it lay a ledger bound in green cloth, fat as a Bible, its spine cracked into soft ridges from handling.

Wren knew that ledger. It was the keeper's log from the lighthouse, retired decades ago when the light went automatic, brought home by her grandfather the way retired men bring home their whole working lives. She had seen it a thousand times as a child, had drawn pictures in its margins once and been scolded gently, kindly, in a way she still remembered.

She opened it expecting tide tables and lamp-oil tallies.

What she found instead was her own handwriting looking back at her.

Not hers—not literally—but the words themselves, quoted line by line in her grandfather's narrow, upright script:

*"Some nights I think the sea is the only thing that listens without wanting anything back."*

Dated March 14th, nine years ago. Beneath it, in the same hand:

*To the writer of this letter: The sea listens, yes. But so do we who keep the lights. Your letter arrived Tuesday with the supply boat. I read it twice by lamplight. You are not alone out there, whoever you are.*

Wren sat down slowly in her grandfather's chair, which groaned under her exactly as it had groaned under him.

She knew those words. Of course she knew them. She had written them herself, at twenty-four, sitting on the floor of a rented flat in a city that had never learned her name, sealing the letter without a return address and mailing it to a lighthouse because she couldn't bear to mail it to a person. *Dear Keeper,* she had begun, every time. *Dear Keeper, who watches the water so the rest of us don't have to.*

It had been a game at first. A ritual. A way of writing honestly when honesty had nowhere else to go. She had told the lighthouse about her mother's illness, about the agent who rejected her manuscript with a form letter, about the man who left and the rain that wouldn't stop and the novel she was afraid to finish because finishing meant someone might read it. She had written maybe four letters a year. Sometimes more. She had never expected an answer. Lighthouses didn't answer. That was the entire point of them—they stood, they shone, they asked nothing.

But here, in her grandfather's ledger, in ink that had faded from black to brown across a decade, was proof that someone had answered. Every one.

She turned pages with hands that had gone unsteady. The early replies were her grandfather's—she recognized the hand from grocery lists and birthday cards, the capital letters slightly too large, the periods pressed hard enough to dent the page. He had answered her anonymously for years. *Dear Writer. Dear Friend Across the Water. Dear One Who Signs No Name.* He had never once revealed himself. Never once mentioned it when she visited in summers, drinking coffee in this very kitchen while her letters sat answered in the drawer between them.

Why? The question rose in her like water in a lock chamber. Why keep the secret? Why let her pour her heart to a stranger when the stranger was thirty feet away?

Then, seven years ago, the handwriting changed.

The new hand was different entirely—looser, quicker, slanting forward as if impatient to reach the end of the sentence. The ink changed too, a warmer black, and the margins filled with small annotations that weren't replies at all but something else: musical notation. Little runs of notes penciled beside her own words, as if her sentences had been melodies waiting to be transcribed.

*To the writer:* this new hand began, in reply to a letter Wren remembered writing the winter her mother died. *"You said the silence after a phone call is the loudest sound you know. I have been thinking about that silence for three days. I think it has a key—something minor, obviously, but not despairing. More like the moment before dawn when the birds haven't decided yet. I've written eight bars trying to find it. They aren't good yet. But your letter is the reason they exist."*

Wren pressed her palm flat against the page.

Someone else had taken up the correspondence. Someone who wasn't her grandfather. Someone who lived close enough to receive her letters—whoever collected the mail at the cove must have passed them along, or perhaps her grandfather had forwarded them before he grew too ill—and who had answered not with comfort exactly, but with *attention*. With the particular, devastating attention of a person who read a sentence about silence and heard music in it.

She read on. The replies came faster now, sometimes two for one of hers, and the marginal notations multiplied—phrases, fragments, whole systems of little marks she couldn't decipher. Once, beside a paragraph where Wren had described walking the harbor road at low tide, the unknown hand had written simply: *This is the theme. This is the whole thing. I've been looking for this for years.*

Beside a letter in which she had confessed, at three in the morning, that she was afraid she would die with all her books still inside her, the reply ran:

*Then write one book so true that it outlives the fear. I will be listening. I am always listening. It is the only talent I have ever been sure of.*

Wren laughed—a strange, cracked sound in the empty house—and wiped her face with the heel of her hand.

Who are you? she thought at the page. Who are you, who answered me for seven years and never said, who wrote music in my margins like my grief was a score?

The last entry was dated five months ago. Her final letter—the one she'd almost not sent, the one where she'd written: *I think I'm going to finish the novel. I think I'm finally brave enough. If this lighthouse could hold a light for strangers for a hundred years, maybe I can hold one for myself.*

Beneath it, in that quick slanting hand, only four words:

*I always knew you would.*

And below that, smaller, almost hidden in the margin, a single line of notation with a word beneath it that Wren had to lean close to read, her breath fogging the old paper:

*Crescent Cove — finale.*

The house ticked and settled around her. Out the window, the lighthouse stood white against the gray afternoon, patient as ever, keeping its ancient stupid beautiful silence. And next door, faintly, through the wall and the wind, Wren heard a sound she hadn't heard in this cove since childhood: a piano, being tuned, note by searching note, like someone feeling their way toward a melody they already knew by heart.

She looked down at the ledger, at ten years of her own life reflected back in a stranger's handwriting, and understood that she had not come home to settle an estate at all.

She had come home to answer a letter.
