## 8. The Dedication Page

The manuscript had been finished for three weeks before I admitted it was a lie.

I sat at my grandfather's old desk in the lighthouse keeper's cottage—my cottage now, though the word *mine* still snagged on something every time I said it—and read the ending I'd written for Marguerite and the cartographer. On the page, they forgave each other in tidy paragraphs. They exchanged confessions like currency, exact amounts, no debts outstanding. The cartographer told her everything; she wept exactly twice; the tide came in on schedule.

It was a beautiful lie. My editor would have loved it.

I closed the laptop and looked out the window at the lighthouse, white against a sky the color of unpolished pewter. October was leaning into November. The gulls had gone quiet in the way they did before weather, and somewhere down the lane I could hear Theo's piano through his open windows—he'd taken to leaving them open even as the cold came in, as if he were storing up sound the way other men stored firewood.

Three weeks of finished manuscript. And all I could think about was the dedication page, which was still blank, which had been blank since my first book, because dedications require a name, and there are some truths you can't type until you've said them out loud to their owner.

*For the one who answered.*

I typed it. Deleted it. Typed it again. Let it sit there on its own line like a lit match.

Then I deleted the last chapter instead.

* * *

Rewriting an ending under deadline is a particular kind of madness. My editor, Dana, had sent four emails that morning with escalating punctuation: a period, an exclamation point, a question mark, and finally just my name in the subject line, which is how editors say *I am begging you.* The final files were due Friday. It was Tuesday.

But the old ending kept looking at me from inside the document like a guest who knows they've overstayed. Marguerite forgiving the cartographer without cost. Without the vertigo of it—the way forgiveness actually feels when you discover the person you loved was also someone you never met, and you have to decide whether the stranger and the beloved can be the same man.

I had decided nothing. That was the problem. That was why the ending rang false.

So I wrote the truth sideways, the way novelists do when the direct road is washed out. I gave Marguerite a decade of unsigned letters. I gave her a ledger in a dead man's hand and then a second hand, younger, more careful, the ink changing color over the years the way a voice changes. I wrote the scene where she quotes her own words back to him and watches his face do the thing Theo's face had done in the kitchen ten days ago—that flicker, that caught breath, the look of a man who has been found out by his own heart.

And then I stopped, cursor blinking, because the scene on my screen required an answer I hadn't gotten yet in life.

Theo had confessed the letters. Everything else—the two hands in the ledger, the first reply in my grandfather's careful script, the ten years of him—I knew now. What I didn't know was what came after confession, whether tenderness could be rebuilt on a foundation that had been poured in secret. In the novel, I could make it come out right. In the cottage down the lane, I had to find out.

I saved the file. I printed the draft—old habit, my grandfather's habit, paper you could hold—and I carried it down the lane through wind that smelled of iron and coming rain.

* * *

Theo answered the door with sawdust on his sleeves. He'd been building something in the back room—I'd heard hammering for days, rhythmic, patient—and behind him I could see the piano crate pried open at last, its lid leaning against the wall like a door left ajar.

"I need you to read something," I said. "Before anyone else does."

He went very still, the way he did sometimes, as if listening were a whole-body occupation. "The book?"

"The ending. I rewrote it." I held out the pages. "It's not subtle. You'll know."

"Wren." He took the stack but didn't look down at it. His thumb moved along the edge of the paper, feeling the weight of it, and something in his face made my chest ache—a hunger so carefully governed it looked almost like calm. "You're sure?"

"No," I said honestly. "That's rather the point."

He sat by the window where the light was best. I watched him read, and I will carry the watching of it for the rest of my life: the way his lips parted at the letter scene, the way his hand flattened against his own sternum when Marguerite quoted the line back—*tell me about the sea from where you actually stand*—the line I had once mailed to a lighthouse in an envelope with no return address. He read the way some people pray.

When he reached the end—the new end, the unfinished end, the page where Marguerite stands in the doorway waiting for an answer the text withholds—he looked up at me with wet eyes and didn't pretend otherwise.

"It's true," he said. "All of it. Even the parts you invented."

"Especially those."

He laughed, and it broke something loose in both of us, and for a moment the room was warm and simple and I thought: *this is the answer, this is what comes after.*

Then he set the pages down, aligned their edges with unnecessary precision, and said, "There's something I should tell you before you send this to your editor."

The temperature of the room changed. I felt it the way you feel a door open onto winter.

"You remember," he said, "at the concert. When my hands kept going after—" He stopped. Started again. He was a composer; he knew how to structure a difficult passage, and I realized with a lurch that he was structuring this one, choosing his key, counting his bars. "I haven't been entirely honest about why I moved here. Why now. Why the symphony had to be finished *now*."

"Theo—"

"My hearing is failing." The words came out level and terrible, a sentence he had clearly rehearsed until it lost its power to wound him, or so he'd hoped. "It's been failing for two years. The audiologist says the window on usable hearing is months, not years. Some days already, sound arrives like it's coming through water. At the concert, mid-movement, it simply—left. I finished from memory. From muscle." He looked at his hands as if they belonged to a stranger who had done him a great kindness. "I moved to Crescent Cove to finish the symphony while I could still hear what I was writing. Your symphony. Our symphony. I couldn't tell you because—" His jaw worked. "Because everyone in your letters was whole. The keeper of your words shouldn't be broken."

"Theo." I crossed the room and took his face in my hands, made him look at me. "Listen to me. Listen—" and I heard the irony of it even as I said it, and so did he, and we laughed again, badly, both of us crying now. "You are not broken. You are the most *intact* person I have ever known. Do you understand what you did at that concert? You finished a movement you couldn't hear. For an audience. Because you refused to let the music stop."

"I refused to let *you* stop," he said quietly. "Every note is one of your sentences. I've been setting you to music for ten years, Wren. I just didn't have the courage to sign my work."

I kissed him then—not the careful kiss of the night after the concert, but the other kind, the kind that admits what it wants. When we broke apart, his forehead rested against mine, and I could feel his breath going shallow and fast.

Then he pulled back, and I saw the grief arrive behind his eyes, the thing he'd been holding at bay through all of it.

"There's more," he said. "The part I can't figure out how to say."

"So say it badly. I'll take it badly said from you over anything well said from anyone else."

He picked up my printed pages again. Held them up. "This," he said, and his voice cracked clean through. "Your words. Your handwriting—I fell in love with your handwriting before I ever knew your face, do you know that? Ten years ago, a letter came to the lighthouse, and I recognized the hand from your grandfather's ledger, and I thought, whoever writes like this thinks in music." He turned the pages over, gently, like they were sleeping things. "And someday soon I'm going to hold a page like this and see nothing. Not blurred. Nothing. Braille takes years to learn and I'm spending all my hours on the score. So when you ask me to read your draft—" He pressed the manuscript back into my hands and folded my fingers over it. "Read it to me. Now, while I can. And then forgive me for the day I can't."

The wind had risen while we talked. It pressed at the windows, worried the loose shingle above the door, and out across the cove the water had gone the dark hammered-silver color that meant the storm the forecasters had been naming all week was no longer a rumor.

I looked down at the manuscript in my hands. Two hundred and eighty pages of a woman learning to love a man she'd only ever met on paper. A blank dedication line. A deadline on Friday.

"Then we don't waste the hearing we have," I said. "Sit down. I'm reading you the whole thing, start to finish, tonight. And tomorrow you play me what you've orchestrated. We trade. Word for note. While we still can."

"That could take all night."

"It's going to take all night," I agreed. "And probably the next one too."

He smiled—wet-eyed, wrecked, radiant—and went to light the lamp against the early dark. Outside, the first hard drops of rain began ticking against the glass, and far off, out past the point, the sea was gathering itself into something enormous.

Neither of us knew yet that the storm would reach us before Friday did. Neither of us knew that in forty-eight hours I would be standing in a rising hurricane with his final pages tearing loose into the wind, my hand pressed to his throat, learning his music the only way left to learn it.

That night, I only knew that I read aloud until my voice went rough, and he listened with his eyes closed and his palm flat against the table, keeping time.
