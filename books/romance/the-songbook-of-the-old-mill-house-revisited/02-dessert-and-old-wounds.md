## 2. Dessert and Old Wounds

The Blackwood house smelled of beeswax and old money, the kind of scent that got into your clothes and stayed there like a judgment. Marisol had dressed carefully — a rust-colored dress that made her feel like autumn itself, confident and warm — but standing in the entrance hall beneath a chandelier that could have paid off her bakery's ovens twice over, she felt like a croissant left out in the rain.

Isandro's hand found the small of her back. "Breathe," he murmured. "They're just people."

"People with a portrait gallery," she whispered back. The staircase wall was lined with Blackwoods going back two centuries, all of them painted with the same narrow eyes, the same mouths set as if they'd just heard bad news about the weather and intended to bear it nobly.

"Rule one," he said. "Don't let them see you flinch."

"Rule one for me or for you?"

His jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "Both."

* * *

Dinner was served in a room long enough to land small aircraft. Marisol counted nineteen place settings, each laid with silver heavy enough to require a permit. She was seated beside Isandro, which she understood within minutes was less an honor than a quarantine — the couple nobody wanted to catch alone.

At the far end, an aunt with a voice like cut glass was holding court. "And how is the vineyard, Isandro? Still... soldiering on?"

"It's producing," Isandro said evenly. "The reserve grenache scored ninety-one points last month."

"Ninety-one." The aunt turned to her neighbor as though sharing a private joke with the entire table. "How industrious. Playing at farming."

A man Marisol took for an uncle — broad, ruddy, with his father's portrait chin — leaned back in his chair. "You know what Grandmother would say. She always said the vineyard would be the death of this family. Looks like she was right, only slower than she expected."

Laughter moved down the table like a draft under a door. Not loud laughter. Worse — the polite kind, the kind that leaves no fingerprints.

Marisol watched Isandro take it. Watched him straighten his spine by degrees, each insult absorbed into some internal ledger he clearly kept balanced through sheer will. His hand under the tablecloth had gone still on his knee, and she recognized that stillness. It was the stillness of someone who had learned young that reacting cost more than it bought. She knew it from her own body, from every health inspection conducted with a smirk, every landlord who called her *sweetheart* while raising the rent.

"So tell us," said a cousin across the table, a woman with a wine glass she never drank from, "when does the bank come for the mill? Christmas? We're taking bets. Aunt Lucia has October."

"Aunt Lucia is optimistic," said the uncle, and the table laughed again.

Something in Marisol's chest went hot.

She hadn't planned to speak. Her contract — three dates, one reunion, a favor from the lawyer cousin — specified nothing about defending him, because neither of them had imagined it necessary. But the dessert course arrived then, a poached pear so elegant it looked embarrassed to be eaten, and Marisol looked at it, and looked at these people who had never in their lives eaten anything they hadn't hired someone else to make, and heard herself begin.

"You know what I noticed?" she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Nineteen faces turned. "Nobody at this table has asked him a single question tonight. Not about the harvest. Not about the wine. You've asked him about his debts four times — I counted — and about his work zero times." She set down her spoon. "I own a bakery. A small one. When my oven broke last winter, I couldn't afford to replace it, so I baked everything in shifts on one working deck for six weeks, and I cried twice, and I kept the doors open. And you know what? Every person who walked in treated me like I was running something. Because I was." She lifted her glass slightly. "He's not drowning. He's carrying a hundred-year-old estate on his back while this family stands around placing bets on when his arms give out. That's not wit. That's just cruelty with better tableware."

Silence. The cut-glass aunt opened her mouth and closed it. Somewhere a clock ticked with enormous self-satisfaction.

Marisol felt the heat rise in her face only after the words were out — too late, too sincere, too much. This wasn't in the contract. Fake girlfriends defended fake boyfriends from exes and nosy coworkers; they did not detonate dinner tables.

Under the table, Isandro's hand found hers. He didn't squeeze. He just held it, warm and shaking very slightly, and when she dared look at him, he was looking at her the way she'd once seen a man look at bread coming out of the oven at dawn — like something he'd stopped believing would ever be real again.

"Well," said the uncle, into the quiet. "She's certainly... spirited."

"She's right," Isandro said quietly. It carried the length of the table anyway. "That's the uncomfortable part."

* * *

Afterward, they escaped through French doors onto a terrace that overlooked the valley, the vineyard rows falling away below them in dark green stitches, the mill house a pale smudge in the middle distance where the creek bent. Marisol pressed cold hands against her burning cheeks.

"I'm sorry," she said. "That was — I broke character. That comes out of my fee."

"Don't." He said it fast, rough. Then softer: "Don't apologize. Nobody has ever—" He stopped, looked out at the dark, started again. "Six years. Six years of dinners like that, and I had a whole system. Jokes that don't reach my eyes. Leaving before coffee. Tonight I just sat there and let you—"

"Go down in flames?"

"Stand up." He turned to her. In the light spilling from the windows behind, his face was open in a way she suspected it almost never was. "Marisol. Why did you do that?"

Because you looked like me, she thought. Because cruelty recognizes cruelty, and I couldn't watch it happen to someone else from inside its blast radius and stay quiet. Because somewhere between the appetizers and the pears, pretending stopped feeling like pretending.

"Professional obligation," she said. "A convincing girlfriend defends her investment."

"Right." He smiled, and it didn't reach his eyes either, but for once that felt like honesty instead of armor. "Your investment."

The night air smelled of crushed grapes and river water. Below them, a light burned in the mill house — a caretaker's lamp, maybe, or just the moon caught in old glass. Marisol followed his gaze.

"That's it, isn't it," she said. "The famous mill house. The one your family won't stop talking about."

"The one my family won't stop talking about," he agreed. "Grandmother left it in trust. There was a lawsuit years ago — before my time — over the deed. My uncle's side won. The property, and the parcels around it, have been Blackwood holdings ever since." He said it lightly, tour-guide casual, gesturing toward the creek bend. "Everything from the mill down to the old commercial strip along River Road."

Marisol went cold in a way the evening air couldn't explain.

"The commercial strip," she repeated.

"Mm. Two blocks of it. Why?"

"My bakery," she said slowly, "is on River Road. Between the laundromat and the shuttered hardware store."

The words seemed to drop between them like stones into still water. She watched him go still — that same careful stillness from the dinner table, but different now, aimed inward.

"Number what?" he asked.

"Forty-six. Why are you looking at me like that?"

"Forty-six." He said it the way she might say *the oven's dead*. "Marisol, forty-six River Road is on the parcel map. It's part of what the deed covers. My uncle's claim — the one the courts upheld — runs right through your lease. If anyone ever successfully challenges that deed..." He trailed off, and she watched him understand it a half-second before she did, watched the color leave his face.

"If someone challenges it, my building changes hands," she finished. "Or my lease gets voided entirely. Three weeks, Isandro. My lease expires in three weeks, and I need your cousin the lawyer because I'm already fighting the landlord, and now you're telling me my bakery is sitting on top of a thirty-year-old Blackwood land grab?"

"I'm telling you it's sitting on top of a disputed one." His voice had gone careful, lawyer-adjacent, and she hated it. "There's a difference."

"Is there? From where I'm standing?"

"From where you're standing, your whole life is on that block. From where I'm standing—" He stopped. Pressed his knuckles against his mouth. Looked out at the vineyard he was mortgaged to the roots to keep, and at the mill house glowing faintly in the dark between them like a coin neither of them could pick up without the other bleeding.

"From where you're standing," Marisol said quietly, "winning that lawsuit might be the only thing that saves the vineyard. And losing it might be the only thing that saves my bakery."

Neither of them said anything. The creek murmured somewhere below, indifferent, patient, the way water always is about other people's disasters.

"We should go back inside," Isandro said finally. "Before they notice we've been gone too long. Before anyone asks questions."

But as they turned toward the lighted doors, Marisol caught his arm. "Isandro. Whatever you find out about that deed — whatever your family did or didn't do — you'll tell me? Before you decide anything?"

He held her gaze for a long moment. Behind them, the dinner party hummed, oblivious, beautiful, cruel. Ahead of them lay the dark valley and the pale smudge of the mill house and a truth neither of them could yet see the shape of.

"I promise," he said.

And later — much later, when everything had burned down and been rebuilt — she would remember that he'd hesitated first, just barely, just long enough for the lie to take root.
