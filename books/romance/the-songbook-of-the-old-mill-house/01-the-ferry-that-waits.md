## 1. The Ferry That Waits

The ferry to the Old Mill House ran twice a day, and Kestrel Pemberton had timed his arrival to the second crossing so that no one would be there to meet him. He wanted the approach alone: the grey water, the island rising out of the mist like something remembered rather than seen, the mill house with its one lit window. He had rehearsed the whole crossing in his head — the smile, the handshake, the easy lie — *I needed quiet to practice, Mum, that's all, the city was too loud* — and he found that even alone, even to no audience at all, the smile sat on his face like something nailed there.

His cello case lay across his knees. He hadn't opened it in eleven weeks. He knew the number precisely because he counted the way other men counted days sober: eleven weeks since the Barbican, since the Dvořák died in his hands mid-phrase, since four hundred people watched his left arm simply stop obeying him, the bow hovering over the strings like a bird that had forgotten what air was for. The silence had lasted nine seconds. He had counted those too.

The boat bumped the dock. He looked up expecting the pilot to call out instructions and found instead a man crouched at the rail's end, greasing something, sleeves rolled over forearms the colour of walnut, dark hair escaping a knit cap. He was humming. Not a song Kestrel knew — something modal, wandering, built on a rising fourth that kept reaching for a resolution and deliberately, almost teasingly, refusing it.

"You'll want to mind the gap," the man said, without looking up. "Third plank from the end lies about itself."

"Lies about itself," Kestrel repeated.

"Gives you dry footing all the way across, then drops an inch when you're not expecting. Like most things that seem reliable." He stood, wiped his hands on a rag that had clearly lost the war against grease years ago, and offered one anyway — the left one, palm up, an offering rather than a shake. "Wren Calloway. I run the boat. And the other boat. We only have the one, but I like to say it like it's a fleet."

"Kestrel Pemberton."

Something moved through Wren's face — recognition, but gentle, the kind that doesn't ask for an autograph. "The cellist."

"The former cellist."

"I didn't know cello had a former tense." He took Kestrel's case before Kestrel could protest, lifting it with an ease that suggested he hauled engines for sport, and set it on the dock with exaggerated care. "Mind the third plank."

Kestrel crossed. The third plank dropped an inch. He decided not to mention it.

* * *

The mill house had belonged to his grandmother, and then to no one, and now to him in the way a debt belongs to you. It smelled of salt and cold stone and, faintly, of the lavender his grandmother had hung in bundles from the beams, long since gone to dust but still insisting on itself. He walked the rooms that first evening, touching nothing, rehearsing.

Tomorrow the family arrived. His mother first, on the morning boat, with her luggage and her expectations and her voice like a tuning fork struck against bone. Then his sister Margot, her husband, the twins, his aunt Cressida who would ask about the Barbican with the delicacy of a woman defusing a bomb she'd planted herself. He stood in the kitchen and said the excuses out loud, testing them like notes.

*I've been composing. Actually composing, for once.* Weak — his mother would ask to see the score.

*The doctors say rest.* Weaker — his mother would ask which doctors, and whether they were the best.

*I'm fine.* Catastrophic. His mother had a gift for hearing *fine* the way a conductor hears a flat note — instantly, and with visible pain.

The problem, he admitted to the cold hearth, was that every excuse was a solo performance, and he had lost faith in solos. What he needed was a duet. Someone standing beside him, sharing the weight of the family's attention, so that the questions would scatter between two targets. Someone whose hand he could hold when his mother's eyes went surgical.

The thought arrived fully formed and slightly shameful: *I need a boyfriend.*

He laughed — one short, ugly note — and the sound bounced off the stone and came back sounding like the nine seconds of silence at the Barbican. He went to bed and did not sleep.

* * *

He found Wren the next morning in a boathouse that smelled of diesel and tar, flat on his back under an outboard motor with a radio playing something orchestral and badly received, all crackle and swooning strings. Kestrel stood in the doorway for a full minute before the man slid out, saw him, and clicked off the radio with his foot.

"Mr. Pemberton. The mill house treating you well, or are you here to complain about the third plank?"

"The third plank and I have reached an understanding." Kestrel hesitated. Everything he'd composed on the walk down — charming, indirect, plausible — evaporated. What came out instead was the truth, stripped: "I need to hire you."

Wren sat up, wiping his hands. "The ferry's included in the—"

"Not the ferry." Kestrel heard his own voice go thin and pressed on before courage left. "My family arrives this afternoon. My mother. And I have spent eleven weeks being the man who stopped playing in front of four hundred people, and I cannot — I am not able to stand in front of them alone and be asked how I am." He made himself look at Wren. "I'd like to hire you to be my boyfriend. For the weekend."

The silence that followed had a texture to it — engine oil, gull-cry, the tick of cooling metal. Wren's expression didn't change so much as deepen, like water going from shallow to deep over the same stone.

"That," Wren said at last, "is the strangest commission I've had, and last summer a woman paid me to scatter her husband's ashes from the boat while I played accordion, which he'd apparently hated."

"I'll pay whatever the ferryman's rate is. Double it."

"See, that's the problem." Wren got to his feet, unhurried, and put the kettle on a camp stove that seemed to live in the boathouse for exactly this purpose. "You're treating it like a fee. And I don't think you've thought through what it actually involves."

"Hand-holding. Some history. Endurance."

"Endurance," Wren repeated, delighted. He spooned tea into two chipped mugs. "All right. But if I'm going to lie to your mother's face — and I assume she has a face worth lying to — I need it to be a good lie. Good lies are mostly true. So." He handed Kestrel a mug and looked at him with eyes the grey-green of the water off the dock. "Tell me something real. One real thing, and we'll build the weekend on it."

Kestrel wrapped his hands around the mug. Steam rose between them. He thought of all the rehearsed sentences, the polished deflections, and found that the only real thing he had was the one he never said.

"I haven't opened my cello in eleven weeks," he said. "It's sitting in the front hall like a coffin I'm keeping for someone."

Wren didn't flinch, didn't soften with pity. He just nodded slowly, as though Kestrel had confirmed something he'd suspected since the dock. "There," he said. "That's the true thing. We'll tell her you've been resting your hands and your heart, and that I'm the reason you came somewhere quiet. That part's nearly true already, and by Sunday it might be entirely true, or it might not. Lies are like tides, Mr. Pemberton. Hard to say where they'll leave things."

"Wren is fine."

"Wren is fine," he agreed, and smiled, and the unfinished melody from yesterday surfaced briefly in his humming — that rising fourth, still refusing to resolve — and Kestrel, who had spent his life hearing where music failed, thought: *he leaves them open on purpose.*

"The boat leaves for the mainland at four," Wren said, checking his watch. "Your family's on it. I'll come up to the house at half three, and we'll get our story straight. Wear something you'd wear for someone you actually like."

He turned back to the motor. Kestrel stood a moment longer, tea cooling in his hands, aware that he had just done the most frightening thing since the Barbican — and that his hands, for the first time in eleven weeks, were steady.

At half three, a knock would come at the mill house door, and the weekend would begin.
