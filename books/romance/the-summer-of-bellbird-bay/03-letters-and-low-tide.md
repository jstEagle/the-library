## 3. Letters and Low Tide

Dear C,

You asked what I do for a living, and I've been sitting here for twenty minutes trying to answer honestly without sounding like a fraud or a cynic, which are the two costumes I own.

I plan weddings. That's the polite version. The truer version is that I manufacture belief. I build a day so beautiful, so airtight against doubt, that two people can stand inside it and convince themselves it will last forever. It's not cynical work, exactly. Some of them do last. But I've stood close enough to enough of them to know that love is mostly logistics wearing a veil.

I used to believe in it anyway. That's the part I can't figure out how to say out loud to anyone who isn't a stranger on paper.

There. Now you know more than my own mother does.

— T

I read the letter four times before sealing it, which was twice more than usual, because this one had teeth marks all over my honesty and I kept wanting to sand them down. In the end I left them. That was the whole point of C, wasn't it? A person who couldn't see me wince.

The library's winter letters program ran on a system of numbered pigeonholes behind the front desk—Mrs. Okafor's innovation, complete with laminated instructions and a strict no-peeking policy she enforced with the serene menace of a woman who had confiscated contraband from children for thirty years. I dropped my envelope in slot 14 and took nothing out, because C hadn't written back yet, and then I stood there like an idiot checking again an hour later, and again at closing, until Mrs. Okafor said, "Third time today, dear. Whoever they are, they're worth it," and I said, "It's research," and she gave me the look librarians reserve for people who lie about why they need the good scanner.

* * *

The trouble with Bellbird Bay in the off-season is that it shrinks. In summer there are tourists to hide behind, strangers to dilute the population. By late June the town contracts to its permanent bones, and everyone knows everyone's business the way family does—which is to say completely, and without mercy.

Which meant that when the booking error happened, there was nowhere for either of us to be but exactly where we were: the guesthouse kitchen, eight in the morning, Callum Reeve holding a confirmation slip that said he had reserved the whole house for a fishing charter group of six, and me holding the actual calendar, which said otherwise.

"This isn't my error," I said.

"It came through your aunt's office."

"I don't run the office. I water the ferns." I tapped the calendar. "And the ferns didn't double-book your boat."

He set the slip down with exaggerated care, the way men do when they'd rather slam it. "Six men arriving Friday. They've paid deposits."

"Then they can sleep on the ferry."

"That's not—" He exhaled through his nose. "Tamsin."

"Don't say my name like we're being reasonable. We're not being reasonable. We're being correct, and I'm the one who's correct."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Something wearier and more complicated, gone before I could name it. "I'll call the charter company," he said finally. "See if some of them will take the pub rooms."

"Fine."

"Fine."

He left his coffee mug on the counter. I washed it. I hated that I noticed he'd rinsed it first, mostly, before abandoning it—a half-consideration, a habit of someone raised properly by somebody—and I hated more that I catalogued it at all.

Here is what you need to understand about Callum Reeve, since apparently I'm keeping a ledger now: seven years ago, my brother Danny stood trial for taking money that wasn't his—from the ferry trust fund, of all things, the town's own small sacred account—and Callum Reeve, twenty-two years old and already running boats, testified that he'd seen Danny at the marina office the night of the discrepancy. Not guessed. Seen. His testimony put the timeline together; his testimony was the hinge. Danny got eighteen months. My mother stopped coming to Bellbird Bay. My father started saying "that town" the way other people say "that war." And I learned to hear Callum's name and feel the specific cold of a door closing.

So yes. When I moved here for the off-season and discovered the ferry operator—the one whose hands I'd have to shake at community events, whose boat I'd have to ride if I ever wanted to leave—was him, I did what any Hartwell would do. I built a wall out of politeness. Politeness is our best material. We're very good with it.

* * *

The fundraiser was Pru Whitcombe's idea, which surprised no one, because Pru had been organizing Bellbird Bay into better behavior since before I was born. The ferry trust fund—the same one, rebuilt, renamed, still sacred—needed a new pontoon, and Pru decided the way to raise the money was a low-tide feast on the mudflats: long tables, lanterns, local everything, tickets at forty dollars a head.

What Pru also decided, in her infinite wisdom, was that the planning committee needed both me—"a professional, finally"—and Callum, "since it's his jetty and his tide charts."

"I don't do events anymore," I told her.

"You did weddings in Melbourne. This is forty people and a raffle. You'll survive."

"I don't think you understand the depth of my retirement."

"And I don't think you understand that I've already told both of you the meeting is Thursday." She patted my arm. "You'll be civil. You're both too well-brought-up to be anything else in public."

She had us there. Hartwells and Reeves were both raised in the church of good manners; we just attended different congregations of grievance.

Thursday's meeting was held at the community hall under fluorescent lights that flattered no one. There were six of us. Callum sat diagonally across the table, farthest from me, which I noted, and noted that I noted, and hated myself for the noting.

"Tables," Pru began. "We need twelve, and seating for sixty if the weather holds."

"The weather won't hold," Callum said. "Low tide's at 5:40. Wind's coming round southerly by evening. We should plan the backup now, not hope."

"We're not calling it a backup," Pru said firmly. "We're calling it Plan B, it sounds less defeatist."

"It's the same plan."

"It has better branding."

I heard myself laugh—one short, involuntary syllable—before I could stop it. Callum looked up. Our eyes met for maybe a second and a half, and something passed between us that I refused to name, something like recognition, like two people who'd both been trapped in meetings run by Pru Whitcombe before and knew the choreography.

"Logistics," I said, to break whatever it was. "If we're doing the flats, we need the tables down before the tide turns and up before it comes back. That's a hard window. Someone needs to own the schedule."

"I'll own it," Callum said.

"Of course you will."

"Tamsin." Pru's voice had a warning note in it.

"No, it's fine. It's sensible. He knows the water." I opened my notebook. "I'll handle catering and the lanterns. Which leaves the raffle, the permits, and the thing nobody wants to do, which is asking Harlan Boyd to move his oyster cages."

Silence around the table. Everyone had been hoping someone else would say it.

"I'll do the oyster cages," Callum said again, and this time I caught it—the faintest twitch at the corner of his mouth, almost a smile, aimed at the table rather than at me, and God help me, my traitor chest did something warm about it.

* * *

That night I wrote to C before I could talk myself out of it.

Dear C,

Today I spent three hours planning a dinner party on a mudflat with a man I've spent a summer perfecting the art of ignoring, and the worst part—the genuinely humiliating part—is that he's good at it. Logistics, I mean. He thinks in tide windows and wind directions and he doesn't decorate his competence with ego, which is more than I can say for half the grooms I've worked with.

I'm not telling you who he is. I'm telling you because I don't understand what's happening in my own head. I decided years ago what this person was. I filed him. And now he's moving chairs onto a beach efficiently and I keep catching myself thinking, huh, and then being furious that I thought it.

Is it possible to be wrong about someone for seven years? Or is it possible to be right about them and wrong about everything else?

Don't answer that. Actually—do. Answer it badly. I want to see what you do with it.

— T

His reply came two days later, and I read it standing in the library doorway because I couldn't wait for a chair.

Dear T,

Badly, as requested.

I think we file people early because it saves us work. The file says: this one hurt me, this one is safe, this one is the reason things fell apart. Files are comfortable. They're also usually written by whoever was most frightened at the time, and frightened people are terrible historians.

I'm not saying you're wrong about your man on the mudflat. I'm saying I once carried a file on someone for a decade, and when I finally opened it and looked at what was actually inside instead of the label, it turned out I'd been angry at a version of events I'd never checked. Checking cost me a year of sleep. But the sleep after was better than the sleep before.

Also: efficient men who don't decorate their competence are rare. Be suspicious of anyone who makes you furious and impressed in the same afternoon. That combination has historically caused trouble.

— C

I laughed out loud in the library. Mrs. Okafor looked over her glasses. I mouthed *research* and she did not believe me.

* * *

The day of the feast, the weather did exactly what Callum said it would. Southerly by five, clouds stacking up over the headland like crockery. We got the tables down at three, lanterns hung by four-thirty, and at five-fifteen the first gust came through and took three of them sideways into the shallows.

What followed was forty minutes of the least dignified, most effective teamwork I've ever been part of. Callum waded calf-deep after lanterns while I re-rigged the lines, shouting measurements at each other across the wind, and somewhere in the middle of it—soaked to the knee, hair full of salt, laughing at the absurdity of chasing light through seawater—I realized I was having a good time. With him. The realization landed like stepping off a stair that wasn't there.

Afterward, the guests arrived, the rain held off, the mudflat glowed, and Pru declared it the best low-tide feast in the history of the trust, which given that it was the second one ever seemed generous but went down well.

We ended up side by side at the cleanup, stacking chairs into the trailer. Neither of us spoke for a while. The tide was coming back in, patient and enormous, erasing the whole evening inch by inch.

"You're good at this," Callum said eventually. Not looking at me. "The event thing."

"I retired."

"Mm." He set another chair down. "For what it's worth, the lantern rigging was clever. The second line. I wouldn't have thought of it."

I stared at the water. My heart was doing something I hadn't authorized.

"For what it's worth," I said, "the tide window schedule saved the whole night."

He nodded. Almost smiled. And then, quietly, like it cost him: "Danny would've liked tonight. He always loved the flats at low tide."

The name went through me like cold water finding a crack. Seven years, and he'd never once said Danny's name to me—he'd had every opportunity, a hundred moments where a crueler or even just a careless man might have tossed it out—and here he was using it gently, like an offering, like he'd been carrying it carefully all this time waiting for a safe place to set it down.

I didn't answer. I picked up the last chair and put it in the trailer harder than necessary, and he let me, and we finished in silence.

That night I sat with C's letter and my own unsent reply, and beneath them both, in the drawer of my aunt's old desk where I'd shoved it weeks ago unopened, sat the envelope with Danny's handwriting on it. Postmarked the week before his arrest. Addressed to me.

I'd told myself I'd found it by accident. I'd told myself I just hadn't gotten around to it.

C wrote: *frightened people are terrible historians.*

I closed the drawer. I did not open the letter. But I lay awake a long time, listening to the tide come in, aware—for the first time—that I was keeping two files now, and that sooner or later, one of them was going to have to be checked.
