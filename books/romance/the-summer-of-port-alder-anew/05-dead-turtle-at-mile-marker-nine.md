## 5. Dead Turtle at Mile Marker Nine

The call came at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, while Xanthe was elbow-deep in a tank of brine shrimp, and it was Priya who took it, the phone pressed between ear and shoulder as she tried to log water temperatures one-handed.

"Dr. Blackwood? There's a—Elio says there's a turtle. On the beach. Past the state park sign." The girl's voice had gone strange, flattened, the way voices do when they're trying not to be what they actually are. "He says you should come."

Xanthe dried her hands on her thighs and did not let herself think about what *should come* meant at six in the morning from a retired fisherman who'd seen every kind of thing the sea could give back.

Mile marker nine sat where the county road gave up its pretense of maintenance and became packed sand, just past the boundary of the state park, where the dunes ran tall and the beach went wild and unpatrolled. Elio's truck was already parked crooked against the dune grass when Xanthe pulled in, and Elio himself stood at the tideline with his cap in his hands, which was how she knew, before she saw it, that the turtle was dead.

Not stranded. Not tangled and gasping and salvageable. Dead, and washed up like an apology the ocean hadn't meant to make.

The hawksbill lay on its back in the wrack line, half-buried in sea foam, its carapace mottled amber and brown, beautiful even now, even emptied. It was a big female, breeding age, the kind of animal that represented decades of luck — dodging sharks, dodging nets, dodging boats, surviving cold snaps and red tides and all the slow violence of a warming sea — only to end up here, at mile marker nine, on a Tuesday, in front of three people who would remember it.

"Oh," Priya said softly, behind her. "Oh, no."

"Get my kit out of the truck," Xanthe said. Her voice came out level. She'd trained it to come out level. "Gloves, the camera, the calipers. Elio — did you touch her?"

"Rolled her over." He said it like a confession. "Wanted to see if she was breathing. She wasn't."

"All right." Xanthe crouched at the tideline, letting the cold water run over her knees through her jeans, and looked. Really looked, the way Dr. Voss had taught her twenty years ago in a lecture hall that smelled like formalin: *the dead will tell you everything if you don't flinch first.*

The carapace was intact. No boat strike, no propeller lacerations, none of the catastrophic shattering she'd have expected from a vessel collision. The plastron showed some abrasion, post-mortem probably, sand-scuffing from the surf. And then —

There. Along the trailing edge of the left front flipper, where the skin met the scale: a groove. Deep, healed-over in places, raw in others, the tissue grown around something that had been there a long time. She put on gloves and lifted the flipper with both hands, gentle as handling scripture, and found the monofilament.

Fishing line. Wound tight, embedded, cutting a channel through living flesh, and wrapped around it — she leaned closer, heart beginning a slow, heavy percussion — wrapped around it were hooks. Three of them, rusted, snapped off short, still seated in the muscle.

"Not bycatch," she heard herself say.

Priya looked up from the kit. "What?"

"Bycatch is messy." Xanthe kept her voice clinical because if she didn't keep it clinical she was going to be sick on the sand in front of a teenager and an old man, and neither of them needed that. "Trawls, gillnets — you get compression injuries, drowning, but not this. This is hook-and-line. Targeted. Somebody hooked this animal, fought her, and either landed her or lost her deliberately." She turned the flipper toward the light so they could see the grooves. "And look at the healing on the older wounds. This wasn't the first time. She's been hooked before. Multiple times. Somebody's been fishing for hawksbills."

Elio's face had gone the color of old rope. "Hawksbills are worth more dead than alive to certain people," he said quietly. "Shell. Meat, in some markets. Eggs, if you know where the nests are." He spat into the sand, an old habit, an old disgust. "I haven't seen this since the nineties. I thought it was done. I thought we'd *finished* it."

"You knew about this?" Priya said. "Before?"

"I knew about it the way everyone my age knew about it and nobody said anything, because the men doing it had fathers, and the fathers had boats, and the boats fed half this town." Elio put his cap back on, and his hands weren't steady. "That was thirty years ago, girl. It's supposed to be thirty years gone."

Xanthe photographed everything. Close-ups of the hooks, the wound channels, the carapace from six angles, the tag scar on her rear flipper — someone, somewhere, had tagged this female years ago, had invested hope in her, and that hope was rotting in the wrack line at mile marker nine. She measured, sampled, bagged the line and hooks as evidence, working with hands that wanted to shake and didn't, because counting was easier than feeling, data was easier than grief, and there would be time for grief later, alone, in the hatchery, where no one could see.

She was wrapping the final sample bag when the county truck pulled in behind hers.

Dev Okafor got out slowly. He was in civilian clothes — gray henley, canvas jacket, no clipboard, no citation book — and that absence of armor made him look younger and more tired than Xanthe had ever seen him. He stopped ten feet away and looked down at the turtle for a long moment, and whatever was on his face, he made no effort to hide it from her.

"Elio called me," he said.

"You're not on duty."

"No." He crouched at the edge of the tide, sleeves pushed up, and studied the exposed flipper, the hooks, the grooved flesh. He was quiet for a long time. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into something she hadn't heard from him before — stripped of the bureaucratic flatness, the dock-inspector authority, all of it. Just a man looking at a dead thing. "Show me the wound pattern again."

She showed him. She couldn't help herself; the evidence demanded showing, and he was, whatever else he was, the only other person on this beach who'd understand what it meant. She talked him through the healing layers, the repeated hooking, the targeted take, and he listened the way she'd never seen him listen to anything — completely, without interruption, without once reaching for a form.

When she finished, he sat back on his heels and looked out at the water.

"There's a boat," he said. "Works the north shelf after dark. No lights most nights, which is illegal on its own. I've written them up twice for safety violations. Both citations came back *resolved* from the county office before I'd finished filing the follow-up." His jaw worked. "I assumed sloppy paperwork. I'm starting to assume otherwise."

"You think the county's buried it."

"I think somebody buried it." He turned and looked at her directly, and the morning light caught the exhaustion in his face and did nothing to soften it. "Dr. Blackwood. Xanthe." The use of her first name landed between them like a dropped tool. "If I file a report on this turtle — poaching, targeted take, evidence of an active ring — it goes up the chain. The same chain that ate my last two citations. By the time anyone acts, whoever did this will have moved their gear, their boat, maybe their whole operation. And your turtles—" he stopped. Corrected himself. "*The* turtles. Your nesting beach. Every female that comes ashore this season walks past their front door."

"So what are you saying?"

He stood. Brushed sand from his knees. Looked, for a moment, like a man standing on the deck of a ship deciding whether to jump.

"I'm saying that officially, this morning never happened. I was never here. You found a stranded carcass, cause of death undetermined pending necropsy, and the samples go to the state lab, which takes eight weeks to process, which buys us eight weeks." He said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "Unofficially — you and I figure out who's running lines at mile marker nine before they find the nesting beach."

Xanthe stared at him. This was the man who'd audited her permits, who'd cited her dock, who'd sat across from Rosa Aguilar with a face like a closed ledger. This was the man whose testimony — she didn't finish the thought; she never let herself finish that thought — and here he was, offering to commit professional malpractice in broad daylight on behalf of a dead turtle.

"Why?" she asked. It came out harder than she intended. "Why would you risk that? For me? For this program? You've spent two months treating my center like a crime scene."

"Because I've spent two months reading your paperwork," he said, "and your paperwork is the cleanest thing in this county, and because I watched you run a necropsy on a beach at seven in the morning with a teenager and a retired fisherman and you didn't miss a single detail, and because—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because whoever did this has been doing it a long time, and everybody looked away, and I am very, very tired of being a man who looks away."

Something moved through her chest that she refused to name. She looked down at the sample bags instead. Counted them. Fourteen. Fourteen bags, one dead turtle, eight weeks.

"The necropsy tonight," she said. "Seven o'clock, the center. If you're going to be complicit, Okafor, you should see the whole picture. Internal injuries, gut contents, everything. And I'll need someone to witness the chain of custody on these samples so it holds up later, when we do this properly."

"You mean when we turn ourselves in for obstructing a federal investigation."

"I mean when we catch them," Xanthe said, "and then explain ourselves to people who can be reasoned with."

For the first time since she'd known him, Dev Okafor smiled. It was a small thing, crooked, surprised out of him, and it changed his whole face, and Xanthe looked away from it quickly, professionally, filing it somewhere she told herself she wouldn't look again.

"Seven o'clock," he said. "I'll bring coffee. Real coffee, not whatever's in that pot at your center — Priya told me about the pot at your center."

"Priya talks too much."

"Priya cares too much," Dev said, glancing once more at the turtle, at the amber shell going dull in the morning light. "Somebody around here ought to."

* * *

The necropsy took four hours, and Dev stayed for all of them.

He stood on the far side of the stainless table in the center's back room under the fluorescent hum, coffee long gone cold, watching her work with an attention that should have been unnerving and instead felt like ballast. She narrated as she went — partly for Priya's education, partly for the record, partly because talking kept the grief in its box — and he asked questions, good ones, the questions of a man who'd clearly gone home the night before and read every paper ever written on hawksbill mortality.

The stomach contents confirmed it. Squid beaks, normal forage — and a length of braided longline leader, swallowed, knotted, working its way through forty years of evolution designed for sponge and jellyfish. The lungs showed the drowning. She'd been hooked, fought, brought alongside a boat, and drowned or suffocated on deck. Shell taken, body dumped. Efficient. Practiced. The work of someone who'd done it many times and expected never to answer for it.

"Forty, fifty years old," Xanthe said finally, peeling off her gloves. "Maybe older. She might have nested on that beach every summer since before either of us was born." She looked at the small, still body on the table and made herself hold the look, made herself feel it, because Voss had taught her that too: *the day you stop feeling it is the day you start losing.* "Eight weeks isn't enough. We need to move faster than eight weeks."

"Then we move faster." Dev rolled up his sleeves — he'd rolled them up an hour ago, actually helping, holding specimen jars, labeling them in his precise inspector's hand — and reached for the chain-of-custody form. "Tomorrow night I'm driving the north shore road after dark. If that boat runs lights-off, I want to see where it launches from. There are only three ramps that make sense."

"You're not going alone."

"It's an observation drive, not a raid."

"Okafor." She planted both hands on the table, on either side of the dead turtle, and held his eyes across her. "Whoever is doing this drowned an animal older than both of us put together and threw her away. They won't hesitate at a parked truck with county plates. You go with backup or you don't go."

"And what backup would that be, exactly? You?"

"I know those waters better than anyone in this county. I know what boat engines sound like at a quarter mile versus a half mile. I know the difference between a poacher's running lights and a crabber checking pots." She lifted her chin. "And unlike you, I have absolutely nothing left to lose, which makes me extremely useful."

He looked at her for a long moment across the steel table, across the small ruined body between them, and something passed over his face that she couldn't read — something that had less to do with turtles than either of them would have admitted.

"Friday night," he said. "North shore road, nine o'clock. We take my truck, because if we get stopped, county plates are the difference between a warning and a cell." He signed the custody form, dated it, and slid it across to her. "And Dr. Blackwood — if we're doing this, we do it smart. No heroics. We watch, we document, we report to each other, and nobody goes near the water. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Xanthe said, and shook his hand across the table, over the evidence, over the dead.

His grip was warm and careful, and she let go of it one beat later than she should have.

It was only after he'd gone — after the door chimed shut and Priya had drifted home and the center had gone quiet except for the pumps and the soft slap of water — that Xanthe remembered the letter waiting in her coat pocket. Stranger B's latest, delivered to the festival committee box that morning, unread. She unfolded it standing alone in the empty hatchery, expecting the usual dry wit about lantern logistics.

Instead, three lines in, she stopped breathing:

*I've been thinking about complicity. About how a person becomes the kind who looks away — and whether it's possible, at some point, to simply stop.*

Below it, in different ink, added later, almost as an afterthought:

*Ask me sometime why I became an inspector. Or don't. Some answers are heavier than the question.*

Xanthe stood in the blue light of the tanks, the letter trembling faintly in her hand, and felt — absurdly, unreasonably — that somewhere in this town, behind some stranger's handwriting, was the only person who might understand what she'd just agreed to do.

She sat down at her desk, pulled out fresh paper, and began to write back.
