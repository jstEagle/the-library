## 12. Midsummer Gala, Unmasked Almost

The Port Alder Centennial Gala was held in the cannery's old sorting hall, a cavern of salt-bleached beams and hanging strings of Edison bulbs that Rosa Aguilar had spent three weeks arguing into existence. Xanthe arrived at seven-forty, having promised Priya she'd come, having told herself it was a strategic necessity — grant funders liked photographs of grantees being charming — and stood just inside the doors with a glass of sparkling cider she had no intention of drinking.

The hall had been transformed. Fishermen's nets were draped from the rafters like captured constellations. Long tables ran the length of the room under white cloths, and the harbor itself glittered through the open bay doors, black and patient. Somewhere a string quartet — actually two retired music teachers and a very serious high school cellist — worked through something Vivaldi-ish, fighting the acoustics of a room built for the roar of canning machinery.

"You clean up like you're hiding evidence," said Priya, materializing at her elbow in a dress covered in hand-painted octopuses.

"That's exactly what I'm doing."

"Good. I sold three raffle tickets to Councilman Brandt by telling him you'd be here. He's already had four of Elio's elderflower cocktails." Priya's grin was conspiratorial. "He calls them elderflower *martinis*. Elio puts one drop of vodka in the whole pitcher. It's basically a placebo with flowers in it."

"Then why is he—"

"Because he *believes*, Dr. Blackwood. That's the whole trick of Elio's bar." Priya pressed a raffle ticket into her hand and vanished toward the dessert table with the efficiency of someone who had been raised by Rosa Aguilar and knew exactly how galas worked.

Xanthe found a post near the wall and did what she always did at events like this: counted. Forty-one attendees. Sixteen lantern centerpieces, battery-operated, biodegradable paper — her own suggestion, she noted with proprietary satisfaction, finally adopted. Three grant funders from the state marine foundation, identifiable by their lanyards and their tendency to stand in a triangle. One harbor inspector, currently pinned against the oyster bar by Rosa, wearing a dark suit that fit him like an argument he'd decided to stop having.

Dev Okafor looked up mid-sentence, as if her attention had weight, and found her across the room.

She looked away first. She hated that she looked away first. She took a sip of the cider, which was warm and too sweet, and told herself the heat in her face was the room, the bulbs, the season.

They had not spoken since the dune grass. Since the stakeout at Mile Marker Nine, when they'd lain shoulder to shoulder in the dark whispering boat positions into a shared silence, and the radio had crackled, and the almost-thing between them had retreated like a tide that intended to come back. They had spoken plenty since — about the cove, about the charts Elio had produced, about Rosa's nephew Marco and what it would mean to name him — but they had not spoken *about* anything. There was a country between them now, and both of them kept pretending it was a parking lot.

*Stranger A,* she thought, and the word landed in her chest like a struck bell. *Stranger B.* She had written him — whoever he was — three days ago, telling him about the person she couldn't stop thinking about, the impossible one, the man whose job it was to find the cracks in what she'd built. And he had written back that he understood, that he was falling for someone equally impossible, someone whose paperwork he was supposed to be dismantling.

The symmetry should have comforted her. Instead it itched, like a tag sewn into a dress. Two people in a small town, both falling for their own impossibles. The math felt too neat. She kept almost doing it, kept almost laying the numbers side by side — Dev's handwriting on the citation forms, the slanted, impatient *g*s; the letter's handwriting, the same slanted, impatient *g*s — and then stopping herself, because the alternative was a conclusion she was not ready to survive.

"Dr. Blackwood." Councilman Brandt had arrived at her elbow with the gravitational confidence of a man who had been drinking flowers and believing in them. He was seventy-ish, silver-haired, with the ruddy complexion of a lifetime of harbor committee meetings. "You'll dance tonight. Rosa's ordered it. It's in the bylaws."

"There are no gala bylaws, Councilman."

"There are *my* bylaws." He winked hugely. "Thirty years on the council. You learn which rules are real." He leaned in, and she caught the elderflower-and-placebo sweetness of his breath. "You know what else I learned? Nothing in this town is an accident. Nothing. You want to know who's really running the harbor, you don't look at the council. You look at whoever's holding the pen."

"That's very cryptic."

"I'm a very cryptic man." He beamed. "Ask anyone. They'll say, that Brandt, he's a locked box."

Across the hall, the quartet shifted into something slower. Rosa, who had the timing of a woman who had run forty festivals, mounted the small stage and clapped twice.

"Friends! Neighbors! People who came for the free oysters!" Cheers. "It's time for the first dance of the Centennial Gala, and I'm not going to stand up here and assign partners like it's a middle school social. The music will do it. Get out there."

The crowd shuffled toward the floor. Xanthe calculated her escape routes — bay doors, dessert table, bathroom window was too dramatic — and was still calculating when a voice behind her said, "They'll notice if neither of us dances. Rosa notices everything. It's her superpower and her curse."

Dev stood beside her, hands in his pockets, tie slightly loosened, looking like a man who had been ambushed by his own reflection in a mirror that morning.

"Rosa already noticed," Xanthe said. "She's been watching us not dance for twenty minutes. I give it five before she engineers something."

"Then we should get ahead of it."

"Is that a strategic recommendation, Inspector?"

"It's a strategic recommendation, Dr. Blackwood." He held out his hand, formal as a subpoena. "One dance. For operational cover."

She took it. His palm was warm and dry and steady, and the contact went up her arm like current through water.

* * *

He was a better dancer than she'd expected, which annoyed her, because she'd spent the walk to the floor constructing a whole private argument about how he'd be stiff and formal and she could hold that stiffness at arm's length like a shield. Instead his hand settled at her waist with unhesitating gentleness, and he turned them into the crowd's slow rotation, and the quartet — the cellist especially, playing with her whole furious heart — carried them.

"You look like you're cataloguing me," he said.

"I'm a scientist. I catalogue everything."

"What's the count so far?"

"One suit, slightly too formal for the venue. One tie, abandoned at the throat, suggesting a man who dressed for a hearing and regretted it. One harbor inspector, dancing with his audit target in front of the entire town, which suggests either poor judgment or—"

"Or?"

She looked up at him. Up close, the strings of lights caught in his eyes and made them warmer than they had any right to be. "Or a man who's given up pretending this is only an audit."

The song turned. Around them the crowd moved — Elio spinning a laughing woman in a yellow dress, Rosa watching everything with her clipboard lowered for once, Priya and three other teenagers doing an elaborate ironic waltz near the oyster bar. The bay doors breathed in the night, and with it the smell of the harbor, brine and tar and the far green smell of the open water where, in a cove that appeared on no current chart, a boat was probably waiting.

"The Marco situation," Dev said quietly, the music giving them cover. "I've been thinking. If we go to the state with the cove evidence, we can frame the ring's leadership as external. Outside operators who recruited local kids. It gives the families an off-ramp."

"You'd falsify the shape of the investigation?"

"I'd *shape* it. There's a difference. Shaping is the whole job." His thumb moved, barely, against her waist — a small unconscious motion, like a man steadying himself. "Marco's nineteen. Nineteen-year-olds who get prosecuted in small towns stay prosecuted their whole lives. I've seen it."

"Voss used to say the same thing. About bycatch. You don't save the ocean by criminalizing the people who live on it."

Something crossed his face at the name — quick, swallowed. "Your mentor had a talent for being right at the worst possible moments."

"Yes," Xanthe said, and heard her own voice go strange. "She did."

The song ended. Neither of them stepped back. The quartet, sensing — as musicians do — that they had an audience's heart in their hands, slid directly into another slow piece, and the crowd, complicit, kept dancing, and Dev's hand stayed where it was, and Xanthe understood with sudden clarity that every person in this room could see what she had spent months refusing to look at.

"This is a bad idea," she said.

"Almost certainly."

"We're being watched by grant funders."

"They're watching us right now. The one in the blue lanyard just told the other one something and they both smiled."

"Then we should—"

"Give them a good photograph," Dev said, "of the program director and the harbor inspector demonstrating inter-agency cooperation," and he turned her, once, a full slow rotation under the lights, and she laughed — actually laughed, unguarded, the sound escaping before her discipline could catch it — and across the room she saw Rosa see it, and saw Rosa's face do something complicated and soft and then, abruptly, complicated in an entirely different way, her eyes flicking toward the committee table, toward a stack of envelopes, toward something she suddenly seemed to be counting herself.

* * *

The trouble came, as trouble in Port Alder generally did, from the oyster bar.

Xanthe and Dev had separated by then — by mutual, unspoken treaty, before the town's attention curdled into gossip — and she was talking to the blue-lanyard funder about hatchling survival rates when Councilman Brandt ascended, unsteadily, to the small stage, took the microphone from its stand, and tapped it with the confidence of a man who had done this forty times and remembered perhaps thirty of them.

"Test, test. Friends. FRIENDS." The quartet, wisely, stopped. "Before Rosa gets up here and does her thing, I want to say a few words about this year's pen-pal project. The Letters Between Neighbors. The healing of the old harbor feud, et cetera, et cetera, very beautiful, very Rosa."

"Marv," Rosa called from the crowd, in a voice that had stopped whaling crews, "come down."

"One moment, one moment." He swayed, found his balance with the mic stand, and grinned out at the room with the terrible benignity of the very drunk. "Because I want to tell you a secret. A little secret. About the pairings."

The room's attention sharpened. Xanthe felt it sharpen, the way water sharpens before a storm. Beside the oyster bar, she saw Dev go still.

"Everybody thinks they drew their numbers fair and square out of the bait bucket. Fair and square!" Brandt waggled a finger. "But I'm on the committee. I've been on the committee since the bucket was *bought*. And I'll tell you—" he leaned into the microphone, and his voice dropped to a carrying, delighted whisper that reached every corner of the hall, "—I'll tell you that some of those pairings were not accidents. Some of those numbers were *placed*. By somebody. On purpose. Somebody on this very committee with a very particular sense of—"

"*Marv.*" Rosa was on the stage now, hand extended, smiling a smile with no warmth in it whatsoever. "The raffle. You promised to do the raffle. The kayak. People are waiting for the kayak."

"The kayak," Brandt agreed grandly, allowing himself to be steered, "is a magnificent vessel," and then, at the top of the steps, turning back to the room with the mic still live, delivering the line that dropped into the evening like a stone into still water: "Ask yourselves, Port Alder — ask your Strangers — *who benefits from who got paired with who?*"

The quartet, after a frozen moment, resumed. The room exhaled into laughter, into the forgiving noise a town makes when one of its own embarrasses himself — *oh, Marv, oh that man, oh the elderflower* — and the gala flowed on around the stone as if the water had closed over it.

But Xanthe stood at the edge of the dance floor with her cider going warm in her hand, and the itch she'd been refusing to scratch was suddenly a fire.

*Numbers placed. On purpose.*

She thought of her own letter in her own pocket — she carried them now, all of them, folded small, a habit she'd stopped examining. She thought of the handwriting she kept almost recognizing. She thought of the symmetry that felt too neat, two strangers falling for their own impossibles, and the question she had never let herself ask because the answer was a door she could not walk back through:

*Who put us in the same bucket?*

She looked up and found Dev across the hall, and he was already looking at her, and his face wore exactly what hers wore — the same cold arithmetic, the same dawning, vertiginous question — and she knew, with the terrible clarity that comes an instant before the ground gives way, that he had done the same math she had. That he was holding the same folded letters in the same pocket of the same dark suit. That somewhere between the elderflower cocktails and the kayak raffle, they had both just understood that the strangers they'd been confessing to might have been standing in this room all along — three feet apart, dancing, lying with their whole bodies.

Rosa caught Brandt's elbow and steered him down the steps, but her eyes, over the drunk old man's shoulder, found Xanthe's and held them for one long second — and in that second Rosa's face was not drunk, not amused, not soft. It was *afraid*.

Then the lights flickered — a bulb string shorting somewhere, Rosa would say later, just the old cannery wiring — and when they steadied, Rosa had turned away, and the committee's envelope table stood unguarded, and Xanthe was already moving toward it.

The bait bucket sat on the corner of the table, empty now, festival-stamped on the side. Beside it, a single envelope lay apart from the stack, its flap unsealed, as if someone had been reading it when the trouble started and fled.

Xanthe picked it up. The handwriting on the front — slanted, impatient, those familiar *g*s — was addressed to *Stranger A.*

It was not her handwriting.

And it was not, she realized as the hall roared on around her and Dev appeared at her shoulder, out of the bucket at all.
