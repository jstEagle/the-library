## 16. The Saboteur's Letter

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was somehow the worst possible day for it. Tuesdays were when Rosa Aguilar ran the festival committee meetings like a woman captaining a ship through weather she refused to acknowledge as weather — all cheer and clipboards and coffee strong enough to strip barnacles. Tuesday mornings were sacred. Tuesday mornings were when Port Alder still believed in itself.

Xanthe was there because Rosa had asked her to give a two-minute update on hatchling projections, and Xanthe had learned in her three seasons back that you did not refuse Rosa Aguilar anything under five minutes. She sat at the folding table in the community hall basement with her notebook closed, watching dust motes drift through the light from the high windows, half-listening to Elio argue about parking for the lantern night, when Priya came down the stairs too fast.

Priya never came down stairs fast. Priya moved through the world with the deliberate cool of a seventeen-year-old who had decided long ago that enthusiasm was a vulnerability. But she came down those stairs two at a time, face white above her festival volunteer t-shirt, holding an envelope like it was something dead she'd found on the beach.

"Rosa," she said. "This was taped to the front door. No stamp. No return address."

Rosa took it the way she took everything, with both hands and a smile that didn't quite reach her eyes anymore — Xanthe had noticed that lately, the smile getting thinner around the edges, the way ice thins near a shore. "Well! Anonymous fan mail. Probably someone complaining about the porta-potty budget again."

She opened it standing up. Xanthe watched her read it, watched the smile die by degrees, watched Rosa's thumb press white against the paper until Xanthe thought it might tear.

"Read it," Rosa said finally, and her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "Out loud. Everyone should hear it at once."

Elio reached for it. "Rosa, maybe—"

"Out loud, Elio."

So Elio read it. His voice, usually a rumble like the tide going out over gravel, got quieter with every line.

*To the Centennial Festival Committee:*

*You should know what you're celebrating. The turtle rehabilitation center operating out of the old Voss lab is running on falsified permits — Permit 4471, renewed last spring under a signature that does not belong to the person who signed it. Ask your harbor inspector. He knows. He's known since June.*

*And ask yourselves why the pen-pal project paired exactly the two people whose ruin would serve each other best. That wasn't chance. Someone on this committee arranged it deliberately — a clerical "error" engineered to force a scandal into being. You've been used as instruments in someone else's redemption arc.*

*Fraud at the center. Rigged pairings at the festival. Check both. Or don't, and celebrate your centennial on a foundation of lies.*

*No signature. You don't need one. You'll know soon enough who wanted you to know.*

The silence afterward had texture. Xanthe felt it settle over the room like fog off the water, cold and total, and beneath the fog her own heartbeat, absurdly loud. *Permit 4471.* The number went through her like a hook through cartilage. Only three people alive knew that number meant anything: herself, Dev, and whoever had gone digging through records they had no business digging through.

"Permit 4471," said Councilman Brandt, who had come for the parking argument and stayed for the blood. He said it slowly, tasting it. "That's the rehab center's permit, isn't it? The one Dr. Blackwood renewed?"

"I'd have to check my files," Xanthe said, and was proud of how level it came out.

"You'd have to check." Brandt smiled. He had a fish-belly smile, pale and soft. "Funny thing about fraud accusations. They always say 'check.' Never 'no.'"

"That's enough, Wendell." Rosa's voice cracked across the room like a line snapping taut. She held out her hand for the letter, and Elio gave it back to her, and she looked at it for a long moment before setting it face-down on the table, gently, the way you'd set down something that might still be dangerous. "Here's what we know. We know one anonymous coward wrote one anonymous letter. We don't know a single fact in it is true. And we are six weeks from the hundredth anniversary of this town, and I will not have it poisoned by — by whatever this is."

"It names the pen-pal project," Priya said quietly. "It says the pairings were rigged."

"They weren't rigged." Rosa said it fast, too fast. "I ran that drawing myself. Bait bucket, numbered slips, fair as tide tables."

"Then why does the letter know about it?" Brandt asked. "Why would a stranger care who got paired with who, unless—" he let it hang, letting the room finish the sentence for him, and the room did, in the way rooms do, with shifted eyes and recrossed arms and the particular silence of people remembering every small favor anyone had ever done them and recalculating its price.

Xanthe looked at the letter lying face-down on the table and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that whoever wrote it hadn't needed to prove anything. That had never been the point. The letter was a match thrown into dry grass. All it had to do was land.

* * *

By evening it was everywhere.

Port Alder was a town of four hundred permanent residents that swelled to two thousand in summer, and it operated on a communication network older than any of them: the counter at the bait shop, the pews at St. Brendan's, the porch railings along Harbor Street where the retired fishermen sat like cormorants drying their opinions in the sun. By four o'clock the letter had been photographed, transcribed, argued over, and embellished. By six o'clock there were three versions of it, each worse than the original. By seven, someone had written FRAUD? in chalk on the walkway outside the rehab center, and someone else had scrubbed it away, and someone else had written it again further down, smaller, like a question that wouldn't stay killed.

Xanthe found Dev at the center after dark, standing in the parking lot looking at the chalk ghost on the pavement, his truck still running behind him like he'd stopped mid-errand and forgotten how errands worked.

"You've seen it," she said.

"I've seen it." He didn't look at her. In the sodium light his face was all planes and shadow, unreadable, and she hated how much practice she'd gotten at reading him anyway. "My supervisor called me twice today. Not about the audit timeline. About whether I had 'concerns' regarding Permit 4471. Somebody fed him the number, Xanthe. Somebody who knows the number means something."

"Who?" She heard her own voice go thin. "Dev, who else could know—"

"Anyone who pulled the county permit file. Anyone at the recorder's office. Anyone my supervisor talked to." He turned then, finally, and his eyes in the bad light were exhausted and fierce at once. "But here's what I keep coming back to. The letter doesn't just name the permit. It names the pen-pal pairings. It says someone on the committee rigged them. And if that part's true—"

"It isn't. Rosa drew the numbers herself. I watched her."

"So did I." He rubbed a hand over his jaw. "I watched her, and I watched the bucket, and I've been turning it over for weeks wondering how the hell I ended up paired with the one person in this town I can't stop thinking about, and now some stranger hands me an answer I don't want." His voice dropped. "Because if the pairing was rigged, then everything we wrote to each other happened inside somebody's design. And I don't know which is worse — that it was random, or that it wasn't."

Xanthe stood very still. The night smelled of salt and diesel and, faintly, of the hatchery behind them, of warm water and the mineral sweetness of sand. Somewhere inside, a pump hummed its patient note. She thought of the letters in the lockbox under her bed — Stranger B's blunt, funny, furious handwriting; the confession about being pressured into testimony he couldn't take back; the last letter, the one about falling for someone impossible. She thought about how she'd read them by headlamp with her heart going like a bird in a net.

"Don't," she said. "Don't decide it was a design. Not yet."

"Why not? It's the only theory that fits."

"Because theories are what I do for a living, and the first rule is you don't fall in love with the one that flatters your fears." She stepped closer. "Who benefits from this letter, Dev? Really. Walk it through. If someone wanted to expose the permit, they'd have sent it to the state, not to a festival committee. If someone wanted to expose the pairings, same. This went to Rosa. To the town. This wasn't written to bring us down. It was written to make everyone doubt each other. That's a different animal entirely."

He was quiet a long moment. A car passed on the coast road, headlights sweeping the lot, and in the sweep she saw his expression change — not softened, exactly, but reorganized, the way a chart reorganizes when new soundings come in.

"The nephew," he said.

"What?"

"Rosa's nephew. Marco. Elio's charts put boats out of the hidden cove going back thirty years, and half those registrations trace to families connected to the poaching ring we've been circling. Marco's boat has been seen near Mile Marker Nine twice this month." He shook his head slowly. "We've been pulling threads for weeks, Xanthe. Maybe somebody felt the pull."

* * *

Rosa Aguilar did not cry at the emergency meeting she called for Thursday night. Xanthe watched her stand at the front of the community hall — the same hall, the same folding tables, but the room had changed temperature — and she understood that Rosa had made a decision somewhere between Tuesday and Thursday to treat devastation as a scheduling problem.

"Forty-one pairs," Rosa said. "Forty-one letters exchanged, minimum eighty-two letters, probably closer to three hundred counting the ones that kept going. Three hundred letters in this town that never existed before this summer. People who haven't spoken since the dock dispute of '09 writing to each other. Old Man Pelletier apologizing to the Delgados for something his father did. Priya's grandmother telling a stranger things she never told her own daughters." Her voice held, barely. "And now every one of those letters is suspect. Every kindness gets audited. Was it real, or was it assigned?"

Nobody answered. That was the answer.

Brandt raised his hand like they were in parliament. "Motion to suspend the pen-pal reveal at lantern night pending investigation."

"Seconded," said someone in the back, and someone else, and Xanthe watched Rosa's jaw tighten around whatever she was swallowing.

"There's no investigation," Rosa said. "There's a letter."

"There's a letter alleging fraud at a facility this committee endorsed," Brandt said, smooth as silt. "Alleging this committee was manipulated. Rosa, honey, I love you like a sister, but you drew those numbers out of a bucket in front of God and everybody, and now a letter says they were fixed. Either the letter's lying or the bucket was. Which one do you want printed in the *Gazette*?"

The room murmured. Xanthe saw Elio open his mouth and close it. She saw Priya, in the back row, gripping her knees. She saw Dev sitting against the wall with his arms crossed, wearing the face of a man watching a structure fail in slow motion and calculating load paths.

And she heard herself speak before she had decided to.

"Point of order." Every head turned. She stood, because standing felt necessary, because her legs insisted. "Wendell's right about one thing. There are two possibilities. So let's test them instead of voting on them. The letter claims the pairings were rigged by someone on this committee. Fine. Rigging forty-one draws so that one specific pair comes out right is a one-in-forty-one manipulation — trivially easy if you control the bucket, statistically invisible otherwise. But here's the thing." She picked up the copy of the letter that had been passed around, held it up. "Whoever wrote this knows about Permit 4471. That number appears nowhere public. It's in county files, state files, and exactly one audit in progress. So our author either works in government records, has access to someone who does, or has been inside the paper trail of this town's worst secrets for a very long time." She let that sit. "Ask yourselves who that describes. Because it isn't me, and it isn't Dev Okafor, and it sure as hell isn't Rosa Aguilar, who has run this festival for eleven years on bake sales and stubbornness."

"And you'd say that regardless," Brandt said pleasantly, "being, as the letter notes, the subject of the fraud allegation."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "I would. That's why you shouldn't take my word for any of it. Take the timeline. The poaching investigation started in June. The letter arrived this week, the moment we started closing in on the cove. Fraud allegations don't wait for convenience, Wendell. Investigations do get interrupted by them."

For a moment — one clean moment — the room tilted her way. She saw it happen, saw doubt redistribute itself, saw Elio nod slow as a buoy. Then a woman in the third row — Mrs. Okonkwo, who brought plantains to the Sunday dinners, who had once told Xanthe she had kind hands — said, not unkindly, not loudly, just saying it: "If there's nothing wrong with the permit, Dr. Blackwood, show us the permit."

And the room tilted back.

Xanthe stood in the middle of the tilt with the document burning a hole in her conscience — Permit 4471, signed in a dead man's forged hand, in a lockbox in Dev's possession, unspeakable — and understood that this was the trap the letter had really built. Not the accusation. The invitation. *Prove us wrong.* Every denial deepened the hole; every silence confirmed it. Whoever wrote it had known exactly what it would cost to answer honestly, and had bet everything on her being unable to pay.

"I can't discuss the specifics of an active audit," she said, and heard the room hear it as guilt, and knew there was nothing she could say that it wouldn't hear as guilt, and sat down.

The motion to suspend the lantern-night reveal passed nine to four. Rosa counted the votes herself, aloud, in a voice like a hull taking water, and thanked everyone for coming, and began stacking chairs while people were still filing out, because Rosa Aguilar processed grief with her hands.

Xanthe stayed to help. They stacked chairs together in the emptied hall without speaking until the stack was done, and then Rosa stood with her palms flat on the table and said, to the table more than to Xanthe:

"I didn't rig the bucket."

"I know."

"Somebody wants this town to think I did. Somebody who knows me. Who knows how much the festival—" Her voice snagged. She cleared it. "Eleven years, Xanthe. I built this thing out of nothing, out of feuds older than my marriage, and in one page of typing somebody made forty-one people wonder if their summer was staged." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "You know who I keep coming back to? Marco. My own sister's boy. Because he's the only person alive who knows I'd rather die than fix a draw, and therefore the only person who'd know exactly how much it would break me to be accused of it."

"Rosa—"

"He was at the hall Tuesday morning. Before the meeting. Said he'd left his jacket here after the fundraiser setup." She laughed, one short note with no music in it. "His jacket. In July."

She picked up her keys and walked out, spine straight as a mast, and Xanthe stood alone in the hall with the folding tables and the dying fluorescent hum and the understanding settling over her like cold water:

The letter hadn't been aimed at her. Or not only at her. It had been aimed at every thread holding this town together, cut all at once, and the hand that cut them knew precisely where each one was tied.

Her phone buzzed. Dev: *Come to the dock. Found something. Bring the letters.*

All of them, the message meant. Everything we have. Whatever's coming, it ends at lantern night — six days out — and they were going to meet it with every card face-up or drown in the dark together.

Outside, the fog was coming in off the water, erasing the harbor one light at a time, and somewhere in it, walking calm as Sunday, was the person who had typed one page and unmade a season.
