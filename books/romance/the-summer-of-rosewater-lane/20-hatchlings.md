## 20. Hatchlings

The call came at 4:47 in the morning, and Eli Navarro was already awake to take it.

He'd been awake most of the night, actually — not from worry, though there was plenty of that stacked like cordwood behind his ribs — but because his body had learned, over eleven years of harbormastering, the particular restlessness that preceded a change in weather. And something had changed. Not the sky, which hung low and rinsed-clean over Merrow Point, scrubbed by the storm that had come through two nights ago and left the whole town wrung out and blinking. Something else. A pressure behind the air. The kind of night, his mother used to say, when the dead walk around rearranging things.

The call was from Dev Chandra, one of Jude's survey volunteers, a college kid with a headlamp permanently strapped to his forehead like a second personality.

"Mr. Navarro? The nest. Nest Four. It's — they're moving. Sand's shifting all over the top of it. Dr. Crane says if we're going to open the trench to the water, it has to be now."

Eli sat up in the dark of the harbormaster's office, where he'd fallen asleep at his desk over a ledger he couldn't make balance no matter how many ways he turned the numbers. "Tell her I'm coming."

He said *her* name in his head as he pulled on his boots — *Jude*, plain and unarmored, the way he'd finally earned the right to say it again — and the word did what it always did now, which was land somewhere soft and unguarded and stay there, warm as a coal.

Two weeks ago he'd stood on the deck of the *Mariana R.* with his hand flat against her wheel, saying goodbye. He'd found a buyer for her — a man from upstate who wanted her for charter fishing, which was a decent enough life for a boat, better than rusting — and he'd been three days from signing when Jude Okonkwo walked into the harbor auction and pledged her entire inheritance to match the developer's bid, in front of God and half of Delmarva County. He could still hear the sound the crowd made. Not applause, exactly. Something older than applause. The sound a town makes when it recognizes itself.

The *Mariana R.* was still moored at slip six. He hadn't signed anything. He wasn't sure yet what he was going to do with her, only that every morning he woke up and she was still there, and every morning it felt less like failure and more like a door someone had quietly left unlocked.

Now he drove the truck down Harbor Road with the windows down, salt air moving through the cab like a benediction, and the town rose around him in the pre-dawn gray — porch lights coming on one by one, because word traveled fast in Merrow Point and faster when the word was *turtles*. By the time he parked above the north beach, there were already twenty people gathered behind the rope line Dev had strung between two posts, their breath ghosting in the cool air, kids wrapped in blankets like burritos, old Mrs. Tavares from the bakery in a bathrobe and rubber boots, holding a thermos the size of a fire extinguisher.

And at the center of it all, kneeling in the sand beside the excavated nest with a red-filtered flashlight cupped in her palm like something holy, was Jude.

She didn't see him arrive. She was talking low to the nest itself, or to the eggs inside it, or maybe just to the night — he couldn't tell, and he found he didn't want to know, because some part of him wanted to keep this version of her forever: barefoot in the cold sand, hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a piece of survey tape, whispering encouragement to ninety-one unhatched loggerheads as if they could hear her, as if they'd been waiting eleven years too and just needed someone to tell them it was safe to come out.

The storm had nearly taken them. That was the thing nobody said out loud, standing here in the hush before sunrise. Two nights ago the surge had come within a handspan of drowning the whole relocated clutch, and it was Jude who'd called him at midnight — not the emergency line, his cell, his personal number she'd asked for weeks ago and he'd given her with his heart hammering like a boy's — and it was the two of them together, drenched and swearing and laughing in the way people laugh when terror and joy get tangled past separating, who'd moved every egg to higher ground by lantern light while the sea came for the old nest site like a debt collector.

They'd almost kissed that night. He knew it the way you know a wave is coming before it breaks — in the soles of his feet, in the shift of pressure. She'd been close enough that he could count the raindrops in her eyelashes. And then Dev's headlamp had swept across them both, and the moment had folded itself away into whatever drawer they kept such things in, the drawer that was getting fuller and fuller these days, stuffed with almosts.

But that was before the auction. Before she'd stood up in front of everyone and bet everything she had on this town, on this harbor, on — though she'd never said it in words, not yet, not plainly — on him.

Eli ducked under the rope line and crossed the sand.

* * *

Jude felt him before she saw him — a change in the quality of the darkness at her shoulder, the way the air reorganized itself around his presence, a phenomenon she had once tried, in graduate school, to explain to a bemused roommate as *electromagnetic* and now understood to be simply, embarrassingly human.

"You got my message," she said, not looking up from the nest.

"I never sleep anymore," he said. "You've ruined me."

She smiled at the sand. Eleven years ago that sentence would have been a knife. Tonight it was a gift, and they both knew it, and neither of them made a ceremony of it, because ceremony would have broken it.

The depression at the center of the nest had begun to breathe. That was the only word for it — the sand rising and settling, rising and settling, in a rhythm too slow and too deliberate to be wind or tide. Beneath it, ninety-one creatures the size of silver dollars were doing the thing they had been doing without instruction since before turtles had names: climbing over each other toward the light, boiling upward in slow motion, taking turns in a choreography no one had taught them.

"They'll wait for the temperature drop," Jude murmured. "Another hour, maybe. Right at first light." She glanced up at him then, and the red light caught his face and made him look the way he'd looked at seventeen, at nineteen, at every age she'd loved him — which was to say it made him look like home. "You came fast."

"You called about turtles," he said. "I come fast for turtles."

Behind them, muffled by distance and decorum, the crowd murmured. Mrs. Tavares was distributing coffee with the gravity of a field medic. Someone's small child asked, in the carrying whisper of the very young, whether the turtles would know where to go, and an older voice — Rosa's, Jude realized, Rosa had come, Rosa who had every reason never to stand on a beach with her again — answered gently: *"They know. It's the one thing nobody ever has to teach them."*

Jude kept her eyes on the nest. Her chest did something complicated.

*They know,* she thought. *It's the one thing.*

An hour passed the way hours pass on the edge of a miracle: slowly, then all at once.

The eastern horizon went from charcoal to pewter to a thin, astonished band of rose. The sand in the nest began to move in earnest now — individual grains trickling downslope, then a whole face of the crater slumping inward, and then, breaking the surface like a swimmer surfacing, the first flipper.

A sound went through the crowd. Not loud. A collective intake, the town inhaling as one body.

The hatchling hauled itself free of the sand and stopped, flippers splayed, head lifted, its shell still soft-edged and dusted with grit, no bigger than the lid of a mason jar. It seemed to consider the enormous world. Then it turned — unerringly, instantly, drawn by some signal older than language — toward the brightening east, toward the shine on the water, and began to walk.

"Oh," said Mrs. Tavares, wetly, from behind the rope. Just that. *Oh.*

Then the nest let go all at once. They came up in twos and threes and handfuls, a slow eruption, dozens of them, dozens more, the sand boiling gently as if the beach itself were giving birth — and it was, Jude thought, it was exactly that, the beach giving birth to the future, and she was on her knees in it with her hands hovering uselessly because the rule, the sacred rule, was *hands off*, you don't carry them, you don't help them, the crawl is the thing, the crawl is how they imprint, the struggle is the map.

"Eli," she whispered. "Look at them go."

"I'm looking," he said, and she heard that he wasn't talking about the turtles.

* * *

The gauntlet ran the length of the beach.

Dev and two other volunteers had cleared a lane of debris and smoothed the sand, and now the hatchlings poured down it in their ancient single-minded procession, sixty, seventy, eighty strong, flippers churning, leaving lines in the wet sand like a script being written. The crowd watched from behind the rope with the discipline of people who understood that love sometimes means keeping your hands in your pockets. A little girl near the front had both fists pressed against her mouth to keep from squealing. Old Sam Petrosian, who hadn't set foot on this beach since his wife died, stood at the rope line with his cap in his hands like a man in church.

Eli stood beside Jude in the middle of the gauntlet, close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them moved away.

"Third one from the left is lagging," he said.

"He's fine. They pace themselves. He's probably just—" She squinted. "Okay, he's a little dramatic."

"That one's yours."

"The drama turtle?"

"That's your turtle. I can tell. You both make everything look easy until it isn't, and then you dig in and refuse assistance."

Jude laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, loud enough that heads turned down the beach — and the sound of it seemed to surprise her more than anyone. She clapped a hand over her mouth. Eli grinned at the horizon like a man watching a ship come safely home.

The drama turtle crested a ridge of sand, tipped sideways, righted itself with an indignant flurry, and resumed its march.

Down the beach, the first hatchlings reached the waterline. A swell came in and took them — lifted them, spun them, carried them out — and the crowd made its sound again, that same caught breath, and this time it broke into scattered, involuntary cheering, quickly hushed, because everyone knew you weren't supposed to cheer, and everyone cheered anyway.

"One in a thousand," Jude said softly. Her eyes were shining, fixed on the place where the swells kept arriving like messengers. "That's what they say. One in a thousand makes it back to this beach to nest. The ocean takes nine hundred and ninety-nine."

"And they go anyway."

"And they go anyway."

Eli was quiet for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into the register he used for true things, the low one, the one she'd spent eleven years trying to forget and had failed to forget even once.

"I used to think you were the one who left," he said. "Eleven years, I built my whole grief on that. You left, I stayed, end of story. Simple math." He watched a hatchling disappear into a swell and reappear beyond it, tiny and unstoppable. "Turns out the math was wrong. Somebody else was doing the arithmetic. But you — you came back and you dug through the wreckage of it with your own hands. You read the letter. You faced what she did, and what it cost, and you didn't look away, and then you stood up in front of this whole town and put everything on the line for a harbor you'd been gone from for a decade." He shook his head slowly. "Turtles know where home is, Jude. Nobody teaches them. Turns out you're the same."

Jude's throat closed. She looked at him — really looked, in the full risen light, gold coming up over the water behind him, gilding the edges of him — and every prepared sentence she owned, every careful fragment, every piece-not-the-whole strategy she'd ever constructed, lay in ruins.

"Eli," she started.

But the crowd gasped, and the moment turned, because the last of the hatchlings — all but one — had reached the foam.

* * *

The straggler was the drama turtle.

Of course it was. It had gotten turned around near the debris line, circling a piece of driftwood as if it were a monument, and now it sat in the wet sand thirty feet from the water while its ninety siblings vanished into the shining sea, and the morning light grew stronger, and the gulls — the gulls were starting to notice.

"No," Jude breathed.

"It's fine," Eli said. "Happens every emergence. One or two—"

"There's a frigatebird. Look." High overhead, a dark shape wheeled on the wind, tilting its eye toward the beach. "If it comes down—"

"Rules are rules, Dr. Crane. Hands off."

"I know the rules, Eli, I *wrote* the—"

"I know." He was already moving. "Which is why I'm the one breaking them."

He crouched, scooped the hatchling into two cupped hands — she caught the flash of his grin, quick and conspiratorial, a boy's grin, the grin from the summer of the letter, the summer of everything — and jogged it down to the waterline, hunched over it like a man carrying a candle through wind. At the foam's edge he knelt and opened his hands, and the drama turtle paused on his palm, considered the Atlantic Ocean with what Jude would swear was attitude, and marched off into the surf without a backward glance.

The crowd lost its mind. Applause, actual applause, whistles, Mrs. Tavares blowing her nose like a foghorn. Somewhere a phone camera clicked, and Jude thought distantly: *by noon it'll be all over the town Facebook page, ELI NAVARRO SAVES BABY TURTLE, and by dinner somebody will have printed it out and taped it to the window of the bait shop*, and she found she didn't mind at all.

Eli walked back up the beach through the light, and the whole town watched him come, and the whole town watched what happened next, and nobody pretended otherwise, because Merrow Point had spent two generations pretending not to notice things and was, as of the auction, formally retired from the practice.

He stopped in front of her. Sand on his knees. Salt in his hair. His hands still cupped faintly, as if the turtle had left warmth behind.

"Jude," he said.

"Yes," she said, before he could ask anything, because she'd decided, sometime in the last hour, somewhere between the first flipper breaching the sand and the last one vanishing into the foam, that she was done making him ask.

He took her hand.

Not furtively. Not in the shadow of the boathouse, not in the privacy of a truck cab, not in any of the hundred dim places where they'd been carefully, painfully, gradually finding their way back to each other. Here. On the open beach, in front of the rope line, in front of Rosa and Dev and Mrs. Tavares and Sam Petrosian and forty other witnesses, in front of the town that had watched them break apart at seventeen and had privately, stubbornly, never once believed the story was over.

His hand was warm and rough and it fit hers the way the harbor fit the boats, the way the tide fit the shore — not perfectly, nothing fits perfectly, but with the deep worn rightness of long acquaintance.

The crowd did not cheer this time. It did something quieter and worse. It exhaled — one long, collective, satisfied breath, eleven years held and finally released — and Rosa Navarro, arms crossed at the rope line, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and mouthed something at her brother that was either *finally* or *idiot*, possibly both.

"Your sister's crying," Jude managed.

"My sister's crying," Eli agreed. His thumb moved once across her knuckles, deliberate, claiming nothing, offering everything. "So are you."

"I'm a marine biologist. It's salt spray."

"It's not salt spray."

"It's absolutely salt spray."

He laughed, and she laughed, and down the beach the last traces of flipper-tracks were filling with seawater, erased and preserved at once, the way the tide keeps everything — and high overhead the frigatebird wheeled away empty-handed toward the horizon, cheated, patient, willing to wait.

Later — after the volunteers packed the gear, after the crowd dispersed up the dune path in clusters, still talking in the hushed voices people use coming out of churches — later, walking the tideline together with their hands still linked, swinging slightly, absurdly, like teenagers, they passed the spot where the nest had been. The sand was already smoothing over it. By tomorrow there would be no sign anything had ever been buried there. By next season the beach would forget entirely.

But Jude stopped, and looked at the smooth sand, and then at Eli, and something surfaced in her — not from the nest, from deeper, from the ledger box at the Crane house, from the second envelope she'd found sealed in her grandmother's handwriting and had carried, unopened, in her bag for nine days now, moving it from purse to jacket to nightstand like a stone she couldn't set down and couldn't swallow.

"Eli," she said. "There's something I have to deliver to you. Today. Before I lose my nerve again."

He looked at her — reading her the way he'd always read her, the way the hatchlings read the horizon, by some signal older than language — and his hand tightened around hers.

"What is it?" he asked.

Jude looked out at the water, where ninety-one small lives were swimming into the wide blue future, and drew the longest breath of her new life.

"It's a letter," she said. "From my grandmother. And it's addressed to you."
