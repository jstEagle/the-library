## 4. Ten Summers of Letters

He came to her door on the fourth morning with a shoebox under his arm, and Petra knew before he spoke that it was over.

She had been watching him from the kitchen window—the narrow window above the sink that framed the strip of dune grass and the gray lip of the sea—watching Elias Ward cross the sand path between their cottages with the unhurried gait of a man who had rehearsed this walk. The box was ordinary. The box was nothing. But his hands held it the way people hold things they are afraid to put down.

She dried her hands on a dish towel that said SANDPIPER POINT in fading blue script, a souvenir from the gift shop she'd bought her first summer here, and she gave herself exactly ten seconds to decide how to be. Ten seconds to choose a version of herself, the way she chose a voice for a memoir subject, a cadence for a CEO, a tender regret for a widow who had never felt tender regret in her life.

She chose *pleasant neighbor, mildly curious, nothing to hide.*

The bell rang. She opened the door with her face already arranged.

"Good morning," Elias said. "I hope this isn't— I found these while unpacking. Or rather, I've had them a while. I thought you should see them."

"You thought *I* should see them," Petra repeated, and heard how the sentence bent wrong, how it was not a question but a confession she hadn't agreed to make.

He didn't come inside when she stepped back. He stood on the threshold with the salt wind moving through his hair, grayer at the temples than the boy she had loved, and he set the box on her kitchen table with a gentleness that undid her more than any accusation could have. Then he lifted the lid.

Letters. Dozens of them. Her letters, in her own hand from the years before she'd learned to type everything, before handwriting became a thing she did only on birthday cards. The paper had gone the color of weak tea. The rubber band holding the earliest bundle had perished into sticky threads. And on top, folded open as if he'd been reading it that very morning, the last one. Unsigned. *The one that ended it.*

"I should make coffee," Petra said, because she had to say something, and her body chose the oldest lie available: the performance of domestic calm.

"I'd like that," he said. "Thank you."

* * *

The coffee maker gurgled. The morning light came through the window in a long pale blade and lay across the letters like something laid out for an autopsy. Petra stood at the counter with her back to him and let the memories come, because they were going to come anyway, and it was better to meet them standing.

*The first letter, the summer she was twenty-two. She had written it on the porch of this very cottage, on paper stolen from Margot's stationery supply—no, not stolen, borrowed, everything between her and Margot had always been a borrowing that no one audited—and she had addressed it to the boy in the blue kayak who had waved to her every evening for a week without ever coming ashore. She had written: You have hands that look like they know how to hold things carefully. I've been watching them from the window and inventing a whole life for you. Forgive me. I make things up for a living, or I will, someday. For now I just make things up.*

She had signed it with nothing. No name. Just a drawing of a sandpiper, because the bird had been there that day, running at the foam's edge, and because a name was a door and she wasn't ready to open it.

*He had answered. Of course he had answered. Elias Ward, who was spending the summer at his grandmother's cottage two doors down, who had read her letter four times and then written back on the back of a tide chart: I don't know who you are, but you're the best thing that's happened to this beach in years. Tell me one true thing about yourself and I'll tell you two.*

*One true thing. Two true things. That had been the architecture of the whole first summer, the whole second summer, the whole architecture of ten years of letters that crossed the country as they both moved, as he went to Seattle and she went to New York, as the envelopes grew thicker and the true things grew heavier. He had never pressed her for her name. He had said, in the third year, in his careful engineer's handwriting: You'll tell me when you're ready. I'd rather have all of you late than most of you now. And she had loved him for it, and she had hated him for it, and she had used it, because every year she didn't sign her name was another year she could remain the best version of herself—the one who existed only in ink, unafraid, generous, brave in the specific way that people are brave when no one can see them flinch.*

The coffee was done. She poured two cups, and her hands were steady, and she despised how steady they were.

"So," she said, setting his cup down. "You've been carrying these around."

"For a long time." He touched the last letter, the unsigned one, without picking it up. "I moved here because of them, Petra. I want to be honest with you, since honesty seems to be—" he stopped, and she watched him decide not to say what he'd been going to say. "I found a mention of Sandpiper Point in one of the early letters. The porch with the crooked rail. I've been looking for this place for years."

"Why?" The word came out sharper than she intended. "Why would you spend years looking for someone who—"

"Who vanished?" He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "That's my question. That's the one I've had for ten years. The last letter said—" he recited it, and she realized with a lurch that he had memorized it, that he had carried her words in his body the way she carried other people's words in her head— "*I have to stop writing to you. Not because I've stopped meaning it. Because I've started meaning it too much, and you deserve someone who can stand in the same room with what they feel. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.* And then nothing. No name, no address, no explanation. Just nothing, for ten years."

The sea filled the silence outside the window, patient and enormous.

"I had my reasons," Petra said.

"I'm sure you did." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the table, a small precise movement, the hands that knew how to hold things carefully. "I'm not here to demand anything. I've imagined this conversation a thousand times and in none of them did I get to be angry, because the truth is I stopped looking, too. I married someone. It didn't—" he exhaled. "It didn't work, and part of the reason it didn't work is that I was writing letters in my head to a woman whose name I didn't know. So I don't come here with clean hands. I come here with a shoebox and a question."

*Don't tell him,* said the voice in her that had kept her alive and invisible for a decade, the voice that sounded, she realized now, exactly like Margot. *He doesn't want the whole truth. Nobody wants the whole truth. They want the shape of it, the presentable version, the memoir version. Give him that. Give him the version with the edges sanded off.*

"I was young," Petra said. "I was writing to you because it was the only place I was honest, and then it got too honest, and I got scared. People do that."

"It's true," he agreed. "And it's also not the whole of it. You write, Petra. Present tense. I've read your letters enough times to know a writer when I hold one in my hands, and I've spent the last week—" he paused, and she watched him arrive at the edge of it, the edge of the cliff she'd built her whole life on the far side of. "Margot Vane's books. The memoirs. *The Salt Year.* I read it on the plane, because I'd learned she summered here, and I thought— I don't know what I thought. That this place might explain how someone writes like that."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere down the beach a gull screamed once and gave up.

"And?" Petra said, and was proud, distantly, of how light she made it. "Did the beach explain her?"

"It explained something." His eyes stayed on hers, and there it was again, that terrible gentleness, the way he had always looked at her letters as if they were fragile and true at once. "The passages that made me cry on the plane—the ones about the stranger on the beach, the unsigned letters, the summer of the sandpiper—they weren't Margot's. I know because they were mine. Because I answered them. I've spent ten years thinking I was in love with a ghost, and then I read a bestselling memoir in which my own words came back to me in a stranger's mouth, and I thought, either this is the most extraordinary coincidence in the world, or—"

"Or," Petra said, and her voice did something she hated, something thin.

He didn't finish it. He gathered the letters back into the box with those careful hands, all but the last one, which he left lying open on the table between them like a wound that had been bandaged for a decade and was now, at last, unwound.

"I'm not asking you to tell me today," he said. "I waited ten years; I can wait a little longer. But I'll ask you this, and then I'll go." He stood, and he was close, close enough that she could smell salt and cedar and the ghost of some soap she'd once known. "The woman who wrote these letters was the truest person I ever knew. And I have spent ten years wondering what happened to her. So whatever name she goes by now, whatever she's had to do to survive—I'd just like to know if she's still in there. If any of her is still in there."

Petra opened her mouth. And the truth rose in her like the tide, enormous, inevitable, the whole of it—the ghostwriting, the signed contract, the money she'd needed when her mother was dying, the way she had sold her letters to Margot's publisher as "source material" because anonymity was the only armor she had left, the way she had watched her own sentences become a stranger's fame and told herself it was fine, it was fine, ghosts don't need bylines, ghosts don't need to be seen—

Her phone rang.

The sound cracked the morning open. She flinched. The screen said MARGOT in white letters, and beneath it the message preview, because Margot never simply called, Margot summoned: *Petra. The study. Now. We have a problem and you're going to fix it.*

Elias looked at the phone. Then at her. Something in his face closed, quietly, the way a door closes when it isn't slammed—just drawn to, gently, by someone who has decided to give you one more chance than you've earned.

"You should get that," he said.

"Elias—"

"Another time." He picked up the shoebox. At the door he stopped, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "For what it's worth—the answer to my question. Whether she's still in there." A pause, the wind moving the dune grass outside. "I think she is. I think she's been in there the whole time, writing everything down, hoping someone would finally read it."

He went. The door clicked shut with the softness of a period at the end of a sentence she had not been allowed to write.

Petra stood in her kitchen with the cold coffee and the unsigned letter and her own heart hammering out the only honest thing left in her: *he knows, he knows, he knows.* Then she picked up the phone, and made her voice into the voice Margot paid for, and walked out into the light toward the big house on the point, where the woman who had bought her life was waiting to tell her what it would cost to keep it.
