## 7. Ink Under the Fingernails

Elias found the pages by accident, which was how he suspected Petra would have preferred it—everything important in her life arriving sideways, unannounced, deniable.

The storm two nights before had left its signature all over Sandpiper Point: shingles scattered across Margot Vane's lawn like dropped playing cards, a rowboat beached in the dune grass where no rowboat belonged, and a leak in the ceiling of Elias's rented cottage that had turned his study into a shallow indoor lake. He'd spent the morning hauling boxes of books to higher ground, and it was in the course of this rescue that he'd knocked on Petra's door to ask if she had a wet-dry vac she could spare, and she, harried, ink-stained, late for one of Margot's dictation sessions, had thrust her spare key into his hand instead of an answer.

"Stack anything you find on my desk," she'd called over her shoulder, already halfway down the path. "Don't read anything you find on my desk."

He had smiled at that. He had even intended to honor it.

But the box was heavy, and the desk was small, and when he set the last armload down beside a manila folder that had been sitting there with the patient confidence of something waiting to be discovered, the folder slid, and pages fanned out across the blotter like a hand of cards dealt by fate, and the first line landed face-up under the gray window light:

*He held the ladder as though it were the only honest thing between them.*

Elias went very still.

He knew that sentence. Not because anyone had said it to him—but because he had lived it. Three weeks ago, maybe four, on the afternoon he'd come down off the roof of his own cottage with a borrowed extension ladder and found Petra standing below him with a spilled box of books at her feet and a first edition clutched against her chest like a shield. He had held the ladder. She had looked up at him through the salt haze with an expression he still couldn't name, and the air between them had gone taut as wire.

Except these words weren't his memory. They were someone else's rendering of it—polished, distanced, third person. And beneath them, in Petra's unmistakable slanted hand, ran a date. A chapter number. *Margot — Ch. 14, revision.*

The floor seemed to tilt gently, the way the deck of the ferry tilted when it came about.

He should have stopped there. He knew that too. But his hands were already moving, lifting the pages one by one, and what he found was not a page or two of borrowed phrasing but a manuscript—hundreds of pages, dense, alive, written in longhand in the margins of Margot's dictated typescript and then transcribed onto loose sheets in Petra's smaller, faster script. Two books braided into one. Margot's voice on the surface, smooth and public and triumphant. And underneath, threaded through every chapter like a second pulse—

Him. Her. The letters.

He recognized the summer they'd buried the time capsule, rendered now in prose so precise he could smell the iodine of the seaweed they'd used for wrapping. He recognized the night on the breakwater when she'd told him she wanted to be a writer and he'd laughed—not cruelly, but carelessly, the way nineteen-year-old boys laugh at things they don't yet understand—and he felt the old shame rise fresh in his throat because here it was, preserved, examined, forgiven in a way he hadn't earned. He recognized the last letter. The one that arrived unsigned, the one that said *I have to stop writing to you before I write myself into a shape you'd never recognize*, the one after which she vanished from his life like tide going out.

It was all here. Their whole ten-year silence, translated into the language of Margot Vane's celebrated life.

Elias sat down slowly on the edge of her desk chair, the pages trembling slightly in his grip, and understood, with the strange clarity that sometimes follows shock, that he was holding the truest book he had ever read—and that it was wearing another woman's name.

* * *

She came back at dusk, when the light through the west windows had gone the color of weak tea. He heard her key in the lock and did not get up. There was nowhere to go; he was sitting in the middle of the evidence, the counter-manuscript spread around him like the aftermath of a card game neither of them had agreed to play.

Petra stopped in the doorway. She was still in her work clothes, a pencil tucked behind one ear, a smudge of blue ink along the side of her right hand—the ghostwriter's tattoo, he thought absurdly, the mark of the trade. Her eyes went from his face to the pages to his face again, and he watched every color drain out of hers except a kind of gray resignation, as if she had rehearsed this moment so many times in her imagination that the reality of it could only disappoint her.

"You read it," she said. Not a question.

"I fixed your ceiling leak's neighbor's problem," he said, "and then I broke my promise." He lifted a handful of pages. "I'm not sorry I read it. I'm sorry you wrote it for her."

"It's not *for* her." Her voice was flat, controlled, the voice he imagined she used with editors. "It's a working document. It's how I keep the chapters straight."

"Petra." He stood, and the movement made her flinch, and that flinch hurt worse than anything else in the room. "Chapter fourteen is us. Chapter nine is the breakwater. Chapter twenty-two—" His voice caught and he hated it, hated the tremor, pushed through. "Chapter twenty-two is the letter you never signed. You've written our whole history into her memoir. Word for word. Moment for moment. Ten years of silence, and she gets to tell it to the world as if she lived it."

"She didn't take it from me," Petra said quickly, too quickly. "I gave it. Years ago. There were contracts. There was money I needed. It's more complicated than—"

"Is it?" He crossed to her, close enough now to see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the way her hands had curled into fists at her sides as though bracing for a blow. "Because from where I'm standing it looks very simple. You wrote the most beautiful thing I've ever read, and you signed it with someone else's name, and now you're going to stand there and tell me that's complicated."

The silence stretched between them, taut, humming.

"Say it," he said softly. "Say that it's yours. Just once. Just to me. Nobody's listening but me."

And he saw it happen—the truth rising in her like water finding its level, reaching her eyes, reaching the edge of speech—and then he watched her push it back down. He watched her do it. It was the most deliberate act of self-erasure he had ever witnessed, and he had spent a decade imagining her disappearance from every possible angle.

"The advance is spent," she said finally, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "The rights are assigned. If I claim those pages, Margot's lawyers will bury me. And worse—" her laugh was thin and terrible— "worse than the lawyers, Elias. Nobody buys stories from ghosts. That's the entire economy of what I do. People want the famous woman's heartbreak, not the invisible girl's. If I put my name on it, it becomes a nobody's book about a nobody's summer, and everything I gave up will have been for nothing."

"That's not true."

"It's the truest thing I know."

"Then you learned it from liars," he said, and the words came out harder than he meant them, and he didn't take them back. "You think anonymity protected you? It starved you. I've watched you for three months, Petra. You flinch when people say your name, like it's a debt being called in. You write like someone apologizing for taking up space on the page. And the cruelest part—" he picked up the top sheet, the one with the ladder, with their beginning rendered in her own hand— "the cruelest part is that this is *good*. This is better than good. This is the book that would make Margot Vane's career look like a warm-up act. And you're going to let her walk onto that stage with it while you stand in the wings holding the prompt cards."

"You don't understand what she can do to me."

"Maybe not. But I understand what you're doing to yourself, and I've watched you do it for ten years." He set the pages down, carefully, the way you set down something broken. "Publish it under your own name. Before her deadline. Take it back."

"And lose everything."

"Or keep the only thing that was ever actually yours."

Her eyes flashed then—anger, finally, real anger, and some traitorous part of him was glad of it, because anger meant she was still in there, still fighting. "You disappeared too," she said. "You stopped looking. Don't stand in my living room and lecture me about courage when you took one unsigned letter as your answer and built a whole quiet life on top of it."

The blow landed exactly where she aimed it. He absorbed it, let it hollow him out, because she wasn't wrong—that was the thing. He had stopped. He had grieved a woman who had never technically existed, mourned a pen name, and married someone kind and wrong for him, and called it moving on.

"You're right," he said quietly. "I stopped searching. That's my confession, and I'll carry it. But Petra—I'm standing here now. I moved three hundred miles to stand in front of a cottage I couldn't afford because a handwriting analyst confirmed what I already knew in my bones. Whatever cowardice is in my ledger, showing up isn't in it. So don't confuse the two."

She turned away from him then, toward the darkening window, toward the sea making its low endless argument with the shore, and her shoulders were rigid, and her voice when it came was barely above the sound of the waves.

"If I claim it, I lose the career, the house, the only version of myself anyone believes in. If I don't, I keep all of it and none of it's mine. Those are the choices. You want me to be brave in a story where brave doesn't exist."

"No," Elias said. "I want you to be brave in the story where it does. The one you wrote. I've read it." He gathered his coat from the back of the chair, because staying longer would become begging and he refused to beg her to save herself. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said the thing he'd carried up the stairs with him from her desk:

"For what it's worth—the girl who wrote those letters? She never needed a famous name. She just needed to sign the last one."

He let himself out into the cold blue evening. Behind him, through the window, he could see her silhouette motionless at the desk, and he walked home in the dark telling himself he'd planted something that would grow.

But by midnight, watching her lamp burn next door hour after hour without going out, he understood he might have done the opposite. He might have shown her exactly where the wall was thinnest—and given her every reason to build it higher.

Two weeks remained until Margot's deadline. And somewhere in the house next door, a woman who had spent ten years erasing herself sat alone with a decision that would either finish the job or undo it entirely.
