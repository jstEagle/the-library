## 9. Launch Day

The ballroom of the Meridian Hotel smelled like money trying to remember it was once salt air. Petra stood at the edge of the crowd in a dress she'd bought three years ago for a launch that wasn't hers—emerald silk, the color of the sea before a storm—and watched Margot Vane hold court beneath a chandelier that threw light like scattered coins.

*The Summer of Sandpiper Point.* Two hundred copies fanned across a linen-draped table, their covers gleaming: a photograph of a shoreline at dusk, a single gull suspended mid-flight. Petra had chosen that photograph. She had written every word inside it. And now she was going to stand in this room full of strangers and tear her own name out of hiding.

Her hands were cold. Her heart was a fist knocking on a door she'd locked ten years ago.

"Petra." Margot materialized beside her in white silk, diamonds at her throat like frost. Up close, the famous face showed its scaffolding—the fine cracks around the eyes, the powder sitting in them. "You look pale, darling. Nervous?"

"I'm always nervous at launches."

"Mm." Margot's gaze slid over her, assessing, the way she assessed everything—as either useful or expendable. "Two weeks early, the manuscript delivered, not one complaint from legal. You've been a marvel, as always." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "And discreet. That's what I pay for, isn't it? Discretion."

It wasn't a question. It was a reminder. *I know what you could do. I know what I'll do if you try.*

"I know," Petra said.

Across the room, Elias stood near the windows with his hands in his pockets, watching her. He wore a gray suit and no tie, and he looked exactly like what he was: a man who had driven four hours to stand beside someone about to burn her life down. When their eyes met, he didn't smile. He gave her something better—a small, steady nod, the kind you give a swimmer before she goes off the high board.

*You can still walk out,* he'd said last night, standing in her kitchen while rain needled the windows. *No one would blame you.*

*Everyone would blame me,* she'd answered. *That's the point. I'm done being unblameable and invisible.*

Now the publicist tapped a glass. The room hushed. Margot glided toward the podium, luminous, victorious, and Petra's stomach dropped through the floorboards of herself.

*This is it. The moment your story stops belonging to you forever—or starts belonging to you again.*

Margot adjusted the microphone. Smiled her television smile. "There's a passage in this book," she began, "that readers have already fallen in love with. My editor calls it the heart of the memoir. I wrote it one night at Sandpiper Point, watching the tide come in, thinking about the summer that changed everything."

She found the page. The room leaned in.

"'I used to think love was a thing that happened to you,'" Margot read, her voice low and honeyed, "'like weather. But love is a verb. Love is showing up at the door with wet shoes and staying anyway. I learned that from a boy who read my letters aloud like scripture, who told me I was the truest voice he'd ever heard—and I have spent ten years trying to be worthy of having been seen so completely.'"

Every word. Every syllable. Petra had written it at two in the morning in a rented cottage, crying over a keyboard, pouring ten lost years into sentences she believed no one would ever trace back to her.

The applause started somewhere near the front and rolled outward like surf.

Petra stood up.

* * *

Elias saw it happen the way you see lightning decide to strike.

One moment she was a still figure in green at the edge of the crowd; the next, she was rising from her chair, spine straightening vertebra by vertebra, and his whole chest went tight with terror and something dangerously close to awe.

She walked forward. Not fast. Not slow. The way water walks when it has decided to become a wave.

The applause thinned as people noticed her. A hundred faces turned. Margot faltered mid-smile, her eyes flicking from Petra to the audience and back, calculating in real time.

"Excuse me," Petra said. Her voice carried further than Elias expected—clear, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had spent a decade making other people sound brilliant and finally intended to use it on herself. "Before we go any further, I think you should all hear where that passage actually came from."

Murmurs. Someone lowered a champagne flute. Near the podium, Margot's publicist took half a step forward and stopped, uncertain whose crisis this was.

"My name is Petra Thornbury," she said. "I am a ghostwriter. I have ghostwritten eleven books, including—" she turned, deliberately, to face Margot, and Elias watched her take the breath that must have cost her everything— "*The Summer of Sandpiper Point.* Every word of it. Including the passage Ms. Vane just read."

The room went silent in that particular way rooms do—not empty silence but charged silence, two hundred people inhaling at once.

"That passage," Petra continued, "is about a man named Elias Ward. It's drawn from letters I wrote him between the ages of nineteen and twenty-two. Letters he kept. Letters I never signed." She lifted a leather portfolio she'd carried all evening like a bomb under her arm. "These are the originals. Dated. In my handwriting. The manuscript quotes them verbatim—I counted forty-one instances. You're welcome to compare."

She opened the portfolio and set it on the corner of the podium table, turning pages toward the room like a dealer laying down a winning hand.

Margot recovered first, because Margot always recovered first. She stepped to the microphone with a laugh that didn't reach her eyes. "Darling, this is—dramatic, certainly. But let's be clear. Petra signed a work-for-hire agreement. Everything produced under our arrangement belongs to me. Legally—"

"Legally, you may well be right," Petra said, and Elias heard the crack in her voice and then heard her push through it. "Maybe I lose in court. Maybe I signed away more than words—maybe I signed away the right to my own summers, my own heartbreak, my own name. But I'm not asking a court tonight. I'm asking you." She swept the room with her gaze. "Whose story is this? The woman who lived it and wrote it in secret? Or the woman who bought it and put her photograph on the cover?"

A phone came up. Then another. Then five. The recording lights bloomed like small red stars.

* * *

From the window, Elias watched her stand in the center of the storm she'd summoned, and he understood suddenly that he had loved her twice—once as a boy who received her letters, and now as a man watching her write herself into existence in real time.

He moved before he decided to move. Through the crowd, to the front, until he stood beside her at the podium, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers.

"There's something I can add," he said into the microphone, and the room stilled again. "My name is Elias Ward. I'm the 'boy' in those letters. I received the first one when I was twenty, postmarked from a town I'd never been to, and I fell in love with a voice before I ever saw a face." He looked at Petra. "She stopped writing ten years ago and I never knew why. Tonight I finally do. She was writing books for other people. She was writing *this* book—for the woman standing next to me now." He turned back to the room. "I moved to Sandpiper Point to find her. I want to say, for the record, that every word in those letters is hers. I'd know. I've had them memorized for a decade."

Someone in the third row said, audibly, "Oh my God." A journalist Elias vaguely recognized was typing with both thumbs, ferocious as a woodpecker.

Margot's composure finally fissured. "This is defamation," she said, sharp now, the honey burned off. "Both of you. I will sue you into the ground, Petra. I will take your advance, your future earnings, everything—"

"With what?" Petra asked quietly. "The truth?" She held up one of the letters, dated in the margin in her own unmistakable hand. "Discovery works both ways, Margot. My lawyer has the complete correspondence. So does yours, as of four o'clock this afternoon."

Something shifted in the room then—Elias felt it physically, a tide change, the collective weight of two hundred people deciding whom to believe. It wasn't just the documents. It was Margot's face, tightening toward cruelty, set against Petra's face, open and terrified and unbearably honest. Audiences forgive almost anything except being lied to beautifully, and everyone in this room had just realized which of these women had done the lying.

"You could have taken this to court," a woman near the front called out. "Why here? Why like this?"

Petra was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice shook only a little.

"Because I spent ten years believing my name was worth nothing unless someone else was holding it up. Because I thought ghosts were safer than people." She pressed her palm flat against the portfolio, against ten years of dated pages. "I'm tired of being safe. This is me, signing my name."

For one suspended second, nobody moved.

Then the woman in the front row began to clap—slowly at first, then joined by another, then another, until the applause built into something Margot couldn't shout down, something that filled the ballroom like water filling a hull. Phones were everywhere now, red lights steady. Somewhere a bookseller was already moving copies of the memoir off the display table, though whether to buy or return them, no one could say.

Margot stood at the microphone in her white silk, and for the first time since Petra had known her, she had nothing prepared. Her mouth opened. Closed. Her eyes went from the applauding crowd to the portfolio to Petra's face, hunting for the version of events she could still control, and finding none.

"This isn't over," she said, low, meant only for Petra—but the microphone caught it, and the room caught it, and the phones caught it too.

"No," Petra agreed. Her hand found Elias's without looking, the way a compass finds north. "But it's mine now."

Outside the tall windows, far beyond the city lights, the direction of the sea was invisible—but Elias could feel it anyway, the pull of it, the tide going out and coming back, going out and coming back, patient as a promise.

Petra lifted the portfolio, tucked it under her arm like a shield she no longer needed, and walked toward the doors with her chin up and her name finally, finally attached to her own life—while behind them the room erupted into questions, and Margot Vane's lawyers began dialing numbers that would ring, within the hour, on Petra Thornbury's phone.
