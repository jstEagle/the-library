## 10. Signed, At Last

The shop window on Harbor Street had always been Margot's territory. For ten years, Petra had walked past it the way you walk past a house where a party is happening that you weren't invited to—head down, pace quick, telling yourself you didn't want to be inside anyway.

Now her own name was in the glass.

*The Letters I Never Signed*, by Petra Thornbury. A slim book, barely two hundred pages, its cover the color of late-afternoon water at Sandpiper Point—that particular green-gold the tide turned just before dusk. It sat propped on a small wooden easel beside the towering display for *Margot Vane: A Life in Full*, and if anyone had asked Petra which arrangement she'd have chosen six months ago, she would have said neither. She would have said she wanted to stay invisible, because invisible was safe, and safe was all she'd ever let herself have.

But no one was asking. And there it was: her name, in ink, in public, unhidden.

She stood across the street with coffee going cold in her hands and let herself look at it for a full minute before crossing over. This was part of the practice now—the looking. Dr. Okafor, the therapist she'd started seeing in March, called it "witnessing your own arrival." Petra called it learning to stand still when everything in her wanted to run.

Inside, the bookseller—a round-faced woman named Della who had sold Petra paperbacks under three different pen names without once suspecting they were all the same woman—looked up from the register and smiled.

"Sold four more this morning," Della said. "And a man came in asking if you'd sign one. I told him you'd probably do better than 'probably.'"

Petra laughed, but something in her chest went tight and warm at once. Signing things was still new. Her hand still hesitated above the page sometimes, the old reflex rising—*don't put your name on it, don't claim it, don't be findable*—before she pressed the pen down anyway.

She signed the display copy with a steady hand. *For whoever needs to hear it: you were always allowed to be seen.* Then she bought a postcard from the rack by the door—one of those thick linen ones with a watercolor of Sandpiper Point at low tide—and carried it back out into the light.

* * *

The settlement had been finalized on a gray Tuesday in April, in a conference room in Boston that smelled of carpet cleaner and fear. Margot's lawyers had come armed; Petra's lawyer, a sharp-eyed woman named Renata whom Elias had helped her find, had come armed better.

The terms were simpler than Petra had expected, though nothing about that day felt simple. The memoir would stand as published—pulling it would have cost everyone more than anyone could bear—but every future edition would carry an acknowledgment page naming Petra as author of the letters, and Margot would never again publish work derived from Petra's writing. In exchange, Petra dropped the most damaging claims, the ones that would have ended Margot's career outright.

They'd met once more after that, alone, in the same conference room. Margot had looked older than she did on book jackets, smaller somehow, her famous composure worn through at the elbows like a coat kept too long.

"You could have destroyed me," Margot said.

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

Petra had thought about it honestly, which surprised them both. "Because I spent ten years punishing myself for wanting to be seen," she said. "I'm not going to spend the next ten punishing you. It doesn't mean I forgive what you did. It means I'm done carrying it."

Margot had nodded slowly, and for one unguarded moment she'd looked almost relieved—as if being caught had been its own kind of release. "Your book," she'd said at the door. "Is it good?"

"It's true," Petra said. "That was harder."

* * *

May came to Sandpiper Point the way it always did—slowly, then all at once, the beach grass going from straw to green, the ospreys returning to their platform by the marsh. Petra had stayed. She'd told herself at first it was temporary, that she'd go back to the city once the settlement closed, but the cottage she'd rented for the winter had a way of holding onto her, and besides: the cottage next door was occupied again.

Elias had never left.

After launch night—after she stood up in that glittering room with her heart hammering and said, *those words are mine, I wrote them, here are the letters, dated, in my hand*—there had been weeks of chaos. Lawyers, journalists, a producer who wanted to option everything. Through all of it, Elias had been exactly what he'd promised to be on the beach the night the storm uncovered the time capsule: steady. Not dramatic about it. Just present. Coffee on her porch in the mornings. Silence offered when she needed silence, words when she needed words.

He had also been honest about his own failures, in the way of a man who had learned that honesty deferred only compounds interest. He told her again about the years he'd stopped searching—how he'd convinced himself the silence meant she'd chosen to vanish, how he'd built a marriage on top of that assumption and watched it fail under the weight of everything he hadn't said. He didn't ask forgiveness so much as he simply kept showing up until forgiveness had room to arrive.

And it had. Not all at once. Forgiveness, Petra learned, was less a door you walked through than a tide—you went out, you came back in, each pass leaving you a little further up the shore. Some mornings she woke furious at him for those lost years. Most mornings she woke grateful he was three hundred feet away, asleep behind windows that faced the same water hers did.

This morning, she sat at her kitchen table with the linen postcard in front of her and a pen she'd bought specifically for this purpose—good weight, good nib, the kind of pen you use when the writing matters.

The last letter she had written to him, ten years ago, had been unsigned. She remembered composing it in a rented room in another city, remembering his hands, deciding that love without a name attached was safer than love with one. She had believed, then, that anonymity was armor. She knew now it had been a wound she kept reopening and calling protection.

She wrote:

*Elias—*

*I've started this letter eleven times, which you'll appreciate, given that you once watched me draft a single paragraph for four hours and then throw the whole notebook into the woodstove.*

*Ten years ago I wrote you a letter and didn't sign it. I told myself it was because signing it would make me vulnerable. That was half true. The other half was that I didn't believe I deserved to be found. I'd built a whole life on being the voice behind other people's names, and somewhere in there I stopped believing my own name was worth anything on its own.*

*You knew. Before I did. You stood in a doorway with a box of spilled books and looked at me like you were reading something I'd forgotten I wrote, and I ran from that look for months because I wasn't ready. Thank you for not running first.*

*My book is in the window on Harbor Street. My actual name is on it. I sign things now—all the time, everywhere, like someone practicing a signature she's only just been given. It still scares me some days. But I've learned that being known isn't the worst thing that can happen to a person. The worst thing is spending your whole life making sure it can't happen.*

*I forgive you for the years we lost. I've forgiven myself too, or I'm close enough to call it done. What I'd like now—not urgently, not desperately, the way I used to want things, secretly and all at once—is the ordinary version. Walks. Bad movies. Arguing about whose turn it is to make dinner. The chance to write you letters for the rest of our lives and sign every single one.*

*Come to the point at sunset. I'll be the woman standing in plain sight.*

*Yours, entirely and on the record,*
*Petra*

She read it twice. Her hand hovered over the signature—old reflex, old fear—and then she wrote her name beneath it in full, flourishing slightly, the way children do when they're proud of learning something new.

* * *

The walk next door took forty seconds. She made it take longer, stopping to note the roses coming in along Elias's fence, the way the light lay gold on the shingles, the sound of the tide doing its patient work below the bluff. His kitchen window was open. She could hear him moving around inside, and the ordinary domesticity of it—dishes, maybe, or the kettle—made her smile.

She slid the postcard into the mail slot on his front door rather than knocking, because some things deserved the small ceremony of discovery. Then she walked down the path toward the point, unhurried, visible against the evening sky for anyone to see.

Behind her, she heard the door open. The pause. The sound of paper being turned over.

"Petra."

Her name in his voice—not the ghost of it, not the memory, but the present-tense fact of him saying it, out loud, in the open air, where anyone could hear.

She turned around. He stood at the end of his path holding the postcard like it was something breakable and precious at once, and the sunset was starting behind him, and the tide was coming in, and her book was in a window in town with her name on it, and none of it—not one single piece of it—was hidden anymore.

"I'll meet you at the point," she called back, already walking. "I'll be the one standing in plain sight."

And for the first time in ten years, Petra Thornbury signed her life over to the daylight—and found that the daylight, all along, had been waiting to sign back.
