## 3. Terms of Engagement

The phone rang at seven in the morning, which meant it was her mother, because only her mother believed that dawn was a civilized hour for delivering judgment.

Paloma stood in the kitchen of the Old Mill House with the receiver cradled between her ear and shoulder, both hands occupied with a coffee mug that had gone cold without her noticing. Outside the window above the sink, the vineyard lay in its morning haze, rows of vines running down the slope like lines on a ledger page — and God, she thought, even her metaphors were bankrupt now. She couldn't look at anything anymore without seeing debt in it.

"Paloma. I'm confirming the numbers." Her mother's voice had the crispness of starched linen. "Margit says thirty-one for dinner Saturday, thirty-six Sunday lunch. The caterer from Vollen needs a final count by Thursday."

"I know, Mamma. I have the list."

"And you've arranged everything else?"

There it was. The question underneath the question, arriving right on schedule, three days after the first phone call and two weeks before the reunion itself. Paloma set down the mug. It clicked against the counter, a small sound, and she heard her mother hear it — heard her catalog it, file it away as evidence of something.

"The rooms are ready," Paloma said. "The mill's being repaired. The tasting room will be presentable."

"That isn't what I asked."

The morning light came through the window in a long pale bar across the floorboards, and Paloma watched a dust mote drift through it, slow as a falling leaf. Ten years ago this house had been full. Ten years ago her mother had called her *min skatt*, my treasure, and meant it. Now every call was an audit.

"You asked if I'd arranged everything," Paloma said carefully. "I have."

"A companion, then." Her mother said the word the way one might say *plumbing* or *roof tiles* — a necessary feature of a well-run household. "You'll be bringing someone. A young man. Margit has already asked me twice whether you're seeing anyone, and I cannot keep saying *I don't know, she keeps everything so private*, because you know what Margit does with privacy. She treats it like a confession."

Paloma closed her eyes. Behind them she saw Saturday evening laid out like a battlefield map: the long table under the chestnut tree, thirty-one place settings, Aunt Margit at the head with her rings and her opinions, cousin Liv with her husband who owned half a hillside in the next valley, Anders with his perfect wife and his perfect smile, everyone measuring the Old Mill House against their own holdings and finding it wanting. Finding *her* wanting. And her alone at the end of the table, the unmarried daughter, the one who'd stayed, the one whose vineyard was three payments behind on a loan the family didn't yet know about — alone, and therefore legible. Readable at a glance. *Failed.*

If she brought someone, the reading changed. Not fixed — nothing could fix what the bank letter pinned under the wine glass knew — but complicated. A man beside her was a shield made of other people's assumptions. They would look at him instead of her. They would ask him questions about himself instead of questions about her solvency. He would be, in the crudest sense, a decoy.

She just needed a decoy.

"I might be bringing someone," Paloma heard herself say.

The silence on the line lasted four seconds. Her mother could do more with four seconds of silence than most people could with a paragraph.

"*Might* is not an answer, Paloma."

"It's the answer there is right now."

"Is it someone serious?" And there — beneath the starch — a thread of something almost soft. Almost hopeful. Her mother wanted this to be true. That was somehow worse than the interrogation; hope was a debt too, and Paloma had learned not to borrow against things that couldn't be repaid.

"It's new," Paloma said. "It's... recent. I'll bring him if it goes well this week."

"This week," her mother repeated. "So he exists."

"He exists, Mamma."

"Then bring him. Or don't come at all and let us all finally stop pretending this estate is anything but what it is." A pause, and then, softer, in the voice she used when she wanted credit for mercy: "I don't want you standing at that table alone while Margit counts your empty chairs, min skatt. You understand me? Bring someone real, or bring no one and be laughed at honestly. Those are the only dignities left."

Her mother hung up before goodbye, as always, as if endings were a courtesy she'd stopped affording anyone years ago.

Paloma stood in the kitchen for a long time. The coffee had gone bitter-cold and she drank it anyway, standing at the window, watching the haze burn off the lower rows. Somewhere down past the mill, faintly, she could hear the first sounds of Theo Marchetti starting his day — the rattle of the truck, the thump of lumber being unloaded, the low whine of a saw spinning up. He'd been at the mill porch for five days now. Five days of steady, unhurried work, of *good morning* said in that quiet level voice, of sleeves rolled to the forearm and hands that moved over old wood like they were asking it questions rather than telling it answers.

He existed. That much was true. Everything else she was about to propose would be a lie.

* * *

She found him at noon on the mill bridge, which surprised her, because she'd expected him to be on the porch where the railing still gaped in two places. Instead he was crouched at the far end of the footbridge that crossed the millrace, running his thumb along a crack in the rail, testing the soundness of a post with small patient pressures. His tool belt hung loose on his hips. Sawdust clung to the dark hair on his forearms, and up close — though she tried not to notice such things — he had the kind of face that aged well in silence: strong jaw, a nose that had been broken once and set slightly proud of center, eyes the color of strong tea held up to light.

"You found the weak post," she said.

He glanced up. "Third one from the end. Someone patched it with green wood twenty years ago. It's been rotting ever since." He straightened, wiping his palms on his thighs. "Everything on this bridge is original except the repairs, and the repairs are all worse than the age."

"Story of this whole place," Paloma said, and heard the bitterness slip out before she could catch it.

Theo looked at her then — really looked, a beat longer than politeness required — and she had the strange sensation of being read by someone who wasn't going to comment on what he found. Most men looked at her and saw either the widow of the vineyard or the daughter of the Eriksen name, and adjusted accordingly. Theo looked at her like she was weather. Something to be noted accurately and waited out.

"There's shade on the porch," he said. "And I saved the last of the cold brew. If you're here to talk business, business goes better sitting down."

It was so exactly what she needed that suspicion flickered through her — how did he know she was here to talk business? — but she followed him anyway, across the bridge, up the path, to the porch he'd spent five days resurrecting. New boards pale as bone among the old gray ones. Two of the four railing sections finished, their balusters white and even. He poured the last of the coffee into a tin cup and handed it to her without asking if she wanted it, and she took it without thanking him, and some part of her brain noted with alarm how naturally they fell into these small economies, as if they'd been doing it for years.

She sat on the new boards. He stayed standing, leaning against the post, arms crossed — giving her the higher ground, she realized. Deliberately. Another thing he did without making a show of it.

"My family arrives in eleven days," she said.

"So you mentioned. The reunion."

"Thirty-six people. My aunt Margit runs it like a board meeting. My brother Anders will arrive in a car worth more than my harvest. And my mother called this morning to inform me that if I show up alone, I should show up prepared to be pitied openly rather than privately." She turned the tin cup in her hands. "Which brings me to the business."

Theo said nothing. He had a way of waiting that made silence feel like courtesy rather than pressure, and Paloma hated how much she appreciated it.

"I'd like to hire you again," she said. "For the weekend. Different work."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "I don't do catering."

A laugh escaped her — short, surprised, real. It startled her more than it seemed to startle him, though something in his face shifted at the sound, some careful composure loosening by a degree. "No catering," she agreed. "I need you to be my boyfriend."

The words hung in the air between them, absurd and enormous, and Paloma watched them land. Watched him go very still — not shocked-still, but thinking-still, the stillness of a man turning something over with great care, like checking a joint for plumb.

"For the weekend," he said at last.

"Friday evening through Sunday afternoon. You'd meet them as— as my partner. We'd have invented a history. How we met, how long it's been, whatever details survive scrutiny. My family interrogates. Margit especially. She once got a cousin's fiancé to admit he'd been married before inside of four minutes."

"And your mother thinks you already have someone."

"My mother thinks I *might* have someone. I told her it was new. Recent." Paloma set down the cup. "This morning I told her you existed. Which was true. Everything after that is negotiable."

Theo uncrossed his arms. Walked to the porch rail — the finished section — and looked out at the vineyard rows descending toward the river, golden-green in the noon light. When he spoke, his back was still to her.

"Why me?"

"Because you're here. Because you're—" *Handsome enough to be plausible. Calm enough to survive Margit. Kind enough that lying with you won't feel entirely like drowning.* "Because you can hold your own in a room full of people who measure each other for sport. Because you already know the property, so the questions about the mill won't trip you. And because you need the work, and I can pay double wages for three days."

That last part landed wrong. She saw it land wrong — saw his shoulders tighten, saw the way his head turned slightly, not quite toward her. For a moment the quiet carpenter looked like a man hearing an insult delivered in the language of generosity.

"Double wages," he repeated.

"To compensate for the indignity," Paloma said. "I'm aware of what I'm asking. I'm asking a stranger to lie to thirty-six people, several of whom are genuinely unpleasant, in exchange for money. It's not a favor. It's a job. I'd rather keep the transaction honest even if the rest of it isn't."

Now he turned around fully. The tea-colored eyes studied her, and there was something moving behind them, something she couldn't name — grief, maybe, or recognition, or the particular exhaustion of a man who has been waiting a long time for a door he both dreads and needs to open.

"If I do this," he said slowly, "there are terms."

"Name them."

"One. I don't discuss my family. If they ask, my parents are retired, they live near the coast, they prefer their privacy. It's close enough to true and it ends the topic."

"Agreed."

"Two. No physical performance beyond what the room requires. A hand at the small of your back. Sitting close. If kissing comes up—" he paused, and something crossed his face too fast to read, "—we invent a reason it doesn't. Long-distance habits. Whatever. I'm not going to use a paid weekend as license."

Something in Paloma's chest tightened unexpectedly. Disappointment, she realized, and was appalled at herself for it. *He's a hired man you met nine days ago. Get a grip.* "Understood."

"Three." He hesitated. This hesitation was different from the others — longer, weighted, like a man stepping onto ice and listening for the crack. "Three. If at any point during the weekend you want to stop — really stop, not just tired, but *done* — you say the word and we walk offstage together, no explanation owed. Either of us. Deal?"

"Deal," Paloma said. "And my term: you get paid whether the weekend goes well or badly. Double wages, Friday through Sunday, plus you keep sleeping in the east room of the mill until the restoration's done regardless. I don't want any part of this feeling like leverage."

"Generous."

"Honest," she corrected. "There's a difference, and my family wouldn't recognize it, which is exactly why I need you."

Theo looked at her for a long moment. Long enough that she became aware of the millrace murmuring below, of bees working the wildflowers along the fence line, of the fact that she was holding her breath without meaning to.

"All right," he said.

And then — and this was the part Paloma would turn over later, at two in the morning, staring at her ceiling, trying to find the shape of what had happened — he said it too quickly. Too easily. As if the answer had been waiting in him all along, rehearsed, and her question had merely given it permission to come out. As if a man who hesitated over green wood and rotten posts had not hesitated at all over the one thing that deserved the most hesitation of all.

"When do we start?" he asked.

"We start now," Paloma said, reaching for the fiction the way you reach for a rope over water. "How did we meet? We need a story by Friday."

Theo smiled then — a small, crooked, private smile, gone almost before it arrived, like something surfacing briefly from deep water.

"Leave the story to me," he said. "I've had practice knowing things about you that I shouldn't."

And before she could ask what he meant by that — before the sentence could snag on anything, before the strange little hook of it could catch — he picked up his hammer and turned back to the railing, and the afternoon filled up with the sound of nails going home into sound wood, one after another, steady as a heartbeat.
