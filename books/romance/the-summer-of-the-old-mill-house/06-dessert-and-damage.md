## 6. Dessert and Damage

Theo had dressed for a war he'd agreed to fight with a smile.

The shirt was the good one — navy linen, bought three years ago for a wedding he'd ended up working instead of attending, the receipt still folded in the drawer where he kept things that reminded him he was capable of hope. He'd shaved twice, once for the lines and once because his hands were shaking and the second pass gave them something to do. In the mirror of the cottage Paloma had lent him above the workshop, he'd practiced saying *I'm here with Paloma* until it stopped sounding like a confession.

It hadn't worked. Every rehearsal sounded like a confession, because every word he said to her these days was one, just not the kind she could hear yet.

Now he stood at the long table on the mill house terrace, under strings of lights Paloma had strung herself that afternoon — he knew because he'd watched her from the porch roof, balancing on the gutter with a staple gun between her teeth, swearing softly at the wind — and waited for the family to arrive like weather.

They arrived like weather.

* * *

The first car was a silver Audi that took the gravel drive at a speed suggesting the driver considered gravel a personal insult. The second was a black Mercedes so clean it looked like it had never encountered a road. By the time the third vehicle — an enormous SUV with a bike rack holding bicycles that had clearly never been ridden — crunched to a stop, Theo had counted eleven Eriksens and assorted attachments, and had been introduced to nine of them twice.

Aunt Margit came last, though she entered first in every way that mattered. She was small and silver-haired and moved through the terrace like a woman inspecting a property she already owned. Her handshake was brief and dry as August. Her eyes did a full inventory of Theo in four seconds — hands, shoes, teeth — and filed him somewhere between *tool* and *temporary*.

"So you're the carpenter," she said. Not a question. A classification.

"Theo Marchetti."

"Marchetti." She tasted it. "Italian?"

"My mother's side. My father's people are from further north." Further north by exactly one county, but Margit didn't need to know that the Marchetti name had been purchased by his grandfather off a merchant vessel's manifest, or that before that, his family had farmed the next valley over — had, in fact, sold fruit to this very vineyard when Paloma's grandfather ran it, back when the Old Mill House still turned a profit and nobody burned anything down.

"You're restoring the mill," Margit said. It wasn't a question either. Everything she said was a verdict wearing a question's clothes.

"Slowly," Theo said. "She deserves slow."

Something flickered across Margit's face — too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Sentimental," she said, and moved on to kiss Paloma's cheeks with the efficiency of a stamp.

Anders arrived with his wife, Lise, who was beautiful in the way magazines are beautiful, and their two children, who were beautiful in the way children of people who optimize everything are beautiful: well-rested, well-mannered, faintly bewildered. Anders himself had gone soft in the middle since the boy Theo remembered — broad shoulders settled into something comfortable, hair thinning at the temples, laugh loud enough to fill rooms he hadn't earned. When Anders shook Theo's hand, his grip lingered half a second past polite.

"Paloma says you're good with your hands," Anders said, smiling.

"I manage."

"Anders," Paloma said lightly, appearing at Theo's elbow with two glasses of the estate's rosé, "be nice, or I'll put you on dish duty."

"Dish duty builds character," Anders said. "Ask my kids." He laughed his big laugh. His eyes stayed flat.

Theo took the wine. Their fingers touched on the stem, and Paloma's mouth did the thing it did when she was performing calm — a small, controlled curve, precise as a trellis wire. Under the string lights her eyes found his and asked a question she couldn't voice in front of all these people: *Are you all right?*

He answered with a nod that meant *I'm fine* and also meant *I've waited ten years to stand beside you at this table*, and hoped the two meanings didn't show.

* * *

Dinner was served family-style on trestle tables draped in white cloth, the estate's own food mostly — roasted pork shoulder from the farm down the road, tomatoes from the kitchen garden, bread from the village bakery because Paloma's oven had died in spring and there was no money to replace it. The wine was the estate's too: a pale gold white from the old block, the vintage Theo knew by heart because he'd helped pick those grapes the summer everything ended. He'd carried crates of it up the hill with burning arms while Paloma rode the tractor and sang badly to the radio, both of them seventeen and sun-stupid and certain the world was a place that would keep making summers like that one forever.

He drank it now and it tasted like standing in a room with a ghost that only he could see.

The conversation orbited the table the way conversation does in families — harvest yields, someone's daughter's boarding school, the price of oak barrels, a cousin's divorce handled in two sentences and a raised eyebrow. Theo performed. He told the story they'd invented on the porch two nights ago, about meeting at the hardware store in town when Paloma came in asking for a part for a machine older than both of them and he'd spent forty minutes pretending not to know what it was called so he could keep talking to her.

"That's not how it happened," Paloma protested, laughing, playing her role beautifully. "I knew what it was called. You just wanted to feel useful."

"I wanted to keep looking at you. The part was secondary."

The table laughed. Even Margit's mouth made a small movement that in a warmer woman might have been a smile. Lise asked how long they'd been together, and Paloma said *eight months* without blinking, and Theo marveled again at how easily lies left her — no, not easily. That was the thing he'd learned watching her these weeks. Nothing left Paloma easily. She'd rehearsed this lie the way she pruned vines, cutting away everything unnecessary, leaving only what would bear weight. Eight months. Met at the hardware store. He lived in the cottage. He was restoring the mill.

All of it false except the last two things, which were true, and which were somehow the most dangerous truths at the table.

It was during the cheese course that the weather changed.

Margit set down her glass with a small deliberate click, the sound of a gavel, and the table quieted around her the way water quiets around a dropped stone. Theo saw Paloma go still — saw it happen in her shoulders first, then her hands, then the careful curve of her mouth reassembling itself into neutral.

"Darling," Margit said, addressing the whole table while looking at no one, "I've spoken with your mother, and with Anders, and we've all agreed it's time we discussed the future. Properly."

"Mum," Paloma said. "This is dinner."

"It's the perfect dinner for it. Everyone's here. Everyone who loves you." Margit folded her small hands. "The bank situation is not a secret, Paloma. Whatever you think you're protecting by silence, you aren't. And we've decided — the family has decided — that we'll buy you out."

The words went into Theo's chest like a nail driven flush.

"Buy me out," Paloma repeated. Flat. Careful.

"The vineyard. The debts — we'd settle them, of course, quietly, no auction, no public embarrassment. And the mill house would stay in the family, which is really what matters, isn't it? Keeping it *in the family*." Margit smiled at her guests. "You've fought alone for ten years, darling. Longer than anyone should have to. This is us telling you: you don't have to anymore. You can rest. You can have a life." Her eyes flicked to Theo, brief and dismissive. "Perhaps travel. A young woman shouldn't spend her best years bailing out a sinking boat out of stubbornness."

"It's not a sinking boat."

"No?" Margit lifted the wine, tasted, and set it down with the delicacy of a woman delivering a verdict. "Three payments behind. The equipment leases called twice in June. Your cellar stock is being sold below cost to keep the lights on. Did you think we didn't know? We know everything, Paloma. That's what family is *for*."

Around the table, the cousins studied their plates. Anders poured himself more wine and said nothing, which Theo noted, and noted hard, because Anders Eriksen never said nothing unless silence had been assigned to him.

Paloma's spine was straight as a fence post. "Thank you for the concern," she said, each word laid down like a stone in a wall. "But the Old Mill House isn't for sale."

"It's mortgaged to its roots, darling—"

"*It's not for sale.*"

"And what exactly is your plan?" Margit's voice didn't rise; it descended, cool as well water. "Another season of selling bottles out of the tasting room to tourists who find you by accident? Another winter of choosing which bills to pay? You're thirty-one years old. You've given this place your entire adult life and it has given you nothing back but debt and dust. For your own good, Paloma—"

"For my own good." Paloma laughed, and it was the worst sound Theo had ever heard her make — bright and brittle, glass about to go. "That's what everyone always—"

"She's not selling."

Theo heard his own voice come out of him and felt, distantly, like a man watching someone else drive his truck off a bridge.

The table turned. Eleven faces. Margit's eyebrows had risen a precise quarter-inch.

"Excuse me?" Margit said.

Theo set down his napkin. Some distant, rational part of him — the part that had rehearsed *boyfriend* like a role, charming, easy, forgettable — was screaming that this was the moment to laugh, deflect, squeeze Paloma's knee and say *she's got this*. But he was looking at Paloma's face, at the wall she was building brick by brick behind her eyes because there was no one in this family she could lean against, not one, not in ten years, not once — and the rational part of him lost the argument the way it lost every argument where she was concerned.

"I said she's not selling," Theo repeated. His voice was low and even and nothing like the voice he used with clients. "Not to the bank. Not to you. Not 'for her own good,' which is what people say when they mean *for our convenience*."

"Theo," Paloma said quietly. A warning. Or a plea. He couldn't tell, and couldn't stop.

"This place isn't failing," he went on. "It's been starved. There's a difference. Anyone who actually looked at the vines this season would know it — the old block is producing better fruit than it has in a decade, and that's with her doing the work of four people on a budget that wouldn't run a lemonade stand. She hasn't failed this vineyard. Everyone at this table has failed *her*."

Silence. The kind with weight and edges.

Lise's fork hovered. One of the children, mercifully, had been taken inside earlier. A cousin opened his mouth and thought better of it.

Margit recovered first, because Margit always recovered first; Theo suspected she'd been recovering from other people's feelings since the cradle. "How spirited," she said. "You've been with the family, what — eight months? And you presume to lecture us about—"

"I've known her longer than eight months."

The words were out before he could catch them, and they hung there, wrong-shaped, too heavy, and he watched Paloma's head turn toward him slowly, her eyes narrowing a fraction — not suspicion, not yet, but the first tremor along a fault line.

"I mean—" He pulled it back, hands open, smile repaired. "I mean it feels longer. When you work beside someone every day. When you watch what they carry." He turned to Margit, and let some of the truth into his voice because he couldn't hold all of it back and a little of it, at least, was safe. "You want to buy her out for her own good. Fine. Then match what she's worth. Because if this family writes her a check, it should be a check that admits what she built here alone — not a settlement that lets everyone sleep better."

Margit regarded him for a long moment. The string lights caught the silver in her hair and the frost in her eyes.

"Well," she said finally, silk over steel. "Isn't it lovely that she's found someone so protective. So *invested*." She smiled at the table. "Let's have dessert, shall we? Paloma, darling, the tart. Before it wilts."

Conversation resumed like water closing over a stone. Someone asked Anders about the marina. Lise complimented the tomatoes. The machinery of the family ground back into motion, and Theo sat in the middle of it with his heart going like a trip-hammer, knowing he'd broken the terms of his own employment, knowing he'd shown the exact face he'd promised to hide.

Then, under the table, warm and deliberate, Paloma's hand found his.

She didn't look at him. She kept her eyes on Margit, kept her performing smile fixed, passed the tart, refilled a cousin's glass — all with her right hand, while her left slid across the white cloth's edge and closed around his fingers beneath the table's shadow. Her grip was fierce. Her thumb moved once across his knuckles, a stroke so small it might have been accidental, except nothing Paloma Eriksen did was ever accidental.

*Thank you,* the hand said.

*I'm sorry,* his answered back, and meant a dozen other things besides.

* * *

Dessert was an apricot tart from the kitchen garden, and Theo ate his without tasting any of it.

He watched Margit work the table after the announcement — watched her move from cousin to cousin, collecting nods, consolidating the vote before it was ever taken, the way she must have done for decades. Watched Anders laugh at his phone under the table, thumbs moving, and felt something cold slide down his spine that had nothing to do with the evening air. Watched Paloma hold court over her own ambush with nothing but posture and a smile, pouring coffee, refusing help, declining sympathy, magnificent and utterly alone.

When the plates were cleared, the family drifted toward the terrace rail to look at the sunset over the valley — the vineyard rows going bronze and violet, the mill's wheel silhouette black against the last light. Theo stood apart, near the table, gathering glasses he didn't need to gather, needing his hands busy.

Paloma came to him. Close enough that no one across the terrace could hear, not over the cicadas and the children being summoned to cars.

"That was not in the contract," she murmured.

"I know."

"'Charming, agreeable, keeps opinions about the estate to himself.' Your words."

"I know."

She looked up at him. In the fading light her eyes were the color of the wine in the old block — pale gold going amber — and there was something in them he hadn't seen in ten years, not since the night the sky over the east field turned orange and everything changed. Not suspicion. Not anger.

Recognition.

"Theo," she said, very quietly. "Who *are* you?"

Behind them, at the rail, Margit laughed at something Anders said, and the sound carried across the terrace like a bell over deep water.

"Tonight," Theo said, "I'm the man who's staying for dessert."

Her hand was still in his under the tablecloth. Neither of them moved to take it back.
