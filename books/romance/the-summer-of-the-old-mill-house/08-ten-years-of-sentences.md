## 8. Ten Years of Sentences

Theo woke before dawn in the narrow room above the mill workshop, the way he had woken every morning for ten years — not to an alarm but to a sentence.

*I saw what started the fire.*

It arrived first, always, before his eyes opened, before the birds began their rough tuning in the vine rows below the window. It sat on his chest like a sleeping cat, warm and heavy and impossible to dislodge. Some mornings he could lie still beneath it for an hour, breathing carefully, letting it purr. Other mornings — like this one — it dug its claws in.

He got up. The floorboards knew his weight now; three weeks of living here had taught them, or he had taught himself their language, the particular complaint of the third board from the door that he'd been meaning to plane flat since his second day. He dressed in the dark out of habit. In the dark, a man could pretend the letter wasn't there.

But the letter was there. It was always there. Folded into thirds inside the inner pocket of the canvas jacket hanging on the back of the door, where he had carried it through four states and two false starts and one entire year of telling himself he would never come back at all. He could feel it from across the room, the way you can feel a stone in your shoe even after you've taken the shoe off.

He made coffee in the dented pot on the hotplate and carried it out onto the porch he had rebuilt with his own hands.

That was the joke of it, if there was a joke. He had spent six weeks straightening the sagging joists of this porch, sistering new timber against old, shimming, leveling, making honest what time and rot had made crooked. And all the while the crookedest thing within a mile was standing right on it, holding a hammer, whistling.

The valley was gray-blue and beautiful in the first light. Mist lay along the vineyard like a sheet someone had laid over furniture in a house left empty. Down the slope, the Old Mill House itself showed only its roofline and one lit window — Paloma's window, though he had no right to know which window was hers except that he did, he had always known, some knowledge outlasting even the person it was about. She was up. She was always up. He had watched her light come on at five-forty every morning since he'd arrived, and every morning he had stood here like a man watching a lighthouse and lying to himself about why he'd climbed the cliff.

*Ten years is long enough for anything.* That's what he'd told himself on the bus from the coast, when the vineyards started sliding past the window and his hands went cold around the strap of his bag. Ten years is long enough. People change. People forgive. People forget.

People do not forget. He knew that now. He had seen her face three weeks ago when she'd looked at him across the mill yard and seen nothing — no flicker, no ghost, just a stranger with a toolbox and a neighbor's recommendation. He should have felt relief. Instead he had felt something closer to grief: the peculiar loss of being unrecognized by the one person whose recognition he had dreaded and craved in equal measure for a decade.

She didn't know him. But she was beginning to know him — the man he'd become instead of the boy he'd been — and that was worse. Because the man he'd become was built entirely on top of a locked room, and she kept walking past the door, trailing her fingers along the wall, saying things like *you have an old soul* and *it's like you've been here before*, and every word was a key testing a lock.

And last night — God, last night. She had told him about the fire. About the harvest festival, the lanterns, the boy named Teo who vanished, the accusation that had settled over her family like ash that never washed out. She had told it plainly, without self-pity, sitting on the steps of the porch he'd repaired, her wine glass forgotten in her hand, and she had said, "They never found what started it. Sometimes I think I'm the only one who still wants to know."

And Theo had gone so still that a moth landed on his sleeve and stayed there, opening and closing its wings, and he had let it, because moving might have broken something open in his chest that could not be closed again.

*I want to know too,* he had almost said. *I know. I've always known.*

He had said none of it. He had said, "Maybe the truth keeps," which was a coward's proverb, and she had looked at him with those gray eyes — older now, guarded, pruned back hard like the vines she tended — and she had said, "Truth doesn't keep, Theo. It ferments." And then she'd laughed at herself and gone inside, and he had sat on the porch until two in the morning listening to the mill creak and rehearsing.

Because that was the thing about a confession carried for ten years: you rehearsed it whether you meant to or not. It lived in you the way a song lives in you, surfacing in the shower, in the truck, in the space between waking and sleep. Theo had versions the way other men had ex-girlfriends. There was the short version — *I saw Anders Eriksen knock over the lanterns* — eleven words, deliverable in four seconds. There was the version for Henrik, spoken once, in a hospital corridor smelling of antiseptic and dying, when the old man had gripped his wrist with terrible strength and said, *You were there, boy. Say it before I can't hear it.* There was the long version, the full one, with the whole architecture of that night in it — the music, the heat, the smell of spilled wine and lamp oil, Anders laughing, stumbling, the flame going up the dry wall like it had been waiting years for permission — and the part after, the part nobody knew, the part that was his alone: Margit's car in the lane at midnight, Margit's voice low and level as a spirit gauge, Margit pressing an envelope into a terrified sixteen-year-old's hand and saying, *You saw nothing. Boys who see nothing go far. Boys who see things stay poor.*

He had taken the envelope. That was the sentence under all the other sentences, the one he never said aloud even in rehearsal. He had taken the money — four thousand dollars, more than his mother made in a season — because his mother was crying in the kitchen over bills, because he was sixteen, because Margit Eriksen had a way of making a bribe sound like a scholarship. He had told himself he was protecting his family. Then he had told himself he was protecting Paloma, because surely the truth would land on her family like a hammer either way, and better a vanished boy than a ruined girl. Then, somewhere around year five, he had run out of stories and admitted the plain shape of it: he had been afraid, and he had been paid, and he had run, and every year he didn't go back the silence grew another ring around him like the trunk of a tree, until he couldn't tell anymore whether the silence was something he carried or something he was.

His lawyer — a patient, tired woman named Okafor whom he paid out of savings that were supposed to be a house down payment — had given him until the end of the month. The subpoena had finally come through in spring; the county was reopening the inquiry, quietly, the way such things are done when enough time has passed that the powerful have stopped watching quite so closely. *We can compel you,* she'd written, *but I'd rather you came willingly. Voluntary testimony reads as conscience. Compelled testimony reads as grievance. Juries know the difference.* And then, in the postscript, the line he'd read forty times: *Theo, whatever you decide, decide soon. Witnesses who wait too long start to look like they're deciding something else.*

Month's end. Nine days.

He drank his coffee and watched Paloma's window go dark as she came downstairs, and he thought about the strange arithmetic of his life: that he had crossed half the country to keep a promise he hadn't yet made, and that the reunion — the family, the creditors, the whole theater of it — had seemed, from a distance, like cover. A reason to be here that wasn't the real reason. A carpenter's job that happened to put him in the valley the same week the Eriksens gathered. Coincidence, he'd called it, when the neighbor recommended him. Guilt wearing coincidence's clothes.

Then she'd asked him to be her boyfriend, and the plan — if a thing so formless deserved the word — had tilted sideways. Because pretending to love Paloma Eriksen turned out to be the easiest work he had ever done, and easy work is dangerous work, and every hour of it pried the locked room open another inch.

* * *

He spent the morning replacing a rotted sill in the mill's north wall, working alone because Paloma had driven into town to meet the bank manager, and he was grateful for the solitude and hated it in the same breath. Solitude was where the sentences lived.

By noon the heat had thickened and he gave up on the sill and went inside the mill proper, into the cool stone throat of the old grinding floor, where the light came down through the lucarne in a single dusty column. He'd restored the mechanism's frame in his first month — new oak teeth for the great gear, trued by hand — and sometimes he came in here just to stand among the beams he'd made honest, because wood didn't lie. Wood told you exactly what it had endured. Grain remembered everything and hid nothing.

If only people were built like beams.

He took off his jacket — the canvas one, the one with the pocket — and hung it on the nail by the stair, and then he stood there looking at it, and then, because nine days is nine days, he took the letter out.

He knew it by heart, but he unfolded it anyway, the paper gone soft at the creases, and read it again in the dusty light.

*Mr. Marchetti —*

*Confirming our conversation of the 14th. The county counsel has scheduled preliminary hearings for the last week of the month. Your statement regarding the night of October 3rd remains the pivotal piece. Without it, the inquiry stalls and the file closes again, likely permanently. With it—*

*With it, we proceed. You understand what proceeding means. The Eriksen name will be in the papers. Questions will be asked about why it took ten years. You will be asked why you left, and where you went, and who paid you, and your answers will help some people and hurt others, and I cannot control which.*

*What I can tell you is this: the injured party — Ms. P. Eriksen — has spent a decade under a cloud that was never hers. Whatever else you weigh, weigh that.*

*Decide soon.*

*— R. Okafor*

*Ms. P. Eriksen.* Even Okafor's letter reduced Paloma to an initial, a party, an injured interest. As if she were a line item. As if she weren't a woman who got up at five-forty to check moisture on leaves that might already be lost, who laughed at her own jokes a half-second before finishing them, who had held his hand under a dinner table and changed the temperature of his blood with her thumb.

He refolded the letter. Put it back. Took it out again. This was the ritual, the rosary of it: fold, unfold, the creases wearing thinner each time, until one day the paper would split along the fold and the words would fall out of it and he would have to say them with his mouth instead of his eyes.

Because here was the arithmetic that kept him awake, the equation with no clean solution:

If he testified, the truth would come out — but so would the scandal, and the scandal would land on Paloma at the exact moment she was fighting for the vineyard's life. Margit's lawyer had already sniffed around; Theo had seen the black sedan idling by the gate twice this week, taking photographs of the mill, of him. Margit knew he was back. Margit had known since the first week, he'd bet his remaining savings on it, and Margit Eriksen did not survive ten years of comfortable silence by letting witnesses get comfortable. If he spoke, Margit would burn the vineyard down around Paloma financially — hidden liens, called-in debts, a whisper campaign dressed as concern — and she would do it smiling, at a family table, with a glass of the very wine she was strangling.

And if he stayed silent — if he let the file close, if he took the coward's exit one final time — then Paloma would live and die under a rumor she'd done nothing to earn, and Henrik would die having heard the truth confirmed by no one but a frightened boy in a hospital corridor, and Theo would spend the rest of his life as a load-bearing beam over a rotten core, holding up a house that deserved better.

Either way, he lost her. That was the part he couldn't say to Okafor, the part that made the letter heavier than paper. Testify, and she learns the man she's falling for is the boy who abandoned her and took the money. Stay silent, and she never learns anything, which is its own abandonment, slower and quieter.

Unless.

He set the letter on the grinding floor's oak sill and pressed it flat with his palm, and for a long moment he let himself imagine the third path, the one he'd been circling for three weeks like a moth around the lucarne light: that he told her first. Not the lawyers, not the county, not the family — her. Before the hearing, before the papers, before Margit could weaponize any of it. He walked up to the Old Mill House, and he found her among the vines or at the kitchen table with her ledger of failing numbers, and he knelt down inside the truth and said: *I am Teo. I am the boy from the festival. I saw your brother knock over the lanterns, and I saw your aunt pay me to vanish, and I took it, and I ran, and I have spent ten years building sentences I never finished, and I am here to finish them, starting with the ones I owe you.*

And she would look at him the way she'd looked at him last night on the porch — wanting to know, wanting it so badly it frightened her — and then she would look at him the other way, the way people look at the person who let them carry a weight alone for a decade, and whichever look lasted longer, that would be his sentence.

He laughed aloud in the empty mill, a short ugly sound that the stones swallowed. Rehearsing the rehearsal now. A man could disappear up his own confession like that, spiraling inward forever, and some days he suspected that was exactly what he'd been doing for ten years — mistaking preparation for penance.

Outside, gravel crunched. A car coming up the drive — early, unhurried, engine note wrong for Paloma's truck. Theo folded the letter along its softening crease, slid it home into the jacket pocket, and stepped out of the mill's shadow into the white heat of the afternoon.

A black sedan sat by the gate. The driver's window lowered, and a man in a gray suit — Margit's lawyer, the one from the photographs, the one with the notebook — looked at Theo the way an appraiser looks at a piece of furniture he's already decided to undervalue.

"Mr. Marchetti," the man said pleasantly. "Got a minute? I think we should talk about what happens at the end of the month."

Nine days. The sentence on Theo's chest dug in its claws, and he walked toward the car anyway, because running was the one version of the story he refused to rehearse ever again.
