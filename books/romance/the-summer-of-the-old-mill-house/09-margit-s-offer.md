## 9. Margit's Offer

The house exhaled when the last car left.

Paloma stood in the gravel drive as the taillights of Anders's Audi shrank down the lane and dissolved into the dark beyond the cypress hedge, and she felt the particular exhaustion that comes not from work but from performance — two full days of smiling, of refilling glasses, of answering *how are you managing* with a laugh calibrated to the millimeter. The reunion was over. The dishes were stacked in the kitchen like evidence. Theo had carried the last of the folding chairs into the barn without being asked, and now his truck sat alone by the mill, its headlights off, and she could see the ember of his cigarette moving slowly on the porch where he'd said he'd wait until she came out or told him to go home.

She hadn't decided which yet.

Behind her, the Old Mill House glowed with the leftover warmth of the weekend — every lamp lit because Margit disliked dim rooms, every window thrown open because Margit disliked stuffiness, and now all that borrowed light spilled across the flagstones like something Paloma would have to pay back later. She thought about the ledger in her desk drawer, three payments behind, the bank's final notice still pinned under the same wine glass where it had lived for eleven days. She thought about how many times this weekend someone had asked her, with varying degrees of delicacy, whether she'd considered *options*.

And then she heard the click of Margit's heels on the terrace behind her, unhurried, deliberate, the sound of a woman who had waited for exactly this moment — the moment when everyone else was gone and the house belonged only to the two of them.

"There you are," Margit said. "I've been looking for a word with you all evening."

Paloma turned. Her aunt stood at the top of the terrace steps in a silk blouse the color of cream, silver hair pinned with architectural precision, holding two small glasses of the estate's own grappa as though they were props she had brought for a scene she had already rehearsed. At sixty-eight, Margit Eriksen-Voss had the posture of a woman who had never once been caught off guard, and Paloma had spent her whole life believing that posture was natural. Only now, watching her aunt descend the steps with the grappa held out like an offering, did it occur to her that everything about Margit was rehearsed. Everything was a scene. It always had been.

"We're out of the good glasses," Paloma said. "You got the last of them."

"I brought my own." Margit pressed one of the small glasses into her hand. Her fingers were cool and dry. "Sit with me a moment, child. The night is finally quiet."

*Child.* Paloma was thirty-one years old and had buried a father and nearly buried a vineyard, but to Margit she would be *child* until one of them died, and possibly after.

They sat on the stone bench beneath the wisteria, which was past its bloom and dropping dry petals onto the flagstones like confetti from a party nobody remembered throwing. From here they could see the mill, black against the deepening blue of the sky, and the single point of Theo's cigarette glowing on the porch like a patient star. Margit followed her gaze.

"Your carpenter," she said. "Marchetti. He's very attentive."

"He's very well paid."

"Mm." Margit sipped her grappa. "That's what I wondered."

Paloma kept her face still. Two days of practice helped. "He's restoring the porch. He's good at his work. That's all anyone needs to know."

"Of course." A pause, measured out like medicine. "It's just that he has a way of looking at you. As if he's checking a calculation. I noticed it at dinner yesterday, when Anders mentioned the harvest yields. And again today, when your mother asked about the barn." Margit turned her head, and in the light spilling from the house her eyes were pale and unblinking, the eyes of a bird deciding whether something was food or threat. "Men don't look at women that way unless there's history in it. Or arithmetic."

"You've been drinking the grappa too long, Aunt Margit."

"Perhaps." Margit smiled, and it was a beautiful smile, the kind that had opened doors for her in three countries. "But let's not waste the evening on him. He'll keep. I wanted to talk to you about something more important." She set her glass down on the bench between them, precisely centered, precisely aligned. "I wanted to talk about your future."

Here it comes, Paloma thought. The options speech. She braced herself the way she braced against the cold wind that came down the valley some nights, the kind that made the vines shudder and the old shutters knock.

"The bank," Margit began, "has been writing to you since March."

"You've seen my mail?"

"I've seen the notices posted on the courthouse board, which is public, and I've spoken with Halvorsen at the bank, who is a friend of thirty years and talks too much when he's flattered." Margit said it without apology, without even the pretense of one. "Three payments behind. A restructuring request denied. An auction date pending confirmation. You have perhaps thirty days, Paloma. Perhaps fewer. I'm telling you this so you understand that I am not speaking to you tonight as a relative making conversation. I'm speaking to you as the only person in this family who can actually help you."

The wind moved through the vineyard below them, a long low breath through ten thousand rows, and Paloma listened to it and said nothing, because anything she said would come out either begging or bleeding, and she refused to do either in front of this woman.

"Here is what I propose." Margit's voice changed — softened, even, taking on the warm reasonable tone she used at family councils, the tone that made disagreement feel like bad manners. "The family will purchase your debts. All of them. Tonight, if necessary — I've already spoken with our lawyer, and the paperwork can be drawn up by Wednesday. The bank is paid in full. The auction dissolves. Your mother stops lying awake worrying, and you—" she reached over and touched Paloma's wrist, lightly, almost tenderly, "—you get to breathe again. Isn't that what you want? To breathe?"

"And in exchange," Paloma said quietly.

Margit withdrew her hand. For a moment the mask flickered — not into anger, nothing so crude, but into something cooler and more honest, the face beneath the face. "In exchange, the estate passes into the family trust. You retain the house — the Mill House itself, your father's rooms, the garden. You'd live here rent-free for the rest of your life, if you wished. A generous arrangement. Better than generous. No one would ever need to know how close you came to losing everything."

"No one would ever need to know," Paloma repeated. "Because the Eriksens don't fail. Is that it? The name stays clean."

"The name is the only thing that survives us," Margit said simply. "Land burns. Vines die. Money comes and goes. But the name—" she lifted her glass slightly, a toast to it, "—the name is forever, if we keep it."

Paloma looked down at her own untouched grappa. The liquid caught the lamplight and threw it back amber and trembling. Somewhere down the slope a nightingale started up, ragged and insistent, the same birdsong that had scored every summer of her childhood.

"And the mill?" she asked. "The mill itself goes into the trust."

Something crossed Margit's face then — quick, involuntary, gone before it finished arriving. Not guilt. Paloma knew guilt; she saw it in the mirror some mornings. This was closer to caution. The look of a woman stepping around a hole she knew was in the road.

"The mill is part of the estate," Margit said. "Naturally."

"Naturally." Paloma set her glass down beside her aunt's, aligning it the way Margit had aligned hers, a small useless mimicry. "You know, I've been sitting here trying to remember when you last visited before this weekend. Do you know what I came up with? Fourteen years. Fourteen years, Aunt Margit. You didn't come when Papa died. You sent flowers and a card signed by your lawyer. You didn't come when the frost took the south field, or when Mama had her surgery, or any of the hundred hard nights this house has had. And now, in the space of one weekend, you've asked me four times about the bank, twice about the mill's structural survey — yes, I heard you asking Anders — and once, very casually, over breakfast, about whether Papa ever found the old insurance papers from before the fire."

The nightingale sang. The cigarette on the mill porch had stopped moving; Theo was standing at the rail now, a still shape against the dark, and Paloma had the sudden irrational certainty that he could hear every word.

Margit was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, the warmth was gone entirely, and what remained was worse: precision. "You always were the observant one. Like your father." She folded her hands in her lap. "Very well. Since you insist on dealing in truths, let me deal you one. There is a rumor. An old one. It surfaces every few years — usually when someone in this family wants leverage, usually around auction season, when strangers start digging into titles and histories. A rumor about the fire. About the summer of the fire."

The words went through Paloma like cold water through cloth. Nineteen years old. The harvest festival. Lanterns strung through the old barn like a constellation brought down to earth. Teo's hand in hers, flour on his knuckles from the festival bread, his laugh under the music. Then the shout, the orange bloom, the heat that drove them all back across the yard while the barn that had stood for a hundred and forty years went up like a paper lantern itself. And Teo — gone. Vanished between one hour and the next, his family's caravan gone from Henrik's field by morning, no note, no goodbye, and by winter the whispers had begun: the Eriksens knew something. The Eriksen girl knew something. Why else did the investigation close so neatly, so quickly, so *quietly*?

"I know the rumor," Paloma said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "I've carried it half my life. Strangers still ask me about it when they learn which family I'm from."

"Yes. And it is unfortunate, and unfair, and it has cost this family opportunities — buyers who withdraw, banks that hesitate, partners who suddenly remember other engagements." Margit leaned forward, and the lamplight carved her face into planes and shadows. "Part of what the family would acquire, in settling your affairs, is the responsibility for that rumor. We would bury it. Properly, permanently, the way it should have been buried years ago. You would never hear it whispered again. Neither would your mother. Wouldn't that be worth something, Paloma? To be free of it? You've paid for that fire your entire adult life — in reputation, in credit, in every door that closed a little faster when the Eriksen name was on the application. Sign the papers, and it ends. All of it. The debt and the ghost together."

For a moment — and she would hate herself for this later, would turn it over at three in the morning like a stone with a worm under it — for one whole moment Paloma wanted it. Wanted it with a physical ache, the ache of a woman who had been swimming for ten years and was being offered a raft. Thirty days from auction. A mother who cried in the pantry. A lifetime of whispers ending with one signature.

Then she looked at the mill.

Theo was still at the rail. He had taken off his shirt — no, rolled up his sleeves, she corrected herself, the sleeves of his work shirt rolled to the elbow the way he wore them every day, forearms bare in the lamplight spilling from the mill window, hands resting on the rail he himself had rebuilt plank by plank over these past weeks. He had straightened the porch posts that had sagged since before her birth. He had replaced the rotted sill without charging her for the extra lumber, saying only, *a building remembers what you do to it.* He had looked at her, sometimes, with that expression Margit had named — checking a calculation — and she had told herself it meant nothing, and she had not believed herself.

A building remembers what you do to it.

And the mill — the mill was where the fire had started. The old barn had stood forty meters from the mill wheel. Whatever truth was buried under that rumor, whatever her family had paid to make quiet, it lived in those stones, in that ground, in the very beams Theo was restoring with such patience. If she signed, the mill went into the trust, and the trust was Margit, and whatever was buried there would stay buried — along with, she understood now, with terrible clarity, whatever Margit had spent fourteen years and no small amount of money keeping buried.

"You didn't come here to rescue me," Paloma said. She stood, slowly, her legs stiff from the stone bench. "You came because the auction would put the estate on the open market. Public records. Public surveys. Strangers walking the mill with flashlights and lawyers. People who might ask questions that family doesn't ask." She looked down at her aunt, and it gave her no pleasure at all, none, to see the stillness in Margit's face — the stillness of a chess player whose opponent has finally touched the right piece. "The reunion was never about concern. It was reconnaissance."

Margit rose too, unhurried, smoothing her blouse. She was taller than Paloma remembered, or perhaps it was only that she had spent a lifetime teaching shorter people to feel small. "Careful, child," she said softly. "Deals like mine don't stay open long. And neither does goodwill." She picked up both empty glasses, stacking them with a small clean click. "Wednesday. Have your answer by Wednesday. Think about your mother. Think about how much longer she can watch you fight this alone."

"She's never watched me fight alone," Paloma said. "She just thinks she has."

Margit paused at the terrace door, silhouetted against the bright kitchen, and for one strange instant she looked old — genuinely old, the architecture showing through the plaster. "Your father said something like that once," she said. "Right before he stopped being welcome at family councils." Then she was gone, heels clicking away down the hall, and the house swallowed her.

Paloma stayed on the terrace a long time. The nightingale gave up. The wind turned and came up from the valley carrying the green smell of the vines, and somewhere in the dark the mill wheel turned slowly in the stream, the way it had turned all her life, patient as a clock.

When she finally walked down the path toward the mill, Theo was waiting at the bottom of the steps. He had heard. She could see it in his face — not the words, perhaps, but the shape of them, the way you can read weather through a wall. He didn't ask. He just stood there in the lamplight with his sleeves rolled up and his hands scarred and steady, and he said, quietly:

"Whatever she offered — whatever she threatened — you should know something first."

Paloma stopped on the step above him, so that for once they were nearly eye to eye.

"What?" she said. "What should I know?"

His jaw worked once, twice, like a man testing a rope before trusting it with his weight. Down in the valley, thunder muttered — the storm the forecasters had promised all week, still hours away but coming.

"The fire," Theo said. "I know things about the fire that you don't."
