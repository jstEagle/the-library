## 1. Measured to the Gram

The oven thermometer read 175 degrees, and Kestrel Okafor trusted it more than he trusted most people.

He stood in the blue-dark of his kitchen at 4:17 a.m., the only light coming from the pilot flames and the streetlamp outside that turned the frost on the window into a field of small white stars. The Sweet Garden was silent except for the hum of the proofing cabinet and the tick of the ancient wall clock his grandmother might have owned, though she hadn't, because Kestrel had bought it himself at a yard sale eleven years ago and never told anyone why he'd wanted a clock that ran four minutes slow.

Four minutes slow meant he was always early. Four minutes slow meant the morning arrived on his terms.

He cracked eggs into a steel bowl, one at a time, each shell tapped twice against the rim—never three times, never once. Twice. The yolks slid out whole and gold. He weighed the sugar on the digital scale: 340 grams. He weighed it again, because the number on the display was the only conversation he'd had in hours that he could trust, and 340 was a good number. A stable number. A number that would still be 340 when the sun came up and the doors opened and the town of Marrow Creek came shuffling in wanting croissants and comfort and, if he was unlucky, conversation.

Emotion, Kestrel had learned at twenty-two and relearned every day since, was an ingredient like any other. It could be measured. It could be portioned. It could be folded into a batter at the right moment, in the right quantity, and it would behave. Grief, for instance: one tablespoon, incorporated slowly, never added to hot fat or it would seize. Joy: use sparingly, and always at the end, as a garnish, because joy left out at room temperature attracted things. Wanting—that was the dangerous one. Wanting had to be kept in the walk-in, sealed, labeled, dated, and thrown out before it could spoil everything it touched.

He had a system. The system had kept him alive for ten years.

The system was three missed payments from falling apart.

Kestrel did not think about this. Kestrel thought about lamination. He turned the cold butter block out onto the floured marble and began the first fold, the dough sighing under the pin, butter pressing into pale layers, and he counted the turns the way other people counted breaths. One. Quarter turn. Two. The rhythm was a metronome, and inside the metronome there was no bank, no letter, no red-stained ledger page waiting in the office drawer with its column of numbers that had stopped being numbers and started being teeth.

Outside, the first snow of the season was supposed to arrive by Thursday. The forecast said a storm, maybe a bad one, the kind that closed the mountain road and emptied the town for days. Kestrel had heard it on the radio while he toasted his one slice of bread and eaten it standing at the counter like a man refueling a machine rather than feeding a body. A bad storm meant no customers. No customers meant the weekend's till would be thin. The till was already thin. The till had been on a diet for eight months and was beginning to look like a ghost of itself.

Fold. Turn. Fold.

The butter tore slightly at the edge. A hairline fracture in the third layer, invisible to anyone but him, a flaw that would become a flat spot, a dense patch, a croissant that rose unevenly. He stood with his palms flat on the marble and looked at it for a long moment.

It was fine. It would bake fine. No one would ever know.

That was the trick, he thought, resuming the pin. That was the whole trick of his life, learned young and perfected in a culinary school kitchen a decade ago: the flaw you felt but no one saw. The layer that had torn. The croissant that rose crooked and got sold anyway, and the customer smiled, and the world turned, and the flaw stayed secret, and the secret got heavier, and you carried it, and you got very, very strong at carrying.

The clock said 5:03. It was really 5:07.

* * *

At 5:40 the delivery truck rumbled into the alley and Kestrel let in the flour, the butter, the crates of apples from the co-op—last of the local season, bruised and honey-sweet, the kind that made his orchard-less heart do something complicated he refused to examine. Dara, his one employee, arrived at six with her hood up and her headphones around her neck and her usual greeting, which was not a greeting at all but an assessment.

"You look like a man who slept in the walk-in again."

"I slept fine."

"Your couch is in your office. I've seen it. It has a blanket on it that I've never seen you wash."

"The blanket is decorative."

"The blanket is a cry for help, Kestrel." She hung her coat on the hook, tied her apron with the efficiency of someone who'd done it a thousand times, and stopped, because Dara noticed things, which was her best quality and the reason he'd hired her and occasionally the reason he regretted it. Her eyes went to the office door, which he'd left closed, which he always left closed. "Letter came yesterday. I put it with the rest."

"With the rest" was the stack on the desk he did not open until after close, when the café was empty and he could sit with the envelopes the way a man sits with a diagnosis. He had a system for that too: bills sorted by due date, notices sorted by severity, the ones with windows cut in them—the government ones, the bank ones—sorted into a pile he privately called *later*. *Later* had grown. *Later* was now a geological formation.

"Did you open it?" he asked.

"I don't open your mail."

"Good."

"But I saw the return address through the window, because I'm not blind, Kestrel, and it was Halloway and Finch, and I know what that firm does because my cousin went through it with her restaurant in Ashford." Dara folded her arms. Flour dusted one elbow. "So."

"So."

"So are we going to talk about it, or are you going to fold butter at it until it goes away?"

"I'm going to fold butter," Kestrel said, "because butter responds to technique."

Dara held his gaze for three full seconds—the longest anyone had held it in months—and then, mercifully, let it go. "The vitellois need to come out in ten. And Kestrel?" She was already walking to the sink. "Butter responds to technique. Banks respond to money."

* * *

He opened the letter at 2:14 p.m., in the dead hours between the lunch rush and the afternoon lull, when the café was down to two customers: an old man doing a crossword in the window seat and a teenager nursing a hot chocolate she'd been stretching for an hour. The rain had started early, a cold needling rain that promised snow, and the windows had gone soft and grey, and the whole café had the underwater hush Kestrel usually loved.

He took the envelope into the office and closed the door and sat on the decorative blanket's couch and opened it with a paring knife, because his hands needed a job or they'd shake.

*Dear Mr. Okafor,*

*This firm represents Greenfield Commercial Lending in the matter of your outstanding obligations...*

He read it the way he read everything now: fast, twice, looking for the number. There it was, in the third paragraph, in the flat language lawyers used for other people's catastrophes. *Three payments in arrears. Ninety days from the date of this notice to bring the account current, failing which the lender will initiate foreclosure proceedings.*

Ninety days. He did the math without wanting to, the math doing itself the way it always did, the way his mother's had, the way arithmetic does for people who grew up watching a parent lose something. Ninety days at his best week's take—last spring, before the highway reroute, before the new bakery chain opened out by the interstate, before everything—ninety days at his best was barely half. At his current average, it wasn't a quarter.

The letter went on. Options, it said. Restructuring, it said. *We encourage you to contact our office at your earliest convenience.*

Kestrel set the letter down on the desk, aligned its edges with the desk's edge, and looked at it for a long time.

Here was the thing about panic that no one told you, or that they told you and you didn't believe until it happened: it didn't feel like screaming. It felt like quiet. It felt like a very still kitchen and a very steady hand and a voice in your head that said, *weigh the sugar, Kestrel, 340 grams, fold the dough, quarter turn, there, you see? Everything is fine. Everything is measurable. Everything is under control,* while underneath the voice, in the dark under the floorboards of you, something was pacing and pacing and pacing.

He had built The Sweet Garden with his own hands. That was not a figure of speech. He had sanded the tables himself, hung the shelves, painted the sign—the sweet garden, in green, with the little apple blossom his mother had sketched for him the year before she died, back when the sketch was a joke between them, back when the garden was a metaphor and not a debt load. He had opened the doors at twenty-six with a loan, a plan, and a firm belief that if he controlled every variable, nothing could hurt him.

The highway had been a variable. The chain bakery had been a variable. The economy, the winter, the year the town's biggest employer laid off two hundred people—variables, all of them, and no technique on earth could fold them flat.

He got up. He washed his hands. He went back out to the kitchen, and Dara looked up from her station, and he said, in a voice so level it surprised even him, "We should talk about the winter menu," and Dara's face did something complicated and sad, and she said, "Sure, chef," and that was that.

That was always that.

* * *

He closed at six. He counted the till twice. He wrote the numbers in the ledger in his small, square handwriting, and the numbers were bad, and he wrote them anyway, because a number written down is a number contained, and contained things can't get out and hurt you.

Then he made himself do the thing he'd been not doing all day. He sat at the little table by the window with a cup of coffee going cold and he thought, properly thought, the way he'd once been taught to think at culinary school, back when he was twenty-two and brilliant and didn't know yet that brilliance was a thing you could lose, or a thing that could be taken from you by a boy with a beautiful mouth and a pen.

Options. List them. Weigh them.

One: the loan. He'd already been refused once, in September, by a woman at the bank who'd looked at his statements with the polite pity of a nurse reading a chart. Refusing to try again was pride, and pride was not an ingredient he could afford.

Two: the chain. Sweetleaf Bakery had offered to buy him out twice this year. The second offer had been almost generous. He'd said no in under a second both times, and he knew exactly why: because The Sweet Garden was the last thing his mother had touched, the sketch on the sign, and selling it would be—

He didn't finish the thought. He poured it out, like overproofed starter. Not today.

Three: something else. Something he hadn't thought of yet. Some door he hadn't noticed because he'd been too busy folding butter at the walls.

He was on his second cold coffee, the rain ticking against the glass, when he saw it.

The town's free paper came on Thursdays, but Dara had left a copy on the counter—the *Marrow Creek Courier*, twelve pages, mostly ads for snow tires and a church supper. He'd read the front page that morning without absorbing it. Now his eye caught on page four, on the arts section, on a headline in a font he knew, because he'd seen that font ten years ago in a student critique that had ended one life and begun another.

*RENOWNED FOOD WRITER THEO MARCHETTI TO JUDGE HARVEST TABLE COMPETITION.*

Below it, a photograph. Theo at thirty-two looked like Theo at twenty-two would look if the world had spent a decade polishing him: taller in the frame than he'd ever seemed in person, dark hair swept back from a face that was all clean angles and warm brown eyes and that mouth, that particular mouth, which Kestrel's body remembered with a specificity his mind had spent ten years denying it had. He was leaning on a counter in the photo, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with flour, smiling at the camera like the camera was an old friend.

*Marchetti, whose weekly column "The Honest Table" is syndicated in forty papers and whose debut cookbook spent six months on the bestseller list, will return to his roots this December to head the judging panel of the regional Harvest Table competition...*

Kestrel read the paragraph twice. Then he read it a third time, and the third time the words stopped being words and became a sound, a low ringing, like a fork struck against a crystal glass.

Theo Marchetti was coming back.

Theo Marchetti—the boy from the second-year pastry practicum who'd beaten him on every technical until Kestrel beat him on the one that mattered, the boy who'd kissed him in the dark of the proofing room with flour on his hands and then, forty-eight hours later, stood up in front of the entire faculty and dismantled Kestrel's final project with surgical, published, *praised* cruelty—*technically proficient but emotionally inert, a technician's dessert from a technician's palate, one waits in vain for the man behind the measurement*—that Theo Marchetti was coming back to Marrow Creek, to judge a competition with a purse big enough to matter, in the same season the bank wanted ninety days of money Kestrel did not have.

Kestrel set the paper down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The clock on the wall said 8:52. It was really 8:56. Outside, the rain had thickened, gone slushy at the edges, the first white flecks of the season coming down through the streetlamp's cone of light. Somewhere under the floorboards of him, the pacing thing had stopped and turned its face toward the door.

He looked at the photograph a long moment—at the smile, at the flour on the forearms, at the byline—and he did what he always did with ingredients that could ruin everything.

He measured it. He portioned it. He put it away.

Then he turned off the lights and stood in the dark of his beautiful, failing café, listening to the snow begin.
