## 2. The Review That Started It

The walk-in hummed like a hive at four in the morning, and Kestrel Okafor stood inside it counting butter the way other men counted sheep.

Forty-two pounds. He'd ordered forty-five. Somewhere between the delivery dock and these steel shelves, three pounds of cultured butter had gone missing, and Kestrel intended to find them or die of irritation trying. He ran his finger down the invoice clipped to a clipboard, then along the shelf edge, then down the invoice again, as if the numbers might rearrange themselves out of shame.

They didn't. Numbers never did. That was the whole point of them.

He backed out into the kitchen, letting the heavy door swing shut behind him, and stood in the blue pre-dawn quiet of The Sweet Garden. The café at this hour was his favorite version of it—stripped of customers, stripped of music, stripped of all the small performances a business required. Just the equipment and him. The proofing cabinet ticking through its cycle. The gas pilot lights burning their steady blue eyes in the dark. A place where everything obeyed.

Kestrel had built his life on that principle. Feelings were ingredients: measurable, portionable, controllable. Grief went in at two tablespoons. Longing at one teaspoon, leveled off. You didn't dump the whole jar in because the morning felt heavy; you measured, you folded, you controlled the crumb. Ten years of practice had made him very good at it. His croissants had ninety-six layers because he refused to let chaos laminate anything, including himself.

He tied on his apron and began the inventory he did every Tuesday, though today's had a second purpose. Today he needed to know exactly how far from drowning they were, and the answer was going to be arithmetic, and arithmetic didn't flinch.

Flour: eleven bags of high-gluten, six of pastry. Sugar: enough for nine days at current volume, seven if the weekend held. Chocolate—the good stuff, the seventy percent single-origin that regulars crossed town for—down to the last four kilos, which was not a stock level but a countdown.

And cash, when he let himself do that column last, which he always did last: enough to cover the note due on the fifteenth. Barely. The payment after that existed only in theory. The one after that lived in the same country as fairy tales.

Three missed payments. That was what Marisol had told him on the phone last week, her voice doing the gentle thing people's voices did when they thought bad news needed padding. *Three more missed payments and the bank starts talking about remedies, Kestrel.* Remedies. As if foreclosure were a cough syrup.

He wrote the figures in his ledger in his small, square hand, each number seated upright like a well-behaved child. When he finished, he closed the book and pressed his palm flat against its cover for a moment, the way you'd steady a bird.

Then he got to work, because work was the one recipe that always came out right.

* * *

It was while he was laminating dough—butter sheet folded into détrempe, the roller humming—that the memory arrived, uninvited as always, wearing the same coat it always wore.

Culinary school smelled like chlorine and ambition. That was the thing nobody told you about those places: beneath the copper pots and the talk of craft, everyone was bleeding, and the floor was slick with it. Kestrel had been twenty-two and certain that precision could save him. He'd grown up watching his mother stretch nothing into dinner, watching feeling wreck things—her marriages, her moods, the whole weather system of their house—and he had concluded early that the safest place to live was inside a scale's calibration. Weigh everything. Trust nothing that couldn't be verified.

Theo Marchetti had been the opposite thesis given arms and legs and an infuriating smile. Theo cooked like the food was a conversation he was winning. He tasted with his fingers. He adjusted mid-recipe, abandoned plating diagrams, once served an examiner a dessert he'd invented during the practical itself because, he said, the rhubarb looked lonely. The instructors adored him. Kestrel despised him with a thoroughness that should have been its own warning sign.

They competed for everything. Top of the practical rankings, swapping places week to week like dancers changing partners. The Lefèvre scholarship, which Theo took by four points. The final showcase slot, which Kestrel took back by six. Their rivalry became the school's background radiation—everyone orbited it, everyone had opinions, and somewhere in the middle of the second year, without either of them announcing it, the rivalry had stopped being only a rivalry.

Because Theo laughed at Kestrel's jokes before he finished them. Because Kestrel started arriving early to practicals just to watch Theo work, telling himself it was reconnaissance. Because one night in the teaching kitchen, both of them staying late over failed brioche, flour on Theo's jaw and frustration on Kestrel's tongue, Theo had said, quietly, "You know we're the only ones who understand each other," and Kestrel had felt the sentence land somewhere no thermometer could reach.

One reckless night. That was all it ever got to be.

December, third year. The holiday showcase had ended; the school emptied out; snow came down past the tall windows like the world was being erased and redrawn. They'd stayed behind—some pretext about cleaning, some pretext about tasting—and the pretexts had run out around midnight. There had been wine someone smuggled in. There had been Theo standing too close at the pass, saying something about Kestrel's hands, how precise they were, how he watched them sometimes. There had been the moment Kestrel turned and found Theo already looking at him, and the look had no irony left in it at all.

They'd kissed against the stainless counter, and it had been nothing like competition. It had been surrender, mutual and total, ten months of antagonism collapsing into something so warm Kestrel's ears rang with it. Theo's mouth had tasted like the wine and like salt and like the future. Kestrel had gripped the front of his jacket and thought, with the last coherent part of his brain: *oh no.*

Oh no, because wanting something that much was a liability. Wanting was his mother's disease—wanting loudly, wanting ruinously, wanting until the wanting broke everything it touched. Kestrel had spent his whole life building walls against that flood, and here he was at twenty-two, soaked to the bone, kissing his rival in a dark kitchen while snow buried the world.

They'd parted near dawn with a plan to talk later. There was always a later, in those hours. Later, they'd figure out what this was. Later, everything would be said.

Later arrived two days later in printed form.

The winter critique assignments had gone out—each student reviewing another's showcase piece, blind except for name and dish. Kestrel had drawn a first-year's competent, forgettable tart. Theo had drawn his.

Kestrel still knew the review by heart, which was its own kind of scar tissue. He could recite it the way other people recited prayers. *"Okafor's entremet is technically immaculate and emotionally vacant—a dessert executed by machine, assembled by someone who has confused control with mastery. Every layer is correct. Nothing is alive. One suspects the chef has never risked a single genuine feeling in his life, and the plate knows it."*

Published in the student journal. Read aloud, laughing, in at least three dorm rooms Kestrel knew of. *Emotionally vacant.* From the boy whose mouth had been on his seventy-two hours earlier. From the only person who had ever seen past the precision to whatever lived underneath—and who had looked at it, and called it nothing.

Kestrel hadn't confronted him. That was the part he was least proud of, in the long inventory of things he was least proud of. He'd simply withdrawn, the way he did from everything that burned. He'd stopped sitting near Theo in lectures. He'd answered questions with single words. And when graduation came, he'd taken a job three hundred miles away and told himself the distance was about opportunity.

He'd never asked why Theo wrote it. He'd never wanted the answer badly enough to risk hearing it. Some wounds you kept clean by never touching them, and Kestrel had kept this one sterile for a decade, bandaged in routine, in recipes, in the small square handwriting of ledgers that balanced because he made them balance.

The roller finished its pass. Ninety-six layers, waiting to become something beautiful and perfectly contained.

* * *

"Chef? You're doing the thing where you murder the dough with your eyes."

Marisol stood in the doorway, coat still on, scarf trailing, holding two coffees and the day's newspaper she'd grabbed from the shop next door. Twenty-four, sharp-eyed, the only employee Kestrel trusted with a key. She set one coffee on his station without asking and shook out the paper across the prep counter.

"I'm resting the dough," Kestrel said. "There's a difference."

"You're glaring at it like it owes you money." She paused. "Which, speaking of—"

"We'll make the fifteenth."

"We barely made the fifth." But she said it lightly, already moving on, because Marisol had learned that Kestrel's composure was load-bearing and you didn't lean on it hard in front of witnesses. She flipped the paper open to the arts section. "Did you see? He's back. Your nemesis. It's like Batman stuff, honestly, I keep waiting for you to have a signal in the sky."

Kestrel's hands went still on the bench scraper.

He'd known, of course. The town was small enough that news traveled faster than gossip deserved. Theo Marchetti's column—syndicated now, the one that had gotten him called *the most honest palate in the region*—was coming home. The regional Harvest Table competition, the biggest culinary event within two hundred miles, and Theo had accepted the judges' chair. His byline would be in every paper from here to the coast. His verdicts would be quoted at dinner tables for a year.

But knowing a storm was forecast and seeing the first cloud were different weathers entirely.

The photograph ran large. Theo at thirty-two looked like Theo at twenty-two who had finally been told he was beautiful and decided to believe it—dark hair pushed back, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with flour in what was absolutely a styled photo shoot and absolutely worked anyway. The headline read: **MARCHETTI RETURNS TO JUDGE HARVEST TABLE — 'A TOWN SHOULD TASTE LIKE ITS PEOPLE.'**

"A town should taste like its people," Marisol read aloud, savoring it. "God, he's good. Remember when he reviewed Delacroix's bistro and used the word 'mausoleum' twice? Brutal." She glanced up, caught Kestrel's expression, and moderated. "He probably won't even come by. Big judges don't do small cafés."

"He'll come by," Kestrel said. His voice came out level, which cost him something. "Judges scout. It's what they do."

He returned to the dough, folding with more force than the lamination required, and Marisol—wisely—retreated to the front of house with her coffee and her silence.

But the paper stayed open on the counter all morning, and Kestrel found his eyes returning to it between tasks. To the photograph. To the byline. To the pull-quote boxed beneath the article, chosen by some editor with a sense of cruelty or fate:

*"Great pastry isn't about perfection. It's about what the baker is willing to risk."*

Ten years ago, Kestrel would have read that as an attack. He'd spent years reading everything Theo wrote as attack—subscribing to the column in secret, hating that he subscribed, hating more that he read every word, cataloguing each review for evidence of the man Theo had revealed himself to be. Somewhere around year five, the evidence had grown confusing. Theo's writing had softened into something searching. He wrote about restaurants the way you wrote about people you missed. Once, reviewing a patisserie in Lyon, he'd written a line Kestrel had memorized against his will: *"The best desserts are confessions. The worst are alibis."*

Kestrel had wondered, more than once, in the small hours when inventory was done and the ledger was closed and the café breathed around him in the dark—who that line was for. Whether alibi was a word Theo had needed for himself.

He shut the thought down the way he shut down all thoughts without a use. Measured, portioned, shelved.

By noon the case was full: the pear frangipane tarts with their lattice tops laid in perfect rows, the éclairs glazed to a mirror shine, the morning's laminated dough transformed into croissants that shattered at ninety-six layers exactly. Everything correct. Everything repeatable. Everything safe.

At half past twelve, the bell above the door rang, and Marisol's voice carried back to the kitchen with a strange new texture in it, bright and careful at once.

"Welcome to The Sweet Garden! Table for— oh."

Kestrel wiped his hands on his towel and stepped through the pass into the dining room, ready for anything, prepared for nothing.

Theo Marchetti stood just inside the door, shaking snow off a wool coat that cost more than Kestrel's oven, looking around the café with an expression Kestrel couldn't file anywhere—until their eyes met across ten years and one room, and the expression resolved into something Kestrel recognized instantly, because he'd been practicing his own version of it since dawn.

Glacial politeness. Perfectly composed. And underneath it, faint as a hairline crack in sugar work, something that had never healed at all.

"Theo," Kestrel said. "Table for one?"

"Kestrel." Theo's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. Outside the window, the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the first flakes were starting to come down—fat, slow, indifferent to anyone's plans. "I hear the apple galette is worth the trip."
