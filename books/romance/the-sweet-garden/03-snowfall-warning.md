## 3. Snowfall Warning

The town hadn't changed, which was the problem.

Theo Marchetti drove the last twenty miles with his hands at ten and two and his jaw set like a man approaching a sentencing hearing rather than a judging table. The road unspooled through bare November fields, past the feed store with its faded mural of a rooster, past the water tower he'd once climbed on a dare, past the turnoff for Miller's Creek where he'd learned to drive his mother's ancient Honda in figure eights until she came out in her bathrobe to yell at him. All of it exactly as he'd left it. All of it smaller. That was the trick of towns like this one—they didn't grow while you were gone; they just waited, patient as dough proofing under cloth, so that when you came back you had no choice but to see how much *you* had.

He'd told himself the trip was professional. The Harvest Table committee had called him in September—*your column, your palate, your name on our poster*—and he'd said yes before the woman finished asking, too fast, the way you say yes to something you've already decided to do and are only now admitting. Regional judge for a competition that fed into the state finals. A weekend. A hotel with a broken hot tub. He could do it standing on his head.

What he'd actually thought, in the half-second before he said yes, was: *Kestrel Okafor is going to enter.*

He didn't know it for certain. But Kestrel had never left this town—not really—and The Sweet Garden still stood on Wren Street with its hand-painted sign and its window boxes that went riotous every June, and a man like Kestrel did not survive ten years of running a café in a town this size without entering every competition within driving distance. And if Kestrel entered, then Theo would be standing three feet from him when the plates came out, close enough to watch those long brown hands work, close enough to finally—

To finally what? Apologize? Ten years late, by way of a scorecard?

Theo took the Wren Street exit mostly on autopilot and hated himself a little for it. He told himself he was just driving past. He told himself a lot of things.

* * *

The café looked better than he'd expected, which somehow made it worse.

He remembered it from before—the summer they were twenty-two, when it had been nothing but a rumor and a business plan Kestrel used to sketch on napkins between lectures, all clean lines and impossible standards. Now it existed. Warm light behind fogged glass, the smell of butter and cardamom reaching him halfway down the block, a chalkboard out front announcing today's specials in handwriting so precise it looked typeset. Someone had planted rosemary along the walkway. Of course someone had. Kestrel Okafor did not plant things casually.

Theo stood outside for a full minute, breath clouding, working up to something he refused to call courage. Then he went in.

A bell over the door announced him. The interior was small and immaculate—six tables, mismatched chairs that had clearly been chosen rather than found, a counter of reclaimed walnut worn pale where customers leaned. And behind it, backlit by the pastry case like an accusation, stood Kestrel.

Ten years should have done more damage. That was Theo's first stupid thought. The man had gone silver at the temples early—a streak above each ear, dramatic as stage lighting—and there were lines around his eyes that hadn't been there at twenty-two, but everything else was intact. The height. The stillness. The way he held his body like a held note. He was piping rosettes onto something chocolate, and he didn't look up right away, and Theo understood that the delay was not accidental. Kestrel had seen him come in. Kestrel was making him wait.

"Welcome to The Sweet Garden." Kestrel's voice was pleasant. Professionally pleasant. The voice of a man reading terms and conditions aloud. He set down the piping bag with surgical care and lifted his gaze, and his expression was a locked door painted to look like weather.

"Theo," Theo said. "Hi."

"Mr. Marchetti." A pause, calibrated. "We're honored."

The word *honored* landed like a slap delivered with white gloves. Theo felt heat climb his neck. Behind Kestrel, a young woman at the espresso machine glanced between them with the frank curiosity of someone who sensed a story and intended to extract it later.

"I'm in town for the competition," Theo said. "Judging. I thought—I saw the lights."

"You saw the lights." Kestrel nodded slowly, as though confirming a hypothesis. "Well. We have coffee, tea, and a case of things I made before six this morning. What can I get you?"

It wasn't hostility. That was the terrible part. Hostility would have been a relief—it would have meant Kestrel still cared enough to burn. This was worse. This was the tone of a man who had taken an old wound, deboned it, rolled it flat, and folded it into something presentable. Theo ordered a coffee and a cardamom bun because refusing food in a pâtissier's shop was a second insult, and watched Kestrel plate both with movements so controlled they seemed rehearsed.

The bun was, infuriatingly, perfect. The lamination shattered in fine, distinct layers; the cardamom was toasted just to the edge of bitterness; the sugar crust crackled. It was the best thing Theo had eaten in three months, and he ate professionally for a living, and he sat there at the little table by the window chewing excellence he had no right to taste, while Kestrel wiped down the already-clean counter with his back turned like a man guarding a border.

"The Harvest Table," Kestrel said at last, without turning around. "You're head judge."

"I am."

"They must pay you well, to travel to places like this." Still that glacial courtesy. "Or maybe it's charity. Judges love an underdog story."

"Kestrel—"

"Cream's by the window if you need it." He turned then, and for one unguarded half-second something moved behind his eyes—something old and banked and furious-sad—before the door swung shut again. "Anything else, Mr. Marchetti?"

There were about four hundred things else. *I'm sorry* was the smallest of them and the largest. Theo looked at the man he had loved clumsily at twenty-two and destroyed efficiently at twenty-three, and said, "No. Thank you. The bun is—" *extraordinary, the best thing I've eaten since the last thing you made me, which was also extraordinary, which is why I wrote what I wrote, because you scared the hell out of me* —"—very good."

Kestrel's mouth did something that was almost a smile and absolutely wasn't. "Glad to hear it. Come again."

Theo left cash on the table, more than the bill required, and Kestrel made a show of bringing him exact change, counting it into his palm coin by coin, which was either pettiness or poetry or both. Outside, the cold hit him like a reset button. He stood on the sidewalk breathing hard, looking back through the glass at the man wiping down tables in the warm light, and felt the guilt settle into his chest where it had lived for a decade, heavier now, newly furnished.

Behind him, the wind shifted. It smelled like snow.

* * *

The forecast turned ugly around four o'clock.

Theo was in his hotel room reviewing judging rubrics he'd helped write and didn't need to review when his phone lit up with the alert: *WINTER STORM WARNING. Heavy snow, 8–14 inches expected overnight through Saturday morning. Travel strongly discouraged after 6 PM.*

He pulled up the map. The storm was a great gray fist rolling down from the lakes, aimed directly at the valley, and the competition venue—the community hall, fifteen minutes across town—was scheduled for setup at seven tomorrow morning with judging at noon. If the roads closed tonight, everything would slide. If the roads stayed open, he'd be driving them at dawn in whatever remained.

He should stay in. Order room service. Sleep.

Instead he found himself pulling his coat back on, because the hotel coffee was swill and because—this was the part he declined to examine—he wanted to see the café one more time before tomorrow put them in the same room for hours. Wanted to study the terrain. Wanted, God help him, another cardamom bun, and wanted even more to find out whether Kestrel's armor had any seams at all.

The sky over Wren Street had gone the color of wet slate. The first flakes came down fat and unhurried, theatrical, like the storm was still deciding whether to commit. Inside The Sweet Garden, the lights were low and the OPEN sign was dark, but the door wasn't locked—prep for tomorrow, probably, trays of components staged under film—and Theo let himself in to the sound of a radio murmuring storm updates and the sight of Kestrel alone at the big steel table, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with flour, trimming strips of something golden.

Kestrel looked up. This time there was no pause, no performance of indifference. Just a flicker of genuine surprise, quickly buried.

"We're closed."

"I know. I need a favor." Theo held up his phone. "Storm warning. Roads close by six, maybe sooner. Tomorrow's setup—if we don't move the equipment and the entries tonight, the whole schedule collapses."

Something shifted in Kestrel's stance. Not softening—recalibration. The competitor in him surfacing through the frost. "They'll postpone."

"They'll postpone to Sunday, and Sunday's forecast is worse, and the state committee won't extend the qualification deadline. You know how this works. One bad weekend and half these bakers lose their shot at regionals." He paused. "You've got people entering. Your assistant—the girl on the espresso machine. She's in the young bakers' division, isn't she?"

Kestrel's jaw tightened. Priya. Of course it was Priya; the kid had been talking about nothing else for weeks. "She has a custard component that needs twelve hours to set. If the hall doesn't get power and refrigeration tonight—"

"Then we move it tonight. Both of us. My rental has a cargo area, and I'm apparently the only judge in town who reads weather reports." Theo shrugged, aiming for casual and hearing, in his own ears, how much it wasn't. "Consider it neutral territory. Logistics. No feelings required."

The last two words escaped before he could stop them, and he watched them land—watched Kestrel go very still, the way water goes still before it freezes. For a moment Theo thought he'd be thrown out. Then Kestrel untied his apron with a single efficient motion and hung it on its hook, precise as punctuation.

"Fine," he said. "But we take my van. Your rental will die on the hill by the church, and I'm not digging it out."

* * *

They worked through the early dark while the storm arrived in earnest.

It was strange, the intimacy of manual labor between people who weren't speaking. They loaded trays of Priya's setting custards into coolers, hauled folding tables, moved crates of entry forms and ribbon rolls and the enormous aluminum urn the committee used for cider. Snow thickened around the streetlights until the world beyond the windshield was a soft gray roar. Kestrel drove with both hands on the wheel and his eyes forward, and Theo sat in the passenger seat holding a cooler between his knees and cataloguing, against his will, everything the decade had changed and everything it hadn't. Kestrel still drove too fast on straightaways and braked too early on curves. Kestrel still hummed—one bar, twice, then caught himself and stopped—when he was calculating something. Kestrel still smelled like browned butter and cardamom, and Theo still noticed, and noticing still felt like a small act of treason against his own peace.

At the hall, they worked by sodium light in the loading bay, breath steaming, snow swirling in whenever the doors opened. Twice their hands met over the same crate. Twice, contact lasted less than a second, and both times Theo felt it like a struck match.

On the third load, Kestrel spoke.

"Why are you really here, Theo?"

Not *Mr. Marchetti*. His given name, dropped like a stone down a well, and Theo heard the splash of it all the way down. He set the crate on the trolley and made himself meet those dark, level eyes.

"To judge," he said. "And—" Honesty rose in him like yeast, unstoppable, warm. "And because I heard you might enter. And I owed you an apology I've been carrying for ten years, and I thought maybe if I stood in a room with you long enough, I'd find a way to say it that you wouldn't throw back at me."

The wind screamed through the gap in the doors. Somewhere inside the hall, a banner flapped loose and settled. Kestrel looked at him for a long moment, snow gathering on his shoulders, melting in his silver-streaked hair, and his face did something complicated—cracked, just slightly, along a seam Theo had put there himself.

"You're right," Kestrel said quietly. "I would throw it back."

He picked up the last crate and carried it inside, and Theo stood alone in the falling snow, guilty and cold and more awake than he'd been in years, understanding that the storm outside was nothing—nothing—compared to the one he'd just walked into.

His phone buzzed. Road closure notice, effective immediately.

They were trapped in the same town, and by morning, possibly the same building, and the night was very long, and very white, and just beginning.
