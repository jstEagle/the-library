## 13. Invisible Man

The morning after the semi-final, I woke in my own bed with the taste of wine and cowardice on my tongue.

That's the thing about adrenaline. It's a liar. It floods you with certainty—*this, now, him*—and then it drains away and leaves you standing in the wreckage of your own judgment, wondering what possessed you. That was the story I told myself at six a.m., staring at the ceiling while the radiator ticked: *adrenaline*. Semi-final nerves. Too much Barolo. A decade of tension finding the only exit available.

Not love. God, no. Not after everything.

I said it out loud to the empty room, the way you rehearse a line before service. "It was adrenaline." The words came out flat and gray, like flour sifted through wet cheesecloth. Even my own kitchen voice didn't believe me.

But belief is a choice, and I had built a whole life on choosing the version of events that kept me intact. So I chose it again. I tied my apron, I lit the ovens, and when Theo came through the door at seven—early, snow still in his hair, carrying two coffees and something hopeful in the set of his shoulders—I was already wearing my armor.

"Kestrel." He set the cups down on the steel counter like an offering. "About last night—"

"We should talk about boundaries." I didn't look up from the laminating dough. The butter block crackled under the pin, cold and obedient, exactly the way things were supposed to be. "What happened was a lapse. Adrenaline, wine, the win. It won't happen again."

The silence that followed had texture. I could feel it against my skin like freezer burn.

"Adrenaline," Theo repeated.

"It's common in competition settings." Now I looked up, because if I was going to gut him I owed him eye contact. His face did something complicated—the hope going out of it not all at once but in stages, like a tide retreating over stones. "The partnership stands. We're good together professionally. But the rest was a mistake, and I'd appreciate it if we could treat it as one."

"A mistake." He said it slowly, tasting it, the way he tasted a sauce he suspected someone had sweetened to hide a flaw. "Ten years, Kestrel. Ten years of you pretending you don't remember a kiss that you clearly remember down to the minute, and last night you kissed me back like you were starving, and this morning it's a *lapse*."

"Theo."

"No." He held up a hand. Not angry. Worse than angry—measured. He was doing my trick, my measured-to-the-gram trick, and watching him do it made me feel like I was looking into a mirror at three a.m. "You want professionalism? Fine. You'll get professionalism. The best you've ever seen. Let's bake."

And he did. God help me, he did.

* * *

For eleven days, Theo Marchetti was flawless.

He arrived at five each morning with his station immaculate, his mise en place aligned like soldiers, his ideas presented in clean bullet points that gave me nothing to push against and no way in. He stopped bringing coffee. He stopped humming when he piped. He stopped looking at my mouth when I talked about caramelization, which—I hadn't known until it stopped—that he had been doing for weeks, and that some animal part of me had been leaning toward like a plant toward a window.

We built the final dessert in near silence. He'd suggest, I'd adjust, we'd taste, we'd refine. Our hands still knew each other's rhythms from the storm, from the test kitchens, from the night we'd kissed among the cooling racks with flour on our wrists. Professionalism couldn't erase that. It just made it obscene—two people who knew exactly how the other moved, working three feet apart behind a wall neither would name.

"You've changed the apple gel," he said on day nine, tasting the base I'd rebalanced at four a.m. "It's brighter. Less of you in it."

"It's more precise."

"It's less *you*." He set the spoon down with a click. "The version you made Tuesday had grief in it. This one has fear."

"Theo—"

"I'm just noting it for the record." He picked the spoon back up. "Chef."

*Chef.* He'd never called me that before. It landed like a slammed door.

I told myself it was fine. Better than fine—it was what I'd asked for. I had spent ten years constructing a self that couldn't be hurt: the man who weighed his feelings, who portioned his affection, who kept every relationship at serving temperature and nothing warmer. And here was proof the construction held. Theo pulled back; I didn't bleed. I felt nothing except—

Except I woke at 3 a.m. thinking about the sound he made when he laughed, which I hadn't heard in eleven days. Except I caught myself reaching for the second coffee cup that wasn't there. Except the café smelled wrong without him in it, like a recipe missing salt—not enough to identify, enough to ruin everything.

* * *

The bank called on a Thursday.

I knew before I answered. There's a cadence to a collections call, a certain apologetic briskness, and Marlene from First Regional had it down to an art. "Mr. Okafor. I'm afraid I have some difficult news regarding the November payment."

The November payment. Payment number three. The number I'd been circling for months like a bird over open water.

"How difficult?"

"There's been a change in how the loan is being serviced. The new holder isn't inclined toward extensions. If the full arrears aren't resolved by the fifteenth—" a pause, soft as a knife going in, "—they'll begin foreclosure proceedings. I'm so sorry, Mr. Okafor. I know how hard you've worked."

I thanked her. I thanked her, because my mother raised me polite even as the floor opened under me. Then I sat down on the flour sacks in the storeroom and did arithmetic until my eyes blurred.

The arrears were fourteen thousand. The competition prize was eight. Even winning wouldn't cover it. The Paris contract—still folded in my inside pocket, still hidden from Theo, its terms glowing in my memory like a pilot light—was ninety thousand a year, housing included, starting January.

Salvation, typed on letterhead. All I had to do was leave.

Leave the town I'd fled once already. Leave the orchard with its stewardship clause ticking quietly in the solicitor's file, the grandmother whose ledger I still hadn't finished reading because finishing it meant feeling it. Leave Theo, who was currently in the front of house inventing a winter menu I would never serve, laughing with Mrs. Adeyemi about cardamom, wearing my café's apron like he belonged in it.

Like he belonged here. Like I was the one who'd never learned how.

* * *

That evening I walked the orchard alone. I hadn't meant to go; my feet decided. Frost had come early and hard, and the old trees stood in the blue dusk with their arms bare, lichen climbing their trunks like slow green fire. My grandmother's pruning marks were still visible on some of them—her cuts healed into knuckles, decades old.

I found her grafting notebook in the shed where I'd left it, and read by the light of my phone until my thumb went numb.

*Grafting rule #1: the scion and the rootstock must be compatible or the join will fail no matter how skilled the hand.*

I thought about Theo's face that first morning, the tide going out of it.

*Rule #2: a failed graft can be cut away and tried again. The tree survives. The tree almost always survives.*

I closed the book. The tree survives, I thought. But what grows at the join—if you keep cutting it away, season after season, out of fear—is scar tissue. Callus. Wood that forgot it was ever meant to carry fruit.

My phone buzzed. Paris. Madame Lefèvre's assistant, confirming receipt of my provisional acceptance, reminding me of the December deadline. *We do require your final signature by the fifteenth, Monsieur Okafor. The position remains yours contingent on the competition result.*

The fifteenth. The same day the bank's hammer fell.

One date. One decision dressed as two. Win the final, sign for Paris, let the prize money buy me thirty days of grace while I packed my life into crates—and the café would still die, slowly, without me, unless I sold the orchard to cover the debt. Sell her trees. Sell the last living thing she'd left me, the thing she'd guarded for a year of my absence specifically so I would have to choose it.

Or stay. Stay and lose the café anyway, probably. Stay and watch Theo become a colleague-shaped ghost in my kitchen. Stay and be poor and rooted and hollow.

There was a third option. It sat in my chest like a stone I refused to look at directly: stay, and tell the truth, and ask him—ask *Theo Marchetti*, who had laid himself open on my floor three nights ago and watched me call it adrenaline—to try again. To graft. To risk the join.

I put the notebook in my coat and walked back through the frozen rows, and I did not look at the stone, and I told myself the cold was why my eyes were burning.

* * *

Back at the café, the lights were still on. Theo was at the corner table with his laptop, the final presentation deck open, and he looked up when the bell rang, and for half a second—half a second, no longer—his face did the unguarded thing. The thing from the storm, from the kiss. Relief. *You came back.*

Then the professionalism slid down over it like a cloche, and he was all business. "Timing breakdown for the final. I emailed it. Also, the sugar work needs a second pair of hands Thursday if you're free."

"I saw the email." I hung my coat. My hands wanted to cross the room. I made them untie my apron instead. "Thursday works."

"Good." He turned back to the screen. A pause. Then, without looking up, quieter: "The gel went back to Tuesday's ratio. I fixed it."

Something in my chest cracked along an old fault line. "Theo."

"Don't." Still not looking at me. His jaw tight. "It's better with grief in it. That's all I'll say. Everything else is yours to figure out."

He gathered his laptop and coat and left, and the bell above the door rang its small bright note, and I stood in my empty café—the café that was dying, the café I might save by leaving, the café that smelled like him even with the door shut against the cold—and understood, finally, with total clarity, what I was doing.

I wasn't protecting myself from Theo.

I was disappearing. Gram by gram, cut by cut, I was pruning myself down to nothing that could be grafted, nothing that could fail, nothing that could be left—because being left had broken me once at twenty-two, and I had sworn, in the wreckage of culinary school and that review and that morning-after silence, that no one would ever have the blade again.

So I'd become invisible. Ungraftable. Safe.

The fifteenth stood in front of me like a frost date: the last warm night before everything tender dies.

I picked up the Paris contract from my bag, unfolded it on the counter, and read my own name in the signature line—*Kestrel Okafor*, printed there in someone else's font, waiting for my hand.

Outside, the first snow of December began to fall.
