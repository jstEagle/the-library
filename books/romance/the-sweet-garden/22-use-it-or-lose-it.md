## 22. Use It or Lose It

### Kestrel

The morning of his wedding, Kestrel Okafor woke at four a.m. out of habit, lay in the dark of the orchard house listening to the birds start their arguments, and realized he had nowhere to be but exactly here.

He got up anyway. Some rituals were load-bearing.

The kitchen of his grandmother's house still smelled like her—woodsmoke and dried apples and the faint ghost of cinnamon that ten months of scrubbing had never quite evicted. He liked that. He'd stopped fighting it somewhere around February, when he'd found her grafting knife in the back of a drawer and started using it for everything, because a good blade deserved work, not mourning.

He scaled out flour the way he always had: to the gram. But these days the scale was a suggestion, not a law. His hands knew the dough by feel now, the way his grandmother's notes said hers had—*when it stops sticking to your fear, it's ready*, she'd written, in the looping hand that filled the ledger now living on his kitchen table. He'd thought that was a joke when he first read it. It was not a joke. Nothing in that ledger was.

By six, the proofing baskets were lined up on the counter like a small sleeping congregation, and Theo's side of the bed was still warm, and out the window the orchard was doing what it had spent a year learning to do again: growing.

The trees had come back slower than Kestrel had hoped and faster than anyone else believed possible. His grandmother's rootstock had been tougher than it looked—neglected, not dead, which was a distinction Kestrel had come to take personally. He and Theo had pruned through the winter with numb fingers and Theo's terrible playlists, and by spring the rows had budded like the orchard was making up for lost time, and now, in the gold of late September, the branches hung heavy with Blenheim Orange and Egremont Russet and the one mystery apple nobody could identify that Theo insisted on calling *la nonna*, because it was stubborn and sweet and slightly crooked.

Today they would get married under it.

Kestrel stood at the window with his coffee and let that fact land, the way he'd learned to let things land instead of measuring them into submission. A year ago he would have made a spreadsheet of his own wedding. He'd made a spreadsheet anyway—Theo had found it and laughed for a full five minutes—but the spreadsheet had one column now, and the column was labeled *things we want*, and the cells were full of things like *enough chairs* and *the good cider* and *let Priya sing, even if she goes off-key, especially if she goes off-key*.

His phone buzzed on the counter. Priya.

*How's the groom? Also I need to know: is the dessert table actually happening or are you two going to be insufferable and say "the cake IS the dessert"?*

Kestrel smiled at the screen. The dessert table was happening. It had been happening since Tuesday, when he'd started baking in earnest—apple galettes with the mystery apple, honey made by the bees Theo had insisted on installing against all of Kestrel's objections (the objections had lasted eleven days; the honey was extraordinary), the brown butter madeleines Theo had taught him to make with his hands over Kestrel's hands, patient and warm and completely unbothered by Kestrel's insistence that he already knew how.

And in the center of the table, under glass, open to a page near the end: his grandmother's ledger.

He'd argued with himself about displaying it. It felt like exposure—her private grief, her tally of the visits he'd never made, laid out where the whole town could see. But Theo had said something that had settled it. *She kept the record so someone would finally read it. Let them read it.* And beside the ledger, Kestrel had placed a small card in his own handwriting, because some things needed saying in his voice, not just his grandmother's:

*She tracked every visit I didn't make. Don't do what I did. The people you love are the only recipe that can't be recreated from memory.*

He turned the card over once more, checking the ink was dry, and heard the floorboard in the hall—the one that announced Theo like a herald.

"You're up," Theo said from the doorway, hair a disaster, wearing the old sweatshirt Kestrel had tried to throw away twice. "It's four hours until you're contractually obligated to look at me like that in front of witnesses."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm the last proof of the day and you're checking whether I've risen."

Kestrel set down his coffee and crossed the kitchen and kissed him, because it was four hours early and he could. Ten years of not doing this had taught him the value of doing it whenever the opportunity presented itself.

"Mm," Theo said against his mouth. "The dough's ready."

* * *

### Theo

By ten, the orchard had transformed.

Chairs in mismatched rows between the trees. Tables dressed in linen the color of apple blossom. Priya directing the setup with the serene tyranny of a woman who had waited a decade for this and intended to enjoy it. The string lights Theo had strung himself—twice, because the first attempt had looked, in Kestrel's word, "like a crime scene"—looped through the branches overhead, waiting for dark.

Theo stood at the end of the third row and did the thing he still did sometimes, the thing he'd never fully trained out of himself: he checked the exits. Not for danger. For escape routes he no longer needed. Old habits of a man who had spent his twenties and most of his thirties keeping one foot out of every room he loved.

Both feet in the grass now. Both feet in this town he'd once written off in six hundred scathing words, this town that had forgiven him faster than Kestrel had, this town that now stopped him at the market to ask when the wedding was and whether there'd be madeleines.

There would be madeleines. There would be four hundred madeleines.

He watched Kestrel across the rows, arguing cheerfully with the florist about the height of an arrangement, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with flour he'd never fully washed off, and Theo felt the thing he felt now daily, the thing that had replaced the old low hum of want and guilt: a kind of astonished gratitude. As if he'd been handed a dessert he'd ordered years ago and assumed lost in the kitchen forever.

"Uncle Theo." His niece, Sofia, age nine, appeared at his elbow in her flower-girl dress with the gravity of a general. "Kestrel said I could help with the dessert table. He said I have good hands."

"Kestrel is correct. You do."

"He also said you'd say that because you're biased."

"Kestrel," Theo said solemnly, "is correct about everything now. It's very annoying."

Sofia grinned and ran off toward the table where the ledger sat under glass, and Theo watched her stop in front of it, frown at the handwriting, and ask Kestrel's mother what it was. He watched Kestrel's mother crouch down and explain, watched Sofia's face do the complicated work of a child encountering adult grief in its gentlest form, and then watched her say something that made Kestrel's mother laugh and wipe her eyes.

The string lights swayed. Somewhere in the rows, the bees went about their business, indifferent to ceremony, and Theo thought: *this is what I was afraid of.* Not the loving. The staying. The being known in one place, permanently, by everyone, with no column in the world left to hide.

It turned out being known was the whole point.

* * *

### Kestrel

The ceremony was short, because they'd agreed on short, and because Priya had threatened to sing a second song if anyone droned.

They stood under the crooked tree—*la nonna*, heavy with fruit, dropping the occasional apple into the grass like applause—and Kestrel held Theo's hands and found that his own were not shaking. He had expected them to shake. He had spent a lifetime expecting his own hands to betray him at the moments that mattered, and here was the moment above all moments, and his hands were steady as a scale at rest.

He'd prepared vows. Of course he had. He'd written four drafts, measured them, cut them, tightened them like a recipe.

He abandoned all four drafts in the space of a breath.

"I used to believe," he said, "that feelings were ingredients. That if I measured them carefully enough, controlled the temperature, kept everything at a safe and consistent level, nothing could ever ruin. I built a whole life on that theory. I built walls out of it. I built a review-proof, love-proof, grief-proof kitchen and I lived in it alone and I called it discipline."

Theo's thumbs moved over his knuckles. Waiting. He was so good at waiting.

"Then a man I'd loved at twenty-two got snowed in with me and told me the truth over proofing dough, and it turns out feelings aren't ingredients. They're the fire. You don't contain them. You learn to cook with them or you starve." He heard his own voice crack and let it. "So. Theo Marchetti. I promise you the unmeasured life. I promise to be brave in the small ways, daily, which is the only way bravery actually works. I promise to read the ledger and not become it. And I promise that every year, under this tree, we graft something new."

Theo laughed wetly and said his vows, which were shorter and worse and perfect, and included the line *"I promise to keep reviewing you for the rest of my life, and I promise the scores will keep going up,"* and then Priya was singing, off-key in exactly the promised way, and the whole town was on its feet in the apple rows, and Kestrel Okafor—who had once believed the safest life was the one where no one could see you—stood in full view of everyone he had ever known and let himself be seen.

* * *

The party went long into the gold hour and past it.

Kestrel found himself at the dessert table at dusk, not eating—though he had eaten, generously, including a slice of the honey cake Theo had made in secret, which was so good Kestrel had accused him of hiding talent, and Theo had said, "I had a good teacher," which had made Kestrel's mother cry again—just standing near the ledger, watching people drift past it. Some read the card. Some read the pages. Old Mr. Adeyemi, who had run the hardware store for forty years, stood before it for a long time and then found Kestrel and pressed his hand and said only, "She was a good woman. Stubborn." And Kestrel had said, "It's where I get it," and they'd both laughed, and it hadn't hurt the way he'd feared. It had hurt the way a scar hurts in cold weather: proof of healing, not of wound.

That was when the apprentice found him.

Her name was June Okafor—no relation, though she'd checked, hopefully, and Kestrel had said "as far as I know" and she'd been delighted. Nineteen, sharp, ferociously talented, hired three months ago out of the regional culinary program on the strength of a galette that had made Theo put down his fork and say nothing at all, which was the highest review in his repertoire. She'd been at the café since spring, learning the Sweet Garden way: the scale, the discipline, the craft. And lately, learning the new way too, the way Kestrel was still learning himself.

She appeared at his elbow holding a madeleine and a question she'd clearly been carrying all evening.

"Can I ask you something kind of personal?"

"You can ask. I reserve the right to answer with a recipe."

She smiled, then steadied herself. "How did you do it? The competition—the final, when you told everyone the truth about—" she gestured vaguely at Theo, at the orchard, at everything, "—all of it. And then turning down Paris. Everyone said you were insane. My instructors said you threw away your career." She looked down at the madeleine. "I have this thing I might want to audition for, and I keep making spreadsheets about it instead of applying. And I keep thinking—Kestrel did it. Kestrel stood on a stage and said the scary true thing. How did you learn to take risks like that?"

Kestrel looked at her. Nineteen, with his old armor already half-built, the spreadsheets already multiplying. He recognized the look because he'd worn it for thirty years, and because his grandmother had worn a version of it too, alone in this house, tracking visits that never came.

He could give her the tidy answer. The recipe answer. *Start small. Measure the risk. Build a framework.* It would be true, partially. It would also be the wall, handed to the next person.

"Someone taught me the heart is trainable," he said. "They were wrong—it's just brave."

June blinked. "That's—okay, that's not actually an answer."

"No," Kestrel said, and laughed. "It isn't. Here's the real one." He turned so they were both facing the orchard, the lights, Theo across the grass with Sofia on his shoulders, reaching for the highest apples. "The risk never gets smaller. You don't get to a point where it's easy. What happens is you finally find something you want more than you want to be safe. And then the math changes. Not the fear—the math." He tapped the ledger under its glass. "My grandmother spent forty years doing the safe math. It cost her everything she actually wanted. I nearly did the same thing with a contract and a plane ticket. The bravest thing I ever did wasn't the stage or the speech. It was a Tuesday. It was calling a man on an ordinary Tuesday and saying, *I was wrong, and I'm staying, and I'm terrified.*" He looked at her. "Apply for the audition. Do it scared. Do it tonight, before the spreadsheet multiplies."

June was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The heart is trainable. They were wrong—it's just brave," under her breath, like she was committing it to memory, and Kestrel realized she was going to write it down somewhere, and that somewhere, years from now, some other terrified nineteen-year-old would hear it from her.

That was how it worked, he understood then. Not recipes. Grafting. You took the living branch of what you'd learned and you cut it into someone else's tree, and you hoped it took.

"Go on," he said. "The madeleines are going fast, and I made four hundred, and I'm counting."

She went. Kestrel stayed a moment longer, alone with the ledger, and read the line he'd read a hundred times now, the last line, in his grandmother's hand, the ink gone brown with age:

*The heart is a muscle. Use it or lose it.*

"Using it," he told her quietly. "Every day. Even the days it hurts. Especially those."

The lights came on overhead, all at once, and someone turned the music up, and across the orchard Theo looked over the crowd and found him the way he always did—immediately, unerringly, like a man who had stopped checking exits because he'd finally found the room he meant to stay in—and raised an eyebrow: *coming?*

Kestrel closed the ledger gently, left it open for the next reader, and went to him.
