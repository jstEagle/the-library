## 2. The Anonymous Assignment

The flyer appeared on my counter the way most things in Sweet Harbor did: carried in by Dot Ferreira, who considered herself less a library board member than a one-woman postal service with opinions.

"Anonymous Pen-Pal Festival," she announced, slapping the paper down next to the register with the confidence of a woman delivering a subpoena. "Every participant gets matched with a secret correspondent. You write letters. Real ones, with ink and envelopes, like civilization intended. At the harbor festival finale, identities are revealed."

I looked at the flyer. It had a clip-art quill on it. A quill. In the year of our Lord whenever-this-was.

"Dot, I run a bakery. I don't have time to write letters to strangers."

"You have time to talk to that oven like it's a disappointing nephew."

"That's different. The oven and I have history."

She planted her elbows on my counter — a liberty only Dot was allowed, and only because she'd been coming here since before I owned the place, back when it was my father's, back when the lemon tarts were his and I was the small girl on a milk crate dusting powdered sugar onto everything within reach, including customers' sleeves. "Casper Almeida. Listen to me. The festival committee is printing two thousand programs this year. Two thousand. Every business in town is fighting for space in those pages. You know what the pen-pal kickoff gets? A full-page feature. Names of participants. Photographs of the letter-writing table at the library."

"And?"

"And you're three missed payments from being a memory, sweetheart. Free advertising is free advertising."

The bell over the door jingled and Mrs. Okonkwo came in for her Wednesday almond croissant, and I used the interruption the way drowning sailors use driftwood. But Dot just waited, arms crossed, radiating the patience of a woman who had once talked the harbormaster out of raising dock fees through sheer refusal to leave his office.

When Mrs. Okonkwo left, I said, "It's anonymous. How does anonymous get me advertising?"

"Because at the reveal, everyone finds out who wrote what. And if your letters are half as sharp as your mouth, people will be *dying* to know it was you." She tapped the flyer. "Also, the board needs participants or the whole thing collapses, and I would owe you a favor, and you know my favors are worth having."

That was true. Dot Ferreira's favors had gotten me a health inspection rescheduled, a parking ticket dissolved, and once, mysteriously, a pallet of free butter. I didn't ask questions about the butter.

"I'll think about it," I said, which in Almeida family language meant *no*, but in Dot Ferreira language apparently meant *yes*, because she kissed my cheek, took a lemon tart without paying — "advertising," she said — and swept out into the gray morning like a ship leaving harbor.

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about running a failing business: the failure part is almost restful compared to the pretending-it-isn't part.

By four o'clock that afternoon I'd done the books twice hoping the numbers would feel guilty and change. They didn't. Rent: due Friday. Account balance: a number I will not repeat here because this is a story with some dignity. The foreclosure notice from March — the first one — was still pinned under the register where I could see it, which my therapist — whom I stopped seeing in January for financial reasons that felt darkly funny — would probably have called something diagnostic.

I made a lemon tart instead. This is my other coping mechanism, and unlike therapy, it produces dessert.

There's a specific hour in a bakery, late afternoon, when the rush dies and the light goes amber through the front windows and the whole place smells like caramelized sugar and warm butter, and for about twenty minutes I understand why I do this. Why I kept my father's café open after he died instead of selling the lease and going to culinary school in Providence like a person with sense. The café isn't a business. It's a séance. Every tart I pull from the oven is a small resurrection.

I set the fresh tart in the case, poured myself the dregs of the coffee pot, and looked at Dot's flyer again.

Anonymous Pen-Pal Festival. *Reconnect with the lost art of correspondence.* *Open your heart to a stranger.*

"Open my heart to a stranger," I said to the oven. The oven, as usual, declined to comment.

But I'll admit something, since we've come this far together: there was a small, embarrassing part of me that wanted it. Not the advertising — okay, mostly the advertising — but underneath that, in the silted-over riverbed of me, there was this: I hadn't told anyone the truth about anything in twelve years. Not the whole truth, not the shape of it, not the way grief sits in a body like a stone swallowed young, so that everything you build afterward has to be built around it. My friends got the edited version. My staff got the competent version. The town got the version they needed — plucky Casper Almeida, keeping her father's dream alive, chin up, flour on her apron.

A stranger wouldn't need any of those versions. A stranger couldn't look at me across a counter and see the girl from the dock.

So that night, after close, sitting at the little table by the window with a glass of the cheap wine I pretend is for cooking, I filled out the entry form. Name: required, but sealed until the reveal, the form explained. Interests: I wrote *baking, long walks on the pier I avoid, sarcasm*. What are you hoping to find in a correspondent? I stared at that question for a long time.

Finally I wrote: *Someone who doesn't know my face.*

Then I felt ridiculous, sealed it in the envelope anyway, and dropped it in the library's brass box on my way home, walking fast, like the envelope might change its mind about me.

* * *

The match came ten days later, delivered by actual mail, on cream-colored stationery that the library board had clearly spent real money on, which told me Dot had fought someone in a meeting and won.

*Dear Participant,* it began. *You have been paired! Your correspondent has chosen the pen name "Anchor." Please address your letters to the Pen-Pal Post at the Sweet Harbor Public Library, where our volunteers will forward them unopened. Remember: no identifying details until the Festival Finale!*

My pen name, assigned by whatever algorithm Dot and her cohorts called an algorithm, was "Lighthouse."

I laughed out loud alone in my kitchen, which is either a good sign or a very bad one.

*Lighthouse,* though. Of course. Of course the universe — or Dot, who absolutely had a hand in the pairings, I'd have bet my last bag of flour on it — would hand me a name that meant *the thing that stands on the rocks warning everyone away.*

* * *

I didn't write the first letter for another week. Not because I forgot. Because every time I sat down to do it, I discovered I had nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound, and I couldn't figure out how to offer either one to a stranger.

In the end, what broke the dam was Tuesday. Tuesday was the day the second rent notice came, red-bordered this time, and Mr. Calloway the landlord stood in my doorway doing the sympathetic head-tilt of a man about to raise your rent while expressing sympathy about it.

"Times are hard all over, Casper," he said, which is what people say when times are specifically hard at your place and fine at theirs.

"They certainly are, Mr. Calloway."

"The festival should bring foot traffic, though. Big year planned. You'll want to be ready for that."

I wanted to say, *Ready how? With what money? Should I bake the tarts out of hope and drywall?* What I said was, "We'll be ready," and smiled the smile that costs me more than any ingredient, and he left, and I stood in my empty café holding a red-bordered piece of paper and thought: *a stranger. I could tell a stranger.*

That night I wrote:

---

*Dear Anchor,*

*I'm told I'm supposed to write to you without telling you who I am, which turns out to be harder than it sounds. Everything I say about my day identifies me. I could write "today a man in a red hat complained about the price of scones," and if you live in this town — and the rules suggest you do — you already know exactly which man in a red hat, and possibly which scone.*

*So instead I'll tell you something true and unlocatable: today I lied to someone kindly. He asked if everything was okay, and I said yes, and it wasn't, and the lie was a kindness because the truth would only have made him uncomfortable, and he's a decent man who doesn't deserve discomfort. But now the lie is sitting in my chest along with all the others, and I'm starting to suspect a person can only store so many.*

*What do you do with yours?*

*— Lighthouse*

---

I read it over four times. Each time I heard a different objection — too much, too strange, too sad, too honest — and each time I overruled the objection, because the whole point, the terrifying liberating point, was that Anchor didn't know my face. Couldn't flinch at me in the produce aisle. Couldn't fold my honesty into the town's file on me, the one labeled *Almeida girl, father drowned, keeps the café going, so brave.*

I signed it Lighthouse. I didn't sign it Casper. I walked it to the library drop box after hours, in the dark, like a teenager mailing mixtapes, and the whole way home I felt lighter and stupider in equal measure.

* * *

The reply came nine days later, forwarded through the library in a plain envelope, and I found it wedged in my mail slot between a coupon mailer and a bill, and I knew what it was before I opened it because nobody else in Sweet Harbor writes their return address as "P.P.P., c/o Library."

I made tea first. This was deliberate. I have learned that I am not to be trusted around things I want; I burn them, or eat them, or in one memorable instance marry-adjacent myself into a situation with a sailing instructor that we will not be discussing. Tea first. Then the letter.

*Dear Lighthouse,*

*You asked what I do with my lies. Honest answer: I stack them. Neatly, like pastry layers. There's a whole architecture in me built out of things I haven't said, and the terrible thing about a well-built lie is that it holds up under weight. People stand on mine all the time. They seem sturdy. Sometimes I forget they're lies at all.*

*Today I told someone the special was going well. The special is not going well. The special is a lavender-honey thing that I defend publicly and privately consider a crime against bees. But admitting that felt like admitting something larger, so I defended the lavender to a woman who then bought six, and now she'll come back for more, and I'll keep making them, and the lie compounds weekly with interest.*

*You seem like someone who understands compounding.*

*Tell me something true again. It's strange — I don't know your face, and somehow that makes it easier to read you than anyone I see daily. Is that the design of this whole festival, do you think? Or an accident?*

*— Anchor*

I read it three times. Then I did something I hadn't done in months: I sat by the window after close with my tea going cold and wrote back immediately, same night, no drafting, no editing, the words coming up out of me like water finding a crack.

*Dear Anchor,*

*Compounding. Yes. That's exactly the word for it, and I hate that you found it before I did.*

*Here's a true thing: I'm afraid of Fridays. Not for any dramatic reason. Fridays are just when the numbers come due, and I've started hearing the week as a countdown instead of a rhythm. Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday — steps down. Thursday is the landing. Friday is the door.*

*And here's a stranger thing, since you asked for true: writing that down made it smaller. Not gone. Smaller. Like it fit inside the sentence instead of the other way around.*

*Defend your lavender if you must. But make one thing you actually love, even if no one buys it. Consider it a prescription from a woman who prescribes herself lemon tarts and follows the dosage exactly.*

*— Lighthouse*

I sealed it, addressed it, and set it by the door for morning. And standing there in my dark café, with the red-bordered notice still pinned under the register and Friday two days out and no plan worth the name, I noticed something I hadn't felt in a long time, and it took me a moment to identify it because it was so out of season.

Hope. Cautious, suspicious, waiting-to-be-disappointed hope — but hope. Somebody out there in this small, salted, gossiping town was reading my sentences and answering them honestly. Somebody was stacking their own lies and calling them architecture. Somebody knew nothing about me except what I chose to tell them, and had decided that was enough to stay.

"Careful," I said aloud to the empty room, because I'm the kind of person who warns herself out loud. "People are never what their letters say."

The room, like the oven, declined to comment.

I turned off the lights and went home, and if I slept better than I had in weeks, that was nobody's business but mine and Anchor's.

What I didn't know — what I couldn't have known, standing there sealing my fourth letter in the dark — was that across town, in a kitchen that smelled like my kitchen, behind a counter that competed with my counter, my anonymous stranger was licking an envelope with hands I'd spent twelve years refusing to shake.

But that's getting ahead of the story.
