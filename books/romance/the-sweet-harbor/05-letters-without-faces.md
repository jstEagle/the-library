## 5. Letters Without Faces

The third letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult, because Tuesdays were the day my ledger and I had our weekly fight and I always lost.

I'd left the ledger open on the counter — *Flour, 412. Sugar, 287. Butter, God help me, 301* — while I went to fetch the mail, telling myself that maybe this week the numbers would have the decency to rearrange themselves out of shame. They hadn't. They never did. But there, wedged between a flyer for gutter cleaning and a postcard from a timeshare company that had clearly never seen Sweet Harbor, was an envelope with no return address and handwriting like a confession: slanted, hurried, the kind of script written by someone who thought faster than they wrote.

I stood in the doorway of my own café holding it like it might go off.

Here is what nobody tells you about anonymous correspondence: it's a trap you build yourself, out of your own loneliness, and then you climb inside and pull the door shut and call it freedom. The library board had sold it to us as community outreach. *Write to a neighbor! Discover the stranger next door!* What they'd actually done was hand every emotionally constipated adult in this town a permission slip to say things they'd choke on if they had to make eye contact while saying them.

I should know. I'd already sent two letters.

The first had been a joke — three paragraphs of wry complaint about tourists who asked whether the lemon tarts were "gluten-free-adjacent," signed with nothing but the festival-assigned number, 47. I'd dropped it in the box feeling clever and slightly ridiculous, like a woman leaving a note in a bottle she didn't actually expect the sea to answer.

The sea had answered.

*Dear 47,* the first reply began, and even through the paper I could hear the wariness in it, the tone of someone who'd been burned enough times to check the stove twice. My correspondent — number 12, per the library board's inscrutable system — had written back four pages about the dignity of bread. Four pages. About *bread*. About how sourdough was "a relationship, not a recipe," and how he'd once kept a starter alive through a hurricane by baking it into his car and running the heater. He hadn't said he was a baker. He'd said it in every line without saying it, the way you can tell someone's a swimmer by their shoulders.

So I'd written back. Not a joke, that time. Something closer to the bone.

And now here was letter three, and my ledger was open on the counter like a witness, and I made the mistake of reading his letter before dealing with my own numbers, which is how I ended up sitting on a flour sack at six in the morning with tears I refused to acknowledge drying on my face.

*Dear 47,* he wrote.

*I'm going to tell you something I haven't told anyone, which is either a compliment or a symptom.*

*My ovens are twenty-two years old. I know because my father bought them the year I turned eleven, and I remember because he let me press the first button, and I remember THAT because it's one of maybe four memories of him that don't hurt. Last month the right deck started heating unevenly. Left side perfect, right side fifteen degrees shy. You can't bake croissants on an oven that lies to you. A repairman came, looked at it the way vets look at very old dogs, and gave me a quote that would cover my rent for two months.*

*I haven't told my mother. She works the register like it's a religion and she still calls the shop "your father's place" when she thinks I'm not listening. If I tell her the oven is dying, I'm telling her the shop is dying, and if I tell her the shop is dying, then what was the last twelve years FOR?*

*So instead I've been rotating trays every seven minutes like some kind of flour-based air traffic controller, and pretending everything is fine, and writing letters to a stranger about it, which apparently is what fine looks like on me.*

*You said in your last letter that you understand failing gracefully. I don't think I do. I think I'm failing loudly and calling it tradition. What does graceful look like from where you're standing?*

*— 12*

I read it twice. Then a third time, standing very still in the middle of my empty café while the harbor light came up gold outside the window, thinking: *this man is describing my life with different props.*

Because here's what I hadn't put in any letter yet, and here's what came pouring out of me anyway, an hour later, on cream stationery I kept for wedding-cake consultations that never came:

*Dear 12,*

*Graceful looks like this: you smile at the landlord when he hands you the notice, and you thank him, because your mother raised you polite, and then you go inside and eat the ugly tart — the one that collapsed, the one you'd never serve — standing over the sink like a raccoon, and you decide that tomorrow you'll figure it out. And then tomorrow comes and you don't figure it out, you just make more tarts, because making tarts is the only prayer you know.*

*You asked what I'm afraid of. Fine. Here it is, since you're a stranger and strangers are the only ones who get the truth: I'm afraid of the silence after. Not losing the café — well, yes, that, obviously, I'm afraid of that like I'm afraid of deep water — but the silence AFTER. The day when the door stays locked and nobody needs me to be open at six. My whole life I have been the girl at the counter. My father built this place and I inherited it like a heartbeat, and I don't know who I am if the rhythm stops. Grief I can do. I've had practice. It's irrelevance that scares me.*

*There. Now we're both ruined. Your turn.*

*— 47*

I sealed it before I could come to my senses, which is the only way I ever manage honesty — fast, like ripping off a bandage, or diving into cold water, or any other metaphor for doing the brave thing quickly enough that fear doesn't catch up.

* * *

The thing about writing to a stranger is that it rewires you slowly, the way water reshapes stone, so you don't notice until one day you catch yourself being a different person and have to decide whether to be embarrassed.

By the fifth exchange, I was telling Number Twelve things I hadn't told Nadia-from-the-library-board, things I hadn't told my own reflection. That I talked to my father's photograph when I proofed dough at night, because the quiet kitchen reminded me of funerals. That I'd stopped going to the harbor festival years ago because the last time I went, someone's kid asked me why my dad wasn't there, and I'd smiled and said something gracious and then sat in my car for forty minutes unable to turn the key.

He wrote back within days, always within days, as if my letters were arriving at his shop at the exact moment he needed them:

*You sat in the car for forty minutes. I do that too. Different parking lot, same ritual. There's a spot behind the marina office where I used to sit after closing and just... not go home yet. Home is quiet in a way that echoes. At least in the car the silence has edges.*

*You said you talk to your father's photo. I talk to my oven. I tell it when it's behaving badly. My staff — both of them, and one of those is my cousin who shows up when he feels like it — think I'm strange. Maybe I am. But you don't strike me as someone who gets to judge, 47. You named your grief a minute ago like it was a regular customer.*

*Question, since we're being ruined together: when did you last make something just for the joy of it? Not for the ledger. Not for the counter. Just because your hands wanted to?*

I sat with that question for two full days, which should tell you something about the state of me. Two days of lemon tarts and almond croissants and the rosemary loaves that old Mr. Okafor bought every morning at 6:15 sharp, rain or shine, and every single one of them made *for* somebody, *for* the till, *for* the survival math. When had I last baked the way I baked at fourteen, sneaking downstairs at midnight to experiment while my father pretended not to hear?

So I made a thing. One thing, at midnight, with the good vanilla I'd been hoarding like a dragon. A cardamom-honey bun, no reason, no slot on the menu, just my hands remembering how to play instead of work. I ate it warm over the sink — the raccoon position, apparently my natural habitat — and I wrote to him about it before I'd even washed the honey off my fingers.

His reply made me laugh out loud alone in my kitchen at 6 a.m., which the delivery driver witnessed and later mentioned, concernedly, to Mrs. Okafor, who mentioned it to everyone.

*You ate it over the sink. 47. We are the same person in different aprons.*

* * *

Here is the part I want to be honest about, because this chapter of my life deserves accuracy: somewhere around the sixth letter, I realized I was in trouble.

Not the dramatic kind. The quiet kind, the dangerous kind, the kind that starts with *it's just letters* and ends with you checking the mail twice a day and knowing the weight of one specific person's envelopes by feel. His paper changed depending on his mood — scratchy recycled stock when he was agitated, smooth cream when he'd taken time. I knew this. I had catalogued this. A grown woman, cataloguing the stationery preferences of a man whose face she'd never seen.

And the worst part — the part I'd deny under oath — was how *known* I felt. There's a particular vertigo in being understood by someone who can't see you. No face to manage, no reputation to defend, no history sitting between you like furniture. Number Twelve knew about the ledger and the raccoon-eating and the car-sitting and the midnight baking, and he knew none of the things people in town used to define me: orphan, the Almeida girl, the one whose father drowned, the one who wouldn't look at the Marchettis.

That last one. I want to be careful here, because this is where the story turns, though I didn't know it yet.

In town, my grief had a shape and a villain and a twelve-year-old border wall, and everyone respected the border. Nobody in Sweet Harbor said the name Marchetti to me unless they had to. And I had built my whole architecture of grievance on that foundation — the boat with the blue hull, the dock rope that frayed, my father in the water, and the Marchetti family who never once came to the funeral, never once knocked, never once said the words that might have made the weight shareable.

But Number Twelve didn't know any of that. To him I was just a woman afraid of silence, and he was just a man talking to his oven, and between us we had constructed something so honest it made everything else in my life look like set dressing.

Which meant that every letter was also a small betrayal — of my father, maybe, or of the version of myself I'd been performing for twelve years. I was laughing at a stranger's jokes about proofing baskets while refusing to buy flour from certain suppliers. I was confessing my fears to someone whose name, if I heard it spoken, might make me walk out of a room.

I didn't know that yet, though. On the good days, in that golden stretch of early autumn, all I knew was that Tuesday had become my favorite day, and that I'd started keeping his letters in the drawer under the register, beneath the fake bottom where my father used to hide the emergency cash, and that opening that drawer felt like a secret I was choosing rather than a debt I owed.

* * *

The seventh letter arrived with something extra: a recipe.

Not a full recipe — a fragment, scrawled on the back of a page torn from what looked like an order pad. *Honey-cardamom twist, adjusted for a lying oven.* He'd taken my midnight bun and reverse-engineered it, tested it, corrected it for his broken equipment, and sent it back to me like a gift-wrapped dare.

*Try it,* he wrote. *If yours comes out better than mine, I don't want to know. Some illusions are load-bearing.*

I made it that night. It came out imperfect — the twist lopsided, the glaze pooling wrong — and it was the best thing I'd eaten in a year, because it had a person attached to it. I stood in my kitchen eating warm pastry and understanding, with sudden terrible clarity, exactly how much trouble I was in.

Then I sat down and wrote the letter I'd been circling for weeks, the one where the mask slips all the way off:

*Dear 12,*

*I need to tell you something, and then I need you to keep being a stranger, because the arrangement is working and I don't want to examine why.*

*I haven't laughed like this in twelve years. I mean that literally, not figuratively — there's a specific date involved, and a dock, and a story I'm not ready to tell you, and maybe never will be. But here's what I will tell you: whatever this is — these letters, this drawer under my register, this honey-cardamom situation — it has done something I didn't think was possible anymore. It made the kitchen loud again. Not sound-loud. Alive-loud. For twelve years I've been baking in a museum of my own life, and you, whoever you are, wherever you are, went and left the windows open.*

*Don't ask me who I am. Don't try to guess. The library board swears the reveal isn't until the festival, and I am begging you, for once in your clearly rule-following life, to respect the rules. Because I like who I am in these letters. She's braver than I am. She says true things. And I'm not ready to find out what happens to her when she has a face.*

*Yours under protest,*
*— 47*

I sealed it. I stamped it. I walked it to the postbox on Harbor Street in the blue hour before dawn, and I stood there a moment with my hand still resting on the slot, watching the sun come up over the water my father died in, feeling lighter and guiltier than I had in years.

Behind me, footsteps on the boardwalk. Early, for the fishing boats. I didn't turn around. Whoever it was, they weren't looking for me, and I had a café to open and a lie of omission to tend.

What I couldn't have known — what I wouldn't know until much later, when the ground finally gave way beneath all my careful certainties — was that the footsteps belonged to Theo Marchetti, carrying his own letter to his own postbox, having stood not ten feet away from me in the gray light while I touched the slot where his words now waited.

Two strangers, back to back at the edge of the harbor, mailing each other the truth.

It would have been funny, later. In the moment, all I felt was the wind coming off the water, cold for September, and the strange conviction — irrational, persistent, like a splinter working its way toward the surface — that something was about to change.

I just didn't know yet that it would start with a notebook.
