## 9. Festival Committee

The summons came on a Tuesday, tucked between a past-due invoice from my flour supplier and a flyer for a lost parakeet named Captain. It was printed on library letterhead, which meant it was official, and it was signed by Dot Ferreira in her unmistakable handwriting — the kind of looping, aggressive cursive that looked like it was trying to strangle the page.

*Dear Casper,* it read. *The Harbor Festival Committee requires your expertise in the Baked Goods Pavilion. We have also extended an invitation to Theo Marchetti, as the festival celebrates all of Sweet Harbor's culinary traditions. Meetings are Thursdays, 6 p.m., at the library. Attendance is mandatory.*

*Mandatory.* From a volunteer organization. Only in Sweet Harbor.

I read it twice, hoping the second pass would reveal some loophole, some asterisk, some small merciful footnote saying *just kidding.* No such luck. Dot Ferreira did not believe in footnotes. Dot Ferreira believed in duty, laminated signage, and dragging this town into community spirit whether it wanted to come or not.

I could have said no. I want that on the record. I could have written back a polite refusal, claimed a scheduling conflict, invented a rare pastry-related illness. But the café was three missed payments from foreclosure, and the festival's Baked Goods Pavilion drew half the county, and half the county had never once set foot inside Sweet Harbor Café because they were too busy lining up outside Marchetti's like it was a religious experience.

So I went. And that is how I found myself, on a Thursday evening in late June, standing in the library's community room in front of a folding table covered in yarn flags, arguing about bunting.

"You can't put the Marchetti banner next to the Almeida one," I said. "It'll look like we're at war."

"We *are* at war," Theo said, not looking up from the clipboard he'd appointed himself keeper of. "A delicious war."

"Then maybe don't hang our flags side by side like we're sharing a honeymoon suite."

He glanced up then, and there it was — that infuriating half-smile he'd been deploying around town for twelve years, the one that suggested he knew something I didn't and found it funny. "You've thought about our honeymoon suite?"

The room went quiet. Dot Ferreira froze mid-lamination. Somewhere near the window, old Mr. Castellanos made a sound like a teakettle deciding against it.

"That's not—" Heat crawled up my neck. "That is not what I said."

"It's what I heard."

"What you heard is slander."

"What you *said*," he said, tapping his pen against the clipboard with the patience of a man who had never once been struck by lightning despite clearly deserving it, "was 'honeymoon suite.' I'm just organizing the information."

"I will put you in the pie toss."

"The pie toss isn't until Saturday."

"I'll move it up."

Dot cleared her throat with the force of a woman who had raised four children and buried two husbands and would not be derailed by whatever this was. "Casper. Theo. The banners go where the sightlines are best, and the sightlines are best together, by the main arch. This is a *unity* festival." She said *unity* the way other people said *nuclear deterrent.* "Twelve years of this cold war between your families has been bad enough. The committee has decided it ends this summer."

"The committee has decided," I repeated.

"The committee has decided," Theo echoed, and I swear to God he was enjoying this.

I looked at the banner mock-up Dot had pinned to the corkboard — SWEET HARBOR HARBOR FESTIVAL: ONE TOWN, TWO TABLES — and felt something twist in my chest that I refused to name. One town, two tables. As if the last twelve years hadn't happened. As if my father's boat hadn't gone down within sight of the Marchetti dock, as if the Marchettis hadn't closed their shutters and their mouths ever since, as if grief were a thing you could resolve with matching bunting.

But I needed the festival. I needed the pavilion traffic. I needed the prize money more than I needed my pride, and my pride, frankly, had been living beyond its means for years.

"Fine," I said. "Side by side. But if his banner droops onto mine, I'm charging him for advertising."

"Noted," Theo said, writing something on the clipboard. I would later find out he had written, verbatim, *"Casper threatens advertising fees. Cute."*

* * *

Theo's version of events, if you asked him — and nobody asked him, but he told people anyway — was that he walked into that first committee meeting already braced for combat and left confused about why he kept reloading.

Here's what nobody tells you about hating someone for twelve years: eventually the hatred gets a routine. It becomes muscle memory. You see them across the farmers market and you do your jaw-clench, your price-check on their chalkboard specials, your little internal monologue about fraud and shortcuts and whatever else keeps the fire fed. It's practically self-care.

What nobody tells you is that routines break when the person starts showing up *every Thursday*, standing three feet away, smelling like lemon zest and stubbornness, arguing with you about trash can placement like the fate of nations hung on it.

"They should be recycling bins," she said, week two.

"They're going to be full of pie plates and corn cobs. That's compost."

"Pie plates aren't compostable, Theo."

"Paper plates are."

"Who's using paper plates at a baking festival? That's like serving wine in a Styrofoam cup."

"Okay, first, some of us are environmentally conscious—"

"Some of us are *correct*—"

"Casper." Dot's voice, from across the room, carrying the weary authority of a lighthouse. "Bins. Both. Labeled. Move on."

We moved on. That was the other thing about these meetings — we moved on, over and over, from argument to argument, and somewhere in the moving-on I stopped keeping score. Which was its own kind of alarming. Twelve years of scorekeeping, undone by municipal logistics.

Week three, she brought samples. She claimed it was for "pavilion planning purposes," but she set a plate of those lemon tarts down in front of me — the ones that had beaten me at the county fair two years running, the ones I had spent an embarrassing amount of time trying to reverse-engineer — and watched my face with the open delight of someone feeding a rival poison and waiting for the dramatic collapse.

I took a bite because refusing would have been worse.

It was perfect. That was the humiliating part. Not good, not solid, *perfect* — the curd bright and sharp as a slap, the shell shattering exactly where it should. I chewed slowly, buying time, aware she was watching, aware my face was giving away everything.

"Well?" she said.

"It's fine."

"*Fine.*"

"There's too much zest."

"There is not too much zest."

"There's so much zest, Casper. It's a zest crime scene."

"And yet it beat you at the fair."

I looked at the tart. I looked at her. Something in my chest did a thing I decided not to examine, the way you decide not to examine a weird noise your car is making.

"Give me the recipe," I said.

"Absolutely not."

"Trade you. My sfogliatelle technique."

Her eyes narrowed. For a second — just a second — something flickered across her face that wasn't hostility. It looked almost like temptation. Then it shuttered, and she pulled the plate back toward her side of the table like a poker player protecting a winning hand.

"Nice try," she said. But she left the plate. And I ate the rest of them when she wasn't looking, and they were perfect, and I hated how much I enjoyed being wrong.

* * *

By week four, Casper had developed a theory she did not like: Theo Marchetti was easy to hate in theory and difficult to hate in practice.

In theory, he was the son of the family that had let her father drown in silence. In practice, he was the man who showed up forty minutes early to every meeting to tape down extension cords before Mrs. Okafor tripped on one again. In theory, he was her direct competitor, bleeding her dry one croissant at a time. In practice, he had quietly told the committee the pavilion should feature both cafés' signature items "because honestly, the tourists come for the harbor, not the turf war," and when Dot had beamed at him, he'd caught Casper's eye across the room and shrugged like he hadn't just handed her a gift.

It was disorienting. It was like discovering your house had a room you'd never noticed — one you'd been walking past for years, and now that you'd seen it, you couldn't stop seeing it.

Thursday, week five. Budget subcommittee. Which meant the two of them alone at a card table with a calculator and a box of stale shortbread, because everyone else had mysteriously developed conflicts.

"Entertainment budget," Theo said, reading off the master sheet. "Band, six hundred. Sound rental, two-fifty. Pie toss reconstruction—"

"Why does the pie toss need *reconstruction*?"

"Because last year Mr. Castellanos threw out his shoulder and his family threatened litigation unless we installed a 'safety backstop.'"

"A safety backstop. For a pie toss."

"Sweet Harbor takes its pies seriously."

She wrote the number down. Her hand brushed his reaching for the calculator, and she yanked hers back like the calculator was electrified, and he pretended not to notice, which was somehow worse than noticing.

"Booth deposits," she said, over-bright. "Both cafés paid?"

"Paid in full." He paused. "Yours came in the same envelope as a note asking if the deposit was refundable if the pavilion collapsed."

"That was a hypothetical question."

"It was a very specific hypothetical question."

"I contain multitudes."

He laughed. Actually laughed — a real one, unguarded, the kind that rearranged his whole face, and Casper stared at him with the expression of a woman watching a dog walk on its hind legs. He caught himself, coughed, straightened the budget sheets.

"So," he said, too casually. "Your pen pal. Still writing?"

The question landed sideways, the way his questions sometimes did lately — aimed at some soft place she hadn't marked on any map. Because yes. She was still writing. Every Sunday night, she sat at her kitchen table with the good pen and told a stranger things she couldn't say to anyone with a face: that the bank had sent another letter, that she'd lied to her mother about how business was, that some nights she stood in the empty café after close and talked to her father out loud like he was going to answer.

"How'd you know I have a pen pal?" she asked carefully.

"Dot announced the whole program at the first meeting. Read out the participation list." He flipped a page. "Half the town's doing it. Even Mr. Castellanos. His letters are apparently all complaints about parking."

"Right." She exhaled. "Yes. Still writing. It's... nice, actually. Saying things to someone with no stake in the answer."

Something crossed his face then — quick, unreadable, gone before she could name it. "Yeah," he said. "No stake. That's the whole point."

They worked through the rest of the budget in something dangerously close to harmony. When the meeting ended, he held the door for her, and she let him, and neither of them mentioned it.

Walking home through the blue July dusk, Casper caught herself humming. She stopped dead on the harbor path, horrified. *Humming.* Over Theo Marchetti. Over *bunting.*

She blamed the tarts. Too much zest. Clearly it was getting to her head.

* * *

Theo noticed things. It was his curse. He noticed that Casper's chalkboard prices hadn't changed in eight months even though butter had doubled. He noticed the new barista at her counter was actually her cousin working for free, and that the espresso machine had a duct-taped steam wand. He noticed that she stayed late at committee meetings reorganizing the same box of flyers, like a woman avoiding an empty building.

And he noticed — this was the one that kept him up at night — that his own letters were getting longer. That Sunday nights had become the best hour of his week. That he'd started telling this anonymous stranger things he'd never told anyone: that the shop was drowning, that his father's ghost ran the ovens better than he did, that some mornings he stood in the back and couldn't remember why the Marchetti name was worth saving if saving it meant becoming his father.

His pen pal wrote back like she understood. Like she'd stood in that same dark kitchen. Once she'd written: *Some nights I talk to my dead father in an empty café. Tell me I'm not the only crazy person in Sweet Harbor.*

He'd held that letter for a long time. Because he *was* the only other crazy person in Sweet Harbor, and he knew it, and he couldn't say so.

Thursday, week six. Final planning session before the festival. Dot had them hanging the actual banners under the main arch, which required a ladder, which required Theo holding it steady while Casper climbed, which required approximately zero discussion but generated enough static electricity to power the pavilion.

"Left," she called down. "My left."

"Your left or my left?"

"If you drop this ladder I will haunt you."

"You'd have to die first, and you're too stubborn."

"Flattery. From you. Mark the calendar."

He adjusted the ladder and looked up without meaning to — up at her, silhouetted against the evening sky, arms full of flag rope, hair coming loose from its clip, laughing at something she'd said herself. And the thought arrived fully formed, unwelcome as a health inspector: *God, I wish I could tell her.*

Tell her what, exactly? That he was her pen pal? That he'd signed up in April because Dot cornered him at the bakery and because — though he'd never admit this to a living soul — he'd hoped, with a desperation that embarrassed him, that the universe might route his letter to *her*? That he'd been writing to her for weeks, peeling himself open one envelope at a time, while she stood three feet away calling him a zest criminal?

"Hey," she said, looking down. "You went somewhere."

"Nowhere," he said.

"You got quiet. It's unsettling. Be annoying again."

"Yes, ma'am." He steadied the ladder. "Careful on the way down. I hear the pavement at these festivals is treacherous."

She descended, and took the banner edge, and together they tied it off — ALMEIDA on the left, MARCHETTI on the right, one knot, shared work. Their hands overlapped on the rope. Neither pulled away fast enough.

"One town, two tables," she said, reading the banner, and her voice had an odd note in it.

"Yeah," Theo said. "One town."

He walked home the long way, along the water, and told himself the tightness in his chest was just festival stress. Behind him, the banners snapped in the harbor wind, side by side, and if you squinted they almost looked like they belonged together.

* * *

Casper got home to find a letter waiting — her pen pal's weekly, postmarked Monday, smelling faintly of cinnamon.

She made tea. She sat by the window. She opened it with the small ceremony she'd started allowing herself, this one indulgence in a life otherwise measured in flour and arrears.

*I held a ladder tonight,* it began. *For someone who drives me completely insane. And for about ten seconds, tying off a rope, I forgot we're supposed to be enemies. It was the best ten seconds of my month. Don't tell anyone I said that. Anyway — you wrote once that you talk to your father in an empty café. I keep thinking about that. What would he say, if he answered? What do you think he'd want for you? Write back. I mean it. Some letters matter more than others, and I think this is one of them.*

Casper read it twice. Three times. Her tea went cold.

There was something in it — in the ladder, in the rope, in the ten seconds — that snagged at her like a loose thread on a sweater. A feeling of having heard this story before. Of having *lived* it, hours ago, hands overlapping on hemp rope under a string of flags.

But that was impossible. Absurd. Her pen pal was a stranger. Half the town was in the program; Dot had read the list aloud herself.

Still, she sat by the window a long time, the letter in her lap, the harbor going dark outside, and the question turned over and over in her chest like dough that wouldn't rise:

*Why does it feel like he was there?*
