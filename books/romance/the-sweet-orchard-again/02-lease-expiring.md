## 2. Lease, Expiring

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for proofing dough and wiping down the front case and pretending the week was ordinary. Beatrix Whitlock found it wedged between a flour invoice and a postcard from a customer who had moved to Tucson and missed her sourdough, and she knew before she opened it what it was, because the envelope was heavy cream stock and her name was typed, not handwritten, and nobody in Sweet Orchard typed.

She read it standing at the counter with her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, then pulled them down and read it again, slower, as though the second pass might soften the language. It did not. *Dear Ms. Whitlock: This letter serves as formal notice that the lease on the premises known as 14 Mill Row will expire on the first of April. Should you wish to exercise the purchase option outlined in your original agreement, funds must be received in full no later than March 31. Absent such payment, the property will revert to the estate and be listed.*

The estate. She set the letter down on the marble slab where she rolled croissants, and the paper sat there looking pristine and terrible against all that flour-dusted wood, like a knife laid on a pillow.

Three months. Ninety-one days, if she was going to count, and she was always counting now—days since the concert, days since her hands had betrayed her in front of two thousand people, days since she had come home to Sweet Orchard with a suitcase full of performance gowns she would never wear again and a bank account that looked like a wound. The bakery had been meant as a lifeboat, not a harbor. She'd signed the lease with money from the insurance settlement—the irony of that had not escaped her—and told herself she would figure out the rest later. Later had arrived wearing cream-colored stationery.

She did the arithmetic at the little table by the window while the ovens ticked and breathed behind her. The purchase option was forty-two thousand dollars. Her savings, after three years of a bakery's thin margins and one catastrophic oven repair, stood at eleven thousand six hundred. She wrote both numbers on the back of a receipt, stared at them, and then turned the receipt over so she wouldn't have to look.

Outside, Mill Row was doing its January impression: gray light, salt-stained snowbanks, the hardware store's flag snapping in a wind that smelled like iron and coming weather. Mrs. Okonkwo passed with her terrier and raised a mittened hand. Beatrix raised hers back and smiled the smile she kept for neighbors, the one that said everything is fine, and then she went into the kitchen and worked bread until her forearms burned, because there were things her hands could still do, and she had learned to hold onto them the way a drowning woman holds a plank.

* * *

He came in on Thursday, ten minutes before closing, when the light through the front windows had gone the color of weak tea and she was already thinking about the walk home.

She heard the bell over the door and said, without turning, "We're nearly out of the rye, but I can wrap you half a brioche loaf if you want it," and then she turned, and the sentence died somewhere in her chest.

Theo Vance stood just inside the doorway, stamping snow off his shoes with the exaggerated care of a man who needed something to do with his body. He was older. Of course he was older; seven years does that to everyone, but it had done particular things to him—a silver thread at his temples, a line between his brows that hadn't been there at thirty, a way of holding his shoulders slightly in, as if he'd learned to take up less space. His coat was good wool, charcoal, city-cut. He looked like what he was: someone who had left and become a person who wore coats like that.

"Beatrix." Her name in his mouth sounded like an apology already in progress.

"Theo Vance." She said the whole name deliberately, the way you'd set a heavy object down. "You're a long way from Boston."

"I'm—" He stopped. Started again. "I was in the area."

Nobody was in the area. Sweet Orchard was ninety minutes from anywhere and proud of it. She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, giving herself time to arrange her face into something neutral, something that would not show him how her pulse had jumped like a startled bird.

"We close in ten minutes," she said. "If you want bread, I can sell you bread."

"That's not—" He glanced toward the kitchen door, the window, the empty shop, anywhere but directly at her. "Could we sit? Just for a minute."

"There's nothing to sit on. I took the chairs out. People linger." It was true; she'd removed the little café table in her first month, unable to bear the sight of customers settling in comfortably, staying, occupying time the way she used to occupy a stage. "You can say whatever it is standing up."

Something flickered across his face—not surprise, exactly. Recognition. He knew her well enough to know what she was doing, and she saw him decide not to fight it. He reached into his coat and took out an envelope, and her stomach dropped before he even held it up, because she recognized the weight of it, the cream stock, the typed address. Heavy paper for heavy news.

"This is from Hollis & Marsh," he said. "They represent the estate of Aldous Wren. You may have received correspondence about the lease—"

"I got the letter Tuesday."

"Right. Yes. That's separate, mostly. This is—" He exhaled. "This is a summons. The estate is filing suit. Over the insurance claim from the concert. They're alleging that the cancellation—the failure to complete the performance—constituted a breach of your contractual obligations, and they're seeking recovery of the policy payout. Plus costs." He said it fast, the way you pull a splinter, and then stood there with the envelope extended between them like a man offering her something live and angry.

She didn't take it. Not at first. She looked at his hands instead—his hands, which had once laced through hers under a table at a restaurant in Vienna while Julian Marsh toasted her debut from across the room, his hands, which had never once, in all the years since, reached out to tell her what was coming. Those hands were holding the instrument of her ruin now, neatly typed, properly served.

"You're their lawyer," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd known, in some cold cellar of herself, from the moment the bell rang. Who else would they send? Who else knew the terrain?

"I'm of counsel. I do conflicts work, mostly, these days. But I grew up here, and the partners thought—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "It doesn't matter what they thought. I should have told you this was coming. I asked to be the one to bring it. I owed you that much."

"You owe me considerably more than that," Beatrix said, "but let's start there."

The words came out level, almost gentle, and she watched them land on him like blows anyway. Good. Let him feel the weight of them. Seven years ago she had stood in a green room with her hands shaking too badly to hold a cup of water, and Theo Vance had kissed her forehead and told her it would be all right, and then he had gone back to Boston and become a lawyer for people like the Wren estate, and she had spent three years learning to knead dough because it was the only thing her hands could do without shaking.

She took the envelope. Her fingers were steady—she made sure of it, made a small private act of will out of it—and she opened it right there, under the weak tea light, and read enough to understand the shape of it. Counts one through four. Negligence. Breach of contract. Unjust enrichment. Something about the settlement disbursement. Numbers with commas in them, numbers that would eat her eleven thousand six hundred dollars and the bakery and the lease option and every small morning she had built out of flour and early alarms.

"They'll want to settle before hearing," Theo said quietly. "That's standard. There's a hearing date in the papers—mid-March, county courthouse. Before your lease deadline." He paused. "I noticed that. The timing. I don't think it's an accident."

"No," she said. "I don't either."

The ovens ticked behind her. Somewhere down the street a truck downshifted on the hill. Ordinary sounds, the sounds of a town that had folded her back into itself and called her the girl who runs the bakery on Mill Row, and here was the past standing in her shop in a charcoal coat, holding out paperwork.

"Why you?" she asked. "Really. Out of everyone at that firm."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might actually answer honestly. Then: "Because I couldn't stand the idea of a stranger handing you this."

"That's not a reason, Theo. That's a feeling."

"I know." His voice roughened. "I've had seven years of reasons, Beatrix. None of them have ever survived contact with you."

She wanted to throw something. A rolling pin. The whole tray of tomorrow's loaves. Instead she folded the summons along its existing crease and set it on the counter, face down, the way you'd turn a photograph over.

"The shop's closed," she said. "You should go. The road over Miller's Pass gets bad after dark, and there's weather coming—they've been saying all week it's going to be a big one."

He nodded, and gathered himself, and got as far as the door. Then he stopped, one hand on the frame, snow-light making him pale.

"For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "the case is weaker than they think. I've read the file. Your medical records alone—" He caught himself, visibly, some professional instinct slamming shut. "Never mind. That's not my place anymore."

"What about my medical records?"

But he was already shaking his head, pulling the door open, letting in a blade of cold air that cut across the floor and found her ankles. "Read the claim carefully," he said. "That's all. Read everything carefully. And Beatrix—" Now he turned, and for a moment he was twenty-eight again, standing in a green room, promising her things the world had no intention of keeping. "Whatever you think of me—and you should think badly of me—you should know I never stopped—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, and she watched him absorb it, watch him decide, again, not to fight. "Just drive safe, Theo. There's a storm coming."

* * *

After he was gone she stood in the empty shop for a long time, listening to the silence reassemble itself around the shape he'd left in it.

Then she took down the summons and read it cover to cover, twice, sitting on the flour sack by the kitchen door with a penlight because she'd switched off the main lights out of habit and couldn't quite bring herself to turn them back on, as if the dark could keep any of this from being true. The estate of Aldous Wren. She remembered old man Wren only vaguely—a board member, a man who liked the intermission sherry and had once told her, gripping her hand too hard after a rehearsal, that girls who played like her made the men nervous. He was dead now, and his estate wanted its insurance money back, and the instrument of collection was the man who had once held her hand under a table in Vienna.

She laughed—one short, ugly sound that bounced off the tile—and pressed the back of her wrist against her mouth.

Eleven thousand six hundred dollars. Forty-two thousand owed. A lawsuit arriving mid-March like a scheduled train. And outside the window, the sky over Miller's Pass had gone the color of a bruise, low and yellowish at the edges, the particular sky that meant the forecasters weren't exaggerating for once.

She thought about the farmhouse. Her grandmother's place, up past the orchard—empty three years, but sound, with the wood stove and the deep pantry and the kind of silence a person could think in. If the storm was going to be what they said, the roads would close, and she would rather be closed in out there than here, staring at four walls that smelled of summons paper.

She thought about Theo's face when he'd said *read everything carefully*. About the thing he'd swallowed—*your medical records alone*—and the way he'd cut himself off, twice, like a man walking a ledge he knew better than to look down from.

Beatrix banked the ovens, tied the apron on its hook, and stood a moment in the middle of her dark shop with her arms crossed, listening to the wind test the windows like fingers testing a lock.

Three months to buy her own life back. A lawsuit aimed at the only money she had left. And a blizzard coming down the pass with something else riding in ahead of it—she could feel it, the way animals feel weather—something the storm had been promised, something still out on the road.

She packed a bag that night. Just in case.
