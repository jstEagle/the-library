## 10. Theo Comes Home

The call comes on a Tuesday, which feels like a cruelty. Tuesdays are for turtle counts. Tuesdays are for the long, flat stretch of beach between the jetty and the dune grass, for the clipboard and the tide chart and the small, orderly arithmetic of a life I built so nothing could knock it over.

The phone buzzes in my pocket while I'm kneeling in the wet sand, logging a green sea turtle's nest coordinates, and I almost don't answer. Almost. The number has a 912 area code I don't recognize, and something in me—the animal part, the part that never unlearned how to flinch—knows before I swipe the screen.

"Juniper." Not a question. My brother's voice is lower than I remember, roughened at the edges like driftwood that's been in the surf too long. "It's me."

"I know," I say. My knees are in the sand and my heart is somewhere up around my teeth. "Theo."

"They moved me up. Good behavior, the parole board, all of it." A pause, and in the pause I can hear him deciding how much to tell me, the way he's always decided, the way our whole family learned to ration the truth like water on a burning boat. "I'm out, June. I've been out for three weeks."

Three weeks. The words land in my chest like stones dropped down a well. Three weeks he's been free and I didn't know, didn't hear, didn't— "Where are you?"

"Bus station. Savannah." A long breath. "I've got a ticket. I wanted to ask first. I know you've got your life there. I know I don't—"

"Come," I say, before he can finish the sentence, before he can build the little cage of apology he's been building for ten years. "Come home."

He's quiet for so long I check whether the call dropped.

"Home," he repeats, testing the word like a man testing ice.

* * *

I spend the next two days in a state of frantic, disguised tidiness. I strip the guest bed and wash sheets that were already clean. I buy coffee I can't afford and eggs and the cheap white bread he used to like, the kind that sticks to the roof of your mouth, because prison food is nothing and maybe a taste of before is worth something. I move the letters.

That's the part that shames me, later. Not the scrubbing or the shopping or the way I reorganized the bookshelf three times. It's standing at my desk on Wednesday night, looking at the box where ten years of unsent letters live—*Dear E, today I saw a leatherback*—and sliding the box into the closet, under the spare blankets, because Theo can't see it. Because Theo can't see the name on the envelopes, or the handwriting he'd recognize, or the fact that his little brother has spent a decade writing love letters to the boy who watched him get convicted.

Because Elias Wren lives next door now, and Theo Crane cannot know that. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

I tell myself it's mercy. I tell myself Theo is brittle and raw and the last thing he needs is to learn that the witness who was kind to him—the kid in the gray jacket who brought him water while the orchard burned and said, *I saw, I'll tell them what I saw*—grew up into the man next door. I tell myself it's protection.

It's a locked door, is what it is. But I'm good at locked doors. I've been building them since I was fifteen.

* * *

The bus arrives Thursday at 4:40, and I'm there at 3:15 because I don't know how to wait for something this large without wearing a groove in the pavement.

He comes down the steps last, the way he came down every stair for ten years—last, careful, hands visible. He's thinner than the photographs. His hair is cropped short in that institutional way that never quite grows out right, and he's wearing a jacket two sizes too big and carrying everything he owns in a duffel bag that has been repaired with what looks like dental floss.

He sees me and stops on the last step, and for a moment we just look at each other across ten years and one fire.

"Hey, June," he says.

"Hey, Theo."

He doesn't hug me. I don't hug him. We stand there in the salt wind with the bus pulling away behind him, two people who love each other and have forgotten, in the long dark, how to reach. Then he shifts the duffel on his shoulder and says, "You got old," and I laugh, and the laugh cracks something open, and he almost smiles, and that's how we begin.

* * *

The cottage is too small for two people who've forgotten how to share air, but he takes the guest room and stands in the doorway of it for a long time, looking at the made bed, the clean window, the jar of wildflowers I put on the nightstand because I didn't know what else to put.

"It's nice," he says, in the tone of a man describing a cell with better lighting.

"Theo—"

"It's nice," he says again, firmer, and I let it go. Ten years inside teaches you to accept small goods without examining them. I know this. I've read the literature. I've sat across from social workers who explained my brother to me in clinical terms: *institutionalized, hypervigilant, a man who will need time.* But knowing it in a pamphlet and watching him check the window latch twice, then a third time, then position his chair so his back is to the wall and his eyes are on the door—knowing it and living it are different animals.

He's watching me too. I feel it all through the first evening, all through the takeout containers and the careful small talk. He's watching me the way he used to watch the yard at the house in Alder County, before. Reading the perimeter. And I understand, with a sick lurch, what he's actually looking for. He's looking for whether there's room for him here. Whether my life—the turtles, the tide charts, the tidy cottage, the decade of safety I built on the foundation of his sacrifice—has a Theo-shaped space in it, or whether he's a ghost who's wandered into a house where he's no longer wanted.

"Tell me about the turtles," he says, when the silence gets too heavy.

So I do. I tell him about nesting season and the temperature of the sand determining the sex of the hatchlings, and he listens with his whole body, leaning forward, and for a minute he's the Theo from before, the one who knew every tree in the orchard by name, who could tell you which apple tree our grandfather grafted himself.

"Warmer sand makes more females," I say.

"Huh." He turns his coffee cup in his hands. "World keeps making more of whatever survives, I guess."

I don't have an answer for that, so I refill his coffee.

* * *

Elias comes by Friday morning.

I hear him before I see him—the scrape of the fence gate, the easy rhythm of his footsteps on the path—and my whole body goes to alarm, a fire bell ringing in my chest. He's carrying two paper cups of coffee from the shop in town and a paper bag that smells like cinnamon, and he's smiling before he sees my face, and then he sees my face and the smile falters.

"Juniper?"

"My brother's here," I say, low, fast, stepping out onto the porch and pulling the door mostly shut behind me. "Theo. He got out early. He arrived yesterday."

Elias sets the cups down on the porch rail, slowly, like he's handling something that might detonate. I watch him understand it in real time—the name landing in him the way it landed in me on the phone, ten years of history compressed into four letters. Theo. The man who took the blame. The man whose conviction is the wound underneath everything, the wound Elias's own family salted and buried.

"Okay," Elias says quietly. "Okay. What do you need?"

The question undoes me a little. Not *who is he*, not *is it safe*, not the hundred reasonable questions. Just *what do you need*, asked in that steady voice, and I have to look at the ocean for a second because if I look at him I'll do something reckless like tell the truth.

"I need you to not be here right now," I say. "And I need—" I swallow. "He doesn't know who you are. What your name is. I haven't told him anything about you."

Something moves through Elias's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning in shallow water. "You haven't told him about me at all?"

"I told him about my neighbor Elias," I say. "I didn't tell him about the letters. Or the fire. Or—" I gesture at the space between us, at whatever this is, this thing we've been building in borrowed time. "He's not ready. He can't know that you're a Wren. Not yet. Theo and that name—" I shake my head. "It would be like handing him a lit match."

Elias is quiet for a moment. The gulls are screaming over the cove. Somewhere down the beach a truck backs up, beeping.

"You're protecting him," he says at last, and there's something in his voice I can't read. Not anger. Something more tired than that. "From me."

"From the name," I say. "Elias. The name is safe. The name is—" I reach out and touch his wrist, just for a second, just because I need to. "The name is the best thing I've got. But the last name, right now, with him—" I take my hand back. "Give me time."

He nods. He picks up his coffee cups and the cinnamon bag, and he says, "I'll bring these another day," and he goes, and I stand on my porch watching him cross back to his cottage, and I feel the crack open up inside me—the old loyalty pulling one way, the new hope pulling the other, and me stretched across the gap like a rope bridge in wind.

* * *

Theo notices everything. Of course he does. Ten years of watching taught him nothing if not how to read a room, a face, a fence line.

"Who's that?" he asks that afternoon. He's standing at the kitchen window with his arms crossed, watching Elias's cottage, watching Elias move around his own yard.

"Neighbor. Elias. He moved in a couple months ago."

"He wave at me this morning?"

I'm washing a dish that's already clean. "Probably."

"He's been here a couple months and you didn't mention him." Theo's voice is light, conversational, and underneath it, a blade. Not suspicious of Elias—suspicious of me. Of what I'm not saying. He knows the shape of a secret; he lived inside one for a decade. "You mention the mailman. You mention Odette Marsh's cat. You don't mention a man who brings you coffee."

"It's new," I say. Which is even true, in the way that matters.

Theo is quiet for a while. Then he says, without turning around, "Does he know?"

The dish squeaks under the sponge. "Know what?"

"June." He turns. His eyes are the same gray as mine, our mother's gray, and they're tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix. "You know what. Does he know about me? About the fire? About who you are?"

And here is the trap I've built, brick by brick, out of love and cowardice in equal measure: if I say *yes, he knows*, Theo will want to know how, and when, and what Elias thinks of him, and whether the whole town is whispering, and the paranoia that prison installed in him will start its engine. If I say *no, he doesn't know*, I'm lying to my brother's face on his second day of freedom, and Theo has had enough people lie to him to last several lifetimes.

"He knows some," I say finally. "Not everything."

Theo nods slowly. He looks back out the window, at Elias's cottage, at the ordinary smoke of an ordinary life next door.

"Some," he repeats. "That's how it starts, June. You know that better than anybody. Some becomes most becomes everything, and then one day somebody's testifying and nobody can take it back." He turns and his voice cracks, just slightly, just enough. "I'm not angry. I just need you to tell me when the ground's about to shift. That's all I've ever needed. Tell me when the ground's about to shift."

"The ground's not shifting," I say.

He looks at me for a long moment, and I know he doesn't believe me, and I know he's decided to believe me anyway, because that's what love looks like when it's been through a fire: you choose to stand on the floor even when you can feel it giving.

* * *

Saturday night I find him on the porch at two in the morning, sitting in the dark with his jacket around his shoulders, watching the cove. I sit down next to him without a word. The tide is out. The stars are doing their indifferent, ancient work.

"Can't sleep inside," he says. "Walls too close."

"I know."

"You don't know," he says, but not unkindly. "You slept outside every night for ten years. Walls were the thing you were hiding from too. Just different walls." He pulls the jacket tighter. "Tell me something good, June. Tell me about the hatchlings. The ones that made it."

So I tell him. About the night in July when two hundred loggerhead hatchlings boiled out of the sand like a small miracle, about the ghost crabs we had to shoo away, about the little ones finding the moon on the water and going for it, every one of them, no hesitation, straight toward the biggest, brightest thing they could see.

"That's the trick," Theo says. "They just go toward the light. Nobody has to talk them into it." He's quiet a moment. "I used to be like that. Before."

"You'll be like that again."

"Maybe." He turns and looks at me in the dark, and his face is half shadow, and he looks suddenly very young, younger than me, the boy who carried me on his shoulders through the orchard, the boy who stood in a courtroom and lied with his whole life to save our family from a truth that would have destroyed us. "June. There's something I need to ask you, and I need you to not do the thing you do. The thing where you go smooth and quiet and agreeable. I need the actual answer."

My heart is already going. "Ask."

"Whatever's happening with the neighbor. Whatever you're not telling me." His voice is steady, which is how I know how much it's costing him. "Is it going to bring any of it back? The fire. The case. Any of it. Because I got out, June, and I don't— I can't—" His breath goes out of him. "I did my time. I did all of it. I just want to sit on a porch and watch the water and not be that anymore. So I'm asking you, brother to brother. Is the ground going to shift?"

And I look at him—my brother, my keeper of the fire, my ten-year debt made flesh—and I say the only thing I have left that's true.

"No," I say. "The ground's not going to shift."

It's the smooth, quiet, agreeable answer. It's the thing he asked me not to do.

And it's a lie, because three days later, I'm going to find the newspaper clipping in his duffel bag—the one about the orchard fire, the one with the Wren family's name in it, the one he's been carrying for ten years like a stone in his shoe—and I'm going to understand that Theo didn't come home to rest.

Theo came home because the ground was already shifting, and he felt it from a hundred miles away.
