## 11. Lantern Practice

The grange hall smelled like sawdust and cinnamon and forty years of the same October.

Elias stood in the doorway with a box of bamboo ribs in his arms and let the noise of it wash over him—the scrape of folding tables being dragged into rows, Odette Marsh's voice cutting through it like a gull through fog, someone's radio playing an old song about harvest moons that everyone seemed to know the words to. In Boston, rooms like this had been boardrooms. They had smelled like carpet cleaner and ambition. Nobody in a boardroom had ever looked up when he walked in and simply said, *There he is. Grab a seat, hon.*

"There he is." Odette spotted him from across the hall, a measuring tape slung around her neck like a stethoscope. "You're late."

"I'm four minutes early."

"You're late for the version of you that showed up on time." She pointed at a long table near the windows where paper and wire frames were stacked in precarious towers. "Lantern station's yours. Juniper's already there. Try not to set anything on fire."

The joke landed before she could soften it, and Elias watched her realize what she'd said—a flicker behind her eyes, quick as a fish turning—and then she was already moving on, barking at two teenagers about banner placement, and the moment closed over like water.

He carried the box to the lantern table anyway.

Juniper had his sleeves rolled to the elbow and a pencil tucked behind his ear, and he was doing something precise and patient to a square of rice paper—creasing it, unfolding it, creasing again along a different axis, his hands moving with the unhurried certainty of a man who had done this every autumn of his life. There was a smudge of flour or chalk on his forearm. His hair was pushed back like he'd run his hand through it a dozen times already this morning.

"Odette drafted me," Elias said, setting down the box.

"She drafts everyone. It's how she loves people." Juniper glanced up, and something in his face did the thing it always did when their eyes met—that half-second hitch, like a tide pulling back before it came in stronger. "You've never folded one of these, have you."

"Is it that obvious?"

"You're holding the box like it might bite you."

Elias sat down across from him. The table between them was scattered with paper squares in autumn colors—amber, rust, a deep red like the inside of an apple. Outside the windows, Saltmere Cove lay flat and silver under a high pale sky, and the town moved through its preparations with the easy choreography of people who had done this together for generations. Somewhere a man was testing a generator. Somewhere children were being organized into a pie-eating contest they would remember for thirty years.

"Show me," Elias said.

Juniper slid a square of amber paper across the table. "Watch first."

And Elias watched. He watched Juniper's fingers find the center of the paper without measuring it, fold a corner to meet some invisible point, press the crease with the side of his thumb in one smooth stroke. He watched him turn the paper, fold again, the shape beginning to suggest itself—not a lantern yet, but the memory of one, the way a seed suggests a tree.

"The trick is you don't fight the paper," Juniper said. "It already knows what it wants to be. You're just reminding it."

"That's very mystical for a fire hazard."

"Everything worth building is a fire hazard. That's what makes it worth building carefully." He turned the half-formed lantern toward Elias. "Your turn."

Elias picked up his own square. Made the first fold. It came out crooked, the crease soft where Juniper's had been sharp, and he felt the old familiar irritation rise—the Wren family heirloom reflex of needing to be immediately excellent at things—and then he felt Juniper's hand come over his.

Not grabbing. Just resting. Adjusting the angle of his wrist by a degree.

"Looser here," Juniper said. "You're strangling it."

"I'm holding it."

"You're strangling it. Paper can tell."

Their hands stayed where they were a beat too long. Elias was aware of it the way you're aware of heat from a stove—not painful, just present, radiating. Around them the hall went on with its noise, and nobody was looking at them, and everybody could have been looking at them, and Elias found that he didn't much care which it was. That was new. That was terrifying. That was the whole point.

He made the second fold. Better. The third better still.

"There," Juniper said quietly, and took his hand back, and the absence of it was louder than the presence had been.

* * *

By noon the table looked like a small orange dawn had broken across it—two dozen finished lanterns in graduated sizes, each one tested against the wire frame, each one signed discreetly on the base with the folder's initials so that Odette could later declare whose craftsmanship held and whose didn't. Tradition, apparently. Everything here was tradition, and the strange thing, Elias thought as he stretched his cramped fingers, was how little the word had ever meant to him until now. His family had used *tradition* the way banks use *security*: as a lock. Here it was a door everyone left open.

A woman named Ruthie brought sandwiches and refused payment. Two kids from the turtle camp—Juniper's volunteers, Elias realized, the ones who spent summers tagging loggerheads—came by to show off a lantern they'd folded crooked on purpose, shaped vaguely like a sea turtle, and Juniper laughed, actually laughed, head tipped back, and Elias sat there holding a half-folded lantern and understood that he was in trouble. The deep kind. The kind you don't walk off.

"You're staring," Juniper said, without looking up.

"I'm admiring the craftsmanship."

"Mm." But his ears had gone pink, and he pressed the next crease harder than he needed to.

Odette appeared at the end of the table with her clipboard, ostensibly checking inventory, actually watching them with the frank, unembarrassed attention of a woman who had run this festival through three recessions and one hurricane and considered human affairs well within her jurisdiction.

"Wren," she said. "Your father sponsored the lantern release eleven years running. Never once folded one himself, far as I know. Sent checks and a photographer."

"He was efficient," Elias said.

"He was absent. There's a difference." She tapped her pen against the clipboard twice. "You'll do the safety briefing this year. Water line, wind check, the works. Town likes to hear it from a Wren. Makes them feel like the family's still—" she paused, choosing the word with surgical care, "—*present*."

After she moved on, Juniper raised an eyebrow at him. "She knows."

"Knows what?"

"Who you are. What your name buys here. She's not recruiting you because you're good with paper, Elias."

"I know." He looked down at the lantern between his hands, the amber skin glowing faintly in the window light, hollow and waiting. "That's the strange part. I don't mind being known here. I minded everywhere else."

Juniper was quiet for a moment. Then: "Maybe because here, being known comes with a lantern instead of a bill."

* * *

They broke for the afternoon and Odette, in a move Elias suspected was neither accidental nor merciful, assigned them jointly to test-fly three prototypes down at the cove—wind conditions, burn time, drift pattern. Purely scientific. Absolutely necessary. Performed by two men walking the tideline with lit paper hearts in their hands while the entire west end of Main Street found reasons to be outdoors.

The first lantern caught a gust and dove into the dune grass; Juniper sprinted after it, stamping out the ember, coming back breathless and grass-stained and grinning, and Elias thought: *I would burn down every fortune I've ever been promised to keep seeing that grin.* The thought arrived whole and unbidden, the way true thoughts do, and he filed it away next to all the other things he wasn't saying yet.

The second flew clean. It rose off Juniper's open palms in a slow spiral, caught the offshore breeze, and drifted out over the cove, a small gold star climbing toward the pale afternoon sky. They stood shoulder to shoulder and watched it go until it was a speck, then nothing.

"My brother and I used to do this," Juniper said.

It was the first time he'd said the word *brother* aloud since the night of the whiskey, and Elias kept his eyes on the horizon, careful as a man crossing ice.

"Yeah?"

"He was better at folding. I was better at launching. We'd put notes inside sometimes. Stupid kid stuff. Wishes." A pause. The tide breathed in and out against the sand. "I don't know if any of them ever came true. We stopped doing it before I'd have known."

"What happened to stopping?"

Juniper didn't answer right away. Down the beach, the third prototype waited in Elias's hands, unlit, patient. Gulls argued over something in the wrack line. When Juniper finally spoke, his voice was light in the way that heavy things are sometimes carried lightly, by people practiced at it.

"You know how it is. You grow up. Things get complicated. You tell yourself you'll pick it back up next year, and then next year, and then ten years go by and you're standing on a beach with a stranger wondering why your chest hurts."

"You're not standing with a stranger."

"No," Juniper said. He turned and looked at Elias then, fully, the sea light in his eyes, and for one suspended moment Elias was certain he was about to say something enormous—about the letters, about the fire, about the boy in the orchard who had handed him a coat in the smoke and changed the shape of a decade. Certain the way you're certain about weather.

Then Juniper smiled, small and real, and said, "No. I suppose I'm not," and reached over and lit the third lantern in Elias's hands.

The heat bloomed against Elias's palms. The paper filled, lifted, tugged upward like something alive, and he let it go—let it rise off his fingers into the wide salt air, and it climbed, wobbling, steadying, joining its sibling somewhere out over the water, and Juniper stood beside him close enough that their shoulders touched, and did not move away, and neither did he.

From the bluff above the cove, Odette Marsh watched them with her arms crossed and her clipboard forgotten under one arm, and her expression was not surprise. It was arithmetic. It was a woman counting something she'd already tallied, and finding the sum both inevitable and expensive.

* * *

They walked back through town as the light went long and honeyed. Shopkeepers were hanging garlands. Someone had chalked a festival countdown on the bakery window: **FOUR DAYS**. Kids chased each other around the maypole frame with more energy than aim. And everywhere, Elias noticed, people greeted Juniper by name, and then greeted *him* by name too—*afternoon, Mr. Wren*, from the hardware man; *you two settling the wind question?* from a woman carrying pumpkins—and each greeting landed on him like a small warm stone dropped into a pocket he hadn't known his coat had.

At the corner where their lanes diverged, Juniper stopped.

"Same time tomorrow?" he asked. "Odette wants two hundred lanterns by Friday and we've got maybe sixty."

"Two hundred. She's insane."

"She's optimistic. Same thing in a town this size." Juniper shifted the crate of supplies in his arms, hesitated, and then—quickly, like a man diving into cold water—said, "There's a thing I keep almost telling you. At the table today. On the beach."

Elias's heart performed a slow, deliberate somersault. "I know," he said. "Me too."

"Oh." Juniper blinked. Something moved through his face—relief, fear, hope, all three fighting for the wheel. "Okay. Good. That's—good."

"Four days till the festival," Elias said. "Maybe we stop almost telling each other things before then."

"Maybe." Juniper's smile was crooked and scared and beautiful. "One thing first, though. Small one. Not the big thing."

"Anything."

"When we fold tomorrow—" Juniper looked down at the crate, at the street, anywhere but at him, ears pink again in the fading light. "Sit on my side of the table. Your folds are better when I can reach you."

He turned and walked up his lane before Elias could answer, which was probably the point, and Elias stood alone in the middle of the golden street with two hundred lanterns' worth of work ahead of him and one sentence repeating in his chest like a tide:

*Your folds are better when I can reach you.*

Behind him, unnoticed by either of them, Odette Marsh finally picked up her clipboard, wrote a single note in her small fierce handwriting, and underlined it twice.
