## 14. Odette Knows

The knock came at seven in the morning, which in Saltmere meant Odette Marsh had been awake for three hours and considered my sleeping in a personal failing.

I opened the door with my hair still wet and found her standing on the step holding a covered dish the size of a hubcap. She was seventy-one years old, five foot two, and she had the posture of a woman who had spent four decades chairing committees that grown men feared.

"You missed the lantern inventory meeting," she said.

"I wasn't aware I'd been assigned to it."

"Everyone's assigned to everything. That's how a festival works." She pushed past me into the cottage without breaking stride, set the dish on my counter, and lifted the foil to reveal cinnamon rolls glazed so thick they gleamed like lacquer. "Eat one. You look like a man who's been skipping breakfast."

"I've been—"

"Folding lanterns with Juniper Crane until midnight. Yes. I know." She said it lightly, the way you'd mention the weather, but her eyes stayed on me while she said it, and there was nothing light in them at all.

My stomach did something complicated. "Small town," I managed.

"Mm." She poured herself coffee from my pot without asking, which told me everything about how this conversation was going to go. She took the chair at my little table—the one with the wobbly leg I kept meaning to fix—and arranged herself as though she owned the lease. "Sit down, Elias."

Not *Mr. Wren*. Not *the new neighbor*. Elias.

I sat.

* * *

Here is what I had believed, for the four months since I'd arrived in Saltmere: that I had been careful. That the name Wren, spoken aloud, would land in this town like a stone into deep water—a ripple, some murmurs, old resentments surfacing—and then sink. My father's company had bankrolled half the festivals on this coast before the empire started eating itself. There were people here old enough to remember when a Wren check could build a boardwalk or bury a lawsuit. I had expected recognition. I had prepared for it.

What I had not prepared for was the possibility that recognition had already happened—that while I was learning tide charts and pretending to be a man between careers, someone had been watching me the way I watched everyone else.

Odette set down her cup with a click.

"August ninth," she said. "You drove through town in a gray Audi with paper plates. You stopped at the gas station on Route 1 and bought a map, even though your phone has GPS, because you wanted an excuse to talk to Denny Kowalski behind the counter. You asked him if the cottages on Larkspur Lane rented year-round."

I didn't say anything. There didn't seem to be anything to say.

"Denny called me that afternoon. He calls me about everything—he's my nephew, twice removed, and he has never once in his life kept information to himself. He said, *Aunt Odette, there's a fella asking after Larkspur Lane, and he's got a face like he's running from something.* And then he described you." She looked at me over the rim of her cup. "I've seen that face before. On magazine covers, at charity galas, in the society pages Camille Wren used to feed to the Boston papers when she was young and hungry. You have your mother's jaw. She was kind to me once, at a benefit in '09, when nobody else at that table would speak to a fishmonger's widow. So I knew exactly who was moving in next door to Juniper Crane."

The name landed between us like a dropped glass.

"And you said nothing," I said finally.

"I said nothing," she agreed. "For four months. Do you want to know why?"

"Yes."

She leaned back in the chair. Outside the window, the morning fog was burning off the cove, and somewhere down the lane a gull was making its idiot argument with the wind. Odette Marsh let the silence stretch until it hurt.

"Because I needed to know what you were," she said. "There are only two kinds of men who come to a town like this under a false name. The kind who are running *from* something, and the kind who are running *toward* it. Toward means a target. A person they want to find, or ruin, or use. And Juniper Crane—" her voice changed, just slightly, a door opening onto a room I hadn't known existed in her, "—that boy has been through enough. Ten years ago his family was destroyed in this town. His brother went to prison. His parents died inside of two years after, one of grief and one of something the doctor called exhaustion and everybody else called a broken heart. And Juniper himself came back here five years ago with nothing but a degree in marine biology and a way of moving through the world like a man trying not to wake a sleeping dog. This town adopted him the way towns do—quietly, sideways, leaving food on porches and not asking questions. We decided, all of us in our own separate ways, that no one was going to dig up what happened to him. Not for gossip. Not for anything."

"So when I moved in next door—"

"When a Wren moved in next door," she corrected, "every alarm I own went off. Because the last time your family intersected with that boy's family, his whole life burned down. Literally. And I will tell you something else, Elias, since we're being honest with each other, which I suspect is a novel experience for you." Her eyes were flint now. "Your family never stopped looking for him."

The room tilted. "What?"

"Investigators. Twice, in the years since the trial. Men in good coats asking polite questions at the hardware store, at the marina, at the church bake sale—did anyone know where the Crane boy had gone, was there any record of him, had he ever said anything about the fire. Polite men with good coats. The second time was three years ago. I served them coffee and lied to them with a smile, and then I made some calls, and they didn't come back." She folded her hands on my table, neat as a verdict. "Wren Industries' legal department, if you're wondering. I checked. It wasn't hard."

I thought of Camille. Of course I thought of Camille. Three weeks ago, across a restaurant table in Boston, she had slid a folder toward me and made her offer—testimony about the orchard fire, about what really happened that night, and the family would restore everything my father had cut off when I walked out. I had told her I'd think about it. I had told Juniper nothing. And underneath both of those silences, I had told myself a story: that my family's interest in the fire was about clearing their name, salvaging reputation, an inheritance dispute dressed up as justice.

But investigators hunting a traumatized man across five states for a decade wasn't reputation management. That was something else. That was a search for a witness—or a scapegoat.

"They weren't looking to help him," I said slowly.

"No," Odette said. "They were looking to close a file. And files get closed one of two ways, honey. Somebody tells the truth, or somebody gets made into the ending the family needs." She let that sit. "Now. Four months ago, a Wren shows up in Saltmere under a fake name and rents the cottage beside the exact man his family's lawyers have been hunting since before you grew facial hair. You'll forgive me for not assuming coincidence."

"It *was* coincidence," I said. "I didn't know Juniper lived here. I picked this town off a map because—"

"Because it was small and far away and nobody would look for you. I know. I believe you, actually, which surprises me more than it should." She studied me with an expression I couldn't read—something between assessment and grief. "I've watched you all autumn. At the committee meetings. At the cove. Folding lanterns with your hands like you were raised in a workshop instead of a boardroom. Watching that boy count turtles like watching him was the best part of your day. And I've decided you're probably telling the truth about why you came here." She paused. "Probably isn't good enough anymore. Something's changed."

My mouth went dry. "What makes you say that?"

"Because you've started lying badly," she said simply. "You used to be smooth. Now you flinch when his name comes up. You left the Harvest Festival meeting early last week and stood in the parking lot for ten minutes staring at your phone like it was a live grenade. And yesterday, Denny saw a black sedan from out of state parked by the marina for two hours, taking photographs of the lane." She leaned forward. "So. Before I decide what to do about you, Elias, you're going to tell me what your family wants. All of it. And don't insult me with a small version."

And God help me—I told her.

* * *

It came out in pieces, the way confessions do. The folder Camille slid across the table. The affidavit they wanted me to sign, swearing I'd seen Theo Crane near the accelerants that night, that the boy who'd pulled me out of the smoke had also set it. The inheritance held out like bait. I told her about the ledgers I'd glimpsed in my father's study as a teenager—the insurance figures that never matched the loss reports—and how I'd spent twenty years not letting myself finish that thought. I even told her about the letters, because Odette already knew too much for me to ration the rest, and because saying it out loud to a stranger felt like the only way to make it real: ten years of unsent envelopes in a box in Juniper's house, every one addressed to me.

Odette listened without interrupting. When I finished, she was quiet for a long moment, looking out at the cove.

"Theo Crane didn't set that fire," she said at last. It wasn't a question.

"No. I don't believe he did. I think—" My voice caught on it, the thing I'd carried since chapter seven of my own life, the newspaper in the attic. "I think my father's company needed the fire to be arson. I think they needed a name on it. And Theo was there, and he was angry, and he was easy."

"And Juniper knows you know."

"He knows pieces. He doesn't know about Camille's offer. He doesn't know about the affidavit." I rubbed my eyes. "He doesn't know I signed a limited version last week to buy time. He doesn't know Camille leaked a fragment anyway, or that his name might end up in a paper beside that fire again because of me."

Odette absorbed all of it. Then she stood, crossed to my window, and looked out at the lane for a while—toward Juniper's cottage, though you couldn't see it from this angle, just the strip of shared fence and the dune grass bending.

"Do you know what I did when your family's investigators came the second time?" she said, without turning around. "After I lied to them, I drove to Juniper's lab and I asked him—casual as anything—whether he'd ever want to move inland. Somewhere new. Fresh start. And he looked at me with those sad careful eyes and said, *Why would I leave? Everything I love is here.*" She turned back to face me. "He meant the turtles, mostly. Maybe the town, a little. But I understood, right then, that I couldn't protect him by moving him. He'd already lost one home to your family. I wasn't going to be the instrument of losing another. So I stayed. I kept watch. I'm an old woman playing sentry against a corporation, and it's absurd, and I did it anyway, because that's what you do for people in a town like this."

"Why are you telling me all this?"

"Because I'm done protecting you from the truth along with him." She came back to the table and stood over me, and up close I could see the fine map of lines around her eyes, the iron in them. "Here's what happens now, Elias. I like you. That's the complication. If I didn't like you, this would be simple—I'd call Juniper tonight and tell him everything, and he'd grieve, and he'd survive, because he's survived worse than you. But I've watched you two for months, and I've seen what you are to each other, and I am too old and too tired to pretend I don't understand what it costs to find one person in this world who actually sees you." Her voice roughened, just barely. "So I'm giving you a choice instead. One choice. And I want you to hear it clearly, because I won't offer it twice."

"I'm listening."

"You can be your father's son." She held up one finger. "You can sign whatever they put in front of you, manage the damage, keep your name clean, walk away from this town with your inheritance intact and your conscience filed under later. And if you choose that, I will tell Juniper everything myself—tonight—so that he hears it from a friend instead of a newspaper. He will hate you. He will survive hating you. He's had practice. But you will have chosen, cleanly, and I will respect the choosing even if I spit at the result."

She raised a second finger.

"Or you can be his neighbor. His—whatever you are to him, and I have eyes, so don't bother defining it. You can be the man who folds lanterns with him and drinks whiskey on his porch and learns his tides. And if you choose that, then you burn your bridges today. You call Camille and you un-sign whatever you signed. You give me everything you know about the ledgers, the investigators, the file they're building—you give it to *me*, because unlike you, I have nothing left for your family to take. And you tell Juniper the whole truth yourself, soon, before the papers do it for you. Not pieces. Not a reasonable version. The whole ugly thing, including the affidavit, including the leak, including the fact that you hid it from him while sharing his bed." She said it flatly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "That's the price of staying. Full exposure. Because a man who loves in pieces leaves pieces behind when he goes, and that boy has been swept up after enough people."

The kitchen was very quiet. Down the lane, the gull had given up its argument. I could hear my own pulse in my ears, and beneath it, faintly, the sound of the sea doing what the sea always does—eroding things, patiently, one honest grain at a time.

"If I choose him," I said, "my family will destroy him anyway. Camille won't stop. The documents she's holding—"

"Then you'd better make sure they can't use them," Odette said. "You told me yourself there are ledgers. Insurance fraud, falsified loss reports, a conviction built on paperwork your father manufactured. Your family spent ten years terrified of what this town's only living witness might say. Which means, sweetheart, that for the first time in your gilded life, *you* are the one holding something they're afraid of." Almost—almost—she smiled. "Power's only cold if you spend it on yourself."

I thought about Juniper's hands folding lantern paper, precise and reverent, like the light mattered. I thought about the box of envelopes with my name on them, ten years of devotion addressed to a ghost. I thought about my father's study, and Camille's smile across the table, and the word *inheritance*, and how small it had sounded lately, like a coin dropped down a well.

"How long do I have?" I asked.

"To tell him?" Odette picked up her empty dish, tucked it under her arm, and headed for the door with the brisk finality of a woman whose work here was done. At the threshold she paused, and for one moment the iron in her face softened into something older and sadder—grace, maybe, the fierce and complicated kind that has to be earned.

"The festival is in nine days," she said. "The lanterns go up over the cove, and the whole town watches, and whatever you two are by then, everyone will see it. So you have until the lanterns, Elias. Tell him before they rise, or don't stay to watch them."

The door clicked shut behind her.

I stood alone in my kitchen with the smell of cinnamon and the taste of ash, and outside, far down the lane, I could see Juniper's silhouette at his fence line, clipboard in hand, counting something only he could see—unaware that the clock on his quiet life had just started running.

Nine days.

Eight, if I counted honestly, starting now.
