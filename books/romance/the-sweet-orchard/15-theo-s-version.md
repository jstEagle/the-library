## 15. Theo's Version

My brother learned to walk on the gravel path between our house and the orchard. I know this because there is a photograph of it—Theo at fourteen months, one fist clenched around our mother's skirt hem, the other reaching toward the apple trees like they were calling his name. Our mother kept that photograph on the mantel for years, and after the fire she kept it in a shoebox, and after the trial she stopped keeping anything at all.

I think about that photograph now because Theo is standing on my porch in the gray light of a Tuesday morning, and he has not yet crossed the threshold. He stands where the gravel used to be, metaphorically speaking, one foot still in the world that convicted him.

"Juniper." He says my name like he's checking whether it still fits.

"Theo." I open the door wider. "Come in."

He does, finally, and my cottage shrinks around him. It's not built for two people's histories. The kitchen table is small; the chairs are small; the life I've assembled here is small by design, and my brother fills every corner of it with the particular largeness of a man who spent six years learning to take up no space at all and then got released into a world that expected him to be normal-sized again.

He looks older than thirty-four. His hair is cropped short in a way that isn't fashion, and his hands have a tremor he keeps trying to disguise by holding things—a mug, a spoon, the edge of the table. When I pour him coffee he wraps both palms around the cup like it might leave if he doesn't.

"You look good," he says. "You look—" He stops. Recalibrates. "You look settled."

"I am."

"That's good." He drinks. Sets the cup down with exaggerated care, the way men do when they're afraid of their own hands. "That's really good, June."

Nobody calls me June except him. The nickname lands somewhere under my ribs and stays there, warm and awful.

We make small talk for twenty minutes. The ferry ride over. The halfway house in Fairhaven. The job program they're making him do—warehouse inventory, three towns away, a bus schedule that owns him now. He tells me about parole conditions the way other people describe weather: flatly, thoroughly, without complaint. Curfew at nine. No leaving the county without written permission. Check-ins every Thursday.

"And you?" he says. "The turtles. You still doing the turtles?"

"Every morning."

"Good. That's good." He nods too many times. Then, because he can't help himself—he was never able to help himself, even as a boy, even before everything—he asks: "Anyone? You seeing anyone?"

And here is the moment. Here is the exact seam in the morning where everything could have gone differently, if I had been braver or stupider or simply honest. I could have said yes. I could have said, his name is Elias Wren, he lives next door, he folds lanterns badly and drinks his whiskey slow and looks at me like I'm a sentence he wants to finish reading. I could have said all of it and let the truth arrive on its own terms instead of being dragged out later, bleeding.

Instead I say, "It's complicated," which is the coward's synonym for *yes*.

Theo goes very still.

I watch it happen—the stillness moving through him like cold water through a pipe. His eyes come up from the coffee cup and fix on mine, and I understand, with the sick clarity of hindsight, that my brother has spent six years becoming an instrument for detecting danger, and I have just struck the tuning fork.

"Complicated," he repeats.

"It's nothing. It's new. It's—"

"What's his name."

"Theo—"

"*What's his name.*"

And because he is my brother, and because some part of me has been waiting ten years for someone to ask me that question with that exact ferocity, I hear myself answer: "Elias."

The word drops between us onto the small table like something heavy falling through ice.

Theo doesn't move. Doesn't blink. But his face does something I've only seen once before—the night of the fire, when the sheriff's deputy read him his rights and Theo looked over the deputy's shoulder at me, sixteen years old and smoke-stained, and made a decision with his whole body that I wasn't allowed to participate in.

"Elias Wren," Theo says. Not a question. A reading. Like he's checking a name off a list he's carried so long the paper's gone soft.

"You know that name," I say, though I already know the answer.

"Everyone from home knows that name." He laughs, and it's the worst sound I've heard in years, worse than the verdict, worse than the door closing on visiting day. "The Wrens. Dad worked their orchard contract for eleven years and they never once shook his hand. And their boy—" He stops. Looks at me. Looks *through* me. "Their boy was there that night. Wasn't he. At the orchard. You told me once—you wrote me, first year inside, you said there was someone who gave you a blanket."

I did write that. One line, buried in a letter full of nothing, because even then I couldn't say it plainly: *Someone was kind to me. I don't know why that matters but it does.*

"He didn't know who I was," I say. "Not then. Not until recently."

"Not until recently," Theo echoes. He sets the coffee down and stands, and the tremor in his hands is gone now, replaced by something steadier and more frightening. He walks to my window—the one facing east, the one where, if you lean, you can see the roofline of the neighboring cottage—and he looks out at Elias Wren's house like a man looking at a fuse.

"June." His voice has dropped into the register he uses when he's about to say something he's rehearsed. "Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"*Sit down.*"

I sit. That's the thing about brothers—you can spend a decade building a life they know nothing about and one word from them puts you back at the kitchen table of your childhood, feet not touching the floor.

Theo turns from the window. What's in his face isn't anger. That's what makes it unbearable. It's pleading.

"You have to end it."

"No."

"You don't even know what I'm going to say yet."

"I know exactly what you're going to say." My own voice comes out harder than I intended, and I realize I'm angry—not at him, or not only at him, but at the speed with which the old architecture has reassembled itself around us, the walls going up brick by brick, me on one side and the truth on the other. "You're going to tell me the Wren family destroyed us. You're going to tell me their investigators never stopped looking. You're going to tell me that if anyone finds out who he is—if anyone connects him to me—it all comes apart. Everything. Your parole. My name. All of it."

Theo sits back down slowly, like the argument has already taken something out of him. "Okay," he says quietly. "So you do know."

"I've always known. That's why I never said his name. To anyone. For ten years, Theo, I wrote letters to him and never sent them because saying his name out loud felt like handing someone a match."

"Then how—"

"Fate. Bad luck. He moved in next door. He didn't know who I was either. We found out the way people find out things—too late and all at once." I drag a hand through my hair. "It's not a scheme, Theo. Nobody's running an angle. He's just—he's a person. He's kind. He's hiding from his family the same way I hid from mine."

"There's no such thing as a Wren who's just a person." Theo says it flatly, without heat, and that's what frightens me—the absence of heat, the way conviction has calcified in him into something geological. Six years in Fairhaven Correctional will do that. Six years of being *the arsonist*, of having one story about yourself and no room to revise it. "Listen to me. Listen." He leans forward, and up close I can see what prison did to his eyes—they're watchful in a way that never fully sleeps. "My appeal lawyer called last week. Before I came out. She said the case file's been flagged. Someone requested documents. Old evidence. The insurance records."

The floor tilts a degree. "Flagged by who?"

"She wouldn't say. But June—nobody requests those files unless they want something. Either someone's finally digging into what really happened"—he lets that hang, and I feel it hook into me, the old hope, the hope I swore I'd killed—"or someone's getting ready to bury it deeper. And either way, the person standing in the middle of it is you. Because you were there. Because you saw what you saw. Because you've spent ten years being conveniently silent, and convenient silences don't stay convenient forever."

"I would testify," I say. "You know I'd testify. I've wanted to—for years I've—"

"*No.*" It comes out of him like a shot, and then he's gripping the table, knuckles white, leaning across the small distance between us. "No. You listen to me now. You don't testify. You don't talk to lawyers. You don't talk to investigators. You don't write any more letters, you don't tell your boyfriend anything, you don't—"

"Why?" My voice cracks on it. "Why not, Theo? If there's a chance to clear your name—"

"Because my name is *all I have.*"

The words land in the kitchen and just sit there, enormous, undeniable.

He breathes. Steadies himself against the table. And then, in a voice gone quiet and terrible, he explains it to me the way you explain something to a child—not because I'm stupid, but because he needs to hear it said aloud, needs the shape of it in the air.

"You think I want the case reopened? You think I lie awake hoping some lawyer digs up the truth?" He shakes his head slowly. "June, I did six years for a fire I didn't set. You know what I learned in there? That the story is the only thing keeping me alive. In Fairhaven, you're somebody or you're nobody, and the only way I was ever *somebody* was as the guy who burned down Crane Orchard. That was my name. That was my crime. Guys respected it. Guards remembered it. It gave me—I don't know—a shape. A container. You take that away, you reopen the case, and what am I? Some middle-aged ex-con whose little brother maybe set the fire when he was a kid, whose family fell apart, who got convicted on bad evidence while the real story stayed buried? That's not exoneration, June. That's just a different cage, and this time everybody gets to watch them build it."

"Theo—"

"And if it comes out that *you* were involved—even accidentally, even as a kid, even if you just ran and hid and let me take it—" His voice breaks, actually breaks, and he presses his lips together until it passes. "They'll come for you. The Wrens' people, the press, everyone. They'll dig up whatever they can find and they'll make you the story. And I will have traded the only shape my life had for watching them dismantle yours. That's not a trade, June. That's just losing twice."

I stare at him. My brother. The boy in the photograph reaching for the apple trees.

And underneath the fear and the fury and the love—all of it tangled past separating—one thought rises in me, cold and clear as cove water:

*He's right.*

That's the horror of it. He's right about the mechanics of it, right about the Wrens, right about what exposure does to people like us. Every word is true. And every true word is a lock clicking shut.

Because here is what I understand, sitting at my own small table in my own carefully small life, listening to my brother beg me to keep the door closed: I have spent ten years believing my silence was protection. Mine, and Theo's. A wall I built out of unsent letters and turtle counts and tide charts, and I called it safety, and I called it loyalty, and I called it love.

But a wall works both ways. It doesn't just keep the world out. It keeps us in.

Ten years, and Theo is still standing in the rubble of a story someone else wrote for him, defending its walls because they're the only walls he has. Ten years, and I am still sixteen, smoke in my lungs, running from a fire I didn't set but couldn't stop, letting my brother carry the weight because carrying it gave him something to hold. We have been locked in together this whole time, my brother and I, two people loving each other through a keyhole, and I called it devotion.

"Elias knows," I say quietly.

Theo's head comes up.

"He knows about the fire. He knows about you. He found the newspaper in his attic weeks ago and he didn't run, Theo. He stayed. He's—" I stop, because the next word is *different*, and I don't know if I believe it anymore, and that uncertainty is its own wound. "His family is the reason we lost everything. But he's not his family. I have to believe that. I *do* believe that."

"You believe it because you want to." Theo's voice is gentle now, which is worse than the shouting. Gentle is what he used when I was sick as a kid, when he'd sit by my bed and tell me the fever would break. "June, I'm not telling you he's a villain. I'm sure he's not. I'm telling you it doesn't matter. Good people drown you same as bad ones when the water's deep enough. His family spent ten years making sure our name meant *arsonist*. You think love fixes ledgers like that?"

I don't answer. Outside the window, gulls are working the tide line, and somewhere beyond the dunes Elias is probably awake, probably making coffee in the kitchen of the cottage I can see from this exact angle, probably not knowing that my brother is sitting four feet away dismantling the future I'd started, cautiously, terribly, to imagine.

Theo reaches across the table and takes my wrist. His grip is careful, the grip of a man who has learned exactly how much force the world allows him.

"Promise me," he says. "Promise me you'll keep quiet. Whatever happens with the files, whoever comes asking—lawyers, reporters, his family, *him*—you keep your mouth shut and you let it pass over. Let it pass over like weather. Promise me, June. It's the only way either of us survives this."

And God help me, because I love him, because he took the blame when we were children and paid for it in years, because his hands are shaking around my wrist and he is begging—

"I promise," I say.

He exhales, long and shuddering, and something in his face unclenches, and he smiles at me for the first time all morning—a real smile, the old one, the one from before—and it should feel like relief.

It feels like turning a key in a lock from the inside.

* * *

He stays another hour. We talk about neutral things—the ferry schedule, the warehouse job, whether the halfway house food is as bad as prison food (worse, apparently, which seems impossible). He compliments the cottage. He asks about Odette Marsh, remembers her from childhood summers, remembers her peach preserves. At the door he hugs me, hard, his chin hooked over my shoulder the way he's done since we were boys, and I hold on longer than I mean to because some animal part of me understands this might be the last uncomplicated hug we get for a long time.

"Thursday check-ins," he says, stepping back. "I'll call after. Same number."

"Same number," I agree.

He walks down the path toward the road, and I stand in the doorway watching him go—my big brother, my keeper, my prisoner, walking away with his shoulders squared like a man leaving a courtroom—and I feel the promise sitting in my chest like swallowed stone.

* * *

I don't go inside. I walk the other direction, down toward the cove, because my legs need to move and my thoughts need the sound of water under them.

Low tide. The flats exposed, ribbed silver and brown, the smell of salt and weed and cold mud. My territory. My church. I walk out along the channel edge where the herons stalk, and I try to pray, or whatever it is I do instead of praying, which is mostly counting.

Counting turtles. Counting tides. Counting years.

But today the counting won't settle. Today every number comes out wrong.

Because here's the arithmetic I can't escape: Theo asked for my silence, and I gave it. And Elias—Elias, who signed something last week he wouldn't show me, Elias who's been pulling away by millimeters, Elias whose sister arrived in town like weather off the ocean—Elias is keeping his own silences. His own promises. His own locks.

Two men I love, each begging me to trust them, each asking me to stay blind.

And the worst part—the part I keep circling like a heron after fish—is that I understand them both. I understand why Theo needs his story. I understand why Elias signs papers and hides phone calls. Everyone I love is doing exactly what I taught them by example: protecting the people they love with pieces and omissions and doors gently closed.

I taught the whole world how to love me small.

I stand on the flats until the cold comes up through my boots, and then I turn back toward the cottages, and that's when I see it.

Elias is standing at the fence line between our properties. He's been there a while—I can tell by the set of his shoulders, the way weight settles when you stop pretending you're passing through. He watched my brother leave. He watched me walk the tideline. He's watching me now.

And in his hand, held slightly away from his body like it's something that might detonate, is an envelope.

My handwriting is on it.

Even at forty yards, even across the gray morning, I know my own address block the way I know my own pulse. One of the letters. One of the hundred and twelve letters I wrote to him over ten years and never sent—which means either he's been in my cottage, or someone gave it to him, or someone *left* it for him to find, and every one of those possibilities arrives in my chest at once like birds hitting glass.

Elias doesn't wave. Doesn't call out. He just stands there at the fence, holding my decade in his hand, waiting for me to come to him.

Behind me, the tide has turned. I can feel it starting to come in, patient and unstoppable, filling the channels I walked through on my way out.

There is no low-tide route home anymore.
