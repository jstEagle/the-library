## 16. The Affidavit

The affidavit arrived the way all of Camille's weapons did: beautifully, and by courier.

Elias signed for it at his own door at seven in the morning, still in yesterday's shirt, Juniper's laugh still caught somewhere in his throat from the night before. The envelope was heavy cream stock, embossed with the Wren crest—a tree, ironically, an orchard tree, its branches pruned into obedience. Inside, three pages of precise legal language and a Post-it note in Camille's handwriting.

*Read it carefully, little brother. It's kinder than what's coming.*

He read it standing in his doorway while the tide went out below him and Saltmere woke up around him—gulls arguing over the harbor, Odette Marsh's truck rattling past with a load of festival lumber, someone two streets over already frying something that smelled like forgiveness.

The document was a limited affidavit. That was the phrase they'd chosen, *limited*, as if restraint were a virtue here. Elias would confirm only what he had witnessed as a child on the night of the Crane orchard fire: that he had been present on Wren property adjacent to the orchard; that he had seen figures near the equipment shed; that he could not identify them. Nothing about his father. Nothing about the ledgers. Nothing about the insurance claim that had been filed eleven days after the fire for equipment that, according to every invoice Theo Crane's lawyers ever produced, had already been sold.

Nothing that would help Juniper. Nothing that would hurt the family. A scalpel shaped like a compromise.

And in exchange: the affidavit would be filed quietly, sealed, used only to demonstrate good faith while "the parties worked toward a comprehensive resolution." His inheritance would be restored in stages. His name would come off the internal watch lists. Camille would stand down.

*Stand down.* As if she were an army. As if he hadn't watched her sharpen herself against their father's approval for thirty years.

He should have burned it. He knew that even then, standing there with gull noise in his ears. He should have carried it down to the cove and fed it to Juniper's wood stove, page by page, and told him everything—the offer, the threat, the documents Camille claimed to have suggesting a child named Juniper Crane had set the fire himself. He should have done the brave thing, the whole thing, the thing Juniper deserved after ten years of letters written to a man who never answered because answering meant admitting he'd read them.

Instead, Elias sat down at his kitchen table with a cup of coffee going cold beside him and did the arithmetic that powerful families teach their children before they teach them to ride bikes.

If he refused, Camille leaked everything—true or fabricated, it didn't matter which, because scandal doesn't check its sources—and Juniper's name went into the paper next to the word *arsonist* before lunch. If he signed, he bought time. Days, maybe weeks. Time enough to find the ledgers himself, time enough to get ahead of her, time enough to build some kind of wall between Juniper and the blast radius of the Wren name.

That was the story he told himself. He could hear how it sounded even inside his own skull—reasonable, measured, protective. His father's voice wearing his own clothes.

He thought about Juniper's hands folding lantern paper, the way he smoothed each crease twice, like apology was a physical act. He thought about the letters. Ten years of them, addressed to a boy who had stood in the smoke and said, *I won't tell anyone I saw you,* and then grown into a man who kept every secret except his own.

Juniper had spent a decade not being seen. And Elias was about to sign a document that made him a piece on a board.

He signed it anyway.

The pen was one of Camille's, left in the envelope deliberately, heavy brass, the kind of object that made a signature feel like a transaction. His hand didn't shake. That was the worst part. Some part of him had been trained for this since childhood—sign here, smile here, swallow here—and the training held even as everything else in him leaned toward ruin.

He sealed the return envelope. He set it on the hall table. He walked out onto the deck and looked at the neighboring cottage, where a light was on and a silhouette moved behind frosted glass, and he felt the exact weight of what he'd done settle onto his shoulders like a coat cut for a smaller man.

Then he went inside, took the lantern-folding supplies out of the closet, and got to work, because work was the only anesthesia he'd ever trusted.

* * *

For six days, it almost worked.

Six days of the ordinary miracle he'd built in Saltmere without noticing: mornings at the fence line with coffee passed over the slats, afternoons of festival logistics in Odette's cluttered office, evenings teaching his hands what Juniper's hands already knew about wire frames and rice paper and the particular patience of a man who counts turtles for a living. Six days of Juniper laughing—actually laughing, unguarded, the sound of a lock opening—and Elias carrying the affidavit around inside himself like a swallowed stone.

Odette noticed first, because Odette noticed everything.

"Your sister called the harbormaster," she said on day four, not looking up from the permit ledger. "Checking ferry manifests. Asking who visits you." She turned a page. "In my town."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked up then, and her eyes were the gray of weather coming. "Boy, I've buried two husbands and outlasted three developers. I know exactly what a family like yours does when it loses its grip on one of its own. It doesn't grieve. It audits."

"She gave me terms. I met them."

"You met them halfway," Odette said. "Families like hers don't do halfway. They do *all the way now* or *all the way later*, and later is always worse." She snapped the ledger shut. "Whatever you signed, whatever you're carrying—you tell that boy across the fence before she does. That's not advice. That's the last free thing I'll say to you."

He meant to. God knows he meant to. He had the whole speech composed by day five, standing on Juniper's porch with two cups of cider and his heart hammering like a fist on a door: *I have to tell you something, and it's going to sound like betrayal, and it is, and I'm sorry, and here is everything, all of it, no pieces.*

But on day five, Theo came to the cottage.

Elias opened his door to find a thin man with prison-pale skin and Juniper's same gray eyes standing on his step, looking at him with the flat, exhausted suspicion of someone who has learned that every stranger is either a threat or a disappointment. They spoke for four minutes. It was the longest four minutes of Elias's adult life. Theo asked careful questions about who he was and where he'd come from, and Elias answered with the truth minus the center—new neighbor, helping with the festival, keeping to himself—and watched Theo file him away under *unresolved*.

Afterward, through the window, Elias watched the brothers on Juniper's porch, heads close, Theo's hands wrapped around a mug like the mug might be taken from him. He saw Juniper lie to his brother—saw the small flinch of it, the way Juniper's eyes came up and away—and understood that lies were contagious now, that his silence was infecting everyone it touched.

On day six, he drafted a text to Juniper: *Tomorrow. I'll tell you everything tomorrow. There's something I signed and something I should have burned and you deserve both truths at once.*

He never sent it. At 11:40 that night, his phone lit up with a message from a number he didn't know, and the world tilted.

It was a photograph of the front page of the *Saltmere Sentinel*—the little weekly that came out Thursdays, that Odette underwrote, that usually ran pieces about the chowder cook-off and the school levy. Someone had gotten to it early. Someone had gotten to it thoroughly.

The headline read: **CRANE NAME SURFACES IN DECADE-OLD ORCHARD FIRE — NEW TESTIMONY EXPECTED TO REOPEN CASE.**

Below it, a fragment of the affidavit, quoted verbatim, stripped of every protective clause Camille had so generously included. Just the bones, arranged to bleed:

*"Witness states he observed unidentified figures near the equipment shed on the night of the fire."*

And beneath that, in the third paragraph, the sentence Camille had clearly saved for last, the knife slipped in where the eye least expects it:

*"Sources close to the Wren family confirm that Juniper Crane, brother of convicted arsonist Theodore Crane, currently resides in Saltmere under an assumed quiet life."*

Elias read it four times. The words didn't change. That was the horror of print—it just sat there, patient, doing its damage forever.

His phone rang. Camille.

"You said sealed," he said, before she could speak. "You said good faith. You said—"

"I said what I said, and our attorneys said what they said, and then our father said what he said." Her voice was smooth as varnish. "There was a leak, Elias. These things happen. Fragments get misfiled. You know how chaotic paperwork can be in a family our size."

"You leaked it."

"I accelerated a timeline." A pause, delicate as a blade laid on silk. "Look at the bright side. The piece doesn't accuse Juniper of anything. It merely reminds people he exists, and whose brother he is, and where he sleeps. Reputation is mostly geography, little brother. We simply updated his coordinates."

"Take it back. Retract it. I'll give you whatever—"

"You'll give me nothing more, because you have nothing more, which is why we settled for your little affidavit in the first place." He heard her exhale, almost fond. "Here's what happens now. The case-reopening motion files Monday. Your testimony becomes public record. And when the Cranes' attorney comes asking who provided the new witness, the answer will be: the son of Aldous Wren, sworn and signed. You did this, Elias. Not me. I just printed what you wrote."

She hung up.

Elias stood in his dark kitchen holding a phone that had gone hot in his hand, and outside, across the strip of grass and the fence line, Juniper's cottage was dark too—but he could see, faintly, the blue pulse of a screen in the front window. Juniper was awake. Juniper was reading.

Everyone in Saltmere read the *Sentinel* on Thursday nights. Odette. The harbormaster. The woman who sold Juniper his coffee. Every person who had spent two months learning to say his name like it belonged here.

Elias grabbed his jacket. He was across the grass in twenty seconds, and he was raising his hand to knock on the door of the one man he had sworn—sworn on a stack of unsent letters, sworn in the dark, sworn in the sweet ordinary days—to protect, when the door opened first.

Juniper stood in the frame with his phone in one hand and the other hanging loose at his side, and his face was not angry. That was the terrible thing. It wasn't anger. It was recognition—the look of a man finding an old wound exactly where he'd left it, in the same body, in the same shape, opened by a different hand.

"The figures near the shed," Juniper said quietly. "Unidentified. Present on Wren property." He recited it like scripture, like something he'd memorized in the last hour and would carry for the rest of his life. "That's you. That's your signature, isn't it? In some lawyer's filing cabinet, right now."

"Juniper—"

"Ten years ago, everyone who traded pieces of my family told themselves they were being reasonable." His voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. Quieter was how Juniper bled. "My father's partners. The prosecutors. The journalists. Everyone was reasonable, Elias. Everyone had obligations. Everyone was protecting someone." He looked down at the phone, then back up, and his eyes were dry and bottomless. "Did you sign it?"

The question hung there, and Elias—who had lied to fences and festivals and brothers, who had lied so long the truth had rusted shut inside him—found that this door wouldn't open to anything but yes.

"Yes," he said. "But if you let me explain—"

Juniper stepped back. Not far. Just far enough that the doorway stood between them, a border drawn in old pine.

"No," he said. "I don't think I will."

And he closed the door—softly, gently, the way you close a door on a room where someone has died—with a click so small it echoed for the rest of the night.

Elias stood alone on the porch. Down the hill, the cove breathed in and out, indifferent. Above him, through the window he could no longer see into, a light went off.

Somewhere in the dark between the two cottages, ten years of letters lay in a box neither of them would open again—not yet, not tonight. Tomorrow the motion would file. Monday the world would arrive with its cameras and its questions. And Elias Wren, who had once promised a smoke-blackened boy he would tell no one what he saw, had finally kept a version of that promise: he had told everyone, in ink, in duplicate, under seal.

He walked home slowly. Behind him, in the window of the dark cottage, a single lamp flicked back on—burning, stubborn, awake.

Juniper wasn't sleeping either. And in the morning, Elias knew, there would be a reckoning at the cove—one or both of them would not survive unchanged.
