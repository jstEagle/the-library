## 6. The Festival of Small Lights

The storm broke in the night, and Paloma woke to a silence so complete it felt like a held breath finally released.

She lay in her old bed beneath the eaves of the lighthouse keeper's cottage, listening to the drip of melting snow off the gutters, the soft collapse of ice-laden branches in her grandfather's garden. Through the frost-ferned window, the world had been remade — white and blinding, the harbor frozen into a sheet of hammered pewter, the sky rinsed clean to a blue so sharp it looked cut from glass. Somewhere below, Ezra was moving around her kitchen. She could hear the particular rhythm of him: cabinet opened, kettle filled, the small clink of her grandmother's blue willow mugs. He moved like a man trying not to be noticed, which was, she thought, exactly how he'd left her twenty years ago. Quietly. With his coat already on.

She got up before he could make himself coffee and escape into the morning.

* * *

By nine, Marrow Bay had begun to dig itself out.

Paloma walked the length of Harbor Street with Ezra beside her, both of them wrapped in scarves, their breath pluming white. The town had emerged from its three-day burial with the practiced efficiency of people who had done this every winter of their lives. Men with snowblowers carved tunnels through drifts taller than themselves. Children sledded down the churchyard hill on trash can lids, shrieking. And everywhere — everywhere — were lights.

They hung from the fishing shacks and the bait shop awnings, from the rigging of the boats frozen in their slips: paper lanterns, jars of candles, strings of colored bulbs gone soft with age. The Festival of Small Lights was four days away, and Marrow Bay was dressing for it the way a woman dresses for a wedding — slowly, lovingly, with an eye toward memory.

"Your grandfather used to string these," Ezra said, stopping beneath the archway of the chandlery, where a rope of tiny amber bulbs swayed in the wind off the water. "He told me they were never about brightness."

Paloma's chest tightened. "He told you that?"

"He said—" Ezra turned his collar up against the wind, and for a moment he looked so much like the boy he'd been at twenty-four that she had to look away. "He said the point wasn't to see far. It was to be seen. One light, lit honestly, is worth more than a floodlight nobody believes."

She remembered that week now in fragments she had spent two years turning into prose: Ezra on the gallery deck of the lighthouse with her grandfather, the two of them arguing happily about Fresnel lenses and dead languages, while Paloma sat on the stairs pretending not to listen, falling in love with the sound of it. Her grandfather had adored him. That was the wound under all the other wounds — when Ezra vanished, he hadn't just abandoned her. He'd abandoned an old man who had already lost too many people to the sea.

"You remember a lot," she said carefully, "for someone who claims he came here only to research oyster farming."

"I'm a novelist. I lie professionally." But he said it without his usual armor of irony, and his eyes stayed on the little amber lights as if they might say something truer than he could.

Down the street, her grandmother stood on a ladder outside the cottage, directing two neighbors in the hanging of the great wreath — a wheel of cedar and holly as wide as a cart tire, studded with white candles. Agnes Oyelaran was seventy-eight years old and climbed ladders the way other women crossed rooms. When she saw them coming, she did not climb down. She simply waited, wreath above her head like a halo she had earned through sheer stubbornness.

"There you are," she called. "Both of you. Good. I need hands."

"Grandmother, you're going to break your neck—"

"I'm going to finish this wreath, which is more than I can say for some people's conversations." She descended with dignity, brushed snow from her sleeves, and fixed Paloma with the look that had governed the family for three generations. "The beacon committee met last night. By telephone, since half of us are buried. They've voted."

Paloma felt it coming the way you feel weather change behind your ribs. "Grandmother."

"They want the old light lit this year. For the festival. For him." Her voice didn't waver; Agnes Oyelaran's voice had not wavered since 1974. "It hasn't turned since your grandfather died, and everyone agrees it's time. The lamp room needs cleaning, the mechanism needs oiling, and the lens — God help us — the lens needs a woman who knows how her grandfather kept it. Which is you, Paloma. Whether you like it or not."

"I don't—"

"You do like it. You simply don't want to feel it yet." Her grandmother turned then, deliberately, to Ezra. "And you. You'll be staying through the festival, of course."

It was not a question, but it landed like one, and Paloma watched Ezra take the full weight of it. The wind pulled at his scarf. Behind him, the harbor glittered, indifferent and beautiful.

"I haven't decided my schedule," he said.

"No," Agnes agreed pleasantly. "You never have. That was always your trouble." And she handed him the wreath, turned, and went inside, leaving the two of them standing in the snow with a hundred pounds of cedar between them like a hostage situation.

* * *

They carried the wreath back to the lighthouse together, taking turns, neither speaking much. It was heavy enough that by the time they hung it on the great iron bracket by the tower door, Paloma's arms burned and her heart was burning worse.

Because she had heard it. *I haven't decided my schedule.* Four days ago he had stood in her kitchen dripping storm water and fury, and something had passed between them in the dark when the generator died — something real, something she had been foolish enough to believe in by morning. And now, offered the simplest invitation, he had reached for the word *schedule* like a man grabbing a railing over open water.

That evening she found him in the lamp room.

She had gone up to face the lens herself — to prove to her grandmother, or to herself, that she could stand in that circular room without weeping — and there he was, sleeves rolled, wiping salt crust from the brass housing with an old rag he'd found, moving with the shy competence of a man who had once known this room intimately and was afraid to presume upon it.

"This isn't your job," she said from the stairwell.

"Neither is yours." He didn't stop working. "But here we are."

The lens rose above them, seven feet of hand-ground crystal, holding the last of the daylight in its facets like a jar of trapped honey. Her grandfather had polished it every Thursday for fifty years. She put her hand against the cold glass and felt the whole weight of the room settle onto her: the dust sheets, the smell of oil and old stone, the memory of her grandfather's voice saying *a light doesn't judge what it guides home, Paloma. It just burns.*

"Ezra." She made herself say it plainly, no blade in it, no armor either. "I'm going to ask you something, and I need you not to perform an answer."

He set down the rag. Turned. In the dimming light his face was careful, and she hated how well she knew that care — the exact architecture of a man preparing exits.

"Stay through the festival," she said. "Not because of the book. Not because of the storm or the roads or whatever excuse is currently loading behind your eyes. Stay because my grandmother asked, and because the town will hang four hundred lights on Saturday night, and because I am asking you — me, Paloma, who has every reason to let you walk back down that hill and never think of you again except as research." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "I'm asking you to stay, and I want to watch your face while you decide, because twenty years ago I got the decision without the face."

The silence stretched. Below them, Marrow Bay was lighting itself piece by piece — a candle in a window here, a lantern on a porch rail there — the town rehearsing its festival the way it always did, early and imperfect, because light was not a thing you saved up. It was a thing you spent.

"I don't know how to be here," Ezra said finally, quietly. "I know how to arrive. I know how to leave. The middle part—"

"The middle part is the entire book, Ezra. Yours and mine."

He laughed — a short, wounded sound — and looked at the lens instead of at her. "Your grandfather made me promise something once. The last night." His jaw worked. "I've never told anyone what it was."

The floor seemed to tilt beneath her. "Then tell me now."

But he shook his head, slow, miserable, and picked up the rag again as though polishing brass could save him. "Not yet. If I tell you now, you'll understand too much too fast, and I'll get to be forgiven without having earned it. Let me stay first. Let me earn it."

Paloma stood very still, her hand on the cold crystal, hope rising in her like water in a lock — dangerous, controlled, one gate from flooding everything.

"That," she said, "is the first honest thing you've said since you got off the ferry."

"It won't be the last." He met her eyes at last. "I'll stay through the festival. All of it. Even the part where your grandmother makes me judge the chowder contest."

"She will absolutely make you judge the chowder contest."

"I know. I'm staying anyway."

They worked side by side until the dark came down, wiping the great lens clean, and when Paloma finally switched on the work lamp, the crystal caught it and threw it outward in a slow golden fan across the snow — one small light, lit honestly, reaching all the way to the harbor road.

She did not see the figure standing at the foot of the tower drive, looking up at them. She did not see the phone pressed to his ear, or hear the words Ezra's agent was saying into it, low and urgent, about film deals and real stories and the price of silence.

But the light saw him. And the light, as her grandfather liked to say, does not judge what it guides home.

It only burns.
