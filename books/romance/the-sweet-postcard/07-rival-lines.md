## 7. Rival Lines

The storm broke overnight, and Marrow Bay woke up glittering, as if the town had been dipped in sugar and left to dry. Ezra stood at the lighthouse kitchen window with a mug of coffee going cold in his hands, watching workmen string lanterns along the harbor rail for the Festival of Small Lights. The snow had buried everything and then revealed everything — every fence post wearing a white cap, every boat a sleeping animal under a tarp. Somewhere down the lane, children were arguing about whether the ice on the pond would hold.

Behind him, Paloma was humming.

She didn't know she was doing it. He'd caught her at it twice already this morning — once over the kettle, once while she scratched something into the margin of her notebook, that quick private tune, three notes rising like gulls. Twenty years ago she'd hummed it too, in a rented room in Lisbon with salt-stiff hair and a paperback face-down on her chest, and he had thought: *I will hear this sound for the rest of my life.* Then he'd spent the rest of his life making sure he never heard it again.

Now it filled her grandfather's kitchen, and his phone was buzzing against the table like something trapped under glass.

He let it ring out. It started again.

"Ignore it," Paloma said without looking up. "That's what I do with mine. The whole town's been calling since the storm. Nadia wants to know if I'm alive. My agent wants to know if I'm *productive*. There's no answer that satisfies either of them."

"It might be my agent."

"Then definitely ignore it." She glanced up, and there was the ghost of their old game in her eyes — the one where they dared each other toward recklessness. "You're allowed one day where nobody gets anything from you. I'm declaring it. Storm law."

The phone buzzed a third time. Something in the rhythm of it — insistent, mechanical, indifferent to storm law — made his stomach tighten. He picked it up.

"Ezra." Dana's voice came through crisp as a paper cut. "Finally. Do you know how many people are trying to reach you?"

"The roads were closed."

"The roads have been open since six. Listen, I don't have time for atmosphere. Are you sitting down? Actually, doesn't matter. Sit down anyway." A breath, and he could picture her in her Brooklyn office, standing at the window even though she always told clients to sit. "The film deal is real. Streamer money, real money, the kind of number that changes what your obituary says. But there's a catch, and you're not going to love it."

He turned slightly, so his back was to the kitchen. Paloma had gone back to her notebook, pen moving, humming.

"They want the story behind the story," Dana said. "The book's been out eight months and nobody's cracked who the male lead actually is. There are threads. Long ones. Some very motivated person online has narrowed it to three men, and two of them are dead. You do the math. The studio wants confirmation. They want *you*, Ezra. They want the press cycle — 'the novelist who lived it.' First-look rights, a producing credit, your name above the title of the adaptation. All you have to do is say the words."

"What words?"

"*It was me.*" She said it gently, which was worse. "One interview. One well-placed profile. You confirm you're the source, you frame it as mutual history, you get ahead of it before some journalist does it for you. Because Ezra — listen to me — someone else is going to figure it out. And when they do, they won't write 'mutual history.' They'll write 'the man she loved and lost,' and they'll dig through everything, and they'll find whatever they find. Wouldn't you rather control that narrative than be ambushed by it?"

He looked at the back of Paloma's head. Her dark hair was pinned up carelessly with a pencil, a strand loose against her neck. She was writing something, and her shoulders were relaxed, and she was humming.

"Dana," he said quietly. "I need to think."

"You've had twenty years to think. You have until Friday. The studio announces their slate Monday morning, and if there's no confirmation, they go with option B — the anonymous-source angle, 'based on a true story known only to the author,' and let the internet do what the internet does. Which, by the way, will be worse for *her* than for you. At least if you claim it, you can shape it. If you stay silent, you leave her alone in the floodlight." A pause, and then, softer, almost kind: "I know how that sounds. I'm sorry. But you should know exactly what staying silent costs."

She hung up. Ezra stood with the phone against his ear long after the line went dead, listening to the ocean inside it.

* * *

He told himself he went upstairs to write.

The guest room Paloma had given him still smelled faintly of cedar and disuse. His notebook lay where he'd left it, open to a page of research notes about lighthouse keepers' rotations, useless now, or useless for the book he'd claimed he came here to write. He sat on the bed with his back against the cold wall and stared at the blank page opposite, and the two sentences fought each other in his chest like animals in a sack.

*I need to tell you why I left.*

*They want the words. Say the words.*

Because here was the arithmetic Dana hadn't spelled out but he'd done himself, lying awake in this room while the generator coughed and the wind screamed: if he claimed the story publicly, he controlled it. If he stayed silent, the internet would find him anyway — someone would match the dates, the geography, the detail of the Lisbon rain — and Paloma would wake up to a headline she never consented to, dissected by strangers, her grandfather's lighthouse photographed by drones. Claiming it was protection. That was the lie he could dress up in decent clothes and wear to breakfast.

But claiming it was also theft. It was walking into her town, her festival, her family's light, and saying *mine* — taking the one thing she'd made from their wreckage and putting his name on the salvage. She had written the truth because he never gave her one. That was the debt. And Dana was asking him to pay it with a press release.

He opened the notebook to a fresh page. He told himself: *a confession.* He would draft it first, get the shape of it, find the words that wouldn't make him sound like a man arriving twenty years late with his hat in his hand. He wrote the date. He wrote *Paloma* — just her name, the letters familiar as his own signature.

Then his hand betrayed him, the way hands do when the mind has already decided and only pretends otherwise.

*Statement regarding THE SWEET POSTCARD,* he wrote. *For immediate release.*

He stared at it. The words looked wrong, clinical, like a stranger's handwriting in his own notebook. He kept going anyway, because the terrible thing about survival is that it always knows the exits.

*While the novel is a work of fiction, I can confirm that certain elements draw upon a period the author and I both—*

Both what? Shared? Endured? Survived? He crossed it out. Started again.

*Certain elements reflect experiences common to—*

Common. As if Lisbon had been a conference. As if her mouth had been weather. He pressed the pen so hard the nib nearly tore the page, and beneath the crossed-out lines he wrote, smaller:

*The author and I were close, once. I can neither confirm nor deny—*

Neither confirm nor deny. The coward's compass, pointing everywhere and nowhere. He could hear how it would read in the profile Dana would place — *Voss, characteristically evasive* — and how it would land in Paloma's ears, which were far more important: *he's selling tickets to our ruin, and he won't even buy one himself.*

Outside, the workmen had finished the lantern strings. From the window he could see them testing the harbor lights, one by one, small gold stars coming awake along the black water. Friday was two days away. The festival was Saturday night. Her grandmother had asked her — he'd heard it through the kitchen door yesterday — to light the old beacon, the one her grandfather had kept, the one that hadn't burned since the funeral. The whole town would be on the seawall. The whole town would be watching her.

And somewhere in that crowd, if he did nothing, would be a journalist with a tip, or a stranger with a search bar, and the floodlight Dana promised would come on whether he stood in it or not.

He looked down at the page. The draft statement sat there like a confession to the wrong crime. Below it, half-formed, the other sentence still waited — *I need to tell you why I left* — patient as a lighthouse itself, burning whether anyone looked or not.

There was a version of Friday where he sent Dana the statement and watched his name climb the trending lists, watched the film deal close, watched his career — the thin, stubborn, starving thing he'd fed for twenty years on scraps and spite — finally eat. There was a version where he deleted the page and said nothing and gambled her privacy on the mercy of the internet, which had none.

And there was a third version. He couldn't see its face yet. He only knew it required him to walk downstairs and open his mouth and say the true thing, the whole true thing — the manuscript, the promise, the dying man's hand around his wrist — and that the saying of it might cost him everything, including her, including this kitchen, including the sound of her humming through the floorboards, which was the only music his life had ever managed to keep.

His phone buzzed. An email this time. Dana, subject line: *Draft? Need by EOD tomorrow.*

Below it, a second notification. A Google alert he'd set up years ago and forgotten, pinging now with cruel timing: *Paloma Oyelaran — new mention.* He tapped it before he could stop himself.

A literary gossip column. The headline was innocuous — festival coverage, a photo of the lighthouse dusted in snow. But the third paragraph stopped his heart mid-beat:

*"Sources close to the author suggest the inspiration for the novel's elusive hero may be closer than fans think — possibly even resident in Marrow Bay this very week."*

Someone was already digging. Someone was already close.

Downstairs, faintly, Paloma was still humming.

Ezra closed the notebook on the ruined statement, and on the sentence underneath it, and made himself a promise with no guarantee attached: tomorrow, before the festival, before Dana's deadline, before the floodlight found her — he would tell her everything.

If he could make himself do it. If the words would come. If twenty years of silence would finally break the way the storm had broken — all at once, leaving the ground glittering and changed.

He took the stairs slowly, rehearsing the first sentence the whole way down, and found the kitchen empty, her coat gone from its hook, and a note on the table in her slanted, unrepentant hand:

*Gone to help Nonna with the beacon. Come find me if you're brave enough. — P.*
