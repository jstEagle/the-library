## 3. The Off-Season Regulars

The trouble with a small island in the off-season is that there is nowhere to hide, and everyone knows exactly where you'd hide if you could.

I learned this on my third morning at the inn, when I came down to the kitchen at seven to find three strangers already installed around Wren's scarred oak table like they held shares in it. A woman in oil-stained coveralls was eating eggs directly from the pan. An ancient man sat by the window with a disassembled alarm clock laid out on a tea towel before him, its organs gleaming like something surgically removed. And a girl of maybe fifteen was reading over my aunt's shoulder, offering commentary on the crossword.

"Hazel," Wren said, with the breezy guiltlessness of a woman who has been planning an ambush for days, "these are your housemates, more or less. Marisol—you've seen her yelling at the ferry. Theo—he's been here longer than the building, as far as anyone can tell. And that's Pip."

"Pip's short for Philippa," said the girl, without looking up from the crossword, "but everyone shortens it because Pip is funnier. Seven across is 'cello part,' by the way. Four letters."

"Oboe," said Theo, not looking up either.

"It's a cello joke, Theo."

"Every joke is a cello joke if you're brave enough."

Marisol pointed her fork at me. "You're the nephew. The musician." She said *musician* the way other people say *plumber*—a trade, a fact, no reverence attached. I would come to understand this was the highest compliment the island paid anyone.

"Cello," I confirmed.

"Huh. Thought you'd be taller."

"I get that a lot."

"You don't get it a lot," Pip said, finally deigning to look at me. She had the frank, unblinking stare of someone who had not yet learned to be embarrassed about curiosity. "You're exactly cello-player height. I looked it up. Average is five-nine and you're—"

"Pip," said Wren.

"—anyway." She went back to the crossword. "Welcome to the island. It gets worse in November."

I want to say I kept my distance after that. I want to report that I maintained the careful perimeter I'd spent a year constructing—the polite nods, the early breakfasts eaten alone, the strategic retreats to my room whenever conversation threatened to include me. That was the plan. The plan was airtight. The plan had load-bearing walls.

The plan did not account for Marisol fixing the inn's ancient boiler unprompted, then standing in my doorway with grease on her hands saying, "You're up. You're awake anyway. Hold this light."

It did not account for Theo, who occupied the parlor every afternoon with his clocks and who never once asked what I did for a living or why I'd stopped doing it, but who did say, on my fourth afternoon of hovering uselessly nearby, "That spring's wound too tight. Hand me the tweezers." And when I handed them to him: "Everything that keeps time needs winding down eventually. Even you look like you're ticking too fast."

It did not account for Pip, who materialized wherever I was with the inevitability of weather, asking questions about rosin and strings and whether I'd ever played Carnegie Hall, and who absorbed my answers—"no," "yes," "once, badly"—with the undimmed delight of someone collecting trading cards.

None of them asked the question. That was the strange mercy of it. On the mainland, everyone wanted to know: *what happened?* Here, nobody asked what happened. They just handed me things to hold.

* * *

The letters started arriving on Tuesdays.

This was the pen-pal program, which I should explain. Wren signed me up for it during week one, while I was unpacking—or rather, while I was staring into a box containing my entire life in sheet music and pretending to unpack. "It's for isolated residents," she said, sliding the form across the counter. "Winter's long here. People write to people. You write to whoever they assign you, anonymously, first names only. It's very civilized."

"Wren, I'm fine."

"You alphabetized the spice rack twice."

"That's called being tidy."

"The oregano is between the nutmeg and the paprika, Hazel. Nobody does that on purpose. Nobody does that *twice* on purpose."

So I filled out the form, mostly to make her stop looking at me like that, and forgot about it entirely until a cream-colored envelope appeared under my door with my name written in handwriting so aggressive it nearly tore the paper.

The first letter read, in full:

*Dear H —*

*I've read your introductory letter four times now. You wrote 340 words and managed to say absolutely nothing. That's genuinely impressive. You described the weather ("mild"), your work ("creative fields"), and your disposition ("generally optimistic") — all three of which are lies, and one of which is a crime against correspondence.*

*Rules, since you clearly need them: One. No weather reports. Two. If you use the phrase "creative fields" again I will report you to the program administrators for fraud. Three. Write something true. Anything. Start small. I'll know if you cheat.*

*— J*

I read it standing in my room with the wind doing its usual business against the shutters, and I felt something I hadn't felt in a year, which was *seen*, and immediately after that, something else, which was *annoyed*, because being seen turned out to feel a lot like being caught.

I told myself I wouldn't answer. Obviously I answered. I stayed up past midnight writing back, and somewhere around the second page my careful prose fell apart and I said things about the year I'd had that I have never said out loud—not to Wren, not to the doctors, not to the mirror. I didn't name any of it precisely. But I said *this year took something from me and I've been pretending I gave it up voluntarily,* and I said *you're right, the optimism was a lie, the truth is I don't know what I am if I'm not the thing I was,* and then I sat there horrified, holding pages that were essentially a confession addressed to a stranger whose only credentials were cruelty and good penmanship.

I mailed it anyway. Tuesday morning, before I could reconsider.

J's reply came the following Tuesday, and began: *There you are.*

* * *

By the third week, the household had noticed.

It was Marisol who caught me first. I'd made the mistake of smiling at my letter over breakfast—an actual smile, unguarded, the kind I thought I'd retired—and she set down her coffee with the slow deliberation of a predator.

"Well," she said.

"Well what."

"You smiled at the mail."

"I did not smile at the mail."

"He smiled at the mail," she announced to the room, to Theo, to Pip, to Wren behind the counter, to the general historical record. "At a piece of paper. With his whole face."

"It's the pen-pal program," Wren offered, insufferably serene. "Hazel has a correspondent."

"A correspondent," Theo repeated, savoring it. He adjusted a gear the size of a lentil with his tweezers. "In my day we called that a sweetheart."

"In your day you also called the telephone 'the devil's wire,' Theo."

"And I was right about that too."

Pip leaned across the table, chin in hands, studying me like a specimen. "Is it a woman?"

"I don't know."

"You know. Everyone always knows. There's a vibe."

"There is no vibe."

"There's a *vibe.*" She turned to the others. "He's got a vibe. Look at him. He checked the mail twice before eight o'clock and it's Sunday. Mail doesn't even come Sunday."

"That proves nothing," I said, having indeed checked the mail twice before eight.

Marisol grinned and went back to her eggs. "First Tuesday of winter, he smiles at paper. By spring he'll be writing poetry. They all do."

"They?" I said.

"Ferry mechanics see everything," she said. "People ride the boat to say goodbye and ride it back to say hello, and I drive the boat both ways. You learn to read faces at the rail. Yours has been doing something new since the letters started." She tapped her temple. "I keep records."

Theo closed the clock face with a soft click, satisfied, and said, to no one in particular, "A man who writes letters is a man who intends to stay."

"I intend to leave," I said. "Eventually. In spring."

Nobody argued with me, which was somehow worse than arguing. Wren refilled my coffee without being asked. Pip slid the crossword toward me and tapped seven across. "Cello part," she said. "Four letters. Help me out, professional."

"*Oboe*," Theo said again.

And I picked up the pencil, because refusing would have required energy I preferred to spend elsewhere, and filled it in, and Pip watched me do it with an expression of such naked triumph that I understood, with a small internal lurch, that I had just been adopted and there was no appeals process.

* * *

Here is what I noticed, in the weeks that followed, though I tried very hard not to notice it:

My hands stopped shaking when I was peeling potatoes next to Marisol while she explained, at length, why the ferry engine was "temperamental, not broken, there's a difference, and everyone treats her like she's broken and she *knows*."

They were steady when Theo let me hold a mainspring once, coiled like a sleeping thing, and said, "Careful. It wants to unwind all at once. Everything does. The art is letting it go slow."

They were steady when Pip dragged a folding chair up beside mine on the porch and asked me to explain the difference between a concerto and a sonata, and I explained, and she said, "Which one are you best at?" and I said, "Concerto," and she said, "Cool. Play me one sometime," with the terrifying casualness of a person who does not know she is standing on a landmine, and I said, "Sure, someday," and she beamed like I'd promised her the moon, and I sat there afterward feeling the lie in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the thing about these people: they had built their warmth around me without checking whether I deserved it. They assumed I was a cellist between engagements. They assumed the case in my room held a working instrument and a working future. Pip had asked to see it once—I'd said it needed restringing—and she'd nodded and never pushed, because that's the kind of person she was, and every day she didn't push was another day I owed her a truth I couldn't pay.

The plan, remember, was containment. Keep the heart boxed, keep the hands quiet, keep the story simple: *a year off, some rest, back to normal by spring.* But plans assume the environment stays neutral, and this island refused. This island kept handing me coffee and crosswords and clock parts and questions, kept learning my laugh before I'd authorized it, kept treating me like a fixture instead of a ruin. Warmth, I discovered, doesn't ask permission. It just quietly moves in and starts rearranging the furniture, and by the time you notice, you live there.

On a Thursday evening in late October, I found myself in the kitchen teaching Pip how to read bass clef on a napkin, while Marisol argued with Theo about whether the ferry horn could be tuned to a B-flat ("everything can be tuned to a B-flat, Theo, that's what tuning *is*"), while Wren hummed along to a radio station that came in fuzzy from the mainland, and I realized I was humming too, and that I had not hummed in fourteen months.

I stopped. Just for a second. Just long enough to feel it.

Then I kept going, because Pip was watching me with her pencil poised over the napkin, waiting, and because stopping would have required explaining, and because—for the first time in a year—explaining felt possible. Not easy. Possible. There's a difference, and the difference is everything.

* * *

The fifth letter from J arrived on a Tuesday, as scheduled, and was longer than the others, and stranger.

*Dear H —*

*You asked what ruined my career, and I lied to you, which I think makes us even, since you've been lying to me since letter one. So here's a true thing instead: I destroyed something important because I was loyal to the wrong person, and I told myself the loyalty was the point, and it took me years to admit the loyalty was just fear wearing a nicer coat.*

*You don't have to answer that. Most people don't. Most people send back comfort, and comfort is fine, comfort is a warm bath, but I've noticed you don't do comfort. You do honesty with the serial numbers filed off. So here's my actual question, the one I've been building to:*

*When you lost the thing you were — and you did lose it, don't bother denying it, I can hear the shape of it in everything you write — did you miss the thing itself, or did you miss who you were while doing it?*

*Answer carefully. I'll know if you cheat.*

*— J*

I read it four times. Then I walked down to the dock, because the dock is where this island sends you when you need to think, and stood there in the cold watching the water do its patient gray work, and I realized two things.

The first was that I was going to answer honestly, because J had asked the exact question I'd been avoiding in the dark for a year, and because some part of me—some part I hadn't consulted—trusted this sharp-tongued stranger with the worst of me.

The second was that the ferry was coming in, late, its horn sounding wrong—a half-step flat, Marisol was going to be livid—and standing at the rail, arms crossed against the wind, wool coat, dark hair whipping loose, was a woman I knew.

I knew her the way you know a scar. Conservatory practice rooms, competition finals, a decade of sharing stages and hating each other beautifully. Juniper Vale. The last person on earth I expected, and the last person I could bear—

She stepped onto the dock. She saw me. Her face did something complicated and fast, and mine, I'm sure, did the same thing back.

"Hazel," she said.

"Juniper," I said.

And behind me, faintly, from the direction of the inn, I could hear Pip shouting something about seven across, and the flat ferry horn moaning its wrong note, and the whole warm ridiculous machinery of the life I'd accidentally built grinding on without me—while the past, wearing a wool coat, walked up the pier to meet me.
