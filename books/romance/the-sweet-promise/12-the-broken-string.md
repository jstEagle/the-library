## 12. The Broken String

The violin arrived on my worktable the way most disasters do: casually, with an apology attached.

"Would you mind?" Juniper had said, setting the case down between us in Theo's workshop like she was handing me a sleeping infant she didn't entirely trust me with. "There's a buzz on the D string, up around fifth position. It's driving me insane. You have better ears than I do."

"I absolutely do not," I said.

"You absolutely do. You used to hear wrong notes in pieces that hadn't been written yet." She was already shrugging into her coat, already halfway to the door, already performing the particular casualness of someone who has just handed over something precious and needs to not watch. "Theo says you're good with your hands. When you're not using them to be insufferable."

"I'm insufferable with or without the hands. It's a package deal."

"Fix my violin, Yarrow." And she was gone, out into the gray November morning, leaving the case sitting there like a dare.

I should mention what the last few weeks had been, so you understand the crime scene properly. Since the night on the pier—the almost, the interruption, the retreat—we had settled into something I didn't have a name for and refused to look at directly, the way you refuse to look directly at the sun or at your own reflection in a shop window when you're crying. We worked on the pavilion. We argued about acoustics. She drank Wren's terrible coffee and insulted it with genuine affection. And twice—twice—our hands had touched over the same plank of wood, and neither of us had pulled away fast enough, and the air between us had gone thick and humming, like a string tuned just past the point of safety.

I had been, against every policy I had established for myself, almost happy.

Remember that. It matters for what comes next.

* * *

Theo's workshop smelled of oil and old wood and the faint metallic sweetness of clock springs. He'd given me a corner of his bench—"You'll ruin your eyes otherwise, boy, hunched over that table in your room like a monk"—and I'd been using it to do small repairs for the inn and, lately, to avoid thinking, which is what my hands did instead of playing. Sanding. Gluing. Tuning. Small, obedient tasks for small, obedient hands.

I opened Juniper's case.

Her violin was a Vuillaume copy, a good one, the varnish gone amber at the shoulder where her jaw had pressed against it for twenty years. I knew this instrument. That's the thing people forget about rivals—we know each other's instruments the way we know each other's faces. I'd heard this violin across audition rooms and orchestra pits for a decade. I'd hated its sound with real devotion, the way you hate anything that beautiful coming from someone who isn't you.

I turned it over under the lamp.

The buzz was easy to find—open seam near the lower bout, a hairline gap drinking the D string's vibration. Fixable in an afternoon. But I'm thorough when I'm avoiding things, so I kept looking. Checked the bridge. Checked the post. Checked the neck joint, the fingerboard, the pegs.

Then I saw the repair mark.

Inside the instrument, visible through the f-hole with the lamp angled just so: a small patch, old, expertly done, with a restorer's mark burned into the wood. Three letters and a number. *K.V. 7.*

The lamp hummed. The clock on Theo's wall ticked. Somewhere in my chest, a door I had welded shut a year ago quietly came off its hinges.

You need to understand what K.V. 7 means to me. You need to understand what I did the week after my hands froze on that stage, when the reviews were calling me *a musician who abandoned his audience* and the conservatory was requesting statements and my agent was not returning calls. I took my cello apart. Not in grief—in investigation. Because a cellist knows his instrument the way a sailor knows his ship, and mine had *changed*. The string heights had drifted. The fingerboard had a warp that hadn't been there at rehearsal. The soundpost sat a millimeter off true, enough to make everything fight me, enough to make the impossible passage—the one I'd played clean a hundred times—feel like wrestling something alive.

I'd found the marks then, too. Inside the ribs. *K.V. 7.* A repair workshop in the city, the kind that serves orchestras and conservatories, the kind with a waiting list and a reputation and, I'd assumed, airtight discretion. I'd taken my instrument in for a routine adjustment six weeks before the concert. Six weeks. And somewhere between their bench and my hands, something had been done—or undone—so subtly that only a player at my level, under performance pressure, would feel it as catastrophe rather than inconvenience.

I'd never proven it. That was the cruelty of it. I'd stood in front of the inquiry with my theory and my marks and my shaking hands, and they'd looked at me with the particular pity reserved for musicians who blame their instruments, and then Juniper Vale had taken the stand in her beautiful gray coat and said, in that clear, carrying voice, that she'd watched me rehearse that week and my intonation had already been failing. That the instrument was fine. That Hazel Yarrow's hands had simply—failed.

Her testimony hadn't broken me. It had *sealed* me. Closed the lid on the box everyone wanted closed, because the alternative—that someone had sabotaged a rising cellist six weeks before his debut concerto—was a scandal with no bottom to it.

I had spent a year building my ruin into a load-bearing wall. *She lied. She buried me. She chose the tidy story over the truth.* And then she'd walked off a ferry into my aunt's town with a wool coat and a restoration contract, and I had spent weeks watching that story erode, grain by grain, under the weight of her laughing at Wren's coffee and knowing my musical phrases before I finished them.

And now here was her violin. Bearing the mark of the workshop that had broken mine.

* * *

I sat with it for a long time. The clock ticked. Theo came in at some point, looked at my face, and left again without a word, which is the most eloquent thing Theo has ever done.

Here's what the suspicious mind does, and mine is a champion. It builds. *She was his student too*—my mentor, Aldous Wren-no-relation, the great pedagogue whose studio had launched both of us, whose favor I had spent my conservatory years failing to fully earn. *Juniper adored him. Juniper owed him. Juniper was in that workshop's orbit—she used the same luthier, everyone in his circle did.* And a violin carries its repairs for decades; the mark could be ten years old. But the suspicious mind doesn't care about decades. The suspicious mind takes a single fact and builds a cathedral on it, and then moves in, and locks the door.

*She testified my hands failed. She knew what had been done. She protected him. And now she's here, in this town, in my aunt's inn, letting me—*

Letting me what? Almost kiss her on a pier? Hand her my phrases like flowers?

I closed the case. I sat there until my tea went cold.

The worst part—the part I keep turning over even now—is that I fixed the seam first. I'm not proud of what I did next, but I want the record to show that I did the work before I did the damage. I reglued the open joint, clamped it, let it set. I adjusted her soundpost with the tenderness I'd have given my own instrument, because my hands are honest even when the rest of me isn't. The buzz was gone. The violin sang again, that dark, particular voice I'd spent a decade pretending not to love.

And then I wrote the letter.

* * *

Not to Juniper. To J.

Because that's the thing about a person split down the middle—you can be cold to one half while the other half is still warm, and tell yourself you're being careful rather than cruel.

I wrote it at my desk that night, with the storm lamp on and the sea making its opinions known below the windows. And I could feel myself doing it—feel the warmth draining out of my prose, feel the gates coming up, the old architecture of self-protection reassembling itself plank by plank. I've been told my letters to J had gotten loose. Open. That I'd written things in them I couldn't say aloud to a living face. Well. This one had its buttons done up to the throat.

*J—*

*I've been thinking about loyalty. About what it costs, and who pays it, and whether the person it's paid to ever deserves the receipt.*

*You told me once you made a mistake out of loyalty to someone undeserving. I've thought about that a great deal lately. I used to think it was brave of you to admit it. Now I wonder if it was just a way of carrying the thing without setting it down. A confession is not the same as a repair. You can tell someone you broke their window every day for a year and the draft still gets in.*

*Something happened here this week. Something that reminded me that people are not always who the letters make them seem. That a person can hand you their truest self in one hand and hold the knife in the other, and mean neither, and mean both.*

*I'm not writing this to accuse you of anything. I don't know enough to accuse you of anything. That's the trouble with knowing someone only on paper—you know their heart, but not their hands.*

*Write back or don't. I find I don't trust my own reading lately. I look at a thing and see what I'm afraid of.*

*—H*

I read it over. It was cold. It was the coldest thing I'd written in months, and every warm sentence we'd built—the jokes, the confessions, the dangerous intimacy of it—stood outside that letter like guests turned away at the door.

And then, because I am a coward with excellent penmanship, I did the thing I've apparently been doing all along: I wrote a second letter and didn't send it. This one started *J, tell me it wasn't you* and got as far as *because I have begun to* before I stopped, folded it, and put it in the drawer.

The drawer. Wren doesn't know about the drawer. The drawer is where my unsent letters live, all the drafts of the man I can't quite afford to be, and it is getting full, and someday someone is going to open it, and I am going to have to have a conversation I have been rehearsing against my will for weeks.

I put the first letter in the outgoing tray by the inn's front door, where Marisol collects the mail for the morning boat. I put Juniper's violin case by the same door, repaired and silent, its secret mark hidden inside where only someone looking for betrayal would ever find it.

* * *

She came for it the next morning, early, before the fog burned off. I heard the door and stayed in the kitchen on purpose, holding a cup of coffee I had no intention of drinking, listening to Wren chat with her, listening to that voice I had spent a decade cataloguing as *rival* and three weeks failing to reclassify.

"Hazel?" Wren called. "Your colleague's asking after her violin."

I went out. Juniper stood in the entryway with the case open, playing a slow scale, testing the D string up through fifth position, and the buzz was gone and the sound filled the little inn the way sound does when a room loves it, and she looked up at me with an expression I recognized because I'd been wearing it in private for weeks—unguarded, pleased, a little undone by the fact of being pleased.

"It's perfect," she said. "What did you do?"

"Glued a seam. Adjusted the post." My voice came out level. I'd rehearsed it. "You should have it looked at properly when you're back in the city. These older instruments collect repairs. Not all of them are honest work."

Something crossed her face. Small, fast, gone—like a fish turning under the surface. I told myself I'd imagined it. I told myself a lot of things that morning.

"Thank you," she said, and she meant it, I could hear that she meant it, and that was somehow the worst part—that the warmth in her voice was real, or sounded real, or was such a flawless forgery that twenty years of knowing her couldn't tell the difference.

She left. The fog was burning off the harbor. The morning boat came in, and Marisol brought up the mail, and there was no letter from J, and I stood in the entryway holding my cold coffee and understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only after you've done the stupid thing, that I had just answered a question I hadn't asked, accused a person I hadn't confronted, and frozen out the one correspondence that had kept me alive this winter—over a mark inside a violin that might be older than our rivalry.

Or might not.

That's the thing about a broken string, I thought, climbing the stairs to my room. You can replace it in an hour. It's the instrument you have to worry about—whether the damage goes all the way through the wood, whether the repair will hold, whether what you're hearing is the true voice or just the buzz gone quiet for a while.

In the drawer, the unsent letter waited with all the others. *Tell me it wasn't you. Because I have begun to—*

Begun to what? I hadn't let myself finish it. I still haven't.

But that night, as I lay listening to the sea, I heard—faint, from the direction of the pavilion, or maybe just the wind in it—someone playing. A violin, alone, in the dark, working through the opening bars of a concerto I knew better than my own heartbeat.

Mine. The one I never finished.

And I lay there with my heart going like a struck string, wondering what, exactly, a woman with nothing to hide was doing playing my ghost in an empty pavilion at midnight.
