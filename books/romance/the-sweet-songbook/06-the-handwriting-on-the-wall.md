## 6. The Handwriting on the Wall

**Poppy**

The rain had stopped by Saturday morning, and Poppy Kavanagh was bored in the specific, bone-deep way of an eleven-year-old whose mother was doing inventory and whose phone was dead.

Theo's cottage door was open. Not unlocked—open, propped with a brick, because he was up on a ladder clearing the gutter and had called down to her, "Let yourself in if you want lemonade, there's some in the fridge." This was the kind of thing Theo said now, like they were people who let themselves into each other's houses. Poppy approved. Theo had good lemonade and better bookshelves.

She got her lemonade. She drank it standing at his kitchen window watching him wrestle with the gutter like it owed him money. Then, because boredom is a kind of hunger, she started looking through the shelf nearest the window—the one with the paperbacks stacked two rows deep, spines out, the way nobody organized anything.

That was when she saw the box.

It wasn't hidden. It was just *there*, on the bottom shelf, a shoebox with MARCHETTI printed on the side in faded store letters, the lid soft with age. Poppy was not a snoop. She wanted that on the record. But the box was open an inch, and inside she could see envelopes, dozens of them, rubber-banded in bundles, and one envelope had come loose and slid forward so its corner caught the light.

The handwriting on it was slanted. Loopy. A little dramatic on the capital letters.

Poppy knew that handwriting the way you know your own front door. It was on every grocery list her mother ever wrote. It was on the sign in the shop window: CLOSED FOR INVENTORY, BACK AT TWO. It was on ten years of letters that lived in a wooden archive box under the register at the Sweet Songbook, addressed to Freya from someone named only *A.*

Poppy had read exactly one of those letters, three years ago, snooping in a way she absolutely would cop to: a page about how grief doesn't shrink, you just grow around it. Her mother had read it aloud once, actually—no. Her mother had never read them aloud. Poppy had stolen that one herself, felt terrible, put it back. But she remembered the words. You don't get over the big losses. You build your life around them like a tree grows around a fence post.

She set down her lemonade very carefully, the way you do when your hands have suddenly become unreliable, and pulled the loose envelope free.

*Dear F,* it began. *You asked me once why I keep writing to a stranger. Here's the true answer...*

The handwriting looped. Slanted. Dramatic capitals.

Outside, Theo swore softly at the gutter.

Poppy sat down on the floor of a near-stranger's cottage with her mother's handwriting shaking in her hands, and understood, all at once and completely, what she was holding.

* * *

**Freya**

Inventory day was Freya's favorite kind of tedium—dust, ledgers, the small satisfaction of numbers agreeing with each other. She was halfway through the poetry section when the bell over the door jangled hard enough to mean business.

Poppy came in like weather. Cheeks flushed, hair wild from running, clutching something against her chest.

"Mom." She was breathing hard. "Mom, I need to show you something, and I need you to not be mad, and I need you to sit down."

"Those are contradictory instructions," Freya said, but she was already setting down the ledger, because Poppy's face had gone past scared into something else—something urgent and almost tender, the way she looked when she'd broken something valuable and decided honesty was worth the consequences.

"I wasn't snooping. Okay, I was a little snooping, but he *said* I could get lemonade, and the box was just *there*—" She thrust the envelope forward. "Look at it. Just look."

Freya looked.

Her own handwriting looked back at her.

No. Not hers. But its twin—the same slant, the same looped descenders, the same slightly theatrical capitals she'd been teased about since school. Ten years of letters lived in her muscle memory; she could have identified this script blindfolded, by touch, in the dark. And here it was on an envelope in her daughter's hands, postmarked eleven years ago, addressed in ink gone brown at the edges.

"Where," Freya said, and her voice came out strange, "did you get this?"

"Theo's cottage. There's a whole box of them." Poppy's eyes were enormous. "Mom. Mom, I think—I think he's A. I think Theo is A."

The shop went very quiet. The rain-fresh smell of the morning came through the door Poppy had left swinging. Somewhere down the block a dog barked twice and gave up.

Ten years of letters. A stranger who had kept her upright through the worst year of her life, who had known when to write and when to stop, who had said *you build your life around it like a tree grows around a fence post* and meant it. And for five months that stranger had lived twenty feet away, had carried flood-damaged books, had let Poppy sleep on his shoulder, had drunk her coffee and argued about Neruda—and had known. Had recognized her handwriting on a returned parcel, obviously, instantly, the way she would have recognized his. Had said nothing. Had let her walk around half-seen while he held the other half in a shoebox.

"Okay," Freya heard herself say. Her voice was calm. She'd trained it to be. "Thank you for telling me. Go wash up, we'll have lunch in a bit."

"Mom—"

"*Go,* sweetheart."

Poppy went, shooting looks over her shoulder like a small anxious lighthouse.

Freya stood alone among the poetry with an eleven-year-old letter in her hand and her heart doing something complicated and structural, like a building deciding whether to hold or come down.

* * *

**Theo**

He knew something was wrong before he was through the door, because Freya was sitting at the reading table with her hands flat on the wood, and Freya's hands were never flat. They moved when she talked. They flew. Stillness on Freya Kavanagh was a storm warning.

"Poppy came home with an envelope," she said.

Theo's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. He had imagined this conversation maybe forty times since April—on the ladder, in the vineyard rows, at 3 a.m. staring at the ceiling of the cottage. In none of those versions did it start this quietly.

"She found the box," he said. Not a question.

"She found the box." Freya turned the envelope over in her fingers. His first letter to her, from eleven years ago, back when he'd still signed *A.* because even then he hadn't trusted himself to be a whole person to anyone. "I want you to tell me it isn't what it looks like."

"It's what it looks like." He crossed the room slowly, like approaching a bird. "Freya—"

"Sit down, Theo."

He sat.

For a moment she just looked at him, and he let himself look back, because whatever came next, he wanted the last honest thing between them to be real. Then she laughed—a short, disbelieving sound with no humor in it at all.

"Do you know what I did, the week you moved in? I told Poppy our new neighbor seemed nice. I *watered his plants.* I lent him *The Tempest.*" Her voice stayed level; that was the terrifying part. He'd have preferred shouting. "And the whole time you knew. You stood in my shop and you knew my handwriting and you knew *mine*, and you said nothing. For months, Theo. Months."

"Yes."

"Why?" It cracked finally, the level voice, right down the middle. "Was it a joke? Was I entertainment? Some—some project for the country boy playing bookseller?"

"No." He leaned forward. "God, no. Freya. I recognized your writing on that returned parcel in the second week, and I couldn't breathe for an hour. Because I knew what I'd find if I looked—that the woman next door was the woman who saved my life on paper, and that I'd have to decide whether to tell her, and either choice felt like a lie."

"So you picked the lie."

"I picked *time.*" His own voice was rough now. "I told myself I'd earn the truth. That I'd be a decent neighbor first, a friend first, that when I told you it would land on solid ground instead of—of shock. And then every week made it harder, because the longer I waited the bigger the silence got, until it was this wall between us that I'd built with my own hands, and I hated it, and I still couldn't make myself climb it. Because the letters were the only place I've ever been fully honest, and I was afraid that if you knew—if you looked at me and saw ten years of your most private nights—you'd realize you'd shown me everything and I'd shown you nothing. That you'd feel robbed."

"I *do* feel robbed." She was crying now, angrily, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like the tears were an administrative error. "Do you know what those letters are? That's the year Daniel left. That's me with a baby and a failing shop and no one—*no one*—and the only person who knew all of it, the only person I was brave enough to be honest with, was you. And you've been eating dinner at my table knowing it. Knowing all of it. Letting me be grateful to a ghost."

"I know."

"You *knew*—"

"I know what I took from you." He said it steadily, because it was the only steady thing left. "I'm not going to defend it. I'm going to tell you the truth, which is that I was a coward, and that being a coward is the one thing I promised myself I'd never be again after the bankruptcy, and I did it anyway, because losing you terrified me more than lying to you. That's the whole ugly confession. There's no version where I come out clean."

The clock over the register ticked. Outside, the town went on being a town—cars, voices, a delivery truck backing up somewhere with its cheerful idiot beep.

"The harvest festival," Freya said at last, very quietly. "The developers. Your family. All of it—you've known about the loan on this building too, haven't you. Who holds it."

Theo didn't answer, which was an answer.

Freya closed her eyes. When she opened them, something had been carefully, deliberately put away behind them—the exact motion he'd watched her make a hundred times without understanding, the containment she'd once described to him in a letter as *the heart is trainable.*

"I think you should go," she said.

"Freya—"

"Please." Her voice didn't break. That was the worst part. "I can't do this today. Maybe not tomorrow either. But I can't look at you right now and not count the months."

He stood. At the door he turned, and everything in him wanted to say the thing from the unsent letter sitting in his desk drawer at home—*every word was true, I have loved you since the first paragraph*—but he had spent five months choosing silence, and he refused to spend one more minute of it pretending silence was protection.

"I'm not sorry the letters happened," he said. "Only that I made you find out this way. I'll fix it. Whatever it costs."

The bell over the door gave its small bright jingle, absurdly gentle, as he walked out into the sun.

Behind him, in the quiet shop, Freya sat down at the reading table and opened the archive box under the register, and began, letter by letter, to reread ten years of her own life—looking, this time, for the man inside the margins.
