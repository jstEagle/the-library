## 9. Harvest Eve

The folding tables had been set out in the square since noon, and by four o'clock they were dressed in white cloths that the wind kept trying to steal. Freya had claimed the one nearest the bandstand—the farthest from the lawyer's table, though she'd told herself the choice was about shade.

The lawyer's table sat under a canvas awning by the fountain, and on it, squared and clipped, was a folder the color of dried blood. *Sale of Freehold Premises: 14–20 Wren Lane.* Her building. Her shop. Her name already printed in the signature line, waiting for her hand like an empty chair.

She'd worn the green dress. Not for them—for herself. If she was going to sign away the Sweet Songbook, she would do it standing straight, in the dress Poppy said made her look like a woman in a novel. She had not decided, in ten years of training her heart, whether that was armor or surrender. She supposed she'd know by nightfall.

The square smelled of woodsmoke and crushed grapes. The Marchetti harvest festival had always been the town's last bright thing before winter—lanterns strung between the plane trees, the first pressing of the season poured free in paper cups, children running underfoot with sticky hands. Freya had come every year since Poppy was born. She had never once come with anyone's hand in hers.

"Mom." Poppy materialized at her elbow, hair braided by Rosa Delgado from the bakery, cheeks already wine-stained from sneaking grape must. "The lawyer guy keeps looking at you."

"Lawyers look at everyone. It's how they bill."

"He looks at you like a hawk looks at a mouse."

"Poppy."

"Sorry. Like a hawk looks at a *very dignified* mouse."

Freya laughed despite herself, and the laugh cracked something loose in her chest. Ten days. Ten days since she'd stood in Theo Marchetti's cottage and seen her own handwriting staring back at her from a letter she'd written when Poppy was four years old. Ten days of not answering his knock, of letting his borrowed books pile on her porch unread, of teaching her heart the one lesson it had never wanted: that some things, once opened, could not be quietly re-shelved.

He was here somewhere. She'd felt him since she arrived, the way you feel weather coming—a pressure behind the ribs. She refused to look for him. She had a folder to sign and a life to rebuild on the other side of the signature, and Theo Marchetti had had ten months of knowing who she was while she'd had ten days of knowing who he was, and the arithmetic of that was not in his favor.

"Ms. Kavanagh?" The lawyer—Pemberton, city shoes ruined on cobblestones, a man who smiled like a contract—appeared at her shoulder with two paper cups of the new pressing. "Before the evening gets away from us. Whenever you're ready."

"I'm ready."

"You're sure? The investors have been generous. Given the market."

Given the market. Given the loan her bank had sold without her consent to men who owned vineyards and, apparently, her future. Given that Theo had stood in his own kitchen and said, *I didn't know about the loan until August,* and she had believed him and hated herself for believing him.

"I'm sure," she said, and picked up her cup so her hands would have something to do that wasn't shaking.

She did not sign. Not yet. The band was tuning, and there was a ritual to it—Pemberton had said the papers would be witnessed at the lighting of the lanterns, eight o'clock, a little theater for the town's benefit, *so everyone sees the investment is welcome.* She had an hour. She would spend it drinking wine she couldn't taste and watching Poppy dance with the Delgado girls, and she would not look for Theo Marchetti.

She looked for Theo Marchetti.

* * *

He was at the far end of the square, at the vineyard's tasting table, and he was not pouring wine. He was arguing—quietly, the way he did everything, shoulders set, jaw tight—with a man in a city coat she recognized from photographs in the *Gazette*: Marcus Marchetti, the brother. The one with the good career and the bad timing.

She watched them the way you watch a fire you're not sure is contained. Marcus handed Theo a document. Theo looked at it, then set it down on the table, face up, and walked away from it. Marcus said something to his back. Theo didn't turn around.

Then Theo looked up—across the whole square, over the heads, the lanterns not yet lit—and found her, and the look on his face was not the look of a man who had won anything.

Her heart did the thing it had been trained not to do.

* * *

At seven-forty, the town began to arrive.

Not drift in. *Arrive.* Rosa Delgado first, flour still on her forearms, then the Okafor twins from the hardware store, then old Mrs. Brandt who hadn't left her house since her hip, then the whole back pew of St. Brigid's, then people Freya had sold books to for fifteen years and never known their names until they were saying them now, saying *Freya, Freya, we're here, we heard, we came.*

They came carrying a folder.

It was blue. It was thick. It was held in the two hands of Devi Okafor, who planted herself in front of Pemberton's table like a woman planting a flag and said, loudly enough for the whole square, "We'd like to make a counteroffer on the Wren Lane properties. Before anyone signs anything."

Pemberton's smile flickered. "I'm afraid the sale is—"

"Pending," Devi said. "Pending signature. Which hasn't happened." She opened the folder on the white cloth, and Freya saw pages of signatures—hundreds, it looked like, columns of them, the whole town's handwriting jumbled together in a document that was not elegant but was unmistakably, ferociously alive. "The Wren Lane Cooperative. Forty-one households. The bookshop, the bakery, the tailor's, the corner flats. We're offering the investors their price. Not a penny less, not a penny more. We've got a credit union behind us and a lawyer of our own, and she's coming up from the city on Monday."

Freya's hand was at her mouth. "Devi. Where did—how did you—"

Devi looked at her, and something in the look was gentle and sorry and amused all at once. "Ask your neighbor," she said.

* * *

She found him at the edge of the lantern-light, half in shadow by the bandstand, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The band had started—something slow, something with a fiddle in it—and the whole square seemed to be holding its breath between notes.

"You organized this," she said.

"I made some calls." He was wearing the same shirt from the cottage, ten days ago, a lifetime ago. His hands were stained to the wrist with grape must. "Devi did the rest. And Rosa. And about two hundred other people who love that street more than any investor ever could."

"Their price. Theo, that's—it's everything the co-op has. Some of them mortgaged—"

"I know what it cost." His voice was rough. "I matched it."

She went still. "What do you mean, you matched it."

"My inheritance. My share of the vineyard. It's pledged against the co-op's loan. If the investors won't take the deal, they can come for my stake instead." He said it flatly, like a man reading a shipping manifest, and she understood suddenly that he had rehearsed it that way so it would sound small. "Marcus gave me until midnight to tear it up and sign the buyout and come home. I told him no about an hour ago. He's driving back tonight."

"You'd lose everything. The vineyard, the career, the—Theo, you *rebuilt* yourself. You told me that. In the letters. That the wine trade was the thing that put you back together after—"

"I know what I told you." He stepped closer, into the light, and she saw he was crying without any fuss about it, the way some men do, as if the tears were simply weather. "I told you in letters because letters were the only place I was brave. I've been brave on paper for ten years, Freya. I'm trying to learn how to do it standing up. It's going badly."

"The silence," she said, and her voice came out harder than she meant, the wound speaking for her. "Ten months, Theo. You knew. You stood in my shop and bought *Middlemarch* and you knew."

"I know."

"I need—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the trained and failing heart. "I can't do this here. Not with the whole town watching and the papers on the table and—"

"Then don't do it here." He reached into his jacket and brought out an envelope, and her whole body recognized it before her mind did—the weight of it, the cream stock, the way he held it slightly tilted, the way he'd held every letter for ten years. "I wrote something. I didn't send it. I couldn't—it wasn't finished. It might never be finished. But you should have it tonight, whatever you decide about me."

She didn't take it. "Why not just say it?"

"Because some things are only true in my handwriting." A ghost of the old smile, the pen-pal smile, the one she'd loved for a decade without a face to attach to it. "And because I'm a coward in the ways that matter and I'm working on it."

"Freya!" Poppy came running through the crowd, braids flying, and skidded to a stop between them, looking from the envelope to her mother's face with the terrible clarity of eleven-year-olds. "The lawyer's lighting the lanterns. He says it's time. Mom, are we—" Her voice caught, and Freya saw it land on her, all of it, the whole year of it. "Are we losing the shop?"

Freya crouched, took her daughter's face in her hands. Behind Poppy, the whole town was watching, and beside them Theo Marchetti stood very still, holding out ten years in an envelope.

"Poppy," she said. "Do you remember the rule? The one about hearts?"

"You can train them," Poppy said. "But you can't starve them."

"That's right." Freya stood. She looked at the envelope. She looked at the man. The lanterns were being lit, one by one down the plane trees, and the square was turning gold, and her heart was doing the thing it had been trained not to do, and for the first time in ten years she was not going to stop it.

She took the envelope.

* * *

She read it by lantern-light at the corner table of her own shop, door open to the square, Poppy's chin hooked over her shoulder, the festival noise a tide against the glass. The handwriting was his—the handwriting she'd known for a decade, the slanted, patient script—and the letter began the way the first one had, ten years and a lifetime ago:

*Dear F.,*

*I have started this letter forty times. In every version I explain myself, and in every version the explanation is smaller than the thing itself, so I'm going to skip it and tell you the true thing instead, which is this:*

*I didn't stay silent because I was protecting the letters. I stayed silent because I was a coward who had been given a second life and was afraid to spend it. You were the best thing I had and the thing I was most afraid of losing, and I confused those two facts for ten months, and I am sorry in a way that letters can't hold.*

*Here is what I know. I know you take your tea too strong. I know you alphabetize by feeling, not by rule. I know you read the ends of novels first when you think no one is watching, and that you cried at page 212 of the book you lent me in September, and that you wrote in the margin: "this is what it costs to be brave." I have been trying to be brave ever since. This letter is page one.*

*The town is signing tonight. If you sign the other paper instead, I will understand, and I will keep writing to you anyway, at your own address, with my real name, for the rest of my life, because ten years of you taught me that love is not something you contain. It's something you survive. And Freya—I want to survive it with you, in person, badly and bravely, starting now.*

*Yours, entirely and no longer anonymously,*

*Theo*

Below the signature, smaller, in the margin, in her own borrowed phrase: *this is what it costs to be brave.*

Poppy was crying. Freya was crying. Outside, the lanterns burned all the way down the square to the lawyer's table, where Pemberton stood with his blood-colored folder and his city smile, and where Devi Okafor stood with the blue one, and where the whole town waited to see which paper Freya Kavanagh would sign.

She stood up. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She took her daughter's hand, and she walked out into the light.
