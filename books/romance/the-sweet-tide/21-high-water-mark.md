## 21. High Water Mark

The cottage at number fourteen had never looked like anyone's home until now.

Noor stood in the middle of the front room with a hammer in one hand and a picture hook in the other, surveying walls that had spent three seasons bare and were finally, stubbornly, filling up. A framed photograph of her mother in the garden in Ibadan, squinting into sun that was nothing like this pale northern light. A watercolor of the harbor she'd bought from the Saturday market stall, overpaying shamelessly because the artist was nine years old and had signed it *Maeve, age 9* in enormous careful letters. And on the wall beside the window — the one that faced east, toward the fence line and the house beyond it — a single sheet of paper in a cheap frame: the ferry timetable for next season, which Lior had given her as a joke and which Noor had decided, without telling anyone, was the truest document she owned.

It listed every departure from April through October. It did not list November through March, because there were none. The sea closed like a hand each winter, and everyone who lived here chose, deliberately, to be held.

She had chosen it too. That was the miracle she kept having to re-learn, the way you re-learn your own address after moving: *I stayed. I am staying. The boxes are unpacked.*

The last box sat open by the hearth, and it was not full of books or dishes. It was full of paper. Ten years of drafts — the letters she'd written and never sent, the ones she'd sent and couldn't bear to reread, the one she'd written on the dock the morning everything broke open and thought would be her last. She had meant to burn them. She'd carried them out to the fire pit twice, and twice had come back inside with the bundle still under her arm, feeling like an arsonist who kept falling in love with the house.

Lior had read them all now. Every word. She'd read them slowly, over three evenings, sitting on the porch swing with a cup of tea going cold beside her, and when she finished she hadn't said *it's all right* or *I forgive you*, because those sentences belonged to a smaller story than theirs. What she'd said was: "You wrote me better than I knew how to be." And then she'd kissed Noor's knuckles, one by one, like punctuation.

So the letters didn't need burning. But they needed somewhere to go, and Noor had realized, standing over the box that morning, that the answer was not a shelf. The correspondence was finished. Not dead — finished, the way a tide finishes, arriving at its high water mark and then turning, gently, toward whatever comes next.

There was only one letter left to write.

* * *

She wrote it at the kitchen table in the late afternoon, with the window open and the smell of salt and cut grass coming through the screen. She wrote it longhand, because ten years of habit dies hard, and because her handwriting — the handwriting Lior had once pulled, damp and coffee-stained, out of a recycling bin, and had never quite forgiven and never quite stopped touching — deserved a final outing.

*Dear Lior,* she began, and then laughed at herself, alone in the kitchen, because after everything they had said to each other — shouted, whispered, wept, murmured half-asleep — *dear* felt both absurd and exactly right. Some forms hold what plain speech cannot.

She told her things she had never put in any letter before. Not comfort, not encouragement, not the careful anonymous tenderness of a stranger who could afford to be brave because she would never have to stand in the light of her own words. This time she told the truth about small things: that she had been terrified the first time Lior handed her sugar across the broken fence, more terrified than she'd been signing any contract in her life. That she had memorized the sound of Lior's laugh in the hospital waiting room a decade ago and had spent years afterward hearing it in crowds, in dreams, in the pause before other people's jokes landed. That she had moved here not hoping to be forgiven or hated but simply unable to imagine dying in a city where Lior Kavanagh did not exist.

And then, near the end, she wrote the part she had rehearsed while sanding the porch steps, while painting the bedroom the color of weak tea, while lying awake listening to the tide do its patient work against the rocks:

*I used to think the letters were the bravest thing I ever did — writing to someone who couldn't write back, loving at a safe distance. But distance was the coward's portion, wasn't it? The brave thing was the fence. The sugar. Staying on this island through a winter with no ferries, with you forty feet away and furious with me, trusting that anger was not the same as ending. You taught me that staying is a verb. So this is my last letter, and my first, and they are the same letter, and it says: I'm not going anywhere. There will be no more envelopes. From now on, if I have something to tell you, I will walk forty feet and say it to your face. Starting now.*

She signed it — her whole name, *Noor Adeyemi*, though Lior knew it now, had known it for months, had said it in a hundred tones including the low one that made Noor forget what she was doing — and folded the page into thirds, and did not seal it in an envelope, because envelopes were over. She walked out her front door, down the path, past the fence they had rebuilt together board by board in June (Declan holding the posts plumb while pretending he wasn't emotionally invested; Priya handing up nails and commentary), and knocked on Lior's door.

Not the doorbell. Her knuckles. Some things you did with your actual hand.

* * *

Lior opened the door with flour on her forearms and an expression that suggested she had been baking as a form of prayer, which, Noor had learned, she often was. Behind her the kitchen smelled of cardamom — Noor's mother's spice, smuggled into this island kitchen via a jar Noor had brought over in July, and look what had become of it, look what took root.

"You're holding paper," Lior said.

"I'm holding paper."

"It's not an envelope."

"No," Noor said. "It's the opposite of an envelope."

Lior dried her hands on her apron with the deliberateness of a surgeon scrubbing in, took the page, and — instead of reading it there in the doorway, quickly, privately — stepped back, held the door wide, and nodded toward the porch. "Read it to me."

Noor blinked. "You want—"

"Out loud. On the swing. If this is the last letter, I want it the way I always wanted it. In your voice." A pause, and something flickered across her face, old grief and older hope sharing a seat. "I spent ten years imagining your voice saying these words. I'd like the real thing, even if I'm late."

So they sat on the porch swing, side by side, the boards creaking their familiar complaint, and Noor read her own letter aloud to the woman it was written for, stumbling once over *coward's portion* and once over *dying*, and finding her voice steady everywhere it mattered. The harbor below went gold and then rose-gold. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked at a gull and lost the argument. When Noor reached the end — *I will walk forty feet and say it to your face* — she lowered the page and found Lior already looking at her, tears running freely and unashamed, smiling like a woman who had finally stopped bracing for impact.

"Say something else to my face," Lior said.

Noor leaned in and did.

When they parted, Lior took the letter, smoothed it flat on her knee, and read it again silently, her lips moving. Then she stood, went inside, and came back with a hammer and a picture hook and the cheap frame from number fourteen's east wall — no, not that frame; a second one, empty, waiting. Noor watched her hang the new frame on the porch wall, right beside the door where every visitor would see it, and slide the letter in behind the glass.

"There," Lior said, stepping back to admire it. "Your last letter gets to live outside. Where I can check on it."

"Check on it?"

"To make sure it doesn't change its mind and move away."

Noor laughed — the real laugh, the one that had started all of this in a hospital waiting room ten years ago, overheard by a stranger whose life was quietly coming apart. "It's not going anywhere," she said. "Neither am I."

Down at the dock, faintly, the evening ferry sounded its horn — not the last of the season anymore, just another boat, just another day, the schedule stretching ahead of them through April and May and all the golden ordinary months. Lior took Noor's hand, flour and all, and nodded toward the kitchen, where two cups waited and something with cardamom was rising under a cloth.

Inside, propped against the fruit bowl, Noor noticed a fresh sheet of stationery in Lior's unmistakable slanted hand, addressed to no one yet, headed with a single line: *Things I should have said sooner.* It was blank except for that.

Lior caught her looking and didn't blush, didn't deflect. She just squeezed her hand and said, "I've got ten years of letters to answer. I thought I'd start tonight — if you're staying for tea."

"I'm staying," Noor said, and the words, for the first time in her life, cost her nothing at all.
