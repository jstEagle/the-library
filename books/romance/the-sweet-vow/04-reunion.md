## 4. Reunion

The Oyelaran reunions had a smell, and the smell was nutmeg.

It hit Juniper at the door of her aunt Grace's house, layered over the salt wind off the channel, and for one disorienting second she was nine years old again, barefoot on linoleum, waiting to be handed a plate. Then she looked down and saw the casserole in her own hands—she'd brought one, because you brought something, that was the law—and the nine-year-old receded, leaving the thirty-one-year-old with damp palms and a rehearsed smile.

Theo stood beside her on the porch, holding a paper bag of lemons from the tree behind her house. She'd told him that morning, in the truck, "If anyone asks, we met in June. At the farmers market. You complimented my tomatoes."

"I don't remember agreeing to lie about tomatoes," he'd said, but he'd been turning the wheel into her driveway as he said it, so she'd counted it as consent.

Now he shifted his weight on the porch boards and said, quietly, "Last chance to trade me for a better lie."

"You're the better lie."

"That's the spirit."

Grace opened the door before Juniper could knock. Her aunt was seventy-three and built like a question mark bent the other way—all forward lean, all appetite for information—and she took one look at Theo and Juniper watched the assessment happen behind her eyes like weather moving across water. Height. Hands. Age. Intentions. Filed.

"So this is him." Grace kissed Juniper's cheek, then extended a hand to Theo with the gravity of a border official. "Grace Oyelaran. I've heard exactly nothing about you, which tells me everything."

"Then I'm arriving at the right time," Theo said, shaking her hand. "I hate showing up after the story's already been told."

Something flickered across Grace's face—the first crack in the border official's composure. "Hm," she said, which Juniper knew from long experience was the highest honor her aunt could pay a stranger on a porch. "Kitchen's through there. Casserole goes on the sideboard. Lemons—" she plucked the bag from Theo's hands and peered inside, "—are from your tree, Juniper, so don't let him take credit."

"He carried them," Juniper said.

"He'll carry worse before this family's done with him," Grace said, and swept back toward the kitchen.

* * *

The house was full the way Oyelaran houses were always full: too many people in rooms built for fewer, cousins stacked three deep on the good couch, children running a smuggling route between the kitchen and the back porch with pilched dinner rolls. The dining table had been extended with plywood and a bedsheet over it, and every square inch of surface held food. Rice and stewed oxtail. A jollof so red it looked dangerous. Grace's macaroni pie, which had its own gravitational field. Someone had driven over from the mainland with a cooler of snapper, and someone else had fried it, and the whole house smelled like Sunday had been distilled.

"Juniper!" Her cousin Adaeze spotted her from across the room and came through the crowd like a heat-seeking missile with a wine glass. "You look—okay? Are you okay?"

"I'm fine."

"She says fine," Adaeze reported to the room at large, in the tone of a woman announcing a suspicious package. "Everyone act natural."

Juniper felt the familiar tightening in her chest, the one she'd spent three months training down. *Fine* was the word she'd practiced in the mirror. Fine meant the vineyard was fine, the money was fine, she was fine, please stop looking at her like the widow of a man who wasn't dead but might as well be, financially speaking.

And then Theo set the lemon bag down, put his hand lightly at the small of her back—not possessive, just present—and said to Adaeze, "She's more than fine. Have you seen what she did with the east block this year? I don't know grapes, and even I can tell it's the best-looking section on the island."

Adaeze blinked. "The east block."

"The rows facing the water. She's been out there since May." He glanced at Juniper, and there was nothing performative in the glance at all—it was warm, specific, *knowing*—and Juniper realized with a lurch that he actually had noticed. He'd been fixing the ferry engine all summer with a view of her vineyard, and apparently he'd been watching.

"You know about the east block," she said under her breath as Adaeze drifted off to spread the news.

"You told me at the dock. Tuesday. You were complaining about mildew."

"I complain about mildew constantly."

"And I listen constantly," he said, and picked up a plate.

* * *

Dinner was a gauntlet run at a table of fourteen.

Juniper had planned for it. She'd briefed Theo in the truck: Uncle Bode asks everyone what they're doing with their lives and then argues with the answer; Cousin Femi will try to recruit Theo into his fishing charter business within ten minutes; the twins, Lola and Simi, age eleven, will interrogate him directly and without mercy, because children are the only honest journalists left.

What she hadn't planned for was how good he was.

Not smooth—smooth would have been wrong, would have smelled like performance. He was quiet in the way that made people lean in. When Uncle Bode asked what he did, Theo said, "I keep the ferry running," and Bode, who had opinions about the ferry schedule dating back to 1998, lit up like a lamp, and they were off. When Femi made his pitch, Theo listened all the way through, asked two questions that proved he'd understood the business model, and then said, "My mother worked boats her whole life. I promised myself I'd never make a living off the water twice," and Femi nodded slowly, respecting the answer, and didn't bring it up again.

He remembered names. That was the thing that kept catching Juniper off guard. He'd met these people forty minutes ago and he was calling Lola by name when she tried to steal rice off his plate, saying "Simi, your sister started it," when the blame needed assigning, asking Adaeze about her nursing exam by its actual date. Juniper watched her family rearrange themselves around him the way water rearranges around a stone dropped in a pond, and felt something complicated happen in her chest that she decided not to examine.

Then Aunt Grace sat down across from her with a bowl of pepper soup and the expression of a woman beginning a procedure.

"So," Grace said. "The vineyard."

"Here it comes," Juniper murmured.

"The bank," Grace continued, undeterred, "is being reasonable, I hope. Your uncle spoke to a man who knows a man at First Coastal, and he says these things can be restructured if you ask early. Before they get ideas." She stirred her soup without eating it. "We could help, you know. Nobody wants to say it in front of the children, but nobody needs to say it in front of the children either. We're not going to watch you drown out of politeness."

Every eye at the table had gone carefully elsewhere, which is how Juniper knew all of them were listening.

This was the moment she'd rehearsed for. The moment where she said *fine* again, where she smiled the trained smile, where she took the pity like a vaccination and moved on. She opened her mouth—

"We're managing," Theo said.

Grace's eyes cut to him.

"Not easily," he went on, calm as a man reading tide tables. "But managing. And she's got a harvest coming in that's better than anyone at this table knows, because she doesn't advertise, because advertising costs money she's spending on fungicide instead. So before anybody offers to rescue her, maybe come out in October and buy a case of wine. That's the help she actually wants."

The table went quiet. Grace looked at him for a long moment, and then at Juniper, and something passed over her face that Juniper had never seen directed at her before: not pity, not worry, but a kind of recalibration. An update to the file.

"Hm," Grace said again, and finally ate her soup.

Under the table, Juniper's hand was shaking. She pressed it flat against her thigh, willing it still, hating that it shook, hating that he'd seen the question coming when she hadn't, hating most of all how badly she wanted to reach for him in front of God and fourteen Oyelarans and not have it be a performance.

* * *

Afterward, dessert happened in the living room, which meant the children claimed the couch and the adults distributed themselves among chairs and floor cushions with plates of Grace's coconut cake. Juniper ended up on the floor against the base of the armchair, and Theo lowered himself beside her with the unselfconscious ease of someone who had already stopped being a guest.

Lola appeared at his elbow. "Is it true you live on a boat?"

"I live above a boathouse."

"What's the difference?"

"A boathouse has an engine in it. My apartment has a kettle."

Lola considered this. "That's sad."

"It's efficient," Theo said gravely, and Lola laughed like he'd said something hilarious, and went off to report the joke to Simi.

Juniper found herself smiling at her plate. "You're good at this," she said, low enough that only he could hear.

"At what?"

"This. Them. All of it."

"I'm not good at it," he said. "I'm paying attention. There's a difference."

"There really isn't."

"Sure there is. Good at it means you could do it anywhere, with anyone. Paying attention means—" He stopped. Looked at his cake. "Means it's this table specifically."

Juniper turned to look at him, and he was already looking at her, and for a second neither of them performed anything at all. The noise of the family went soft around them, the way sound goes soft underwater. She thought: *this is the part where I remember it's fake.* She thought: *any second now.*

That was when she felt his hand find hers on the floor between them. Not grabbed—found, the way you find a rail in the dark. His fingers threaded through hers, and his thumb moved once across her knuckles, absent, unconscious, a man steadying himself against something he hadn't named.

Her heart did something she would later refuse to describe.

Because here was the problem: it wasn't in the terms. They'd signed a napkin. No hand-holding was specified, no thumb-across-knuckles, no looking-at-her-like-that-when-he-thought-she-wasn't-watching. And yet her hand fit his like it had been consulted years ago and had simply been waiting for the appointment.

Across the room, Grace caught her eye. Just for a moment. Her aunt looked at their faces—at whatever was happening on them—and then away, with the smallest possible nod, like a judge accepting evidence.

Juniper should have pulled her hand back. That was the play. That was the whole architecture of the deal: perform in public, retreat in private, keep the ledger clean. Instead she turned her palm up and let him hold on, and ate her coconut cake one-handed, and told herself the warmth climbing her throat was the pepper soup.

It was not the pepper soup.

* * *

They stayed until the children fell asleep in piles and the dishes were done by committee. On the porch, in the blue dark, Grace followed them out with a foil-wrapped slab of cake and pressed it into Theo's hands.

"For your kettle apartment," she said. "You'll come back. Both of you. October, for the wine—I'm holding you to your own speech."

"Yes, ma'am," Theo said.

Grace hugged Juniper last, hard, and whispered against her ear: "Whatever that is, don't you dare manage it. Manage the vineyard. Not that."

Then she was gone, and the door closed, and it was just the two of them and the crickets and the walk down to the truck.

Theo opened her door for her—an old habit already, three weeks old and feeling older than that—and went around to his side. The engine turned over. Neither of them spoke for the first mile, the foil cake sitting between them on the seat like a chaperone.

"They liked you," Juniper said finally.

"They liked you," he corrected. "I was furniture with good timing."

"You weren't furniture." She looked out the window at the dark shapes of the vines rolling past. "You remembered the east block. You remembered Lola's name. You remembered—" *my father's favorite row,* she almost said, except he hadn't known that, she'd never told him that, and yet when Uncle Bode had mentioned the old plantings Theo had nodded like a man who understood exactly what those rows cost to keep alive. "You were too good, Theo. That's the problem. You were so good I forgot."

"Forgot what?"

She turned to look at him. The dashboard light caught half his face, the rest in shadow, his hands easy on the wheel.

"That it isn't real," she said.

The truck hummed along the coast road. He didn't laugh it off. He didn't say *it's fine, it's the deal, we're professionals.* He drove twenty yards in silence, jaw working slightly, and then said, "Yeah," in a voice she couldn't read at all. "I forgot too."

Neither of them said anything else the rest of the way home. But when he pulled into her drive and she reached for the door handle, his hand brushed hers on the seatbelt release—deliberately, this time, she was almost sure—and neither of them acknowledged it, and both of them felt it.

She watched his taillights until they disappeared past the tree line, then stood in her dark kitchen unwrapping Grace's cake she didn't want, thinking about a napkin contract with three rules and no clause for whatever had just happened under that table.

There was a rule missing, she realized. Something about what happens when pretending stops feeling like pretending.

She'd have to raise it with him tomorrow.

Assuming she could figure out how to phrase it without telling him the truth—that somewhere between the pepper soup and the coconut cake, holding his hand had stopped being acting, and she had no idea what to do about a man who paid attention like that, or about herself, standing alone in a kitchen full of borrowed family warmth, wanting him to come back and mean it.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, the ferry's horn sounded once across the water—late, she thought, for a Tuesday—and went quiet.

She didn't think anything of it.

Not yet.
