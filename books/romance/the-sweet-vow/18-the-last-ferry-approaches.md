## 18. The Last Ferry Approaches

The duffel bag had been under his bed since June.

Theo pulled it out on a Tuesday morning, the last week of September, and set it on the mattress like a diagnosis. It smelled faintly of diesel and old canvas, of the summer he'd spent two seasons ago working the Alaska run, before he'd come back to this island because there was nowhere else that wanted him and nothing else he knew how to do. He unzipped it and stared at the emptiness inside, which was honest in a way most things weren't.

He'd told himself he wasn't deciding anything. That was the trick he'd learned young—never decide, just let things happen to you and call it fate afterward. The job offer from Halloran Marine in Bellingham was still technically open. He'd never refused it. He'd also never accepted it, and the distinction had felt meaningful for about six weeks, back when six weeks seemed like a long time.

Now the season was ending. The ferry schedule taped inside the wheelhouse said so in black ink: *Last crossing October 14th, 4:40 PM.* After that, the island went dark until spring. No mail boat, no supply run, no mechanic work worth the name. Everyone who was going to leave would leave by then, and everyone who stayed would spend the winter being the kind of person who stays.

He started folding shirts.

* * *

He'd been careful all week not to see her, which on an island this size meant he'd been a coward with a schedule. He knew her patterns the way he knew tide tables—Tuesdays she drove the truck into town for feed, Thursdays she met the co-op rep at the dock, Sundays she did nothing at all, or claimed to. He'd rearranged his own days around the gaps. It was a skill, avoidance. His mother had been better at it than anyone.

The truth was simpler and uglier: he couldn't look at her without seeing the lawyer's envelope.

Ruthie at the post office had mentioned it offhand, the way small-town people mention everything—*saw some suit out at your girl's place Thursday, real shiny shoes for a dirt road.* Theo had driven past the vineyard that evening without stopping, slow enough to see the man's sedan parked crooked in the yard, fast enough that no one would flag him down. He'd sat at the overlook above the harbor for two hours after that, watching the water do its indifferent glittering, and by the time the sun went down he'd built the whole story brick by brick, the way you build any story you need to survive:

She took the settlement. Of course she took it. Sign away the vineyard, walk free, debt forgiven, no hearing, no Voss, no war. Why would she fight? Fighting meant testifying, meant putting her name in the paper next to his mother's name next to the word *fraud*, meant months of lawyers and a bank that still owned half the island's paper and didn't lose. Fighting meant staying. And everything Juniper Oyelaran had done since April—the rules, the napkin contract, the careful distance, the way she'd gone cold after the kiss like he'd broken something she'd asked him not to touch—everything said she was a woman who kept her exits marked.

She'd hired him to be temporary. He'd signed the terms himself. Nothing after the last ferry in October.

He'd just forgotten, somewhere between the boathouse and the storm and the night she fell asleep against his shoulder on the seawall, that he was the kind of man who read contracts closely. He should have added a clause for himself: *Party of the second part agrees not to mistake any of this for a future.*

* * *

Wednesday he went out to the boathouse.

Not to pack it—he wasn't ready for that, might never be—but to sit in it awhile, the way you visit a grave. The afternoon light came through the gaps in the boards in long dusty blades. His mother's tools were still on the pegboard where she'd hung them twenty years ago, each one traced in marker so anyone who borrowed could put it back right. He'd grown up thinking every family did that. He'd grown up thinking a lot of things.

Margaret Calloway's testimony sat in a folder on the workbench, the copy Juniper had made him. Margaret's voice was in it, halting and fierce by turns: *I saw Arthur Marchetti sign those papers, and I saw Dale Voss hold the pen wrong, and I was too much of a coward to say so then, but I'm saying it now.* An old woman risking whatever remained of her reputation because Juniper had stood in her parlor and traded her own silence for it.

Theo turned the pages without reading them again. He knew what they were worth. He knew what they'd cost.

And he knew—this was the part that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone—that if Juniper testified, she'd be doing it alone, because he would already be gone. That was the shape of it now, the shape he'd chosen by not choosing. Voss had made him an offer in a parking lot three weeks ago: money, a mainland job, a quiet word about calling certain loans due within thirty days if anybody talked. Theo had smiled and said he'd think about it, and Voss had smiled back like they were both men who understood how the world worked, and ever since, Theo had been thinking about it the way you think about a door left open in winter—not deciding to walk through it, just declining to close it.

If he stayed and Juniper fought, Voss would call her loan due. Thirty days. She'd lose the vineyard anyway, only faster and meaner, and she'd know it was because of him. But if he left, the threat lost its handle. Voss couldn't squeeze a man who wasn't holding anything.

That was the version he told himself at night. It had the advantage of being partly true, which is the best kind of lie.

* * *

Thursday morning the harbor was flat as poured glass, and the *Marianne V* sat low on her mooring looking tired, the way boats do at the end of a season. Theo worked on her cooling system for four hours without talking to anyone except the engine, which was his preferred ratio. Sal Petraglia came by around noon with coffee and news, because Sal considered silence a personal insult.

"You hear about the county hearing?" Sal said, settling onto a crate like a man planning to stay a while. "Ninth of October. They're subpoenaing half the bank's loan files. Whole island's going to be there."

"Is that so." Theo kept his eyes on the impeller.

"Heard they might even call witnesses. Regular people, I mean. Folks with grievances." Sal drank his coffee and watched Theo over the rim. "You'd know something about grievances."

"I'd know something about engines."

"Yeah," Sal said, standing, brushing off his pants. "That's what I heard too."

After he left, Theo sat in the engine room's diesel-smelling dark and let himself imagine it, just once, just briefly: the county building in October, folding chairs and bad acoustics, and Juniper standing up in front of all of it with the ledgers in her hands. Her chin up. Her voice steady. She'd be terrified and she'd do it anyway, because that was the thing about her that had undone him from the start—she treated fear like weather, something you dressed for, not something that got to decide where you went.

And he'd be forty miles north, torquing bolts for Halloran Marine, learning about it eventually, maybe from Sal's letters, maybe never.

His hands were shaking. He looked at them with mild interest, the way you look at someone else's problem, and then he wiped them on a rag and went back to work.

* * *

Friday he packed in earnest.

It went faster than he expected, which was its own kind of verdict. Eleven years on this island since his mother died, and everything he owned fit into one duffel and two boxes of tools. He left the pegboard traces alone. He took the photograph of his mother at sixteen, standing on the dock of the land that used to be hers, laughing at whoever held the camera. He took the napkin contract out of the drawer by his bed—it had migrated there sometime in August without his permission—and held it a long moment under the lamp.

*Terms of Engagement,* in Juniper's slanted handwriting. *1. No real names to his past. 2. No questions about her father's debts. 3. Nothing after the last ferry in October.* Both their signatures. Hers signed with a flourish like she was mocking the formality; his signed carefully, like he meant it.

Clause three. He'd written clause three. He'd thought he was protecting himself, and instead he'd written the exact dimensions of the wound in advance, measured it out, agreed to it in ink. There was a lesson in that. He declined to learn it.

He folded the napkin into his breast pocket, which was stupid and sentimental and he did it anyway.

* * *

Saturday evening, against every plan he'd made, he found himself driving the truck out the coast road toward the vineyard.

He told himself it was about the ledger copies—she had documents of his mother's, he should collect them before he left, tidy endings, that was all. The lie had a thin, whining quality even to him, but he let it drive. The light was going gold and long across the water, the kind of light that made the island look like a place worth fighting for, which was cruel timing.

Her truck was in the yard. A second car was there too, unfamiliar, dark, expensive-looking.

Theo stopped at the gate and didn't go in.

Through the kitchen window he could see them: Juniper at the table, and across from her a man in a good jacket, papers spread between them. The lawyer again, or another one. Juniper's face was unreadable at this distance, which was unusual—usually he could read her across a crowded reunion, could find her laugh in a room full of noise. But tonight her face was closed, and the man was sliding a document across the table, turning it to face her, setting a pen beside it with a small ceremonial care that Theo recognized from his own worst memories. That was how it had looked when the bank men came for the boathouse. That was how surrender paperwork always looked: polite, unhurried, final.

Juniper picked up the pen.

Theo's whole body went tight, waiting—*don't, don't, don't*—and then the angle changed, she shifted, and he lost the window. Just lost it, a curtain of her shoulder, thirty seconds, maybe less. When he could see again the pen was down and the document was squared neatly in front of her and he couldn't tell, couldn't tell, whether she'd signed it or pushed it back.

It didn't matter, he told the truck, the road, the gold light. It didn't matter, because either way he'd already decided, hadn't he? Either way the duffel was packed and the job was open and the ferry left in nine days. If she'd signed, he'd been right to leave. If she hadn't—if she was actually going to stand up at that hearing and say her father's name and her own name and *fraud* into the record—then she'd be doing it while Voss called her loan due, and the only thing keeping that from happening was his absence. His leaving was the shield. He'd built it himself. He could stand behind it.

Both stories ended with him on the 4:40 boat on the fourteenth of October. That was the elegance of it. That was the peace of it.

He sat at the gate until the kitchen light went off, until the strange car pulled away and passed him without slowing, until the yard was dark and ordinary. Then he turned the truck around and drove home with his headlights off for the last quarter mile, an old habit, a boy's habit, hiding from a house that had already been taken.

* * *

Sunday he finished packing. Monday he gave notice at the dock, and Sal shook his hand too hard and said *didn't figure you'd stay, none of you Marchettis ever figured out how*, which was meant as sympathy and landed like a prophecy. Tuesday he serviced the *Marianne V* one last time, unasked, unpaid, because a boat deserved to winter well even if her people didn't.

Wednesday it rained, a soft end-of-season rain, and he walked the beach below the boathouse from one property line to the other—the old line, his mother's line, the one Margaret's testimony said had been stolen with a forged signature and a smiling banker. The sand was empty. Gulls argued over something dead. He stood at the boundary marker, a rusted iron pin half-swallowed by forty years of tides, and thought: *This is yours. This was always yours. And you are leaving it.*

But leaving it was the point. Leaving it was mercy. Every version of the story where he stayed ended with Juniper bleeding for it, and he had watched one woman he loved get bled dry by this island already. He would not arrange a second showing.

That was the frame he hung it in, Wednesday night, sitting on his cot with the duffel zipped and the boxes taped and the ferry schedule glowing under the lamp: *quiet resignation mistaken for peace.* Though he wouldn't have called it that. He'd have called it realistic. He'd have called it protecting her. He'd have called it, if pressed, the only adult decision available to a man with one duffel bag and a folder full of other people's courage.

He slept badly and woke early and told no one.

* * *

October 13th. One day before the last crossing.

The harbor was busy with end-of-season traffic—crab pots hauled and stacked, docks coming up section by section, everyone trading the same jokes about who'd winter where. Theo loaded his boxes onto a hand cart at dawn, before the dock got crowded, because he didn't want to explain himself to anyone and small towns don't allow departures to be private. Word would travel by lunchtime. *Marchetti's gone. Took the mainland job. Always figured he would.* By tomorrow it would be a fact with no author, and facts with no authors can't be argued with.

He rolled the cart down the ramp as the morning boat came in, bringing crates and a handful of off-season faces. Ruthie waved from the post office window. He didn't wave back fast enough and hated himself for it.

At the fuel dock, the radio was playing—the little weather band he kept tuned out of habit, more static than signal this time of year. He was coiling a line when the broadcast cut through the crackle, the announcer's voice thin and tinny and completely indifferent to what it was carrying:

*"—county commissioners have confirmed the hearing on Coastal First Bank lending practices will proceed as scheduled, tomorrow morning, tenth of October—"* wait, no, the ninth, he'd gotten the date wrong, it was tomorrow, it was *tomorrow*—*"—scheduled witnesses include former bank employees and private citizens. Among those expected to testify—"*

A gust of wind ate the name.

Theo stood with the coil of rope in his hands and the harbor noise washing over him and waited for the radio to repeat itself, the way radios do, the way the sea gives everything back twice. The announcer moved on to tides and barometric pressure, and the name did not come again, and Theo discovered he was breathing like a man who'd been underwater.

Because he'd heard enough. Two syllables of a first name spoken through static, and a rhythm underneath it he'd have known blindfolded, in a crowd, in his sleep—the cadence of a woman who treated fear like weather.

She was going to testify. Tomorrow. In front of the county, the bank, Dale Voss and his good coat and his thirty-day threats.

Alone.

He looked down the length of the dock, at the *Marianne V* riding her lines, at the schedule taped in the wheelhouse—*last crossing October 14th, 4:40 PM*—and felt the whole careful architecture of his quiet, merciful, reasonable exit shudder once, like a hull taking water.

The hearing was at ten tomorrow morning. The ferry left at 4:40 the day after.

There was time. There was almost no time at all.
