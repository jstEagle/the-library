## 11. Signed, Anonymous

The envelope was heavier than usual.

Jude noticed it the way she noticed everything about the letters now — with a small, involuntary quickening she had stopped pretending wasn't there. She'd taken to collecting them from the post office on Tuesdays, when Marge Kettleman was on shift and too busy gossiping about the Hendersons' divorce to watch anyone's face. The program's rules were printed on a card the clerk had given her back in April: *Correspondents remain anonymous until both parties consent to reveal.* She kept the card in her desk drawer, under a stack of ribbon swatches, and she had never once thought of it as anything but a formality.

She poured coffee first. Ritual mattered; her mother had taught her that much before the rest of her lessons curdled. Then she sat at the kitchen table — not the office, never the office, where wedding binders multiplied like rabbits and Denny came by to argue about lawyers — and slit the envelope with the good scissors.

The handwriting had changed over the months. The first letters had been careful, block-printed, a man writing the way he drove: hands at ten and two, no sudden moves. Somewhere around June the letters had loosened. The pen pressed harder. Words crowded the margins. Whoever he was, he had stopped performing for her.

*Dear Wren,* (her name in the program — she'd chosen it because wrens were small, loud, and nobody's idea of romantic) *—*

*I keep thinking about what you said last week, about how you can tell when a couple is lying at their own wedding. I've been thinking about whether I'm lying too. Not to you. To myself, maybe, about some things I did or didn't do.*

*It rained here Tuesday. Hard. The kind of rain that makes you pull over because the road disappears. I ended up sitting in the boathouse down by the creek for an hour, just listening to it come off the tin roof. I don't know why I'm telling you this. Maybe because it was the first hour in months I didn't feel like I was failing somebody.*

*You asked once if I believed people could change. I said I wanted to. That's still true. But wanting is cheap. I'm starting to think the real question is whether you can forgive yourself for the things you got wrong while you were trying to get them right.*

*Yours, —M*

Jude read it twice. Three times. Her coffee went cold beside her elbow and she didn't touch it.

*The boathouse rain.*

There was only one boathouse on Junebug Creek that mattered — the old Marchetti-era structure behind the Hollis property, tin roof gone rust-orange, floorboards soft as bread. She knew it because her family had fished that stretch of water for four generations, because Denny had kissed his first girl on its sagging dock, because she herself had sat inside it during exactly one storm, three weeks ago, soaked through and laughing, talking for hours with a man whose badge she could see glinting every time lightning found the window.

Sheriff Eli Marchetti. Sheltering from the midsummer squall after the canoe incident, trading truths neither of them had any business trading.

Her pen pal had been in the same storm. In the same boathouse.

Or —

No. She set the letter down flat, smoothing it with both palms like she was pressing a wrinkle out of silk. Don't leap. You're a professional. You don't build a cathedral on one beam.

But her heart was already doing something unprofessional, climbing up into her throat and setting up housekeeping there.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't make go away: the county had exactly two people who wrote letters like this — patient, wounded, self-interrogating — and one of them was dead. Her father. And her father had never once written her a sentence that made her feel seen instead of managed.

Which left the man who arrested her brother. The man she'd spent six months glaring at across council chambers. The man who, three weeks ago in a boathouse, had told her — voice low, rain hammering the tin like applause — that he no longer trusted his own instincts, that he lay awake wondering if he'd been wrong about things he'd once been certain of.

The same words. Nearly word for word. On paper, from a stranger. In person, from Eli Marchetti.

People repeated themselves, she told herself. People had stock phrases, private liturgies they recited to whoever would listen. It meant nothing. It was coincidence, the way two brides independently chose peonies, the way every groom cried at the same moment in the vows.

Except.

Except that *M*'s letters had started arriving in April, and the boathouse conversation had happened three weeks ago, and the phrasing hadn't appeared in the letters until now — as if something had happened between the writing of the last letter and this one. As if the storm had loosened something.

As if the letters and the man were the same man, and always had been.

Jude stood up so fast her chair skidded. She paced the kitchen, past the window where the creek glittered in the distance like a dropped ribbon, past the fridge with Denny's magnetized court date reminder, past everything ordinary, while her mind ran the tape backward through six months of correspondence looking for fingerprints.

He knew about weddings — but everyone in Junebug Creek knew she planned weddings; it was in the paper twice a year. He knew she didn't trust marriage — public knowledge; she'd said as much at the grange hall, loudly, to Eli Marchetti's face. He knew she liked her coffee black and her honesty blacker — half the town could testify to that.

And she had told him things. God, she had told him things. About the courthouse, about Marcus signing the annulment papers with a pen borrowed from the clerk while she stood there holding a bouquet she'd bought herself. About how she'd learned to treat the heart like a muscle — trainable, containable, useful only within its designed range. Things she'd never said aloud to anyone but her pen pal.

And the man in the boathouse had listened to her talk about the courthouse betrayal — she'd told him that too, that night, rain coming down — and he had gone quiet in a way she'd catalogued at the time as *respectful* and was now re-cataloguing as *guilty*.

"Oh," she said, out loud, to the empty kitchen. "Oh, you son of a bitch."

The fury arrived first, hot and clean, and she welcomed it the way you welcome an old friend at the door. Because fury was familiar. Fury was load-bearing. She had built half her life on it since the courthouse, and it had never once let her down.

But underneath the fury, unwelcome as a draft under a door, was the other thing. The longing. Because if it was him — if the man who wrote her the only honest sentences in her life was the man she'd spent six months hating — then the hatred had been living in a house with a false wall, and behind it was everything she'd confessed on paper, all those nights she'd bared herself to a stranger because a stranger was safe, and none of it had been safe at all, and all of it had been *received*, truly received, by the one man in the county she'd sworn never to need.

She sat back down. She took out paper. Her hand was shaking, which annoyed her, so she pressed it flat and waited, the way she waited out nervous grooms.

Fine, she thought. If it's him, he'll hang himself with his own rope. And if it isn't — if some other poor soul in this town happens to share a boathouse and a vocabulary with Eli Marchetti — then I've lost nothing but my dignity, and I haven't used that in years.

She wrote:

*Dear M,*

*A question, and I want you to answer it honestly, whatever else you've been honest about.*

*That night in the boathouse — the tin roof, the storm, the hour that felt like failing nobody — you weren't alone. There was a woman there. Stranded, soaked, arguing with you about creek land until the argument wore itself out and turned into something else. She talked too much. She told you about a courthouse and a bouquet she bought herself.*

*Tell me what color the boat was on the rack above us. The old canoe, hanging from the rafters. Tell me its color, and tell me why a man who doesn't trust his own instincts would sit in the dark with a stranger and listen like listening was the last thing he knew how to do.*

*If you answer wrong, I'll know you're a kind liar, and I'll forgive you, and we'll go on as we were.*

*If you answer right —*

*Answer right.*

*— Wren*

She read it over twice, hunting for mercy, finding none, deciding that was correct. She sealed it before she could soften. Then she walked it to the mailbox at the end of her lane in her bare feet, gravel biting, and stood there a long moment with her hand on the flag before she raised it.

The flag went up like a signal fire.

* * *

Three days. She'd forgotten how long three days could be when you were waiting on a verdict.

She filled them the way she filled all her waiting: with work. The Delacroix-Pruitt wedding needed a seating chart rebuilt after the mothers declared war over table nine. A cake tasting. A venue walk-through at the orchard where she caught herself, twice, staring at the tree line beyond the property — the direction of the creek, the direction of the boathouse, the direction of a man's voice saying *I don't know why I'm telling you this* while rain came off the tin like the sky was confessing too.

Denny came by Wednesday evening with takeout and grievances, and she let him talk about Voss and the buyout paperwork and the lawyer in Carthage who wanted a retainer they didn't have, and she nodded in the right places, and the whole time a second conversation ran underneath like groundwater: *Should I tell him? Is it him? What does it mean if it is?* She almost said something — Denny of all people had a right to know if the sheriff was writing love letters to their family's enemy — but the words snagged on a hook she couldn't name yet, and she let them go.

Thursday night the dream came again, the courthouse dream, except this time when she looked down at the bouquet in her own hands it was a letter, unsigned, and Marcus's borrowed pen was writing itself across the page in blocky, careful print that slowly loosened into a hand she recognized from council meeting agendas and a traffic citation she'd contested and lost.

She woke at four a.m. with her heart going like a paddle wheel and lay in the dark thinking: *You already know. You've known since Tuesday. You're just waiting for permission.*

Friday. The mailbox.

She made herself wait until noon. She made herself pour the coffee. She made herself use the good scissors, though her hands wanted to tear.

One letter. His handwriting, and she saw it differently now — saw the ten-and-two control of the early letters, the gradual unraveling, the way the pen dug in on certain words like it was bracing against something.

*Dear Wren,*

*You asked me to answer right. Here is my answer, and I'm going to give it to you straight because I think you'd rather be cut than handled.*

*The canoe was green. Faded green, almost gray, with a hairline crack along the port gunwale that someone tried to patch with epoxy that never matched. I know because I've looked at that canoe my whole life. My grandfather hung it there.*

*The woman in the boathouse talked about a courthouse and a bouquet she bought herself, and I sat in the dark and listened because it was the first time in years anyone had told me the truth without asking me for anything. I should have told her then who I was. I didn't. That's on me, and I'll carry it.*

*Wren — Jude.*

*I've known since the festival. Maybe longer. I've read your letters and heard your voice in every line, and I kept writing anyway, because they were the truest words I had and I couldn't bear to stop saying them even if saying them was a lie of omission. I'm sorry. I'm not sorry. Both are true.*

*What I told you in the boathouse was true. What I've written you was true. The only lie was my name, and I'm giving you that now because you deserve to decide what to do with it.*

*Burn this if you want. I'll understand. But if there's any part of you that wants to hate me in person the way you've loved me on paper — I'll be at the station. Or the creek. Whichever you choose.*

*— Eli*

Jude read it standing up, because sitting felt like surrender.

Then she read it again, and the vertigo hit — the full, tilting, horizon-drops-away vertigo of a world rearranging itself around a fact she'd already known and refused to know. Six months of letters swung on their hinges and showed her their other faces. *I no longer trust my own instincts* — spoken across a council table in a voice like gravel and regret. *The first hour in months I didn't feel like I was failing somebody* — the man at the grange hall, worn thin as paper, defending evictions he clearly didn't believe in while she flayed him in front of the whole town. Every barb she'd thrown him, he'd carried home and answered with tenderness to a stranger who was her.

He had known. At the festival, maybe longer. He had sat in that boathouse listening to her bare her worst wound and said nothing, and then he had gone home and written to her as a stranger and told her things he'd never told anyone, knowing — *knowing* — that the stranger was the woman whose brother he'd arrested, whose land he'd been ordered to help take.

Fury rose, and this time it didn't come alone. It came braided through with something hotter and more frightening, and Jude stood in her kitchen holding five pages of a sheriff's soul and understood that she was in terrible trouble, because she didn't want to burn it.

She wanted to write back.

She wanted to write back *so badly* her teeth ached.

"Damn you," she said quietly, to the letter, to the creek, to the man in it. "Damn you for making it impossible to hate you."

Because here was the ruin of it: the muscle theory. Her creed. The heart is trainable; keep it in its range; lend pieces, never the whole. She had run that doctrine for six years like a well-managed business, and it had worked, and it had kept her safe, and it had kept her *small*, and now one man with two names had walked through every wall she'd built without breaking a single one — because she'd handed him the bricks herself, letter by letter, and called it anonymity.

He hadn't tricked her. That was the worst part. He'd lied by omission, yes, cowardice dressed as caution, and she'd flay him for it in person, she promised herself that much. But the words themselves — the confessions, the doubts, the boathouse rain — those he'd given freely, to *her*, knowing her, and no one had ever done that. Marcus had married her image. This man had read her drafts and stayed.

Jude put the letter down. Picked it up. Put it down again.

Outside, the afternoon light was going long and gold over the water, and somewhere down that water a man was waiting at a station or a creek bank, having laid his name on the table like a man laying down a hand of cards, waiting to see if she'd match it or walk away.

She should burn it. Burn the letter, close the account, request a new assignment, retreat behind the muscle theory and lock the door. That was the smart play. That was the play she'd have advised any client to make.

Instead she found herself reaching for paper, and her hand — the traitor — was steady now, steadier than it had been all week.

She wrote one line before she stopped herself, one line that scared her more than the whole revelation had:

*Dear Eli —*

The ink dried on the page like a door swinging open, and downstairs in her chest she could hear the whole architecture of her armor shifting, groaning, deciding.

Down the hall, the phone rang. Denny's number on the screen. She stared at it, letter half-written, heart fully unguarded for the first time in six years, and reached for the phone with the hand that wasn't trembling.

"Jude." Denny's voice was wrong — tight, winded, like a man who'd been running. "You need to get down to the station. Now. They've got Eli in an interview room, and Voss is there, and Jude — they're saying something about the records office. About a break-in."

The letter lay on the table, half-finished, its opening line gleaming wet in the last of the light.

*Dear Eli.*

She grabbed her keys.
