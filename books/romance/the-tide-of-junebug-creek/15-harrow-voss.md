## 15. Harrow Voss

The trouble with small towns, Harrow Voss had learned in his forty-nine years, was that everyone believed they were the hero of the place. The farmer with his fence line. The widow with her garden club. The sheriff with his badge and his tired eyes and his stubborn little code of honor. They all walked around Junebug Creek like the town owed them something, like their names were carved into the water tower instead of just painted over every few years by a man on a county payroll who took his checks from the same account Harrow did.

Harrow knew better. He'd known since he was eleven years old, standing in the back of his father's feed store, watching men shake hands over deals that would take twenty years to ripen. Nobody was the hero of anything. There were only people who understood how things actually worked — paper, patience, pressure — and people who didn't.

He was, if he said so himself, excellent at all three.

He sat in his office above the old bank building on Cordelia Street, which he owned through an LLC that owned two other LLCs, and he looked out at the town he was about to finish buying, and he allowed himself the rare luxury of satisfaction. Below him, the Creek ran brown and lazy through the June light, past the Coldharbour dock, past the Grange Hall, past everything he intended to turn into something called Vosswater Landing once the last signatures came in Friday morning.

Friday. Four days out. He turned his wrist and checked his watch even though he didn't need to. A man who kept time in his head didn't need a watch; he wore it because other people needed to see it. Gold, understated, expensive enough to be noticed and not so expensive as to invite questions. Everything about Harrow Voss was calibrated exactly that way.

His phone buzzed. He glanced at it. A text from Dale Pruitt, one of his fixers, the kind of man who could break a kneecap or file a lien with equal efficiency:

*Coldharbour woman was at the annex again Tuesday. Pulled deed records for parcels 40 through 47.*

Harrow read it twice, unhurried, the way he read everything. Then he set the phone face-down on the blotter and looked back out at the creek.

So. The sister was digging.

He wasn't surprised. He'd been surprised exactly three times in his life — once when his first wife left him, once when the 2008 crash hadn't touched his portfolio, and once when a jury in Knoxville actually acquitted a client he'd expected to buy — and none of those surprises had cost him more than money. Jude Coldharbour pulling county records was not going to be the fourth surprise. It was, in fact, going exactly as planned.

That was the thing nobody ever gave him credit for: Harrow didn't just anticipate obstacles. He *built* them. Or better — he built the conditions under which obstacles built themselves, and then he stood back and let human nature do the heavy lifting while he played golf or attended a ribbon-cutting or wrote a generous check to the volunteer fire department, which was the best advertising money could buy in a town this size.

The pen-pal program was his favorite kind of work. Elegant. Deniable. And so perfectly, deliciously ironic that he sometimes caught himself smiling about it in church.

* * *

It had started, as most of his best work did, with a problem.

Eighteen months ago, the Coldharbour problem had been simple: Denny Coldharbour was loud, beloved, and constitutionally incapable of letting a grievance die, and half the families along the creek listened to him the way farmers listen to weather reports. When the consortium — Meridian Ridge Development, a name Harrow had chosen precisely because it sounded like nothing — began making offers up and down the waterway, Denny organized. Meetings at the Grange Hall. A petition. Talk of a historical designation that would freeze development cold for years.

Harrow could have fought it the ugly way. He'd done ugly before; there were men in three counties who still didn't make eye contact with him at the Piggly Wiggly. But ugly left fingerprints, and Harrow had learned from watching lesser men fall that the perfect crime in a small town wasn't making your enemy disappear. It was making your enemy *distract himself*. Give a man a fire close to home and he'll never notice the smoke rising off yours.

So he'd studied the Coldharbours the way he studied everything — patiently, through intermediaries, over long lunches with people who talked too much after their second glass of sweet tea.

What he learned delighted him.

Denny Coldharbour carried a grudge against Sheriff Eli Marchetti like a stone in his shoe — the arrest, five years back, the charge that never stuck, the humiliation of being cuffed on his own street while neighbors watched. Denny would never forgive it. Denny would never *need* to forgive it, as long as someone kept the wound open.

And Jude Coldharbour — ah, Jude. The wedding planner. The woman who'd watched her own marriage dissolve in a courthouse hallway and come out the other side preaching some little gospel about hearts being trainable muscle, about love being a discipline you could schedule like a fitting. She ran flawless weddings for people she pitied, kept her own heart locked like a safe-deposit box, and glared across every council meeting at Eli Marchetti with a hatred so disciplined it was practically architectural.

Two siblings. One sheriff. Three people whose emotions, properly arranged, would generate all the friction a growing town needed.

Then the clerk's office handed him the instrument.

Mabel Truitt ran the reconciliation pen-pal program out of the county annex — a little civic balm Harrow had quietly funded through the Rotary, because a program that made the town feel healed was worth ten times its cost in goodwill, and because Mabel was the sort of woman who filed things exactly where she said she filed them, which made her predictable, and predictability was the raw material of all great engineering.

When the program launched, Harrow suggested — casually, over coffee at the diner, in his capacity as concerned councilman — that the pairings be handled with care. That feuding parties shouldn't be matched together, obviously. That the whole point was strangers healing strangers. Mabel agreed enthusiastically and promised to keep the pairing sheets confidential.

She did keep them confidential. From everyone except the councilman who'd helped fund the program and who stopped by the annex most Tuesdays to discuss the historical society's budget.

Harrow had spent one evening in his office with the applicant list and a legal pad, and what he produced was not a clerical error. It was a masterpiece wearing a clerical error's clothes.

He paired Jude Coldharbour with Eli Marchetti.

Not directly — no, that would have been madness, they'd have recognized each other's names instantly. The program used pseudonyms; applicants drew them from a fishbowl at signup, which meant Mabel held the key, and Mabel held the key because Harrow made sure the fishbowl drawing happened on the one afternoon Mabel's niece was in the hospital having a baby and Mabel's temporary replacement, a sweet retired schoolteacher named Dot Fenwick, was running the desk alone. Dot followed instructions beautifully. Instructions Harrow had written.

The letters would flow through the annex postbox, anonymous, untraceable to any casual eye. Two guarded, wounded, intelligent people, writing to a stranger they thought was safe. Harrow knew what happened to guarded people given a safe stranger. He'd built a fortune on it. Guarded people didn't loosen up — they *flooded*. Every word they couldn't say at the council meeting, every doubt Eli Marchetti swallowed behind that badge, every loneliness Jude Coldharbour papered over with seating charts — it would all go into those letters, addressed to a nameless confessor, and it would bind them tighter than any matchmaker's scheme, and they would never know why they felt so seen.

And here was the beauty of it, the part Harrow appreciated the way other men appreciated a fine bourbon: the pairing would either draw them together or drive them apart, and both outcomes served him.

If they fell for each other on paper and then discovered the truth — that their anonymous soulmate was their sworn enemy — the betrayal would gut them both. Jude would retreat into that armored competence of hers, twice as hard to reach. Eli would drown in guilt and recuse himself from anything touching the Coldharbour file. Either way, the creek lands closed without opposition.

If they somehow found their way through the deception to each other — well. A sheriff in love was a sheriff compromised. A compromised sheriff hesitated. And hesitation, in Harrow's experience, was worth more than bribery, because you couldn't prosecute a man for thinking twice.

He'd raised a glass of actual bourbon to himself that night, alone in the office, and toasted the town clerk's "clerical error."

* * *

Now, four days from closing, he reviewed the board the way a general reviews a map.

He kept it literal — an actual corkboard, because digital left metadata and metadata left subpoenas. Pins and index cards. Parcel 40 through 47 down the east bank. Signed, signed, signed, signed. The Hollis place, signed under pressure of medical debt. The Abernathy farm, signed by heirs in Seattle who hadn't seen the creek since Reagan. The Marsh land, signed by a widow who cried in his office and thanked him afterward, which had been almost embarrassing in its ease.

And at the center of the board, one card with no pin yet: **COLDHARBOUR — 11 ACRES — CLOSES FRI 9 AM**.

Eleven acres. The last holdout. The keystone. Without it, Vosswater Landing would have a hole in its heart, a ragged stretch of working dock and generational memory right where his renderings showed the marina promenade. With it, he owned the creek's entire eastern approach, and the historical designation died in committee next month for lack of contiguous protected land, and Junebug Creek became, at last, what it had always been underneath: an asset awaiting optimization.

The Coldharbours wouldn't sign. He'd known that from the start, and he hadn't wasted charm on them the way he had on the others. Denny was unbriable — not principled, exactly, but proud in the wrong direction, the kind of man who'd sleep in his truck before he'd take a check from Harrow Voss. And Jude — Jude was worse than unbriable. Jude was *attentive*. She read documents. She asked questions in meetings with that flat, pleasant voice that made lesser men feel like they'd been patted down.

Which was why the paperwork on her parcel had been handled with special care. The offer letter, the appraisal, the closing timeline — all of it clean, all of it technically impeccable, because Jude Coldharbour would find a sloppy forgery in an afternoon. What she wouldn't find — what almost nobody found — was the chain two links upstream: the signature on the original 1987 easement transfer, the one that quietly redefined her family's boundary line by thirty feet of creek frontage, executed in a hand that wasn't her grandfather's. Harrow had bought that document from a probate attorney in Knoxville who owed him more than money, and it was sitting in a fireproof drawer waiting to surface at exactly the right moment — probably in court, probably in front of a judge, probably on the day the Coldharbours finally realized they'd never owned what they thought they owned.

But that was the contingency plan. Contingencies were for things that might go wrong. Harrow preferred to focus on things that were already going right.

He picked up his phone and dialed Dale Pruitt.

"Dale. The annex visits. How many?"

"Three that we know of. She photographs stuff on her phone. Doesn't ask the clerk for help — pulls the boxes herself."

"Of course she does." Harrow smiled at the creek. "Leave her alone. Don't spook her, don't follow her, don't so much as look at her car. If she finds the Voss connection on her own, I want her to find it feeling clever. Clever people make mistakes clever people always make — they think discovery is the same thing as victory."

"You got it." A pause. Dale had the particular silence of a man deciding whether to say something. "One more thing. Marchetti's been asking around too. Quiet-like. About the deed chain on the east bank parcels. He pulled the title history on the Hollis place Monday."

Harrow went very still, in the way he did when the stillness itself was a decision.

Eli Marchetti. His sheriff. His problem.

He'd counted on Eli's conscience the way he counted on gravity — a force to be managed, not defeated. For months it had performed beautifully. Eli suspected the deeds were wrong; Harrow knew he suspected, because suspicion was written all over the man's face at every council session, in the way he stalled serving the eviction notices, in the letters he'd started writing to the state attorney general's office that Harrow's contact there forwarded to him unopened-looking and thoroughly read. But suspicion without evidence was just insomnia, and Eli had no evidence, because Harrow had made sure the evidence lived in exactly two places: a fireproof drawer, and a ledger locked in the county annex's old records vault that no living person knew connected back to him.

Except now the sheriff was pulling title histories. And the Coldharbour woman was photographing deed boxes. Separately. Uncoordinated. For now.

"Dale," Harrow said pleasantly, "what's our read on Marchetti and the Coldharbour girl? Any chatter?"

"That's the funny part. There ain't chatter. They hate each other in public — you saw the Grange Hall meeting. But my cousin works the annex post desk, and she says both of 'em got thick envelopes coming and going through the pen-pal box. Same handwriting week after week. Regular as church."

Harrow laughed. It came out warm and genuine, which was the trick of it — his laughter was always genuine, because he genuinely enjoyed himself.

"Well," he said. "Small towns."

"You want us to do something about it?"

"No." He let the word sit. "I want you to do *nothing* about it, and I want you to enjoy how easy that is. Those two are doing my work for me. A sheriff distracted is a sheriff defanged. A Coldharbour distracted is a Coldharbour signing late. Let them write their little letters. Let them fall in love or tear each other apart — honestly, Dale, I could watch either one with a bowl of popcorn." He turned from the window, from the creek, from the town that still thought of him as the man who'd gotten the new playground equipment funded. "What I need from you is different. Friday morning, nine o'clock, the Coldharbour closing happens whether they sign or not. I need the default judgment paperwork finalized, the judge briefed, and the surveyor's affidavit — the corrected one — filed by Thursday noon. Can you make Thursday noon?"

"Judge Pell's golfing Thursday."

"Then he can brief between holes. He golfs with me Fridays; I'll mention it Saturday and he'll have forgotten I mentioned it by Sunday, which is the point."

"And if the Coldharbour woman takes what she's finding to somebody? Press? State AG?"

Harrow considered. Outside the window, a heron stood in the shallows below the Coldharbour dock, motionless, patient, the oldest strategy in the world: be still until the moment is exact, then strike.

"If she goes to the law," he said, "the law is me. If she goes to the press, the press prints a story about a plucky town fighting a developer, and then Friday the deed closes anyway and the story becomes a story about a plucky town that lost, and nobody runs sequels about losers. If she goes to Marchetti—" he paused, savoring it— "then she's trusting the man who cuffed her brother, and either he betrays her or he burns his career for her, and I win on both branches of that tree."

"So we're clean."

"We're immaculate." He hung up and stood a moment longer at the glass.

* * *

There was one loose thread, and Harrow Voss did not tolerate loose threads, so he took it out after dinner the way other men took out a cigar.

He drove himself — he liked driving himself through his own future holdings, windows down, one hand on the wheel, nodding at constituents who waved. He parked behind the annex and let himself in with the key Mabel Truitt didn't know he had a copy of, and he stood in the dark little mail room where the pen-pal letters came and went, and he read.

He read Jude Coldharbour's outgoing letters first. Dot Fenwick sorted them into cubbies on Wednesdays; Harrow read them Wednesday nights. The woman could *write*. That was the surprise, the small fourth surprise he permitted himself — not that she was digging through deed records, but that under all that armor she was funny, and lonely, and honest in a way that made him, briefly, understand why a certain tired sheriff kept writing back. Her latest letter was full of a man she refused to name, a man she "could hate if he weren't so tired," and Harrow read that sentence twice and felt something adjacent to admiration. You couldn't engineer a sentence like that. You could only build the room it got said in and hope.

Eli Marchetti's letters were harder, plainer, braver than Harrow had predicted. Confessions, nearly. Doubts about his own instincts. Guilt about an arrest. A man dismantling himself on paper for a stranger.

Harrow put them back exactly where they belonged, aligned to the millimeter, and stood in the dark feeling the way he imagined composers felt — not guilty, never guilty, but *aware of the music*. He had taken two people's worst wounds and turned them into instruments, and the music they were making was keeping them both looking at each other instead of at him, and on Friday morning at nine o'clock, while whatever was going to happen between them happened, a judge would sign eleven acres into his name and the tide would come in on schedule.

He paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and allowed himself a final inventory. Evidence: contained. Sheriff: compromised. Coldharbours: divided, distracted, and legally outflanked. Town: fond of him. Program: funded, praised, and functioning as surveillance.

Every piece placed. Every player cast.

Almost every.

Because there was one variable he hadn't fully priced, and it nagged at him the way a wrong note nags at a composer — small, deniable, ignorable, and fatal if ignored. It was this: he had engineered the letters to distract them from each other's *threat*, and they were working. But letters, in his considerable experience, did something no engineer could fully control. Letters told the truth. And two people trading truths, even anonymously, even accidentally, even as enemies — two people trading truths eventually stopped needing anyone else's version of events.

Including his.

Harrow Voss locked the annex door behind him, stood a moment in the moths and the porch light, and made a quiet adjustment to his timeline. Not Friday. Sooner. If the letters accelerated — if the sheriff and the wedding planner got to the truth of each other before he got to the truth of the ledger — he'd need the annex vault emptied and the ledger moved by Wednesday night.

He got in his car. Somewhere across town, he knew, Jude Coldharbour was probably awake, hunched over photographs of forged signatures, and somewhere else Eli Marchetti was probably awake, hunched over a letter he hadn't decided whether to send, and the thought that kept Harrow company all the way home, pleasant as a nightcap, was this:

They had four days to find each other.

He'd give them three.
