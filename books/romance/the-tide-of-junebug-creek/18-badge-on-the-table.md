## 18. Badge on the Table

The ledger sat on the passenger seat of my truck like a sleeping animal I was afraid to wake.

I had wrapped it in a canvas tarp — old habit, evidence protocol — but it didn't help. Every time I stopped at a light I could feel it there, that weight that wasn't weight. Forty pages of names and dates and numbers written in Harrow Voss's careful hand, going back nine years. Nine years of quiet purchases, quiet favors, quiet men paid to make paperwork move faster than it should have. The original. Not a copy. The thing itself, pulled out of the annex at three in the morning while Jude held the flashlight and I bled through my shirt.

My ribs ached when I breathed deep. My left eye was still swollen enough that the mirror this morning showed me a stranger wearing half my face. Doc Prewitt had taped two of them and told me to take a week off, and I'd said sure, Sheriff, whatever you say, and driven straight home to sit at my kitchen table until dawn with a cup of coffee gone cold and the badge lying next to the salt shaker like it belonged there.

That's where it was now. In my shirt pocket. Over my heart, which is where they tell you to wear it, though nobody mentions how heavy it gets after eleven years, or how you stop noticing until the day you finally do.

I parked behind the Grange Hall at six forty-five. The council meeting wasn't until seven-thirty, but I wanted to watch them arrive. Old habit too — you learn more from how a man walks into a room than from anything he says in it.

They came in ones and twos under the sodium lights. Councilwoman Adele Ferris first, always early, carrying her binder like a shield. Then the Kettleman brothers, who farmed the north bend and hadn't spoken to each other since 2019 except through their lawyers. Then the townsfolk, dozens of them, more than usual — word travels fast in Junebug Creek, and what had happened at the annex had traveled fastest of all. I heard my name in three separate conversations before I even got out of the truck. *Sheriff Marchetti got jumped. Somebody broke into the county annex. They say Coldharbour's girl was there.*

*They* were right on both counts, and by morning everyone would know it anyway, so I'd made my peace with that part somewhere around four a.m., sitting in the dark, listening to the creek I couldn't see but could always hear if the wind came off the water right.

What I hadn't made peace with was the other thing. The thing I kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it.

Voss knew we'd been in the annex. He had to know — his fixers were there within twelve minutes of the silent alarm, which meant they'd been close, which meant they'd been waiting. And if he knew we'd been in the annex, he knew what we'd taken, because there was only one thing in that building worth sending three men with tire irons for. Which meant that by seven-thirty tonight, Harrow Voss would already have a plan for the ledger. Men like him don't panic. They *proceduralize*. By now he'd have talked to Judge Ansel or whoever else he owned, and there'd be a warrant drafted, an injunction filed, some clean legal instrument designed to make the ledger disappear into an evidence locker and never come out.

I could hand it to the state attorney general. I'd thought about that all night too. Mailing it to Tallahassee, letting the machinery grind. It was the *right* way. The way the manual says.

But I'd spent eleven years learning what machinery does in this county when the man who oils it sits on the town council. The machinery grinds slow, and meanwhile the final deed closes Friday, and Friday is not a concept the state attorney general understands. Friday is a concept that only one room in this world understands: a hot room full of folding chairs in a Grange Hall that smells like dust and decades of potluck suppers, where the people of Junebug Creek come to be told what's going to happen to them.

So I was going to tell them instead.

* * *

Jude found me by the side door at seven-ten.

She looked like she hadn't slept either, and she'd done what she always did about it — turned exhaustion into armor. Her hair was pinned up like she was heading into a boardroom, her blazer sharp enough to draw blood, and only her hands gave her away. She was holding a manila envelope against her chest with both arms, the way you hold something you're afraid will be taken.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Doc says I'll live."

"Docs lie." She glanced past me at the hall, at the light spilling out the propped door, at the sound of folding chairs scraping and voices layering. "Eli. Tell me again what you're doing, because I've been running scenarios all afternoon and every single one ends with you in handcuffs."

"That's one of the endings, yeah."

"Eli."

"I resigned," I said. "Paperwork's on the mayor's desk as of five o'clock. Effective the moment I open my mouth in there." I watched it land on her — the flinch she tried to hide, the quick calculation of everything that word costs. Eleven years. A pension. The only identity I'd had since I was twenty-six years old and too dumb to want anything else. "It's done, Jude. Don't make it harder than it has to be."

She was quiet a moment. Out across the lot, somebody's dog barked twice and quit. When she spoke again her voice had dropped into something rawer. "You know what I keep thinking? I keep thinking about the letter. Your third one, maybe. You wrote—" she stopped, and I watched her decide whether to say it, and then she said it anyway, because that was the whole point of us now, "—you wrote, *I'm tired of being the law's hands when the law won't look at its own face.* I read that sentence maybe forty times. I just didn't know I was reading my future."

"I didn't know I was writing it."

"No." Almost a smile. "Nobody ever does."

Inside, the meeting was called to order. Through the door I could see Voss at the council table, front and center, silver-haired and sun-tanned and radiating the particular warmth of a man who has never once doubted himself in public. He wore a pale linen suit. He was laughing at something Adele Ferris said. His eyes moved over the crowd the way mine used to move over a traffic stop — cataloguing, weighing, dismissing.

He saw me come in. I watched him see me. There was a half-second where something moved behind all that charm — not fear, nothing so honest as fear. Recalculation. A man adjusting a number in a column.

Then he smiled wider and gestured me forward like a friend. "Sheriff Marchetti! Good Lord, son, what happened to your face?" Loud enough for the whole room. Sympathetic enough to sting. "Somebody ought to catch whoever did that to you."

"Somebody did," I said. "Last night. At the county annex."

The room went still in that particular way small rooms do — a hundred people inhaling at once.

Voss's smile stayed exactly where it was. That was the thing about him; I'd finally understood it last night, bleeding on the annex floor. His smile wasn't an expression. It was infrastructure.

"I'm sure the sheriff's department is investigating," he said smoothly.

"The sheriff's department is resigning," I said, and took the badge out of my pocket, and set it down on the council table in front of him with a click that sounded, in that silence, like a cell door.

* * *

I'd rehearsed it in the truck. Not the words — I'd given up on words I could control. Just the mechanics. Where to stand. How loud. What to do with my hands. Because here's what nobody tells you about the moment you burn your own life down on purpose: your body doesn't believe you mean it. Your body keeps trying to protect the thing you're destroying. My hand actually shook reaching for the ledger, and I had to press it flat against the canvas until it remembered whose idea this was.

"This ledger came out of the county records annex," I said. "Original documents. Nine years of land transactions along Junebug Creek. Purchases routed through four shell companies — Bayou Light Holdings, Marshline Group, two others — all of them traceable back to a single account." I opened it. My voice carried better than I expected; the acoustics of a Grange Hall are built for exactly this, for one person telling a roomful of neighbors something they need to hear. "Every parcel bought below market. Every seller pressured, rushed, or misled. And on page thirty-one, gentlemen, ladies — the disbursement that paid Councilman Voss's campaign consultant, dated six days after the Kettleman north-bend sale closed."

Adele Ferris stood up so fast her chair went over backward. "This is out of order! This is a stolen document, this is—"

"It's not stolen," I said. "It's public record, improperly sealed. Which is a crime, by the way. Ask your counsel."

"There *is* no counsel present—"

"Then let's all be careful what we say."

And that's when it came apart, the way I'd known it would, the way I'd counted on. Because a room like this doesn't explode all at once. It detonates in sequence. The Kettleman brothers were on their feet shouting at each other — one of them had sold, one of them hadn't, and the ledger had just made one of them a victim and the other a fool. Old Mrs. Tally from the feed store was crying, loud, unashamed, saying her husband's name over and over like a prayer. Somebody near the back yelled *read it, Marchetti, read the whole damn thing,* and somebody else yelled that I had no authority anymore, and both of them were right.

Through all of it, Voss sat very still with his hands folded, watching me the way a chess player watches a piece that has just moved somewhere unexpected. And when the noise crested, he raised one hand — just slightly, the gesture of a man calming children — and the room, God help us, *quieted*, because eleven years of charm buys you that.

"Sheriff," he said gently, "you were assaulted last night. You're concussed, clearly emotional, and standing in front of these good people waving papers nobody can verify. I understand you're hurting. But this stunt—" he spread his hands, sorrowful, magnificent, "—this stunt looks a great deal like a desperate man trying to explain away a burglary he committed himself. Along with his accomplice." His eyes found the back of the room, found her, and the whole hall turned like a tide to follow them.

Jude stood by the door with the manila envelope hugged to her chest, chin up, absolutely still.

"Ms. Coldharbour," Voss said warmly. "Welcome. I'd ask what brings you, but I think we can guess." He turned back to the table. "Adele, I move we refer this matter to the district court first thing tomorrow, and that the sheriff and his accomplice be detained pending—"

"Detained?" That was Denny Coldharbour's voice, from the middle of the room, and I felt the whole hall shift again, because everybody in Junebug Creek knew Denny's history and everybody knew what that word meant coming out of his mouth. He stood up slow, big hands opening and closing. "You want to talk about detaining Coldharbours, Voss? Let's talk about it. Let's talk about all of it, right now, in front of everybody—"

"Denny." Jude's voice, low, cutting across the room like a blade through cloth. "Not yet."

But it was too late to unring. The room was a boil now, chairs scraping, voices stacking, Adele Ferris pounding a gavel that nobody respected, and above it all Voss rising at last, unfolding to his full height, pointing one manicured finger at me and then at Jude and saying the words I'd known were coming since the annex floor:

"Sheriff Marchetti, you are relieved and you are *finished*. Ms. Coldharbour, you and your brother are under citizen's arrest for burglary, trespass, and theft of county records, effective immediately, and I'd advise everyone in this room to remember that the law in this county still has teeth—"

"—whose law?" 

It was Mrs. Tally. Small, wet-eyed, ancient, standing there in her feed-store apron with her fist clenched around a hankie, and her voice cracked the room open wider than any gavel ever had. "Whose law, Harrow? Yours? The one that took Ray's shop? The one that sent letters to my granddaughter telling her she had thirty days?"

And then — I'll write this down exactly as it happened, because someday someone should — the doors at the back banged open and Deputy Ana Reyes walked in, in uniform, and set a box of files down on the nearest chair, and said, clear as a bell: "Councilman Voss. I've been copying annex records for three weeks. I'd like to add my statement to the sheriff's."

Behind her, filing in, came the Kettleman who hadn't sold. Came Tomás from the boatyard. Came half the congregation of St. Brigid's and a man I'd arrested twice and a woman I'd once written a warning ticket at two in the morning and let drive home. They didn't shout. They just kept coming in and standing there, filling the hall wall to wall, faces I'd spent eleven years protecting and failing and protecting again, until Voss looked out at all of them and I watched the arithmetic finally fail behind his eyes.

I picked the ledger back up.

"Page thirty-one," I said, and began to read.

* * *

They tried to take us, afterward. Of course they did. Voss produced a printed affidavit — printed, I noted, on paper still warm from some office machine, prepared hours before I ever walked in, which Ana Reyes quietly photographed with her phone while he thrust it around — and two men he introduced as "county process servers" moved toward Jude and me with the confidence of men who'd done this kind of errand before.

They got exactly four steps.

Because Denny Coldharbour stepped between them and his sister, and then Tomás stepped up beside Denny, and then Mrs. Tally, hankie and all, planted herself in front of me like a fence post, and the aisle filled, and the two process servers looked at the wall of Junebug Creek looking back at them and decided, wisely, to serve their papers another day.

Voss gathered his linen suit around him and paused at the door long enough to deliver the exit line he'd clearly been saving: "This isn't over, Marchetti. Courts don't run on sentiment."

"No," I agreed. "They run on evidence. And I've got the original."

For the first time all night — maybe for the first time in the nine years I'd known him — Harrow Voss had no smile ready. He looked at the ledger in my hands, at the crowd between him and it, at Ana Reyes writing something down, and something in his face went old and gray. Then he pushed through the doors into the dark, and the hall erupted behind him — cheering, crying, the Kettleman brothers shaking hands like the war was over, Adele Ferris quietly asking Ana for a copy of everything.

Jude reached me last. The hall was still roaring around us and she didn't say anything at all, just put her hand flat against my chest, over the place where the badge used to sit, over the bruised ribs underneath, and left it there.

"Friday," she said quietly. "The deed still closes Friday unless we stop it."

Above the din, at the front of the hall, Denny Coldharbour climbed onto the stage, took the microphone from its stand, and tapped it once. Twice. The room began, slowly, to hush.

"My name's Denny," he said, "and I've got something to say about the night Sheriff Marchetti arrested me. And y'all are gonna want to hear it."
