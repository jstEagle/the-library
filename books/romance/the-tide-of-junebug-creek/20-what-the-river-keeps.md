## 20. What the River Keeps

**Eli**

The review board met in the county annex, in a room that smelled like pencil shavings and old carpet, and Eli Marchetti sat at a folding table under a fluorescent hum and answered forty-one questions about the worst and best night of his career.

He had rehearsed none of them. That was the strange part. For twenty years he had rehearsed everything — traffic stops, domestic calls, the careful choreography of a man whose word had to hold in a town where everyone knew his grandmother's maiden name. But when the board asked him why he had resigned his badge in front of two hundred people rather than let a sealed ledger disappear into a councilman's filing cabinet, he found he didn't need a script.

"Because the badge is a promise," he said. "And I wasn't keeping it."

The woman across the table, an investigator from the state attorney general's office named Okafor, wrote something down. She had been writing things down for three hours. Once, she had looked up and said, quietly, "You understand you'll almost certainly not be reinstated."

"I understand," Eli had said, and found the sentence weighed nothing at all. That was the strangest part. He had carried that badge so long he'd assumed it was load-bearing. It turned out the load had been something else entirely.

They asked about the annex. They asked about the blood on the floor — his, mostly, though the lab report had found his prints on a window latch and a doorframe and, absurdly, a filing cabinet drawer he had no memory of touching. They asked about Jude Coldharbour, and here Eli paused for the first time, not because the answer was hard but because it was easy, and easy answers had gotten him into trouble before.

"She had evidence. It was stolen from her office. She asked for my help getting it back. I gave it."

"You were, at that time, the sitting sheriff of Calloway County."

"I was."

"And you chose to commit a burglary."

Eli looked at his hands on the table. There was a scar on the left knuckle from the annex door, still healing, still tender when it rained. He thought about how to say it in a way that would fit on a form.

"I chose to keep a promise," he said. "The burglary was incidental."

Okafor almost smiled. He saw it start and die, professional discipline winning out, and he thought — not for the first time that week — that the whole apparatus of the law was just people trying very hard not to feel things on the record.

They dismissed him at four. Outside, the September light was doing that thing it did in late afternoon, turning the courthouse lawn gold and long-shadowed, and Eli stood on the steps with no radio on his hip and no cruiser in the lot assigned to him and felt, for one disorienting moment, like a man who had walked out of his own life and forgotten to take anything with him.

Then his phone buzzed.

*The creek. Six o'clock. Bring paper. — J*

No signature. There didn't need to be. There was a time — he could measure it now, weeks, a whole season — when unsigned letters had been the only honest thing in his life. He was learning that the signed ones could be honest too. It just took practice.

**Jude**

Jude Coldharbour had planned two hundred and seventeen weddings, and she could tell you, to the minute, how long a bride's nerve lasted before the vows. Forty minutes, on average. Long enough to fix a hem, short enough that the trembling hadn't started. She had built a career on the knowledge that love, properly scheduled, was manageable.

Permanence was another matter.

She had spent the afternoon in her office — repaired door, new lock, the faint smell of paint where Voss's men had splintered the frame — doing the thing she did when she was scared, which was making lists. *Florist: call Marta. Venue: the creek, obviously, the north bank where the willows came down to the water. Guest list: smaller than any I've ever done. Dress: God help me.*

The list kept snagging on one line she hadn't written and couldn't stop seeing: *forever, unstructured, no exit.*

Because that was the truth she hadn't said out loud yet, not to Eli, not to Denny, not to the pen pal who had become the man and the man who had become the pen pal. She had learned to lend her heart in installments. An engagement here, a first year there, interest paid in tenderness and principal withdrawn at the first sign of default. Her marriage had taught her that. The courthouse, the lawyer with the kind eyes who turned out to have kinder ones for someone else, the way a promise could be notarized and still be worth nothing — she had taken all of it and built a creed, and the creed had kept her safe for nine years, and then a man she'd hated had started writing her letters under a false name and the creed had come apart like wet paper.

She wasn't scared of Eli. She was scared of the thing after Eli. The Tuesday mornings. The growing old. The part where you stopped performing and just *were*, witnessed, every day, by someone who knew where the bodies were buried — and in her case, that was nearly literal, given what they'd dug out of the county records together.

Denny found her at the window at five-thirty, holding a list and not writing on it.

"You're doing the face," he said.

"What face."

"The one where you're planning a wedding like it's a hostage negotiation." He leaned against the doorframe. He looked better than he had in years — the trial testimony had taken something out of him and given something back, some load set down after a decade of carrying it. "You know you don't have to plan it, right? You could just... have one."

"I don't know how to just have one."

"I know." He came over and took the list out of her hand, gently, the way you'd take a knife from someone who didn't realize they were holding it. "That's kind of the point of him, though. Isn't it?"

She looked at her brother. The man Eli had arrested on a charge that never stuck, carrying something that night that was neither what Eli thought nor nothing. The man who had stood up in a town meeting and said, in front of everyone, *I lied, and here's why, and the sheriff was wrong about the what and right about the something.* It had taken Denny ten years to say it and thirty seconds, and the whole room had changed shape around the saying.

"Go," Denny said. "He's waiting. He always waits. It's the most annoying thing about him."

"It's the best thing about him."

"Yeah," Denny said, handing back the list with only one line added at the bottom, in his terrible handwriting: *stop planning. start living.* "Go on."

* * *

**Eli**

The north bank of Junebug Creek was where it had started, or where it had started to start — the willows, the flat stone Jude called the preaching rock because her grandfather had supposedly proposed on it, the water moving the way creek water moves in early fall, low and clear and patient. Eli got there first, which was also the most annoying thing about him, and sat on the bank with two sheets of paper in his jacket, feeling like a man about to defuse something.

She came down the path at ten past six, and his chest did the thing it always did now, the untrained thing, the thing he had stopped trying to train out of it.

"You look like you're awaiting sentencing," she said.

"I've had a lot of practice lately."

"How was it? Today. The board."

"Long." He patted the grass beside him and she sat, close, her shoulder against his, the contact easy now in a way that still astonished him. "Okafor asked me why I did it. The annex. I told her the burglary was incidental."

Jude laughed — a real one, surprised out of her. "What did she write down?"

"Something. Probably 'subject remains a smartass.'"

"Probably 'subject is telling the truth, which is disorienting.'" She pulled her knees up. The creek talked to itself over the stones. "Did they say? Reinstatement?"

"They didn't say. They'll take weeks." He watched the water. "I keep waiting to feel worse about it than I do."

"And you don't."

"I don't. I keep checking. Like a tooth. Nope. Still fine." He turned to look at her. "You?"

"Me what?"

"You've got the list face. Denny told me about the list face."

Jude made the list face. "He's a traitor and I'm disowning him."

"Jude."

"I'm scared," she said, to the creek, because it was easier than saying it to him. "Not of you. Of after. Of the part where there's no event to plan and it's just — Tuesdays. Forever. And I can't schedule my way out of being known." She hugged her knees. "I've spent nine years lending my heart out in installments. You're asking for the whole thing, and I keep finding rooms in it I didn't know were there, and some of them are a mess, Eli, some of them I haven't opened since the courthouse—"

"Jude."

"—and you'll see all of it, and one day you'll do the math like my husband did, and—"

"Jude." He said it the way he'd learned to say it in the boathouse, in the rain, with her shaking and the storm coming down: not to interrupt, but to anchor. "I arrested your brother on a charge that didn't stick, and you married a man who betrayed you in a building with a flag outside, and we both did our worst damage in the name of doing things right. And I'm still here. You're still here. Whatever's in the rooms, I've got time."

She was quiet a long moment. The creek filled the quiet, the way it always had, the way it kept everything — her grandfather's proposal, Denny's childhood, the forged deeds, the night she'd stood on this bank and decided to fight, the night Eli had stood on this bank and decided to quit. Rivers kept things. That was the whole secret of them.

"I brought paper," she said finally. "Like I said."

"I figured. I brought paper too."

* * *

They wrote in the last of the light, sitting a few feet apart on the flat stone, not showing each other, which felt absurd and necessary at once. Eli wrote fast, because he'd learned with her that the fast words were the true ones and the slow ones were the edited ones.

*Dear Jude Coldharbour,* he wrote. *Signed this time. I've been practicing your name in my head for months under a different set of rules and it turns out it's better out loud. I don't know what I'll be after the board decides. I don't know if I'll be a sheriff or a security consultant or the guy who reads the ledger at town meetings for the rest of his natural life. I know I was a better man in your letters than I was in my uniform, and I'd like to be him with my name attached. I know that when you said "the whole heart" in the boathouse you were talking about yours, but I want you to know you have mine, all of it, the rooms that are a mess and the ones I haven't opened either. I was trained my whole life to keep things sealed. You're the first unsealed thing I ever trusted. — Eli Marchetti. Sheriff, retired, pending, whatever. Yours.*

Jude, beside him, wrote slower, crossing things out, and he watched her do it and didn't mind, because watching Jude think was one of the great privileges of his life and he intended to say so in about four minutes.

When they finished, they traded pages the way they'd traded letters for a season — folded, handed over, held at a distance — and then read.

She read his twice. He watched her do it, watched her mouth move slightly on the second pass, watched her press the paper flat against her knee like she was afraid it would blow away.

"Yours," she said. "You signed *yours.*"

"I did. It's accurate."

She handed him her page. Her handwriting was fierce, slanted, the handwriting of someone who wrote fast when it mattered:

*Dear Eli Marchetti, whose name I now get to use in sentences. I planned two hundred and seventeen weddings and believed in none of them, and then you wrote me a letter about not trusting your own instincts, and I believed that, instantly, completely, and it ruined everything. Here's my confession, since we're doing those: I'm not scared of loving you. I'm scared of being loved on a Tuesday. But I talked to the creek, and the creek says it doesn't keep the good days only — it keeps all of them, the flood years and the dry ones, and it's still the creek. So. Whole heart. No installments. No exit clauses. You get the mess rooms and the sealed ones and the ones I haven't found yet, and I get yours, and we'll be terrified together on Tuesdays, which I'm told is what marriage actually is. — Jude Coldharbour. Wedding planner, retired, pending, whatever. Yours.*

Eli read it twice. On the second pass his eyes did something embarrassing and he didn't fight it, because he was done fighting things.

"Retired, pending, whatever," he said.

"It's a transitional period."

"Jude." He took her hand. Her fingers were ink-stained and cold from the evening air and he held them like something that had been entrusted to him, because it had. "Ask me."

She looked at him. The light was almost gone, the willows black against the last copper band of sky, the creek keeping everything below them in its patient dark.

"Eli Marchetti," she said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised them both. "Will you marry me? On this bank, in front of this whole ridiculous town, on a day I will inevitably over-plan and you will inevitably let me?"

He laughed, and it broke open into something else, and he said, "Yes. On one condition."

"Name it."

"That I get to write you letters anyway. Signed. Forever. Even when I'm in the next room."

Jude Coldharbour, who had stopped believing in marriage in a courthouse nine years ago, who had built a creed out of wreckage and lived in it like armor, looked at the man the river had kept for her all along, and smiled the smile that had no training in it at all.

"Deal," she said. "But fair warning — I'm going to plan it like a hostage negotiation."

"I'd expect nothing less."

They sealed it the old way, the way the letters had taught them: folded pages, exchanged, kept. And downstream, the creek went on keeping everything — the deeds and the confessions, the flood years and the dry ones — already holding the date she would set, the dress she hadn't chosen, and the small imperfect wedding that was coming, whether the bride's nerve lasted forty minutes or not at all.
