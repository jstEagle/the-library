## 3. Ninety Days

**Callum**

The county records office smelled like toner and old coffee, and Callum Reyes had been standing in front of the deed for eleven minutes before he let himself read it a second time.

The clerk—a woman named Dot with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the unimpressed expression of someone who had processed forty years of other people's disasters—had already explained it twice. He'd heard her. He just hadn't believed her.

"Joint tenancy," she said again, tapping the document with one lacquered nail. "Both names. Both signatures required to sell, both signatures required to keep. That's what the transfer says."

*Hollow Orchard — 61 acres — transferred from Elias Fairweather to Marisol Fairweather and Callum Reyes.*

His name looked wrong there. It sat next to hers like a stranger at a funeral, wearing a suit he didn't own.

"It's a mistake," he said. "It has to be. Elias Fairweather would sooner have deeded that orchard to the devil than to me."

Dot shrugged with the whole architecture of her shoulders. "Mistakes happen. But this one's notarized, witnessed, and filed eight months before Mr. Fairweather passed. If you want it corrected, that's a petition, a filing fee, and a judge." She slid a form across the counter. "Or you two work it out between yourselves. Ninety days from notice of the error to settle title or the county can entertain third-party claims. And Mr. Reyes—" her eyes flicked up, knowing, "—there's a gentleman who's been in here twice this month asking after exactly that parcel. Pell. Ambrose Pell."

The name landed in Callum's chest like a dropped anchor.

"I know," he said.

Of course he knew. Ambrose Pell had found him three days ago, in the rented room above Hollis Hardware where Callum lived out of two duffel bags and a box of books. Pell had stood in his doorway without being invited in—smart man—and made an offer so clean and so insulting that Callum had almost admired it. Full market value for whatever interest Callum held or might come to hold in the Hollow Orchard. Cash. Fast. *No fuss,* Pell had said, smiling with all the warmth of a man reading a menu. *You've had enough fuss in your life, Deputy.*

*I'm not a deputy anymore,* Callum had told him.

*No,* Pell had agreed pleasantly. *You're not.*

He hadn't taken the offer. He told himself it was because something about Pell's certainty—the way he talked about the orchard like it was already his—had raised every hackle Callum owned. But underneath that, in the place where he kept the truths he didn't look at directly, was another reason, simpler and more dangerous:

Marisol Fairweather's name was on that deed. And if Callum signed anything, anything at all, she would hear about it within the hour, and she would look at him the way she'd looked at him at Danny's funeral—like he was a door closing.

Eight years since they'd spoken more than four words. Eight years of nodding across the diner, of taking the long way around the square, of living in the same small town like two planets in different orbits, careful never to collide.

Now the county had lassoed them together and called it property law.

"When does the ninety days start?" he asked.

Dot checked a ledger. "Notice went out Monday. So—" she counted on her fingers, though she clearly already knew, "—you have until the first week of October. Settle it between you, sell it together, or watch Mr. Pell pick up the pieces."

Callum folded the form into quarters, then eighths, until it was a hard little square in his fist.

"Is she coming in today?" he asked, before he could stop himself. "Ms. Fairweather."

Dot's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "She's been here since nine."

**Marisol**

Marisol Fairweather had read the letter fourteen times, and each time her grandfather's name at the bottom of the transfer felt less like a signature and more like a sentence.

*To my granddaughter Marisol, and to Callum Reyes, jointly and equally, the Hollow Orchard, its trees, its soil, and everything rooted in both.*

Everything rooted in both. She'd memorized the phrase without meaning to. It kept surfacing when she tried to think about anything else—while she'd driven the coast road into town, while she'd parked under the sycamore outside the records office, while she'd sat in her truck for twenty minutes rehearsing how she would behave if Callum Reyes walked through that door.

She had decided on: civil. Brief. Businesslike. A woman who kept a lighthouse and wrote novels nobody knew about could certainly manage ninety days of paperwork with a man she hadn't spoken to since the worst day of both their lives.

Then the door opened, and the bell above it rang, and every speech she'd prepared evaporated.

He looked older. That was the first stupid observation, the kind her heroine would make, and Marisol hated herself for making it. Older, broader through the shoulders, with new lines around his eyes that hadn't come from laughing. His hair was shorter than it had been at nineteen, when Danny used to wrestle him into the creek behind the orchard and Callum would come up dripping and grinning, and she—

She cut the thought off at the root. Pruned it clean, the way her grandfather had taught her: *cut back to the bud, Mari, or the tree spends itself trying to heal what you won't let go.*

"Fairweather," Callum said.

"Reyes."

Dot, behind the counter, watched them with the open enjoyment of a woman who had no television at home.

"The deed," Marisol said, because someone had to say something. "It names us both."

"So I'm told."

"You sound surprised."

"I am surprised." He came closer, stopping a careful six feet away, as if proximity were a hazard he'd been trained to assess. "Elias didn't trust me to borrow a ladder. Why would he leave me half his orchard?"

That stung more than it should have, mostly because it was true. Her grandfather had been polite to Callum Reyes for eight years in the way you're polite to weather—acknowledging its existence, keeping your distance, never turning your back.

"I don't know," Marisol said. "Maybe it's a clerical error. Maybe the county typed the wrong name off some old list."

"Dot says it's notarized."

"Dot says a lot of things."

Behind the counter, Dot said nothing at all, which somehow managed to be loud.

Marisol set the letter on the counter and smoothed it flat with both hands, an old habit, pressing wrinkles out of pages the way she pressed them out of drafts. "Here's what I propose. We file the correction petition. Whatever mistake put your name on this gets fixed, the orchard stays mine, and you never have to think about Maple Hollow land—or me—again."

There. Civil. Brief. Businesslike.

Callum looked at the letter, then at her, and something moved through his face too fast for her to name it. Not anger. Something rawer.

"That's your proposal," he said.

"That's my proposal."

"Took you eight years to say five sentences to me, and those are the five."

The words hit harder than shouting would have. The office seemed to contract around them—the shelves of bound volumes, the humming photocopier, the dusty light through the blinds striping the floor like fence rails.

"My grandfather is dead," Marisol said quietly. "I don't owe anyone a conversation I don't want to have."

"No," Callum agreed. "You don't." He pulled the folded form from his jacket, unfolded it, refolded it along a different crease. "But here's my problem, Fairweather. Somebody wants that orchard, and it isn't either of us. Pell's been to see me. Has he been to see you?"

The question knocked her off balance. "Pell? No."

"He will." Callum's jaw worked. "And if we spend our ninety days filing petitions against each other, we're going to hand him the parcel gift-wrapped. So maybe—*maybe*—the smart play isn't deciding in the first ten minutes whose fault this all is."

"Whose fault what is?"

"You know what."

"I really don't," Marisol said, and heard her own voice go cold and precise, the voice she used on editors. "Enlighten me, Reyes. What exactly do you think happened eight years ago?"

For a moment she thought he would answer. Something gathered in him—she saw it in the tendons of his hands, in the way his breath changed—and then he looked away, toward the window, toward the street, and let it go like a man setting down something heavy on the far side of a wall.

"Nothing," he said. "Forget it. Let's just get copies of everything and figure this out. Separately."

"Separately," she repeated. "Fine."

They moved to the photocopier together, because there was only one, because the universe had a sense of humor, and stood shoulder to shoulder in its green fluorescent glow while Dot fed the original deed into the tray.

The machine whirred. Light swept under the glass like something swimming.

"Your copy first," Callum said.

"Yours."

"Take it."

"I said *yours*—"

"For God's sake, Marisol, take the damn copy."

Her name in his mouth. It had been eight years, and he still said it the same way—not *Mari*, the way everyone else in town said it, not even *Ms. Fairweather*, but *Marisol*, all four syllables, like he was handling something with edges.

She took the copy. Their fingers didn't touch, but the air between their hands did something electric and dishonest, and she snatched the page back fast enough that the machine's output tray rattled.

"Ninety days," she said, to the copier, to the room, to the whole unjust arrangement of things. "We settle this in ninety days and never have to stand in a room together again."

"Never's a long time in a town this size."

"Then we'll perfect the art of avoiding each other. You've had practice."

It was cruel and she knew it the moment it left her, and worse, she watched it land—watched the flinch move through him like a current through wire. For one naked second he looked nineteen again, standing in the rain outside the church after the funeral, saying *I'm sorry, Mari, I'm so sorry* while she stood silent because silence was the only weapon she had that wouldn't break her open.

"I have," he said quietly. "Practice. Yeah."

The copier finished. Dot handed over the last warm copy with the ceremony of a woman dealing cards.

"Anything else?" Dot asked. "Certified copies run extra."

"No," Marisol said.

"No," Callum said, at the same moment, and then neither of them moved, both trapped for one absurd heartbeat in the same sentence, the same rhythm, the same terrible muscle memory of two people who once finished each other's thoughts.

Marisol left first. She made herself walk, not run, down the courthouse steps and across the square, the deed copy folded against her ribs like contraband. It wasn't until she reached her truck that she let herself look back.

Callum was still standing at the counter, talking to Dot, his head bent, his shoulders carrying weight she remembered from a different lifetime. As she watched, he laughed at something the old woman said—a short, startled sound, like a man remembering a language he'd been forbidden to speak.

She got in the truck. She gripped the wheel. She recited her creed the way she recited it every morning, the way she'd built it brick by brick over eight years: *hearts are muscles, muscles can be trained, train yours to want nothing.*

The words had never sounded so much like a lie.

Behind her, the records office door opened and closed. Footsteps on the pavement, unhurried, familiar in a way that made her chest ache. Then Callum's voice, just beside her window, low:

"Fairweather. One more thing."

She rolled the window down two inches. Enough for words. Not enough for anything else.

"What."

"Pell offered me cash for my half. This morning, before I came in." His eyes were steady on hers, and there was something underneath the wariness that she refused to identify. "I turned him down. I wanted you to hear it from me and not from the diner."

The engine ticked. Somewhere down the square, a dog barked at nothing.

"Why?" she asked. "Why turn it down?"

Callum stepped back from the truck, hands in his pockets, and gave her a ghost of the grin she remembered from the creek, from summers, from before—except this one didn't reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Because whoever engineered this mess wanted me gone easy," he said. "And I've been run out of one life already. I'll be damned if I get run off this land twice."

He turned and walked away before she could answer—which was just as well, because Marisol Fairweather, keeper of lighthouses, author of four published novels, trainer of hearts, had absolutely nothing to say.

She sat in the truck until her hands stopped shaking, and then she drove home along the coast road with the deed riding against her ribs, and all she could think, all the way past the point and around the bend where the lighthouse rose white against the gray water, was:

*Ninety days. Ninety days, and then I never have to feel this again.*

She already knew, in the place where she kept the truths she didn't look at directly, that she was lying.
