## 13. Witness

The address came to us the way most truths do in Maple Hollow—secondhand, smudged, and half a rumor. A retired clerk named Edith Vann, who had spent thirty years behind the records office counter and knew where every body was buried because she'd filed half the paperwork herself, had pressed a scrap of paper into Callum's hand on Tuesday. On it, in faded ballpoint: *Walter Okafor. Mile marker 41, past the dunes. Blue door.*

"He doesn't like visitors," she'd said, not looking up from her ledger. "But he liked your grandfather, Marisol Fairweather. That might count for something."

So on a Thursday morning with the sky the color of unpolished pewter, we took my truck down the coast road, past the last of the town's streetlights, past the point where the pavement gave up and became gravel, past everything familiar until there was nothing left but dune grass bending in long silver strokes and the gray Atlantic hammering itself flat against the shore.

Callum rode in the passenger seat with the case file open on his knees. He'd barely spoken since we left the lighthouse. I could feel him reading the same pages over and over, the way you touch a bruise to check if it still hurts.

"Say it," he finally said, eyes still down. "Whatever you're thinking. You've been doing that thing since the bridge."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you compose a whole paragraph and then decide I can't handle it."

I kept my eyes on the road. The wipers thumped against a fine salt mist that wasn't quite rain. "I was thinking that Walter Okafor signed the original fire marshal's report," I said. "And that the altered version has his signature too. And that either he lied twelve years ago, or someone made him complicit, and either way we're driving three hours to ask a stranger to confess."

"That's it? That's the paragraph?"

"And I was thinking," I said, before I could stop myself, "that if he says no, we have nothing. Pell's option contract is voidable only if the conviction is vacated, and the conviction stands unless the report is discredited, and the report is *his* report." I exhaled. "We're betting everything on one old man's conscience."

Callum closed the file. When I glanced over, he was looking out at the water, jaw tight, hands folded over the folder as if holding something shut.

"Then we'd better be convincing," he said.

* * *

The cabin sat alone at the end of a track that wound through wind-bent pines, and it was exactly what I'd expected and nothing like it at all. I'd pictured something weathered and defeated, a man hiding from his own history. Instead it was small and fiercely tidy—cedar shingles gone soft silver, gutters clean, a woodpile stacked with geometric precision under the eave. The door was blue, a deep harbor blue, freshly painted. Someone here was not giving up. Someone here was tending things.

Callum knocked. No answer. He knocked again, harder, and from somewhere behind the house came the sound of a saw stopping mid-stroke.

"Nobody's home," said a voice, gravel-deep, before its owner rounded the corner—a broad-shouldered Black man in his seventies, sawdust clinging to his flannel shirt, a hand saw resting across his shoulder like it belonged there. His eyes went from Callum to me and settled on me with an expression I couldn't read. "You're too tall to be selling anything and too young to be death. So which Fairweather sent you?"

"None," I said. "My grandfather's dead. Elias Fairweather. I'm Marisol."

Something moved through his face then—slow, like weather changing. He lowered the saw.

"Elias," he repeated. "Lord." He looked out toward the water for a moment, the way people do when they need a second to arrange themselves. Then: "You'd better come in. Coffee's old but it's honest."

* * *

Inside, the cabin smelled of cedar and coffee and lamp oil. There were books everywhere—not arranged so much as accumulated, stacked on shelves and chairs and windowsills, spines cracked with use. On the wall above the stove hung a single framed thing: not a certificate, not a photo, but a child's crayon drawing of a fire truck, signed *For Mr. Walt* in wobbling letters. I found myself staring at it while he poured three cups without asking whether we wanted any.

He set the cups down and sat across from us, folding his big hands on the table, and waited. It was the waiting of a man who had spent a career asking questions and knew how to make silence do the work.

"We need to talk about the Hollow warehouse fire," Callum said. "Twelve years ago. Your report."

"I know why you're here." Okafor's voice was even. "Edith called me Tuesday night. First time in six years she's used my number." He turned to me. "Before your young man says another word—I want to hear it from you. Why does a Fairweather care about a conviction that ruined nobody in her family?"

"It ruined somebody she—" Callum started.

"I asked her." Still even. Still patient. The patience of a man who had testified in courtrooms and knew that the truth, given room, would walk forward on its own legs.

I wrapped my hands around the hot cup. "My grandfather forged a deed transfer three days after the fire," I said. "To give Callum half the orchard. To give him something to come back to. Because my grandfather believed you got the wrong man." I met Okafor's eyes. "And because I believed it too, and I never said so out loud, and a man lost twelve years to everyone else's silence. Including mine. I'm here because I'm done being quiet about it."

The old man studied me for a long moment. Then he looked at Callum, and something in his gaze shifted—the look of a man re-weighing evidence he'd already weighed a hundred times.

"You want to know if I falsified that report," he said. "No. I didn't. But I signed a version of it that wasn't mine." He rose, joints cracking, and went to a bookshelf—not for a book, but for a metal document box that had been standing behind a row of paperbacks like a soldier hidden among civilians. He set it on the table between our cold cups.

"My original report ran forty-one pages," he said. "Origin and cause: electrical, panel box, third-floor junction. Documented. Photographed. Witnessed by two junior men who've since scattered to Florida and God knows where. Two weeks later I'm called into the county office and handed a revised version—origin undetermined, accelerant residue noted, and a name attached to the accelerant finding that hadn't appeared anywhere in my investigation." He tapped the box. "In there is my draft. My notes. My photographs, undeveloped negatives, and a memo I wrote the day after I was made to sign, addressed to no one, because there was no one to send it to."

"Why did you sign?" Callum's voice was rough.

Okafor sat back down. For the first time, he looked tired—not old, but tired, the specific exhaustion of carrying something heavy for a very long time.

"Because the revision came with a conversation," he said. "Not a threat. Nothing so crude. A friendly man from the county attorney's office explained to me that my findings were 'inconsistent with the investigative needs of the county,' that my pension review was coming up, that these things go easier when everyone pulls in the same direction. And he mentioned—casually, the way you'd mention the weather—that the Reyes boy's father had been a difficult man, and difficult men attract difficult attention, and wouldn't it be kinder to everyone if the matter resolved cleanly." He smiled, and it was the saddest smile I've ever seen. "I was fifty-eight years old. I had a wife going through treatment at St. Mary's. I told myself I'd fight it later. Later is a country you never reach, son. You just keep telling yourself the road exists."

"Who was the friendly man?" I asked.

"Hollis Pell." He said the name plainly. "Ambrose Pell's uncle. Then-chair of the county commission. Dead now, God rest him, though I notice his nephew inherited both the money and the methods." He opened the document box and drew out a thin folder, yellowed at the edges, and laid it on the table with both hands, almost ceremonially. "This is my sworn statement. Every fact in it is documented. I'll testify to all of it."

Callum stared at the folder like it might dissolve. Twelve years of weight, sitting on a pine table between three coffee cups. I watched his throat work. He didn't say thank you. He couldn't yet. Some gratitude is too large for the mouth.

"There's a condition," Okafor said. "Not from me. From reality." He leaned forward. "The hearing on this deed matter—it goes before Judge Merrick Weatherall. Hollis Pell's golf partner. A man who once ruled in Hollis's favor in a case where Hollis was *representing himself*. Ambrose Pell will have requested that calendar assignment personally, and Weatherall will have granted it before your paperwork was dry." He looked at each of us in turn. "I've testified maybe two hundred times in my career. I know how testimony dies. It doesn't die from lies. It dies from procedure—from a judge sustaining objections until the jury forgets your name, from scheduling that buries your witness after the story's already written. Weatherall won't need to rule against you. He'll just need to manage the room."

"So we're too late," Callum said quietly. "It's already fixed."

"I didn't say that." Okafor gathered his cups, carried them to the sink, and stood with his back to us, washing them slowly. "I said you're borrowing time. There's a difference. Borrowed time can still be spent well—but you have to spend it fast, and you have to spend it loud." He turned around, drying his hands. "A quiet hearing is a dead hearing. If it happens in a half-empty courtroom on a Tuesday morning with no press and no public, Weatherall can do whatever he likes and answer to no one. But packed rooms make careful judges. Sunlight makes careful judges. Your job—both of yours—is to make sure that when I walk into that courtroom, there are enough eyes in it that Weatherall can't afford to blink wrong."

Outside, the light had begun to fail, the pewter sky deepening toward slate. We stood by the truck while Okafor walked us as far as his gate, sawdust still on his sleeves, the blue door bright behind him.

"Your grandfather," he said to me suddenly, "once drove out here—must be eight years ago now. Sat on my porch and drank bad coffee and didn't ask me for anything. Just talked about grafting, of all things. How you can cut a limb from one tree and bind it to another, and if the cambium layers meet, they grow together. One tree bearing two kinds of fruit." He looked at me with those patient courtroom eyes. "I thought about that for a long time afterward. I think he came to tell me, without saying it, that it wasn't too late for things to take root again. Tell me, Miss Fairweather—when's the hearing?"

"We don't know yet. The clerk's office won't post the calendar until—"

"Find out tomorrow. First thing." He gripped the gate latch. "Because if Pell controls the calendar, the first weapon he'll use is speed. He'll want that hearing held before you're ready, before witnesses are noticed, before anyone can object. Watch the docket like a hawk watches a field. The moment it appears, you'll have days, not weeks." He opened the gate. "Borrowed time, Miss Fairweather. Spend it loud."

We drove back along the darkening coast with the folder riding in a plastic sleeve on my lap, and neither of us spoke for a long time. The headlights carved a tunnel through the mist. Somewhere behind us, an old man stood at his gate until we were out of sight; I felt it the way you feel a hand between your shoulder blades.

It was Callum who finally broke the silence, his voice low, almost wondering.

"Forty-one pages," he said. "He kept forty-one pages for twelve years." He shook his head slowly. "All that time I thought I was the only one who knew it was wrong."

"You weren't the only one," I said. "You were just the only one who paid for it."

He turned to look at me then, and in the dashboard glow his face was unreadable and wide open at once—and whatever he saw in mine made him turn back to the windshield, and reach over, briefly, and rest his hand over mine on the gearshift. Three seconds. Maybe four. Then gone, as if it had never happened, except that my hand burned the rest of the way home.

We reached Maple Hollow near ten. The lighthouse beam swept its slow circle over the harbor, over the orchard's black sleeping rows, over the town where a man with a judge in his pocket was, at that very hour, perhaps making a phone call. Tomorrow we would find out how much borrowed time we had left.

The answer came before breakfast.

At 6:47 a.m., my phone lit the dark kitchen: an email from the clerk's office, cc'd to every party of record. *Notice of Hearing: In re Deed Transfer, Hollow Orchard parcel. The Honorable M. Weatherall presiding. Scheduled nine days from today, 9:00 a.m., County Courthouse.*

Nine days. Not the ninety we'd been promised. Nine days—enough to notify a witness, not enough to prepare one. Enough to panic, not enough to plan.

I stood in the cold kitchen with the phone in my hand and heard Walter Okafor's voice as clearly as if he stood beside me: *the first weapon he'll use is speed.*

Down the hill, in a rented room above the hardware store, Callum was reading the same email. I knew it the way I was beginning to know everything about him—by certainty rather than proof. I dialed.

"I saw it," he said, before I could speak. No good morning. No shock. Just that low, coiled steadiness that had carried him through twelve years of being the town's cautionary tale. "Nine days, Marisol. Weatherall isn't even pretending anymore."

"Then we don't pretend either," I said. "We spend it loud."

There was a pause on the line, and when he answered, I could hear something new in it—not hope exactly, hope was too soft a word—but the hard bright edge of a decision being made.

"Then we'd better start tonight," he said. "Because I just realized something. If Pell wanted this hearing fast, it means he's afraid of something. And I think I know what."
