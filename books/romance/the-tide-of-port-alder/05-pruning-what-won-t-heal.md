## 5. Pruning What Won't Heal

The first thing I learned about pruning was that Theo Marsh had opinions about it. The second thing I learned was that he intended to share every single one of them.

"Higher," he said from the ground, arms crossed, watching me work the long-handled shears in the third row of Gravensteins. "You're cutting like you're mad at them."

"I am a little mad at them," I said. "They're dead."

"That's a dead branch. The tree is alive. There's a difference, and if you can't tell the difference, you're going to kill an orchard that's been here since 1911."

I looked down at him — at the set of his jaw, the sawdust already in his hair despite the fact that we'd been out here twenty minutes — and felt the old familiar tug-of-war inside my chest. Twelve years ago I would have laughed and thrown an apple core at him. Now I adjusted my grip on the shears and said, "Fine. Show me."

He climbed the ladder without being asked twice, which told me something: he wanted to teach me. He wanted me to know things. He just didn't want to want that.

"See here." His hand closed around a branch, turning it so the morning light caught the bark. "Dead wood is gray. Brittle. Snap it and it breaks clean. Living wood bends. It's got give to it." He snapped the dead branch; it cracked like a knuckle. Then he pressed his thumb into the living wood beside it, and it flexed, stubborn and green-hearted underneath. "You prune what won't heal so the rest of the tree can spend its strength where it matters."

Something about that landed harder than a horticultural lesson should have. I looked away, out past the orchard rows to the gray-blue line of the sea, and pretended to be very interested in the horizon.

"Did you hear me?" Theo said.

"Heal where it matters. Spend its strength. Yes, professor, I heard you."

His mouth twitched — almost a smile, killed before it could commit to anything. "Just cut where I showed you."

* * *

The orchard, I discovered over the following week, was dying.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. It was dying the way a marriage dies, or a friendship — quietly, row by row, in ways you could ignore right up until you couldn't.

I found the first dead row on a Tuesday, when I'd gone out early with a thermos of coffee and a clipboard, because data collection was the one language I spoke fluently. I walked the rows counting trees the way I counted turtles: methodically, gratefully, letting numbers stand between me and everything else. Row fourteen had forty trees when the deed was drawn up, according to the survey. It had thirty-one standing now, and of those, nine were bare skeletons, bark peeling, branches white as bone against the sky.

The second dead row took me two days to find, because it was hidden behind the barn, in the low ground where fog pooled all night like something spilled. The third I didn't find at all. Theo did. He came looking for me at noon, found me sitting on the tailgate of his truck eating a sandwich, and said, without preamble, "Row six is gone too. Fire blight, probably, three or four years back. Nobody pruned it out, so it spread."

"Nobody was here to prune it out," I said.

"My grandmother was eighty-six, Silas. She did what she could."

"I'm not blaming her." I wasn't. I was blaming the empty space where four people used to be — two grandmothers, a grandfather, and whatever Theo and I had once been, all of us thinned down to two strangers sharing a deed. "I'm saying nobody was here. That's not an accusation. That's just what happened."

Theo sat down on the tailgate next to me, leaving a careful six inches of space between us, which was more space than he used to leave and less than I expected. He smelled like salt and two-stroke engine oil, the fisherman layered under the farmer.

"How bad is it?" I asked.

He was quiet for a while. Out in the channel, a fishing boat — his boat, the *Maren Lee*, named for his mother — dragged its wake across the water.

"Bad enough that Marcus Hale stopped by again last week."

My sandwich stopped halfway to my mouth. "Hale. The developer?"

"You've heard of him."

"Ruth mentioned him at the harbor. She called him 'that smiling vulture.'" Ruth Okafor, the harbor master, had become my unofficial source of Port Alder intelligence during my turtle surveys — a woman who dispensed coffee, gossip, and unsolicited life advice in equal measure, and who had taken one look at the co-inheritance situation and said, "Oh, honey," in a tone I was still thinking about.

"That's generous of her," Theo said. "Usually she calls him worse." He turned the ring of his coffee cup slowly on the tailgate. "He wants the orchard. Has for years. Offers my grandmother money every spring like clockwork, and she sends him off with a jar of crabapple jelly and instructions to never come back. But she's gone now, and the offer he made me—" He stopped.

"Theo."

"It was generous," he said flatly. "That's the problem. Generous enough that a man with a boat that needs a new engine and a deck that leaks has to sit with the number for a while before he throws it away."

"And did you throw it away?"

He looked at me then, and there was something raw under the anger he usually wore like armor. "I haven't signed anything, if that's what you're asking. But I haven't burned it either. A man can only burn so many lifelines before he starts wondering why he's drowning."

We sat there in the sun, two people pretending the silence between us was comfortable, and I understood with terrible clarity that the orchard wasn't just failing. It was failing *at us*. Three dead rows and a developer circling, and neither of us willing to say out loud what both of us were thinking: that maybe some things are past saving, and the kindest thing is to stop pouring yourself into them.

I hated that thought. I hated it the way you hate a diagnosis.

"We should get an arborist out here," I said finally. "A real assessment. If any of it can be saved—"

"If." He stood, brushing off his jeans. "You always were good at that word."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing." But it wasn't nothing, and we both knew it, and he walked back toward the barn without looking at me, and I sat on the tailgate with my cold sandwich and my useless clipboard and felt the word *if* sitting in my chest like a stone.

* * *

The recipe box appeared on Thursday.

I'd been sent to the farmhouse attic — "sent" being generous; Theo had grunted in the direction of the attic when I asked where the old orchard records might be, which in Marsh meant enthusiastic encouragement — to look for spray schedules and soil tests. Instead I found dust, a taxidermied pheasant that I chose not to make eye contact with, and a cedar chest full of my grandmother Edith's things.

And tucked inside the chest, wedged between tablecloths yellowed to the color of weak tea, a wooden recipe box with ALDER ORCHARD burned into the lid in someone's careful teenage woodshop lettering.

I knew before I opened it whose handwriting that was. Theo's grandfather, Walter Marsh. My grandmother had talked about Walter exactly twice in my whole childhood, and both times she'd gone quiet in the middle of the sentence, the way you do when you step on ground you didn't realize was hollow.

The box was full of recipes, but not the kind you'd expect. Oh, there were pies — apple pie, obviously, crabapple jelly, a chutney with an annotation reading *too much clove, EDITH, I TOLD YOU* in different ink — but between the recipe cards were other things. Pruning notes. Grafting diagrams. A card from 1963 that read, in Walter's blocky hand:

*Gravenstein, row 3, grafted onto rootstock from the Hale farm stock (before Hales went strange). E. says the scions need wax, I say they need faith. We'll do both.*

E. Edith. My grandmother.

I sat down on a dusty trunk, the pheasant looming behind me, and read card after card after card. Every tree in the orchard had a card. And on nearly every card, in two different hands — Walter's square print, Edith's looping cursive — there were notes from both of them. Arguments about fertilizer. Jokes about each other's grafting technique. A running dispute, spanning decades, about whether the Baldwin apples in row nine preferred to be spoken to kindly or insulted. (*Baldwins respond to flattery. — W.* / *Baldwins respond to being threatened with the axe. They're like men. — E.*)

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in a dusty attic, until my eyes stung — and then kept reading because I couldn't stop, because somewhere in the middle of the box I had realized what I was holding.

This orchard had never been one person's work. It couldn't have been. Half these cards required two sets of hands — one up the ladder, one below. One to hold the scion, one to make the cut. Forty years of notes, and not a single tree planted or pruned or grafted by Walter alone or Edith alone. The trees themselves were partnerships. Living ones. Every apple in every bin, every jar of jelly, every argument about cloves — all of it was two people, season after season, choosing to keep showing up.

I carried the box downstairs on legs that didn't feel entirely reliable.

Theo was in the kitchen, mending a net, and he looked up when I came in, and whatever was on my face made him put the net down.

"What."

I set the box on the table between us. "Your grandfather and my grandmother wrote this together."

He went very still. "That's Walter's box. It's his handwriting."

"Open it."

He opened it the way you open something you already suspect will hurt — carefully, like a letter from a doctor. He read the first card. Then the second. Then he pulled out a handful and spread them across the table, and I watched him arrive, card by card, at the same place I had: the understanding that the two old people we'd loved had built this place together, deliberately, in tandem, for longer than either of us had been alive.

"They never told anyone?" he said quietly.

"Would you have? Look at this one." I tapped the Baldwin card. "*They're like men.* Your grandfather kept this for sixty years and never once mentioned that my grandmother insulted his trees on a regular basis."

The corner of his mouth moved. "She wasn't wrong, though. You do have to threaten Baldwins."

"You've met my family, then."

It slipped out before I could catch it — a joke, an offering, a small bridge extended across twelve years of water. Theo looked at me, and for a moment the anger in his face wavered like a candle in a draft, and I saw underneath it the boy who used to laugh at everything I said, and I think he saw me seeing him, because he looked back down at the cards fast.

"There's a grafting diagram here," he said, voice rougher than usual. "Row nine. Walter planned to graft a new variety onto the old Baldwin rootstock. Never did it. Date on the card is 1998." He traced the diagram with one finger. "Grandpa died in '99."

"So the tree's still waiting."

"Yeah." He cleared his throat. "Still waiting."

We stood there in the kitchen with forty years of somebody else's love spread out on the table between us, and I felt something shift — not forgiveness, not yet, nothing so clean as that. More like the living branch under Theo's thumb that morning. Bent, not broken. Still green underneath, still with give in it, still spending its strength somewhere whether we asked it to or not.

"We should take the box to Ruth," I said. "She'd know if there are people in town who remember them. Together, I mean. Remember them together."

"Together," Theo repeated, like the word was in a foreign language he was relearning.

But he closed the box and picked it up, and he didn't put it back in the attic, and when we walked out to the truck he held the passenger door open for me without seeming to notice he'd done it — a habit from another lifetime, surfacing through the rust like a green shoot through dead wood.

* * *

Marcus Hale came on Saturday.

I was in row eleven with the pruning saw when the silver SUV crunched up the drive, all gleaming chrome and tinted windows, a creature so wrong for the orchard's dirt lane that the lane itself seemed offended. Theo met him at the gate. I hadn't been invited to the meeting, exactly, but nobody told me not to listen, and the wind was coming off the water carrying voices the way it carries everything in Port Alder — straight to whoever's listening.

"—final figure, Theo. And I'll be straight with you, because I respect your grandmother." Hale's voice was warm, reasonable, the voice of a man selling you a ladder while you stand on solid ground. "That land's worth more than fruit. It's worth views. It's worth a resort that brings two hundred jobs to a town that's losing its fleet. You've seen the boats rotting in that harbor. You know I'm right."

I couldn't see Theo's face. I could see his shoulders, though, and they were very still, the stillness of a man holding something heavy.

"Three dead rows," Hale said gently. "I walked the property last month. Beautiful bones, but bones. You can't eat nostalgia, son."

Then the SUV turned around, slow and patient as a tide, and drove away, and Theo stood at the gate a long time afterward, not moving, and I stood in row eleven with my saw in my hand, not moving either, both of us looking at the same thing: the long rows of living trees bending in the wind, green-hearted and stubborn, waiting for someone to decide what they were worth.

That night I found the recipe box open on the kitchen table where Theo had left it, and a new card tucked among the old ones, in his handwriting, dated today:

*Row 9. Baldwin rootstock still sound. Grafting window is spring. If there's going to be a spring.*

Underneath, smaller, almost erased:

*I don't know how to want things anymore. Ask E. how she did it.*

I stood in the dark kitchen holding a dead man's orchard and a living man's doubt, and outside, far down the beach, I could hear the tide coming in — patient, inevitable, carrying everything back eventually. Even turtles. Even, maybe, us.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would call the arborist.

And tomorrow, I decided, I would ask Theo about the paper Hale had left folded on the gatepost — the one with a number on it big enough to make even grief do arithmetic.
