## 16. The Accusation

The harvest festival smelled like cider and fried dough and the particular sweetness of apples that had spent too long in the sun — a smell that had, over these weeks, become something dangerously close to *home* to me, which should have been my first warning.

I stood at the Alder Orchard booth in the town square, arranging Honeycrisps into a pyramid that Ruth had already knocked sideways twice by sampling from the base, and I tried not to check my phone for the fourth time that hour.

The email was still there. Of course it was. Emails didn't evaporate out of shame.

*Dear Dr. Dunmore, we are delighted to offer you the position of Director of Marine Sanctuary Programs, Azores Archipelago...*

*Start date: October 14th.*

Harvest ended October 12th.

I turned the phone face-down against the table, next to the cash box, as though hiding it from the apples would hide it from myself.

"You're doing the thing," Ruth said, appearing at my elbow with a paper cup of cider and the expression of a woman who had been watching me all morning and had run out of patience around eleven o'clock.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look like a heron deciding whether to eat the crab or let it go." She sipped her cider. "You've checked that phone forty times since we set up. Either you've got a lover or a problem, and since your lover is currently arguing with Earl Pemberton about pie-judging ethics across the square, I'm guessing problem."

I followed her gaze. Theo stood by the judging tables in his good flannel, gesturing at a pie with the intensity of a man defending a thesis, while Earl — seventy-three, deaf in one ear, and constitutionally incapable of losing anything gracefully — shook his head with glacial finality. Even from here I could see the argument was hopeless and that Theo was enjoying it anyway. His laugh carried across the square. It always had. Twelve years ago it had been the sound I planned my whole future around, and now it was just a sound I noticed whether I wanted to or not.

"It's nothing," I said. "A work thing."

Ruth looked at me the way she looked at fishermen who claimed they hadn't touched their quota. "Silas."

"Ruth."

"You're going to tell me eventually. You always do. You're constitutionally incapable of keeping things bottled up for more than—" she checked an imaginary watch "—oh, about twelve years, apparently."

"That's low."

"I know." She patted my shoulder. "Drink your cider. The lantern lighting's at seven, and if I have to listen to Mayor Okafor give that speech about community roots one more year without moral support, I'm going to heckle him, and then I'll be banned from the council again."

She wandered off toward the pie tables, presumably to watch Theo lose his battle with Earl, and I was left alone with my pyramid of apples and my phone and the offer sitting inside it like a stone at the bottom of a well.

Here is what I told myself, standing there in the golden late-afternoon light with bunting snapping overhead: *I'll tell him after the festival. After the last row is picked. There's no point ruining today.*

Here is what was actually true: I was afraid. I had found, in the letters in the rafters, two old women who had loved each other across a fence line for fifty years and never once said so, and I had felt the terror of that like cold water — the terror of being someone else's second chance, someone else's *last* chance, and getting it wrong. And now the Azores had come along like a door opening in a burning building, and some cowardly animal part of me wanted to walk through it before anyone could ask me why.

The festival went on. Children bobbed for apples. The high school band played something almost recognizable. Mrs. Adeyemi won the pie contest on a technicality that made Earl apoplectic, and Theo bought me a fried dough dusted with cinnamon sugar and ate half of it without asking, which was either intimacy or theft depending on how charitable I felt, and I laughed, and he looked pleased with himself, and for about ninety minutes I forgot about the email entirely.

That was the cruelest part, looking back. How easy it was to forget. How much I wanted to forget.

* * *

The trouble started at the lantern lighting.

Every year, Port Alder floated paper lanterns off the pier at dusk — one for each person the town had lost, and lately, one for wishes too, because Mayor Okafor believed traditions should grow like everything else in a healthy ecosystem. The whole town gathered on the beach below the square, and the water filled up with small warm lights drifting out on the tide, and it was, objectively, beautiful, and subjectively, a minefield for anyone carrying secrets.

Theo found me at the tide line, lantern in hand, his face lit orange from below by the paper glow.

"Yours says 'for Nana June,'" he said, nodding at the lantern in my hands. "Mine too." He held his up. In careful block letters: *FOR GRAN AND JUNE. TOGETHER.* He'd drawn a little apple tree under the words. My chest did something complicated.

"They'd have hated this," I said. "All this sentiment."

"Nana June cried at dog food commercials."

"She claimed it was allergies."

"For thirty years." He smiled, and for a moment we just stood there, shoulder to shoulder, watching the first lanterns go up — wobbling, catching, rising — and I thought, with a lurch, *I could tell him now. Right now. While it's soft.*

"Theo," I started.

"There you are!" Marcus Hale materialized out of the crowd like a well-dressed fog, clapping Theo on the shoulder. "Marsh! Been looking for you. Got those documents in my car — thought you might want to look them over tonight, given the deadline's Friday." He said it pleasantly, loudly enough that three nearby townspeople glanced over, and I watched Theo's whole body change — shoulders dropping a quarter inch, jaw tightening, the light going out of him like someone had pinched a wick.

"Not tonight," Theo said.

"Friday, though," Hale said, all warmth and teeth. "The preliminary signature. After that it's just surveys and paperwork, but Friday's the commitment. You understand. I've got other parties interested if—"

"I said not tonight."

Hale raised his hands, all innocence, wished us a lovely evening, and drifted away toward the cider stand, leaving behind him a silence with edges on it.

"What documents?" I asked.

Theo didn't answer immediately. He was looking at the water, at the lanterns, at anything but me. "Nothing."

"Theo."

"It's nothing, Silas. Business."

"Business that makes you look like that?"

"Like what?" And there it was — the flint striking. I knew that tone. I'd heard it in week two, when I'd suggested hiring help for the dead rows. I'd heard it when I'd mentioned the trust idea and he'd gone quiet for two days. That tone meant *you don't get to ask,* and every time I heard it, something in me that had been carefully, patiently unclenching for months clenched right back up.

"Like a man signing something he hates," I said.

"Maybe I'm signing something practical." He turned to face me fully, and the lantern light caught the anger in his face and made it look almost like grief. "Maybe some of us can't afford to be sentimental. Maybe some of us have boats going into repossession and crews who need paychecks, and maybe the orchard is a beautiful idea that doesn't actually keep the lights on."

"So it's true. You're really selling."

"I'm *considering*—"

"After everything. After the letters, after the storm, after—" I stopped, because my voice had cracked on *after*, and cracking in front of him felt like handing him ammunition.

"After what?" he said quietly. Dangerous-quietly. "Say it, Silas. After what? We pruned some trees? We found some letters? We had one good night nailing tarps down in the rain?" He shook his head. "None of that changes arithmetic."

"No," I said, and something hot and panicked was rising in my throat now, the old panic, the twelve-years-ago panic, the *run* panic, and God help me, I let it talk. "No, it doesn't change arithmetic. It changes everything else. But sure. Sell. Take Hale's money. Prove that the only thing you ever learned from our grandmothers is how to quit on an orchard."

The moment it left my mouth I wanted it back. His face went white around the mouth.

"That's rich," he said, very softly, "coming from you."

"Theo—"

"Because here's what I can't figure out." He stepped closer, and the crowd seemed to thin around us the way crowds do when they smell weather coming. "We've spent three months side by side. Three months. You laughed at my jokes. You cried over that damn turtle. You held my hand during the storm — don't look at me like that, you know you did. And the whole time, the entire time, you already had one foot out the door, didn't you?"

My stomach dropped through the sand. "What are you talking about?"

"The email." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst part; it stayed level, terrible, precise. "I saw it. This afternoon. On your laptop, at the barn, when I came to bring you the grafting knife you left in the truck. Sanctuary director. Azores. Start date October fourteenth." A breath. "Fourteen days after harvest ends. You had it for — what, a week? Two? And you didn't say a word."

The lanterns kept rising behind him. The town kept singing. Somewhere a child shrieked with joy. None of it seemed to be happening in the same world as the one where Theo Marsh stood in front of me with my secret in his hands like a dead bird.

"I was going to tell you," I whispered.

"When? At the closing? On the plane?" He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd ever heard him make, because it wasn't a laugh at all, it was twelve years of rust breaking loose. "God. I actually thought — I sat in that barn and I thought, no, she wouldn't. Not again. Not after everything. People change. That's what the letters were supposed to teach us, right? That's what they *wanted*." His voice finally broke on the last word, and I saw how much it cost him, and I hated myself with a completeness that felt almost peaceful.

"Theo, please, it's not — I haven't decided—"

"You're already leaving." He said it flatly, like reading a measurement off a gauge. "Aren't you? You're already gone. You've been gone since the day that email came. I just didn't see it because I'm an idiot who keeps waiting for you at fences."

And the panic did what panic does. It reached for the sharpest thing in reach.

"Yes," I said. "Fine. Yes. Maybe I am. Maybe I should have left the first time instead of leaving a note like a coward — except at least the note was honest, Theo. At least I didn't stand there holding lanterns pretending we were building something!"

The words landed. I watched them land. I watched them go into him exactly where the old wound was, dead center, and I watched him decide — visibly, physically decide — to stop bleeding in public.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Good. Now we're honest."

He set his lantern down on the sand, upright, careful, the way he did everything even while destroying things, and walked away across the beach toward the parking lot, and I stood there with my grandmother's name glowing in my hands and realized I had just done the exact thing I had spent twelve years and one entire summer promising myself I would never do again.

I had run. I just hadn't moved my feet.

* * *

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that Ruth found me. Long enough that my lantern had gone out, the little flame drowned by sea spray, Nana June's name dark against the paper.

Ruth didn't say anything at first. She just came and stood beside me, facing the water, where dozens of lights were drifting out past the breakers like a slow exodus.

"He told you," I said.

"Saw him walk past looking like a shipwreck, put two and two together." She handed me her cider. I drank it. It was terrible and warm and I was grateful for it. "Do you want to tell me your side?"

"My side is that he's right."

"Probably. He usually is; it's insufferable." She took the cup back. "But being right and being finished are different things, Silas. I've watched that boy love you since he was seventeen years old and dumb about it. He'll be angry till Thursday. Then he'll be sad. Then he'll do something stupid."

"Something stupid like what?"

Ruth nodded across the beach, toward the parking lot, where a car I recognized as belonging to Marcus Hale was pulling out onto the coast road — and where Theo's truck sat with its headlights just clicking on.

"Oh no," I said.

"Mm," said Ruth.

"He wouldn't. Not tonight. Not after—"

"Honey, I've known Theodore Marsh since he was in utero. He is a man who responds to heartbreak by making the most expensive possible decision available to him. Remember the tattoo?"

"He has a tattoo?"

"1998. A dolphin. Don't ask." She sighed and looked at me with genuine pity. "Go home, Silas. Sleep. Tomorrow you can fix it."

I did not go home. I stood on that cold beach until the last lantern sank beyond the bar, telling myself that he wouldn't, that even furious, even gutted, Theo Marsh wouldn't sign away our grandmothers' orchard in a fit of hurt — that the man who slept in the barn loft during storms to keep the trees company, who knew every tree by the shape of its shadow, who had drawn a little apple tree under *together* —

But I had seen his face when he walked away. And I knew — the way you know a storm is coming before the radio says so, the way I knew which turtles would nest by the weight of the moon — I knew exactly what he was driving toward.

At 11:47 that night, my phone buzzed on the nightstand. Not a call. An email confirmation, forwarded automatically from the county records office to both deed-holders:

*Preliminary sale agreement received and filed. Party of the second part: T. Marsh. Witnessed: M. Hale.*

Below it, a text from a number I knew by heart, four words long, no punctuation, no mercy:

*You should've told me.*

I sat on the edge of the bed in the dark rental house with the ocean loud outside the window, and I understood that I had finally done it — that I had taken the one thing we were rebuilding and burned it down with my own hands, and that this time there was no note, no twelve years, no second chance waiting at the end of it.

Except.

Except that at dawn, I was going to walk down to the nesting beach, and I was going to watch a hatchling fight its way across the sand, and I was going to figure out how to say the true thing at last — the whole true thing, the ugly middle of it, the fear I'd never named out loud.

Because I had lost him twice by running.

I was not going to lose him a third time by standing still.
