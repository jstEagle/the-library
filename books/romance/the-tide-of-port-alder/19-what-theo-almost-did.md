## 19. What Theo Almost Did

I woke before dawn with the grandmothers' letters spread across my bed like fallen leaves, and I knew two things with absolute certainty: the orchard had been a love story from its very first sapling, and Theo Marsh was about to do something unforgivably stupid.

The second thing was less a certainty than a dread. It sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. Because I'd seen his face at the festival — not just angry, but *cornered* — and cornered men sign things. Ruth had told me that evening, her voice low over the harbor rail: "Hale's been circling Theo's boat like a gull after bait fish. Offered to buy out his debt if the orchard deal closes." She'd looked at me then, harbor-master eyes missing nothing. "You want to fix this, Silas Dunmore, you'd best be quick about it."

So I dressed in the dark, stuffed the letters into my canvas bag along with the note — *my* note, the one I'd finally read last night, the one with Theo's line added beneath it in ink gone brown with age: *I would have waited* — and drove to the orchard while the sky was still the color of oyster shells.

His truck was already there.

I found him in the barn, standing at the old workbench where our grandmothers' recipe box still sat, and he was holding papers. Hale's preliminary agreement. I recognized the heavy cream stock, the developer's logo embossed in the corner like a wax seal on a tomb.

"Theo."

He didn't turn around. His shoulders were rigid under his flannel shirt, and for one terrible moment I thought I was too late — that the papers were already signed, that I was looking at the back of a man who had already chosen.

"I read them," he said quietly. "All night. Every clause." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter ever went through a wood chipper first. "Did you know there's a section on 'aesthetic preservation'? He's going to keep three of the trees, Silas. Three. Potted ones by the resort pool. Like taxidermy."

"Theo, listen to me—"

"He offered to clear my boat loan." Now he turned, and his face was wrecked — sleepless, furious, ashamed. "Full payoff. All I had to do was let him bulldoze everything our grandparents planted so rich tourists can drink cocktails next to a potted apple tree and pretend they're rustic." His voice cracked down the middle. "And God help me, Silas, I stood in my kitchen at two in the morning with a pen in my hand, and I wanted to say yes. I *wanted* to."

"Then why didn't you?"

He looked down at the papers in his hands. And then, slowly, deliberately, he tore them in half.

The sound was small — paper is such an ordinary thing to destroy — but it went through me like a shot. He tore them again. And again, until the cream-colored pieces fell from his fingers onto the barn floor like the first snow of a winter we hadn't earned yet.

"Because of this," he said, and pulled his wallet from his back pocket.

* * *

I knew that wallet. I'd known it twelve years ago, cracked brown leather, the stitching coming loose at one corner, the way it smelled of salt and engine oil. He'd carried it when he was seventeen. He carried it still, older now, softer, the stitching long since surrendered.

He opened it with hands that weren't quite steady, and slid out a fold of paper so worn it had gone translucent at the creases. Yellowed. Soft as cloth from handling.

My handwriting. My sixteen-year-old handwriting, looping and uncertain:

*Theo — I can't. I'm sorry. Don't wait for me. — S.*

"You kept it," I whispered. It wasn't a question. Claire had told me, but hearing it secondhand and seeing it were different animals entirely. "Twelve years, Theo. You kept it in your *wallet*. You've carried it through—" I gestured helplessly at all of it: the fishing seasons, the years, the fiancée, the whole life he'd built on the far side of my leaving. "Why?"

"Because it was the only piece of you I had left." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd state the tide table or the weather. "I tried to throw it away once. The summer after college. Stood over the trash barrel behind the fuel dock with it in my hand." His mouth twisted. "Couldn't do it. Felt like drowning you twice."

"And Claire—"

"Claire deserved better than what I gave her, which was a man rehearsing someone else's name." He winced. "That's not fair to her either. I loved her as much as I could, which I'm learning isn't the same thing at all. But yes. I proposed to her on that beach. Same spot. I told myself if I could do it again — say those words again, mean them again — then I'd finally be done." He shook his head slowly. "She said yes and all I felt was relief. Relief, Silas. That should have told me everything."

The morning light was coming through the barn boards now in pale stripes, laying itself across the torn agreement on the floor, across the recipe box, across the space between us that twelve years and one night of festival cruelty had widened and narrowed and widened again.

"I read your note," I said. "Last night. The whole thing."

His jaw tightened. "Silas—"

"You wrote underneath it." My voice was failing me, so I made myself finish anyway. "*I would have waited.* Theo, I have spent twelve years building a life on the belief that you hated me. It was easier that way. Hating me meant you'd healed. Meant I hadn't broken anything that couldn't scar over." I took a breath that hurt going in. "But you didn't hate me. You *waited*. You waited for a girl who never came, and then you punished yourself for waiting, and then you punished me for making you wait, and I did the same thing to myself from the other direction, and we have spent twelve years being cruel to each other because we were both too much of a coward to be kind."

"That's quite a speech," he said hoarsely.

"I'm a marine biologist. We're trained to observe patterns over long time periods." I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, annoyed at my own tears, which had never once respected my dignity. "The pattern, Theo. Do you see the pattern? Your grandfather loved my grandmother and never told her. They planted an orchard instead. Two hundred trees, every single one a word neither of them said out loud. And then they watched us almost make the same mistake, and they died rather than let us finish it, so they left us a will instead of a warning — because they knew us. They knew we'd need to be *trapped* into staying long enough to get honest."

I dug into my bag and brought out the letters, fanned them on the workbench between the pruners and the twine. Edith's careful script. Walter's looser, warmer hand. Fifty years of friendship folded into devotion and never once unfolded into anything more.

"Read them," I said. "Not now. Tonight. But read them. Because I am done letting dead people and developers and my own cowardice decide what happens to this orchard." I pointed at the torn pieces of Hale's agreement on the floor. "How much does that cost you? Truly?"

He was quiet a long moment. "The boat," he said finally. "Or close enough. Hale was going to buy out the loan. Without that..." He exhaled. "I don't know. I'll sell the *Meridian*, maybe. Fish someone else's deck. It's a boat, Silas. Boats can be replaced." His eyes came up to mine, and there was something in them I hadn't seen since I was sixteen and he was teaching me to skip stones off the point — something unguarded, unarmored, terrifyingly open. "The orchard can't. Neither can—" He stopped. Started again. "Neither can what we almost were."

* * *

We didn't fix everything in the barn that morning. I want to be honest about that, because the movies would have you believe forgiveness arrives all at once, whole, like a delivered package. It doesn't. It comes in installments, and some of them are overdue with interest.

What happened was smaller and stranger. Theo picked up the torn pieces of the agreement, methodically, and dropped them in the woodstove, and we watched Hale's logo curl and blacken. Then he put the kettle on the camp burner he kept in the barn — because of course he did, because he was still Theo, the boy who'd once brewed tea in a rusted coffee can over driftwood fire — and we sat on upturned crab buckets and drank it too strong, the way he always made it, the way I'd secretly missed.

"I owe you an apology," he said, staring into his cup. "For the festival. For what I said in front of half the town. You were trying to tell me something and I—" He shook his head. "I saw the Azores email and every fear I own stood up at once and started shouting."

"I hid it," I admitted. "Which was its own kind of leaving. I've gotten good at leaving in ways that let me pretend I haven't." I wrapped both hands around my cup. "I'm not going to take it. Not like that. Not as an escape hatch. If I go anywhere, it won't be running."

"If?"

A ghost of a smile. "If. I'm keeping the conditional. Consider it scientific caution."

He laughed — really laughed, rusty and surprised at itself — and the sound did something to the air in the barn, loosened it, warmed it. Twelve years of winter cracking along its seams.

Then he reached into his wallet again, and my heart did something complicated, and instead of the note he produced a photograph, creased white with folding. Two young women in front of a blossoming apple tree, arms linked, laughing at whoever held the camera. Our grandmothers. Younger than I'd ever known them. Younger than I was now.

"Walter kept it in the same drawer as the deed," Theo said. "I found it the week after the funeral. Look at how she's looking at him taking the picture."

I looked. Edith Fairweather, caught mid-laugh, her whole face turned toward the camera — toward Walter Marsh behind it — with an expression I recognized with a jolt that went all the way down to my keel. I'd seen that exact expression in my own bathroom mirror three days ago, after Theo handed me a ladder and told me not to fall, and I'd caught myself smiling like an idiot at nothing.

"Oh," I said softly.

"Yeah," Theo said. "Oh."

We sat with that for a while, the kettle ticking as it cooled, the letters waiting on the bench, the smell of burnt developer's contract still faintly in the air like incense after a small private ceremony.

"Theo," I said, when I could trust my voice. "Your note. What you wrote under mine. *I would have waited.*"

"Don't," he said gently. "You don't owe me a response to that. I wrote it for me, not you. It was the truest sentence I owned and I needed it somewhere I'd find it."

"Well, I'm responding anyway, because I'm tired of being a coward, and apparently courage is like pruning — you just have to start cutting somewhere and trust the tree." I set down my cup. "I would have come back. If I'd known you'd wait. If I'd believed for one single day that I was worth waiting for. So the failure was mine, but the waiting — yours and his—" I nodded at the photograph, at Walter behind the camera, at fifty years of unsaid words hanging in the blossoms. "The waiting wasn't wasted. It grew something. It's *still* growing something. Two hundred trees and counting."

He reached across the space between our buckets, slow, giving me every chance to move away, and took my hand. His palm was rough with rope calluses and warm as a sunlit deck, and my whole nervous system, which had spent twelve years pretending it didn't know this exact grip, lit up like bioluminescence.

"So what now?" he asked. "Hale's deadline is Friday. The harvest's half done. My boat's mortally wounded, your dream job is sitting unanswered in your inbox, and we've got a barn full of dead people's love letters."

"Now," I said, "we finish what they started. All of it. The harvest, the letters, the truth, the—" I squeezed his hand. "The rest. In order. Starting with the last row of Gravensteins before the rain comes Thursday."

"And the boat?"

"We'll figure out the boat. Ruth owes me a favor, and the harbor master's discretionary fund exists for exactly one fisherman's stubborn pride." I smiled at his expression. "Yes, I already asked. Yes, she said yes. Yes, you're going to have to endure the entire town knowing you got bailed out by a woman, and no, I will not be accepting complaints."

"God," he said, with feeling, "I forgot how insufferable you are when you've planned three moves ahead."

"You didn't forget. You kept my note in your wallet for twelve years. Insufferable was clearly part of the appeal."

He laughed again, and this time I laughed too, and the sound of it filled the barn the way cider fills a press — sweet, and under pressure, and finally, finally going somewhere.

Later — much later, when the light had gone gold and slanted and we'd loaded the truck with crates and letters and one carefully preserved photograph — he walked me to my car and stopped, hands in his pockets, suddenly seventeen again around the eyes.

"Silas. The beach. Where we were supposed to—" He cleared his throat. "Tomorrow. After the last row. There's something I want to ask you. Properly this time. No notes."

My heart performed a maneuver no marine biologist should have to endure without hazard pay.

"No notes," I agreed. "Just stay."

"Just stay," he echoed, like a promise, like a tide chart, like the truest sentence either of us owned.

And as I drove home past the orchard rows glowing in the evening light, I thought about hatchlings — how they dig out of the sand together, dozens of them, because no single turtle makes it to the water alone. Tomorrow we'd finish the harvest. Friday we'd beat Hale. And someday soon, on a beach at moonrise, I was going to say yes to a question twelve years in the asking.

But first — I grinned at the windshield, at the road, at the whole ridiculous forgiving world — first I had a row of Gravensteins to pick.
