## 22. Alder and Salt

The first thing I did on the morning of the anniversary was stand in the orchard in my bare feet and argue with a tree.

Not the new ones — the saplings had been in the ground barely a year and hadn't yet earned the right to be argued with. I meant the old Gravenstein, or what remained of her: the storm-split trunk we'd left standing at the heart of the orchard, hollowed and silvered, wearing her wounds the way old sailors wear theirs. The town had wanted to haul her out the week after the storm. Theo had put his hand flat against the bark and said, *Not yet,* in the voice he used for things he intended to win, and so she stayed, and now she was less a casualty than a monument, and children climbed her, and someone — I suspected Ruth, though she denied it with the sincerity of a woman who had absolutely done it — had wedged a coffee mug of wildflowers into her split.

"You're showing off," I told her, because her few surviving limbs had blossomed that spring, defiant and ridiculous, a fistful of white against all that gray. "Nobody asked you to do that."

The blossoms said otherwise. Blossoms generally do.

Behind me, the gravel crunched, and I knew the rhythm of it before I turned — Theo walked the way he worked, unhurried, deliberate, a man who had made peace with the fact that the tide comes in when it comes in. He was carrying two mugs and a clipboard, which was the most honest portrait of our marriage anyone could have painted. Coffee and administration. We were that kind of couple now, the kind with a laminated harvest schedule on the refrigerator and a shared calendar that Ruth had color-coded without being asked.

"Ruth says the first nest's due to boil any day now," he said, handing me a mug. "She's got a betting pool going. Claire has money on sunset."

"Claire's been wrong twice already."

"Claire's learning the beach. That's more than most people manage." He looked at the old tree, then at me, and something in his face did the thing it did lately — softened, settled, like water finding its level. "You're talking to her again."

"She started it."

He laughed, and the sound went out over the rows the way it always did, and I thought — not for the first time, but with the fresh astonishment of a person who still can't quite believe her luck — *this is mine, this ordinary morning, this man, this dirt under my feet.*

A year ago I had arrived in Port Alder with a duffel bag, a turtle survey permit, and a plan to feel nothing at all. The plan had failed comprehensively. I recommend it.

* * *

The trust had been Theo's idea, technically, though he'd deny that too. What he'd actually said, the night he tore up Marcus Hale's preliminary agreement in front of me — tore it with both hands, a man unmaking a mistake with the full force of his shoulders — was, "We figure it out together or we lose it together." It was Ruth who said the word *trust*. It was Claire, of all people, who knew the lawyer. It was the town that showed up.

That was the part I still hadn't gotten over, a year on. The showing up.

Port Alder was not, by its own admission, a town that agreed on things. It had spent forty years arguing about the harbor dredging and twenty about the diner's hours and would presumably spend forty more about whether the new crosswalk was an improvement or an insult. But when the notice went up — *Alder Orchard: community land trust, buy-in by the acre-foot, all welcome, no one turned away* — people came out of the woodwork like termites of goodwill. The fish market put in. The diner put in. Mrs. Okonkwo from the hardware store bought a share in memory of a husband who had proposed to her under the Gravenstein in 1971, which explained a great deal about her lifelong hostility to the developer's survey stakes. Even old Mr. Pell, who had not spoken to Theo since an incident involving a crab pot in 2019, bought in, and then pretended he hadn't, and paid in cash, and left before anyone could thank him.

We'd closed the trust eleven days before Hale's deadline. I remembered the afternoon less for the paperwork than for what happened after — how Theo had walked out onto the porch, looked at the orchard glowing gold in the late light, and said nothing at all, just stood there with his hands in his pockets, and how I had stood beside him and also said nothing, and how the nothing had been the fullest conversation of my life.

Now the orchard ran the way a good thing runs: loosely, with too many cooks, with Ruth's grand-niece running the cider press like a warship, with a hand-painted sign at the gate that read ALDER ORCHARD — COMMUNITY TRUST — MIND THE WASPS. School groups came through in spring. The historical society had put up a small plaque by the old tree, and Theo and I had argued for a full week about its wording before compromising on something neither of us loved, which is what a marriage is.

And the turtles. God, the turtles.

I'd been counting them for a decade and had never seen a season like this one. The word that came to mind was *gratitude*, which is not a scientific term, but science can cope. The nesting numbers were up across every survey arc — not just on our beach but along the whole coast, as if the ocean itself had heard about the trust and decided to celebrate. Ruth said the turtles could smell a town that had gotten its act together. I told her turtles don't smell civic virtue. She said, "Explain the numbers, then, Dr. Dunmore," and I couldn't, and we both enjoyed my defeat enormously.

There was one female — the old tagged girl, the one I'd released as a hatchling the summer I turned nineteen, the one who'd come back to nest on the same beach two years running — and when her tag number came up on the scanner this season, I'd sat down in the sand and laughed until Theo thought I was crying. Maybe I was, a little. Twelve years ago I had come to this coast to hide inside my data, to count instead of feel, and the data had turned around and counted me instead. Every one of those turtles was a small argument that you can leave a place and have it still be waiting for you. That you can leave a *person* and have it still be waiting.

I knew something about that. He kept my note in his wallet. He'd told me so on the beach, in the wreckage and grace of that final week, and I had made him show me, because I needed to see the crease in the paper, the softness of it, the way a thing gets when it's been held and held and held.

* * *

We planted the saplings on a Saturday in late April, when the light came long and low across the water and the whole town turned out for what Ruth had insisted on calling a ceremony and what Theo and I had insisted on calling a workday.

The hole was dug where the Gravenstein's roots still ran — we'd consulted an arborist, who said the old roots would welcome the young ones, which felt less like botany and more like a benediction. Two saplings: a Gravenstein, for her, and an Alder-bred cider apple that the agricultural extension had been sitting on for years, for him. We'd planted them close, closer than the spacing guide recommended, close enough that in twenty years their branches would lean into each other and eventually, if the arborist was right, grow together at the touch point, the way trees do when no one prunes them apart.

"Two trees, one hole's worth of future," Ruth announced to the assembled crowd, who had come for the free cider and stayed for the sentiment. "That's more commitment than most marriages I've seen, and I've seen a lot of marriages, mostly from behind the counter of the diner."

"Ceremonially," Theo said to me, low, as we lowered the first root ball into the earth together, our hands overlapping on the trunk the way they had overlapped on a hundred things that year — the deed, the harvest, the hatchling buckets, each other. "We're doing this ceremonially, right? Because I already did the other thing, and I don't want to have to do it twice."

"You did it once in a courthouse with Ruth as witness and Claire crying into her bouquet."

"Claire cried at everything that month. She cried at a parking ticket."

The crowd laughed at whatever our faces were doing. The sapling went in. The soil went around it. Mrs. Okonkwo watered it from a watering can she had clearly brought from home, and Mr. Pell, who had sworn he wouldn't come, was there at the back holding a shovel he also had clearly brought from home, and I decided that some kinds of love in a town are deniable and that's how they survive.

Then it was my turn. Ruth had been badgering me for a month: *You're going to read it out loud, Silas, or I'll read it for you, and my rendition will involve interpretive gesture.* She was referring to the letter — the one I'd written in March, in the barn, at the desk where the grandmothers' letters had been found in the rafters, sealed into the same tin box, addressed the way theirs had been addressed: *To whoever tends this next.*

I had written it for Edith Dunmore and Alma Marsh. I had written it knowing full well that neither of them could read it, and knowing, too, that this had never once stopped a letter from arriving.

I stood by the saplings with the page in my hands and the whole town pretending not to watch, and Theo beside me with his shoulder against mine, and I read it.

* * *

*Dear Edith and Alma,*

*You should know that we found you. All of you — the letters, the sixty years of them, the whole architecture of almost that you built between these rows. Theo read the last one out loud in the barn and I had to sit down on a crate of twine and put my head between my knees, which he tells me was very dignified.*

*We know what you did. You wrote a will that was really a door, and you locked it behind us, and then you hid the key in a recipe box and a tin box and the hearts of two stubborn grandchildren, and you waited. You must have waited a long time, the way your trees wait, the way your turtles wait — I've learned this year that waiting is not the same as doing nothing. Waiting is a kind of faith with dirt under its nails.*

*Edith — Gran — I'm sorry it took me twelve years and a dead orchard and a turtle with excellent homing instincts to get here. You told me once that the Dunmores love like the tide: all the way out, all the way back. I thought you meant it as a comfort. I understand now it was a warning, and an instruction, and a promise.*

*Alma — Theo says you'd have hated all this fuss, and then he says you'd have secretly loved it, and then he can't say any more because his voice does the thing. We planted your trees today. We planted them close, so they'll have to grow together, because we learned from you that nothing in this orchard was ever meant to stand alone. Not the trees. Not the two of you. Not us.*

*The orchard is a trust now. Everyone's. The town bought it back one share at a time, out of spite and love and Mrs. Okonkwo's memories, which are apparently the same thing. You'd have argued about the bylaws. You'd have loved arguing about the bylaws.*

*The turtles came back in numbers I've never recorded. Ruth says it's because the town got its act together. I've stopped arguing with Ruth's theories; they're load-bearing.*

*We're staying. That's the whole letter, really. Everything else is just how we got here, and you already know that part, because you were there before we were.*

*With love, and salt, and apples,*
*Silas and Theo*

*P.S. — Edith, the recipe box is safe. Theo has finally accepted that the plum jam requires this much sugar and is not a negotiation.*

* * *

I got through it. Mostly. There was a moment at *waiting is a kind of faith with dirt under its nails* where I heard Theo's breath change beside me, and at *his voice does the thing* the crowd made a sound like a wave, and by the end Ruth was dabbing her eyes with a napkin from the diner and denying it, and Claire — Claire, who had come back to Port Alder for good that winter, who ran the trust's festival committee with terrifying competence and had become, improbably, my friend — Claire yelled "Finally!" at a letter that had taken me a year to read aloud, and everyone laughed, and the saplings stood in their fresh earth looking unimpressed by all of it, which is the correct posture for a young tree.

Afterward, when the crowd had thinned to the diehards and the diehards had thinned to Ruth, Theo and I walked the rows with the last of the cider, doing the slow evening round that had become as natural to me as a tide table. The orchard was in full leaf. The three dead rows from that first terrible spring had been cleared and replanted — whips of green now, knee-high, absurdly hopeful. The old tree stood in the middle of everything with her mug of flowers, and the two saplings stood beside her, and the light came down through all of it the way it does in the last hour, gilding the ordinary until it looks like what it actually is.

"Ruth wants to know about next season's festival theme," Theo said. "She's proposing 'Rooted.' Claire's proposing 'Alder and Salt.'"

"Both. Ruth can have the banner and Claire can have the cider names."

"You can't have two themes."

"We're a trust, Theo. We can have nine themes and a subcommittee."

He laughed and put his arm around me, and we stood there in the row where it had all begun — the contract row, we called it, where he'd handed me those cold terms a year ago and I'd signed them with a breaking heart and a steady hand — and I felt the year settle over me like the evening itself: not a weight, a warmth.

Because here is what I know now, what the orchard taught me and the turtles confirmed and the letters sealed: you do not get to choose the year the storm comes. You only get to choose what you plant after. And if you are very lucky — luckier than you deserve, luckier than two grandmothers scheming from beyond could reasonably guarantee — you get to plant it with your hands over someone else's hands, in dirt that remembers everyone who ever loved you, beside a sea that brings back everything the tide took, eventually, if you wait with your nails dirty and your heart all the way out and all the way back.

The first nest boiled that night, right at sunset, exactly as Claire had bet. We went down to the beach with buckets and headlamps and the whole town behind us, and I knelt in the sand and watched a hundred small lives make their wild run for the water, and Theo knelt beside me, and the tide came in, all the way in, the way it always does, the way we finally learned to.

"Same time next year?" he said.

"Every year," I said. "That's the deal."

And the turtles ran for the water, and the water took them home, and the orchard stood behind us in the dark, growing.
