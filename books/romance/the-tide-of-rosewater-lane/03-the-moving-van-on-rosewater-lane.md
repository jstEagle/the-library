## 3. The Moving Van on Rosewater Lane

The van came on a Tuesday, which felt like an omen, though Felix Marchetti had stopped believing in omens the same year she'd stopped believing in second chances—somewhere around the first anniversary of the last letter, when she'd stood in her kitchen and told herself, with the crisp authority of a woman cataloging inventory, that silence was a kind of answer too.

She heard it before she saw it: the laboring groan of an engine taking the hill from the harbor road too fast for the grade, gears grinding the way they always did when strangers underestimated Rosewater Lane. She was at the sink, rinsing her teacup—one cup, one saucer, one of everything, a household calibrated to a single life—and she looked up through the window over the basin to see the vehicle rounding the bend by the old fish cannery.

It wasn't a local truck. It was too clean, too white, its sides unmarked except for a phone number stenciled in a city font. It pulled up—not hesitantly, not with the circling uncertainty of tourists who had taken a wrong turn—but directly, decisively, in front of the Harrow Inn.

Felix dried her hands on the towel hanging from the oven door. Folded it back into thirds afterward, because some habits were load-bearing.

*No,* she thought. *Nobody's buying the Harrow.*

The Harrow Inn had been dying since before Felix moved to Rosewater Lane eight years ago. It sat at the end of the lane like a ship run aground, three stories of weather-grayed clapboard with a widow's walk that listed slightly to starboard, its porch columns furred with salt rot, its windows either boarded or clouded to the color of old eyes. The FOR SALE sign out front had faded from red to the pink of a healing scar. Children dared each other onto the porch; gulls owned the roofline. The town had held two meetings about it and concluded, both times, that the sea would take it eventually, the way the sea took everything, and that this was perhaps for the best.

Felix had never agreed. She'd written three letters about it—to W, naturally, because W was who she wrote to about things that mattered—and W had answered, in that slanted hand, *Some things are worth saving even when saving them costs more than letting them drown. I think you know that better than most.*

She hadn't thought about that letter in months. She thought about it now, standing at her window with wet hands, watching a woman step down from the driver's seat of the van.

* * *

The stranger was not what Felix expected, though Felix prided herself on expecting very little and being right about most of it.

She was tall, or tall-adjacent, with the posture of someone who had spent years standing in courtrooms—spine straight, chin level, weight balanced as if prepared for an objection. Her hair was dark brown shot through with premature silver at the temples, cut short and practical, still holding the flattened evidence of a long drive. She wore jeans that were new enough to crease sharply and a canvas jacket that was old enough to have earned its fraying. City boots. City hands, uncalloused but steady.

And her face—that was the thing Felix noticed second, after the boxes, and it took her a moment to understand why it unsettled her. It wasn't beautiful, exactly, though it had the architecture for it. It was *closed*. Not unfriendly. Closed, the way a house is closed for the season: shutters drawn, furniture sheeted, nothing left out that could be read. A face that had learned, recently and expensively, how to stop giving anything away.

The woman stood in front of the Harrow for a long moment, looking up at it the way people look at graves or verdicts. Then she walked around to the back of the van, hauled open the rolling door, and began to unload.

Alone. That was the third thing Felix noticed. No movers, no crew, no helpful neighborly fleet of pickup trucks such as any arrival in their town would normally summon within the hour. Just the woman, lifting boxes down and carrying them up the rotted porch steps two at a time, as if she didn't trust anyone else to touch what she owned.

Felix should have gone back to her novel. She had a chapter due to herself by Friday—a discipline she enforced with the gentle tyranny of a woman who had built her whole life out of small enforceable rules—and the morning was already half-spent. Instead she stayed at the window, teacup forgotten in the rack, and watched.

It was the fourth box that did it.

The woman set it on the porch rail while she wrestled the door key into a lock that hadn't turned willingly since the Clinton administration, and the box faced Felix's window full-on. It was labeled in black marker, in handwriting that made Felix's breath do something complicated and unauthorized.

Slanted. Distinctively, unmistakably slanted—the letters leaning east like grass bent by a prevailing wind, the *W*s sharp as gull wings, the crossbars of the *t*s struck fast and low so they looked almost like plus signs. A hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the cardboard. A hand Felix knew better than her own, because she had spent ten years reading it, tracing it, memorizing its moods across a decade of Tuesdays—the confident days when the letters came in fountain pen, the exhausted ones when they came in ballpoint on legal pads, the last one, two years ago now, scrawled on the back of a hotel memo sheet as if written standing up, in an airport, in the act of leaving:

*I have to stop before I ruin this.*

Felix's hands found the edge of the sink without her telling them to. The kitchen went very quiet, the way rooms go quiet when something inside them has shifted—a picture frame settling, ice cracking in a glass—and she stood there and let the recognition move through her like cold water through a wetsuit, total and inescapable.

*No,* she thought again. But it was a different *no* now. The first no had been about the inn. This one was about the whole shape of the morning, the whole architecture of her certainty, which had been built on a simple foundation: W was far away. W was a stranger. W was safe precisely because W was nowhere, a voice without a body, a pen without a face, the one relationship in Felix's contained and carefully ordered life that she had never tried to control because there had been nothing to control. Letters arrived. Letters stopped. The heart, trained like a muscle, held its shape.

The woman got the door open. It swung inward with a groan, releasing ten years of trapped salt air, and Felix watched her pause on the threshold—watched her shoulders rise once, deliberately, like a swimmer about to go under—and then carry the box inside, into the dark of the Harrow Inn, out of sight.

Felix stayed at the window a long time after that.

* * *

By noon she had cataloged the situation, because cataloging was what Felix did instead of feeling things at full volume. She made tea she didn't drink and opened the notebook she kept for novel research and wrote, in her own tidy upright hand:

*New neighbor. Female, 35–40. Bought the Harrow outright (verify). Unloaded alone. Closed expression. Handwriting: —*

Her pen stopped above the dash.

Here was the problem, laid out as cleanly as a plot problem, which was the only way Felix could bear to lay out problems: handwriting could be forged. Handwriting could resemble. Ten years ago she had received a letter in a similar slant from a different person entirely—no, that wasn't true, she'd never received another letter in that hand, that was the point, that was the entire point, the hand was singular, the hand was *known* to her the way a voice is known through a wall—

But bodies could be coincidences. Faces could be coincidences. A woman moving to a dying seaside town with boxes marked in a familiar slant could be—what? What were the odds? Felix had spent her career inventing plausible coincidences and then engineering their destruction; she knew better than anyone that coincidence was the cheap mortar of bad fiction. Real life was supposed to be more rigorous than that.

Except real life, she was beginning to suspect, had been running a long con on her for two years.

She went out to the fence line at four o'clock, because four o'clock was when the light came low and gold across the lane and because waiting longer would have been cowardice dressed as propriety. The Harrow's front door stood open to the afternoon, propped with a brick, and from inside came sounds of industry—the scrape of furniture, the ring of a hammer testing joists, once, sharply, a curse swallowed before it finished forming.

Felix stood at the low stone wall that divided her cottage's garden from the inn's weedy forecourt and waited, hands folded, the picture of a neighbor with nothing on her mind but introductions.

It took eleven minutes. She counted.

The woman came out carrying an armload of rotted curtain rods, saw her, and stopped. For a half-second—Felix would swear to this later, under no oath whatsoever—the closed expression flickered, and underneath it was something startled and raw, a door blowing open on a lit room before slamming again. Then the shutters came down, smooth as practice.

"You're the neighbor," the woman said. Not a question. Her voice was lower than Felix had imagined, and steadier, and it cost her something audible to keep it that way.

"Felix Marchetti." She extended her hand across the wall. "You've bought yourself a famous ruin."

"Wren Ashford." The handshake was brief, dry, firm. Her fingers were cold from the inn's interior. "And yes. I have."

"Out of curiosity—or nosiness, take your pick—which is it?"

"A bit of both, probably." Wren Ashford set the curtain rods down on the wall between them, a small barricade of rust. "I wanted to be near the water."

"That's what everyone says until November."

"I've been told." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more the place where a smile had been removed. "It's a nice cottage. Yours."

"It was my grandfather's. He kept the lighthouse." Felix nodded toward it, the white tower rising behind her house at the lane's dead end, dark these twenty years, its lamp room fogged with salt and bird droppings. "I inherited the cottage and the habit of staying."

"The lighthouse doesn't work?"

"Not since he died." She said it evenly, the way she said all the settled things. "The Coast Guard automated the light further up the coast. Ours was decommissioned. It's just a tower now. A very tall, very empty room with a view."

Wren Ashford looked past her at it, and her expression did something Felix couldn't parse—something older than the conversation, heavier, as if the lighthouse meant something to her that it had no business meaning. As if she'd seen it before, somewhere other than the end of a lane.

"You knew the previous owner?" Felix asked, and watched the question land.

"No," Wren said. Too quickly. Then, with the careful recalibration of someone used to choosing words under pressure: "I mean—not personally. I read about him. When I was researching the property. There were mentions. Of the keeper's logs, the history." She picked up the curtain rods again, ending the interview by loading her arms. "Well. Thank you for coming over. I'm sure I'll be seeing you around."

"Undoubtedly," Felix said. "We're neighbors. It's a small lane."

"Yes," said Wren Ashford. "It is."

She carried the curtain rods inside. The brick-held door swung shut behind her, and Felix remained at the wall in the gold-going-cold light, doing the arithmetic she had been avoiding all day.

Ten years of letters. Two years of silence. And now, forty feet away, behind a door that had never once been opened for Felix Marchetti, lived a woman with a slanted hand and a closed face who flinched at the mention of lighthouse logs and lied badly about knowing them.

*Coincidence,* said the trained part of her mind, the part that kept the heart like a muscle, high-rep, low-weight, never strained.

*Liar,* said the rest of her.

She walked back to her cottage slowly, past the garden she kept in strict rows, past the window where her teacup still waited in its rack, and inside she did not write her chapter. Instead she took down the shoebox from the top of the wardrobe—the one containing ten years of Tuesdays, tied with string—and set it on the kitchen table in the last of the light.

Then she went to the window, where she could see the Harrow Inn's silhouette against the darkening water, and watched until, sometime after nine, a single light came on upstairs in the ruined house. One lamp. One room. One person, presumably, sitting in it.

Felix lifted the first letter out of the box, unfolded the familiar slanted lines, and began, methodically, to compare worlds.
