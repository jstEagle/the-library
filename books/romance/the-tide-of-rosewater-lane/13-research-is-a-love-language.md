## 13. Research Is a Love Language

The Rosewater Public Library smelled of salt-bleached paper and the lemon oil Mrs. Okonkwo rubbed into the reading tables every Monday morning, as though the furniture itself might be persuaded to stay young. Felix had been coming here since she was nine years old, back when her grandfather would walk her down Harbor Road with his hand heavy and warm on her shoulder, saying, *A library is the only church where nobody checks your sins at the door.* She had written three novels in the corner carrel by the north window—the one with the wobbly leg she'd learned to shim with a folded matchbook—and it was there, on a gray Tuesday that couldn't decide whether to rain, that she finally stopped being a woman avoiding a story and became a woman hunting one.

It started, as most of her obsessions did, with a sentence.

She'd gone to the library to work on her novel—chapter eleven, which had been dying a slow death for six weeks—and instead found herself staring at the photocopies Wren had given her of the lighthouse logs. Her grandfather's handwriting marched across the pages in that confident, slightly left-leaning script she could have identified blind: *Wind NE fresh. Fog thick after 8. Vessel sighted running dark, no lights, heading SSW toward Gull Point.* Running dark. A ship with no lights, in fog, moving toward a stretch of coast where the charts showed nothing but rocks and an old loading pier that hadn't appeared on any map since before the war.

Felix had read the entry a dozen times since Wren spread the logs across the inn's sawdust-covered table. But this morning, in the flat gray light of the reading room, something clicked—not loudly, not dramatically, but with the quiet, terrible precision of a lock turning.

Her grandfather had not simply recorded the dark ship. He had recorded it *eleven times* over four years. And he had never reported it.

* * *

"Mrs. Okonkwo," Felix said, carrying the logs to the front desk like they were made of eggshell, "how far back do the harbor records go?"

The librarian looked up over her spectacles. She was seventy-three and had run the library for forty years, and she possessed the particular gift of small-town archivists everywhere: she remembered everything, filed everything, and told no one anything unless she decided to. "Official records? County keeps those. Microfiche from 1951 forward, digital after '98." Her eyes narrowed slightly, the way they always did when Felix asked questions that weren't about novels. "Why?"

"I'm researching a book."

"You're always researching a book." Mrs. Okonkwo took off her glasses entirely, which meant she was interested. "Your grandfather's book, is it? The lighthouse one you mentioned last spring?"

Felix hesitated. Lying to Mrs. Okonkwo was like lying to a tide chart—possible, but the sea would know. "Something like that. I need shipping manifests. Cargo registrations. Anything tied to vessels using the harbor between—" she checked the log dates "—1974 and 1981."

"That'll be the county archive, mostly. But we've got the local papers on microfilm going back to 1922, and the Chamber of Commerce directories." Mrs. Okonkwo paused, then added, in the tone of a woman opening a drawer she kept locked, "And there's the Hollis Collection."

"The what?"

"Bequest. Old man Hollis, harbor master before your grandfather took the light. Left us his personal papers when he died in '89. Nobody's touched them in thirty years—they're in the back room, twelve boxes, mostly water damage." She studied Felix over the desk. "You want them?"

"Yes," Felix said, before caution could catch up. "God, yes."

* * *

The back room of the Rosewater Public Library was less a room than a closet that had ambitions, lit by a single buzzing fluorescent tube and smelling of mold and old ink. Mrs. Okonkwo handed Felix the key on a red ribbon and said only, "Lock up when you're done. And Felix—" a pause, weighted "—your grandfather was a good man. Whatever you find, remember that people are bigger than their worst page."

Felix carried that sentence into the closet like a lantern.

The first three boxes were exactly what Hollis's daughter had probably imagined when she donated them: tide tables, correspondence about dredging permits, a decades-long argument with the county about dock fees conducted in increasingly testy carbon copies. Felix worked through them with the methodical patience her grandfather had taught her—*everything the sea takes can be tracked, Fee; you just have to know which ledger it fell into*—photographing pages with her phone, building a timeline on the legal pad she'd brought.

By the fifth box, the pattern emerged.

Hollis, it turned out, had kept two kinds of records. The official ones—clean, typed, boring. And then, tucked inside manila folders labeled with innocuous things like DREDGE SCHEDULES and WEATHER CORRESPONDENCE, a second set: handwritten notes, undated scraps, cargo tallies that matched nothing in the official manifests. Tonnage that arrived but was never logged. Tonnage that was logged but never arrived. In one folder, a single sheet in Hollis's cramped hand:

*Third time this quarter. Asked R. directly. He laughed at me. Said the harbor master's job is to keep the channel deep, not ask what floats in it. Considered writing the county. Considered longer.*

*R.* Felix wrote the initial on her pad and stared at it. It could be anyone. It could be nothing. But her skin had gone cold in the way it went cold in the third act of a novel, when the writer in her knew the shape of the thing before the facts caught up.

She pulled out the Chamber of Commerce directories. 1976. The harbor businesses listed in neat columns: Bait & Tackle, Chandlery, Boatworks, and—Reeve Maritime Shipping Co., Pier 3. Proprietor: E. Reeve.

E. Reeve. Father, presumably, of Callum Reeve, the man from the Harbor Trust who had come to the inn with his smooth voice and his eyes like a man appraising a horse he intended to put down. Felix sat back on the rickety stool and let the cold move through her properly now, all the way in.

Her grandfather had watched ships run dark toward Reeve's pier. Hollis had confronted an *R.* and been laughed at. And two years ago, a prosecutor named Wren Ashford had buried a case involving—she still didn't know exactly what; Wren kept that door shut—but the timing, the geography, the way Reeve had materialized at the inn within weeks of the logs surfacing...

Purpose settled over Felix like a coat. Not comfort—purpose was warmer than comfort and fit worse, dug in at the shoulders—but she welcomed it. For six weeks she had been paralyzed, orbiting Wren Ashford at the fence line, saying polite nothing, feeling everything. This was better. This was a task with edges. Research was the one love language she'd ever been fluent in; she had spent ten years loving a stranger through paper, through archives of each other's minds. Of course it would come to this. Of course she would try to save Wren the only way she knew how: by finding the truth and writing it down where no one could burn it.

* * *

The microfilm room was a windowless cell at the end of the stacks, home to a machine older than Felix and a spool cabinet that groaned like a ship in weather. She fed in the *Rosewater Gazette* for 1977 and cranked through the weeks, the headlines flickering past—Harvest Festival, Council Budget Dispute, a photo of a flounder the size of a car door—until March, when a small item on page six stopped her hand.

*PIER 3 EXPANSION APPROVED. The council voted 5–2 Tuesday night to approve the expansion of Reeve Maritime's Pier 3 facility, over objections raised by harbor master Elias Hollis regarding "unresolved tonnage discrepancies." Mr. Edmund Reeve assured the council the discrepancies were "clerical errors, long corrected."*

Clerical errors. Felix photographed the item, then kept cranking. There—a follow-up letter to the editor in April, signed E. Hollis, dry as driftwood, asking whether clerical errors might explain why Reeve Maritime's declared imports were forty percent below its fuel purchases. And beneath it, the next week, a reply from Edmund Reeve himself, all charm and condescension, thanking Hollis for his "vigilance" and noting that the harbor master's position came up for county review in the fall.

The review, Felix discovered three spools later, had gone badly for Hollis. A November item: *HARBOR MASTER REPLACED. Citing budget constraints, the county declined to renew Elias Hollis's contract, appointing instead deputy master Gerald Pike. Hollis, 61, said he planned to spend more time fishing.* Two months later, the county had offered the lighthouse keeper's post to a younger man with a spotless record and no history of asking inconvenient questions.

Thomas Vane. Her grandfather. Who had taken the light in January 1978, and whose logs—she checked again, heart hammering—began recording dark-running vessels within the year.

They hadn't hired him despite his honesty, Felix realized. They'd hired him *because* of it. An honest man in the lighthouse gave the operation its alibi. If Thomas Vane ever testified, he could testify only to what he'd seen—and what he'd seen, carefully managed, could be spun as rumor, as an old man's mistake. Or perhaps they'd simply believed he wouldn't look too hard at what he saw, so long as his paychecks cleared and his granddaughter had shoes.

Felix pressed her palms flat against the microfilm machine's warm metal housing and breathed until the fury became something usable. Her grandfather had died in a fall from the lamp room in 1994—an accident, everyone said, an old man and a wet stair. She had been twenty-two. She had grieved without questioning, because questioning felt like betrayal.

Now the question arrived anyway, uninvited, wearing Reeve's smile: *What did he see that made him dangerous enough to remove?*

* * *

She worked until the fluorescent tube in the back room buzzed its evening complaint and Mrs. Okonkwo knocked gently on the doorframe with two cups of tea.

"Find what you were looking for?" the librarian asked, setting a cup beside the sprawl of photographs and notes.

"I don't know yet." Felix wrapped her hands around the cup. "I found what I wasn't looking for. That's usually how it goes."

Mrs. Okonkwo surveyed the table—the Hollis folders, the directory pages, the legal pad dense with Felix's small slanted handwriting, which looked, Felix noticed with a strange lurch, remarkably like another handwriting she knew. "You know," the old woman said, "Edmund Reeve used to come in here. Every Thursday, years ago. Read the shipping news and the stock pages, never checked anything out." She sipped her tea. "His boy Callum did the same when he visited summers. Sat right where you're sitting, actually. Both of them had a way of looking at this town like it was inventory."

"And my grandfather?"

"Thomas?" Something softened in Mrs. Okonkwo's face. "He came in for poetry, if you can believe it. Neruda, mostly. Said a man who spent all night alone with a lamp needed words that glowed." She set down her cup. "He also came in the winter before he died. Asked me to help him request some county records through interlibrary loan. Manifests, I think. I never asked what for." Her eyes met Felix's over the steam. "The request form came back stamped *withdrawn by requester*. Three days later he was dead."

The tea went cold in Felix's hands.

* * *

She walked home along Harbor Road in the full dark, the wind coming off the water with teeth in it, her canvas bag heavy with photocopied pages she'd promised Mrs. Okonkwo she'd take care of. The town was quiet in the way it got after the last train—9:40, right on schedule, its whistle already fading behind the dunes—and the streetlights made long amber islands of the pavement with darkness between.

At the top of Rosewater Lane, the Harrow Inn glowed. Not fully—the scaffolding side was dark, the roofline skeletal against the clouds—but the downstairs windows threw warm rectangles onto the wet grass, and the porch light burned steady, the way it had burned every night since the storm, since the night Felix had slept on the inn's couch under a quilt and woken at dawn to find Wren watching the light come up over the water with an expression she'd shut away the moment she noticed she was being seen.

Felix stood at the fence line, breath fogging, and let herself look at the lit windows the way she'd once looked at envelopes postmarked from a city she'd never visited.

*I'm doing this for you,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because correction was what she did, containment was what she did. *I'm doing this because an innocent person shouldn't drown for other people's cargo. Because my grandfather deserves better than *accident*. Because Hollis deserved better than a laugh.*

All true. All also, somewhere underneath, in the hold of the ship: *for you.*

Inside the inn, a shadow crossed the downstairs window—Wren, moving with that careful prosecutor's economy, pausing at the glass. For a moment Felix thought she might come out, might stand at her own fence the way they'd stood at the shared one, trading politeness like fencers trading touches. Instead the shadow lifted something—a phone, pressed to an ear, Wren's posture going rigid in the way Felix had learned to read as *bad news.*

Felix turned up the lane toward her own cottage, her mind already rearranging itself around the new architecture of the thing. Tomorrow she would pull the land records—who owned Pier 3 now, who had owned it in '94, whether the Reeve name had ever left the deed. Tomorrow she would request the coroner's file on her grandfather's death, or whatever the county would release to a next of kin. Tomorrow she would start a proper file, dated, cross-referenced, duplicated and hidden, because she had read enough thrillers and lived through enough of this week to know that paper got stolen.

Tonight, she climbed to her attic office, spread the copies across the drafting table, and began to write it all down in her own hand—her grandfather's hand, really, the family inheritance neither of them had ever discussed—in a notebook she would keep under the floorboard where the drafts of her novels lived.

At the top of the first page she wrote: *Everything the sea takes can be tracked.*

Then, underneath, smaller: *So can everything people bury.*

Down the lane, the inn's porch light burned on against the dark, patient as a promise. And Felix, pen moving, felt the paralysis of six weeks dissolve into something with momentum and teeth—the novelist's oldest intoxication, the sense that the story was finally telling her where it needed to go.

She was three pages deep when her phone buzzed on the table. A text from an unknown number, no greeting, no signature:

*Whatever you're pulling at the library—stop. Please. Before he notices you too.*

Felix stared at it for a long moment, the lamp humming above her, the wind worrying the eaves. Then she looked at the slant of the letters, the distinctive left-leaning hand even a keyboard couldn't quite disguise, and understood that Wren Ashford had just made her first confession without meaning to.

She picked up her pen and kept writing.
