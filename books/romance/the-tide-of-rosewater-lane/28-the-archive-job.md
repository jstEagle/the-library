## 28. The Archive Job

The county archive smelled of dust and cold iron, the way old paper smells when it has been left alone too long to remember being read. Felix stood at the entrance for a full minute before going in, letting her eyes adjust, letting her pulse settle into something she could work with. Fear was a tide. Her grandfather had taught her that much without ever meaning to. You didn't fight a tide; you read it, you timed it, you moved with it or you drowned.

*Thursday, 2:14 p.m.* The archive kept county hours, which meant it emptied after lunch and stayed empty until four. The clerk's desk sat just inside the door, a slab of government-issue oak behind which Callum Reeve himself had taken to sitting—not because he loved the work, but because he wanted every hand that touched these records to pass through his own. He'd been there three days now. Wren had confirmed it that morning over coffee neither of them drank: *He's making a show of it. He wants us to know he's watching.*

Which was exactly why they were doing this today. A man who performs vigilance is a man who believes no one would dare.

Felix wore her good coat, the gray wool one, and carried a canvas tote that held nothing but a paperback novel, a pencil, and—tucked flat against the lining where the seam sagged—a camera no bigger than a cigarette case. Not her phone. Phones could be seized, searched, their histories subpoenaed by a man who still knew every clerk of court in three counties. The camera was old technology, analog, deniable. She had bought it from a pawnshop in Marsh Harbor with cash, wearing sunglasses, feeling absurd and alive in equal measure.

And pinned inside her lapel, borrowed from a friend of a friend at the historical society, was a laminated badge that said *RESEARCH AFFILIATE — COASTAL HERITAGE TRUST*. It was not forged, precisely. It was real, issued to a Dr. Eleanor Voss, retired, who had lent it out with a wink and the observation that nobody had checked her credentials in forty years either. It was, Felix told herself, technically true that she was affiliated with coastal heritage. She had grown up in a lighthouse. That was practically a doctorate.

"You're early," Reeve said, looking up as she entered. He had a voice like warm oil, pleasant and slightly wrong, the way a room smells faintly of gas before you realize why. "Most researchers come at the top of the hour."

"Most researchers don't mind waiting," Felix said. "I do."

She let him see the badge. Let him see her sign the register with her own name—*F. Marchetti*, because hiding it would be worse; a false name was an admission, and her name was clean, her purpose plausibly scholarly. *Provenance research, maritime holdings, Rosewater Lane vicinity.* Every word of it defensible. She had learned this from Wren, in one of their cold-alliance evenings at the kitchen table: *Never lie about what matters. Lie about everything around it until the truth has nowhere to point.*

Reeve studied the signature a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near his eyes.

"Marchetti," he said. "Rosewater Lane. You're the novelist."

"I'm a lot of things."

"The lighthouse keeper's granddaughter." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that was the most frightening thing he'd done all day. "Your grandfather kept remarkable records. Remarkable. The Trust has been meaning to do something about that collection for years."

"Then I'm sure you'll get right on it," Felix said, and walked past his desk into the stacks.

Her heart was going like a gull in a storm, but her hands were steady. That was the trick of it—the division of labor. Give the fear to the body and the work to the hands, and never let them meet.

* * *

The maritime holdings lived in the back third of the archive, in gray boxes shelved floor to ceiling under fluorescent light that hummed like something trapped. Felix had spent two evenings here earlier in the week, openly, legitimately, pulling harbor records for what she'd told Reeve was her novel. Novelists were harmless. Novelists were romantic. Nobody watched a woman with a notebook.

But the harbor master's ledger—the original, the one with her grandfather's initials countersigned against ship movements that existed nowhere else—was not on the open shelves. It lived in the restricted case, the locked glass cabinet at the end of Row 12, the one whose key hung on a board behind Reeve's desk.

She had known it would come to this. Wren had argued for another way—there wasn't one—and then stopped arguing, which for Wren was its own kind of surrender.

The plan was simple, and simple plans were the only kind that survived contact with a man like Reeve. At 2:30, Wren would walk through that front door in her best blazer, all city polish and prosecutorial gravity, and ask to see the acquisition records for the Harrow Inn property—records that lived, conveniently, in the same restricted system, requiring the same key, the same retrieval process, the same clerk's attention. Wren Ashford was a name Reeve could not ignore. Wren Ashford asking questions was a fire he would have to stand and watch.

While he watched her, Felix would be here.

Felix checked her watch. 2:26. She used the minutes to rehearse, standing in Row 9 among the tax assessor's records, breathing slow, running the sequence in her mind the way her grandfather had run the lamp mechanism: *crank, counterweight, rotation, beam.* Unlock. Photograph. Reshelve. Lock. Ninety seconds if the pages cooperated. Two hundred if the ledger was fat. She would know within ten seconds of opening it.

At 2:31, the front door chimed.

She heard Wren's voice before she saw her—that measured, unhurried tone she used like a scalpel, each word placed rather than spoken. "Mr. Reeve. Callum. I'll be honest, I didn't expect to find you behind the desk. I thought the county paid someone for that."

"And I thought you'd have more sense than to show your face in my archive, Ms. Ashford."

"Your archive? Last I checked, public records belonged to the public. Or has that changed since my disbarment?" A pause, silk over steel. "I want the acquisition file for parcel 44-B, the Harrow Inn. All of it. Deed history, transfer documents, the environmental survey the county commissioned and never released. I have reason to believe the survey was suppressed, and I intend to find out whether that suppression happened before or after your family's company made its offer on the access road."

Silence. Felix could feel it from thirty feet away, the particular quality of a man recalculating. Then Reeve laughed—short, genuine despite itself, the laugh of a chess player watching an unexpected move land. "You always did talk like a closing argument."

"And you always did flinch when someone asked for paperwork. The file, please. I have all afternoon, and unlike you, I'm not afraid of what's in these cabinets."

Chairs scraped. Keys rattled on the board. Footsteps moved toward the back office, Wren's voice trailing, demanding, occupying, *being*—and Felix moved.

Row 12. The glass case. Her fingers found the small brass lock Wren had described from memory—she'd been in this archive once, years ago, in another life, prosecuting a theft case whose evidence had included half this building's contents—and the hairpin Felix had bent into a tension wrench slid in and caught. First pin. Second. The lock was old and the mechanism older, and it gave with a click so soft it might have been the building settling.

The ledger was there, under a folder of bridge inspection reports. Leather corners worn to cardboard, spine cracked, the county stamp faded to a ghost. *Harbor Master's Ledger, 1968–1987.* Her grandfather's years. Her grandfather's hand.

She opened it on the reading table, angled the lamp, and began.

The camera whispered. Page after page, she worked in the rhythm she'd learned restoring her own manuscript pages—left hand flattening the paper, right hand shooting, thumb turning, never lingering. The entries marched past: tides logged, fuel receipts, lamp oil inventories. And then, beginning in the spring of 1974, the anomalies she and Wren had mapped from the copied logs: night arrivals, cargo weights that didn't reconcile, and in the margin, in her grandfather's small fierce script, initials countersigning each entry. *E.R.* Edwin Reeve. Callum's father. Initials beside shipments that officially never happened, payments recorded as "harbor maintenance" that no maintenance schedule could explain.

Her grandfather hadn't just noticed. He had documented. For thirteen years, knowing what it might cost him, he had written it all down in a ledger he must have known would sit in a cabinet no one opened.

Felix's throat tightened. She kept shooting. Grief was a tide too. You didn't fight it; you moved with it, and you finished the page.

* * *

She was three-quarters through when the voices changed.

It was subtle at first—Wren's tone shifting half a degree, gaining an edge Felix recognized from the night Wren had told her about the buried case. Then Reeve's laugh again, but different now, thinner. Then footsteps, moving—not away, but *around*, circling toward the stacks, and Wren's voice rising deliberately to cover the sound:

"—and that's the third discrepancy, Mr. Reeve, so unless you'd like to explain it to me here, I suggest you pull the file—"

Felix flattened herself against the shelf, camera against her sternum, ledger open under the shadow of her body. Through the gap between two gray boxes she could see the aisle's mouth, and Reeve standing in it, half-turned, listening to Wren with the expression of a man deciding whether to be amused or alarmed.

"Ms. Ashford," he called back, "you'll forgive me. I have a nose for people touching things they shouldn't."

"Then your nose is broken," Wren said, stepping into his sightline, blocking the aisle with her whole body, her voice dropping into something Felix had never heard her use outside of courtrooms—or nightmares. "Because the only person handling anything improper in this building is whoever suppressed an environmental survey. Now. The file. Or I start making calls, and I promise you my calls are better than yours these days. Disgrace makes excellent phone manners."

A long moment. Felix did not breathe. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the walls, pipes ticked like a clock counting down something other than time.

Then Reeve exhaled through his nose, turned, and walked back toward the front desk, and Wren followed, talking, always talking, her voice a rope thrown across the widening dark between Felix and discovery.

Felix finished the last pages in ninety seconds flat. Reshelved the ledger beneath the bridge reports, squared its corners to the millimeter—her grandfather would have approved—closed the case, and worked the hairpin backward until the lock clicked home. She wiped the reading table with her sleeve, straightened the lamp, and walked up Row 11, out through Row 4, signing nothing further, nodding to no one, her face arranged into the mild boredom of a woman who had found her novel's research wanting.

At the front desk, Wren stood with a single thin folder and an expression of magnificent disdain. Reeve sat behind the oak, watching Felix approach with eyes that were pleasant and bottomless.

"Find what you needed?" he asked.

"Some tide tables," Felix said. "For a scene. My editor wants authenticity."

"Tide tables." He smiled. "The sea keeps better records than we do, wouldn't you say, Ms. Marchetti?"

"That's the trouble with the sea," Felix said, meeting his gaze without blinking, her heart a drum, her voice a flat calm. "It remembers everything. Even the parts people would rather it forgot."

She walked out. Wren fell into step beside her, and neither of them spoke until the door chimed shut behind them and the cold sea wind hit their faces on the archive steps.

* * *

They made it four blocks before Wren's composure cracked—not into panic, but into laughter, low and shaking and slightly hysterical, the laughter of a woman who had spent forty minutes performing courage while her entire nervous system screamed. Felix started laughing too, helplessly, leaning against the brick of the fishmonger's wall, the camera a hard little weight against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"You blocked the aisle," Felix managed. "With your *body*."

"You picked a lock with a *hairpin*. Who taught you that?"

"A mystery novel. Chapter eleven." Felix pressed a hand to her chest. "I have never been so frightened in my life. Including the night someone broke into my cottage."

"Liar. You were magnificent. Your hands never shook."

"My hands shook the entire time. They just agreed to keep it private."

Wren looked at her then—really looked, the way she did rarely and completely—and something passed between them that had no need of letters or words, ten years of correspondence compressed into a single glance on a windy street. Then Wren's expression sobered, and she nodded toward the camera bag.

"How many frames?"

"All of them. Every anomaly, every initial, every 'harbor maintenance' payment from 1974 to the year he died." Felix's laughter drained away, replaced by the cold bright clarity that had been settling over her for days now. "Callum. It's enough. With the fisherman's statement and the witness letter, it's enough to build the complaint on."

"Then we move fast," Wren said, and her voice had gone quiet and hard, the courtroom voice, the voice of a woman who had once believed in the law enough to lose everything to it. "Reeve will figure it out eventually—he checks that cabinet, he notices the register, he does the arithmetic. We have a window, and windows close." She glanced back down the street toward the archive, toward the man behind the desk who was even now smiling at some new arrival, warm oil and bottomless eyes. "We draft the complaint tonight. Everything. Logs, ledger, testimony, all of it, sealed and sent before he can—"

She stopped.

Felix followed her gaze. Across the street, outside the archive's tall window, Reeve had risen from his desk and was speaking on the telephone, his back to the room, his free hand resting flat on the oak as though steadying himself against news. Even at this distance, even through old glass, Felix could see the change in his posture—the performance dropped, the warmth gone, a man receiving information and deciding, in real time, what to do about it.

"He's calling someone," Wren said quietly.

"Who?"

"I don't know. But Callum—" Wren's jaw tightened. "Nobody calls from that desk unless something's already wrong. And we've been gone four minutes."

The wind came off the harbor, sharp with salt and diesel, carrying the cry of gulls and the far-off groan of the closed lighthouse that had not spoken in years. Felix clutched the camera to her side and understood, with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life tracking what the sea takes, that the tide had just turned against them.

"We need to move," she said. "Now."
