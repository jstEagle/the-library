## 21. Boil Season

The season turned the way seasons do on a barrier island—overnight, and without apology. One week the morning air still carried the green bite of late summer; the next, it smelled of salt and drying marsh grass, of change, of things letting go. Coral stood at the top of the dune crossover with her clipboard under her arm and watched the town of Sandpiper Point come up the beach in a loose, chattering line, and she felt something she had not felt on this sand in ten years.

She felt like hosting a party.

"Is that the Hendersons?" Elias said beside her, shading his eyes. "They brought their grandkids."

"They brought half the elementary school." Coral counted—out of habit, then caught herself, then let herself do it anyway, because this time the counting was a kind of joy. "Fourteen kids. Maybe sixteen. There's another car parking."

Six weeks ago, the sight of strangers on her beach would have tightened every muscle she owned. The nesting beach had been hers the way grief was hers: private, guarded, not to be touched. She had catalogued eighty-three nests with clinical precision and told herself that precision was professionalism, that the flatness in her voice when she gave public presentations was composure, that the heart was a trainable muscle and she had simply trained hers very well.

She knew better now. She had known better for a while. Knowing it and living it were different animals, and living it looked like this: a September Saturday with the tide low and generous, sixty townspeople carrying buckets and flashlights and folding chairs, and nest nineteen—the famous one, the midnight one, the one whose boil she and Elias had guided by lantern light while the world slept—due to erupt any day now.

"You're smiling," Elias said. He said it like an accusation, but his voice was warm as sun-baked wood.

"I'm allowed."

"You're allowed," he agreed. "I just want to document it. For science." He patted the camera hanging at his hip—one of his father's old ones, restored, the leather strap replaced with cord Elias had braided himself during the long evenings after dinner, when they sat on her porch or his and traded small domesticities like people who had been doing it for years instead of weeks.

Coral bumped her shoulder against his arm. It still astonished her, how easy the gesture was. How little it cost. For a decade she had rationed touch like a woman stranded with a finite water supply, and now here was this man beside her, solid and salt-smelling and hers in the quiet, unannounced way that mattered most, and she could lean against him on a public dune in front of the Hendersons and nobody would think anything of it because everybody already thought everything of it.

Maren had told her so, the last time she'd called. "The whole town's had a pool going since the hospital," she'd said, laughing. "You two were the worst-kept secret on the coast. Aunt Dee had money on you before you did."

Below them, the crowd gathered where Coral's stakes and screens marked the active nests. She had given the same orientation speech forty times over the years—to volunteers, to interns, to the occasional skeptical county official—and she gave it now, but differently. Looser. She told them about loggerhead incubation, about how the sex of the hatchlings depended on the temperature of the sand, about how the babies would wait beneath the surface until some signal none of them fully understood—a cooling, a collective decision, a consensus reached in the dark—told them it was time.

"And then what happens?" called a small girl in the front row, maybe seven, with a gap where a front tooth should be.

"Then they boil," Coral said. "All at once, all together. Like popcorn."

"Like popcorn," the girl repeated reverently, and the whole front row giggled, and Coral felt the laugh move through her own chest, easy and unguarded, and did not hate it at all.

* * *

Ninety minutes later, the sand began to shiver.

Coral saw it first—or rather, she felt it, that trained instinct honed across a hundred boils, a tremor in the substrate like the beach itself taking a breath. She raised a hand and the crowd went quiet in a way that still amazed her, sixty people hushing to near-silence because a biologist asked, because they wanted to be worthy of what came next.

The sand dimpled. Collapsed. And then the first head broke through—small, dark, determined—and behind it came the rest, dozens of them, dozens more, the nest boiling up in a rush of flailing flippers and flying sand exactly like popcorn, exactly like the girl had said, and the crowd made a sound Coral had never heard a crowd make: not a gasp, not a cheer, but something lower and more wondering, a collective exhale of awe.

"Flashlights off," she called softly. "Phones away. We walk them down with the moonlight only. Nobody touches unless I say."

And then she did what she had done alone for ten years—knelt at the edge of the boil and began to guide, clearing the path of debris, turning back the few hatchlings confused by porch light glow from the town behind them—but she did not do it alone. Elias knelt on her left. Maren, who had driven down for the weekend, knelt on her right. The Henderson grandchildren formed a corridor with their bodies, solemn as honor guards, and old man Pruitt who had spent thirty years complaining about turtle restrictions on his fishing routes crouched at the waterline waving off a gull with his hat and muttering, "Not today, not today, not on my watch."

One hundred and twelve hatchlings made the sea that evening. Coral knew the number—not because she needed it, not because the number was a wall between her and the world, but because the number was a gift. One hundred and twelve small lives, each no bigger than a bottle cap, paddling out into the dark Atlantic against odds she could recite in her sleep. One in a thousand would return to this beach to nest. She used to find that statistic devastating. Tonight it felt like hope with teeth: the ocean kept what it loved, and so, she was learning, did she.

Elias found her afterward, when the crowd had thinned to stragglers and the sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, purple and gold at once. He came up behind her at the waterline and wrapped his arms around her waist, resting his chin on her shoulder, both of them sandy and salt-sticky and wrung out with happiness.

"One hundred and twelve," he said into her ear.

"One hundred and twelve."

"My father would have loved this." He said it easily now, the way he'd learned to say his father's name over the past months—grief still in it, but company too. "He used to talk about the hatchlings. Said the light wasn't really for the boats. Said it was for everything out there trying to find its way home."

Coral turned in his arms. His face was rough with stubble and lit gold at the edges by the dying day, and she looked at him and felt the full strange architecture of their story rise around them: the letters, the lighthouse, the drowning night, the decade of almosts. Ten years ago she had written her first letter to a stranger because a stranger was safe. Now the stranger's arms were around her on the beach where her brother died, and she was not drowning. She was standing on the sand, breathing salt air, held.

"I have something for you," she said.

"For me? On boil night? You shouldn't have."

"Shut up and come sit down."

* * *

They sat at the base of the dune, backs against the weathered crossover posts, the lighthouse rising behind them—reprieved, repainted below the gallery, its lantern room dark but dignified, waiting for the relighting ceremony the town council had finally, officially scheduled. Elias had been climbing it every few days, restoring the gallery rail bolt by bolt. Some evenings Coral climbed with him and they watched the sunset from the top like royalty surveying a kingdom of water.

From her bag, Coral took two envelopes.

They were soft at the corners, handled often. The one in her left hand was addressed in her own handwriting to *Elias Marsh*. The one in her right was addressed in his handwriting to *Coral Ravensworth*. Neither had ever been mailed. Both had been written in the last week, independently, secretly—because Maren, meddlesome and wise, had suggested it at dinner one night: "You two spent ten years saying everything in letters. Now you can't say anything at all out loud because you're too busy being happy. Write it down. Sign your names this time."

They had laughed at the suggestion. Then, separately, in separate houses, on separate nights, they had each done it.

"On three?" Elias said, when she explained.

"On three."

They tore the envelopes open at the same moment. The pages inside caught the last light, and Coral read hers—his words to her—in the blue dusk, her heart beating in the old familiar letter-reading rhythm, that cadence her body had never forgotten even after the correspondence ended and the truth arrived and everything changed:

*Coral—*

*I've been trying to write this letter for ten years, which is funny, because I've written you hundreds. But every one before this was wearing a costume. Tide's voice. Safe, anonymous, useful. This one is just mine.*

*Here is what I know now that I didn't know then: I didn't start writing to you to comfort you. I started writing because I couldn't carry my father's death alone, and you were the only other person alive who understood what that night cost. I told myself I was helping you grieve Danny. Really, we were holding each other up, and neither of us would admit it.*

*I love you. I want to say it plainly, in ink, with my real name attached, the way I couldn't for ten years. I love the way you count things when you're happy. I love that you cry at hatchlings and not at funerals. I love that you stayed on this awful, beautiful point all these years keeping watch over nests and ghosts. I love that you chose the truth when it would have been easier to keep hiding.*

*My father spent his life keeping a light for strangers. I spent ten years writing letters to one. Turns out we're the same kind of stubborn.*

*Yours — actually, truly, signed —*
*Elias*

Coral read it twice. When she looked up, Elias was watching her, his own letter trembling slightly in his hand, his eyes bright.

"Read yours out loud," he said. "I'll trade you."

So they traded, there in the darkening dunes with the surf keeping time, and Coral heard her own words in his voice, and it undid her in the gentlest possible way:

*Elias—*

*I rehearsed one sentence for ten years and never managed to say it out loud until I said it to you on this beach. So you'd think a letter would be easy. It isn't. Letters were our hiding place, and I'm tired of hiding places, even beautiful ones.*

*But Maren's right, and you know she's right, so here it is, signed: I was counting turtles tonight and I realized I've stopped counting to keep from feeling and started counting to celebrate, and that is entirely your fault. You walked into the cottage next door with sand on your hands and familiarity in your voice and you dismantled me the way you dismantle everything—carefully, patiently, bolt by bolt.*

*I loved you before I knew your name. I loved the name before I knew the man. Now I know both, and I'm not going anywhere. My brother taught me, in the worst way, that time is not guaranteed. So I'm spending mine here, with you, on this point, under that light.*

*Come home. That's all. Come home.*

*Coral Ravensworth*
*(the C.R., at last, in full)*

When she finished reading, neither of them spoke for a long moment. The first stars were out over the water. Somewhere down the beach, the Hendersons' grandchildren were still shrieking happily about turtles, and the sound carried on the wind like small bells.

"So," Elias said finally, his voice rough. "That's settled, then."

"That's settled."

He kissed her then, unhurried, the taste of salt between them, and above them the old lighthouse stood black and patient against the deepening sky, its gallery rail gleaming faintly where Elias's hands had polished it, waiting—like both of them had waited—for the night someone would finally turn the light back on.

Two weeks, the council had said. Two weeks until the relighting.

Coral leaned her head against Elias's shoulder and watched the waves roll in, wave after wave after wave, and thought: *some tides you survive.* She was beginning to understand the rest of it.
