## 2. Salt and Second Chances

The storm came in off the Atlantic on the third night of October, the way storms always had in Porthollow—without apology, without warning worth trusting, arriving like a relative who knows you'll let them in.

Xanthe heard it first as a change in the sound of the sea. He had been sitting at the desk in his room above the inn, transcribing Caspian Vane's rambling third session into something resembling prose, when he realized the window behind him was no longer rattling with wind but booming with it, a deep percussive thud like a fist against the whole side of the building. The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, Mrs. Penhale's dog began to howl.

He should have stayed at the desk. There was a discipline to ghostwriting that he had spent eleven years perfecting: you sat where you were put, you wrote what you were told, you did not follow strange sounds into strange weather. The heart was a trainable muscle. He had trained his to do nothing at all.

But then he saw the light.

It came from across the courtyard, from the old mill house—a lantern swinging wild, its glow strobing through rain-lashed glass, and beneath it a figure moving along the mill's outer wall toward the sluice gate, small and quick and unmistakably familiar even at a distance of forty years' worth of memory compressed into forty yards of storm.

Merrin Tallow was going down to the water alone.

* * *

The mill house stood at the elbow of the river where it bent to meet the tide, and its floodgate was the village's oldest argument with the sea. Xanthe remembered his father complaining about it; remembered Merrin's father oiling its iron hinges every spring with the patience of a man who understood that the sea never stopped trying. The gate had been failing for years, everyone said. Everyone said someone ought to do something.

Everyone, apparently, except the one person who actually did.

By the time Xanthe reached the mill yard, rain had already found the seam between his collar and his neck and was running down his spine in an icy finger. The lantern light bobbed at the top of the sluice stairs. He climbed after it, shouting her name over the wind, and she turned—and there she was, hood thrown back because hoods were useless here and she'd known it since childhood, rain plastering dark hair to her face, eyes bright with something between fury and relief.

"You shouldn't be out," she shouted back.

"Neither should you."

"I live here." A gesture at the mill, vast and black behind her, its wheel locked fast against the surge. "You're just visiting."

The word *visiting* went through him like a splinter. He didn't argue. She was already turning back to the gate, and he saw the problem at once: the lower hinge pin had sheared, or rusted through, and the gate hung crooked in its frame, letting the rising tide push a foot of churning water into the millrace where it had no business being. Another hour of this and the water would find the wheel housing. Another hour after that, the foundations.

"There's a spare pin in the boathouse," she called. "Iron. My father kept two."

"Your father's been gone eight years."

"And his pins are still better than anything the ironmonger sells." She was already moving, sure-footed on wet stone, and Xanthe followed because following her was apparently still the default setting of his body, some eleven years of deliberate reprogramming notwithstanding.

* * *

The boathouse door stuck. It had always stuck. He hit it with his shoulder exactly where he'd hit it at seventeen, and it gave exactly the way it used to give, and for one disorienting second he was seventeen again, breathless and laughing, Merrin beside him with a stolen bottle of cider and salt on her lips—

He shut that door inside himself as firmly as he'd opened the other one.

The spare pin hung on a nail, wrapped in oilcloth, exactly where a careful man would have left it for a daughter who might need it after him. Xanthe carried it out into the storm and found that his hands remembered things too: how to brace against the gate's dead weight, how to seat the pin's head while she drove it home with the long iron mallet from the tool shed, how to read her movements without words. *Left. Higher. Now.* They worked in a rhythm they had never rehearsed in this decade, only in another one, and the horror of it—that his body could simply pick up a conversation his mind had sworn was finished—was worse than any cold the sea could throw at him.

The gate seated with a groan and a shudder. The water in the millrace began, grudgingly, to drop.

They stood back, soaked through, chests heaving, and watched the lantern light shiver over the dark churn. Merrin pushed her hair out of her face and laughed—a short, startled bark of a laugh, the same laugh, God help him, the exact same laugh.

"Well," she said. "That would have been a bad night."

"That would have been a bad decade." He meant it lightly. It came out heavier than he intended, and she looked at him sharply, and he busied himself coiling the rope that didn't need coiling.

"The innkeeper said you were here for Caspian Vane," she said, after a moment. "The writer. You're writing his book?"

"His memoirs. I'm—" The old reflex rose smooth and practiced. "A researcher. I assemble material."

"You're a ghostwriter." She said it flatly, not cruelly, the way you'd say *you're a carpenter*. "Village telegraph, Xanthe. Mrs. Penhale has known since Tuesday."

Of course she had. Porthollow had never needed the internet; it had Mrs. Penhale. He felt the familiar urge to qualify, to diminish, to explain that ghostwriting was honorable work, skilled work, work that paid well and asked nothing of you but your absence. He swallowed it. Something about the rain made lying feel especially pointless tonight.

"It pays well," is what he said instead.

"And it means nobody ever knows it's you." She picked up the lantern. "Come inside. You're blue around the mouth and I have soup whether you want it or not."

* * *

The kitchen of the mill house was unchanged in the way that only poor kitchens are unchanged—the same scrubbed table, the same black range, the same row of chipped enamel mugs on the same nail. But there were differences, and the differences were the thing that undid him. Her father's chair was gone. In its place, a workbench under the window, scattered with brass fittings and a jeweler's loupe, and pinned above it, a diagram of the floodgate mechanism drawn in a precise, confident hand.

She lived alone here. He understood it before she said a word.

"My father left me the mill," she said, setting soup in front of him—fish soup, thick with leeks, tasting of a recipe he had no right to remember this well. "Everyone told me to sell. The bank told me to sell twice." She sat opposite him, wrapping both hands around her own mug. "I told them the mill keeps the river honest. Somebody has to mind the gate."

"Eleven years," he said. "Alone."

"Ten years, eleven months." She said it without accusation, which was somehow infinitely worse than accusation. "And not entirely alone. There were offers. There was a man from Truro who wanted to marry me and move me somewhere with central heating." A small smile. "I couldn't leave the gate."

"Why not?" His voice came out rougher than he wanted. "Merrin. Why stay? This village gave you nothing but—"

"This village gave me everything but you." She said it quietly, looking into her mug, and the rain filled the silence that followed, drumming on the slate roof like applause for a truth neither of them could take back.

He set down his spoon. His hands were not steady. "I'm sorry."

"I know." She looked up then, and her eyes were dry and level and very old in a way that thirty-one-year-old eyes should not be. "That's the worst part, Xanthe. I always knew you were sorry. Sorry people write letters. Sorry people come back. You just—" She stopped herself, shook her head. "No. Not tonight. Tonight we fixed a gate together and you're eating my father's soup and that's enough. That's more than I expected to get."

"What did you expect to get?"

"Nothing." She smiled again, and this time it reached all the way up, tired and true. "That's what eleven years teaches you. Expect nothing, and the floodgate holds."

* * *

He stayed until the storm eased past midnight, helping her check the race by lantern light, standing beside her in the doorway while she listened to the wheel turn free and easy in the falling water. Small things kept happening that he could not account for: she handed him a towel and he took it left-handed because she'd held it out to his left hand, knowing; he reached the high shelf for the tin of tea without being asked, because he was the tall one, because he had always been the tall one. They moved around each other in the narrow kitchen like dancers returning to a routine their muscles had secretly kept in trust.

Walking back across the courtyard near one in the morning, wrung out and warm in a way that frightened him more than the flood ever had, Xanthe made himself say the disciplined thing. Made himself feel the disciplined feeling. *This is research,* he told the man in the mirror of the inn's dark hallway. *Material. A chapter about tides and gates and the girl who stayed.*

But discipline, he was beginning to suspect, was just another kind of unsent letter.

He was halfway up the stairs when he heard voices below—Mrs. Penhale, up far too late, talking to someone in the taproom. And one of the voices, low and amused and carrying that particular note of a man enjoying his own legend, belonged to Caspian Vane.

"—the letter, my dear woman. The letter changes everything. I've had it for years. Wait until the festival."

Xanthe stood on the stairs in the dark, water still dripping from his coat, and felt the cold come back into him all at once.
