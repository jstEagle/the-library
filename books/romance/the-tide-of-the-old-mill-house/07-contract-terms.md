## 7. Contract Terms

The email arrived at half past six in the morning, when the light through the inn's window was still the color of weak tea and Xanthe had been awake long enough to have memorized every crack in the ceiling plaster. He had learned, over eleven years of other people's lives, to read his inbox the way a doctor reads a chart—quickly, clinically, braced for the worst. But nothing in his training prepared him for the subject line:

**FESTIVAL KEYNOTE — FINAL RUN OF SHOW — VANE**

He opened it because it was addressed to him, because he was, on paper, Caspian Vane's collaborator, his amanuensis, his ghost. The attachment was a run-of-show document, forty-one pages of stage directions and cue timings for the autumn tide festival's centerpiece event. He scrolled with the numb efficiency of a man defusing something, and then his thumb stopped moving.

*Item 14. Climax — The Letter.*

*Caspian will read, in full, "the letter that changed everything" — an unsent confession discovered (per approved legend) among the effects of a deceased mill keeper. This is the emotional keystone of the keynote and of Chapter Nine of the memoir. House lights down. Single followspot. Do not rush the final paragraph; let it land.*

There it was. His letter. *His* letter—the one he had written at nineteen with ink gone blotchy from his own shaking hand, sealed, carried to the post office in Porthollow, and then carried back out again unmailed because he could not make his fingers let go of it. The letter that had lived for eleven years in a tin box, then in a jacket pocket, then—God knew how—in the possession of a junk dealer, and now in the possession of Caspian Vane, who intended to read it aloud under a followspot to four hundred villagers as the crowning jewel of *his* legend.

Xanthe set the phone face-down on the bedspread. Then he picked it up again, because he was the kind of man who read painful things twice, hoping the second reading would soften them, and it never did.

The approved legend. That was the phrase that did it, more than anything else. Not stolen, exactly—Caspian would say he'd *found* it, or inherited it, or dreamed it; the provenance would be vague in the way that made legends durable. And Xanthe had signed a contract that said, in clause after clause after clause, that everything Caspian Vane remembered, invented, or purchased belonged to Caspian Vane. Including, apparently, the truest thing Xanthe had ever written.

He got up and dressed without showering, which he noticed only later, and went downstairs into the courtyard where the tide festival was already assembling itself around him like a body around a wound. Bunting between the inn and the chandlery. A man testing a microphone that coughed feedback into the gulls. Children chalk-drawing tides on the cobbles that the morning damp was already erasing. Everyone preparing to celebrate a village he had once fled, on behalf of a man who was about to eat the last honest sentence Xanthe had ever written.

Merrin was across the courtyard at the mill, hauling a crate of festival pamphlets up the steps. She looked up as if she felt his eyes—which she always had, which was the whole catastrophe of her—and lifted one hand, not quite a wave. Something in her face was still bruised from their argument at the mill, from the word *forgery*, which he had chosen out of all the available words because it was the most cowardly one. She had looked at him then the way you look at a door that has closed on your own hand.

He raised his hand back. It was the best he could do before seven in the morning, and it was not enough, and he knew it.

* * *

His editor called at nine.

Nadia Okafor had ghost-edited half his career from a desk in London, a woman who communicated primarily in sighs calibrated to specific failures. When she picked up, he could hear the sea behind him and the keyboard behind her, two different weathers.

"You've seen the run of show," she said. It wasn't a question. Someone at the publisher had forwarded it to her with a flag.

"I've seen item fourteen."

"And?"

"And it's my letter, Nadia."

"It's *in his book*, is what it is." Her voice went flat and professional, the voice she used when she was about to hurt someone efficiently. "Xanthe. Sit down, wherever you are."

"I'm standing in a courtyard."

"Sit anyway. Clause eleven. Non-disclosure, materials and provenance. Anything you draft, discover, or acquire in service of the memoir is work-for-hire. His name goes on it. If you stand up at that keynote and claim authorship, you're in breach, and the penalty clause isn't theoretical—it's the advance clawback, it's legal costs, and it's every publisher in London learning that the ghost who broke Vane's NDA will break theirs. You know how this industry works. We don't punish people for lying. We punish them for telling the truth at the wrong volume."

"He's going to read a love letter I wrote to a woman I abandoned, and tell four hundred people it proves *he* once loved someone."

"Yes," Nadia said. "And in eighteen months it'll be a paperback, and in five years it'll be taught in a workshop somewhere, and no one will ever know it was yours, because you built a career on no one ever knowing anything was yours. That was the deal you made with yourself, love. I'm sorry. I genuinely am. But you signed it in your own handwriting, and God knows your handwriting is legible enough—you've told me often enough how proud you are of it."

He laughed, because there was nothing else to do with it. "You're saying stay quiet."

"I'm saying think about whether the letter is worth the rest of your life. Because that's the price tag. That's the whole invoice." A pause, and then, softer, in the voice she saved for maybe twice a year: "Unless it isn't. In which case, God help you, but do it with your eyes open."

She hung up. The sea kept going behind him, indifferent, tidal, doing the one thing it knew how to do.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day doing what he did best, which was disappearing into work. He transcribed three hours of Caspian's rambling dictation about a fishing trip that had possibly never happened. He restructured a chapter about grief so that it landed harder, so that the fictional Caspian seemed to feel things more deeply than the real one ever had. He was, he realized somewhere around page forty, doing it again—polishing another man's soul while his own sat in a drawer somewhere, unsent.

At four o'clock Caspian summoned him to the big house above the harbor for a rehearsal.

Caspian Vane received people in a glass-walled room furnished to look like a fisherman's cottage, all distressed wood and artful nets, a set designer's idea of honesty. He was seventy-one and beautiful the way driftwood is beautiful, bleached and sculpted by weather into something that no longer needed to be alive to be admired. He stood at a lectern with the run-of-show pages in front of him and greeted Xanthe with genuine warmth, which was somehow worse than cruelty.

"Xanthe! Good. Listen—" he tapped item fourteen— "I want your professional ear on this. The letter. It's the heart of the whole book, the whole *life*, really. I've marked where I think the pause goes." He cleared his throat, adjusted his glasses, and began to read aloud.

It was obscene, hearing it in that voice. *Merrin—if you are reading this, then I failed to come back, which means I failed at the only thing I ever promised anyone.* Caspian read it beautifully. That was the unforgivable part. He gave the sentences a tremor they hadn't had on the page, a lifetime's weight, and Xanthe stood by the window with his hands in his pockets and watched his nineteen-year-old heart get performed by a man who had never meant anything he said in his life.

"Well?" Caspian lowered the pages. "Too much?"

"It's very moving," Xanthe heard himself say.

"The junk dealer swears the family wanted it burned. Can you imagine? Burning this." Caspian held the letter up between two fingers, reverent, proprietary. "Some things survive for a reason, don't you think? They wait." He smiled, and for one strange moment his eyes were almost kind. "You understand better than anyone, Xanthe. You've spent your whole career giving other men their souls. There's a generosity in that. There's also—" he folded the letter away— "a kind of vanishing."

* * *

The inn room had a mirror above the washbasin, old glass gone slightly silver at the edges, so that whoever stood before it appeared framed in fog, like a photograph developing or drowning.

Xanthe had avoided it for six days. This was not unusual—he had been avoiding mirrors, in one way or another, since he was twenty-two, since the first time a byline went out under someone else's name and he'd caught his reflection in a train window looking like a man traveling without luggage. Ghosts didn't need mirrors. Mirrors were for people with faces.

But tonight he stood in front of it with the bathroom light on and made himself look.

The man looking back was thirty-three and tired in a way sleep couldn't fix. He had his mother's jaw and his father's habit of lowering his eyes. He looked, Xanthe thought, like exactly what he was: a man who had gotten very good at being furniture in his own story.

"You wrote it," he said to the mirror. His voice sounded strange in the small room, stripped of performance. "You wrote the letter. You wrote it and you couldn't send it because you were afraid she'd find out you weren't worth waiting for. And then you left anyway, which proved you right, and you've spent eleven years being right at the expense of everything else."

The man in the mirror said nothing. He was good at that.

"You let Caspian buy your one true thing for the price of a junk dealer's afternoon. You signed the contract. You lied to her face—at the mill, with the water running—and she knew, Merrin has always known, she can read you like weather. And tomorrow night he's going to stand under a spotlight and read your apology to the woman you love, and you're going to sit in the audience and applaud, because Nadia's right, because the penalty is real, because the industry doesn't punish liars, only truth-tellers at the wrong volume."

His hands were flat on the basin now. The glass had fogged at the center from his breath.

"But here's the thing," he said quietly. "Here's the actual thing. You keep waiting for forgiveness like it's going to arrive from outside. From her, if you're brave enough to ask. From the village, from Caspian, from some editor's blessing. But the person who has to forgive you first is standing right here, and he's been waiting eleven years, and he is so tired."

The fogged patch in the mirror was clearing from the edges inward, the way tide leaves a stone.

"You're not a ghost," he told the man in the glass. "Ghosts are dead people who don't know it. You're worse—you're alive and acting like it. And tomorrow night, either you stand up, or you spend the rest of your life as a footnote in a fraud's memoir, and honestly? Honestly, I think you'd rather be ruined than erased. I think you finally would."

The man in the mirror looked, for the first time in years, like someone Xanthe might be able to live with. Not forgiven yet. But willing to be.

He slept badly and briefly, and woke before dawn with his heart going like a drum on the harbor, and lay in the grey listening to the tide come in over the flats—higher than it should have been, higher than the almanac promised, the water climbing the mill's old steps with a sound like something returning.

By full light, the lane to the mill house was underwater, and Merrin's kitchen window glowed orange against the dark, and she was inside alone.
