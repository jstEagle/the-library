## 4. Terms and Conditions

**Orsen**

Pru Halloway's office smelled like old paper and lemon polish, and it was the only neutral territory in Willow Creek where both of us could sit without one of us storming out. I knew this because we'd already tried three other locations.

The diner had lasted four minutes — Wren had walked in, seen me in the corner booth, and walked back out with the observation that she'd "rather negotiate in a dumpster." The bakery itself was off the table for obvious reasons; she wasn't about to let me behind her counter any earlier than legally necessary. And the orchard — well. We'd tried the orchard, and I'd made the mistake of commenting on how much the Gravensteins had grown, and she'd informed me that a man who couldn't tell a Gravenstein from a Gala had no business admiring them.

So: Pru's office. Two chairs on opposite sides of a conference table that Pru had clearly cleared of everything except the will, two glasses of water, and a box of tissues she'd placed dead center like a warning.

"Before we begin," Pru said, settling into her chair with the air of a woman who had refereed estate disputes between siblings, spouses, and once an entire church board, "I want to remind you both that Ida Dunmore drafted this document herself, reviewed it twice, and refused all my suggestions to make it, quote, 'less of a headache.' She said headaches build character."

"Of course she did," Wren muttered.

I kept my eyes on the will. It was easier than looking at Wren, who sat across from me with her arms crossed and her jaw set at an angle I remembered from when we were eleven and I'd knocked her into the creek. Twelve years, and she still had the same stubborn line to her chin. Twelve years, and I still felt like the kid who owed her an apology the size of a county.

"The terms are straightforward," Pru continued. "Both heirs must operate Dunmore's Orchard Bakery as co-owners through the conclusion of the Harvest Festival — that's October fourteenth — or the property reverts to the town trust, which, I'll remind you, would sell it to the highest bidder. Probably the developers who've been circling Main Street for years."

Wren went very still. I understood why. The bakery wasn't just a building to her; I'd gathered that much in our first hostile forty-eight hours. It was Ida's kitchen, Ida's recipes, Ida's whole life pressed into flour and stone fruit. The idea of it becoming condos was, apparently, the one thing that could make her sit at a table with me.

"Fine," Wren said. "We run it together until the festival. Then he sells his half to me, and this is over."

"That's not—" Pru began.

"I have conditions," Wren said, talking over her, and pulled a folded piece of paper from her jacket like she'd been carrying it around waiting for exactly this moment. She smoothed it flat on the table. "Ground rules."

I looked at the paper. It was handwritten, numbered, and thorough in a way that suggested she'd spent at least one sleepless night on it. My chest did something complicated about that — the evidence that she'd been thinking about me, even if only as a problem to be managed.

"May I?" I asked, reaching for my own bag.

"Oh, you have *conditions* too," Wren said flatly.

"I came prepared."

"Of course you did." But there was something almost grudging in it, a flicker of respect she tried to smother before it could take hold.

Pru looked between us with the expression of a woman recalculating her fee. "I'll just... observe."

* * *

**Wren**

His list was typed. Of course it was typed. Orsen Dunmore had left this town at nineteen with a duffel bag and no goodbye, and he'd come back twelve years later a man who typed up his ground rules like he was submitting a grant proposal.

I read his list anyway, because I'm not petty enough to refuse reading something just because it's neat.

*1. All operational decisions require mutual agreement.*
*2. Financial records open to both parties at all times.*
*3. Shifts divided equally, alternating weeks.*
*4. Personal history is off-limits during working hours.*

That last one stopped me. Not because I disagreed — the opposite. Because he'd written down the thing I most wanted, which meant he knew I wanted it, which meant he'd thought about what questions I might ask, which meant somewhere in that marine-biologist brain of his he'd been bracing for me to interrogate him about why he left.

Good. He should brace.

"Number four," I said. "Define 'working hours.'"

He blinked. "The hours we're working."

"You're staying above the bakery. In Ida's apartment. Which means technically you're always at work."

A muscle jumped in his jaw. "The apartment was part of the inheritance."

"The apartment has one entrance, and it goes through my kitchen."

"I noticed," he said, dry as August. "Believe me, I've noticed. I've been doing a kind of obstacle course every morning to avoid you."

Something in me wanted to laugh at that and I strangled it immediately. No laughing. Laughing was how he used to get out of things. Laughing was his native language, and I'd forgotten that for about six hours after he showed up, and then remembered it hard when I caught myself smiling at some terrible joke about tide pools and hated myself for a full day.

"Amendments," I said, and clicked my pen. "One: separate counters. You work the register side, I work the ovens. You don't touch my starter."

"Your starter?"

"She has a name."

"She has— fine. I won't touch the named sourdough."

"Two: split shifts don't alternate weekly. They're daily. You open, I close, handoff at noon, fifteen minutes overlap maximum. Less time for—" I gestured vaguely at him. "*This.*"

"This being professionalism?"

"This being whatever you think you're doing with your face right now."

Pru made a small sound that might have been a suppressed laugh or a prayer for strength.

Orsen leaned back in his chair. Up close, in decent light, I could see the years on him — a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there at nineteen, gray coming in early at his temples, a tiredness around his eyes that didn't match a man of thirty-one. I catalogued these details against my will and then resented myself for each one.

"Counterproposal," he said. "Daily shifts are fine. But the noon handoff gets thirty minutes, because fifteen isn't enough time to reconcile the till, and I'm not signing onto a system where I get blamed for your arithmetic."

"*My* arithmetic? I've balanced Ida's books since I was twenty-two."

"And I've managed research budgets for teams of twelve. So we're both insufferably competent. Thirty minutes."

"Twenty."

"Twenty-five, and I'll take the morning deliveries, including Sam's honey order, which I understand involves lifting crates heavier than anything you can manage alone."

I narrowed my eyes. That was either courtesy or condescension, and I genuinely couldn't tell, and the not-knowing was its own small torture. "Sam's honey comes at seven. You're opening at five-thirty. Fine. Twenty-five minutes. But I keep the recipe book."

His face did something then — a flicker, quick as a fish turning. "Which recipe book?"

"The one in the kitchen. Leather cover. Ida's handwriting."

"I know the one," he said quietly, and for a second the hostility dropped out of the room entirely, and we were just two people who had both loved the same old woman and lost her, sitting in a lawyer's office with lemon polish in the air.

Then he straightened, and the wall went back up, brick by brick.

"Add it to the list," he said. "You keep the recipe book. I keep the ledgers."

"Deal."

* * *

**Orsen**

We shook hands on it, which was a mistake, because her hand was warm and calloused in the places dough had trained into her palms, and I held on half a second too long and watched her notice me noticing.

Pru slid a document across the table. "If you'd both sign here, acknowledging the terms of the will and your amended operating agreement. And I'll note — for the record, and because Ida made me promise to say this — that she anticipated every single one of these negotiations. There's a letter in the file addressed to 'the children, when they start acting like lawyers.' She asked me to hold it until, quote, 'they've drawn their little treaty.'"

Wren and I looked at each other for the first time in twenty minutes.

"When do we get it?" she asked.

"When I decide you need it," Pru said serenely. "Could be next week. Could be October. Ida trusted my judgment on timing, and I intend to honor that trust by enjoying it thoroughly."

"That's ominous," I said.

"That's Willow Creek," said Pru.

We signed. Her signature slanted left, mine slanted right, and on the page they looked like they were leaning away from each other, which felt accurate.

Outside, the afternoon had gone gold at the edges. Wren stuffed her list into her jacket and headed for her truck, then stopped, hand on the door, and turned around with the expression of someone about to do something against her better judgment.

"The noon handoff," she said. "Tomorrow. Don't be late."

"I'm never late."

"You were late to your own grandmother's funeral."

The words landed like a dropped pan. I stood there on the courthouse steps with my bag of typed conditions and took it, because I deserved it, because I had missed the funeral, because I'd been on a boat counting turtles when she died and told myself the research season couldn't be interrupted, and there was no version of that decision that survived daylight.

"I know," I said.

She studied me for a long moment, looking for the flinch, maybe, or the excuse. I didn't give her either. Then she got in her truck and drove off, and I stood there a while longer, watching the dust settle on Main Street, in the town I'd run from at nineteen and inherited at thirty-one along with a woman who had every reason to hate me and a treaty that fit on one page.

* * *

**Wren**

The first week of the treaty held the way a dam holds — technically, and with visible strain.

He opened. I closed. At noon we reconciled the till in a silence so complete you could hear the proofing dough breathing, if dough breathed, which Milo insisted it did and which I'd stopped arguing about because the boy needed his mysteries.

It should have been unbearable. Mostly it was. But there were moments — I'm admitting this under duress, to nobody, in my own head — moments where the machinery of him surprised me. He learned the regulars' orders in three days. Dot's rye toast, extra crisp, tea steeped seven minutes exactly. He fixed the wobble in the front case without being asked. On Thursday he covered my shift when Sam's bees swarmed into my yard and I spent four hours in a beekeeping suit learning new vocabulary.

He didn't ask me about the bees afterward. Rule four. But he left a slice of honey cake on my station with a sticky note that said *For the apiarist,* and I ate it standing up, furious, and it was the best thing I'd eaten all month, and I hated that too.

On Friday, the power flickered twice during the dinner rush prep, and I heard him upstairs stop moving, and then come halfway down the stairs, and stand there in the dark doorway like a man deciding whether to cross a border.

"Storm forecast," he said. "Coastal cell, hitting around midnight. Should I—"

"I've baked through storms since before you could read a nautical chart," I said.

"I know," he said. "I just thought you should know the forecast."

He went back upstairs. I stood in my kitchen holding a whisk and realized I'd braced for a fight and gotten weather information instead, and that somehow was worse.

At 11:40 that night, the lights went out for real.

* * *

**Orsen**

I heard the generator catch downstairs — Ida's old backup unit, temperamental, coughing like a two-pack-a-day man — and then I heard it die.

Then I heard nothing, which in a bakery at midnight is a specific and terrible silence, because I'd spent enough evenings now listening to the building's rhythms to know what should be there: the hum of the proofing cabinet, the tick of the deck oven cooling, the compressor in the walk-in. A hundred-year-old starter lives in that kitchen. Wren had mentioned it exactly once, in passing, with a softness in her voice she'd shown nowhere else, and I'd done the math immediately: no heat, no warmth, and the cold front rolling in off the water tonight could kill a century of sourdough by morning.

Rule four said no personal questions. Rule four said nothing about personal emergencies.

I put on my boots.

The kitchen was black except for the pilot lights, those small blue constellations along the ovens, and Wren was already there with a flashlight clenched in her teeth, dragging the proofing cabinet away from the wall.

"Don't," she said, around the flashlight.

"I haven't said anything."

"You were going to say 'I told you so' about the storm."

"I was going to say the pilot flames put out heat but not enough for a cold snap, and the old wood stove in the back room holds temperature for hours if you feed it, and Ida showed me how to bank it when I was fourteen."

The flashlight swung to my face. Even in the dark I could feel her weighing it — whether accepting help from me cost more than losing the starter.

"We banked it together," I added quietly. "Once. You remember."

A long silence. Rain started ticking against the windows.

"The wood room's through there," she said finally, and turned away, and that was as close to a thank-you as the treaty allowed.

We worked the rest of the night mostly without words — me feeding the stove, her wrapping the starter crock in towels and setting it near the warming bricks, both of us moving around each other in a kitchen built for two people who used to share it without thinking. Somewhere around three a.m. the rain turned serious, hammering the roof, and she sat down on an upturned crate with her flashlight off, conserving batteries, and I sat on the other side of the stove, and neither of us spoke, and it was the least hostile hour we'd spent in each other's company in twelve years.

I woke at dawn on the floor with flour on my sleeve I couldn't account for and the smell of bread already rising, and Wren was at the counter with her back to me, shaping loaves in the gray light like nothing had happened.

"The starter's fine," she said, without turning around. "Power's back. Go to bed."

"Wren."

"Don't." A pause. The dough slapped the counter, once, twice. "Thank you. There. It's said. It's off the record and it violates rule four retroactively, so we're even."

I went upstairs. Behind me, I heard her exhale — long, unsteady, the sound of someone whose armor had taken a hit and was being checked for cracks.

* * *

**Wren**

By Monday I'd rebuilt the walls and reinforced them. New rule, unofficial: no gratitude, no shared stoves, no remembering things together. The treaty was working. The treaty was *fine.*

Then Dot came in at seven-fifteen, settled onto her stool, and looked between me at the counter and Orsen restocking the register side with the narrow-eyed assessment of a woman who'd captained ferries through worse weather than this.

"Well," she announced to the room. "You two are exhausting."

"Good morning, Dot," we said, in unison, with identical bitterness, and then glared at each other for having done it.

Dot sipped her tea, deeply satisfied. "Ida used to say the two of you argued like it was a love language. Took me forty years to figure out what she meant." She set down her cup. "Anyway. Festival committee meets Thursday. You're both on it now. Congratulations."

"We're *what?*" 

But Dot was already buttering her toast, and across the counter, Orsen caught my eye — one second of shared, horrified understanding before we both looked away, treaty-bound, professional, and completely out of our depth.

Thursday, then. Committee meeting. Co-chairs, apparently.

I went back to my ovens and told myself the feeling in my chest was dread.

It mostly was.
