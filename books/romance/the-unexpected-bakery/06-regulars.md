## 6. Regulars

By the third week at Dunmore's Orchard Bakery, Orsen Dunmore had learned three things about small-town mornings.

First: the bread came out of the oven at six-forty, not six-thirty, no matter what the schedule said, because the oven had opinions and Ida's ghost apparently still negotiated with it. Second: Wren Ashby could slice a baguette, take a phone order, and glare at him simultaneously, a skill he suspected she'd developed specifically for his benefit. And third — the third thing was the one that kept catching him off guard, like a wave from an unexpected direction —

The regulars were coming for *him*.

He noticed it first with Dot.

Dot Hargrove arrived every morning at seven-oh-five in a pickup truck that predated most national holidays, wearing a canvas jacket with "FERRY CAPTAIN" embroidered on the pocket in thread that had faded to the color of weak tea. She'd run the Willow Creek–Harrow Point ferry for thirty-one years before retiring, and she ordered the same thing daily: one black coffee, one cardamom knot, and one piece of unsolicited commentary on whatever Orsen happened to be doing.

"You're holding that tray like it's going to bite you," she said on Monday, watching him ferry a dozen rolls to the case. "Loosen your wrists. You're a baker now, not a man defusing something."

"I'm a marine biologist," Orsen said. "Temporarily."

"Mm." Dot bit into her cardamom knot with the decisive air of a woman passing judgment on both pastry and person. "That's what my second husband said about being retired. Temporarily. He lasted nine days."

Orsen didn't ask what happened after nine days. He suspected the answer involved Dot, which it did — she told him anyway, unprompted, over the course of four consecutive mornings, until he knew the entire biography of her marriage to a man named Gus who'd once tried to sail to Alaska in a boat held together partly by epoxy and partly by stubbornness.

The strange thing — the thing Orsen kept turning over like an unfamiliar shell — was that he didn't mind. In the lab, in the field, in the graduate housing where he'd spent six years eating microwave rice above people whose names he never learned, conversation had been transactional. Data, deadlines, who's using the centrifuge. Here, Dot talked *at* him, generously, expecting nothing back except that he keep her coffee full, and somehow the monologue felt less like noise and more like... ballast.

"Your grandmother," Dot said one morning, apropos of nothing, "used to save me the end piece of the cinnamon loaf. Nobody else wanted it. She said captains get the ends because we see everything coming." She looked at him over her cup. "You've got her hands. Did you know that?"

Orsen looked down at his own hands, flour in the knuckle creases, a burn healing along one thumb. He hadn't known. He hadn't known anything about Ida Dunmore's hands, or her habits, or why she'd saved end pieces for a ferry captain, because he'd spent twelve years making sure he wouldn't.

"No," he said quietly.

"Well." Dot refilled her own coffee, which was against several health codes and entirely within her rights as a captain. "Now you do."

* * *

Milo was a different species altogether.

The boy was sixteen, all elbows and nervous energy, apprenticing at the bakery four mornings a week through some arrangement with the school that Pru Halloway had apparently brokered and Wren had grudgingly inherited. His job, officially, was dishes, deliveries, and dough prep. His actual job, as far as Orsen could tell, was asking questions.

"How do you know when the starter's hungry?"

"It gets looser. Smells sharper. Like —"

"Like nail polish?"

"That's actually — yes, exactly, that's acetone, it means —"

"And how come you know turtle hearts but not bread?" Milo asked this while scrubbing a sheet pan with the intensity of someone exfoliating a whale. "Wren says you study turtles."

"Sea turtles. Their physiology. How they navigate."

"So you're like, a doctor for turtles."

"A researcher, mostly. I count them. Track where they go."

Milo paused mid-scrub. "You *count* turtles? For money?"

"For science."

"That's the best job I've ever heard of." The boy resumed scrubbing with renewed moral conviction. "Better than my last placement's guy, who sold gutters."

*Last placement.* The words dropped into the room and Milo moved past them so fast — already onto whether sourdough could be made with orchard apples, already asking if the oven light stayed on all night — that Orsen almost missed it. Almost. But he'd spent enough years around young researchers to recognize the flinch-and-pivot of someone who'd said more than they meant to.

He didn't push. He just handed Milo the next pan and said, "Gutters are underrated. Structural integrity matters."

Milo grinned up at him, gap-toothed, flour on his chin, and something in Orsen's chest did a slow, unfamiliar roll — the feeling of a current shifting under a boat you thought was anchored.

By Thursday, Milo had appointed himself Orsen's unofficial education committee. He wanted to know about the Azores. About nesting beaches. About whether turtles really cried when they laid eggs (they did, sort of — salt glands, not grief, though Orsen admitted grief wasn't entirely off the table). He asked questions the way gulls worked a fishing boat: relentlessly, joyfully, with total confidence that something would be given.

And Orsen gave. He caught himself explaining osmoregulation to a teenager at seven in the morning, drawing a diagram of a sea turtle's salt gland on the back of a receipt, and realized two things at once: one, that he hadn't talked about his work with this much delight since his first year of grad school; and two, that Wren was standing by the ovens watching them with an expression he couldn't file anywhere — not soft, exactly, but no longer armored. Something between suspicion and surrender.

She turned away before he could name it.

* * *

Sam arrived with honey.

This was not a metaphor. Sam Okafor, beekeeper, appeared every Saturday at eight sharp with a jar under each arm like a man delivering state secrets, and would not leave until someone tasted whatever the hive had produced that month. The first Saturday, Wren took the jars. The second Saturday, Sam set one directly in front of Orsen and waited.

"Wildflower," Sam said. "Late season. Tell me what you get."

Orsen tasted it, because refusing seemed somehow dangerous. "It's..." He tried again, slower. "It tastes like the end of summer. Like things ripening too fast."

Sam's whole face changed — a quiet, lit-up satisfaction, as though Orsen had passed a test written in a language Sam rarely got to use. "*Yes.* That's exactly it. Most people say 'sweet' and stop there." He sat down across from Orsen uninvited, in the way of a man who'd decided the seating chart was a suggestion. "You taste like someone who pays attention."

"I count animals for a living. It's mostly attention."

"Mostly attention is the rarest kind there is." Sam turned his jar slowly on the table. "Ida used to trade me bread for honey. Thirty years. She always said my bees and her starter were the same animal — something alive you have to feed whether you feel like it or not." A pause, gentle as a landing. "She talked about you, you know. Every few months. 'My grandson the ocean man.' Never angry about it. Just keeping the door propped open."

Orsen's throat did something complicated. "I didn't know."

"Doors don't need you to know they're open," Sam said. "They just wait."

He left twenty minutes later, and Orsen sat with the honey jar a long time, turning it, the late-summer smell rising faintly even through the lid.

* * *

It was Dot who named the thing, because Dot named everything.

It was Friday, nearly ten, well past the end of Orsen's shift — his shift ended at nine, and he knew this, and he was nonetheless elbow-deep in teaching Milo how to shape boules without degassing them, and Dot was on her third coffee long after any reasonable captain should have sailed.

"You're still here," she observed.

"My shift ended an hour ago."

"Mm-hm." She sipped. "Funny how that keeps happening."

Orsen looked down at the dough, at Milo's lopsided attempt beside it, at the flour map of the morning spread across the counter. Through the window, the orchard rows ran gold and green toward the ridge. Somewhere out there Sam's bees were working the last of the apple bloom. Behind him, Wren was pulling a tray of festival-test rolls, and she handed him one without looking, the way you hand something to a person who's simply *there*, part of the furniture of the place, and he ate it standing up and it was the best thing he'd eaten in years and he couldn't have said why.

"In the navy," Dot said, to the window rather than to him, "we had a word for a crew that stops being a roster and starts being a unit. Takes about ninety days, usually. Sometimes faster, if the weather's bad." She finally looked at him, eyes shrewd and warm under the faded cap. "You've been here nineteen days, ocean man. Weather's been bad. Do the math."

"I'm leaving after the festival," Orsen said. It came out automatic, rehearsed, the sentence he'd been carrying in his duffel next to a letter he hadn't opened again since it arrived. "There's a research post. Two years. It's — it's everything I've worked for."

Dot considered this with the gravity of a woman who had weathered actual storms and found most other weather overrated.

"Sure," she said. "Probably true." She stood, gathered her jacket, left exact change plus a tip that suggested she was either generous or making a point. At the door she paused. "Your grandmother used to say the tide doesn't ask the harbor's permission. But the harbor," she added, tapping the doorframe twice, "remembers every ship that ever meant to come back."

Then she was gone, and the bell over the door settled, and the bakery hummed around him — oven tick, Milo's off-key humming, Wren scoring the next batch with quick sure cuts.

Orsen washed his hands. Tied the apron back on. His shift had ended an hour and ten minutes ago.

"Wren," he heard himself say. "Show me the shaping again? I lost the tension on the last one."

She glanced at him — one beat of surprise quickly filed away — and slid a bench scraper down the counter to him. "Watch my hands," she said. "You've got her hands. Might as well use them."

He froze. "Did Dot tell you that too?"

Wren's mouth curved, barely. "Ida did. Years ago. She used to hold her hands up next to photographs of you and complain that genetics had wasted good baker's hands on a boy who ran off to count fish."

"Turtles."

"Fish," Wren repeated serenely, "is funnier."

And Orsen laughed — actually laughed, surprised out of him, easy as breathing — and shaped his first decent boule of the season while the morning regulars drifted in and out around him like water finding its level.

That night, walking back to the cottage with flour still in his hair, he caught himself planning tomorrow. Not the fellowship timeline. Not the departure math he'd been doing in the margins of his notebook for two weeks. Tomorrow: Milo wanted to learn the croissant fold. Dot had threatened to bring photos of Gus's boat. Sam had mentioned, casually, that the hive by the east orchard wall might need checking soon, if anyone were interested.

If anyone were interested.

Orsen stopped halfway up the lane, in the blue hour, and realized with the mild shock of a man finding dry land underfoot that he had stayed past his shift every single day that week — and that somewhere in those nineteen days, without filing a request or signing anything, he'd been drafted into a crew.

The letter in his duffel weighed exactly what it had weighed before. But tonight, for the first time, it felt heavier than everything he'd be leaving behind.

Inside the cottage, he took it out and set it on the table, face-down, and went to sleep without opening it.

The deadline inside it — six weeks before the festival's end — sat in the dark like a tide chart, patient, waiting for him to do the math.
