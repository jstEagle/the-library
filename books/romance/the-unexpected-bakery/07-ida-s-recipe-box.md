## 7. Ida's Recipe Box

The recipe box sat on the highest shelf of the pantry, exactly where it had always lived, and Wren had been avoiding it for three days.

Not consciously. She'd simply found other things to do — re-caulking the prep sink, alphabetizing the tea tins twice, having a spirited argument with the dishwasher about whether it was dying or merely dramatic. But on Thursday morning, with the festival menu due to Pru Halloway's committee by Monday and a legal pad of half-formed ideas sitting reproachfully on the counter, she ran out of excuses.

"Okay," she told the box, hauling it down. It was heavier than she remembered, tin dented at one corner where Milo had knocked it off the counter when he was eleven and Ida had said, *good arm on that boy.* "You and me. We're going to make some decisions."

"You talk to boxes now?"

Wren didn't jump. She'd stopped jumping at Orsen Dunmore about two weeks ago, which was its own alarming development. He came through the back door with a crate of Sam's honey jars balanced on one hip, hair still damp from the morning fog, looking — annoyingly — like he belonged in her kitchen.

"It's not talking if the box can't hear me," she said. "It's delegation."

"Delegation." He set the crate down and peered into the pantry after her. "To a recipe box."

"To Ida, technically. The box is just the medium." She carried it to the big worktable and set it down between them like a challenge. "Festival menu's due Monday. Everything I know how to bake is already on this list." She tapped the legal pad. "Everything Ida knew how to bake is in here. I've been putting off going through it because—" she gestured vaguely at her own chest, "—all of that."

Orsen was quiet for a moment. Then he pulled out the stool across from her and sat, rolling up his sleeves like a man settling in for surgery.

"I'll do the reading," he said. "You do the vetoing."

"That's not how grief works, Dunmore."

"No," he agreed. "But it's how teamwork works. Come on. You read me the weird ones first. There are always weird ones."

There were always weird ones. Wren opened the lid and the smell rose up out of the tin — cardamom and old paper and something faintly smoky, like the ghost of a hundred wood-fired loaves. Her throat went tight. She'd spent more hours of her childhood in this kitchen than in any other room of her life, and most of those hours had been governed by this box.

She handed him the first handful of cards.

*Apple cider doughnuts, Ida's way — don't you dare use a ring mold.*

*Brown bread for funerals. Double the molasses. Grief is sweet work.*

*Rhubarb custard pie (for when Marlene Pruitt is being impossible).*

"Who's Marlene Pruitt?" Orsen asked.

"Committee chair before Pru Halloway. Ida feuded with her for nine years over the pie contest judging rubric." Wren took the card back and looked at it. "This pie won the feud. Marlene cried. It was beautiful."

Orsen laughed — really laughed, head tipped back, and Wren pretended to be very busy sorting cards so she wouldn't stare. Twelve years ago he'd laughed like that all the time, loud and unguarded, the kind of laugh that got them both shushed out of the library. Then he'd gone quiet for twelve years, and apparently all it took to bring it back was a dead woman's grudge pie.

They worked through the morning like that. He read; she vetoed or approved. The pile of approved cards grew: the funeral brown bread, a plum torte that made Orsen say *oh* out loud, something called Orchard Slab that appeared to be less a recipe than a dare. The sun climbed. Dot came in for her coffee and left without being served, muttering something about *young love doing inventory,* which Wren chose not to dignify.

It was nearly noon when Orsen went still over a card.

"What?" Wren said. "Is it the eel pie? Because I'm telling you now, we are not making the eel pie, that was a punishment recipe—"

"It's addressed to someone." He turned it around so she could see.

In Ida's square, unhurried handwriting, across the top of the card where a recipe title should be:

*For Wren. When you're twenty-five and stubborn.*

Below it, no ingredients, no instructions. Just: *You will want to fix everything yourself. Don't. Bread teaches patience. So does love. Ask for help before you're drowning, not after. — I.*

Wren stared at it. The kitchen noise — the fridge hum, the tick of the cooling oven — seemed to recede.

"She wrote me letters," she said slowly. "On recipe cards."

"Keep going," Orsen said quietly. "There might be more."

There were more. They sorted through the whole box twice, and by the end they'd found seven cards that weren't recipes at all. Two addressed only to Wren. Two only to Orsen — those he read silently, jaw working, and slid face-down beside his elbow without sharing, and Wren didn't push, though every curious bone in her body screamed. And three — three — addressed to both of them.

*For Wren and Orsen. Whenever you find these.*

The first one said: *A starter is a promise you keep daily. So is everything else worth having. Feed what you want to live.*

The second: *You will fight about the oven. Everyone fights about the oven. The trick is remembering you're on the same side of it.*

And the third, the one that made Wren's hands go cold on the tabletop:

*I have watched you two since you were small enough to fit in my flour bin together. I know how stories like yours go — somebody leaves, somebody waits, everybody gets proud. So here is your instruction, and I expect you to follow it the way you never followed my scone method: come back to each other before you're ready. Nobody is ever ready. Readiness is a lie pride tells. — Ida, who loves you both, and planned accordingly.*

*Planned accordingly.*

Wren read it four times. Then she looked up at Orsen, who had gone very pale in the way he did — she'd catalogued his faces by now, the sea-calm, the flinch, this one, the one that meant something had landed in his chest and cracked it open.

"Wren." His voice was rough. "When did she write these?"

"I don't know." Wren turned the card over, as if the date might be hiding there. Nothing. Just flour ground permanently into the fibers. "There's no — she didn't date anything. She said clocks were for ferries."

"But the will." He was doing the thing he did when his scientist brain grabbed hold of something and wouldn't let go, leaning forward, eyes bright. "The will requires us to run this place together. Through the festival. Pru said the terms were *unusual.* She said Ida rewrote it herself, twice, against advice. Wren — she wrote these cards knowing. Knowing I'd left. Knowing you'd be here. Knowing we'd end up at this table."

"Of course she knew," Wren said, and heard her own voice come out strange. "That's what I keep trying to tell everyone. Ida didn't hope things. She *engineered* things. She grafted apple trees that didn't take for six years running because she was sure the seventh would work. She put Milo's apprenticeship clause in her will before his caseworker even knew he needed one." She spread the three shared cards out in a row, touching each one lightly. "She decided we'd come back to each other and then she built the building it would happen in."

The silence stretched. Outside, Sam's bees droned in the orchard. Somewhere down the lane, Dot's truck rattled past, late for nothing as usual.

Orsen picked up the third card again. Read it. Set it down with exaggerated care, like it might bruise.

"My grandmother," he said, "who I spoke to maybe five times in twelve years, spent her last years writing me homework." His laugh came out cracked down the middle. "I thought she left me the bakery because I was family and there wasn't anyone else. I thought it was an accident of paperwork."

"It was never an accident," Wren said. "Nothing in this kitchen ever was."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he'd been almost-looking for weeks, the look that kept getting interrupted by ovens and raccoons and Milo's terrible timing — and Wren felt the whole room tilt toward him, slow as syrup.

"The ones for me," he said. "Do you want—"

"Yes," she said, before pride could catch up. "Yes, obviously, yes."

He turned the two cards over. The first said: *For Orsen. When you finally stop running. The ocean is a fine place to visit. It is a lonely place to live. You were never running toward it, sweetheart. You were running away, and away doesn't have a kitchen.*

Orsen made a sound Wren had never heard from him, low and wounded, and pressed the heel of his hand briefly against his eyes.

The second card said: *For Orsen. For the day you understand why she left you the bakery instead of the boat. Because boats go places. Bakeries stay. And staying, my darling boy, is the bravest thing a runner ever does.*

Wren reached across the table without deciding to and put her hand over his. He didn't pull away. His fingers turned under hers and held on, flour-dusted and warm, and neither of them said anything for a long moment because there was nothing in either of their vocabularies equal to the moment, and Ida, wherever she was, was probably tapping her foot about it.

"So," Wren managed finally, because someone had to, and her voice came out wet and wobbly and she didn't care. "We're a project. We're a seven-year graft. We're — she planted us, Orsen. On purpose. In the same orchard."

"Yeah." He cleared his throat. Squeezed her hand once, deliberately, and let go — but slower than he had to. "Yeah, we are." He looked down at the legal pad, at the festival menu that suddenly seemed like the smallest possible version of what they were actually building. "So what do we make?"

Wren looked at the cards. All of them. The feud pie and the funeral bread and the letters disguised as recipes, a dead woman's voice still feeding people from a dented tin.

"All of it," she said. "Every single card. The whole box. If Ida planned a reunion, we're going to plan a festival worthy of her." She stood, energized, already pulling a fresh sheet onto the pad. "Starting with the pie that made Marlene Pruitt cry."

"Obviously," Orsen said, and picked up a pencil.

They worked until the light went gold in the windows, arguing happily about rhubarb ratios, and it was only later — much later, when he'd gone home and she was locking up alone — that Wren noticed the small envelope taped inside the lid of the recipe box, in handwriting she'd known her whole life.

It was addressed to both of them.

And it said, in Ida's square, unhurried letters: *Open together. Before the festival. No peeking, Wren.*
