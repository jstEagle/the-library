## 12. First Kiss, Burnt Batch

The plan had been simple: test six festival pastries, log the results, be in bed by one.

It was now two in the morning, and Wren Ashby was wearing a paper crown made from a flour bag, which was Orsen's fault, and she was laughing so hard she had to grip the edge of the worktable, which was also Orsen's fault, and the galettes were in the oven, which was — she'd admit it — her fault, because she'd been the one who said, "We should test the honey-apple galette too, Ida had a version with thyme," and now the kitchen smelled like a bakery that had been awake too long and was starting to get silly about it.

"You look ridiculous," Orsen said. He was sitting on the flour bin with his sleeves rolled to the elbow, a smear of pastry cream on his jaw like he'd lost a fight with a éclair and hadn't noticed. His hair was doing something unmanageable. He'd stopped trying to manage it around midnight.

"You gave me the flour bag," Wren said. "You folded it. There was *craft* involved. This is premeditated ridiculousness."

"I stand by my work." He tilted his head, considering her. "It suits you."

"Nothing with a flour bag on my head suits me."

"That's where you're wrong." He said it lightly, the way he said most things now, but his eyes stayed on her a beat longer than the joke required, and Wren felt that look land somewhere under her ribs and settle in.

This was the problem. This had been the problem for weeks, actually, and she'd been managing it the way she managed a temperamental oven — carefully, constantly, with a healthy respect for what would happen if she looked away.

Because somewhere between the treaty negotiations and the raccoon incident and the night he'd kept the pilot flames lit without being asked, Orsen Dunmore had stopped being the boy who left and started being — this. The man who showed up early to haul flour so she wouldn't have to. Who'd learned Dot's coffee order without being told. Who'd sat on the orchard ladder with Milo for two hours teaching him to identify birds by call, badly, both of them laughing at the wrong answers. Who looked at her sometimes across the kitchen with an expression she refused to name because naming it would make it real, and real things could be taken away.

She'd done the math on him a long time ago. Nineteen-year-old Orsen had walked out of her life without a note, a call, a goodbye. She'd built a whole architecture around that fact — walls, load-bearing grievances, a roof of fine, she's better off. And then he'd come back and started quietly dismantling it, beam by beam, not with apologies but with *presence*, which was so much worse, because you can argue with an apology. You can't argue with a man who remembers exactly how you like your bench scrapers sharpened.

"Earth to Wren." He waved a hand. "The galettes. Timer."

"Timer's set."

"You're staring at the wall."

"I'm staring at the recipe card." Which was a lie; she was staring at the middle distance where his face had been. She pulled Ida's card from the box instead, the thyme-galette one, written in that slanted, confident hand. *Trust the crust. Trust yourself. Same thing, mostly.*

"Ida would've loved this," Orsen said, nodding at the chaos of the kitchen — the flour dust in the air like weather, the six test batches cooling on racks, the crown on her head. "The 2 a.m. testing. She used to do it, you know. I found receipts. She ordered a second delivery of butter every festival season and I could never figure out why until I realized she was running secret trials at night."

"She never told me that." Wren felt a small, familiar pang. "Twelve years in that kitchen and she still had secrets."

"Maybe they weren't secrets. Maybe they were just hers." He hopped off the flour bin and came to stand beside her at the table, close enough that she could smell oven heat and cinnamon and, faintly, the sea salt lotion he used that he'd never once explained and she'd never once asked about. "Or maybe she was waiting to have someone to do it with."

The timer went off.

* * *

Here is what Wren knew about the galette, in retrospect: the oven had been running hot since Tuesday, and she'd meant to have Sam look at the thermostat, and in the excitement of the honey-thyme experiment she had set it for Ida's old oven's temperament, which ran twenty degrees cooler than this one, which meant the batch had maybe four minutes of grace instead of ten.

Here is what Wren did not know at 2:14 a.m.: anything at all, because Orsen flicked a pinch of flour at her.

It was, she would maintain later, completely unprovoked.

"You've got cream on your face," he said, which was true, but was not a reason.

"That's what towels are for," she said, and hit him with one.

The towel caught him across the shoulder and left a pale stripe on his black t-shirt, and something in his expression shifted — that boyish glint she'd been seeing more and more of lately, the one that belonged to a twelve-year-old who used to dare her to jump off the dock — and he reached into the bin beside him with the unhurried deliberation of a man loading a weapon.

"Don't," she said.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

He threw the flour anyway.

What followed was not dignified. Wren grabbed the sifter. Orsen grabbed the sifter's twin. They took positions on opposite sides of the worktable like duellists, and the kitchen filled with white clouds, and Wren laughed the kind of laugh she hadn't laughed since she was a kid — the ugly, honking, full-body kind that you can't take back — and Orsen laughed too, doubled over, flour in his hair, flour in his eyebrows, looking like a ghost that had lost a fight with a pie.

"You," she gasped, "are *covered*—"

"You started it—"

"I absolutely did not—"

He came around the table. She didn't retreat, which was a tactical error, or maybe it wasn't, because then his hands were on her shoulders, dusting flour into her hair with terrible gentleness, and she was reaching for the last of the flour in the bowl beside her, and neither of them was actually throwing anything anymore, and the kitchen went very quiet except for the oven ticking and both of them breathing.

His hands stopped on her shoulders. Stayed there.

"Wren," he said. Not a joke voice. Not a treaty voice. Something underneath both.

"Don't say anything smart," she warned. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

"I never do."

"That's the problem," she said, "you're always—" and then she was on her toes and he was bending down and they were kissing, flour and all, in the middle of Ida's kitchen at two in the morning, and it was not careful or negotiated or in any way consistent with the terms and conditions, and Wren could not have cared less about the terms and conditions.

He kissed like he'd been waiting a long time and hadn't wanted to rush it. One hand came up to cradle the back of her head, careful of the paper crown, which was still on, which meant she was being kissed while wearing a flour-bag crown, and somehow that was the detail that made her laugh into his mouth, and he laughed too, and they broke apart just far enough to look at each other.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi," she said.

"You've got flour on your—"

"Everything. I know. So do you."

"We look like the ghost of baking future."

"We look like an incident report." But she didn't move away, and neither did he, and his thumb traced a small absent circle at her shoulder like he wasn't aware he was doing it, and Wren thought: *oh no.* Thought it with enormous clarity and enormous feeling. *Oh no, oh no, this is the part where I'm in trouble.*

Because she'd done this before, in a way. Not the kissing. The believing. She'd believed in Orsen Dunmore when they were kids — believed he'd be at the dock on the last day of summer, believed he'd write, believed the silence meant something other than what it meant. And she had spent twelve years learning the cost of that belief, and here she was at two in the morning, flour-dusted and paper-crowned, signing up for it all over again with her whole chest.

"Orsen," she said.

"Mm?"

"The galettes."

He blinked. "What about—"

The smell reached him at the same moment it reached her. Not the warm honey-thyme smell of a batch on schedule. The sharper, darker smell of a batch that had crossed over.

"*The oven—*"

They broke apart in a scramble of elbows and flour, and Wren wrenched the door open to a billow of smoke and six galettes that had gone from golden to deeply, definitively charcoal. The honey had carbonized into little black lakes. The thyme had given its life. The smoke alarm, that ancient traitor, finally deigned to notice and began shrieking from the hallway.

"No," Wren said. "No no no—"

Orsen grabbed the towel — the same towel, now a casualty of war — and fanned the alarm while she pulled the trays out and set them on the stovetop, where they sat smoking like tiny disgraces.

They stood side by side and regarded them.

"Well," Orsen said.

"Well," Wren said.

A long pause. Then Orsen picked up the least burnt galette, examined it, and took a bite. Chewed. His face went through a journey.

"That bad?"

"It's like eating a campfire's regrets." He set it down. "We can't serve these. These are a biohazard."

"The festival's in nine days. That was the honey-thyme test. Ida's version." Wren pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "We have to redo the whole batch, and the oven runs hot, and I *knew* the oven ran hot—"

"Hey." His hand found her back. "It's six galettes. We'll run the test again tomorrow night. Tonight. Later tonight. Whatever this is." He glanced at the clock. "We'll figure out the oven. It's fine."

And the ridiculous thing — the thing she'd remember — was that it *did* feel fine, standing there in the smoke with him, her crown askew, his shirt ruined, the batch dead. It felt like something she could get used to. That was the terrifying part.

"We're going to smell like smoke for a week," she said.

"Dot's going to know immediately."

"Dot's going to know *everything* immediately. Dot has a system."

"We could open a window and lie."

"Dot would see through it. Dot has seen through better lies than ours. Dot once deduced that Sam was sweet on the veterinarian from the *brand of cough drops* he started buying."

"Then we don't lie. We just don't say anything and let her draw her own conclusions."

"She'll draw them with a banner."

He laughed, and the sound of it in the smoky kitchen did something warm and dangerous to her, and she looked up at him — flour in his hair, smoke in the air, the first grey hint of not-quite-dawn not even close yet — and felt the happiness hit her all at once, unguarded, the way a wave hits you when you're looking the wrong way.

*Careful,* said the old voice in her, the one that had kept her upright at fifteen when her family came apart, at nineteen when the dock stayed empty. *Careful. You know how this story goes. He leaves. He always leaves. He left once without even a note.*

But that was the old story, she told it. He's not nineteen anymore. People change.

People change, the voice agreed. That's what makes it worse. You can't guard against a man changing back.

"Wren?" He'd caught her face doing something. He always caught her face doing something now. "You okay?"

"Smoke," she said. "Gets in your eyes."

He looked at her like he didn't quite buy it, but he let it go, which was its own small mercy, and together they opened the windows to let the night air in, and the kitchen slowly filled with the sound of crickets and the smell of apples from the orchard instead of char, and they cleaned up side by side without talking much, brushing flour off surfaces, scraping trays, their shoulders bumping now and then in a way that neither of them stepped away from.

* * *

The panic arrived on schedule at 7:40 a.m.

Wren knew it was 7:40 because that was when Dot came in for her coffee and her gossip, and Dot stopped in the doorway, sniffed once, like a customs beagle, and said, "Something burn in here?"

"Test batch," Wren said, with heroic steadiness, sliding the coffee across the counter.

Dot looked at the counter. Looked at Wren. Looked, with terrible slowness, at the faint flour handprint still visible on the back of Orsen's t-shirt as he restocked the case — he'd changed shirts, but he'd missed the back, and the handprint sat there between his shoulder blades like a signature.

Dot looked back at Wren and raised one eyebrow with the precision of a woman who had spent forty years captaining a ferry and reading her passengers.

"Mm," Dot said. "Test batch."

"That's what I said."

"That's what you said." She took her coffee, walked to her table by the window, and sat down facing the room, which she never did, which meant she was settling in for observation. "Milo!" she called. "Come tell me about your birds."

Wren retreated to the kitchen and put her forehead against the cool of the walk-in door for a moment. Get it together. Six galettes. Redo the test tonight, fix the oven, and maintain — *maintain* — a professional demeanor in front of the regulars, who were, all of them, bloodhounds.

The morning went on. Milo arrived for his shift, took one look at the smoky ghost still clinging to the rafters, and said, "Did you guys burn something?" with the innocent scalpel-sharpness of teenagers. Orsen said "test batch" at the same moment Wren did, in the same tone, and Milo looked between them with dawning, delighted comprehension and said nothing, which was somehow worse.

By ten, Wren had catalogued the evidence against them: the flour handprint (still undiscovered by Orsen), the second coffee Orsen made her without being asked and set beside her elbow mid-rush, the way he handed her the piping bags handle-first because he'd noticed weeks ago that she hated fishing them out of the drawer point-first, the way she'd caught herself doing the same for him. The regulars were noticing. Sam had come in for his usual and paused mid-sentence, looked from her to Orsen and back, and smiled into his beard like a man watching a sunrise he'd bet money on.

And Wren stood at the counter, ringing up a dozen cider doughnuts, and felt the joy from the night before sitting in her chest like a lit lantern — and felt, curled right up against it, cold as a key in a lock, the fear.

Because here was the thing she hadn't let herself think at two in the morning, dancing in flour smoke: the fellowship letter. She didn't know about the fellowship letter — not yet, not consciously — but she knew Orsen's duffel had been sitting by the back door for a week, packed tighter than a man needs for a bakery shift. She knew he'd gone quiet at his phone twice lately, in the doorway, in the orchard. She knew the sea was in him the way the orchard was in her, planted deep, and she knew that the last time he'd had to choose, he'd chosen the horizon without even telling her goodbye.

She didn't know what was in the duffel. But she knew the shape of a man with a bag packed.

"Order up," Orsen said beside her, sliding a tray into the case, and his shoulder brushed hers, and he smiled at her — small, real, the smile of a man who had kissed her at two in the morning and was carrying it around with him like a stone in his pocket, touching it every chance he got.

Wren smiled back. She meant it. That was the worst part — she meant it completely.

And then the mail came, and Milo brought it in from the box at the road, and there was a thick envelope in the pile with an Azores postmark and a university crest, addressed to Orsen Dunmore, and Wren watched him take it from Milo's hand and go very still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"That's probably the festival committee," Orsen said.

"Sure," Wren said.

He tucked it under his arm, unopened, and smiled at her again, and this time she watched him do it — watched him build the smile, brick by brick, over the thing in his eyes — and the lantern in her chest flickered, and the cold thing beside it stirred, and Wren Ashby turned back to her counter and began, very carefully, to plan a second batch of galettes, and to wonder exactly how much a packed duffel bag weighs.
