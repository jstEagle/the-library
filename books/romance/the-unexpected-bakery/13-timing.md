## 13. Timing

The letter had lived in his duffel for eleven days.

Orsen knew the exact number because he'd started counting the way he used to count turtle nests — methodically, obsessively, marking each day off in the back of his mind like a data point he couldn't bring himself to publish. Eleven days of folding it back into its envelope, wedging it beneath a rolled pair of socks, and telling himself that tomorrow he'd deal with it. Tomorrow he'd tell her. Tomorrow he'd figure out what a man was supposed to do when the two halves of his life finally arrived at the same address and refused to speak to each other.

Tomorrow kept not coming.

It was a Tuesday morning, gray and soft with the smell of rain that hadn't committed to falling yet, and Orsen came down the back stairs of the bakery at five-forty to find Wren already there — which wasn't unusual — sitting on an overturned milk crate behind the ovens with something white and official-looking in her hands.

His duffel sat open beside her.

He stopped on the bottom step. The floorboard under his right foot creaked the way it always did, the one sound in the whole building that never lied about anything, and Wren looked up.

She didn't look angry. That was the first thing he noticed, and somehow the most frightening thing of all. He'd spent eleven days rehearsing for anger. He'd drafted speeches in the shower, arguments in the orchard, whole courtroom dramas in which she threw flour at him or told him to get out or — worst of all — said nothing at all while her face did the thing it did when she was hurt, which was go very still and very bright, like glass about to crack.

Instead she just held up the letter. "You could've at least hidden it better than your sock drawer."

"It's a duffel," he said stupidly. "I don't have a sock drawer."

"Exactly my point." She set the letter down on the crate beside her, face-up, the university letterhead catching the low light from the oven window. *Dr. Okafor's office* in crisp serif type. *Fellowship appointment.* *Two-year post.* *Departure date: October 4th.*

Six weeks before the festival ended. Six weeks before the deed's terms were satisfied. Six weeks before everything they'd built — the starter, the regulars, the almost-kiss under the oldest apple tree, the kiss that hadn't been an almost at all, the burnt batch they'd laughed over until three in the morning — would either be enough or wouldn't.

"Wren." His voice came out rough. "I was going to—"

"Tell me. I know." She stood, brushing off her apron, and crossed to the proofing shelf where the hundred-year-old starter sat in its crock like a sleeping animal. She checked it without looking at it, the way she did a dozen times a day, hands moving on autopilot while the rest of her stayed very carefully somewhere else. "That's why you hid it in your socks. Because you were going to tell me."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't." She turned around. And here was the second terrible thing: she wasn't even being cruel. Her voice was level. Almost gentle. Like she'd already done all her raging somewhere he couldn't see, alone, probably at four in the morning, and had arrived at the other side of it before he ever got the chance to witness it. "Orsen, sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Sit down anyway. You look like you're going to bolt, and I'd rather not have this conversation through a screen door."

He sat. On the milk crate, where she'd been, which still held the warmth of her. The ovens ticked as they heated. Somewhere out front, rain finally made up its mind and began tapping against the windows, soft and apologetic.

Wren pulled a second crate over and sat across from him, knees nearly touching his, close enough that he could see the flour dusted along her jaw and the small tired crease between her eyebrows that had gotten deeper since September began.

"When did you get it?" she asked.

"Eleven days ago."

She closed her eyes briefly. "Eleven days."

"The fellowship is—" He stopped. Started again. This was the part he'd rehearsed, and now that he was actually saying it, every word felt like it belonged to someone else. "It's the lead position. Principal investigator on the nesting study. Two years in the Azores, full funding, my own team. It's the job I've been working toward since I was nineteen years old. Since—" He swallowed. "Since before Willow Creek. Before any of this."

"I know what it is. I read the letter."

"Then you know I can't just—"

"You're right." She leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and looked at him with those steady hazel eyes that had been cataloguing his flaws since they were seven years old and had never once flinched. "You can't just turn it down. Not for me. Not for the bakery. Not for any of us."

The words landed wrong. He'd expected them to land like absolution, and instead they landed like a door closing.

"Wren—"

"Here's what I keep thinking about." She held up one hand, ticking points off on flour-white fingers, falling into the rhythm she used when she walked Milo through a new recipe — patient, precise, merciless. "One. You didn't tell me for eleven days, which means some part of you already knew how I'd feel about it, and you were hoping the problem would solve itself. Two. The departure date is six weeks before the festival ends, which means if you leave, we both lose the bakery, and Grandma Ida's will makes sure of it, and you'd be doing exactly what you did at nineteen except this time you'd have a really excellent reason and a letterhead. Three."

Her voice caught. Just barely. Just enough that he heard it.

"Three," she said, quieter, "you kissed me nine days ago, and you've been carrying this letter around ever since, and you let me think—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started over. "You let me plan a festival menu with you. You let Milo pick out paint colors for the new sign. You let Dot teach you to make her husband's rye bread, which took her four weeks and cost her more than she'll ever say out loud. You let all of us start believing you were staying."

The rain picked up outside. Orsen sat very still, the way he did when a wave was coming and there was nothing to do but let it break.

"I wasn't letting anyone believe anything," he said. "I was believing it myself. That's the truth, Wren. I kept telling myself I'd figure it out. That maybe I could defer. That maybe the timing would—"

"Timing." She laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd heard in twelve years, because it wasn't bitter — it was sad, and tired, and old. Older than this kitchen. "Of course. Timing. You know what my mother used to say about timing? She said it right before she left, too. *The timing's just wrong, baby.* Right after Dad's funeral, right before she met the guy from Reno. Timing is what people call it when they're leaving and they want the leaving to sound like weather. Like nobody chose it."

"That's not what this is."

"Isn't it?" She stood, and the space where she'd been sitting went cold. "Because from where I'm standing, it looks a lot like a nineteen-year-old boy standing in my driveway with a duffel bag telling me the scholarship starts Monday and the timing's just wrong. Twelve years later, same boy, same bag, fancier letterhead. At least this time you gave me nine days of warning instead of none. That's growth, Orsen. Congratulations."

* * *

He followed her into the front of house because he didn't know what else to do, and because watching her walk away felt like a rehearsal for something he refused to perform.

She tied on her work apron and started pulling trays for the morning bake with the fierce, efficient calm she got when she was hurting — the same calm she'd had the first week he arrived, back when she'd salted his coffee and assigned him to scrub the walk-in freezer for six hours. Back when making him miserable had been a stated goal.

This was worse than that. This was her being kind to him while she did it.

"Milo gets here at six-thirty," she said, sliding a tray of shaped loaves onto the rack. "Dot at seven. Sam brings honey at eight for the festival glaze trials. We have a committee meeting Thursday, and the pie contest brackets need to be posted by Friday, and the cider press rental falls through if we don't confirm by Wednesday noon." She turned to face him, and her eyes were wet, and her voice absolutely was not. "So take the job, Orsen."

"What?"

"Take it." She said it clearly, deliberately, the way you'd hand someone a knife handle-first. "Call Dr. Okafor today. Tell her yes. Book the ferry. I will run the bakery through the festival myself, and I will satisfy the terms of the will, and Pru can sort out the legal mess afterward however she has to. I am not going to stand in front of this town and explain why the co-owner vanished mid-harvest, so do it cleanly. Do it soon. Do it before the regulars learn your coffee order any better than they already have."

"You don't mean that."

"Don't tell me what I mean." Now there was heat in it, finally, a single flare before she banked it again. "You want me to fight? Is that it? You want me to throw something and yell and give you a reason to feel noble about leaving? No. I watched my mother do the leaving, and I watched my father's ghost do the staying, and I learned the difference. I am not going to beg a Dunmore to stay in a kitchen he doesn't want. Grandma Ida did enough begging for this family for three generations."

"She wanted you to have this place."

"And she wanted *you* to have it too, which apparently she was wrong about, which is a sentence I never thought I'd say about Ida Dunmore." Wren pulled the oven door open and slid the first tray in, and the heat rolled out over both of them like a tide. When she straightened, she was looking at him over the steam, and her voice dropped to something almost private. "Do you know what hurts the most? It's not the job. I would never ask you to give up the ocean. I've watched you light up reading tide charts, Orsen. I've seen you talk about sea turtles to Milo for forty-five minutes straight. I know what the water means to you. What hurts is that you didn't trust me with the letter. Eleven days. You decided for me what I could handle. Again."

The word *again* hung in the warm air between them.

"I was trying to protect—"

"Us? From what? From having a conversation? From getting to choose?" She shook her head slowly. "You keep doing this thing where you decide what everyone else can survive, and then you carry it alone like it's heroic. It's not heroic. It's just lonely. And I am not—" Her breath hitched, and she mastered it. "I am not going to spend another twelve years being somebody you protected instead of somebody you trusted."

The bell over the front door jangled.

Milo came in out of the rain, sixteen and damp and radiating the particular energy of a teenager who'd biked through weather for the privilege of shaping dough, and stopped dead halfway across the floor. His eyes went from Wren to Orsen to the letter still sitting on the milk crate in the back, visible through the doorway, university letterhead glowing under the kitchen lights.

"Oh," Milo said. "Is this a bad time? Because I can come back. I can definitely come back."

"It's fine," Wren said, already moving, already smoothing herself into the version of her the world got to see. "Wash your hands. We're doing the festival brioche trial today."

Milo hesitated at the sink, looking at Orsen with the naked, unguarded concern that only teenagers and golden retrievers can manage. "You okay, Orsen?"

"Yeah, kid." Orsen found his voice somewhere near the floorboards. "Fine."

"He's taking a job overseas," Wren said lightly, cracking eggs into a bowl, not looking at either of them. "Marine biology. Very exciting. Tell him congratulations, Milo."

Milo's face did something complicated and young. "Oh. Cool. That's... cool." He dried his hands. "When do you leave?"

"October fourth," Wren answered for him, and the date came out of her mouth precise as a recipe, and Orsen understood suddenly that she had memorized it. That she'd read the letter more than once. That she'd sat here in the dark this morning, holding his future in her hands, learning it by heart.

* * *

They baked in silence for two hours.

It was, Orsen thought numbly, the most articulate silence he'd ever stood inside. Every movement said something. Wren's knife-work said *I trusted you.* The careful distance she kept at the shared bench said *not again.* The way she corrected Milo's fold technique with extra patience said *some things are worth teaching twice.* The way she didn't look at him once, not once, in a hundred and twenty minutes, said everything the letter hadn't needed to.

At seven, Dot arrived, read the room in approximately four seconds with the radar of a woman who'd captained ferries through fog for thirty years, and said only, "I'll take the corner table this morning," which was Dot-speak for *whatever storm is happening, I will observe it from a safe distance and gossip about it later.*

At eight, Sam brought the honey and left it on the counter without a word, eyes flicking between them like a man who understood hives: something had stung somebody, and the whole colony was agitated.

And Orsen stood in the middle of it, flour on his hands and a fellowship acceptance burning a hole in his duffel upstairs, and realized that Wren had done the cruelest possible thing.

She'd believed him capable of leaving.

Not angrily. Not bitterly. She'd simply, quietly updated her model of him — the one she'd been building since they were children, the one that had survived twelve years of silence and one midnight rainstorm and one kiss that tasted like burnt sugar — and filed him under *people who go.* And she'd started planning around the gap he'd leave, the way water plans around a stone, without drama, without asking him to be different.

He'd spent eleven days afraid of her rage. He should have been afraid of her grace.

At ten minutes past eight, she untied her apron, handed it to Milo, and said, "Cover the counter till the ten o'clock lull," and walked past Orsen close enough that he caught the smell of yeast and rain and the shampoo he'd stopped being able to smell without smiling.

At the door she paused. Didn't turn around.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I hope the turtles are extraordinary."

The bell jangled. The door swung shut behind her, and through the rain-streaked window Orsen watched her cross the street toward the orchard, shoulders straight, head high, not running — walking, deliberate as a woman keeping a promise to herself — until the apple trees took her.

Behind him, Milo cleared his throat. "So," he said, into the silence. "October fourth, huh."

Orsen looked down at his hands. Flour in every line, under every nail, worked deep into the skin in a way that no amount of washing ever quite fixed anymore. Twelve days ago these hands had been full of her.

"Yeah," he said. "October fourth."

Upstairs, in his duffel, under the rolled socks, the letter waited — accepted, signed, dated, real. And across the street, in the orchard where the oldest tree stood dripping in the rain, Wren Ashby was doing whatever she did when she refused to cry, and Orsen Dunmore stood in the warmest room in Willow Creek feeling colder than he had in twelve years, understanding at last that she hadn't given him permission to go.

She'd just stopped waiting for him to ask her to stay.
