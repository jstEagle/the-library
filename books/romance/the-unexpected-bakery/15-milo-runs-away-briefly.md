## 15. Milo Runs Away (Briefly)

The morning Milo didn't show up for his shift, Wren burned the first two batches of morning buns and blamed the oven.

It was easier to blame the oven. The oven had been temperamental since 1987 and couldn't defend itself. The alternative explanation — that a fifteen-year-old who had never once missed a Saturday shift in eight months of apprenticeship simply hadn't come — sat in her chest like a stone she kept trying to swallow.

"Maybe he overslept," Dot said from her corner table, where she'd been nursing the same coffee for an hour and pretending not to watch Wren pace. "Teenagers are famous for it. I once slept through my own wedding rehearsal."

"You did not."

"I did, and Harold married me anyway, God rest him." Dot took a sip. "Call his foster mother."

Wren had already called twice. The first time, no answer. The second time, a woman named Carla answered on the fourth ring with a voice like static, saying *yeah?* in a tone that suggested Wren had interrupted something important, which was probably a game show or a nap.

"Hi, this is Wren Ashby, from the bakery — Milo works for me?"

"Milo's got a job?" A pause. "Huh. Yeah, he ain't here. Left last night sometime. He does that sometimes. Comes back."

*He does that sometimes.* Wren stood in the kitchen holding the phone against her ear and felt something cold slide down her spine. "How often does he do that?"

Another pause, longer. Somewhere in the background a television laughed at its own joke. "Look, lady, he's got a bed here and food in the fridge. If he wants to sulk in the woods, that's on him. You seen my other kid's jacket? Blue one?"

"No," Wren said. "I haven't."

She hung up and stood very still in the middle of the kitchen, flour on her forearms, the smell of burnt sugar hanging in the air like an accusation.

* * *

By ten o'clock she'd called everyone Milo might conceivably have gone to. Sam the beekeeper hadn't seen him. Neither had the librarian, nor the woman who ran the bait shop, nor Milo's history teacher, who sounded genuinely alarmed and promised to make some calls of his own. Pru Halloway offered, unprompted, to look into the foster placement situation, which told Wren that Pru had already guessed what kind of situation it was. Small towns keep files in their heads.

At eleven, the bell over the bakery door jangled and Orsen came in still wearing his rain jacket, hair flattened by mist, carrying that particular energy he got when he'd been thinking hard about something and hadn't yet decided whether to say it.

"He wasn't at the dock," Orsen said. No hello. They'd moved past hellos somewhere around the pie contest argument, into a language of shorthand and unfinished sentences that Wren refused to examine too closely. "I checked. And the boathouse. And Sam said maybe try the creek bend."

"Why would he be at the creek bend?"

"Because when I was fifteen and the world was too loud, the creek bend was where I went." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't match the lightness. "I checked there. Nothing. But his bike's not at his house either, so he went somewhere on purpose."

Wren wiped her hands on her apron. "Carla says he 'does that sometimes.' Comes back. Like he's a cat."

Orsen's jaw tightened. It was a small thing, barely visible, but Wren had spent weeks learning the geography of his face again — the way anger moved through it like weather, arriving and passing without ever quite landing where it wanted to. "She said that?"

"She said he's got a bed and food in the fridge."

"And that's the whole job, apparently." He pulled off the rain jacket and dropped it over a chair. "Where else would he go? Think. Where does a kid go when home stops being home?"

And because Wren's mind had already been circling it all morning, circling it with a dread she'd been refusing to name, she heard herself say: "The treehouse."

* * *

Ida's treehouse sat in the oldest apple tree at the far end of the orchard, forty feet up in a crown of branches that had been bearing fruit since before either of them was born. Ida had built it herself, allegedly, with help from a neighbor and a level of stubbornness that could have bent steel. Wren had spent whole summers up there as a girl, reading comic books and eating apples with more ambition than sense. She'd assumed it would have rotted through years ago. Instead, someone had been maintaining it — new boards on the ladder rungs, a tarp lashed over the roof, a rope swing rehung with fresh knots.

Milo's work. Obviously. The boy treated that treehouse like a cathedral.

They walked out through the rows together, wet grass soaking their shoes, neither speaking. The orchard in late October was all gold light and falling leaves, beautiful in the way that made Wren ache a little, though whether for the season or for the man walking beside her — close enough that their hands brushed once, twice, and neither of them corrected it — she couldn't have said.

At the base of the tree, Orsen stopped and looked up. "Milo," he called, not loudly. "It's us. Just checking you're okay."

Silence. Then, from somewhere in the high branches, small and defiant: "Go away."

Relief hit Wren so hard her knees nearly buckled. He was alive. He was alive and rude, which was Milo's natural state, and she could have wept.

"We're not going away," she called back. "But we're also not climbing up. Your choice."

More silence. Then the sound of movement, boards creaking, and Milo's face appeared over the edge of the platform — pale, unwashed, eyes red-rimmed, hair sticking up like he'd slept in a wind tunnel. Which, judging by the blanket bunched beside him, he had.

"How'd you find me?" he demanded.

"You rebuilt the ladder rungs in June," Wren said. "You varnished them. Nobody varnishes a ladder they don't love."

Milo's face did something complicated. He looked at Orsen, then at her, then down at his own hands gripping the railing. "I'm fine."

"You're sleeping in a tree in October," Orsen said.

"It's a good tree."

"It is a good tree," Orsen agreed. "Best in the county. Still doesn't answer the question."

"What question?"

"The one where you tell us why."

Milo's chin came up in the exact posture of a kid preparing to lie, and Wren watched the lie assemble itself behind his eyes — *nothing happened, I just wanted some space, it's not a big deal* — and then watched it collapse. His shoulders dropped. He looked suddenly, terribly young.

"Can you just—" His voice cracked and he hated it, she could see him hating it. "Can you come up? Both of you? I'm not doing this shouting distance."

* * *

The ladder held. The treehouse, once they'd hauled themselves through the hatch, was warmer than it had any right to be — Milo had dragged up an old camp lantern, a sleeping bag, a milk crate of supplies that included three granola bars, a flashlight, a paperback with a cracked spine, and, inexplicably, a whisk.

"That's mine," Wren said, pointing at the whisk.

"You were gonna throw it out. The handle's loose." Milo shrugged, defensive. "I fixed it."

Of course he had. Wren sat down on the platform boards across from him, Orsen folding himself beside her with the careful grace of a large man in a small space built for children. For a moment nobody spoke. Below them the orchard breathed, leaves ticking against leaves.

"Carla's boyfriend moved in," Milo said finally, to the floor. "Full time. Two weeks ago. And he's—" Milo's jaw worked. "He's not even mean, exactly. That's the stupid part. He's just always *there*. Taking up the couch, eating everything, talking about his truck. And Carla's different now. Laughing at his dumb jokes, doing whatever he wants. And last night he said—" Milo stopped. Started again, quieter. "He said the spare room should be an office. Since I'm 'practically gone all the time anyway.'"

Wren's chest went tight.

"So I figured I'd save everybody the trouble and be gone all the time." Milo picked at a splinter on the board between his knees. "I had it figured out. Sam said I could sleep in the honey house till I turn sixteen and get emancipated or whatever. I looked it up. It's a real thing."

"Milo." Wren kept her voice steady with effort. "Emancipation is a court process. It takes months. It requires money and lawyers and—"

"I know how Google works."

"Do you know how cold the honey house gets in November?" Orsen asked. Mildly. Too mildly. Wren recognized the tone; it was the one he used when he was furious and had decided to be useful instead.

"I've got a sleeping bag."

"You've got a sleeping bag and a whisk."

"A fixed whisk."

Despite everything, despite the stone still sitting in her chest, Wren felt her mouth twitch. Milo glared at them both, daring them to laugh, and then — betraying himself completely — his eyes filled. He scrubbed a sleeve across his face fast, like he could get ahead of it.

"They're supposed to want me there," he said, muffled. "That's the whole point of a placement. They sign papers. They *say* they want—"

"Hey." Orsen leaned forward. "Look at me."

Milo looked, wary.

"Twelve years ago I ran away from a house where I technically had a bed and food in the fridge," Orsen said. "I ended up four hundred miles away before anybody caught up with me. And you know what I learned?" He paused. "Running fixes nothing except the first hour. The first hour's great. After that you're just cold and hungry in a place where nobody knows your name, and the problem you ran from is waiting for you, fully intact, whenever you get back."

"That's a terrible pep talk," Milo said.

"It's the only honest one I've got." Orsen glanced sideways — at Wren, briefly, and something passed between them that she'd think about later, dissect later, when she had privacy and a strong cup of tea. "But here's the part that matters. You didn't run four hundred miles. You ran forty feet up a tree in the one place in this town where people know exactly who you are and what you're worth. That's not running away, kid. That's coming home to the wrong address."

Milo blinked at him. "That doesn't make any sense."

"It makes perfect sense," Wren said. Her throat was thick. "It means you already know where your family is. You just haven't let yourself say it."

The silence stretched. Milo looked down at the whisk, turning it over in his hands, testing the handle where he'd glued and wrapped it. When he spoke again his voice was very small.

"If I go back, the office thing's gonna happen anyway. It's already decided. I heard them talking about desks."

"Then we skip going back," Wren said.

Both of them looked at her.

"Not forever — there are proper channels, Pru Halloway's already sniffing around the placement, and she's a shark in a cardigan, it'll be handled right. But you are not spending tonight in the honey house, and you are not going back under that roof while that man is in it. Not when there's a perfectly good spare room above the bakery that Ida used for storing holiday ribbon and has been empty since March."

Milo stared. "You're offering me your spare room."

"I'm offering you Ida's ribbon room. There's a difference. It comes with obligations. You'll keep working Saturdays, you'll fix that loose step on the back stairs, and you will stop stealing my whisks and simply ask for them like a person."

"I *fixed* the whisk—"

"Ask. Like a person."

A sound escaped Milo that was half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled. He looked at Orsen, as if checking whether this was allowed, whether some adult somewhere was going to veto it. Orsen just nodded once, slow and certain, and Wren watched something in the boy's shoulders unlock — a tension he'd probably been carrying for two weeks, maybe two years, letting go all at once.

"Okay," Milo said. "Okay. Yeah."

* * *

They climbed down in the gold afternoon light, Milo going first with the sleeping bag stuffed under one arm and the whisk held carefully like contraband. At the bottom of the ladder he hesitated, then surprised Wren completely by hugging her — quick, fierce, embarrassed, gone before she could return it properly. Then he hugged Orsen, which appeared to surprise Orsen even more; he stood rigid for a full second before his arms came up, and Wren saw his eyes go bright and determinedly elsewhere.

"Nobody at school hears about this," Milo announced, stepping back, restoring order to his universe.

"Obviously," Wren said.

"I mean it. Especially not the part about crying."

"Especially not that part," Orsen agreed solemnly. "As far as the historical record shows, we found you doing inventory."

"In a treehouse."

"Very thorough inventory."

Milo rolled his eyes, but he was smiling, actually smiling, and Wren thought: *this*. This is what Ida bought with that impossible deed. Not a bakery. This.

They walked back through the orchard together, Milo a few steps ahead, chattering now — nervous, relieved chatter about school and the festival menu and whether the ribbon room had wifi, and Wren fell into step beside Orsen, and for a while they walked in the easy quiet they'd somehow earned.

"You didn't call anyone first," she said quietly. "When you found him. You just — brought him home. Decided he was ours."

Orsen looked over at her. The late light caught the side of his face, and there was something unguarded in it that made her heart do an unhelpful thing.

"There wasn't anyone to call," he said. "There was just us."

*Us.* He said it like it was simple. Like it cost nothing. Wren looked straight ahead at Milo's retreating back and the bakery rising warm-lit beyond the trees, and she thought about a letter hidden in a duffel bag six weeks from now, and said nothing at all.

Behind them, the old apple tree held its branches out over the empty treehouse, keeping watch, the way it always had.
