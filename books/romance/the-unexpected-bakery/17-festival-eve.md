## 17. Festival Eve

By six in the morning, the bakery smelled like a small country had declared independence from sleep.

Wren stood at the center counter with a clipboard she'd stopped reading an hour ago, watching Milo attempt to carry three crates of apples at once because he'd heard Dot say something about "initiative" and taken it as a personal challenge. Sam was in the back with the honey shipment, arguing cheerfully on the phone with his supplier about whether wildflower or clover better suited the festival's signature cake. Dot had claimed the front table as her command post and was directing foot traffic of volunteers like a retired ferry captain, which — Wren reflected not for the first time — was exactly what she was.

And Orsen was at the far oven, sleeves rolled to his elbows, flour to his wrists, pulling the fourth batch of test loaves into the light.

He didn't look up when he said, "The crust's catching on the left rack. Heat's uneven."

"I know. I've been telling Pru we need a new oven since March."

"You've been telling Pru?"

"Pru owns a quarter share of the building's boiler. It's a long story involving a poker game Ida won."

"Of course it is." He set the loaves down and came over, and without either of them deciding anything, he took the rag off her shoulder where she'd draped it and wiped a streak of flour from her cheekbone with his thumb. Casual. Automatic. Like they'd been doing it for years instead of weeks.

Wren's heart did something complicated and inconvenient.

"Left rack runs hot," he said, already turning back. "I'll rotate everything fifteen minutes early."

"Rotate at twelve minutes," she said. "It's the edges that catch."

"Twelve minutes," he agreed, and that was it. No argument. No negotiation. Somewhere between the raccoons and the rain and the burnt batch of festival pastries they'd both pretended not to remember out loud, they had developed this — a shorthand, a rhythm, two people operating one kitchen like a single pair of hands.

She hated how much she loved it.

* * *

The day went by in lists.

Wren kept lists the way other people kept diaries: pie contest entries (forty-one, plus one suspicious entry from Dot labeled "Dot"), cider press schedule, booth assignments, the bunting situation (Milo had promised the scouts would handle it; the scouts had, historically, handled nothing). She wrote ORSEN next to eleven tasks and crossed out nine of them by noon because he'd finished them before she got there.

That was the thing about him she'd never expected, back when she was still planning to make him so miserable he'd sell: Orsen Dunmore worked like someone making up for lost time. He arrived early. He stayed late. He learned the names of every regular and remembered that Captain Dot took her morning roll torn, never cut, and that Sam's daughter was allergic to cinnamon so there was always one plain pastry set aside under a cloth, just for her.

At two o'clock, he appeared beside her at the counter with two cups of coffee and set one down without asking how she took it. Black, splash of cream, no sugar. He'd known for weeks.

"Festival committee wants a final walkthrough at four," he said. "Pru called twice. The second time she used the word 'mandatory,' which from Pru means she's bored."

"We just did a walkthrough Tuesday."

"She says the bunting is 'a moral question.'"

"Milo's bunting."

"Milo's bunting." He leaned against the counter beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, and looked out at the bakery — at the garlands going up, the crates of apples stacked by the door, the chalkboard where Milo had written FESTIVAL TOMORROW!! in letters visible from space. "You know what my research vessel smelled like? Diesel and salt. Six months, and you stop noticing. Then I walk in here some mornings and the yeast hits me and I think—" He stopped. Shook his head slightly. "Never mind."

"What?"

"Nothing. Twelve-minute rotation." And he was gone again, back to the ovens, leaving Wren holding a cup of coffee and a sentence he hadn't finished.

But she'd heard it anyway. She'd heard the shape of it.

* * *

Four o'clock became six o'clock became nine o'clock, and the town square slowly transformed around them. Wren stood on a ladder hanging lanterns while Orsen steadied the base and handed things up to her, and they argued about spacing — "Evenly means evenly, Orsen, not 'close enough'" — and he said, "You're a tyrant," and she said, "I'm right," and he said, "Those are often the same thing," and neither of them mentioned that his hand had stayed at her ankle a beat longer than balance required.

Pru Halloway materialized beneath them with her clipboard and her air of a woman who had seen every mistake Willow Creek could produce and billed accordingly.

"The pie contest judging table goes *there*, not there," she said, pointing. "If it goes there, the afternoon sun hits the meringues and we have a structural failure. I've witnessed it. 2011. There were casualties."

"Casualties," Wren repeated.

"Emotional ones." Pru consulted her list. "Dunmore. You're doing the bread stall?"

"Whatever Wren needs," Orsen said, easy as breathing.

Pru looked up at Wren on the ladder. Looked at Orsen. Looked back at Wren with the particular expression of a lawyer who has read a will very carefully and drawn certain conclusions. She said nothing, which from Pru was louder than most people's speeches, and walked away.

When Wren climbed down, Orsen caught her elbow to steady her, and for a moment they stood closer than the task required, lantern light above them, the whole square smelling of cut grass and cider and tomorrow.

"You should get some sleep," he said quietly. "Big day."

"So should you."

"I will. After I do one more thing." He didn't say what. He smiled — that rare, unguarded smile she'd spent twelve years forgetting and three months relearning — and headed back toward the bakery with his hands in his pockets.

Wren watched him go and felt the fear arrive on schedule, punctual as the ferry.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told anyone, not Dot, not Milo, not the notebook she kept under her bed: somewhere between the starter and the storm and the recipe cards in Ida's handwriting — *To Wren and Orsen, who will find these together, because some dough needs two pairs of hands* — she had stopped wanting him to leave and started wanting him to stay, and those were not the same want, and only one of them was safe.

Ida had engineered this. Wren knew it now. Knew the old woman had sat in this kitchen with her box of cards and her hundred-year-old starter and her stubborn, scheming heart, and decided that grief-stricken nineteen-year-old boys deserved second chances and that granddaughters who built walls deserved someone worth letting through them.

And if Ida could scheme from beyond the grave, then the universe could certainly take him back. A fellowship. A boat. A phone call. Anything.

So Wren stood alone in the half-lit square, surrounded by lanterns she'd hung too close together, and made herself a promise with the specific, aching discipline of someone who has been left before:

*I will not ask him to stay.*

Not tonight. Not tomorrow. Not with the festival lights on and the whole town watching and every soft advantage stacked in her favor. If he went, he would go knowing the bakery was his to return to — not a debt, not a guilt, not a woman's arms around his ankles. Love that held too tight wasn't love; it was just another kind of leaving first.

She would keep the door open. That was all. The door, and the light, and the twelve-minute rotation.

* * *

Eleven o'clock, and the bakery was the last lit window on the street.

They worked the final prep in near silence — proofing the overnight doughs, staging the pie crusts, covering racks with cloth. Wren found herself moving around him the way water finds its channel, no thought required. He reached for the bench scraper exactly when she stepped aside to give him room. She turned with the sheet pan just as his hand came up to hold the door. Once, their fingers tangled reaching for the same cloth, and neither pulled away fast, and neither said anything, and the silence was so full it hummed.

"This time last year," Orsen said finally, shaping a boule with quick, sure hands, "I was counting turtles."

"Do you miss it?"

He was quiet long enough that she regretted asking. "I miss the water," he said at last. "I don't miss being the kind of person who was easier to be out there than anywhere on land." He glanced up. "That's not a riddle. It's just true."

"It's not a riddle," she agreed softly. "I knew that kid. The one who was easier somewhere else."

Something moved across his face — gratitude, maybe, or grief for the boy they'd both been. "Wren—"

"Don't." She said it gently, wiping her hands on her apron. "Whatever speech you're loading up, save it. Tomorrow's going to be chaos and we need sleep and I refuse to have a Moment in a room that smells this strongly of proofing dough."

A huff of laughter. "Understood. Moments postponed indefinitely."

"Not indefinitely," she said, before she could stop herself. "Just... until the timing's right."

He looked at her then, really looked, the way he'd looked at the horizon off the stern of the research vessel, like something vast and worth measuring. "Timing," he repeated. "Yeah. I've been thinking about timing."

"Well, think faster," she said, and threw a dish towel at him, and he caught it, and they finished the night's work grinning like idiots in the empty bakery while outside, the lanterns swayed gently in the dark, waiting for morning.

* * *

She found the note at midnight, when she went back for her forgotten jacket.

It was tucked under the flour bin, weighted with a pebble from the orchard — Ida's pebble, one of the smooth gray ones she used to line the windowsill. Orsen's handwriting, careful and a little rushed:

*In case I'm gone before you're up — everything's proofed, rotations marked, left rack runs hot so check at twelve. You know all this. You taught me all this.*

*Whatever happens tomorrow, thank you. Not for the bakery. For the twelve years' worth of grace you gave me in three months. Ida picked right. So did I, this time.*

*— O*

*Gone before you're up.* Wren stood in the doorway with the note in her hand, the paper trembling faintly, and read it three times. Gone where? Gone home to sleep, probably. Gone to the dock, maybe, to look at the water the way he always did when something was sitting heavy on him. Gone to —

No. She wouldn't do this. She wouldn't spend festival eve spiraling over a man's handwriting like a fortune-teller reading tea leaves.

But her eyes snagged on one line, the one she couldn't unsee: *whatever happens tomorrow.*

People didn't write *whatever happens tomorrow* unless tomorrow had versions.

Wren folded the note along its crease and put it in her pocket, over her heart, where it sat like a stone. Across the dark harbor, the night ferry slid past with its running lights burning, bound for the mainland, and she stood in the doorway of everything Ida had built and made her promise one more time — quieter now, harder now, her knuckles white around the strap of her jacket.

*Don't hold on too tight. Keep the door open. Let him choose.*

Then she saw it: his duffel bag, still sitting by the coat hooks where it had lived for three months, packed fuller than it had any right to be this time. Packed like a man ready to travel far.

The ferry horn sounded once across the water, low and long, and Wren Ashby closed her eyes.
