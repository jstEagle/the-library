## 5. Collision Course

The call came at 6:47 on a Thursday morning, which was how Mirabel knew it would be a bad day before they'd even opened their eyes.

Nobody called Mirabel Ishikawa at 6:47 a.m. with good news. Good news arrived by email, formatted politely, with subject lines like *Confirmed: Linens for the Ashford-Vance Reception*. Bad news arrived by phone, breathless, usually from Priya, who ran their second-string crew and who had never once in four years of employment woken them up to say everything was fine.

"Mirabel." Priya's voice crackled through, already fraying at the edges. "The Whitfield cake. The three-tier. It's—okay, so the delivery van got clipped on the bridge."

Mirabel sat up slowly, the way one does when the floor might be lava. "Clipped how."

"Clipped *clipped*. The top two tiers are—" A pause. A sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the beginning of weeping. "There is no top two tiers anymore. There's buttercream on the windshield and there's buttercream on the guardrail and there's a seagull eating what I'm fairly sure was the sugar magnolia topper."

Mirabel closed their eyes and did the math, because math was safe, math never betrayed anyone. The Whitfield wedding was at noon. Ceremony at the maritime museum, reception in the glass pavilion overlooking the harbor. Two hundred guests. A bride who had cried actual tears of joy at the tasting, who had hugged Mirabel's arm and said *you understood me*, which was the highest compliment in the business and also the heaviest weight.

"Options," Mirabel said, swinging their feet to the floor. "Go."

"Petal & Crumb can't do it—Marta's booked solid until Tuesday. The hotel pastry kitchen says maybe, but their quote is ugly and their fondant work looks like drywall. And there's—" Priya hesitated, and Mirabel heard her brace herself. "There's Okafor's. On the harbor. Rosa's place. Nia mentioned they do custom work now, since Jun came on. She said the man does things with laminated dough that should be illegal."

The name landed in Mirabel's chest like a stone dropped into still water.

They hadn't spoken since the market. Since Mirabel had stood in the produce aisle holding a bag of blood oranges like a shield and watched Jun Okafor—older, broader through the shoulders, a scar cutting through one eyebrow that hadn't been there eleven years ago—turn pale as milk and say *hello, Mirabel* in a voice that cracked down the middle. They'd exchanged perhaps forty words. None of them had been the right ones. Then Mirabel had paid for oranges they didn't want and walked out into the rain without an umbrella, which felt appropriately biblical.

That had been nine days ago. Nine days of taking the long way to avoid Harbor Street. Nine days of rearranging their mental map of the neighborhood around one bakery the way water rearranges itself around a stone.

"Mirabel? You still there?"

"Text me the address," Mirabel said. "And call Marta anyway. Beg if you have to. Offer her your firstborn."

"I don't *have* a firstborn."

"Offer her mine. I have several metaphorically." They were already pulling on jeans. "I'll handle it."

They hung up and stood in the middle of their immaculate bedroom—the bed made with hospital corners, the week's outfits pressed and hung in chromatic order, the corkboard on the wall mapped out six weekends deep—and let themselves feel, for exactly thirty seconds, the full unfairness of it. Of all the bakeries in all the towns in all the world. Of a universe that had apparently decided, after eleven years of respectful silence, to start throwing Jun Okafor at them like weather.

Then they splashed water on their face, braided their hair back tight enough to survive a hurricane, and went to war.

* * *

Okafor's Bakery sat at the crook of the harbor where Harbor Street bent to kiss the water, wedged between a chandlery that smelled forever of tar and a bait shop whose owner, Sal, had been feuding with Rosa Okafor over a parking space since roughly the Nixon administration. It was the kind of storefront tourists photographed without knowing why: hand-painted sign gone soft-edged with salt air, window boxes spilling geraniums even in October, a door that chimed with an actual brass bell instead of some electronic chirp.

Mirabel had been inside exactly twice in the past decade. Both times had been for Theo's sake—his kids' birthday cakes, ordered over the phone, picked up at the counter by a teenage apprentice with flour on her nose. Mirabel had engineered those transactions with the precision of a heist. No lingering. No browsing. In and out in under four minutes.

Today there would be no such luck. Today they needed a miracle in buttercream by noon.

The bell chimed. The smell hit them first—yeast and caramelized sugar and coffee dark enough to stand a spoon in—and underneath it, faintly, something Mirabel had no business recognizing: cardamom. The same cardamom buns that had once been sold out of a folding table at the farmers' market by a nineteen-year-old with paint-stained fingers and a laugh that carried across three stalls.

"Be right with you!" called a voice from the kitchen, warm and gravelly, unmistakably Rosa.

Mirabel gripped the strap of their bag and made themselves look at the case instead of the door behind the counter. Cakes under glass domes. A lemon olive-oil number with candied peel arranged like sun rays. A chocolate thing crowned with torched meringue. Competent, lovely work—but not the work of someone who could rebuild two tiers of a wedding cake in five hours.

"You'll want to talk to Jun," Rosa said, emerging through the swinging door wiping her hands on her apron, and Mirabel realized with a lurch that she'd known who was standing at her counter before she'd ever come out. That this whole greeting had been staged. That the twinkle in her eye was not the twinkle of an innocent woman. "He's in back. JUN," she bellowed toward the kitchen, at a volume that suggested she'd spent forty years shouting over ovens and harbor wind and had lost all interest in moderation, "CUSTOMER FOR YOU."

"Rosa, I can just—" Mirabel started.

"You can just stand there looking pretty while my nephew gets his apron off," Rosa said cheerfully, pouring two coffees nobody had asked for. "Sit. Sit! You're making the floor nervous."

There was exactly one free table, small and round, tucked by the window where morning light came in gold off the water. Mirabel sat because refusing would take more energy than complying, and accepted the coffee because Rosa Okafor's coffee was, according to everyone in a half-mile radius, non-negotiable.

It was excellent. Of course it was. Everything about this place was infuriatingly excellent.

The kitchen door swung open and Jun came through it backward, carrying a sheet pan of something golden, talking over their shoulder to someone unseen—"—no, the proofing has to go longer, tell Nia if she opens that oven again I'm hiding her headphones"—and then they turned, saw Mirabel, and stopped so abruptly the pan tilted and a cardamom bun slid majestically onto the floor.

Silence. The bell above the door chimed as another customer entered, oblivious. Somewhere a radio played low.

"Hi," Jun said.

"Hi," Mirabel said. "You dropped a bun."

"I dropped a bun." Jun looked down at it as though confirming the report. Then, because they had always been the kind of person who met disaster with absurd calm, they set the pan down, retrieved the fallen bun with a napkin, and said, "Rosa. We're going to need a minute of silence for this soldier."

"We'll observe it after you take Mirabel's order," Rosa said, with zero subtlety whatsoever, and vanished back into the kitchen with the smugness of a woman who had waited nine days for this exact moment.

Jun wiped their hands on their apron—a habit unchanged, Mirabel noted traitorously, from eleven years ago; Jun had always had flour somewhere on them, wrists, jaw, once memorably in their hair—and came around the counter. Up close they were different and not different. The scar. A few silver threads at the temple. Hands broader, knuckles nicked from years of work Mirabel knew nothing about. But the eyes were the same: dark, watchful, always seeming to hold a question slightly behind the teeth.

"Priya called ahead, I'm guessing," Jun said. "About the Whitfield cake."

"She called the shop?"

"Nia answered. Nia then informed me, in these words, that 'the wedding person' needed help and that I 'owed the universe a favor.'" Jun's mouth twitched. "She's seventeen and believes in karma the way other teenagers believe in astrology."

"The universe," Mirabel repeated carefully, "or you."

Jun held their gaze. The café noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. "Both, maybe," they said quietly. "Look—I know this is the last place you want to be. If you want to walk out, walk out. I won't—" They stopped. Started again. "I won't make it harder than it already is."

And there it was: the old Jun, the one who had always offered Mirabel the exit first, who had never once cornered them, who had loved them—if that word could be trusted anymore—in a way that left doors open. It would have been easier if Jun had been cruel. Cruelty was simple. You could build a wall against cruelty and live behind it for decades.

Instead Jun slid a legal pad across the table, clicked a pen, and said, "Tell me what survived and what we're rebuilding. We've got five hours. What are we working with?"

Mirabel looked at the pad. At the pen. At the hands that had once sketched harbor gulls in the margins of their notebooks during lectures neither of them attended properly.

"Two tiers gone," they heard themselves say. "Bottom tier intact but the transport board cracked. Palette was ivory and blush, sugar magnolias, textured buttercream, the bride wants it to look like sea foam. Which—" they exhaled, "—is actually perfect for this town, so if we lean coastal instead of fighting it—"

"Sea foam," Jun said, and something lit in their face, quick and real. "You'd want a palette knife drag, horizontal, layered. Like the tide line. I've been playing with a technique—hold on." They were already moving, pulling a phone from their apron pocket, thumbing through photos. "Here. Did this for the Chen anniversary cake. Rosa thought it looked like dishwater. Rosa is wrong."

The photo showed a cake that did, unmistakably, look like the moment a wave pulls back from sand. Mirabel stared at it and felt the dangerous warmth of professional kinship, which was worse than any other kind, because it couldn't be dismissed or defended against. It simply *worked*, the way certain hands worked together, the way—

No. Focus. Sea foam. Five hours.

"It's good," Mirabel admitted. "It's better than good. Okay. Here's what I need from your kitchen."

What followed was ninety minutes of the most focused collaboration of Mirabel's career. Jun cleared a station. Rosa produced eggs and cream from sources she refused to disclose ("A woman has her secrets"). Nia—the apprentice, all elbows and fierce competence, headphones around her neck—was drafted as runner and turned out to have a piping hand that made Jun say *again, slower* in the tone of a teacher trying not to show pride. Mirabel rebuilt the structural tiers while Jun spun sugar magnolias with a speed that suggested muscle memory deeper than technique, and twice their hands came within inches of each other reaching for the same offset spatula, and both times the air between them went taut as wire.

"You still count under your breath," Jun said at one point, quietly, when Mirabel was timing a ganache pour. "I could hear you from across the room. *Four hundred grams, four hundred grams.*"

"It's not counting under my breath. It's quality control."

"It's counting under your breath." But they said it softly, almost fondly, and Mirabel found they had no rebuttal prepared, which was unprecedented, and deeply annoying.

At 11:20 the cake stood finished on the marble slab: three tiers of tide-line buttercream, sugar magnolias catching the light like they'd been dipped in dawn, structurally sound enough to survive a second bridge incident. Nia took a photo for the shop's wall. Rosa cried a little and pretended she hadn't.

"It's beautiful," Mirabel said, meaning it, meaning more than the cake, hating how much they meant.

"So are you," Rosa said loudly from the doorway, and Jun said "*Tía,*" in a strangled voice, and Mirabel laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of them, bright and unguarded—and the sound seemed to surprise everyone in the room including themselves.

* * *

Priya collected the cake at 11:40 with the reverence of a bomb-disposal technician. By noon it was standing in the pavilion, flawless, and the bride would never know she'd nearly married beneath a seagull's lunch. Mirabel texted Priya confirmation, then stood in the parking lot outside the museum with their phone in hand and realized they had no reason left to be anywhere near Harbor Street, and that they wanted one anyway.

They told themselves it was about the spatula. They had, in the chaos, used Jun's favorite offset spatula—the one with the worn wooden handle—and it was in their bag. Returning it was basic decency. Basic, uncomplicated, purely logistical decency.

The bell chimed as they pushed open the bakery door at 2 p.m., and Rosa looked up from the register with the expression of a woman whose trap had sprung precisely on schedule.

"Well, if it isn't our hero," she said. "Back so soon."

"I borrowed something." Mirabel set the spatula on the counter. "I wanted to return it. And to say thank you. For today. For—for all of it."

"Thank Jun, not me. He's in back." Rosa leaned forward, lowering her voice to a stage whisper audible in the next county. "Between us? He hasn't stopped checking the door all day. Every time the bell rang he looked up like a dog hearing a car pull in. Don't tell him I said that. Or do. Actually, do."

"Rosa—"

"He's icing cinnamon rolls," Rosa continued mercilessly. "Alone. With his thoughts. Which, knowing that boy, are probably terrible company. Go on. I'll watch the register. I'll watch anything. I have nowhere to be for the rest of my natural life."

Every instinct Mirabel owned—the ones built brick by careful brick over eleven years, the ones that had kept them safe and solvent and alone—said *leave*. Say goodbye, walk out into the October light, keep the memory of the laugh and the tide-line cake and let it stay a single perfect day, contained, risk-free.

But the letter was still sitting unopened in the drawer of their desk at home, postmarked eleven years ago, patient as a landmine. And Jun was thirty feet away behind a swinging door, icing rolls alone with his terrible thoughts, and Mirabel was so tired of doors.

They pushed through into the kitchen.

Jun looked up from the tray, a bag of icing in one hand, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Flour hung in the slanted afternoon light. The oven ticked as it cooled.

"I returned your spatula," Mirabel said.

"I noticed." Jun set down the bag. Wiped their hands. "You could've just left it with Rosa."

"I know."

Another silence, but a different one—slower, warmer, dangerous. Jun glanced toward the doorway, then back, and when they spoke their voice was low. "There's something I need to ask you. About eleven years ago. About the night before I left." Their jaw tightened. "There's a version of events I've carried all this time, and seeing you at the market—it doesn't fit anymore. Something happened that week, Mirabel. To both of us. And I think somebody made sure of it."

The oven ticked. Outside, faintly, a gull cried over the water.

"What do you mean, somebody?" Mirabel asked.

Jun reached into their apron pocket and set something on the steel counter between them: a photograph, creased white with age, showing a man in a gray suit standing outside the old courthouse steps. Mirabel had never seen his face before.

But they recognized the name printed on the developer's placard behind him, the one being interviewed by a reporter whose mic read *Channel 7*:

**MERIDIAN DEVELOPMENT — HALLORAN, V.P. ACQUISITIONS.**

"I mean," Jun said quietly, "that I think the same man who took me away from here is the one trying to take this bakery now. And I think you've seen him before too. Haven't you, Mirabel?"

The flour drifted down through the light like slow snow, and Mirabel, who planned everything and risked nothing, felt the ground begin—very gently—to tilt.
