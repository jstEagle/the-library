## 6. What the Letter Said

The letter sat on Mirabel's kitchen table for six days before they touched it.

They had carried it home from the estate sale in a canvas tote between a chipped teapot and a stack of hymnals Mrs. Adeyemi's family hadn't wanted, and then they had set it down in the exact center of the table—the way you'd set down something live, something with teeth—and they had built their week around it. Grocery runs that took the long way so they wouldn't pass the table. Dishwashing done standing at the sink with their back to it. Two client consultations conducted by phone in the living room, voice pitched carefully casual, as if the letter might be listening and report back to whoever sent it.

Eleven years of careful living, and one envelope had turned their apartment into a minefield.

On the sixth night—the night after Rosa Okafor had pressed a box of day-old almond croissants into their hands and said, *You look like a woman who hasn't slept, mija, take these and stop arguing*—Mirabel came home, put the croissants on the counter, and sat down across from the letter like an opponent across a chessboard.

It was smaller than they remembered letters being. A plain white envelope gone soft and tea-colored at the edges, the adhesive long dead, the flap lifting slightly at one corner as if it had tried, over the years, to open itself. The postmark was smudged but legible: the second week of October, eleven years ago. Four days before Jun disappeared. Four days before Mirabel's life had closed like a door.

Their name was written on the front in Jun's handwriting—slanted left, the *M* with its dramatic first stroke, the kind of handwriting that had opinions. Mirabel had spent three years of their early twenties reading that handwriting on napkins, on program margins, on the fogged window of Jun's ancient Corolla. *Mirabel Ishikawa,* it said now, formal as a summons, no endearment, which somehow was worse than if it had said *my love.*

They made tea they didn't drink. They lit the lamp instead of the overhead light because the overhead light felt like an interrogation room and whatever was about to happen deserved better than interrogation. They sat. They stood. They walked to the window and looked down at Harbor Street, where the bakery's sign glowed its warm butter-yellow against the blue dark—*Okafor & Sons, est. 1924*, though there were no sons anymore, only Rosa, only flour dust on the doorstep every morning like snow that refused to melt—and they thought: *Jun is four hundred meters away right now. I could walk down there and ask. I could knock on the door of the apartment above the ovens and say, explain yourself, explain all of it, out loud, where I can watch your face.*

But they didn't trust themselves to watch Jun's face. That was the truth of it. Eleven years ago they had watched a face—that face, those eyes going somewhere distant and final—and they had believed what they decided it meant, and believing it had cost them everything. They were not going to do it again. If there was an explanation, it would be in writing, where it couldn't change expression halfway through.

Mirabel picked up the letter.

The paper inside was thicker than the envelope, folded in thirds, and when they drew it out two more sheets slid free behind it—no, not sheets. Half-sheets. Torn edges. Their heart did something complicated and painful, and they made themselves smooth the pages flat on the table under the lamp, made themselves read from the beginning even though their eyes kept snagging on the torn edge of the last page like a tongue finding a broken tooth.

*Dear Mira,*

*I've started this letter nine times. Theo says if you start something ten times you have to finish it or admit you never meant to. So this is number ten.*

*By the time you read this I'll be gone, and I need you to hear me all the way through before you decide what it means, because I know how your mind works—I love how your mind works—but you will build a whole cathedral of blame in about ninety seconds and move in before the paint dries.*

Mirabel's breath caught. They looked up from the page at nothing, at the dark window, at their own ghosted reflection holding lamplight like a held breath.

*So here it is, plainly, because you deserve plain: I didn't leave because of you. I didn't leave because of what you said. I need you to believe that even if you can't believe anything else.*

*After your testimony on Tuesday, someone came to see me. Not at the shop—at my mother's place, which scared me more than anything else, because how did he know where she lived? He knew things, Mira. He knew what I'd told you in confidence. He knew what you'd left out of your statement and why, and he laid it all out on her kitchen table like he was dealing cards. He said if I didn't leave town—if I stayed anywhere within reach of you—he would make sure the half-truths you told became perjury charges. He had documents. He had a transcript with certain passages highlighted. He said your reputation, your license, your whole future could be arranged to disappear, and that the only version of events anyone would ever hear was the one he chose to tell.*

Mirabel stopped reading.

The apartment was very quiet. Down on Harbor Street a delivery truck hissed past, and the sound seemed to come from another world, another weather system. Mirabel read the paragraph again. Then a third time, moving their lips slightly, the way you do when the words refuse to convert into meaning.

Someone had come to Jun. Someone had threatened Jun—with *Mirabel*. With the testimony. With the half-truths Mirabel had told to protect—

To protect whom? That was the question they had spent eleven years not asking, the load-bearing wall of their entire architecture of guilt, and now the letter was tapping on it with one finger.

*He gave me until Friday morning. He said if I was still in the city by Friday noon, you'd be finished—not just embarrassed, finished. Charged. And Mira, I know what you did, why you did it, and I would rather break both my own hands than be the reason you paid for protecting me.*

Protecting him.

The words went through Mirabel like cold water through cloth. Because that was it, wasn't it—that was the thing they had never said aloud to anyone, not to Theo, not to the lawyer, not to the mirror. Eleven years ago, twenty-six years old and terrified, called to give a statement about the accident at the marina—the collapsed scaffolding, the injured worker, the safety violations everyone on the waterfront knew about and no one would name—Mirabel had softened their account. Had misremembered, politely, strategically. Had placed Jun across the harbor at the time of the inspection, when Jun had been right there beside them, because Jun had begged them, because Jun's aunt's bakery lease and Jun's whole family's livelihood hung on keeping his name out of the deposition, because the developer's people had made it very clear what happened to businesses that cooperated too fully with investigators.

And then Jun had vanished, and Mirabel had been left holding a statement that was half lie, and the investigation had collapsed, and the story that spread through the harbor was that Mirabel Ishikawa had lied on the stand to cover for a lover who ran at the first sign of trouble. Their event-planning clients had evaporated. Their name, which their grandmother had carried across an ocean and handed to them like an heirloom, had become a cautionary tale told at harborside bars. *Careful with that one. Careful what she'll say and what she won't.*

Mirabel had built a new life out of the wreckage—a life of other people's perfect days, days planned down to the minute, days where nothing was left to chance because chance had already taken everything once. Plan everything. Risk nothing. And underneath it all, the foundation stone, the thing the whole structure rested on: *Jun chose to go. Jun let me take the fall. Jun ran.*

And the letter, in Jun's slanted, opinionated hand, was saying: *I was pushed.*

*He said—*

Mirabel turned the page. The next lines were pressed hard into the paper, the pen nearly tearing through:

*He said the strangest thing before he left. He said, "Tell them the harbor always sells. Tell them everybody has a price; yours is just higher than most." Like it was a script he'd used before. Like he enjoyed it. I wrote down everything I remember about him while it was fresh—height, coat, the ring he wore, the way he said your full name like he'd practiced it. It's in my notebook. If anything happens to me, find the notebook. Don't trust the police here, some of them eat from his hand. Trust—*

The sentence stopped.

Not ended. Stopped. Mid-word, almost—Mirabel tilted the page to the lamp and saw the ink trail off in a short drag, as if the pen had been lifted abruptly, or the writer interrupted. *Trust—* and then nothing. No signature. No *love, Jun.* No *yours.* Just the torn edge, ragged where the page had been ripped away, and behind it the other fragments: two more partial pages, continuation text whose beginnings were missing, orphaned sentences about a train ticket and a phone call and *Rosa can't know, he watches the shop*—and then those too simply ceased, cut off mid-thought, mid-word, mid-*trust.*

Mirabel set the pages down very carefully, the way you set down something that might detonate.

Then they stood up, walked to the sink, and gripped the counter's edge with both hands, and breathed, and breathed, and breathed.

The ground was tilting. That was the only way to describe it—not falling, not breaking, but tilting, the whole floor of the last eleven years angling slowly beneath their feet so that everything they had stacked on it began to slide. The betrayal. The abandonment. The eleven years of *they chose to leave, they let me burn.* All of it resting on an assumption Mirabel had made in the wreckage of one terrible month, an assumption no one had ever confirmed, an assumption that had hardened into fact through sheer repetition until Mirabel had built a career and a personality and a fortress on top of it.

*He said if I didn't leave, you'd be finished.*

Every certainty re-examined itself in the lamplight. The wedding they'd fled from planning, the clients who'd whispered, the sister-in-law's gentle questions about why Mirabel never dated—*you're so good at love, Mira, for other people*—all of it refracting now through a new lens. Mirabel had thought they were living in the aftermath of Jun's choice. They had been living, all along, in the aftermath of someone else's threat. A man with a ring and a rehearsed cruelty, who had walked into Jun's mother's kitchen and dismantled two lives with a folder of highlighted transcripts.

And the half-truths—God, the half-truths. Mirabel had carried the shame of them like a stone in the chest for eleven years, the private knowledge that they had lied under pressure, that their integrity had a crack in it. But the letter reframed even that. *I know what you did, why you did it.* Jun had known. Jun had understood that the lie was protection, coerced protection, and had been willing to disappear forever rather than let that protection turn into a prosecution.

Which meant the man in the kitchen hadn't just wanted Jun gone. He had wanted Mirabel isolated. Ruined-adjacent. Credibility shredded just enough that no one would ever listen to them again. And it had worked—it had worked beautifully—because here was Mirabel, eleven years later, a person so thoroughly discredited in their own eyes that they planned strangers' weddings and testified to nothing, believed nothing, risked nothing.

*Everybody has a price; yours is just higher than most.*

A name surfaced through the tilt and slide of Mirabel's thoughts, unbidden, unwelcome: *Halloran.* The fixer. The man who had sat across from twenty-six-year-old Mirabel in a conference room that smelled of carpet cleaner and menace, adjusting his cufflinks, saying *we'd hate for this to get complicated for you, Ms. Ishikawa, or for the Okafor family—leases are such fragile things.* The same calm, the same rehearsed sentences, the same enjoyment worn thin as a veneer over something colder. Mirabel had told themselves for years that memory had exaggerated him, made him monstrous to excuse their own collapse. But Jun's letter described him exactly—the precision of it, the *he said your full name like he'd practiced it*—and memory, it turned out, had not exaggerated anything.

Mirabel sank back into the chair. The croissants sat untouched on the counter, filling the kitchen with the smell of almonds and butter, absurdly domestic, the smell of the bakery, of Rosa, of the life that had gone on without Jun for eleven years while Jun—where? Doing what? Living what kind of years?

And the missing pages. That was the thing that finally got Mirabel up and moving, pacing the small kitchen with the letter clutched in one hand. Because the letter didn't end. It was amputated. *Trust—* whom? What happened after the threat was delivered? Where did Jun go, who helped, what did the notebook say, what was in the transcript with its highlighted passages—Halloran's transcript, Halloran's copy of a deposition that should have existed in exactly one place? Pages didn't tear themselves out of envelopes. Someone had intercepted this letter, read it, removed what mattered, and mailed the rest—or mailed it late, deliberately, a wound delivered eleven years tardy. Either way, the missing pages existed somewhere. In a drawer. In a file. In the belongings of a dead neighbor who had collected other people's undelivered mail the way some people collect stamps.

Mrs. Adeyemi. The estate sale. The box marked *misc. correspondence* that Mirabel had bought mostly out of guilt, mostly because no one else would.

Mirabel looked at the tote bag still sitting by the door where they'd dropped it six days ago, the hymnals and the teapot and the rest of Mrs. Adeyemi's paper ghosts, and felt the ground finish its tilt and settle into a new angle—one that pointed, unmistakably, toward those boxes.

Down on Harbor Street, the bakery's yellow sign burned steady against the dark. Somewhere above the ovens, Jun was asleep, or awake, or staring at their own ceiling thinking about torn pages and old threats. Tomorrow Mirabel would have to stand in that bakery and hand over a catering order and pretend the earth hadn't moved. Tonight, they had boxes to open.

They fetched a knife, knelt by the tote bag, and slit the tape on the first box of Mrs. Adeyemi's paper life.

* * *

Three hours later, surrounded by forty years of a stranger's correspondence—birthday cards, utility bills, a letter from a senator, recipes written on index cards—Mirabel found it: a rubber-banded bundle labeled in Mrs. Adeyemi's careful schoolteacher hand, *Harbor business — keep.* Inside, newspaper clippings about Meridian Development's early acquisitions. A photocopied zoning notice. And a note, dated the same October as the postmark, in the same hand:

*Mr. H. asked me what I saw at the marina that Tuesday. Told him I see nothing, hear nothing, same as always. He didn't like that. Watched the mail truck extra close after. Some letters are worth more than others, I think.*

Mirabel sat very still among the paper ghosts, the lamp humming, dawn still hours away, and understood two things with total clarity.

The missing pages had passed through Mrs. Adeyemi's hands.

And somewhere in these boxes—or wherever she had hidden the things she thought were worth more than others—was everything Halloran had spent eleven years making sure no one would ever read.
