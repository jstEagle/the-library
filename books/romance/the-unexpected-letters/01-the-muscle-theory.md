## 1. The Muscle Theory

The heart is a muscle. That was the first thing August Eriksen had ever decided about love, at nineteen, standing in a hospital corridor that smelled of antiseptic and cold coffee, and it was the thing she still believed at thirty-four, on the third Saturday of June, watching two hundred guests rise to their feet as a string quartet swelled beneath a canopy of white peonies.

Muscles could be trained. Muscles could be contained. A muscle did what you told it to do, if you were disciplined enough, if you rehearsed enough, if you never — not once — let it act on its own initiative.

She stood at the back of the ballroom at Calloway House with her hands folded and her weight even on both feet, and she ran the wedding the way a conductor runs a symphony: with her whole body listening.

The cake had arrived at 2:15, forty minutes early, which meant the florist would try to move the dessert table six inches left to accommodate her own light rig. August had already anticipated this. She caught Marta's eye across the room and gave the smallest shake of her head — *not yet* — and Marta, who had worked with her for six years and understood that August communicated in a language of micro-gestures and silence, drifted casually into the space between the table and the florist like a bodyguard who happened to be holding a clipboard.

The groom's mother had been drinking since noon. August knew this because she knew everything: because she had memorized the seating chart until it lived behind her eyelids, because she had catalogued every fault line in both families the way a seismologist catalogues faults, because competence was the only prayer she'd ever trusted. The mother would make it through the toasts. She would wobble during the father-daughter dance. At approximately 9:40 she would attempt to corner the bride near the restrooms and say something unforgivable, and August would already be there, already steering, already offering her arm and a glass of water and a distraction shaped like a compliment.

This was the work. Not romance — logistics. Not love — containment. Everyone in this room believed they were witnessing the beginning of something; August knew she was managing the first hours of its decay, or else its improbable survival, and either way her job was identical: keep the machinery running, keep the illusion intact, keep everyone believing for one more night that they were the exception.

*The heart is a muscle,* she recited silently, the way other people said grace. *It can be trained. It can be contained. What it wants is not what it needs.*

The bride, Priya, came down the aisle at 4:02 on the dot, two minutes ahead of schedule, which August counted as a personal victory. The sunlight through the east windows did exactly what August had promised it would do when she'd walked Priya through the venue eleven months ago — laid itself across the floor in long gold panels, so that the bride appeared to walk through light rather than past it. Priya cried. Dev cried harder. Two hundred people made the sound that crowds make when they witness something they want to believe in, that soft collective exhalation, half sigh and half surrender.

August felt nothing.

That wasn't quite true, and she was honest enough with herself — brutally, clinically honest, it was one of her few indulgences — to know it wasn't true. She felt the pleasure of execution. The clean click of a plan meeting reality without friction. Somewhere under her sternum there was a small warm hum, the way a machine hums when it's running well. But the thing the guests were feeling, the swelling, the ache, the belief — that passed over her like weather over glass.

She had run sixty-one weddings. She had watched sixty-one couples promise each other things neither of them could guarantee. She kept a private ledger, unwritten but exact, of every way love failed: the slow drift, the sudden betrayal, the resentment that calcified like limescale, the deaths that broke the survivor instead of the marriage, the affairs that began in exactly the rooms where she now arranged centerpieces. She had seen grooms cry real tears at altars and file for divorce within eighteen months. She had seen brides whisper *I'll always choose you* and then, four years later, choose someone else in a hotel bar in another city. Love was not a force. Love was a weather system, and weather could not be promised to anyone.

But a muscle — a muscle responded to training. You could teach it restraint. You could teach it to hold its shape under load. You could take the wildest, most disobedient organ in the human body and, through sheer repetition, teach it to stay.

She had been doing it for fifteen years. Her heart had never once acted on its own initiative.

* * *

Cocktail hour on the terrace, golden hour, the harbor going copper and rose beyond the hedge. August moved through the crowd with a tablet she never looked at, because looking at it would suggest she needed it, and August Eriksen did not need anything. She adjusted a boutonniere by two degrees. She redirected an uncle away from the bride's college friends before he could begin his story about the yacht. She tasted the signature cocktail — elderflower, gin, something with too much citrus — and sent a single raised eyebrow to the bar captain, and by the time she reached the far end of the terrace the recipe had been corrected.

"You're doing the thing," said Marta, falling into step beside her.

"What thing."

"The thing where you're everywhere at once and nowhere at all. It's unsettling. The guests think you're a ghost."

"Ghosts don't carry tablets."

"You're not carrying the tablet. You're carrying it like a prop." Marta lowered her voice. "Priya's grandmother wants to give a toast at dinner. Unplanned. Priya says if you say no, it breaks her heart, and if you say yes, her mother will be insufferable about it all night."

August's jaw tightened fractionally. Unscripted toasts were variables, and variables were how flawless evenings died. "How long?"

"Three minutes, she promises."

"Everyone promises three minutes."

"She's eighty-six, August. What's she going to do, stage-dive?"

August considered. An eighty-six-year-old woman, three minutes, a room full of people softened by wine and sentiment. Risk assessment: moderate. Emotional yield for the client: high. And Priya had been a difficult bride in the best way — particular, exacting, worth pleasing.

"Between the entrées and the toasts proper," August said. "I'll cue her myself."

Marta grinned. "You have a heart in there somewhere. I've heard rumors."

"It's a muscle, Marta. I work it out."

* * *

Dinner was served at 7:30. By 8:50 the plates had been cleared from the last course, and August moved to the head table's edge and touched Priya's shoulder, and Priya's face lit with the specific gratitude of a bride whose planner has remembered every promise, and August lifted her chin toward the microphone stand by the dance floor.

Mrs. Kamala Whitfield rose from her seat the way old women rise when they have decided to rise — slowly, entirely, with a kind of ceremonial inevitability, smoothing her sari, refusing the arm offered by her grandson-in-law-to-be. She was small and straight-backed, with white hair braided in a crown and eyes that seemed amused by everything, including, possibly, August.

She reached the microphone. She did not tap it. She simply waited, with the patience of someone who had outlasted empires, until the room quieted on its own.

"I am going to tell you about letters," she said.

August, standing at the periphery with her hands folded, felt the first faint prickle of attention — the professional instinct that registers an unscripted element entering the field.

"For sixty-one years," Mrs. Whitfield said, "I have kept every letter my husband ever wrote me. Every one. Even the terrible ones. Especially the terrible ones." A ripple of laughter. "When we were engaged, Arjun was working in Colombo and I was here, and we wrote to each other twice a week for fourteen months. Fourteen months before I saw his face again. Do you know what you learn about a person from fourteen months of letters?"

She paused. She didn't need the pause — the room was already hers — but she took it anyway, the way a musician takes a fermata, because the silence was part of the music.

"You learn what they think about when no one is watching. You learn how they apologize — because everyone writes apologies eventually, even the proud ones, especially the proud ones. You learn whether they are curious. Curiosity, my darlings, is the only reliable predictor of a long marriage. Passion is common. Beauty is common. But a person who asks you questions, on paper, week after week, with no audience and nothing to gain—" she shook her head, and her crown braid caught the candlelight, "—that person has given you the only vow that matters. Which is: *I am still paying attention.*"

August's hands, folded at her waist, had gone very still.

"Arjun died nine years ago," Mrs. Whitfield continued, and her voice did not break; it deepened, like water finding a lower channel. "And do you know what I do, some mornings? I make my tea, and I sit by the window, and I read one letter. Just one. And he is alive again — not the way photographs make him alive, which is flat, which is finished, but the way a voice makes someone alive. Because a letter is a voice. A letter is a person thinking at you, slowly, honestly, in their own handwriting, with all their mistakes still visible." She smiled out at the young couple, at Priya's tear-bright face. "You two will text each other a thousand times a day, I'm sure. Wonderful. Efficient. But I want to tell you what nobody tells you: the messages delete themselves, dears. They evaporate. They leave nothing you can hold. So write to each other sometimes. On paper. Badly, if you must. Write when you're angry and don't send it. Write when you're tender and do. Keep them. In sixty years, one of you will want to hear the other one thinking."

She raised her glass. "To Priya and Dev. May you always be paying attention."

The room rose. Glasses lifted. Dev was wiping his eyes with his napkin, and Priya had turned to look at him with an expression so nakedly hopeful that August had to look away.

She looked out the terrace doors instead, at the harbor, at the last copper light going gray-blue at the edges, and she found that her recitation wouldn't come. *The heart is a muscle. It can be trained. It can be contained.* The words were there, in their usual place, but they sounded different tonight — thinner, somehow, like a hymn sung in an empty church. Because the old woman had described something August's theory had no shelf for. Sixty-one years of letters. A man dead nine years, still thinking at his wife across a kitchen table every morning. Attention as a vow. Handwriting as a voice.

A letter was evidence. That was the trouble with letters. August knew this better than anyone — she had a box of them herself, in the bottom of her closet, in her brother's slanted handwriting, and she had not opened it in four years, because Marcus's letters were the one archive she could not bear to reread and could not bring herself to destroy. Evidence of what he'd wanted. Evidence of what he'd believed about her. Evidence, in the end, of the night everything at the Meridian Hotel had come down, along with the east atrium and her brother and Jonah Vale's career and, though no one had ever said it aloud, August's ability to believe in anything unsupervised.

*Keep them,* the old woman had said. *One of you will want to hear the other one thinking.*

August's chest did something unauthorized. Small — a single off-beat, a skipped stitch — but unauthorized, and she noted it the way she noted a cake arriving early or a mother drinking since noon: catalogued, contained, scheduled for management.

By 9:38 she was standing near the restrooms with a glass of water and a compliment ready, and the groom's mother never got within ten feet of the bride.

* * *

The last guests left at midnight. The band packed up. The candles were extinguished in order, west wall to east, and the ballroom returned to being a room — just a room, with chairs stacked against walls and the smell of snuffed wax and peonies beginning to brown at their edges. August walked it once, alone, the way she always did, checking the corpse of the evening for anything left unfinished.

In the empty foyer, she found Mrs. Whitfield waiting for her car, sitting very straight on a bench with her clutch in her lap.

"Your grandmother is still outside," August said. "They're bringing the car around."

"I know. I asked for a moment." The old woman looked up at her, and her eyes were as amused as ever, but there was something underneath the amusement now, something appraising. "You're the one who makes it all happen. The girl behind the curtain."

"I'm the planner, yes."

"Mm." Mrs. Whitfield patted the bench beside her. August, after a fractional hesitation, sat. "You didn't laugh at my toast."

"It was a lovely toast."

"You didn't laugh, and you didn't cry, and you stood by the door the entire time like a woman guarding something." The old woman said it without accusation, almost idly, the way one remarks on the weather. "Sixty-one years of letters, and the only person in the room unmoved was the one who arranges love for a living. Isn't that interesting."

August kept her face pleasant and professional. "I find my work moving in different ways than the guests do."

"Of course you do." Mrs. Whitfield studied her for a long moment, and August had the disorienting sense of being read — thoroughly, accurately, the way she herself read rooms. "My granddaughter tells me you are the best in the city. That nothing surprises you. That you planned this wedding down to the minute and it went like clockwork." She smiled. "Clockwork is beautiful, dear. But it isn't alive. When you're ready to be surprised by something, come find me. I'll lend you a letter or two. The terrible ones especially."

Her car arrived. She rose, refused help again, and paused at the door.

"One more thing," she said. "Arjun's first letter to me was rude. Genuinely rude — he criticized a book I loved. I nearly didn't answer it." Her eyes glinted. "Best decision of my life was answering a stranger's rude letter. Remember that, if it ever happens to you."

Then she was gone, into the night, leaving August alone in the foyer with the candles out and the peonies browning and a sentence lodged under her ribs like a splinter.

*Answering a stranger's rude letter.*

She stood there longer than she meant to. Then she checked her watch, confirmed the load-out was on schedule, and walked back into the ballroom to finish killing the lights.

* * *

Monday morning, Delia Marsh was waiting in August's office with two coffees and the expression she wore when she wanted something — bright, warm, faintly predatory, the smile of a woman who had built the most powerful planning firm on the West Coast and intended to keep it.

"You were extraordinary Saturday," Delia said, setting a cup on August's desk. "The Whitfields are telling everyone. Priya called me personally — personally, August — to say you'd thought of everything."

"We got lucky with the light."

"Don't be modest, it doesn't suit you." Delia settled into the chair across the desk, crossed her legs, and produced, from her bag, a glossy folder embossed with the city seal. "Which is why I need you. The Harborline Pen-Pal Project — the mayor's little civic initiative, strangers writing strangers, community bonding, all of it. Terrible press coverage so far. The *Gazette* called it 'well-meaning and forgettable.' But Marsh & Eriksen is a pillar of this community, and I've committed us to a partnership. Our name on the program, our staff enrolled as ambassadors." She slid the folder across. "Yours is already in. First assignment comes this week."

August looked at the folder without touching it. "Delia. I plan weddings. I don't write to strangers."

"You write beautifully. I've read your client letters — people frame them." Delia's smile widened a degree. "Besides, it's PR. One letter a month. Think of it as a muscle you haven't been working."

Outside the office window, the harbor lay flat and silver, and somewhere across the city a computer — or a hand nudging it — was preparing to place a name beside hers.

August picked up the folder.
