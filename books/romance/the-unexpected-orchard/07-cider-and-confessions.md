## 7. Cider and Confessions

The bonfire at Hollowmere was the kind of fire people built when they wanted to remember something — tall enough to see from the road, crackling loud enough to fill the silences that families carry like heirlooms.

Emrys had been at the reunion for six hours, and she had already lied forty times. She'd lied about how she and Wren met (a rainy Tuesday, a shared umbrella outside a bakery — Wren's invention, embarrassingly charming). She'd lied about her favorite season (autumn, apparently, though she'd always been a spring person; autumn felt like the correct answer for a woman dating an orchard boy). She'd lied with her hand in his, which was the strangest lie of all, because it hadn't felt like lying. It had felt like a hand.

Now the sun had gone down behind the western rows of trees, turning the sky the color of bruised plums, and someone had pressed a mug of fresh cider into her hands — Ida, probably, who had taken one look at Emrys's half-empty cup and refilled it without asking, the way grandmothers do when they've decided something about you.

"Careful," Wren said, dropping onto the bench beside her. "That's not grocery store cider. That's my mother's press."

"Your mother's press?"

He nodded toward the far edge of the firelight, where an old wooden contraption sat under a tarp like a sleeping animal. "She used to run it every October. Whole town came. You'd bring apples, you'd leave with jugs." He turned his mug slowly in his hands. "Aldous wants to sell it as scrap."

Emrys took a sip to keep from saying something reckless. The cider hit her tongue like a decision — sweet first, then sharp, then something darker underneath, almost bitter, almost honest. It was nothing like the pasteurized stuff. It tasted alive, slightly wild, like drinking the orchard itself.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's unfair."

"Right?" He was smiling at her, and the firelight did something dishonest to his face, softening the tiredness around his eyes into something warmer. "Told you. Unfair."

They'd been careful all day. Careful in front of the cousins, careful in front of Aldous, careful in the way two people are careful when they're balancing on a story that could tip over at any moment. But now the family had scattered into smaller constellations — children chasing each other between the trees with sparklers, aunts arguing amiably about pie crust, Ida holding court in a lawn chair like a small, formidable queen — and the careful space around them had widened just enough to become dangerous.

"You're good at this," Wren said quietly.

"At what? Lying?"

"At being here." He said it simply, without flattery in it, which was why it landed somewhere beneath her ribs. "My cousin Devan cornered me earlier and asked if I'd finally lost my mind, bringing a stranger home. And I realized I didn't have a good answer. Because you don't feel like a stranger."

"That's the cider talking."

"That's three sips of cider talking. You're a lightweight."

"I'm *economical*," Emrys said, and he laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she suspected he rationed out carefully the rest of the year. The sound went through her like warmth through cold hands, and she looked back at the fire before he could see what it did to her face.

*Rule three,* she reminded herself. *No falling for anyone.* She had written the rules herself. She had negotiated them across her own counter like a lawyer, because rules were how she survived things: no real names, no touching after dessert, no falling. Clean lines. Load-bearing walls. She had built her whole life out of them since Daniel, since the ring came off and the lease came due and she learned that love was a thing that could be repossessed.

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, a child shrieked with delight over a sparkler.

"My mother would have liked you," Wren said.

Emrys's cup paused halfway to her mouth. All day he had spoken about his mother in the conditional tense — *she used to, she always would* — but never directly, never in a way that invited response. Now he had opened a door and stood beside it, not quite looking at her.

"You think so?"

"She'd have liked that you argue with me." He turned the mug again. "Everyone in my family either agrees with Aldous to keep the peace or fights him so loudly nobody listens. You do this third thing. You just—" he gestured vaguely at her, at all of her, "—quietly refuse."

"I charge extra for refusing."

"I know. I've seen your rates." But he was still watching the fire, and his voice had dropped into a lower register, the one people use when they're approaching something carefully, the way you approach a dog that's been kicked. "Can I tell you something? Off the record. Not fake-girlfriend Emrys. Just—"

"Wren." She said his name and watched him startle slightly, as if he'd forgotten she knew it. "Off the record."

He exhaled. "I haven't been back here in six years. Did I tell you that?"

"You said you don't come home much."

"That's the polite version." He drank. The firelight moved on his face. "The real version is that after Mom died, I couldn't stand the smell of this place. Apples. Everyone thinks apple smell is cheerful, pie and autumn and school starting. But grief gets wired to whatever was happening when it happened, and for me it was apples. Six years I couldn't walk past the produce section without—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I took a job in the city partly because it was far away from anything that grew."

Emrys held very still. She knew this territory. She knew the way grief rewires a person's geography, turns whole categories of ordinary life into landmines. For her it had been restaurants — she and Daniel had gotten engaged in one, and for a year afterward she couldn't sit across from anyone at a candlelit table without her chest tightening.

"What changed?" she asked.

"You did. Sort of." He huffed a laugh at himself. "You walked into my mornings with flour on your apron and opinions about my order, and suddenly I was standing in a bakery every day choosing to be there. Around ovens and fruit and sweetness. It took me months to figure out why that mattered." He finally looked at her. "I came back here because of the reunion, officially. But I think I came back because some part of me thought — if I could stand in this orchard with you next to me, maybe the apples would just be apples again."

The fire cracked. A log collapsed inward, sending up a fountain of sparks, and Emrys used the motion to hide the fact that she had forgotten to breathe.

*Rule three,* said the sensible voice in her head. *Rule three, rule three.*

But there was another voice underneath it, older and tireder, the one that had been keeping score since the bank started calling: *when was the last time anyone told you the truth like it cost them something?*

"That's a lot to put on a fake girlfriend," she said, aiming for light and missing by a mile.

"Yeah." He winced. "Sorry. That was—"

"No." She shook her head. "No, it's—" She looked into her cup, at the pale gold surface trembling with the heat of her hands. And because the cider had loosened something, or because the fire made confession feel safe the way darkness does, or because she was simply tired — bone-tired — of carrying everything alone behind a counter, she heard herself say: "I was engaged once."

Wren went still beside her.

"Three years ago. His name was Daniel. We were together five years, engaged for eight months." She turned the mug in her hands, mimicking his gesture without meaning to. "He left eleven days before the wedding. Not dramatically. No affair, no fight. He just sat me down at our kitchen table and said he'd realized he didn't want the life we'd planned. Said it like he was returning a sweater." A short laugh escaped her, brittle at the edges. "Eleven days. Do you know what you can cancel in eleven days? Almost nothing. The caterer kept the deposit. My aunt flew in from Oslo anyway. I ate the top tier of our wedding cake alone in my apartment for a month, because I'd already paid for it and I don't waste things."

"Emrys."

"And everyone kept saying, *you'll love again, you're young, better now than later.* Like love is a bus. Like another one just comes along." She swallowed. "What nobody tells you is that after something like that, you stop trusting your own judgment. Every instinct I had told me Daniel was forever, and my instincts were wrong, so what are they worth now? So I stopped listening to them. I built the café instead. Recipes don't leave. Dough doesn't sit you down at a table and take back five years." She glanced at him, and the fire made his expression hard to read — something pained and attentive all at once. "So that's why I deflect. When you flirt. It's not you. You're — objectively, you're very—" She gestured at him, giving up on the sentence. "It's that I decided letting people in means losing them, and I decided that before I met you, and I've been very committed to the decision."

The silence that followed wasn't empty. It was full — of sparks rising, of distant laughter, of the particular quality of attention Wren was giving her, which was total, which asked nothing, which was somehow worse and better than any response.

"Thank you," he said finally.

"For the saddest story you've ever heard at a bonfire?"

"For trusting me with it." He hesitated, then added, softly: "For the record, your instincts brought you here. To this orchard. To help a stranger fight for something that matters. So maybe they're not broken. Maybe they were just pointing somewhere new."

Emrys looked at him and felt the dangerous thing happen — the thing she'd felt only twice before in her life, the ground shifting under an assumption she'd built a wall on. She reached for her cider and drained it, because the alternative was answering him honestly, and rule three existed for a reason.

* * *

The evening deepened. The fire burned down to a broad orange heart, and the cold crept in from the tree rows, that specific orchard cold that seems to rise out of the ground itself. Blankets appeared — Ida produced them from somewhere, distributing wool like sacraments — and the family drew inward, closer to the flames, voices dropping into the drowsy register of people who have eaten too well and drunk just enough.

Emrys had lost count of the cider. Not lost track exactly — she knew it was somewhere between two and too many — but the edges of the evening had gone soft and golden, and Wren Calloway was sitting close enough that their shoulders touched, and neither of them had moved away.

He was telling her about the trees.

"That one," he said, pointing his mug at a massive shape silhouetted against the stars, its branches spread like arms mid-embrace. "The big one by the fence line. That's the oldest tree on the property. Mom called it the Grandmother Tree. She swore it was older than the town. When we were kids, my brother and I used to climb it and she'd yell at us from the kitchen window, but she never actually made us come down. She'd just watch until we got bored." He smiled at the memory, and then the smile changed, went tender and rueful at once. "One summer she nailed a ladder up the trunk and put a plank between two branches. A reading platform. She'd go up there with her journals and sit for hours. Writing."

"Journals," Emrys said. "Recipe journals?"

"Mm. Recipes, notes, sketches of the trees. She wrote everything down. She said a recipe is just a letter to whoever's hungry next." He said it lightly, but Emrys caught the weight underneath it — the way his thumb pressed white against the side of his mug.

Somewhere beyond the firelight, a door closed. Uncle Aldous's silhouette crossed between the house and the barn, phone glowing blue against his jaw, moving with the unhurried confidence of a man who has never once doubted the outcome of anything. Emrys watched him go and felt, without knowing why, a cold thread pull through the warmth of the evening.

"Wren," she began, "your uncle—"

"Don't." It came out fast, sharper than anything he'd said all day. Then he softened it immediately, reaching over to touch her wrist — brief, apologetic. "Sorry. I just — tonight I don't want to think about him. Tonight I want to remember it's still ours. Can we do that? One night where it's still ours?"

The word *ours* hung in the air between them, doing work it had no business doing. He meant the orchard. Obviously he meant the orchard. But the word landed elsewhere, in the place where their shoulders touched, and Emrys took a slow breath of woodsmoke and cold air and let it go unexamined.

"Okay," she said. "One night."

The party thinned by degrees. Cousins drifted toward guest rooms; children were carried in asleep over shoulders; the fire settled into embers that breathed rather than blazed. Ida was the last holdout besides them, and eventually she rose from her lawn chair with the groan of the victorious elderly, wrapped her shawl tighter, and paused beside them on her way to the house.

"Wren," she said, and there was something in her voice Emrys couldn't parse — fondness braided with gravity. "Come find me tomorrow morning. Before breakfast. There's something I want to show you. Something of your mother's."

His head came up. "What kind of something?"

Ida's eyes flicked toward the house — toward, Emrys noticed, the lit window of what must have been the study — and then back to her grandson. Something passed across her face, quick and guarded, gone before Emrys could name it.

"Something you'll want to see," she said only, and squeezed Emrys's shoulder on her way past — a touch that lingered a half-second longer than politeness required — and then she was a shape moving up the path, swallowed by the dark of the porch.

Emrys turned to ask Wren what that was about and found him staring after his grandmother with an expression she hadn't seen on him before. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has just heard the first distant thunder of a storm he'd been pretending wasn't coming.

"Wren?"

"Hm?" He surfaced slowly. "Nothing. She's — she does this. Cryptic grandmother things." But his hands had gone still around the mug, and the ease of the last hour had drained out of his shoulders.

The cold won the argument shortly after. They banked the fire together — he shoveled earth over the embers while she stacked the mugs, an easy domestic choreography that neither of them commented on — and walked back toward the farmhouse along the row of trees, their breath ghosting silver in the dark.

Halfway up the path, Emrys shivered. It was involuntary and immediate and she hated it, because she saw Wren clock it in profile, saw the decision move through him.

He stopped walking. "Hold on."

"It's fine, I'm—"

"Here." He shrugged out of his jacket — no, not the jacket; from the bench near the door he'd grabbed a blanket earlier, one of Ida's, thick gray wool that smelled faintly of cedar and woodsmoke — and before Emrys could construct a protest, he stepped behind her and drew it around her shoulders.

And then he did the thing that broke her rule.

He tucked it closer. Both hands gathering the wool at her collarbone, drawing the edges together against the cold, his knuckles brushing her throat, his chest a breath away from her back. It lasted four seconds. Maybe five. It was nothing — a practical act, a chivalrous reflex, the kind of thing a man does for a woman he's pretending to love in front of a family that has mostly gone to bed.

Except the family had mostly gone to bed. Except there was no audience. Except his hands stayed one moment longer than the cold required, and she felt his breath catch — actually catch, a small hitch, barely audible — and she knew he'd noticed the same thing she had: that the rule said *no touching after dessert*, and dessert had ended hours ago, and neither of them had moved.

"Thank you," Emrys said. Her voice came out lower than she intended.

"Anytime," Wren said, and it sounded less like courtesy than like a promise he was making to himself.

They walked the rest of the way in silence, side by side, not touching, the blanket around her shoulders like a shared secret. At the porch steps they stopped. The farmhouse windows glowed warm; inside, the family murmured and clattered dishes. Tomorrow there would be more performances, more lies, more of Aldous's smooth smile, and — if Ida's cryptic morning appointment meant what it seemed to mean — perhaps something else entirely, something with teeth.

"Goodnight, fake girlfriend," Wren said softly.

"Goodnight, fake boyfriend."

Neither of them moved to go inside. For a moment they just stood there in the cold, two liars wrapped in true wool, and Emrys thought: *this is the exact moment I should remember to be afraid.*

Then Wren smiled at her — tired and real and unbearably gentle — and went inside, and Emrys stood alone on the porch a while longer, holding the blanket closed at her throat where his hands had been, telling herself the warmth was from the fire.

It was not from the fire.

Above the tree line, past the dark ranks of the orchard, the Grandmother Tree stood black against the stars, older than the town, holding its secrets. And somewhere in the lit study window, a lamp clicked off, and Uncle Aldous's shadow crossed the glass one last time — unhurried, certain, going nowhere.
