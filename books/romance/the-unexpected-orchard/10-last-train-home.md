## 10. Last Train Home

*Emrys*

The last morning of the weekend arrived the way all endings do — too bright, too early, and smelling faintly of someone else's coffee.

Emrys stood at the kitchen window of Ida's guest room, watching mist lift off the orchard rows like breath off a cooling pie. Below her, Wren was already outside, feeding the woodstove in the old press house, his shoulders hunched against the chill in a way that looked less like cold and more like armor. He'd been quiet since breakfast preparations began. Not cold-quiet. Bracing-quiet. She recognized it because she wore it herself most mornings — the particular stillness of a person counting down to something they didn't want to reach.

Two days. She'd been Wren Calloway's girlfriend for two days, and she had never once had to act.

That was the part that frightened her.

She'd told herself on the drive up that this would be a performance, a paid role with a costume and a script: smile at the relatives, deflect the nosy questions, collect the check, go home. She was good at roles. Roles had edges. You knew where they ended.

But somewhere between Grandmother Ida pressing a second helping of apple cake onto her plate and Wren's small cousin teaching her to braid grass crowns by the pond, the edges had dissolved. Last night at the bonfire he'd tucked her blanket closer around her shoulders without thinking about it, the way you'd done it a thousand times for someone you loved, and she had let him, and neither of them had called it what it was.

Now there was a train at 11:42 and a suitcase by the door and a rule — *no falling for anyone* — that she had broken so thoroughly and so quietly that the crime scene was already cleaned up. No evidence. Just her, standing at a window, memorizing the way steam curled from the press house chimney.

She went downstairs. Ida was in the kitchen wrapping leftover tart slices in wax paper with the efficiency of a woman who had fed armies.

"You'll take these," Ida said. It wasn't a question. "For the train. And you'll come back, Emrys. The apples won't keep forever, and neither will I."

"I don't know when—"

"Nobody ever does." Ida smoothed the wax paper flat and looked at her with eyes the same gray-green as Wren's, older and more weathered but cut from the same stone. "My daughter used to say that. *I don't know when.* She said it about everything she loved until one day there wasn't anything left to come back to." She tied the parcel with string, tugged the knot tight. "Don't be like my daughter."

Emrys took the parcel because her hands needed something to hold. "Ida, I should tell you—"

"Should you?" Ida asked mildly, and turned back to the counter, and Emrys lost her nerve the way she always did, the truth dying somewhere between her ribs and her teeth.

*Wren*

He found her on the porch, suitcase at her feet, looking like a woman waiting for a verdict.

"Grandmother packed you contraband," he said, nodding at the parcel.

"She threatened me with mortality. It was very effective."

He laughed, but it came out wrong, frayed at the edges. He shoved his hands in his pockets. There were things stacked up behind his teeth — the journals, Ida's suspicions, the locked drawer, Aldous's smile like a knife wrapped in velvet — and none of them could come out here, on this porch, with the weekend still technically alive and her train leaving in ninety minutes.

"Emrys." He said her name just to hear how it sounded in the morning air. It sounded like something he wanted to keep saying.

"Don't," she said softly. Not unkindly. "Whatever speech you're loading, don't. We had terms."

"We did."

"No touching after dessert."

"It's not dessert yet."

"It's almost dessert." She looked out at the orchard instead of at him, and he watched her jaw tighten around whatever she was actually feeling. "It was a good weekend, Wren. Your family is — God, your family is a lot, but they're a lot in the best way. Ida alone is worth the invoice."

The word landed between them like a dropped glass. *Invoice.* He watched her flinch at her own mouth.

"That's not—" she started.

"It's fine." It wasn't fine. "That was the deal. That was always the deal."

"Yes." Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't; she pressed them flat against her skirt. "So. Station?"

"So. Station."

They drove in silence that was full to bursting, the kind of silence that has too many passengers. The orchard fell away behind them in the rearview mirror — row after row of trees his mother had planted or pruned or mourned, now scheduled for auction in three weeks unless a miracle arrived in handwriting. He gripped the wheel and said nothing, because telling her would make it real, and because some stubborn, stupid part of him wanted to protect her from the weight of it. She had enough weight. He'd seen the bank notices peeking out of her bag the first morning, before she'd learned to hide them better.

At the station, the platform was nearly empty. A woman with a wheeled cart. Two teenagers sharing earbuds. The departure board flickering its indifferent litany of destinations.

Emrys bought her ticket with exact change. Of course she did. Emrys Iversen did nothing in approximations except feel things.

"This is the part," she said, "where we shake hands and agree it was professional."

"Is that the part?"

"That's the part in the contract. Section four. Professional conduct through final departure."

"I don't remember section four."

"You wrote it drunk on cider. Your handwriting was terrible."

He smiled despite himself. Then the smile faded, because the train was coming — he could feel it before he heard it, a tremor in the rails under their feet, the whole platform humming with arrival.

"Emrys." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Thank you. For — all of it. They loved you. Ida *loved* you. That's not — that doesn't happen by accident."

"It happened because I'm very good at my job."

"Stop doing that."

"Doing what?"

"Selling yourself back to me at a discount so it hurts less."

The words hung there. Her face did something complicated — a whole weather system moving through in two seconds — and then flattened into composure, and he hated how well she could do that, hated that he understood why she'd learned.

The train rounded the bend. Headlights through the morning haze.

"Well," she said. "Goodbye, fake boyfriend."

"Goodbye, fake girlfriend."

Neither of them moved.

*Emrys*

The train doors opened with a hydraulic sigh, and Emrys stepped aboard because that was the plan, because plans were the only thing she trusted anymore, because the bank did not accept feelings as legal tender.

She found a seat by the window. The parcel of tarts sat on her lap like an accusation. Through the glass, Wren stood on the platform with his hands jammed in his pockets, watching the train as if watching might change something, and God, that was the problem, that was the entire problem — he kept looking at her like she was real.

The doors chimed. The conductor moved down the aisle. Somewhere ahead, the engine gathered itself.

*Go*, she told herself. *This is the safe ending. Safe endings are the only kind you get.*

She thought about Daniel, three years ago, standing in her kitchen saying *you never let me all the way in, Emrys, loving you is like knocking on a door that's painted on* — and she had told herself he was cruel, and maybe he was, but cruelty and accuracy aren't mutually exclusive.

She thought about the café. The final notice. The number she owed, which had grown another digit of interest while she'd been away playing house in an orchard.

And underneath all of it, quieter than the rest, she thought about Ida's voice: *the recipe journals.* About the way Wren's face had shuttered whenever Aldous entered a room. About the ledger she'd glimpsed on the study desk Saturday night, and the strange, wrong smoothness of the signature on it — she'd noticed it without knowing why, the way a baker notices a dough that hasn't proofed right. Something off. Something forged.

She hadn't imagined it. She was almost sure she hadn't imagined it.

The train lurched. Began to move.

Wren raised one hand on the platform — a small wave, half salute, half surrender — and something in Emrys's chest tore cleanly down the middle.

*This is about the journals,* she told herself, already rising, already shoving the parcel into the arms of a startled teenager. *This is about property law and forged signatures and a family losing the only thing that matters to them. This is strategy. This is due diligence. This is absolutely, definitely not about the way he tucked your blanket closer.*

"This is my stop," she announced to no one, to everyone, to the universe at large.

"Ma'am, we're moving—"

"I'm aware." She was already in the aisle.

She got off at the last second — literally, the last second, the conductor catching her elbow as she swung down onto the platform, the train exhaling past her and gathering speed, carrying her ticket and her dignity and her carefully constructed exit off toward the horizon.

Wren stood frozen ten feet away, staring at her like she was a hallucination he was afraid to trust.

"You got off the train," he said.

"I got off the train."

"The train was leaving."

"Noted." She brushed a strand of hair out of her face, heart slamming, mind scrambling to build a structure around the reckless thing she'd just done before it collapsed into honesty. "I've been thinking. About the ledger in Aldous's study. The signature on the debt documents — your mother's name. I saw it Saturday night. The letterforms were wrong, Wren. The capital M. Whoever wrote it didn't know how she crossed her letters. I cross-reference handwriting every day on order slips and invoices. I know what a faked signature looks like."

He blinked. "You — what?"

"And Ida mentioned journals. Recipe journals. If your mother kept records — dates, accounts, her own hand — those pages could prove the debts are fabricated. Before the auction." She lifted her chin, all business, voice only slightly too fast. "I want to help find them. Consider it — an extension of the contract. Unpaid. Pro bono. Whatever."

Wren stared at her for a long moment. The empty rails hummed behind her. Somewhere down the platform, the woman with the cart clattered away into the morning.

"You got off a moving train," he said again, softer now, "to talk about handwriting analysis."

"I also have tarts."

A slow, disbelieving grin broke across his face — the first real one all morning — and he crossed the distance between them and stopped just short of her, hands still in his pockets, honoring section four even now, and somehow that restraint undid her more than any touch could have.

"Emrys Iversen," he said, "get in the car. We have journals to find."

She followed him across the parking lot, telling herself the whole way that this was about evidence, about justice, about a debt she could actually afford to pay.

She was lying, and for the first time in years, she didn't entirely mind.

Behind them, on the abandoned platform, the departure board flickered to its next destination — and in Wren's jacket pocket, folded tight against his heart, sat a photocopy of a journal page he still hadn't shown her, and a secret that was going to cost them both.
