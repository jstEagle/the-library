## 20. Aftermath of Apples

The gavel never came down.

I kept waiting for it — that final, irrevocable *crack* that would end the orchard the way a snapped branch ends an apple's life, one moment hanging and the next in the dirt. The auctioneer stood at his podium with his hand raised over the block, frozen mid-swing like a man who'd forgotten which direction time moved. The crowd of maybe two hundred people — neighbors, farmers, half the town of Hollowmere crammed onto folding chairs under the white auction tent — held its breath so completely I could hear the wind moving through the trees beyond the tent flaps.

Then the police detective stepped forward, said something quiet to the lawyer at the front table, and the whole fragile architecture of Uncle Aldous's morning collapsed like a soufflé pulled from the oven too soon.

I didn't watch Aldous. I want to say that was out of some nobility, but the truth is uglier: I couldn't. Twenty-nine years of Thanksgivings and christenings and funerals, of the man teaching me to skip stones on the mill pond, of him crying harder than anyone at my mother's funeral — twenty-nine years, and I couldn't look at his face while two officers walked him toward a car with its lights off. So I watched Emrys instead.

She stood at the edge of the crowd with her hands folded in front of her like a woman at a viewing, and she watched everything. She watched Aldous go. She watched the detective take custody of the ledger — my mother's ledger, the forged entries matching the torn page Emrys had carried across three states in a box of packing paper without knowing what she carried. She watched Ida, small and straight-backed in her good blue dress, sign something the county clerk slid across the folding table, and when the clerk handed back the deed — the actual deed, the one with my mother's true signature on the original loan documents, the one that made the orchard hers again — Ida held it against her chest the way you hold a baby.

And then Ida looked up, found me in the crowd, and pointed one crooked finger directly at my chest.

"You," she called, in a voice that had once commanded a kitchen full of harvest workers and could still cut through a tent full of murmuring strangers. "And your girl. Come here."

*Your girl.* Emrys went pink to the ears. I felt my own face do something embarrassing. We threaded through the chairs together, close enough that our shoulders bumped, not holding hands — we hadn't managed that yet, not officially, not after everything — and stopped in front of my grandmother, who looked us both up and down with the appraising eye she'd once used on bruised fruit.

"The orchard is mine again," she said. "Which means it's a problem again." She thrust the deed at me, then snatched it back before I could take it. "No. You'll wrinkle it. Listen instead. Two hundred acres, forty years of neglect since Ruthie got sick, a press that hasn't run since before you were shaving, and a barn roof I can see daylight through from the kitchen window. I'm seventy-eight years old. I am not doing it."

"Grandmother—"

"I'm deputizing you." She jabbed the finger at me, then at Emrys. "Both of you. Wren, you know every tree on this land by its scar. And Emrys—" here her voice did something softer, though her expression didn't change a degree, "—Ruthie's recipes came home today because of you. That's not a debt I pay with a thank-you. That's a debt I pay with work. You'll both take your share."

Emrys opened her mouth. I watched her do the thing she does — the quick arithmetic behind her eyes, the instinct to calculate cost before commitment, the reflex of a woman who'd learned that everything given has a price tag hidden somewhere. I watched her catch herself doing it, too. Watched her close the ledger in her head.

"I don't know anything about orchards," she said.

"You know dough," Ida said. "Dough is patience. Trees are patience with worse weather. You'll learn."

"And the café—" Emrys glanced at me. We hadn't finished that conversation either, the one about the partnership, the one we'd started in her shuttered café surrounded by boxes while I apologized for keeping secrets and she handed me a torn piece of my mother's handwriting like it was a heart she'd been keeping safe. "The lease situation is... complicated."

"Complicated things get uncomplicated by people who show up," Ida said, with the finality of a woman closing an oven door. Then her voice cracked, just slightly, just enough. "Ruthie would have wanted this place full again. Full of people. Full of noise. Full of—" she waved a hand at the two of us, at whatever it was standing between us that neither of us had named yet, "—whatever this is. Now go on. Everyone's staring."

Everyone was staring. Half the tent was pretending not to, which is worse.

* * *

By late afternoon the tent was gone, the folding chairs stacked into a rented trailer, and Hollowmere Orchard belonged to itself again. The police tape around Aldous's study came down; the investigation would take months, the detective told us, fraud cases always do, but the auction was dead and the deed was iron. Neighbors drifted up the lane all afternoon anyway, drawn like bees to a bloom nobody had announced — Mrs. Okafor from the farm stand with a basket of late plums, the Hollis brothers who'd worked the press as teenagers, old Tom Verrill who remembered my mother's cider and hadn't tasted it in fifteen years and said so twice, tearfully, both times.

Ida set up card tables on the barn's good side, out of the leak, and put everyone to work. It wasn't a party. It was an inventory. She marched through the rows with a clipboard she'd apparently brought for exactly this occasion — of course she had; Ida Calloway did not attend the potential collapse of her family's legacy without a clipboard — calling out assessments while the rest of us hauled, counted, and catalogued.

"The Gravensteins are done for, these six. See how the trunks lean? Root rot. They come out in spring." Tap of the pencil. "But the Newton Pips—" she laid her palm flat against a trunk the way you'd touch a horse, "—the Newtons are sound. Forty more years in them, easy. Your mother thought so too. It's in the journal, 1998, March. Go read it if you don't believe me."

I believed her. I'd spent the last month living inside those journals, comparing handwriting, hunting signatures, learning my mother's shorthand for weather and grafting and grief. *The Newtons will outlive us all,* she'd written, the year before I was born. She'd been right about the trees. She'd been wrong about nothing else that mattered.

Emrys worked beside me all afternoon, and I tried not to make a study of it and failed completely. She hauled ladders like someone settling a personal grudge against gravity. She climbed higher than I liked and ignored every comment about it. When she found the old press shed, she went quiet for a long time, running her fingers along the screw mechanism my father had oiled every October, and then she turned to me with an expression I was starting to collect like pressed flowers — that look she got when something landed deeper than she wanted to admit.

"This could run," she said. "You know that, right? This whole setup. With new hydraulics on the screw, a proper wash line—" She caught herself, laughed, shook her head. "Listen to me. I've owned a café for four years and I've never once talked about equipment like it had a future. Only a past I was trying to outrun."

"What changed?"

She looked at the press. At the light coming through the shed door, gold and low, catching the dust. At me, briefly, and away again.

"I think I stopped counting," she said, and didn't explain, and didn't have to.

* * *

Dusk came the way it does in October — all at once, like a lamp switched off in a far room, the gold going to copper going to violet while you're mid-sentence. The neighbors had gone home with promises and borrowed tools. Ida had been driven back to the house by Mrs. Okafor over her loud objections, and the orchard settled into that particular evening silence that I had forgotten I knew: wind in ten thousand leaves, the creak of old branches, somewhere down the slope the mill pond slapping its bank.

We ended up walking the rows because neither of us suggested anything else and neither of us wanted to leave. Emrys had flour still on her forearm — she'd baked a batch of her tarts in Ida's kitchen that afternoon, using the last of the orchard's apples, feeding the story of the day back to us in pastry — and she walked with her hands in her jacket pockets and her shoulder occasionally brushing mine, and the whole length of the row between the oldest trees felt like a held breath.

My mother had planted this row. Journal entry, April 1987: *Put in the last of the Coxes today. Wren kicked the entire time, which I choose to interpret as enthusiasm.* I'd been a fetus with opinions. I told Emrys this, because I tell her things now, because that's the whole point of what we're building — the thing we nearly lost twice, once to his secrets and once to mine.

"She wrote about you before you were born?" Emrys said.

"She wrote about everything. Weather, grafts, debts, dreams. She wrote about the man she hoped I'd become." I ducked under a low branch, held it up for Emrys to pass. "I used to think the journals were evidence. Proof, ammunition, a weapon to point at Aldous. And they were. But mostly they were just... her, talking. All this time I thought I was reading a case file. I was reading letters."

"To whom?"

"To whoever picked them up next." I looked at her. "To you, mostly, it turns out."

She stopped walking. The row opened ahead of us into the clearing where the oldest tree stood — the Bramley, older than the orchard's records, older than Ida, the tree my mother had written about more than any other. In the last violet light it looked less like a tree than a monument, its trunk wide as a doorway, its crown holding the first stars.

"Wren." Her voice had that careful quality, the one she used when she was about to be honest at personal expense. "At the auction. When Aldous stood up and said I was hired — when he said it in front of all those people — part of me wanted to disappear. Part of me wanted to be exactly what he said, because then none of it was real, and nothing real could be taken away." She turned to face me fully. "But I stood up anyway. Do you know why?"

"Because you're braver than you let yourself be."

"Because I was tired," she said, and laughed, wet-eyed. "God, no — both. Both things. I stood up because I was tired of protecting myself from things I wanted." She took a breath. "I wanted to protect you. I wanted to protect the orchard. I wanted to stand in front of a room full of strangers and say *this is real, he is real, what we built is real*, even if it cost me everything I had left. And it turns out that's what wanting something actually feels like. Nobody warned me."

"Someone should write that down," I said. "In a journal, maybe. Somewhere a fool might find it later."

She laughed properly then, and the sound went up into the branches, and I swear one of the old trees creaked in answer.

I reached for her hand. Not the almost-touches of the bonfire, not the accidental brush of shoulders, not the charged near-miss at the station platform where she'd gotten off the train and rewritten both our lives. I reached for her hand the way you sign something. Officially. On the record.

Her fingers were cold from the evening air and they closed around mine like they'd been waiting for the paperwork.

"So," she said. "Deputy."

"That's Sheriff Calloway to you."

"Sheriff." She squeezed. "What's the first order of business?"

I looked down the row — forty trees marching into the dark, bare-limbed now, patient, holding next year inside themselves the way a held note holds the song. Spring planting. New grafts. A press to rebuild. A café lease to untangle, a partnership to draw up on the back of a napkin and then, knowing her, in triplicate with clauses. My mother's recipes coming back one journal page at a time. Ida's clipboard. The whole enormous, impossible, ripening future of it.

"Order of business," I said, "is we come back tomorrow. And the day after. And every day after that until this place is full again."

"Full of people," she said. "Full of noise."

"Full of whatever this is."

Above us, in the oldest tree, an apple we'd missed — one late straggler, high in the crown, holding on past all reason — let go in the dark. I heard it land soft in the grass, and Emrys heard it too, and we stood there hand in hand in the row my mother planted, listening to the orchard let go of one season so it could begin another.

"Tomorrow, then," she said.

"Tomorrow," I said. And for the first time in longer than I could remember, tomorrow sounded like a promise instead of a bill.
