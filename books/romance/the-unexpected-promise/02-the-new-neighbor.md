## 2. The New Neighbor

The truck arrived on a Tuesday, which Zephyrine would later think was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days nothing ever happened at Thornfield.

She was in the barn office—her father's old office, though she'd long since stopped calling it that—with a cup of coffee gone cold beside her and the ledger open to the page she visited too often, the one where the red numbers lived. She'd been staring at them with the particular hopelessness of a woman doing arithmetic she already knew the answer to when the sound came: diesel engine, air brakes, the low groan of something heavy negotiating the gravel turnoff onto Hollis Lane.

She looked up out of habit more than interest. The Hollis cottage had stood empty for eleven years—since old Mrs. Hollis died and her nephews, who lived in Sacramento and had no use for a drafty two-bedroom with good light and bad plumbing, put it on the market at a price nobody in the valley could justify. It had sat there through her whole tenure of Thornfield, its windows going gray with dust, its garden reverting to wild fennel and mustard weed, a ghost house she passed every morning on her walk and no longer saw.

Now there was a moving truck backed up nearly to its porch, and two men were lowering a sofa onto the gravel, and a third figure—a man, tall, dark-haired, sleeves rolled—was standing in the middle of it all directing traffic with his hands.

Zephyrine watched for perhaps thirty seconds longer than curiosity strictly required, then made herself look back down at the ledger. New neighbors meant new complications. New neighbors meant someone eventually knocking on her door asking about fence lines and water rights and whether her dogs—she had no dogs—barked at night. She had spent ten years cultivating a life with the exact surface area required for one person, and she did not intend to expand it because someone had bought a cottage with good light and bad plumbing.

Still. When she walked the rows that evening, she found herself taking the long way back along the fence line, past the gap in the oleander where you could see the Hollis place clearly. The truck was gone. Lights burned warm in windows that hadn't held light in a decade. A bicycle leaned against the porch rail. Someone had already hung a wind chime—a small bronze thing that caught the last of the sun—and the sight of it did something strange to her chest, some ache she couldn't name, as if the cottage had been holding its breath all these years and had finally, quietly, exhaled.

*Someone lives there now,* she thought. *Someone chose this valley.*

She went home and wrote half a letter to W before deciding it was too early in the month and putting it away unfinished, which she never did, and lay awake listening to a wind chime she couldn't actually hear from her bedroom but heard anyway.

* * *

He came on Thursday, bearing bread.

She was re-stapling netting over the young Chardonnay block when a shadow fell across the row, and she turned with the staple gun still in her hand—it occurred to her later that she must have looked like she was defending the vines—and found him standing at the end of it with a cloth-wrapped loaf held out like an offering.

"Hi," he said. "I'm Callum. I'm next door—the Hollis place." He said it the way people say such things in wine country, with a small self-deprecating tilt, acknowledging that everyone within five miles already knew about the sale and had opinions. "I've been told by three separate people at the feed store that I'm legally required to bring you bread."

"Are you," said Zephyrine.

"It's a county ordinance, apparently." He unwrapped the loaf as evidence: a dark, dense sourdough, the crust split and floury. "Baked it myself. If it's terrible, we can agree the ordinance has been satisfied and never speak of it again."

It was not terrible. She broke off a piece right there in the row, because refusing seemed like more effort than accepting, and the crumb was open and faintly sweet, the crust singing faintly when she tore it. She chewed, and considered, and hated how much she wanted to say something generous.

"It's good," she admitted.

His whole face changed when he smiled—not just the mouth but around the eyes, a quick brightening like sun coming through cloud—and she noticed, in the way one notices things one doesn't want to have noticed, that he had a small white scar through his left eyebrow and that he watched people the way she watched weather. Not staring. Tracking.

"Callum," he said again, and offered his hand.

"Zephyrine."

"Zephyrine," he repeated, and something flickered across his face—there and gone so fast she might have invented it—before he said, "That's a name with a story."

"My mother read too much poetry."

"And your father let her get away with it."

"He encouraged her." She surprised herself with the answer; she hadn't spoken about either of her parents to a stranger in years, and here she was handing out family history with the bread crumbs. "He named the vineyard blocks after her favorite poems. The north block is 'Ozymandias.' He thought it was funny. Look on my works, ye mighty."

Callum laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and then went quiet in a way that struck her as odd, a beat too long, his gaze moving past her shoulder toward the north block as if checking. As if he already knew which block was which.

"You know the vineyard," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've read about it." He said it easily enough, but his eyes came back to hers a half-second late. "Thornfield's got a reputation among people who care about old-vine stuff. Your Chardonnay plantings are older than most of the wineries around here."

"We don't make wine anymore. We grow fruit. Sell to bigger houses." This was the speech she gave everyone, the tidy summary, the fence. "The winery closed before I took over."

"Before?"

"A while ago." She handed him back the loaf, deliberately ending the subject, and was annoyed to find herself adding, "My father ran it. Then things went badly. Then he died. Then it was me."

The plainness of it seemed to land on him harder than grief usually landed on people. He looked down at the bread in his hands and worked the cloth between his fingers.

"I'm sorry," he said, and it sounded less like politeness than anything anyone had said to her in years. "That's—that's a lot of weight for one person."

"It's the usual amount. It just doesn't come distributed."

* * *

He kept coming back. That was the thing she couldn't account for.

Not intrusively—he seemed to possess an instinct for exactly how much presence a person could bear, and he never exceeded it—but steadily. A Saturday morning with a box of peaches ("the orchard stand on Route 12 was giving them away, which was a lie, they cost four dollars"). A Tuesday evening appearing at the fence line with two bottles of beer and no agenda, drinking his share in companionable silence while she hosed down equipment, saying almost nothing until suddenly saying something that mattered. He asked questions the way other people gave gifts: wrapped carefully, timed well, never more than you could carry.

And the questions themselves—

"Who taught you to prune?" he asked, one cold bright morning, watching her work with the secateurs.

"My father. And Ramón Delgado, who ran our crew for twenty years." She cut, twisted, dropped the cane. "Everyone else scattered after the winery closed. Ramón stayed two more seasons, until his knees quit. Best hands in the valley."

"What happened to the crew after that?"

"Hired day labor, mostly. Smaller crews. You do what the budget allows." She glanced at him. "Why do you ask?"

"I'm interested in how places survive." He said it simply, without apology. "The big houses, the famous ones—they survive on money. Places like this survive on memory. Somebody has to remember how everything was done, or the knowledge just... goes out with the tide."

"That's very poetic for a man I met four days ago."

"I bake bread. Poetry is a coping mechanism."

But it wasn't only charm. That was what unsettled her. On Friday he asked, casually, leaning on her gate: "Was the tasting room the original building, or is that newer?" And when she said original, 1948, her grandfather built it, he nodded and said, "I thought the stone looked older than the rest," which was true, which was precisely, specifically true, the kind of detail you got from standing in front of the building or from reading something written by someone who had.

On Sunday: "Did the fire in '09 reach this far north? I've seen the burn maps and Thornfield sits right on the edge of the old perimeter."

"How do you know there was a fire in '09?"

"Valley history. Everyone talks about it."

Nobody talked about it. Nobody under sixty talked about it. She had lived here eleven years and had mentioned it to exactly two people, both of whom had lived through it.

She began, without meaning to, to test him. She'd mention a name—Ramón, or the Hollises, or old man Petrucci whose walnuts bordered her east field—and watch his face. He never flinched, never scrambled. But sometimes there was that half-second delay, that fractional pause, like a man translating from a language he pretended not to speak.

"You're very curious about this place," she said to him once, at the fence line, beer bottle sweating in her hand.

"I'm curious about everything." He smiled. "Occupational hazard of being new."

"Where did you move from, exactly? You've managed to be vague about it twice now."

"Have I?" He looked genuinely amused. "Seattle. Before that, a lot of places. My father moved around for work."

"What work was that?"

Something crossed his face then—quick, deep, gone. Like a fish turning under clear water: you saw the shadow, not the shape.

"Viticulture," he said. "Management side. Estate operations."

Which was, she thought later, the most precise non-answer she had ever received. Not a lie. Worse than a lie—an answer shaped exactly to fit the hole it was plugging.

* * *

Marisol came for their standing Sunday dinner—Zephyrine cooked, Marisol criticized, this was the ancient covenant—and caught Zephyrine looking out the kitchen window toward the Hollis cottage for the third time in an hour.

"So," said Marisol.

"So what."

"The new neighbor. Tall. Dark hair. Brought you bread, according to Dora at the post office, who knows everything, including things that haven't happened yet."

"He brought bread because of a fake ordinance."

"Mm." Marisol ladled soup with the serenity of a woman laying a trap. "And the peaches. And the beers at the fence line. And whatever it is you're doing with your face right now, which appears to be trying not to smile at a cottage."

"I am not smiling at a cottage."

"You're smiling adjacent to a cottage." Marisol set down the ladle and studied her with the frank, unembarrassed attention that was simultaneously the best and worst thing about her. "Zeph. When's the last time you talked to a man who wasn't delivering something or fixing something?"

"I talk to the co-op rep."

"The co-op rep is seventy-three and calls you 'young lady.'"

"He's being respectful."

"He's being ancient." Marisol sat, folded her hands. "Look. I'm not going to make a production. I'm just going to say one thing and then we'll eat soup and never discuss it again unless I decide otherwise. One thing: you lit up today. Three times. I counted. You don't light up. You glow at approximately the intensity of a dying flashlight battery, and today you were a whole fixture."

Zephyrine stirred her soup. "He asks a lot of questions."

"People who like you ask questions."

"No—" She tried to find the shape of the unease. "People who like you ask questions about *you*. He asks about the vineyard. About the history. About my father, about the crew, about the fire, about the buildings. Like he's—" She stopped.

"Like he's what?"

"Like he's studying for something," she said slowly. "Like there's an exam."

Marisol considered this. "Or," she offered, "like he cares about the thing you care about, which is the only door you leave unlocked, so naturally that's where he knocked."

Zephyrine didn't answer. Outside, the evening had gone violet, and somewhere beyond the oleander a wind chime turned over in a breeze she couldn't feel.

* * *

That night she took out W's latest letter—arrived the first of the month, as always, ten years unbroken—and reread it by lamplight, an old ritual, a hand on the shoulder.

*You asked once whether I thought places remember us,* W had written, in that careful, slanted hand she knew better than her own. *I think they must. I think a vineyard keeps every pair of hands that ever worked it, the way wood keeps the smell of smoke. Whatever happens to Thornfield, it isn't starting over. It's continuing. Whoever understands that will be worthy of it.*

She read the line three times. Her eyes kept snagging on it.

Because that afternoon, at the fence line, beer bottle in hand, Callum Reyes—or Callum whoever-he-was, surname still conveniently vague—had looked out over her struggling, beautiful, debt-ridden rows and said, almost gently, as if to himself:

"This place isn't starting over. It's continuing."

She had felt it then, too—a prick, a hook, something familiar in a way that had no business being familiar. She'd told herself it was coincidence, the way strangers arrive in wine country already fluent in its scripture. She told herself lots of things. She was very good at telling herself things; she'd had a decade of practice.

But lying in the dark with the letter on her nightstand and the wind chime turning somewhere in the night, Zephyrine Thornbury allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to wonder.

Then she rolled over, and decided it was nothing, and slept badly anyway.
