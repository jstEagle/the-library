## 6. Bud Break

Zephyrine felt it before she saw it—the way you feel a change in the weather, or someone watching you from across a room. She was halfway down the third row of the south block with her coffee going cold in one hand when she stopped, crouched, and pulled back a curl of frost-killed leaf litter.

Green.

A shoot no longer than her thumb, pale as new money, pushing up out of the old wood like it had somewhere important to be.

"Oh," she said, to nobody, to everybody, to the vine itself. "Oh, there you are."

She should have been expecting it. Bud break always came in the second week of April, give or take a frost, and she'd been walking these rows twice a day for a week pretending she wasn't checking. But knowing a thing was coming and standing in front of it were different animals entirely. Ten years ago, bud break had meant her father's voice carrying down the rows—*Zephyrine, come look, come look, they're early this year*—and his hand rough and warm on the back of her neck as they bent together over the first green of spring. Five years ago it had meant arithmetic: enough shoots to justify another season, or not enough. This morning it meant only this: the vines had decided to try again, and so, apparently, would she.

She walked the rest of the row fast, coffee abandoned on a post, touching each vine's crown like a midwife counting heartbeats. South block: green, green, green, a gap where the fourth vine had died two winters ago—green again by the sixth. North block, the old block, the one her grandfather had planted: slower, grumpier, but waking. By the time she reached the end of the last row her knees were wet through and she was laughing at nothing, which was how Marisol found her.

Marisol Delgado ran the tasting room three miles down the valley road and had been Zephyrine's best friend since the winter they'd both been twenty-three and broke, sharing a futon and a bottle of something criminal labeled *Chardonnay-Style Beverage*. She arrived at Thornfield most mornings under the pretense of "checking on things," which mostly meant drinking Zephyrine's coffee and offering opinions.

"You're laughing," Marisol said from the head of the row, arms crossed, suspicious. "It's seven in the morning and you're alone in a field, laughing. Should I call someone?"

"Bud break."

Marisol's whole face changed. She came down the row in her town boots without a thought for the mud, crouched where Zephyrine pointed, and went quiet in the way that people who weren't raised in vineyards never quite managed. "Look at that. Little idiots. Don't they know it could still freeze?"

"They know." Zephyrine shrugged. "They come anyway."

"Like some people I could mention." Marisol stood, dusted her knees, and gave her a look that Zephyrine pretended not to interpret. "So we're celebrating. Tonight. Properly. I'll bring the good bottle, the one you've been hoarding since Christmas, don't argue."

"There's a crew to pay and a sprayer that sounds like it's dying—"

"Tonight," Marisol said, in the tone of a woman who had once talked a health inspector out of a citation using nothing but eye contact and flan. Then, softer, bumping her shoulder: "Ten years ago this week, Zeph. You know it is. Let them be early this year too."

Zephyrine looked down the rows instead of answering. Ten years ago this week, her father had been alive, and the valley had been green, and she had not yet learned that a person could lose everything between one bud break and the next.

"Tonight," she said finally. "But I'm opening my own bottle. The hoarded one stays hoarded."

"That's the spirit. Hoard away. Grief and wine both keep."

* * *

The crew came at eight, and Zephyrine stood on the porch step and told them, and the cheer went up the way it does among people who work outdoors for a living—loud, brief, immediately followed by practical questions about frost cloth. But there was something underneath it this year that hadn't been there before. They knew what Thornfield had been. Some of them remembered her father. And they knew, in the way small crews always know, how close the place had come to going under more than once.

"We should do it right," said Tomás, who'd been pruning for her since her second spring and treated the vines with the tenderness of a man who talked to his truck. "Old way. When Mr. Thornbury hit bud break, he'd walk every block himself and then he'd buy the crew breakfast. Eggs and those terrible sausages from the gas station."

"The sausages were terrible," agreed Rosa, who had picked for the Thornburys since before Zephyrine was born and considered this fact a professional credential.

"He'd stand right here," Tomás said, tapping the porch rail, "and he'd say—" and then he stopped, because everyone realized at once that Tomás had been about to imitate a dead man in front of his daughter, and the morning went briefly, horribly still.

But Zephyrine heard the words arrive anyway, unbidden, in her father's voice, clear as if he were standing on the step behind her: *Well, they've done their part. Now we do ours.*

"Eggs and terrible sausages," she said. Her voice held. Mostly. "On me. And after lunch, we walk every block together, all of us, and count what we've got. That's the old way. We're doing the old way."

Rosa smiled with all her teeth. Tomás whooped. And Callum Reyes—who had been standing at the edge of the group for three weeks now, present but peripheral, a man who seemed to have made a study of exactly how much space to occupy—smiled too, and Zephyrine caught herself cataloguing the smile before she could stop: warm, a little crooked, arriving a half-second late as though it had to be approved by some internal committee first.

He'd been at Thornfield nearly a month. He pruned well—better than well; his cuts were clean and confident in a way that had made Tomás narrow his eyes and ask where a man from Hollis cottage had learned to work wood like that, and Callum had said *my father managed an orchard, growing up*, which was true in the way that a keyhole is true. He asked good questions. He listened like listening was the task at hand rather than a pause before speaking. And he was easy to be near, which was the dangerous part, the part Zephyrine kept having to remind herself to be careful about. Easy was not the same as known.

"Breakfast run?" Callum said now, already pulling keys from his pocket. "Tell me the terrible sausage situation and I'll handle it."

"You haven't earned the sausages yet," Rosa told him severely. "You have to suffer them first. It's tradition."

"I love tradition," Callum said gravely. "I'll suffer anything."

And Zephyrine laughed—really laughed, out loud, in front of everyone—and caught Marisol across the yard wearing the expression of a woman filing evidence.

* * *

The celebration that evening was small and perfect. Marisol brought a bottle from her own cellar instead, overruling all objections, and they ate on the winery's back steps while the light went long and gold over the valley. Tomás told the story of the flood year, when half the crew had bailed water out of the lower blocks with five-gallon buckets and a borrowed pump that quit every eleven minutes. Rosa corrected the details. Marisol got Zephyrine to tell the story of the night she'd slept in the cellar among the barrels because the house felt too big, and Zephyrine told it lightly, the way she'd learned to, and noticed Callum listening with his forearms on his knees and his whole attention aimed at her like sunlight through a lens.

"You should write these down," he said, when the stories wound down. "All of it. Somebody should."

"Somebody did," Zephyrine said, before she could think better of it. "Somebody—there are letters. A friend. We've written for years. He knows all the old stories better than I do, probably."

The word *he* slipped out and she watched Marisol's eyebrows perform an entire silent aria.

"A friend," Marisol repeated.

"A correspondent," Zephyrine amended, too late.

"Ten years," Marisol said, to the group, delighted, merciless. "Ten years of letters and none of you knew. This woman tells me everything about her soil pH and I find out about a ten-year epistolary romance at a bud break party."

"It's not a romance. It's—" Zephyrine gestured vaguely at the darkening hills, at everything she couldn't explain. "It's the one place I say true things."

Callum said nothing at all. But he turned his bottle slowly in his hands, label to the light and back, and when he spoke his voice was careful. "Does your friend know? About the vineyard now. The harvest coming."

"He knows everything. That's the arrangement. Everything, and no names, no faces." She heard how it sounded even as she said it—a confession dressed as a policy—and hurried past it. "More wine. Someone refill the wine."

Later, when the others had gone and Marisol was stacking glasses with elaborate innocence, Zephyrine stood alone on the steps looking at the black bulk of the north block and let herself feel the hope properly for the first time all day. Green shoots. A full crew. A harvest coming that might—if the weather held, if the market held, if the bank held—actually be hers.

*If the bank held.* The thought arrived on schedule, punctual as a bill. There was a payment due in nine days. Not the big one—that reckoning waited until summer—but a working-capital installment that would empty the account she used for payroll, and the letter from First Valley Savings sat in the drawer where she kept all such letters, face-down, a habit she'd developed sometime around the third year and never broken.

She had a plan. She always had a plan. Sell the truck, maybe. Take the advance Marisol kept offering against the tasting room partnership she kept refusing. Small safe steps. Enough. It had been enough for ten years.

She went inside and did not look at the drawer.

* * *

The call came four days later, on a gray Thursday, while Zephyrine was thinning shoots in the south block with sap-stained fingers and a bandana gone dark at the hairline.

"Ms. Thornbury? This is Denise Okafor at First Valley Savings." A pause with paperwork in it. "I'm calling about your April installment. I show it as—resolved. Paid in full this morning, by wire."

Zephyrine stood very still among the green rows. "By wire."

"From the Hollis Trust. Same as your February deferral fee, actually, and the insurance gap coverage last fall—I only just matched the accounts myself, honestly, our system flags—" Denise's voice did the thing voices do when a person realizes mid-sentence that they may have said too much. "In any case. You're current. Congratulations on the season."

Zephyrine thanked her in a voice she hoped sounded normal and hung up and stood in the row with her phone in her hand and her heart doing something complicated.

The Hollis Trust.

She knew that name. Of course she knew it. For ten years, help had arrived at Thornfield wearing that name like a coat: the anonymous grant the winter the well pump died. The scholarship fund that had quietly covered Rosa's granddaughter's tuition. The loan guarantee, three years back, that had appeared out of nowhere and vanished just as fast when she'd tried to trace it. Every time, the same signature. Every time, the same dead end—the trust's administrator, a polite law office two counties over, declining to disclose its benefactor. *The donor wishes to remain anonymous,* they always said. *The donor asks only that Thornfield continue.*

She had built a small shrine of theories over the decade. A former employee her father had helped. A rival grower with a guilty conscience. An eccentric widow. Once, drunk on New Year's Eve and lonelier than she'd admit, she'd let herself imagine it was W—her letter-writer, her paper ghost, who knew every number in her life because she'd told him, and who had money she'd never asked about and never explained. She'd dismissed the thought by morning. It was too much like a story, and her life had taught her the difference between stories and the plain hard machinery of luck.

But now the name sat in her phone call log, attached to a date, attached to a sum, and the plain machinery had a shape she couldn't unsee.

She walked the rows without working them. Green shoots everywhere now, the whole vineyard fuzzed with new growth, the air smelling of sap and cut grass and rain that hadn't fallen yet. Beautiful. Fragile. One late frost and every shoot on every vine would blacken overnight; every grower in the valley lived with that knowledge folded into every hopeful morning. Hope in a vineyard was never uncomplicated. Maybe that was why the thought came so easily:

Someone was keeping Thornfield alive on purpose. Had been, for years. Patiently, anonymously, at intervals timed—she was almost sure now, walking faster—to land precisely when she'd have lost otherwise. February. Last fall. Today.

Who did that? Who watched a stranger's vineyard for ten years and paid its bills from the shadows?

Her phone buzzed. A text from Marisol: *Lunch tomorrow? Also WHO is the Hollis Trust asking around the valley about you?? Mrs. Peña says a lawyer called the co-op.*

Zephyrine stared at it. Asked around. Asking around.

She typed back: *Tomorrow. Yes. And I think I might finally know who W is.*

She didn't know why she'd written that. It wasn't true—or it wasn't yet—but her thumb had sent it before her mind caught up, and standing there in the young green rows with the Hollis Trust humming in her recent calls, she realized the two threads had tied themselves together somewhere in the back of her mind without her permission. The letters. The money. The same patient, invisible devotion.

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor, Callum came around the end of the row with the post-hole digger on his shoulder, saw her face, and stopped.

"You okay?"

"Fine." She made herself smile. "Bud break's holding. No frost in the forecast."

"Good." He shifted the digger, hesitated. "You sure? You've got your thinking face on."

"My what?"

"The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic on somebody." He said it lightly, easily, a joke between coworkers—and then, for half a heartbeat, something moved behind his eyes. Something that looked, absurdly, like a man waiting for a verdict.

"Just counting shoots," Zephyrine said.

He nodded and went on toward the fence line, and she watched him go—all that easy competence, that careful warmth, that uncanny habit of being exactly where help was needed—and told herself she was being ridiculous. Told herself the timing was coincidence, that the world contained generous strangers, that ten years of mystery didn't resolve into the man who'd shown up a month ago with bread and unsettlingly good questions.

Told herself. Twice.

It didn't take.
