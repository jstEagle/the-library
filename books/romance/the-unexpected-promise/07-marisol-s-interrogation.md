## 7. Marisol's Interrogation

Marisol arrived on a Thursday evening with two bottles of her own wine, a wheel of cheese she'd "liberated" from the co-op, and the expression of a woman who had been waiting all week for a fight.

"Sit," she said, before Zephyrine could even offer her a chair. She set the bottles on the kitchen table with a decisive clink. "Not there. Here. Where I can see your face."

"I always sit where I can see your face."

"Not tonight you don't. Tonight you'd sit by the window so you could look out at the vineyard and pretend to listen while actually composing letters in your head." Marisol pulled out a chair and pointed at it. "Here. Facing me."

Zephyrine sat. It was easier than arguing, and besides, there was no use pretending she didn't know what this was about. Marisol had been circling for days like a hawk that had spotted something small and foolish in the grass.

The kitchen at Thornfield was the warmest room in the house—had been since her father's time, when it smelled perpetually of coffee and crushed grape skins and the pipe smoke he swore he'd given up twice a year. Now it was just Zephyrine and the hum of the old refrigerator and, lately, a new awareness of the cottage next door, its windows lit gold against the darkening hills.

Marisol opened the first bottle without ceremony. It was one of hers—a Tempranillo from her family's little operation two valleys over, rough-edged and honest, the kind of wine that didn't pretend to be anything it wasn't. She poured two glasses and pushed one across the table.

"Now," she said. "Tell me about Callum."

"There's nothing to tell."

"Mm." Marisol drank. "Interesting. Because when I said his name just now, you did a thing with your mouth."

"I did not do a thing with my—"

"You did. A very small thing. Like the word tasted interesting." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, cheese forgotten. "Zeph. I've known you since we were nineteen and you cried into my hair because a boy in your lit seminar said *Wuthering Heights* was overrated. I know every face you make. That was a face."

Zephyrine wrapped both hands around her glass, mostly for something to hold. Outside, the last light was going amber over the north block, and somewhere down the rows Callum was probably still working—he seemed to work until the light was fully gone, then a little past it, as if darkness were a personal insult.

"He's a good worker," she said. "That's all."

"A good worker."

"Yes."

"The kind of good worker who makes you do a thing with your mouth."

"*Marisol.*"

"What? I'm asking a serious viticultural question." But her eyes weren't joking. They were warm and steady and slightly merciless, the way they'd been since college, since the night Zephyrine's father died and Marisol drove four hours through rain without being asked, without saying much of anything, just sat with her through the worst hours of her life eating cold takeout on the cellar floor.

That was the trouble with old friends. They knew where the soft places were.

"He asks questions," Zephyrine admitted finally. "About the vineyard. About my father. And he listens like—" She stopped, restarted. "Like the answers matter. Most people ask about the vineyard to be polite. He asks like he's trying to understand something."

"Like he's trying to understand *you*."

"The vineyard is me. Same thing."

"No," Marisol said gently, "it isn't. That's exactly the problem, and we'll get there in a minute. First: the blushing."

"I don't blush."

"You blushed Tuesday. In the barn. He handed you the pruning shears handle-first instead of blade-first, which frankly any farmhand should know, and you went pink to the ears and thanked him like he'd handed you a bouquet."

"It was courteous."

"It was *handle-first*, Zeph. It was basic competence. You thanked him like he'd written you a sonnet."

Zephyrine drank her wine to buy time. The truth—which she had been carefully not examining for weeks now, the way you don't examine a suspicious vine until the whole row is infected—was that she had started noticing things about Callum Reyes that had nothing to do with pruning. The way he rolled his sleeves. The fact that he laughed more easily than anyone she'd spent time with in years, and that his laugh made her feel like someone she used to be. The way she caught herself, some mornings, walking a little slower toward the rows because it meant a few more minutes of talking with him before the work silenced them both.

She had also noticed, with a smaller and sharper feeling, that she'd begun saving things up to tell him. Small observations, jokes, fragments of vineyard lore—and each time she saved one, she felt a flicker of guilt, because those same observations were the ones she would have put in a letter.

*Dear W. Today the light came through the fog in a way I wish you could have seen.*

*Dear W. There's a new man working the rows and he laughs like the world hasn't disappointed him yet, and I find myself telling him things I meant to tell you.*

She hadn't written that second one. But she'd thought it. And thinking it had felt like a small betrayal, though she couldn't have said of whom.

"Fine," she said aloud. "I like him. He's easy to be around. Is that a crime?"

"No. It's a beginning." Marisol refilled her glass. "Here's my question, and think carefully before you answer, because I've been rehearsing this all week and I have follow-ups prepared. When you talk to Callum—when you're standing in the cold at seven in the morning teaching him how to prune, and he says something that makes you laugh so hard you snort, which yes, you did, Lupe told me—"

"I did not snort."

"—when that happens, do you feel *seen*?"

Zephyrine opened her mouth. Closed it.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, not even Marisol, though Marisol knew about the letters in the vague way one knows a friend has a habit—one drink too many, a superstition, a cigarette she pretends she's quit. Marisol knew Zephyrine wrote monthly to someone called W and had done for ten years, and had long ago stopped asking questions about it, because the one time she'd asked, early on, Zephyrine had gone so still and so distant that Marisol had apparently decided some doors stay shut.

But Marisol didn't know what the letters *were*. What they had become. How, on the worst nights—the anniversary nights, the bank-call nights, the nights when the silence of the house pressed down like snowfall—W's replies were the only proof she had that she existed to anyone outside this valley.

And here was the other thing, the thing rising in her now like sediment shaken loose: when Callum listened to her, when his attention settled on her like sunlight, she felt seen. Truly seen. And it terrified her, and it thrilled her, and it felt—

It felt familiar.

"Oh," she said quietly.

"There it is," Marisol said.

"That's not fair. You can't just—"

"I absolutely can. I brought wine specifically so I could." Marisol's voice softened, but only a degree; she was a woman who believed kindness worked best at close range. "You feel seen by him. And you already feel seen by someone else, someone you've never met, someone safe. Someone who can't show up at your door, can't see the dust on your floors or the worry on your face or the way you talk to yourself when you think nobody's listening. Someone who gets the best version of you—the composed version, the version you write on purpose."

"That's not what the letters are."

"Then tell me what they are."

Zephyrine looked down at her glass. The wine was dark and still, holding the reflection of the ceiling light like a small red moon.

"They're..." She tried to assemble the answer she'd carried for ten years, the true and noble one. *They're a lifeline. They're the reason I survived. They're the one place I'm honest.* All of it true. And underneath it, newer and less noble, something she'd only recently begun to suspect: *they're also a wall with excellent views.*

"When my father died," she said slowly, "I stopped being a person people checked on. Everyone grieved him and then everyone went home, and I was left with the debts and the vines and this enormous quiet. And W wrote back. Every month, no matter what I sent—even the months I sent almost nothing, even the months I was cruel or flat or barely coherent. W answered. And I built my whole... my whole emotional life inside that exchange, because it was safe. W couldn't leave. W couldn't see me fail. W couldn't stand in my kitchen and notice that I hadn't repainted the hall even though I said I would, three years ago."

"You haven't repainted the hall?"

"Focus, Marisol."

"I'm multitasking. Go on."

"And now there's Callum." Zephyrine turned the glass a quarter-turn, watching the wine move. "And he sees me. Actually sees me, in person, in daylight, with dirt under my fingernails. And instead of it being wonderful, it's—I keep flinching. I keep catching myself performing. Giving him the letter version of me. The curated one. And then hating myself for it, and then going home and almost writing to W about him, which feels insane, like cheating on a ghost with a living man."

Marisol was quiet for a moment. Outside, a night bird called once and gave up.

"Do you know what I think?" Marisol said at last. "I think you fell in love with the letters because the letters couldn't hurt you. And I think you're falling for Callum because he might be able to. And I think part of you wants to run back to the paper man because paper doesn't require you to be brave."

"That's—"

"Wait. One more." Marisol held up a finger. "When was the last time you let anyone get this close? Before Callum. An actual human being, in the actual valley."

Zephyrine searched backward through the years. There were the crew, whom she paid well and kept kindly at arm's length. There were the neighbors, waved to across fences. There was Marisol herself, once a month, dinners like this one—but notice how Marisol had to bring an interrogation to get past the front door. Notice how many of these evenings had ended with Zephyrine tired in a way that had nothing to do with the day's work, tired of guarding.

"Ten years," she said finally. "Give or take."

"Ten years." Marisol said it without triumph, which somehow made it worse. "Ten years, and the one person who got through did it on paper. And now a flesh-and-blood man moves in next door and starts getting through in person, and you don't know what to do with that, because you built a whole architecture around being unreachable."

"I reached out to W. That counts for something."

"It counts for everything and nothing. You reached out to someone who could never reach *back*." Marisol leaned in. "Zeph. Sweetheart. Love of my platonic life. Do you hear yourself? You found the one form of intimacy that requires no risk, and you've been calling it survival for a decade. And maybe it started as survival! Maybe it genuinely saved you. But somewhere along the way it became a hiding place with very nice stationery."

Zephyrine wanted to argue. She assembled several arguments—about grief, about circumstance, about how easy it was for someone with a living family and a functioning winery to lecture the woman running a haunted estate on hope and credit. But the arguments wouldn't come out. What came out instead, after a long silence, was much smaller.

"I don't know how to be known," she said. "In person. I genuinely don't. I know how to be competent. I know how to be polite. I know how to write a paragraph that makes a stranger feel less alone. But if Callum—if he ever saw the whole thing, the debts and the fear and how much of my confidence is scaffolding—" Her voice thinned. "He'd realize I'm not the person he thinks I am."

"Or," Marisol said, "he'd realize you're a person. Which is the actual prerequisite for loving one."

Zephyrine laughed despite herself, a wet, surprised sound. "You've been rehearsing that one all week too."

"Since Tuesday. I nearly texted it to you and ruined everything." Marisol reached across the table and covered Zephyrine's hand with her own. Her palm was warm and rough, a farmer's hand. "Here's what I actually think, and then I'll let you drink in peace. I think you're falling for Callum. I think it scares you more than the bank does. And I think the letters—wonderful as they are, grateful as you'll always be—are partly the reason you're forty-one years old and flinch when a man remembers how you take your coffee."

"Thirty-nine."

"You're thirty-nine? God, we're ancient." Marisol drained her glass and stood, gathering the cheese and the second bottle, pressing the latter into Zephyrine's hands. "Keep that. Drink it the next time you catch yourself performing for him instead of just being there. And Zeph—"

"Mm?"

"Next time he says something that makes you want to write it down for W—don't save it for the page. Say it to his face. Practice. Consider it bud break."

She let herself out, and Zephyrine stood alone in the kitchen with the second bottle and the honest wreckage of the evening, listening to Marisol's truck grind away down the lane.

The house was quiet. The vineyard was quieter. And down the hill, faintly, she could see the gold rectangle of the Hollis cottage windows, and—she was almost sure—a figure moving behind them, and she stood there longer than she meant to, watching.

Later, at her desk, she took out her current letter to W—half-written, three weeks stale—and read it with new eyes, Marisol's eyes. It was a good letter. Warm, wry, carefully shaped. The best version of her, arranged like flowers in a vase.

*There is a man working the harvest with me now,* she had written. *He is steady and strange and he asks the right questions. I will tell you more when I understand it myself.*

She looked at that sentence for a long time. Then she picked up her pen, crossed it out, and beneath it wrote the thing she had never once put in ten years of letters:

*I am afraid, W. Not of the vineyard. Of being known. Isn't that ridiculous? Ten years of writing to you and I've never said it plainly. I hide inside these pages and call it honesty. I think I've been using you as a shelter, and I'm sorry, and I don't know how to stop.*

She signed it, sealed it, and set it by the door for morning pickup—then sat for a while afterward in the lamplight, feeling strangely lighter and strangely exposed, like a vine after pruning: cut back hard, bleeding a little sap, ready for whatever spring intended.

She was still awake at midnight when she heard, through the open window, a car start down at the Hollis cottage—engine turning over far later than any farmhand's bedtime—and headlights sweep slow past her gate and down the valley road, going somewhere in the dark that a man with nothing to hide, she thought, had no particular reason to be going.
