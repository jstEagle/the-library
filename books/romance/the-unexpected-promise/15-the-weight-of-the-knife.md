## 15. The Weight of the Knife

The cottage had never really been his. That was the first honest thing Callum Reyes admitted to himself in three days, and it came to him not as a revelation but as a physical fact: the walls were Hollis's walls, the floors were Hollis's floors, and every box he taped shut felt less like packing and more like returning something borrowed.

He'd been careful about that, once. When he'd moved in, he'd kept his boxes few and his footprint light, as if he might be asked at any moment to prove he belonged here. Now, standing in the middle of the small front room with a roll of packing tape in one hand and a half-empty coffee mug going cold on the windowsill, he understood that the caution had never been about the cottage at all.

He'd been living lightly inside his own life for ten years.

* * *

He'd given himself until Friday. The bank's letter had come Tuesday—Zephyrine wouldn't see hers until Thursday, maybe Friday, depending on how long it sat unopened in the office she avoided—and Callum had done the arithmetic twice, then a third time with a pencil because he didn't trust what he wanted the numbers to say.

Without the anonymous transfers, without the quiet wire that had bought her through bud break, Thornfield would miss its deadline by eleven days. Eleven days. He could have covered it. One more transfer, one more lie stacked neatly on top of the others like crates in a cellar.

But she'd walked away from him at the fence line without a word, and her silence had done what no accusation could: it had made him finally hear himself.

*You've been managing her,* he'd thought, sitting in his truck afterward with the engine ticking and his hands shaking on the wheel. *Ten years of managing her. Keeping her alive the way you keep a fire going—not because you trust it to burn, but because you can't stand the dark.*

So: Friday. He would be gone by Friday, and before he went, he would do the one thing he'd spent a decade failing to do.

He would tell the truth. All of it. In writing, because God knew he couldn't be trusted to say it out loud—he'd rehearsed a hundred confessions on the drive over from Hollis cottage, in the shower, in the dark rows between vines, and every single time his courage had folded like wet cardboard.

A letter, then. The last one. Signed this time.

* * *

The packing went slowly, which was its own kind of confession.

He found the letters on Wednesday morning, in the second box from the bottom of the bedroom closet—the box he'd told himself he'd leave sealed. Ten years of Zephyrine's handwriting, bundled in rubber bands by year, soft at the edges from handling. He hadn't reread them since moving in. He hadn't needed to; he knew them the way he knew the layout of his own hands.

But he sat down on the floor anyway, back against the bed frame, and untied the earliest bundle.

*Dear W,* the first one began, dated eleven days after Eduardo Thornbury's funeral. *I don't know who you are or why you wrote, but I'm answering, because the alternative is talking to my father's photograph, and he was never much of a listener when he was alive.*

Callum remembered writing that first letter. He'd been twenty-four, hollowed out by his father's funeral—half the town had come, and half of those had come hoping to spit on the grave—and he'd sat in his mother's kitchen with a pen and a debt he couldn't calculate, because his father had stolen it all from the family whose grief was loudest that week.

*I'm sorry* had been the whole first draft. Three words, crossed out. He hadn't earned them yet. So instead he'd written something stranger—a note about the vineyard, about how he'd heard the old man used to talk to the vines in the mornings, and how somebody ought to keep doing it, and how he hoped whoever did knew they weren't alone in caring whether Thornfield lived or died.

And she'd written back. Of course she'd written back. She'd been so alone that even an anonymous stranger's concern had been enough to build a room around.

He read for two hours. He let himself. It was the last time, he figured, that he'd have any right to.

* * *

Marisol came by Thursday afternoon, ostensibly to return a casserole dish, actually to look him in the eye and assess the damage.

"So you're leaving." She stood in his doorway holding the dish like evidence.

"I'm leaving."

"She's not eating. I want you to know that. In case it helps you pack faster, or slower, or whatever it is you're angling for."

"Marisol—"

"No, you don't get to Marisol me." But there was no real venom in it; there never had been. She set the dish on his counter with exaggerated care. "I liked you, Callum. Which annoys me now, professionally. You made her laugh. Do you know how hard that woman is to make laugh? I've been working at it for fifteen years and you strolled in with bread and a decent jawline and just—did it."

"It wasn't—" He stopped. Started again. "Everything was a lie, Marisol. Not the laughing part. Not—" His voice caught on something. "Not most of it, actually. That's the worst part. Most of it was true."

She studied him for a long moment, arms crossed, head tilted, the way she looked at a glass of wine she suspected was corked but hadn't decided to condemn.

"You know," she said finally, "she keeps every one of your letters. Did you know that? In a tin under her bed. I found it once looking for a corkscrew. She nearly took my hand off."

"I know."

"And you're still leaving."

"I'm leaving *because* I know." He pressed his palms against the counter, against the solid wood of it, as if it might hold him up. "Every good thing between us, I built on ground I stole from her. If I stay and she takes me back, I'll never know if she forgave me or just got tired. And she'll never know either. That's not love, Marisol. That's another wire transfer."

Marisol was quiet for a moment. Then, at the door, without turning around:

"For what it's worth—and it isn't worth much today—she reads the letters on bad nights. The early ones. The ones where she sounds least like herself." A pause. "Fix that somehow. Or don't. But know that you're leaving the person she talks to when she can't sleep."

The door clicked shut behind her, and Callum stood alone in the borrowed kitchen and finally, finally picked up a pen.

* * *

He wrote badly at first. Four false starts went into the woodstove, each one too careful, too arranged, too much the work of a man still trying to control how the story landed. *My father was a thief* —true, but it sounded like an alibi. *I never meant* —he burned that one fastest, because it was the sentence of a coward and he was done being that, or wanted desperately to be.

It was nearly midnight when he gave up on structure entirely and simply began, the way he had at twenty-four, with the plainest thing he knew.

---

*Zephyrine,*

*You won't want to hear from me, and you especially won't want to hear from W, and I'm both, so consider this letter doubly unwelcome. Read it anyway. Please. It's the last one, and it's the only one I'll ever sign.*

*My name is Callum Reyes. My father was Tomás Reyes, and for nine years he was your father's vineyard manager, and for at least six of those years he was robbing your family blind. Skimmed deliveries. Ghost invoices. A second set of books so clean it took an auditor four months to find the first one. By the time it surfaced, the money was gone—spent, lost, scattered; I've spent ten years trying to trace it and mostly what I've traced is my father's talent for making nothing out of everything. Your father confronted him in the cellar office on a Tuesday in March. There was shouting. My father resigned that night and was dead of a heart attack within the year, and I have never known how much of that was guilt and how much was just a weak heart finding its excuse.*

*Your father died believing my family stole Thornfield from him. Because we did. I was twenty-three when the audit closed and twenty-four when he died, and I carried two things out of that wreckage: my mother's shame, which she wore like a coat she couldn't take off, and the knowledge that the girl next door—the one I'd watched grow up, the one who once spent a whole summer teaching me to tell pinot noir from pinot gris and laughed at me for getting it wrong—was left alone in a house full of debts my father dug.*

*So I wrote to you. The first letter was supposed to be an apology. It came out sideways, the way apologies do when you haven't earned them, and then you answered, and then—God help me—it became the truest thing in my life. Every month for ten years. Two jobs, Zeph. Days at a distributor in Santa Rosa, nights loading freight, and later the money from selling my mother's house after she passed, and every spare dollar wired to you through a shell of anonymity I maintained partly out of cowardice and partly—I see this clearly now—out of control. As long as you didn't know who I was, I got to decide what the story was. I got to be the hand in the dark instead of the son of the man who broke you. That wasn't only kindness. Don't let anyone tell you it was only kindness, including me.*

*Then I moved in next door knowing exactly who you were, and let you teach me to prune, and let you laugh at my jokes, and let myself fall in love with you in person while I was already in love with you on paper, and told myself every night that I'd confess soon. Soon. I have never in my life used a word more criminally.*

*Here is what I'm leaving you, and it isn't a gift, because gifts imply I have anything of my own to give. The deed to the Hollis cottage is in the envelope with this letter. It's mine outright—bought with the last of my mother's estate, no liens, no bank's fingers on it. Take it to Whitcombe. Use it as collateral for the loan. It should cover the gap with room to spare, and if it doesn't, sell it; it was never really mine anyway. Nothing here ever was.*

*I'm not asking you to forgive me. I want to be very clear about that, because I've spent ten years scripting your side of our correspondence along with my own, and I am done deciding things for you. Forgive me or don't. Hate me or don't. Keep the cottage or burn it. What I ask—what I beg—is only this: stop carrying the belief that you survived wrong. You survived. You kept your father's vines alive with your hands and your stubbornness and yes, with money you thought fell from nowhere. It didn't fall. It was owed. Every dollar was owed, and paying it back was the only honest thing my family ever managed, and I did it badly, secretly, and for the wrong reasons until loving you made it almost right.*

*You taught me that pinot tells the truth about where it grew. I think people are the same. This letter is where I grew. It's sour and it's late and it's yours.*

*—Callum Reyes*

*(W. Always, in every way that mattered, W.)*

---

He read it through twice by lamplight, hating pieces of it, unable to find a single lie in it, and decided that was the best he could do. The weight of the knife, his mother used to say—you hold it by the blade or you cut yourself either way. He'd held this one by the handle for ten years. Time to offer it up sharp edge first.

At dawn he drove to the main house, quiet as a thief in reverse, and left the envelope wedged in the office door where she couldn't miss it: letter, deed, and beneath them, flat and final, the brass key to the cottage.

Then he loaded his truck, and sat in the driver's seat with his hands at ten and two, and did not start the engine, because starting it would mean the story was over, and some cowardly remainder of him wanted to sit in the last chapter a while longer.

Through the windshield, across the fence line, the vines stood heavy with fruit, weeks from harvest, purple-black in the early light. Her harvest. Finally, truly hers.

He turned the key.

The engine caught, rough and reluctant, and Callum Reyes pulled out of Thornfield for the second time in his life—this time with nothing hidden in the truck but boxes, and nothing hidden in his chest at all, which hurt far more and weighed almost nothing.

Behind him, in the office doorway, an envelope waited with his whole life in it, and somewhere inside the house, a woman who hadn't slept was already awake.
