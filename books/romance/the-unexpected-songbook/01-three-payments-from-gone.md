## 1. Three Payments from Gone

The final notice arrived at 7:14 in the morning, folded into the same mail slot as a coupon for gutter cleaning and a postcard from a dentist Mirabel had never visited. She knew what it was before she opened it. The envelope had changed color three months ago — from white to yellow to this pale, apologetic pink, like the bank was embarrassed to keep saying it. *Final Notice: Commercial Loan Account 4471-B.* Three payments from gone.

She stood in the doorway of Songbird Café with the envelope in one hand and her keys in the other, watching the light come up over Halcyon Street the way it did every morning — slow, honeyed, indifferent. The café had been her mother's idea before it was ever hers. *A place where people linger,* Amara Okonkwo used to say, tapping the counter with two fingers like she was testing a drumhead. *Lingering is a lost art, Miri.* Ten years since she'd said anything at all. Ten years today.

Mirabel let herself in, disarmed the alarm that cost forty dollars a month and felt like a luxury, and put the kettle on. The espresso machine — secondhand, temperamental, named Bertha after no one in particular — groaned through its warm-up like an old woman climbing stairs. Outside, the street was doing its morning arithmetic: the bakery two doors down opening on time, the florist arranging her buckets, the empty storefront that had been a bookstore, then a candle shop, then nothing, its windows papered over with a listing that promised *great foot traffic.*

The foot traffic came, as it always did, but not enough of it, and not often enough, and never quite the kind that bought pastries instead of asking to use the bathroom.

She pinned the final notice to the corkboard behind the register, where its predecessors hung in their graduated colors — white, yellow, pink — a little weather system of doom. There were eleven other papers up there too: the utility reminder, the produce invoice, the letter from the landlord whose handwriting got friendlier as his patience got thinner. Mirabel looked at them the way you look at a scar. Not new. Just permanent-seeming.

Then she tied on her apron, because grief and debt both kept business hours, and opened the doors.

* * *

By four o'clock she had sold nine coffees, two scones, and one lemon tart to a man who ate it standing up and told her it reminded him of his late wife, which he seemed to mean as a compliment and which landed on Mirabel like a small, well-aimed stone.

Because today was the day for lemon tarts. Every year, on the tenth of September — though the date mattered less than the ritual — Mirabel baked her mother's lemon tarts. Amara's recipe, written in her slanted hand on the back of a setlist from some long-ago open mic: *zest first, always zest first, let the fruit tell you when it's ready.* Her mother had believed lemons had moods. She'd believed songs had them too, and debts, and daughters. She'd been right about most things except the contract she signed in 2015, the one Mirabel still kept in a shoebox under her bed, the one that sold her mother's entire song catalog — eleven finished songs, nine fragments, a lifetime of melodies — for a sum so insultingly small it could only have been a formality. A signature collected after the fact. Amara died six weeks later, and the royalties went somewhere else, and Mirabel stopped asking questions because asking questions required a kind of energy she didn't have.

But she baked the tarts. That she could do.

Tonight's batch went into the oven at seven, after close, when the café belonged to her again — to her and Bertha and the smell of butter shortening in the case. She zested four lemons while the radio played something orchestral and sad, and she talked to her mother the way she always did, low, half-embarrassed, stirring.

"Ten years," she said. "I still can't hear a piano without turning around."

The tarts needed twenty-five minutes. Mirabel gave them thirty-five, because she sat down on the flour-dusted floor behind the counter with her back against the cabinet and let herself cry the way she only let herself cry once a year — efficiently, furiously, with a timer running — and when she remembered the oven, the kitchen smelled like caramel gone wrong, like sugar crossed some bright line into bitterness. The tarts came out bronze-black, their custard blistered, their rims burned to lace.

She stood holding the tray with two dish towels and laughed, actually laughed, a wet cracked sound, because of course. Of course. Even her grief couldn't hold itself together anymore. Even the memorial was three payments from gone.

She scraped the tarts into the compost, all twelve of them, and washed the tray, and turned off the lights, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the refrigerator hum the note it always hummed — a B-flat, she'd decided, though she had no evidence — and locked the door behind her.

* * *

He was sitting on her front steps.

Mirabel saw him from half a block away and her body reacted before her mind caught up — a jolt, a heat, a reflex ten years old. Theo Ashford. She would have known the silhouette anywhere: too tall for his own comfort, shoulders slightly hunched like a man perpetually apologizing for the space he took up, hands wrapped around a paper cup he wasn't drinking from. He hadn't changed much. Broader maybe. The boyish softness edited out of his jaw. But he sat the way he'd sat in the library senior year, taking up as little room as possible, waiting to be noticed.

"Theo." She stopped three steps below him. "It's ten o'clock."

"I know. I'm sorry." He stood, and the streetlight caught his face, and she saw that whatever had brought him here had already been eating him for a while. "I called the café. Your machine said—"

"I don't check it."

"Right." A pause. His eyes went to the box under her arm — the shoebox, she realized; she'd brought it home to look at the contract tonight, as she did every tenth of September, as if this year the paper might finally confess something. He looked away quickly, politely. "You look good, Miri."

"No, I don't. I look like someone who burned twelve memorial tarts and cried on a kitchen floor. What do you want, Theo?"

He flinched. Good, some petty part of her thought. Then worse — he didn't deny there was something he wanted. In college, Theo Ashford had been many things — brilliant, awkward, generous in the specific way of rich kids who feel guilty about it — but he had never once asked her for anything. That had been the whole architecture of their friendship. She gave him honesty; he gave her distance dressed up as discretion. Then graduation, then silence, then ten years of reading about the Ashford family in the business section the way you read about weather in a country you'll never visit.

"I need a favor," he said. "An enormous one. And I need you to hear me out before you say no, because I think you're going to want to say no, and I understand why, and I'd say no too if our positions were—" He stopped himself. Breathed. "Can I buy you a coffee?"

"My café closed four hours ago and I'm not going back inside."

"Then can I stand here and ask you anyway?"

Mirabel looked at him. At the paper cup going soft in his grip. At the Ashford jaw clenched like a man about to sign something. Behind her, the dark windows of Songbird Café held their reflection — the two of them, ghosted against the glass, framed by the hand-painted letters of her mother's naming: *SONGBIRD,* with the little gold bird over the O that Amara had painted herself, the bird that had once been a joke about her own catalog, her own caged songs, before any of it meant anything else.

"Three minutes," Mirabel said. "And then I'm going upstairs to sleep, and tomorrow I'm going to figure out how to lose my café with dignity."

"That's the thing," Theo said quietly. "That's exactly the thing. I can fix that part. The café, the debt — all of it. That's the easy part of what I'm asking."

"The easy part." She climbed one step closer, arms crossing, the shoebox pressed between her forearm and her ribs like a shield. "Theo. It's ten o'clock on the anniversary of my mother's death, and I have burned tarts in the compost and a pink envelope on my corkboard, and you show up out of a decade of nowhere telling me you can make my problems disappear. People who say that want something. So tell me what you want, and tell me now, because my three minutes are burning."

He told her.

He talked fast, the way he used to talk right before exams — the family reunion at the vineyard, Ashford Hollow, three days of curated nostalgia; his grandmother Odette's ultimatum delivered over Sunday lunch with the whole board within earshot: *arrive with a serious partner or surrender your seat*; the seat he needed, the seat that was apparently the only vantage point from which he could see clearly into the machinery of the estate, into accounts his brother Marcus kept like a fiefdom, into a rights transfer signed in 2015 that Theo had found referenced in a drawer and could not stop thinking about.

"2015," Mirabel repeated. The word came out flat and strange, like a coin dropped on tile.

"You've heard something."

"I've heard nothing," she said, which was true, and also the least true sentence she'd spoken in years. Her thumb moved over the shoebox lid without her permission. "Go on."

He wanted her to come. As his girlfriend. As the serious partner, the plus-one, the proof of stability. He would cover her debts — no, he corrected himself immediately, seeing her face, he would *lend* her the money, terms in writing, interest fair, everything documented, because he knew how she felt about being given things. Three days at the vineyard. Two weeks of preparation. A backstory they'd rehearse until it bored them.

"And in exchange," Mirabel said slowly, "you want me to lie to your entire family."

"I want you to help me find out whether my family has something to lie *about*." His voice dropped. "Miri, I found a document reference I can't explain. My father signed it weeks before he died. Marcus won't answer questions. And I can't walk into that house alone — I can't walk in as the brother who left, the heir who wandered off, asking questions with no one beside me. I need someone I trust absolutely. There's exactly one person, and she's standing on these stairs looking at me like she's deciding whether to call the police."

Mirabel laughed — the wet cracked sound again, but different this time. Sharper. Grief does that, she thought. It doesn't soften you. It whittles you down until you're mostly edge.

Her mother's songs, sold for a formality. Her café, three payments from gone. A locked drawer in a vineyard estate with her mother's death year written on it, more or less, in the language of contracts.

If she said yes, she'd be lying. If she said no, she'd be lying too — lying to the ten-year-old grief that had spent a decade whispering *something was wrong with that contract, Miri, look again.*

"One condition," she heard herself say.

"Name it."

"When we get there — whatever we find, whoever it implicates — we follow it all the way. No protecting anyone. Not your brother. Not your grandmother. Not your father's memory." She lifted the shoebox slightly, just enough that he'd notice, just enough that he'd wonder. "I don't do partial truths anymore. They're expensive."

Theo looked at the box, then at her face, and something moved across his expression — recognition, or fear, or the beginning of understanding. "Deal," he said.

"Then I'll need the terms in writing," Mirabel Okonkwo said, and held out her free hand, and shook on it under the streetlight, on the anniversary, three payments from gone and one enormous lie from whatever came next.
