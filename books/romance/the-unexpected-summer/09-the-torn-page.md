## 9. The Torn Page

The storm had passed two days ago, and the vineyard still smelled of it — wet gravel, crushed green leaves, the mineral breath of the hills after rain. Wren had learned the smell the way she'd learned everything else here: by accident, by proximity, by the slow accumulation of mornings that were starting to feel less like a visit and more like a life she was trying on.

She'd come to Bellweather chasing a dead woman, and instead she'd found a man who moved through his days like he was trying not to leave footprints.

That morning she'd been sent to the office for the spray schedule. Noor had gone to the far block with the crew — she'd watched his truck rattle down the access road twenty minutes earlier — and the office, a narrow room off the tasting hall with a desk that predated both of them, was empty. The window was open. The curtain breathed.

The spray schedule was on the desk, weighted under a stone. But so was something else: a manila envelope, old and soft at the corners, and beneath it a sheet of paper that had been torn — not cut, torn — the ragged edge still furred with fiber, as though someone had ripped it in a hurry or with a shaking hand.

Wren didn't mean to look. That was what she would tell herself later, standing in the doorway with the heat of the day climbing behind her. She didn't mean to look. But the handwriting on the torn sheet caught her the way a voice catches you across a crowded room — a voice you'd know anywhere, even after eleven years of silence, even after death.

She knew that handwriting. She'd grown up inside it. It was on every birthday card her mother had ever made her, on the labels of the jam jars, on the grocery lists and the notes left on the refrigerator and the last page of the notebook Elise Okafor had kept in the drawer by her bed, the one Wren had read so many times after the funeral that the ink had begun to ghost through the paper.

*—and I know he will never forgive me, but I would rather he hate me for leaving than—*

The sentence stopped where the page had been torn away.

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Wren stood in the doorway of the office with her hand still on the frame and her heart doing something strange and arrhythmic, and she looked at the torn paper, and at the envelope beneath it, and at the stone weight, and she understood — not the whole of it, not even a fraction, but the shape of it — that Noor Halloran had known her mother's handwriting all along. That he had been carrying it. That he had lied to her face on the first day, standing at this same gate with his arms crossed and his eyes anywhere but on hers, and said *I barely remember her.*

*—I would rather he hate me for leaving than—*

Than what?

She picked up the torn sheet. Her hands were not steady. The paper was old, yellowed along one edge, and the postmark on the envelope beneath it — she turned it over before she could stop herself — read a date from eleven years ago. Eleven years. Wren had been fourteen. Her mother had been alive, driving her to school, burning the toast, humming in the kitchen, and somewhere in all of that ordinary life she had written a letter to a man in California and never sent it, or sent it and had it come back, or — Wren's mind skittered across the possibilities like a stone across water — hidden it here, in this office, in this house of a man who claimed not to remember her.

The letter was gone from the envelope. Only the torn fragment remained on the desk, and the envelope, empty, soft as an old leaf.

Wren put the paper down. She picked up the spray schedule. She walked back out into the sun and did not remember the walk.

* * *

She didn't confront him. Not then. She was her mother's daughter, and her mother had raised her on patience the way other mothers raised children on prayers — a little every day, until it was bone-deep. So she worked. She worked the rest of that day and the next, hauling hoses and thinning the canopy and laughing at Marcus's terrible jokes in the rows, and all the while a small cold thing sat behind her ribs, growing.

She watched Noor. That was the truth of it. She watched him the way you watch weather.

He was careful — she'd noticed it the first week and filed it away as grief or shyness or the ordinary armor of a man who lived alone. But now the carefulness looked different. Now she saw the seams. The way he checked the office door was locked when he thought no one was looking. The way his eyes went, once, twice, to the cellar door when they passed it with the crew. The way he never spoke her mother's name — not once, not in three weeks of harvest, not when Wren had said it herself a dozen times, offering it up like a key, like an invitation. *Elise. My mother's name was Elise.* And Noor would nod, and go quiet, and change the subject with the smoothness of a man who had practiced.

On the third evening after she found the torn page, the light came through the tasting room windows all gold and long, and the crew had gone home, and Wren was walking back from the cottage with her hair still damp from the shower when she saw it.

Smoke. Not the sweet smoke of the burn pile they kept for canes and clippings — this was thinner, acrid, wrong. It was coming from the office window, the one that had been open three days ago, the curtain breathing.

She should have gone to her cottage. She knew that even as her feet took her across the gravel, past the old oak, up the two steps to the tasting hall door. She knew it and she did it anyway, because the cold thing behind her ribs had been growing for three days and it wanted feeding.

The office door was ajar.

Noor stood at the small iron stove in the corner — the one he lit in winter, she'd assumed, though it was August — and he was feeding papers into it. Not many. A few. He moved slowly, deliberately, the way he did everything, and the firelight moved on his face and made him look younger and older at once, made him look like a man standing at a grave.

On the desk beside him lay a stack of papers, and on top of the stack, face-down, was an envelope with a postmark she had already memorized.

"Noor."

He didn't startle. That was the first strange thing — he turned with the unhurried stillness of a man who had perhaps been waiting to be caught, or had spent so many years alone that being caught no longer frightened him. The fire popped behind him.

"You should knock," he said.

"It's your office." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "I knocked for three weeks. You never hear me."

He looked at the stove. He looked at her. And then, with a small exhale that might have been surrender, he stepped aside — not far, just enough — and she saw what was in the fire. The corner of a page curling, blackening, and on the unburned edge of it, in handwriting that stopped her heart the way the first fragment had stopped it—

"No." The word came out of her before she knew she was going to say it, and she crossed the room in four steps and reached past him, into the stove, into the heat, and pulled the burning page out and dropped it on the stone hearth and stamped it with her boot until the flame died. The room filled with the smell of scorch. Her palm was burned; she barely felt it.

What was left was a corner of paper, maybe three inches of it, charred at every edge. She picked it up with both hands, carefully, the way you'd pick up a bird with a broken wing.

Her mother's handwriting. A few words surviving at the margin, in Elise's slanted, urgent script:

*—he doesn't know. He can never know. Promise me, Noor, promise me you'll—*

Wren read it three times. The room was very quiet. Noor had not moved. The fire in the stove ticked and settled.

"He doesn't know," she said. Her voice sounded like someone else's. "Who doesn't know? Who is *he*, Noor?"

"Wren—"

"Is it me?" She turned to face him and the question came out of her whole body, out of three weeks of watching and twenty-nine years of not knowing and one torn page on a desk that had cracked her open like an egg. "Am I the one who doesn't know? Is that what she wrote? Is that what you've been burning?"

He didn't answer. That was the answer. She watched it land on his face — the flinch he tried to hide, the way his jaw set, the way his eyes went to the window and the door and anywhere that wasn't her. Twenty years of smallness, Tomas had almost said once, at the co-op, before catching himself. Twenty years of a man keeping his life the size of a coffin.

"There's a letter," Wren said.

Noor's shoulders dropped a fraction. "Wren."

"There's a letter. From her. To you. It came eleven years ago — the postmark's still on the envelope, I saw it — and it's torn. There's a page missing. And you've been sitting on it for three weeks while I told you about her, while I showed you her photographs, while I—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, hated him for hearing it. "While I grieved at you. While I stood in your kitchen and cried about my mother and you let me. You let me do all of that, and the whole time you had her handwriting in your desk."

"It's not what you think."

"Then tell me what it is." She took a step toward him. The burned fragment was still in her hands, cradled like something holy and ruined. "Tell me why she wrote to you and never told me. Tell me why you said you barely remembered her. Tell me why you're burning her words in August in a room where it's ninety degrees, Noor, because I have been very patient, and I have been very polite, and I am done."

The silence stretched. Outside, a dove was calling from the oak, that low, hollow sound like the vineyard itself breathing. Noor looked at the stove, at the small fire he'd built to destroy the past, and when he spoke his voice was low and rough, like something dragged over gravel.

"There's a letter," he said. "You're right. She wrote it eleven years ago and it came to the vineyard and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I never opened it. Not for a long time. And when I did, it was—" His hand made a small, helpless gesture, and Wren realized with a strange distant shock that his hands were shaking. "It wasn't whole. A page was torn out. The page that mattered. And what was left was an apology for something I still don't understand, and a promise of something I never got, and I have spent eleven years trying not to think about it, and then you walked through my gate with her eyes and her photographs and I—"

"You lied to me."

"Yes." He said it simply. No excuse attached. Somehow that was worse.

"Why?"

"Because remembering her was the one thing I couldn't survive doing twice." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Because you showed up asking questions I didn't have answers to, and the letter was the only answer I had, and it was torn in half, and I thought if I could just get through the harvest, if I could just—" He stopped himself. Closed his eyes. Opened them. "I was wrong. I know I was wrong."

Wren looked down at the fragment in her hands. *He doesn't know. He can never know.* The char had eaten the rest. Her mother's voice, silenced twice over — once by death, once by fire.

"What did the rest of it say?" she asked. "The part you still have. The part you haven't burned."

Noor was quiet for a long moment. The dove called. Somewhere down the rows, the irrigation pump cycled on with its familiar shudder.

"She was sorry," he said finally. "She was sorry for leaving without saying goodbye. She said there were things she couldn't explain, not then. She said—" His voice caught, and he mastered it, and Wren watched him master it and understood that he had been mastering it for eleven years, alone, in this office, with the door locked. "She said she'd explain everything when she came back."

"But she didn't come back."

"No. She didn't."

"And the torn page? Where is it?"

"I don't know." He said it too quickly. Or maybe he said it honestly — Wren couldn't tell anymore, and that was the cruelest part, that three weeks of trust could be dismantled in three minutes, that she would spend the rest of her life, however long she stayed, checking his face for seams. "I've looked. I tore the office apart the night I read it. It was never there."

"Or you tore it out yourself."

The words left her like a slap, and she saw them land — saw the hurt move through his face like weather — and she didn't take them back. She was too angry, and anger, she knew, was just grief wearing armor, but knowing didn't help.

"I would never," Noor said quietly.

"You said you barely remembered her. You let me think my mother was a stranger to you. You burned her words tonight, Noor. Tonight. While I was fifty yards away taking a shower." She set the burned fragment down on the desk, next to the stone and the spray schedule and the empty envelope, arranging it with a care that was almost ceremonial. "So you'll forgive me if 'I would never' doesn't carry a lot of weight."

He flinched. He actually flinched, this man who never flinched, who moved through heat and debt and loneliness like they were weather.

"What do you want me to say?" he asked, and his voice had gone rough and low, stripped of all its carefulness. "Tell me what you want and I'll say it."

"I want the truth. All of it. What she wrote, what she was sorry for, why she left, why she never—" Wren's voice broke clean in half this time, and she let it. "Why she never told me any of you existed. Any of *this* existed. I want to know what my mother was doing here that was so important she tore a hole in a letter and left a hole in everyone who loved her."

"I don't have it, Wren. I don't have the answers. I have half a letter and twenty years of—"

"Then I can't stay."

The words came out before she'd decided to say them, and once they were out she understood they were true — had been true for three days, since the moment she'd stood in the doorway and seen the torn edge of that page. Not because she was angry. Anger passed. But because she could not build anything on a foundation with a hole in it. Her mother had taught her that, in all the wrong ways.

Noor went very still. "You're leaving."

"I'm leaving." She said it to the window, because she couldn't say it to his face. "Tomorrow. I'll finish out the week if you need me for the Merlot block, but I can't— I can't stand in your rows every day and wonder what you're not telling me. I did that with her for twenty-nine years. I'm not doing it again."

"Wren—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, and her burned palm ached, and she was glad of the ache. "Don't ask me to stay. Don't tell me you're sorry. Just—" She looked at him then, finally, and what she saw frightened her: not a liar, not a keeper of secrets, but a man so hollowed out by the same woman who had hollowed her out that they were almost the same shape. "Just find the rest of the letter, Noor. Or find the truth. Whichever one you actually have. And if you find it before I'm gone—" She stopped. Swallowed. "If you find it before I'm gone, you know where I'll be."

She walked out. The evening had gone purple at the edges; the first star was out over the western hills; the dove had stopped calling. Behind her, through the open office window, she heard nothing — no movement, no protest, not even the fire. Just the silence of a man standing alone in a room full of ashes, and she hated how much it sounded like her mother's kitchen in the last year, and how much she already, unbearably, wanted to go back in.

She made it as far as the old oak before she stopped, her hand flat against the bark, and stood in the dark listening to the vineyard breathe.

Tomorrow, she told herself. I'll leave tomorrow.

But even as she said it, her feet were already memorizing the way back to the office door.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She lay in the narrow bed of the pickers' cottage with the window open and the moths bumping the screen, and she turned the fragment over and over in her mind like a stone in a pocket. *He doesn't know. He can never know. Promise me, Noor, promise me you'll—*

Promise him she'd what? Keep a secret? Raise a child in the dark? Leave?

And who was *he*? Noor? Some other man entirely? Her mother had left Bellweather in a hurry — Tomas had said as much, that night at the co-op, before going carefully quiet. *She wasn't running from this place, mija. She was running toward something. Or away from something. Those looked the same on her sometimes.*

Around three in the morning, Wren got up. She dressed in the dark. She took the flashlight she'd bought at the hardware store in town her first week — she'd told herself it was for the cellar tours, but some part of her had known even then — and she walked out into the cold blue hour, past the sleeping rows, past the crush pad still damp from the storm, to the old cellar door that Noor's eyes always went to when they passed it.

The lock was old. The hinge screamed. Inside, the cellar breathed its cold, mineral breath, and her flashlight found the barrels, the racks, the far wall where the stones were older than everything else.

She didn't know what she was looking for. A torn page. A truth. A reason to stay, or a reason to go.

What she found, wedged in the gap between two barrels at the back of the oldest rack, where the dust lay undisturbed except for one set of recent boot prints leading in and none leading out — was a tin box, small and rusted at the corners, with a postmark date eleven years old, and her mother's handwriting on the front, and the flap already opened.

And inside, folded twice, a single page in Elise's hand that began:

*If you're reading this, Noor, then you finally tore the wall open — or you finally told her the truth. Either way, I'm sorry I made you wait so long. There are things I could only say on paper, and one thing I could only say to you, and I've been a coward about both. So here it is, all of it, and the first thing you need to know is about Wren—*
