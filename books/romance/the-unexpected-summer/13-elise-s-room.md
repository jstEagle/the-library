## 13. Elise's Room

Tomas drew the map on a napkin.

They were sitting in the co-op's cramped tasting room after closing, the smell of oak barrels and old wine soaking into everything, and Wren had asked him—again, more carefully this time—where her mother had lived during those two harvests at Bellweather. Tomas had gone quiet in a way she was learning to recognize, the way men went quiet when a memory had weight to it, and then he'd pulled a napkin from the dispenser and a pen from behind his ear and started drawing.

"The pickers' cottage," he said. "North end of the property, past the equipment shed, up where the hill starts. Nobody's lived in it for—god, fifteen years? Maybe more. Noor keeps it locked, but the lock's older than both of us." He tapped the napkin. "Elise had the corner room upstairs, the one facing west. She used to say she wanted to watch the sun go down over her own vines someday." His voice caught on the last word, and he covered it by clearing his throat. "She'd have liked that you came looking."

Wren folded the napkin along his pen strokes and put it in her jacket pocket, where it stayed for three days while she worked the rows beside Noor and said nothing, asked nothing, let the plan ripen like the fruit around her.

She told herself it was simple curiosity. Her mother had lived here; the cottage was part of that story; it was natural to want to see it. But there was something else underneath, something with sharper edges. Noor had lied to her the first day at the gate—she was sure of it now, sure the way you were sure of weather coming. He'd said he barely remembered Elise, and then he'd looked at Wren's photographs of her mother laughing and his whole face had changed, the way a locked room changes when someone finally turns the key. And he had a letter. She'd seen Elise's handwriting in his office, in the orange glow of a burning barrel, and he still hadn't told her what it said.

So she went to the cottage the way you go looking for anything true these days: alone, early, before anyone could stop her.

* * *

The morning was cool and gray, the kind of coastal summer morning that couldn't decide whether to be kind. Fog sat low in the vine rows, beading the leaves, and Wren's boots left dark prints on the gravel road as she walked north past the equipment shed, past the irrigation pumps humming to themselves, up the gentle rise where the vines gave way to scrub grass and a single wind-bent cypress.

The cottage sat at the top like something the hill had grown.

It was smaller than she'd imagined—two stories, maybe, but narrow, with a steep shingled roof gone soft with moss and a porch that listed slightly to the east. The paint had been white once. Now it was the color of old bone, peeling in long curls, and the windows wore a skin of dust so fine it looked like frost. Someone had planted roses along the front path years ago; they'd gone feral, a thorny tangle swallowing the walk, still stubbornly blooming anyway, small white flowers opening in the gray light.

Wren stood at the gate a long moment.

Her mother had walked this path. Had pushed through these roses, probably, cursing the thorns, laughing—Wren could almost hear it, that laugh that lived now only in photographs and in the strange echo of it she'd heard once from Noor and never mentioned. Her mother had climbed these steps with her boots on, twenty-six years old, carrying whatever she was carrying—the fear, the running, the whole hidden architecture of a life Wren was only now beginning to see the shape of.

The door was locked, exactly as Tomas had promised. The padlock was a rusted thing, but the hasp screws had loosened with decades of weather, and when Wren braced her shoulder and pulled, the whole plate groaned away from the doorframe with a sound like a tooth coming out. She winced, looked back down the hill. No one. The fog had the whole vineyard to itself.

Inside, the cottage smelled the way all abandoned places smell—dust and dry wood and something faintly sweet underneath, like fruit left too long in a drawer. A narrow hallway ran the length of the ground floor. To the left, a common room with a long table and benches, the kind where twenty pickers could eat together after a day in the rows. Someone had left a calendar on the wall, its pages yellowed, still open to a July from another decade. To the right, a kitchenette with a sink stained brown at the drain and a window over it that looked out on nothing in particular.

She climbed the stairs.

They creaked in a rhythm, and she found herself memorizing it without meaning to—the exact music of her mother's footsteps, maybe, coming home tired at night. Upstairs there were four doors. Three stood open onto small bare rooms: a mattress frame, a nail where a coat had hung, a windowsill holding a dead fly and eleven years of light. The fourth door, at the end, facing west, was closed.

Wren's hand was steady on the knob. She noticed that. She noticed everything now, the way you did when your whole life had tilted a few degrees off its axis and you were trying to find something fixed to hold.

The corner room was the largest, and it was empty—except that it wasn't, not really. Empty rooms were never empty. This one held the ghost of arrangement: the rectangle of less-faded wallpaper where a poster had hung, two small holes in the plaster where something had been pinned, a windowsill wiped cleaner than the others, as if a hand had rested there a thousand times, looking out at the view.

The view was the whole western valley. The vine rows falling away in long silver-green lines, the old oak standing alone in the middle distance like a sentence someone had underlined, and beyond it, the hills going blue into fog. The sun, when it came, would set right there, over her mother's someday-vines.

Wren sat down on the floor with her back against the wall and let herself cry for a minute—not the big grief, the one she'd carried for two years since the funeral, but a smaller, stranger ache, the ache of a daughter standing inside a room her mother had loved and feeling how far away that mother still was, even here, even now. Especially here.

Then she wiped her face, because she hadn't come to cry. She'd come to search.

* * *

She went through the room the way Tomas had taught her to go through a vine row: systematically, without sentiment, trusting the work.

The mattress frame had nothing under it but mouse droppings and a flattened wine cork. The closet held a coat hanger and, folded in the far corner, a woman's work shirt, faded blue, small enough to be Elise's. Wren held it to her face and breathed in, and got dust, and got nothing, and put it down gently anyway. The floorboards were old and loose; she pried up four of them with a flathead screwdriver from her rental car's emergency kit and found darkness, spiders, one 1978 penny. The windowsill. The wallpaper. The light fixture, the baseboards, the space behind the door where the plaster had cracked.

Nothing. An hour in, her knees ached and her hope was thinning, and she sat back on her heels in the middle of the room and looked around at the walls that had held her mother and felt the old frustration rise—not at Elise, never at Elise, but at the silence itself, at the way the dead kept their secrets the way the living kept their letters: torn, hidden, unsent.

Then she saw the wall behind the headboard's ghost-rectangle.

The wallpaper there was less faded, yes—but there was something else, a faint vertical seam she'd taken for a repair. She crossed the room on her knees and put her fingers to it. The seam wasn't a seam. It was an edge. Someone had cut the wallpaper—cut it cleanly with a blade, all four sides, and pasted it back down over whatever was behind it.

Her heart was suddenly very loud.

She worked the screwdriver under the bottom edge, gently, the way you'd open something you were afraid of waking. The paper peeled back in one piece, old glue surrendering, and there in the plaster was a cavity the size of a shoebox, cut into the wall itself, its interior lined with a scrap of the same wallpaper like a drawer in a jewel case.

Inside: a tin.

Not a box—a tin, the kind shortbread came in once, red and gold, the lid dented as if it had been dropped and picked up and dropped again. Wren lifted it out with both hands. It was heavier than it looked. Her mother had cut open a wall and hidden this, had papered over her own secret, had slept every night with her back against it.

She sat on the floorboards in the gray light and opened the lid.

The first thing was a rosary, cheap beads, the kind sold in airport gift shops, wound into a knot. The second thing was a folded newspaper clipping, yellow and soft as cloth—a local paper, eleven years old, a small article about a vineyard harvest festival, and in the photograph, blurry, a man in a wide-brimmed hat laughing with a woman whose face was turned away but whose posture Wren knew the way she knew her own hands.

The third thing was a photograph.

It was a real photograph, glossy, the kind that got printed at a drugstore counter, and it had been handled so much that the corners were rounded soft and the surface was clouded with fingerprints—the ghost-geography of a thumb and forefinger returning, returning, returning, over years.

It showed a baby.

A baby on a blanket, a few months old, fat-cheeked and furious-eyed, one fist raised in what looked like a demand for the world to explain itself. The blanket was knitted, uneven, the work of someone learning. And the background—Wren's breath stopped—the background was a kitchen window with a vine visible through the glass, and a coffee mug on the counter, and the corner of a stove, and she knew, she *knew*, with a certainty that arrived whole and unbidden, that this was a kitchen she had stood in. That this baby had been photographed in this valley, in this life, in the middle of the story her mother had never told.

She turned the photograph over.

There was writing on the back in blue ballpoint pen, in a hand she had never seen before and recognized instantly, because she had spent three weeks now watching it appear on chalkboards and work orders and the tags on the vine rows: a narrow, careful, slightly old-fashioned hand, the letters leaning a little, as if the writer was always in a hurry to say the next true thing.

It said:

*Wren, six months. She looks like you when you're about to argue. God help us both. — N*

And below that, smaller, pressed hard enough to nearly tear the paper:

*Come home. Both of you. Anytime. Always.*

The cottage was very quiet. The fog outside the window had begun to burn off, and a blade of sun came through the glass and lay across the floorboards, across Wren's knees, across the photograph in her hands, and she sat in it without moving for a long time.

*N.*

Noor.

The word didn't explode in her so much as settle, the way a key settles into a lock it has been cut for. She thought of his face when she'd shown him the photographs of Elise laughing. She thought of the way he'd said *I barely remember her* and how his hands had been very still while he said it, the stillness of a man holding a door shut from the inside. She thought of the letter in the fire's glow, Elise's handwriting, his refusal. She thought of the way he watched her work the rows—not the way a boss watched a new hire, but the way you watched something you were afraid of wanting to keep.

*She looks like you when you're about to argue.*

Her mother had shown someone this photograph. Her mother had sat with this man—Noor, young, in his wide-brimmed hat, laughing at the harvest festival—and shown him a picture of Wren at six months, and he had written on the back of it, and Elise had kept it, hidden in a wall, kept it through whatever running and hiding and fear had driven her away from this valley, kept it until the day she died.

Which meant—

Wren's mind moved carefully now, the way you walked on wet rocks.

Which meant her mother had been here after Wren was born. Or had come back. The timing blurred and reassembled itself: the harvest years Tomas had described, the two seasons at Bellweather, the baby on the blanket in a kitchen with vines outside the window. Her whole childhood, her mother had been a single parent who answered questions about Wren's father with a closed face and a change of subject—*he wasn't a bad man, he just wasn't staying, it's complicated, drink your juice.* Wren had built a hundred fathers in her imagination over the years and dismantled them all. She had stopped building them at all.

And now one was sitting in the farmhouse down the hill, drinking the same coffee every morning, keeping his life small as a coffin, lying to her face with his very still hands.

He might not know. That was the thing that made her chest hurt in a new way. He might genuinely not know—Elise had torn pages out of letters, cut holes in walls, run from a man named Ray whom Tomas would only call *trouble* and no more. A woman that good at hiding could hide a child's paternity from its father. Could hide it so well that the father spent twenty years alone in a vineyard, not knowing that the family he thought he'd never had had existed all along, had a name, had a fist raised at the camera, had his own handwriting on the back of her only picture.

Or he did know. And he'd looked Wren in the eye at the gate and lied.

She didn't know which possibility was worse. She sat with both of them, turning them over like the tin in her hands, and the sun climbed, and the fog burned off the valley, and somewhere down the hill a truck engine started—Noor's truck, she knew the sound now, she hated that she knew the sound now—and she understood that in an hour he would be standing in the rows expecting her, and she would have to look at him, and she would not be able to unknow what she knew.

Wren put the photograph in her jacket pocket, over her heart, where the napkin map had been. She wound the rosary around her wrist. She folded the newspaper clipping into her back pocket. The tin she left open on the floorboards, facing the window, facing the west, facing the view her mother had loved—because it seemed wrong to close it up again, wrong to re-hide what had been hidden long enough.

At the door she stopped and looked back at the corner room, at the peeled wallpaper and the open cavity in the wall, at the rectangle of brighter plaster where a photograph had hung for years above a hidden tin, and she thought: *You were going to tell me. Someday. You were keeping it all for someday, and someday ran out.*

The stairs creaked behind her in their old rhythm, her mother's rhythm, and then she was out the door, down the steps, through the feral roses that caught her sleeves and held on, and down the hill toward the rows, toward the man in the wide-brimmed hat, toward the conversation that was going to change everything, whether he was ready for it or not.

Whether she was.
