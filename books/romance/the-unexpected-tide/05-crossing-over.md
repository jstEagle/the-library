## 5. Crossing Over

The *Gull's Wing* smelled of diesel and old paint, the way every boat to Gull Island had smelled since Mara was nine years old. She stood at the rail with her ticket crumpled in her fist, watching the harbor shrink behind them, and told herself that the tightness in her chest was seasickness.

It was not seasickness. Seasickness she could have treated with ginger chews and a fixed horizon. This was something else—the specific vertigo of a woman who has just paid a stranger four hundred dollars to hold her hand at her mother's table.

Theo Marsh was at the bow, ostensibly inspecting the railing. He'd been doing a lot of ostensible things since they boarded: checking his phone, studying the safety placard, examining the rivets along the gunwale as though their structural integrity were a personal concern. Every so often he glanced back at her, quick and assessing, like a man confirming a chess piece hadn't moved.

She'd bought him a clean shirt yesterday—a soft blue oxford from the outlet mall on Route 1—and he'd worn it without comment, sleeves rolled to the forearm, collar open. It had cost her thirty-eight dollars and it made him look like someone worth introducing. She tried not to think about what it meant that she'd noticed.

"Water's calm today," he said, materializing beside her with two coffees from the galley. He handed her one. "Captain says we might even beat the fog."

"I didn't know you'd talked to the captain."

"He let me look at the engine on the way aboard." A shrug. "Occupational habit."

Of course. The mechanic. She kept forgetting, in the intervals when he wasn't reminding her, that Theo Marsh fixed ferry engines for a living—that the man she'd hired out of a dockside handshake was, by trade, a man whose hands spent their days inside machines. His hands were clean now, but she'd seen the calluses, the grease shadowed into the creases of his knuckles that no scrubbing fully lifted. There was something steadying about those hands. Something honest. Which was ironic, given what she'd hired them for.

"So," he said. "Cover story."

"You've memorized your half?"

"Mara Eastwick, freelance writer, lives in Boston, met me—" he paused, ticking through it "—at the ferry terminal in Rockport, six months ago. You dropped your notebook in the harbor and I fished it out."

"You fished it out with a boat hook."

"With a boat hook," he agreed solemnly. "Very romantic. Very maritime."

"And I offered to buy you coffee as thanks, and you said yes, and we've been together since March."

"Since March." He nodded slowly, then frowned. "One problem."

"What?"

"The notebook. What was in it? People will ask. Your family will ask. Mothers especially—mothers are trained interrogators with better lighting."

Mara looked out at the water. The mainland was a gray-green smudge now, already becoming somewhere else, some other life. "Poems," she said. "Bad ones. About the tide."

"About the tide," Theo repeated.

"It's a small lie. Small lies float; big ones sink. That's the whole art of it."

He studied her for a moment longer than the joke deserved. "You've done this before," he said. Not accusingly. Curiously, the way he'd probably listened to an engine and identified which part was lying.

"No," she said. "But I've lied professionally for eleven years. This is just the first time I've done it in person."

She hadn't meant to say that. It slipped out the way truth does—sideways, unguarded, taking advantage of a lurch in the deck. Theo went still beside her, coffee halfway to his mouth.

"Professionally," he said.

"Forget it." She turned back to the rail. "Forget I said that."

But he didn't forget it. She could feel him not forgetting it, standing there beside her, recalibrating. And she understood, with a sinking feeling that had nothing to do with the swell, that she had just handed this stranger a thread, and that men like Theo Marsh—men who noticed things, men who read safety placards for something to do—were exactly the kind who pulled threads.

* * *

The trouble with crossing water is that there is nowhere to go. On land, a misstep can be walked off, a conversation abandoned mid-sentence, a confession left unfinished in a parking lot. But thirty minutes into the crossing, with Gull Island still a low dark line ahead and the mainland already dissolved behind them, Mara realized she was trapped in the one place where her lies would have to keep her company.

They sat on the leeward bench, out of the spray, knees almost touching. Theo had produced a paperback from his jacket pocket—some battered thriller with a cracked spine—and was reading it with the particular concentration of a man avoiding conversation. Mara watched the island grow.

Her mother would be at the dock. Her mother was always at the dock, because arriving people could be assessed before they'd had time to compose themselves. Mara rehearsed the assessment: *You look tired, darling. Is that what writers wear? And who is this?*

*This is Theo. We met in Rockport. He fishes my notebooks out of harbors.*

It would hold. It would hold for three days if she managed it right, if she kept Theo close and the questions shallow and her own face arranged into the pleasant, unremarkable expression that had always been expected of her—the face of the Eastwick daughter who did *something* with words, wasn't it, dear, nothing anyone had heard of.

The shame of it rose in her throat like bile. Eleven years. Eleven years of bestsellers leaving her hands under other names, of confidentiality clauses thick as fog, of being introduced at parties as "a writer" in the tone reserved for people who collected spoons. And now she was bringing home a rented boyfriend to prove that the years away had amounted to something.

"Your turn," Theo said suddenly, closing the book.

"My turn for what?"

"Lie to me. Practice run. I'll be your mother."

"That's absurd."

"It's free rehearsal space." He set the paperback down, folded his hands, and transformed—shoulders squaring, chin lifting, mouth settling into a thin line of polite appraisal—with such accuracy that Mara laughed despite herself. "Hello, Mara. You look tired. Is that what writers wear?"

"How did you—"

"Lucky guess." His eyes glinted. "And who is this?"

Mara took a breath. Played it straight. "Mother, this is Theo Marsh. We met in Rockport in the fall. He works on boats."

"What kind of work, Theo?"

"Engine repair. Ferries, mostly." He delivered it evenly, no apology in it, and Mara felt something loosen in her chest. He wasn't ashamed of it. He wore the job like a fact, not a wound—which was more than she could say for herself and hers.

"And how long have you been seeing our Mara?"

"Since March."

"And what do you see in her? Forgive me, I only ask because Mara has always been difficult to know."

There it was. The barb, delivered with a smile, exactly the way her mother delivered them. Mara braced for Theo to falter—but he only tilted his head, considering, as though the question genuinely interested him.

"She writes like she's apologizing," he said. "I keep waiting for her to stop."

The wind took the words sideways across the deck. Mara stared at him. "That's not—you can't say that to her."

"Why not? It's true. I've known you four days and I've watched you apologize for existing at least twice. Once for asking for extra napkins."

"That was a different situation."

"It's a pattern." He picked up his coffee, entirely unbothered. "Anyway, your mother will love it or hate it, and either way she'll remember it, and being remembered is half the battle at these things. Trust me. I've been the invisible man at enough family tables to know."

Something in that sentence snagged on her. *Been the invisible man.* But he was already moving on, nodding toward the island, and she let it go, filing it away in the growing drawer of things about Theo Marsh that didn't add up.

* * *

The island came up fast then, gray rock and pitch pine, the Victorian houses strung along the harbor like pearls on a wire. The Eastwick house stood third from the point, white clapboard gone silver, its widow's walk visible above the tree line. Mara's stomach performed a slow revolution.

"We should talk money," Theo said quietly. "Before the dock."

"Four hundred, as agreed. Half now." She reached for her bag.

"Half now, half Sunday." He waved her off. "That's not what I mean. I mean—what happens if it stops being pretend?"

Mara's hand stopped over the zipper. "It won't."

"People say that." He was looking at the island, not at her, voice pitched under the engine noise. "People say it won't, and then somebody's mother asks a real question, and somebody answers honestly to make it convincing, and then—" He shrugged. "I'm just saying. Know where the exits are."

"There are no exits," Mara said. "We're on a boat."

"I noticed."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The ferry swung wide around the point, and the Eastwick house came full into view, and Mara saw that the porch flag was up, which meant arrivals were expected, which meant her mother had been watching the channel since morning.

"One rule addition," Theo said. "If either of us wants out, we say the word 'undertow.' No explanations. We just... separate, and handle it."

"Undertow," Mara repeated. It sounded like a word from one of her own manuscripts—too literary, too neat. "Fine. Undertow."

He held out his hand. She shook it, formal and absurd, a contract renewed at sea, and his grip was warm and dry and lingered perhaps half a second past professional.

Then the horn sounded—one long blast, arrival—and the deck tilted beneath them as the ferry came about, and there was no more time for rules or rehearsals or anything but the dock rushing closer, and the figures on it resolving into detail.

Her mother first, naturally, in linen the color of heavy cream, sunglasses pushed up into silver hair. Beside her, her brother Grant, arm around his wife, both of them wearing the matched smiles of people whose lives had gone according to plan. And at the end of the dock, half-hidden behind a stack of supply crates—

A man in a navy peacoat, tall, dark-haired, laughing at something the harbormaster said.

Mara's whole body went cold.

Julian Vane. On Gull Island. Three days early, standing on her family's dock as though he belonged there—as though he had ever once asked permission for anything in his life.

Beside her, Theo followed her gaze. "Friend of yours?" he asked lightly.

"No," Mara said, and heard how the word cracked down the middle. "No one. Yet."

The ferry bumped the pilings. The lines went across. And Mara Eastwick stepped onto the island with her hand in a stranger's hand, walking straight toward the one person she had crossed an ocean of years to avoid.
