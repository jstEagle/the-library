## 15. What the Tide Takes

The wind off the channel had teeth that morning.

Mara felt them before she opened her eyes—rattling the shutters of the guest cottage, worrying at the eaves, carrying the smell of a storm still two days out but already announcing itself. She lay in bed and listened to it and thought: *this is what it sounds like when everything you've built comes apart.*

Theo's face when he'd walked away from the cliffs kept replaying behind her eyelids. Not anger. That would have been easier. It had been worse than anger—it had been *recognition*, the look of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish. *You rewrote my sentences. You've been in my books for years. Which parts of me did you make?*

She hadn't answered. She hadn't been able to.

Now she sat up, pulled her laptop onto the quilt, and stared at the blank document until the cursor stopped blinking like a heartbeat and started blinking like an accusation.

*Dear Celeste,* she typed. Deleted it. Typed it again.

*Dear Celeste. Effective immediately, I am withdrawing from the Vane memoir project.*

There. Fourteen words. The easiest hard thing she'd ever written, and her hands shook anyway—not from fear of Celeste, not from the breach-of-contract clause she could already recite from memory, but because without the memoir she was nothing. No byline. No name on any spine anywhere. Just Mara Eastwick, who had once written a novel so true it scared her, and who had spent eleven years since then writing other people's truths instead.

Her mother's voice drifted up through the floorboards, bright and performative, directing someone to move the good chairs onto the porch before the rain came. The reunion churned on around her like weather of its own. Her brother's laugh. Her sister-in-law's flute-bright commentary. A family functioning beautifully, publicly, with one member missing—and everyone too polite to say which one.

Mara finished the resignation in twenty minutes. Short, professional, dignified. She read it twice and hated how dignified it sounded, how much it sounded like her—the woman who exited rooms cleanly, who left no fingerprints, who had made an art form of disappearing.

Then she opened a second document.

This one took longer.

*Theo,* she wrote. *I need to tell you something I should have told you on the ferry, or maybe before the ferry, or maybe the first night you ate my terrible chowder and didn't complain about it.*

*I didn't just rewrite your manuscript once. I've done it for years. Every book under R.T. Marsh's name since the third one—I've been the hand inside the glove. Your editor sends me pages marked "author unresponsive" and I fix them, and your publisher pays me through a shell LLC I set up so no one would ever trace the work back to either of us. You think your career survived on momentum. It survived on me.*

*I told myself I was protecting you. That's the lie ghosts tell. I wasn't protecting you. I was keeping you small enough to manage, and keeping myself invisible enough to stay safe, and calling both of those things love.*

*Here is the part I can't take back: some of the best sentences in those books are mine. And some of the worst years of my life are in them too, disguised as yours.*

*I don't expect anything. I'm resigning from the memoir, I'm leaving the island on whatever boat will take me, and I'm going to write a novel under my own name even if nobody buys it. But I wanted you to know that the woman you met at the dock was real. The rest of it—the contract, the rules, the story we invented—none of that was real except the part where I stopped being able to tell the difference.*

Mara read it back and felt something crack open in her chest—not pain exactly, more like pressure finding a vent. Eleven years of silence, and it fit on two pages.

She printed both letters. Paper felt more honest than pixels; paper could be handed over, held, kept or burned. She folded them together, tucked them into the pocket of her canvas jacket, and went out into the wind.

* * *

The dunes east of the house were empty this time of morning. The tide was far out, the flats gleaming like hammered tin, gulls standing ankle-deep in their own reflections. Mara walked until the house was a gray smudge behind her and the wind was the only voice left.

She took out Theo's letter first. Read it standing up, one last time, the pages snapping in the gusts.

What she felt wasn't relief. It was terror—the clean, clarifying kind. If she gave him this letter, she couldn't control what happened next. He might forgive her. He might never speak to her again. He might sell the story to a trade paper and end her career with a paragraph. All of those futures were possible, and all of them were hers, and for the first time in eleven years she wasn't reaching for the exit.

She folded the letter closed. Held it out over the flats like an offering.

And the wind took it.

Not gently—one hard gust off the water, the kind that comes ahead of a front, and the pages tore free of her fingers and went cartwheeling down the beach, white wings against gray sand, tumbling toward the tideline. Mara ran. She ran in her boots through the soft sand, snatching at air, and the wind laughed and lifted the pages higher, higher, out over the wet flats where the gulls wheeled curious and indifferent, and then they were gone—out over the channel, two small white shapes shrinking to nothing against the steel-colored water.

She stood there breathing hard, sand in her cuffs, salt on her lips.

Gone. Both pages. Her resignation and her confession, delivered to the sea, which accepted all correspondence and replied to none.

She should have felt freed. Instead she felt hollowed out, like a shell the tide had decided against. The words were still in her—she could rewrite them, she always could—but the moment had passed through her hands, and moments like that didn't come twice.

Behind her, footsteps in the sand. She knew the stride before she turned.

Julian.

He was dressed for sailing, of course—cream sweater, collar open, hair doing exactly what it had done at twenty-two when he'd lean across the library table and say *you're the only one here who reads the way I do.* He stopped a few feet away, hands in his pockets, and looked at her with an expression she remembered too well: warm, patient, certain of his welcome.

"You look like a woman who just lost something," he said.

"The wind," Mara said. "It's aggressive today."

"I saw." He nodded toward the water. "Looked important."

"It was."

Something flickered across his face—curiosity, quickly banked. He came closer, stood beside her, both of them facing the emptied channel. For a moment they were twenty-two again, and the flats were the quad, and everything was still possible.

"Celeste called me," Julian said. "Last night. She's furious about the memoir—says you're stalling, says the new chapters are cold." He paused. "She also said you quit. This morning. By email."

Mara closed her eyes. Of course. The laptop. She'd hit send on the resignation before printing it, on autopilot, wanting it done. "I did quit."

"Why?"

"Because the book she wants isn't a memoir. It's a monument. To you." She turned to face him. "She wants me to write you as the hero of a story where you broke my heart, Julian. I can't do that anymore. I'm done writing other people's versions of my life."

The wind pushed between them. Julian studied her, and she watched him assemble the old charm, deploy it, watch it fail to land.

"There's another option," he said quietly. "Celeste floated it. I'm offering it."

"I can't wait."

"Co-credit." He said it simply, the way he'd once said *come away with me* and meant it. "Your name on the cover next to mine. *The Vane Memoir, as told to Mara Eastwick.* Real credit. Real money. Your name in print for the first time in a decade—under a major imprint, with publicity, with everything you've wanted since you were nineteen years old."

Mara's breath caught. Because he was right—that was everything. That was the thing she'd wanted so badly she'd built her whole life around not wanting it. Her name. In print. On a real book.

"And in exchange?" she asked, though she already knew.

"In exchange, the book stays the book. My version. Our version." Julian's voice softened into something almost gentle. "And the mechanic goes back to being a stranger. You tell him the arrangement ended, you pay him off, you let him walk. Nobody ever has to know what he figured out on those cliffs—or what you figured out about yourself. One contained story. Clean."

"He's not a stranger."

"Mara." Julian stepped closer, and there it was, the old gravity, the certainty that had once felt like destiny and now just felt like weight. "You've spent eleven years being invisible so you wouldn't get hurt again. I'm offering you the opposite of invisible. Your name, in lights, on the one condition that you keep lying to one man in a town full of people who'll forget him by October." He smiled, and it was the smile from the library, from the dock, from every crossroads of her life. "That's not a cruel trade. That's just the price. There's always a price. You taught me that."

She looked at him—at the man she had loved so completely at an age when she didn't know love was also a currency—and she understood, finally, with total clarity, that Julian wasn't offering her a future. He was offering her a beautifully furnished past, with her name engraved on the door.

And Theo—Theo, who patched engines and wrote sentences that made her jealous and looked at her like she was legible—Theo would spend the rest of his life not knowing why the woman he'd started to fall for had vanished between one tide and the next.

"That's the deal," Julian said. "Co-credit, and he disappears. Yes or no."

Out on the flats, the water had begun its long crawl back in, filling the channels first, silver threads widening into veins. The tide coming home for things the wind had missed.

Mara thought of two white pages tumbling out over the channel, and of all the words she'd written under other people's names, and of a man on a dock who had said *no real feelings* and then broken his own rule in a rainstorm with his hand an inch from her face.

She opened her mouth to answer—

—and stopped, because down the beach, bent into the wind, a figure was walking the tideline. Stopping. Picking something up. White paper, sodden, flapping.

Theo stood at the edge of the water with her confession in his hands, and even at fifty yards she could see him reading.
