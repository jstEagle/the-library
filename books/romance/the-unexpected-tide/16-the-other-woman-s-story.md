## 16. The Other Woman's Story

Celeste Vane had always believed that the truth was a kind of tide. You could stand on the shore and pretend it wasn't coming, but it came anyway, patient and enormous, and when it arrived it rearranged everything you'd built on the sand.

She was thinking about tides on a Tuesday in late September, sitting in her glass-walled office above the Hudson with the manuscript open on her laptop and her assistant's coffee going cold beside her. Below her, the river moved the way it always moved—unbothered, certain of its destination.

On the screen: Chapter Fourteen. The chapter about Julian. The one Mara Eastwick had written so beautifully that Celeste had read it three times and cried twice, which was not something she admitted to anyone, ever.

*He had a way of standing in doorways as if he'd just arrived from somewhere better and might return there at any moment.*

Mara had written that. Of course Mara had written that. Celeste had watched Julian stand in doorways for twenty years and had never once found language for it. That was the difference between them, and it had always been the difference between them, and it was the reason—though no one knew this, though no one would ever know it unless Celeste chose to tell them—that Celeste had hired Mara Eastwick in the first place.

Not despite what Mara had taken from her.

Because of it.

* * *

Here is what people believed about Celeste Vane: that she was effortless. That she had been born luminous, that success sat on her like light sits on water, that she had never once stood in front of a blank page and felt the specific terror of having nothing to say. Her memoir proposal had gone to auction with nine houses bidding. Her face had been on the cover of two magazines before she'd written a word of the actual book. When the deal closed at a number her agent whispered across a restaurant table like a confession, the waiter brought champagne nobody had ordered.

What people did not believe—or rather, what they never thought to ask—was whether Celeste Vane could actually write it.

The answer, which she kept in a drawer the way other women kept old love letters, was no. Not alone. Not the way it needed to be written.

She had tried. God, she had tried. There were four abandoned drafts in that drawer, each one beginning with the same sentence—*I was born in the summer the ferry first ran*—and each one dying by page thirty, because every time she approached the true material, the material about Julian, about the house on Gull Island, about the year everything broke, her prose turned to varnish. Pretty, protective, dead. She wrote herself the way the world saw her, and the world's version of Celeste Vane had no wounds, and a memoir without wounds was just a press kit with feelings.

So she did what powerful women do. She went looking for someone who could bleed on her behalf.

The agency sent three candidates. The first was a man who talked for forty minutes about "narrative architecture" and never once asked what it had felt like. The second was a woman who flattered her so relentlessly that Celeste stopped hearing the words after ten minutes. The third appointment canceled. And then, a week later, an email arrived from a name Celeste hadn't seen in fifteen years, attached to a writing sample that made her sit very still in her chair until the stillness became its own kind of answer.

*Mara Eastwick,* the agent's note said. *Referred. Strong voice. A little raw, but that can be sanded.*

A little raw. Celeste almost laughed. She read the sample twice—the sample was a profile piece, twelve hundred words on a lighthouse keeper off Maine, and it contained a sentence about loneliness that Celeste copied out by hand and kept in her notebook for a month—and then she opened a browser window and did what she should have done years ago, in a dorm room, with a letter she'd intercepted instead of delivered.

She looked Mara Eastwick up.

* * *

People assumed Celeste hadn't known. That was the comfortable story, the one that let everyone sleep: the great Celeste Vane, deceived by a ghostwriter, a victim of literary fraud. It would be delicious if it ever came out. *Vane Memoir Written by College Rival.* She could see the headline. She could see the think pieces. She could see Julian's face when he read it, that particular softening around his mouth he got when the world confirmed his sister needed protecting.

But here was the thing about being underestimated your whole life: you learned to see clearly. You learned that the girl everyone dismissed—the scholarship student, the quiet one, the one who loved her brother with a devotion so naked it embarrassed everyone at parties—was watching. Always watching. Taking notes.

Celeste had been twenty years old the semester she understood exactly who Mara Eastwick was.

It was the letter that did it. Mara's letter to Julian—three pages, folded into quarters, left in his campus mailbox on a Thursday in November. Celeste had found it first because Celeste checked her brother's mailbox the way other people checked horoscopes: compulsively, superstitiously, looking for signs. She had read it standing under the flickering light of the mail room, and by the second paragraph her hands had gone cold, because the letter was good. Not sweet-good. *Good*-good. Sentences that turned. Images that landed and stayed landed. Mara Eastwick, whom their whole circle treated as Julian's pleasant shadow, had written her brother a love letter that belonged in a book, and Celeste had stood there holding it and understood two things simultaneously.

First: her brother would fumble this. He would be kind about it, because Julian was kind the way weather was kind—indiscriminately, without commitment—and he would let Mara down so gently she'd spend a decade mistaking gentleness for her own failure.

Second: Celeste wanted whatever this was. This ability. This power to make a person feel seen on paper. She wanted it the way she'd wanted everything since childhood—completely, and without much thought about cost.

She didn't deliver the letter. She told herself later that she'd lost it, that it was an accident, that the universe had simply intervened. But the truth, which she finally allowed herself at forty-one, alone in an office above the Hudson, was simpler and uglier: she put it in her desk drawer and said nothing, because a Mara who was heartbroken and humiliated would retreat, and a retreated Mara would never become the thing Celeste needed her to become, and also—this part she could barely look at, even now—because some small cold engine inside Celeste wanted to know what Mara would do without him. Wanted to watch. Wanted, in the way that only the overlooked understand wanting, to take something and see if anyone noticed.

Nobody noticed. Julian met someone else by spring. Mara transferred schools the following year and vanished so thoroughly that even Celeste lost the thread of her. And Celeste went on being luminous, collecting successes the way the shore collects shells, and every few years, in the drawer with the failed drafts, she'd find the letter again—she'd moved it from apartment to apartment, office to office, a relic she couldn't explain to any of the men who helped her move—and she'd reread it and feel the same double sensation: grief for what she'd stolen, and hunger for what it proved.

Then the email came. Fifteen years later. *Mara Eastwick, referred, strong voice.*

And Celeste had understood, with the clarity of a wave finally breaking, that the tide had come back around.

* * *

She hired her within the week. She paid above market—generously above, enough that Mara could never quite afford to leave—and she set terms that would have raised flags in anyone paying attention: total confidentiality, no byline, no acknowledgment, no contact with the publicity team. She told her agent it was about privacy. It was about privacy the way a locked door is about privacy. What she actually wanted was to keep Mara close and unnamed, the way Celeste herself had once been kept—visible, useful, erased.

She told herself it was penance. Some days she even believed it.

Other days she was honest enough to admit it was something stranger: it was the only intimacy she trusted. Here, at last, was a relationship where she knew the whole truth and the other person knew none of it. After a lifetime of being seen incorrectly—admired by strangers who didn't know her, resented by family who did—it was a relief beyond measure to hold someone else's secret instead. To know that the woman drafting her childhood summers had once loved her brother. To know that every careful sentence Mara wrote about the Vane family was written by a woman who had stood at the edge of that family and been turned away. To know, and to say nothing, and to watch what Mara did with the knowing.

What Mara did with it was write the best chapters of her life.

That was the part Celeste hadn't predicted. She'd expected resentment to leak through—bitter marginalia, subtle sabotage, a ghostwriter's revenge hidden in adjectives. Instead the manuscript grew tender. Chapter Seven, about the summer their mother got sick, made Celeste's editor cry. Chapter Eleven, about Julian leaving for London, captured a grief Celeste had never managed to articulate in fifteen years of trying. Mara was doing the thing Celeste had hired her to do—bleeding on her behalf—but she was bleeding *generously*, and this undid Celeste more than malice ever could have. Every beautiful page was a gift from a woman who didn't know she was giving it. Every act of grace was performed by someone with every right to withhold it.

And so, somewhere around Chapter Nine, Celeste began to plan the amends.

Not exposure—she'd considered it, early, in a moment of spite she was ashamed of, imagining the headline that would finally make the world see her brother's quiet shadow as the talent she'd always known she was. No. Something better. Dual credit. Shared byline. A public restoration, engineered from above, that would give Mara back her name in the most visible way possible. Celeste had spent fifteen years benefiting from a theft; she intended to end the book by returning it, with interest, and she wanted to watch Mara's face when she understood that the rival she'd feared for half her life had been, all along, the only person who truly knew what her sentences were worth.

There was only one problem, and her name was also Mara Eastwick, and she had apparently decided to solve all her problems by running away to Gull Island with a ferry mechanic.

* * *

The video call, when Celeste finally placed it, had gone badly. She'd demanded the Julian rewrite—fair, she still maintained; the chapter was too hard on him, or perhaps too easy, she honestly couldn't tell anymore, loving her brother was like trying to focus on a moving train—and Mara had lied to her. Badly. A background that didn't match her apartment. A hesitation Celeste could have measured with instruments. *I'm home, I'm working, I'll have it Friday.*

Celeste had ended the call and sat for a long time looking at the dark screen, feeling something she hadn't felt since the mail room: caught out. Because she recognized the lie. She had invented that lie. *I'm fine, I'm working, everything's normal*—the signature sentence of a woman hiding in plain sight, and Celeste had heard it in her own voice for twenty years, and now here it was coming back to her across a bad connection from an island she'd fled at nineteen, wearing the voice of the woman she'd wronged.

The mirror was complete. That was the joke of it, the elegant, terrible symmetry: Celeste had hired her ghost precisely because the ghost had once been her—a talented girl erased by proximity to the Vanes—and now the ghost had become her entirely, lying beautifully, hoarding secrets, disappearing into a role. Mara Eastwick was living Celeste's life. And Celeste, reading her own memoir in draft form, discovering her own history rendered more honestly than she'd ever lived it, was slowly becoming Mara: the woman behind the glass, watching, taking notes, waiting for the tide.

Her phone buzzed. Her agent.

*FYI — gossip feed says Julian Vane spotted on the Gull Island supply boat this morning. Thought your ghostwriter might want to know her ex is visiting the reunion. Also: storm system moving in, ferries may stop early. Whatever deadline drama is happening out there, it's about to get literal.*

Celeste stared at the message. Julian. On the island. With Mara, who had spent fifteen years building a life on the foundation of a letter he'd never read. With whoever this mechanic was, whose name Celeste had extracted from a ferry company website in eleven minutes flat—Theo Marsh, age thirty-four, no publications under his legal name, which meant either he was exactly what he appeared to be or he was the most interesting man on that island, and Celeste had learned long ago to bet on the second option.

She opened the manuscript. Scrolled to Chapter Fourteen. Read the doorway line again.

*He had a way of standing in doorways as if he'd just arrived from somewhere better and might return there at any moment.*

"No," Celeste said aloud, to the empty office, to the river below, to the girl in the mail room twenty years gone. "You don't get to leave through the door this time."

She picked up her phone and dialed the number she'd sworn she'd never use again—the landline of the house on Gull Island, where her mother still answered on the third ring, every time, as if the intervening decades were a rumor she refused to confirm.

"Mother," Celeste said, when the familiar voice came. "It's me. Don't ask questions yet. Is there a room free this weekend?"

There was a pause, and then her mother said the thing she always said, the sentence that had shaped both daughters' lives in opposite directions:

"For you? There's always room. As long as you behave."

Celeste looked out at the Hudson, already turning gold with the late afternoon, already moving toward the sea.

"I'm never behaving," she said. "Book me on the morning boat."
