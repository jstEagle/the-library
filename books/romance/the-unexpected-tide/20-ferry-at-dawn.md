## 20. Ferry at Dawn

### Theo

The harbor at dawn was the color of hammered tin, and Theo Marsh had never been so afraid of a piece of paper in his life.

He'd rehearsed this. All night he'd rehearsed it, pacing the narrow bunk of the *Nellie Gray* while the storm's last breath rattled the halyards against the masts. He'd tried the speech out loud once and abandoned it—*Mara, I read your pages, I know what you did for me, I know what you gave up*—because every version sounded like either an accusation or a eulogy, and he wanted neither. He wanted something simpler than words. So he'd settled on the simplest thing he owned.

The dedication page.

It had taken him until three in the morning to get it right. Not the sentiment—that had been there since chapter one, since the first winter he'd spent rewriting his own life into fiction while the ferry engines ticked cold around him—but the name. For six years the manuscript had carried a dedication to no one, a blank line where a blank line belonged, because Theo Marsh did not hand his heart to people who might drop it. Last night he'd filled the line in by hand, in ballpoint, because the island's one printer had died with the power flicker and because some things should look like what they are: imperfect, ink-smudged, irreversible.

*Mara Eastwick,* it said. *Who saw the book before anyone else did.*

Which was true in more ways than she knew yet. She'd seen it before anyone else twice over—once as the stranger who'd bought his self-published novel from the dockside cart and left a review that changed nothing and everything, and once as the woman who had quietly, anonymously, without credit or complaint, rewritten his sentences into the versions the world fell in love with. He'd spent two days being furious about that. He'd spent last night understanding it. There was a difference, and the difference was her confession pages, still folded in his jacket pocket, soft as cloth now from handling.

*You were the only reader who ever mattered,* she'd written. *So I made sure the world read you right.*

He stood at the top of the dock ramp with the page inside his coat, watching the crowd assemble. The final crossing of the season had moved up to seven-forty, and half the island had turned out the way they always did for the last boat—not to leave, but to watch other people go, to stand in the wind and feel the season close over their heads like water. The Eastwicks were there in force: her mother in a camel coat too fine for salt air, her father silent beside her, her brother Grant with his perfect wife. And Julian Vane, standing slightly apart, hands in the pockets of a coat that cost more than Theo's truck, wearing the expression of a man who believed the ending was already written.

Theo knew better. He'd spent his whole career learning how endings worked. Endings weren't written; they were chosen, one line at a time, usually under deadline.

Seven-twelve. The *Nellie Gray*'s horn would sound at seven-thirty whether Mara came or not.

He checked the ramp again. Empty.

Then, up the hill road, a figure in a gray coat, walking fast, hair whipping loose in the wind—and behind her, calling her name in a voice pitched to carry, Julian Vane breaking from the crowd like a man who'd just realized the race had started without him.

### Mara

She had not slept. She had instead spent the night doing the hardest writing of her life, harder than the memoir, harder than the novel she'd buried in a drawer at twenty-six: she had written herself down. Every year she'd vanished. Every name she'd signed away. The letter to her family sat finished on the desk in the guest room, addressed and stamped, to be mailed after the boat left. The resignation from the Vane memoir sat beside it. And the third thing—the thing she hadn't managed to finish, the letter to Theo that the wind had stolen off the cliff path two days ago—she had rewritten from memory in the dark, and this time she wasn't going to lose it, because she was going to say it out loud instead.

Assuming he was at the dock. Assuming he hadn't already decided that a woman who built a man's career in secret and then lied about it was simply another tide that comes and goes.

Her mother had cornered her at breakfast with the co-credit offer Julian had floated at dinner—*a partnership, darling, think of your future*—and Mara had said no so cleanly that the silence afterward had its own weather. Her father, who had not spoken a full sentence to her in nine years, had looked up from his coffee and said, "Your grandmother kept a boat here, you know. Before any of you." It wasn't forgiveness. It was a door, left unlocked. She'd take it.

Now the hill road, the wind, the smell of diesel and brine rising from the harbor, and below her the whole town gathered at the rail like witnesses. She saw him before she let herself look for him: Theo Marsh, standing at the top of the ramp in his work jacket, hands empty—which meant no ticket, which meant he wasn't leaving, which meant—

Julian caught her arm halfway down the hill. "Mara. Wait."

She stopped. She didn't shake him off; that would be a scene, and she was done with scenes that weren't hers. "I'm catching the boat."

"I heard about the dedication." His voice dropped, went warm, went *old*, the voice from the library stacks at nineteen, the voice that had once been the whole music of her world. "Whatever he's offering you—a mechanic, Mara, a man you've known two weeks—you can't trade nine years of history for a stranger."

"You're right," she said, and watched surprise move through his face like light through water. "I can't. That's why I'm not trading anything. Nine years ago I chose a story where I disappeared so you could stay the hero. I'm done choosing stories. I'm choosing people." She gently freed her arm. "Goodbye, Julian. Write your own book."

She walked past him down the hill, past her mother's stricken face and her father's almost-smile, past the gulls wheeling and complaining overhead, and stopped in front of Theo Marsh.

Up close he looked like a man who hadn't slept either, and like a man who had decided something anyway. He pulled a single sheet of paper from inside his jacket—creased, ink-smudged, handwritten—and held it out flat on his palm between them.

Not a ticket. A dedication page.

Her own name, in careful ballpoint, sitting on the line where six years of blankness had been.

"Mara Eastwick," he said, loud enough that the dock could hear, loud enough that the wind had to fight for it. "Who saw the book before anyone else did. Who fixed my sentences when I couldn't see them straight. Who lied to me, and then told me the truth on paper because telling it out loud nearly killed her." His voice cracked and he let it. "I'm not asking you to get on this boat. I'm asking you to be in the book. Whatever we write next. Co-authorship. Your name first if you want it—I owe you that and about forty thousand dollars in uncredited royalties, which we can discuss on the crossing."

A laugh broke out of her, wet and sudden, and someone in the crowd—her father, God help her—actually chuckled.

"Theo." She took the page from his hand. Her name. Her name in his handwriting, offered in front of everyone, claimed in front of everyone. "I resigned from the memoir this morning. I mailed nothing. I burned nothing. I wrote my family a letter telling them every year I vanished and why, and it's on the desk upstairs, and I am never again going to be the ghost in anyone's story—including mine." She held up the dedication page so the crowd could see it, so her mother could see it, so Julian, standing rigid at the rail of the hill path, could see it. "This is the only credit I want."

The horn sounded. One long note rolling across the harbor, shaking the water, scattering the gulls.

Theo held out his hand—palm open, the old gesture from the dock two weeks ago, when she'd hired a stranger and gotten a whole life instead.

Mara Eastwick took it.

They walked down the ramp together, past the deckhand who touched his cap, past the rail where her family stood in a row like figures on a cake, and boarded the *Nellie Gray* as the lines came off the pilings. At the stern rail, wind tearing at them both, Mara slid the dedication page into her coat, over her heart, and Theo wrapped his arms around her from behind, his chin on her hair, and the island began to slide away.

On the dock, small in the morning light, Julian Vane turned and walked back up the hill alone.

Mara watched him go without flinching. Then she turned her face into Theo's shoulder and breathed salt and diesel and dawn.

"So," he said against her ear, as the harbor opened wide and blue ahead of them. "Chapter one. You start."
