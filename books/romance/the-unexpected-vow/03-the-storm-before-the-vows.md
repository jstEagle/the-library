## 3. The Storm Before the Vows

I had planned the sabotage the way I planned everything: in triplicate, with contingency columns.

The Whitmore-Hayes wedding, Saturday, four o'clock, the clifftop lawn at Gullwing Island. Two hundred guests. A bride who wanted "effortless" and a mother of the groom who wanted "flawless," which meant they wanted me. Ten years ago I would have said they wanted the best. Now I knew better. They wanted the name Jonah Delacroix had spent a decade rebuilding from the wreckage Marcus Vane left of it.

And Marcus Vane would be standing three feet away, in his venue, wearing his understated cashmere, taking his percentage.

Here was the beauty of it. A wedding planner controls everything and appears to control nothing. The seating chart. The processional order. The moment the string quartet starts and stops. I could arrange a small catastrophe of timing—candles unlit, the bride's grandmother stranded at the dock, the toast sequence scrambled so the drunk uncle spoke before the father—and every failure would land on the venue. On him. In front of the island's most connected guests, the ones whose phone numbers I'd been quietly collecting all season.

I had a folder on my laptop titled *Q4 Prospects.* It was really a list of witnesses.

I rehearsed it in the mirror of my cottage bathroom, the way I'd rehearsed the other sentence, the one from the letters. *I forgive you, and I forgive myself.* Ten years I'd carried that sentence like a stone in my shoe. This week I'd decided I was done carrying it. Justice was lighter. Justice fit better.

The storm came in Thursday night, ahead of schedule, like everything Marcus did.

* * *

By Friday morning the ferry authority had suspended service. The bride's family was already on the island—forty of them, installed in the guesthouses—but the groom's side, a hundred and sixty guests, was scattered across the mainland, watching the radar turn the color of a bruise. The forecast said the front would break Saturday at dawn. The forecast also said a lot of things.

Marcus called an all-hands in the great hall at seven a.m. I stood at the back with my clipboard like a weapon I hadn't decided to fire.

"Here's what we know," he said. No slides, no hedging. "The front breaks early. First ferry runs at six, weather permitting. That gives us ten hours to move a four o'clock ceremony to noon and get a hundred and sixty people fed, seated, and married before the second front hits at five." He looked around the room, and his eyes passed over me the way they always did—carefully, like I was something breakable or something dangerous, he hadn't decided which. "Jonah runs the day. Nobody touches her timeline. If we lose the wedding, we lose the season. Questions?"

I had questions. I had a folder full of them.

But what I said was, "The florist's cooler is on the mainland with the peonies."

"Then we find peonies on this island," he said, "or we make the bride fall in love with something else before she knows she's grieving."

And that was the problem with working beside Marcus Vane in a crisis. He was good. He was so good it made my teeth ache, the way he moved through the hall delegating without raising his voice, the way he'd memorized the generator locations and the drainage grades and which staff member had a sister who worked at the mainland flower market.

My sabotage required a functioning schedule. The storm had eaten it.

* * *

We worked through Friday like people digging out of snow. I rebuilt the timeline twice. He stood next to me at the long table in the planning office, close enough that I could smell rain on his sweater, and when I said "the string quartet can't make the six o'clock boat," he said "there's a pianist at the tavern who played at my sister's wedding," and picked up the phone.

At eleven that night the power flickered and died. The backup generator caught the kitchen and the great hall and left the guesthouses dark, and forty relatives of the bride descended on the lit building like moths, frightened and loud, and I watched Marcus hand his own dinner—actually hand the plate, mid-bite—to a panicking groomsman's mother and say, "Eat. You'll think better."

"You haven't eaten either," I said, when the room emptied.

"I'll eat when the wedding's done." He gathered plates without being asked. "You should sleep."

"I don't sleep during events."

"I know," he said. "I remember."

I let that sit between us like a knife on a table. He didn't flinch. That was the other problem with Marcus. He never flinched, which meant I never got the satisfaction.

* * *

At two a.m. the rain came sideways and we were in the boathouse securing the ceremony chairs, forty of them stacked and strapped, when he said it.

"The letter you wrote. About the garden your mother kept. The one you lost."

My hands went still on the strap.

"You wrote that losing it taught you that nothing you love is safe unless you stand over it every minute," he said. "I've thought about that sentence for two months."

"You don't get to quote my letters back at me."

"No," he agreed. "I don't."

"Then why did you keep writing?" The rain hammered the roof. "You knew by letter two. You've admitted it. You knew, and you kept—what, collecting material? Studying the enemy?"

He set down the chair he was holding. In the lantern light he looked older than his reputation, tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

"Because it was the only honest conversation I've had in six years," he said. "Because every other person on this island talks to the version of me that owns the venue. And because—" he stopped, and I watched a man who never hesitated hesitate. "Because you were the one person whose disappointment I couldn't buy my way out of. I wanted to know what that felt like. From the other side."

"You destroyed my firm."

"I know."

"You stood in that ballroom and told the investors my accounts were inflated. You watched them pull out in a week. You took my clients, my staff, my name."

"I know what I did, Jonah. I've known every day since." He picked up another chair, set it on the stack, aligned it with a precision that looked like prayer. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm telling you the man who did that is the man who's been writing to you, and you can decide what that's worth. Nothing. It can be worth nothing. But don't decide tonight. Tonight we have a wedding."

I strapped the last chair and said nothing, and hated how much of my hatred had quietly stopped being load-bearing.

* * *

Saturday broke clean and cold, the sky wrung out and rinsed. The six o'clock ferry came through with the first light, and a hundred and sixty guests poured onto the dock into a machine we'd built in the dark. The tavern pianist played the processional. The peonies never came; I put the bride in armfuls of island gorse and wild roses, and she cried, and her mother said it was better than the original plan, and I let Marcus hear her say it.

At the reception, the caterer's lead came to me, frantic—the kitchen had lost the seating cards in the shuffle. Marcus was already there. "My team's fault," he said, loud enough for the nearby tables. "We'll have it fixed in ten minutes, and dessert's on the house." It wasn't his team. It was my binder, my shuffle, my error. He took the blame like it was a coat he'd been waiting to wear.

I found him afterward at the edge of the lawn, watching the water where the second front was already stacking up on the horizon, gray and patient.

"You gave them my mistake," I said.

"They'll remember the wedding was perfect," he said. "Let them remember whose it was."

"Marcus." My voice came out strange. "The toast sequence. The candles. The grandmother at the dock. I had it all planned. I was going to burn your season down in front of every guest you have."

He nodded slowly, like I'd confirmed something he'd already read in my handwriting.

"I know," he said. "The folder's named Q4 Prospects. You left it open Tuesday."

The wind came up off the water, cold and honest. Somewhere behind us the last of the guests were dancing to a pianist who'd learned the first dance that morning.

"The last ferry is tomorrow at four," he said. "I'll be on it. You can decide what my letters were worth before then, or you can decide they were worth nothing, and I'll spend the rest of my life knowing what I traded." He turned to go, then stopped. "The bride danced to the gorse, Jonah. That was yours. All of today was yours. I just want you to know that someone knows."

He walked back toward the hall, and I stood at the edge of the lawn with my rehearsed sentence and my abandoned sabotage and ten years of justice, watching the storm come in over the water—and understanding, with a clarity that felt like drowning, that tomorrow at four o'clock I would be at that dock, and I did not yet know which version of me would be standing there.
