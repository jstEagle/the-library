## 3. A Date at the Bakery

The Kavanagh reunion had a smell, Quentin decided somewhere around hour two: sunscreen and potato salad and the faintly desperate sweetness of aunts pretending not to interrogate you.

She had stationed herself at the far end of the picnic shelter at Gull Point Park, as far from the folding tables of deviled eggs as the geometry allowed, holding a paper cup of lemonade like a shield. Below the bluff, Port Alder did its slow Saturday work — fishing boats nosing out past the breakwater, gulls wheeling over the cannery ruins, the whole town looking like it had been left out in the weather for forty years and had made its peace with that. It was, she had to admit, beautiful in the way that tired things are beautiful. She was in no mood to admit it.

"Quentin! There you are."

Her aunt Denise materialized with the inevitability of tide, dragging behind her a tall man in a linen shirt that said *I own a boat* even though everyone knew he rented it by the hour.

"This is Gerald — Denise's friend's son — he's in *finance*," Denise said, pronouncing the word like a benediction. "Gerald, this is my niece Quentin. She plans weddings. Big ones. Portland, Seattle."

"Weddings," Gerald repeated, with the flat enthusiasm of a man reading his own obituary. "That must be... rewarding."

"It's logistics," Quentin said. "With cake."

"Well," Denise said brightly, and then did the thing Quentin had been dreading all week, the thing that had a runway and a flight plan and was now beginning its descent. "And how are things with you otherwise? Still on your own?"

*Still on your own.* Four syllables, delivered with the gentle cruelty only family can manage, implying that Quentin Kavanagh was a house left vacant, a garden gone to seed, a problem someone else would eventually have to solve.

"I'm seeing someone, actually."

The words came out of her mouth before any part of her brain authorized them. She felt them leave — heard them hang in the salt air between the three of them — and understood, with the detached clarity of a woman watching her own car roll into the harbor, that there was no taking them back.

Denise's face performed a small sunrise. "You're *what?* Quentin! Why didn't you say anything? Who is she? Is it serious? When do we meet—"

"She's — local," Quentin said, because apparently her mouth had gone rogue and intended to see this through. "She lives here. In town. We've been — it's fairly new. But serious." She took a long drink of lemonade. It did nothing. "Actually, I'm meeting her right now. At her shop. So if you'll excuse me—"

"At her *shop*?" Denise leaned in, delighted, predatory. "What kind of shop?"

Quentin looked down the hill toward Front Street, where the shops of Port Alder stood in their crooked row, and seized on the first lit window she could see. Warm light. Gold lettering. The smell, even from here, of bread.

"Bakery," she said. "She owns a bakery. On Front Street. I have to go, we're — she's expecting me."

She was moving before Denise could formulate a follow-up question, cup abandoned on the bench, Gerald's baffled wave unanswered behind her. Her heart was doing something embarrassing against her ribs. Five years of careful, professional composure, undone by one aunt with a casserole dish and a radar for loneliness.

*You're seeing someone.*

Well. As of eleven minutes ago, she supposed she was. She just hadn't met him yet. Or her. Whoever owned the bakery.

* * *

The bell over the door announced her with a cheerful jangle that felt like mockery.

Inside, Vega's — the gold letters read *Vega's*, hand-painted, slightly flaking — was small and warm and smelled so profoundly of butter and yeast and cinnamon that Quentin's shoulders dropped half an inch without her permission. Rattan baskets of rolls lined one wall. A chalkboard menu listed things in looping handwriting: *cardamom knots, morning buns, sourdough (ask about the rye)*. Behind the counter, a woman with dark hair escaping a low bun was sliding a tray of something golden out of the oven, and the sight of her — flour dusted along one forearm, sleeves pushed up, an expression of pure concentration — hit Quentin with an absurd, unwelcome pang of *oh no*.

Because the woman was beautiful, and Quentin had just lied to her entire extended family about being in love with her.

"Hi," the woman said, glancing up. Her voice was warm, unhurried, with an accent that softened her vowels. "You look like you're running from something."

"That obvious?"

"You came in like the door owed you money." The woman set down the tray. Up close her eyes were dark brown, nearly black, ringed with the fine creases of someone who smiled often whether or not she meant to. "We've got cardamom knots fresh out. Those fix most emergencies. What's yours?"

Quentin opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked back through the front window, where she could see, halfway down the block, Denise's sun hat bobbing in her direction with terrible purpose, Gerald trailing behind like a kite in a headwind.

"Okay," Quentin said. "I'm going to tell you something insane, and I need you to understand that I know it's insane, and I would never ask this under normal circumstances, but my aunt is currently crossing the street and I panicked and told her I have a girlfriend who owns a bakery on Front Street, and yours is the only bakery on Front Street, and I am begging you — professionally, desperately — to be my girlfriend for approximately four minutes."

Silence. Somewhere in the back, an oven ticked. A fan hummed.

The woman blinked once. Then, horribly, delightfully, she began to smile — not a polite customer-service smile but a real one, crooked, spreading slowly like warmth through dough.

"Four minutes," she repeated.

"Maybe five. Denise asks follow-up questions."

"And what's my name in this scenario? Or should I just make something up? Because I feel like I'd commit harder than you'd expect."

"You'd — wait, really?" Quentin gripped the edge of the counter. "You'd actually do this?"

"I closed out my register twenty minutes ago. My afternoon plans were inventory and a radio drama about lighthouse keepers." She wiped her hands on her apron, unhurried, as if strangers requested fake relationships daily. "This is better than the lighthouse keepers. Marisol," she added, extending a flour-dusted hand. "Marisol Vega. Your girlfriend, allegedly."

"Quentin Kavanagh." Her hand was caught in a grip that was warm and firm and lasted exactly one beat longer than a stranger's handshake should. "I want to state for the record that I am not a crazy person. I'm a wedding planner. Lying is basically my job description — I tell brides their mother-in-law's seating chart is *fine*."

"A wedding planner who can't plan her way past one aunt."

"My aunt is a special case. She once got a funeral moved because it conflicted with her timeshare week."

Marisol laughed — a quick, surprised sound, hand coming up to cover her mouth — and Quentin felt something in her chest loosen that had been tight since the ferry dock. Which was dangerous. She filed that away to worry about later.

The bell jangled. Denise swept in with Gerald, bringing a gust of heat and triumph.

"There you are! Oh — *look* at this place. Smells like heaven." Denise's eyes went from Quentin to Marisol and back again, doing arithmetic. "And you must be—"

"Marisol." She came around the counter without hesitation, wiping her hand first, and offered it. "You must be family. Quentin talks about you."

"*Does* she?" Denise clasped both of Marisol's hands like a woman receiving a blessing. "Only good things, I hope."

"Mostly that you once got a funeral rescheduled."

Denise crowed with laughter. Gerald, sensing his role was decorative, drifted toward the pastry case with the grateful silence of a man finally given instructions.

"How did you two meet?" Denise asked, and Quentin's stomach dropped — because she hadn't prepared a story, hadn't prepared anything, she'd had four minutes' notice—

"The farmers market," Marisol said smoothly. "Last month. She bought the last of my apricots and tried to pay me double so I'd give up the second basket. I said no. She came back the next week and bought the whole stand."

"I did not buy the whole stand," Quentin said, before she could stop herself, and then realized she was arguing details of a fiction and pressed her lips together.

"She haggled," Marisol confirmed, eyes dancing. "Very aggressively. For fruit she didn't even want. I thought, this woman has never lost anything in her life and it's making her unbearable. And then she apologized with a coffee and stayed until closing talking about table linens, and here we are."

It was seamless. It was infuriatingly seamless. Quentin watched her aunt melt — actually melt, shoulders softening, the matchmaking machinery visibly standing down — and understood that she had stumbled into the company of either a gifted liar or a very good improviser, and couldn't decide which possibility alarmed her more.

"Well," Denise breathed. "Well. Gerald, aren't they lovely together? You can just *tell*. How long?"

"About six weeks," Marisol said, at the same moment Quentin said, "A month," and the discrepancy hung in the air for one horrible second before Marisol covered it without blinking: "Six weeks since the market, but we didn't call it anything for the first two. She's cautious." She said it fondly, teasingly, her shoulder a warm inch from Quentin's, and Quentin thought: *she's good. She's terrifyingly good.*

"Cautious," Denise repeated, charmed beyond salvation. "Oh, honey, don't be cautious. Life's short. Look at this town — you blink and the cannery's gone." She patted the counter. "Marisol, sweetheart, you'll come to the clambake Thursday? Family thing, but family's whoever you feed, that's our rule."

"I—" Quentin started.

"We'd love to," Marisol said.

They stood in the doorway watching Denise and Gerald recede down Front Street, Denise already turning to speak to the Hendersons outside the hardware store, no doubt, the news propagating outward like rings in water. By sundown all of Port Alder would know. By Thursday, the entire Kavanagh clan.

"Six weeks," Quentin said faintly.

"You said a month. I was correcting your timeline. Couples argue about their timeline; it makes us seem real." Marisol turned, leaning back against the counter, arms folded, studying Quentin with open amusement. "So. Wedding planner. Fake girlfriend. Do you want to explain what that was, or should I just enjoy the mystery?"

Quentin exhaled, long and slow, and told the truth — most of it. The reunion. The questions. The five years of *still on your own*, delivered at every Thanksgiving and christening and random Tuesday phone call, each one landing on the same bruise. She kept the specifics vague — the engagement, the collapse, the reason she no longer believed in any of it — but she gave Marisol the shape of it, and Marisol listened the way few people listened: completely, without filling the silences, flour still on her forearm, the ovens ticking behind her.

"So when she asked," Quentin finished, "my mouth just — went. And then I saw your window."

"My window."

"It was lit. It looked friendly. It was the closest building."

"I'm a strategy now. A lit window." But she was smiling again, that crooked smile, shaking her head. "You know most people just say they're busy."

"Have you met Denise? Busy is a challenge. Busy means she sends Gerald to your house." Quentin rubbed her eyes. "Look — I will fix this. I'll tell her tomorrow it ended. Amicably. Long distance. Something dignified."

"Or," Marisol said, and paused, and Quentin watched her weigh something, watch her decide, "you could not do that."

"...Come again?"

"The clambake. Thursday." Marisol untied her apron, folded it over the counter, casual as anything, though a faint color had risen in her cheeks that Quentin chose not to examine. "My aunts are worse than yours, believe it or not. Three of them. They run a rotating interrogation out of my kitchen every Sunday and the subject is always why I'm alone, and I have run out of creative answers. If you showed up — well. Two birds. One very elaborate lie."

Quentin stared at her. "You want to *continue* this."

"I want Thursday's clambake to be survivable. You want Thursday's clambake to be survivable. I fail to see the problem."

"The problem is that this is insane."

"Mm." Marisol reached into the case, pulled out a cardamom knot, golden and glistening, and set it on a plate between them like a treaty. "Eat this and tell me it's insane."

Quentin ate it. It was, objectively, transcendent — layers of butter and cardamom sugar pulling apart in ribbons — and she hated how much it proved.

"It's insane," she said, with her mouth full.

"Your face says otherwise."

"My face says a lot of unprofessional things right now." She set down the plate. Outside, the afternoon had gone gold; a fishing boat was coming in past the breakwater, and the light on Front Street made everything look like a memory of itself. "Okay. Thursday. But we need rules. Ground rules. This is a contract situation, and I don't enter contracts blind anymore."

That landed strangely — she saw it land, saw something flicker behind Marisol's eyes, some private recognition — but Marisol only nodded. "Rules. Fine. Rule one: you buy something every time you come in. I run a business, not a cover operation."

"Easily agreed. Rule two: we agree on our story beforehand. No more timeline discrepancies."

"Rule three," Marisol said, and her voice shifted, lighter on the surface, something more careful underneath, "nobody gets hurt. That's the real rule. Whatever this is, it ends clean. Nobody gets hurt."

Something in the way she said it made Quentin think Marisol wasn't only talking about the lie.

"Nobody gets hurt," Quentin agreed.

They shook on it, flour and all, and the handshake lasted that same beat too long again, and neither of them mentioned it.

Quentin bought six cardamom knots — *rule one*, Marisol reminded her, ringing her up with theatrical gravity — and pushed open the door into the salt-bright afternoon. Halfway down the block she stopped, turned back. Through the window she could see Marisol watching her, and when their eyes met, Marisol raised one eyebrow, unashamed, and went back to her trays.

Quentin walked home carrying a warm paper bag and the distinct, alarming feeling that she had just signed something far more binding than any contract she'd ever drafted.

Behind her, the bell over the bakery door chimed once, cheerfully, like a witness.
