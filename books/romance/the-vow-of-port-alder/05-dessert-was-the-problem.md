## 5. Dessert Was the Problem

The Kavanagh family reunion dinner was held every year at the Anchorage Grange Hall, a building that smelled permanently of cedar shavings and decades of pot roast, and which had hosted exactly three kinds of Kavanagh events: reunions, funerals, and the occasional christening that doubled as both. Quentin stood at the entrance with Marisol's hand in hers and felt the particular dread of a woman walking into a room where forty people already knew her entire biography — including the parts that weren't true.

"You're doing the thing," Marisol murmured beside her. She wore a dress the color of late-summer plums, something soft and unhurried, and she had flour on her wrist that she either hadn't noticed or hadn't cared to remove. "The jaw thing. You're clenching like you're cracking walnuts."

"I'm preparing for combat."

"With your mother."

"My mother is the general. The aunts are the cavalry. Uncle Dennis asks questions that are technically about weather but are actually interrogations." Quentin exhaled through her nose. "You can still leave. There's a side door. I won't tell anyone you escaped."

Marisol squeezed her hand once, brief and warm, and did not let go. "I've survived dock strikes and a grease fire in my own kitchen. I think I can handle aunts."

Something in Quentin's chest performed a small, unauthorized maneuver. She ignored it.

* * *

They made it eleven minutes before the questions started.

The first came from Aunt Colleen, who approached with the smiling menace of a woman who had been waiting all evening. "So! Marisol, was it? And how did you two meet?" She said *you two* the way other people said *exhibit A*.

"The farmers market," Marisol said instantly, without so much as a glance at Quentin for confirmation. "She bought the last of my lemon tarts and then argued with me about whether they were worth six dollars."

"They were worth four," Quentin said.

"She paid six."

"Under protest."

Aunt Colleen's eyes narrowed with delight. "And how long has this been —"

"Three months," Quentin said, and was startled by how easily the number arrived, as though it had been waiting in her mouth. Three months felt right. Three months was long enough to be serious, short enough to explain why nobody had heard. Three months was a lie with excellent structural integrity.

Across the hall, her mother, Diane Kavanagh, stood very still near the punch bowl, watching them with the expression of a woman witnessing a solar eclipse — rare, luminous, and not to be interrupted.

The evening unfolded from there with an ease that Quentin found increasingly suspicious. They told the story of the market again to Uncle Dennis, who asked his weather-question — *"So Marisol, you planning on staying in Port Alder through the winter?"* — and Marisol answered honestly, which somehow made the whole fiction sturdier. They were cornered by Cousin Bethany's twin daughters, aged nine, who demanded to know if Marisol would make their birthday cakes, and Marisol crouched down to their level and negotiated terms with the gravity of a treaty. Quentin watched her do it and forgot, for nearly a full minute, that any of this was performance.

That was the problem. That was the whole problem, and it arrived quietly, somewhere between the salad course and the toasts: pretending had stopped feeling like pretending.

It happened in small ways. When Marisol laughed at Uncle Ray's terrible joke about the fishing fleet, her knee pressed against Quentin's under the table and stayed there, and neither of them moved it. When Diane asked Marisol what her family thought of Quentin, Marisol said, "My mother would have adored her," in a voice so unguarded that Quentin's throat went tight, because she knew — from the bakery, from nothing Marisol had ever explained but everything she'd failed to — that Marisol's mother was gone. She reached under the table without deciding to and found Marisol's hand already open, waiting.

Diane saw that. Of course Diane saw that. Diane saw everything; it was her superpower and her crime.

* * *

The dessert course was, as always at Kavanagh functions, a potluck catastrophe of ambition — seven pies, two trifle dishes, and a gelatin mold that had been appearing at reunions since 1987 like a cursed heirloom. Quentin had brought nothing, being a guest of strategic insignificance, but Marisol had arrived that afternoon with a single box, and when she opened it now, the table went quiet.

Inside were twelve small pastries, each one different — a tiny custard tart glazed to look like sea glass, a chocolate thing dusted with cocoa shaped like a wave curling, a lemon square with a candied peel curled on top like a question mark.

"You *made* these?" Aunt Colleen demanded. "Today? While also working?"

"They're just leftovers," Marisol said, which was transparently false, because nobody had leftovers that looked like this.

Quentin took one bite of the custard tart and had to set down her fork and stare out the window at the parking lot for a moment, composing herself, because it tasted like someone had decided, deliberately, that she deserved something beautiful.

"Dessert was the problem," her mother announced later, appearing at Quentin's elbow while Marisol was mobbed at the far end of the table by relatives suddenly interested in ordering cakes for events six months out. Diane held a napkin with crumbs on it and the expression of a woman about to say something devastating. "You know what I used to say, when you were little? Dessert was always the problem with you. You'd sit through the whole dinner being polite, being careful, and then the second the pie came out, there you were. All of you. No armor."

"Mom."

"I'm just saying." Diane looked across the room at Marisol, who was laughing at something Uncle Ray had said, her head tipped back, one hand resting on Quentin's shoulder without either of them noticing it had landed there. "I haven't seen you eat dessert like that since Daniel."

The name landed like a dropped glass. Quentin kept her face still through long practice. "Mom, don't."

"I'm not saying anything. I'm saying you're laughing. I'm saying I haven't seen you laugh at a dinner table in five years, and I know whose fault I decided that was, and maybe I decided wrong." Diane smoothed her napkin, folded it, refolded it. Her voice, when it came again, was smaller. "She looks at you like you're real, Quentin. Not like a project. Not like a disappointment. Like you're real."

Quentin's chest did the unauthorized maneuver again, harder this time. "We've only been together three months."

"Mm," said Diane, who had raised four children and believed nothing any of them said about relationships, ever. "Three months." She patted Quentin's arm and drifted away toward the gelatin mold, leaving Quentin standing in the wreckage of a sentence she couldn't parse.

* * *

By ten o'clock the hall was emptying, folding chairs screeching into stacks, cousins herding children toward cars. Quentin found Marisol outside on the gravel lot under the one flickering sodium lamp, holding the empty pastry box against her hip like a shield.

"Your family," Marisol said, "is a lot."

"I warned you."

"You warned me about interrogation. You didn't warn me that your aunt would cry actual tears over a lemon square." She shook her head, but she was smiling, that crooked half-smile that Quentin had catalogued without permission sometime during week one of the arrangement. "And your mother hugged me. Twice. The second time wasn't even a greeting hug, it was a — a *meaning* hug."

"Yeah." Quentin shoved her hands in her jacket pockets. The night off the water was cold and smelled of salt and cut grass. "About that. My mother thinks we're — she got ideas. From tonight."

"What kind of ideas?"

"The kind where she starts talking about Thanksgiving. And possibly Christmas. And possibly whether the grange hall is available in June." Quentin heard her own voice go light and brittle and hated it. "Which is fine. This was one dinner. We did the job. You can walk away clean, tell everyone it fizzled, nobody blames anyone for a three-month thing that ends quietly—"

"Quentin."

"—and I'll handle my mother, and you'll get your evenings back, and—"

"*Quentin.*" Marisol stepped closer, into the bad yellow light, and Quentin shut up, because there was flour still on her wrist and her eyes were doing something complicated and Quentin had spent the entire evening not looking directly at it. "Did any part of tonight feel fake to you?"

The question hung there between them, dangerous as wet paint.

Because here was the truth Quentin had been avoiding since the farmers market, since the moment a stranger agreed to hold her hand and it fit: nothing had felt fake. Not the knee under the table. Not the way Marisol had said *my mother would have adored her*. Not the custard tart, or the laughter that arrived before Quentin could vet it, or the fact that she'd caught herself, twice, mid-evening, forgetting to perform. Somewhere between the salad and the pie, the arrangement had quietly stopped being an arrangement, and Quentin had no idea when it happened, only that she'd been the last to notice.

"No," she said finally. Quietly. "No part of it."

Marisol nodded slowly, looking at the box in her arms instead of at Quentin. "Okay," she said. "Then here's my problem. If I walk away tomorrow, your mother cries, your aunt Colleen interrogates me for the rest of my natural life, and I lose the only regulars I have on the north side of town. But if I keep pretending—" She stopped. Started again. "If I keep pretending, I have to keep having nights like tonight. And I don't know how to pretend to have nights like tonight. They don't make those."

The sodium lamp buzzed overhead. Somewhere down the hill, the ocean kept its old argument with the shore.

"So what are you saying?" Quentin asked, and her voice came out rougher than she intended.

Marisol lifted her head. In the flickering light she looked, for a moment, entirely unguarded — the same woman who'd handed a panicking stranger a lemon tart and played along without asking why. "I'm saying the wedding is in three weeks. Your whole family will be here. And I'm saying that if we're going to keep doing this, we should probably talk terms. Real ones. Because I don't think I can survive another dinner like that one by improvising."

Quentin stared at her. Terms. A contract. God help her, a *negotiation* — the one language she'd always been fluent in, offered now by a woman holding an empty pastry box under a dying streetlight, and every professional instinct in Quentin's body said *walk away, this is how people get hurt*, and every other part of her — the part that had eaten dessert without armor for the first time in five years — said something else entirely.

"Okay," Quentin heard herself say. "Come by the bakery tomorrow. We'll talk terms."

Marisol smiled — small, wary, real — and turned toward her car, and Quentin stood alone in the parking lot realizing she had just agreed to extend a lie she no longer wanted to end, with a woman she was beginning to want too much, and that the terms of engagement, whatever they turned out to be, were going to be the most dangerous contract she'd ever signed.
