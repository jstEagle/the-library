## 18. The Archive Room

The county records office smelled of dust and cold coffee and the particular despair of paper that no one had loved in decades. It occupied the basement of the Port Alder municipal building — a low-ceilinged room lit by humming fluorescent tubes, where the light fell in flat gray sheets over rows of green metal shelving. Somewhere above, a radiator knocked like a polite ghost.

Quentin had been there since eight-fifteen in the morning.

She sat at a scarred oak table with a legal pad, two sharpened pencils she wasn't using because everything was on her phone now, and a growing conviction that she was either about to find something or about to waste the one free morning she'd had in eleven days. The wedding was nine days out. The lease deadline was twelve. And Corliss Hume's eviction notice — served early, citing some technicality about "structural noncompliance" that Marisol's lawyer said was flimsy but not *nothing* — hung over Larkspur Lane like weather coming in off the water.

*Chain of title,* her lawyer had said, in the tired tone of a man billing at small-town rates. *If you want to fight it, you'd have to go back to the original deed. 1998. Hume's company claims they acquired the whole block from Alder Point Holdings. If that chain is clean, we're done. If it isn't—*

He hadn't finished the sentence. He didn't have to. Quentin had spent five years learning exactly what happened when documents weren't clean, and who paid the price when nobody looked closely enough to see it.

She pulled the deed book for 1998 off the shelf. It was heavier than it looked — a bound volume the size of a paving stone, its spine cracked, its pages the color of weak tea. She carried it back to the table with both arms and set it down with a thump that made the clerk at the front desk glance up over his reading glasses, decide she was harmless, and return to his crossword.

"Book forty-one," she murmured to herself. "Alder Point Holdings. Come on."

Her phone buzzed against the table. A text from Marisol: **Theo says you're "doing lawyer stuff." Should I be worried?**

She typed back: **Doing research. Don't feed him anything with more than three ingredients before noon.**

**Too late. He found the ganache.**

Quentin smiled at the screen longer than she meant to. That was the trouble with Marisol Vega — she made smiling feel involuntary, like breathing, like something the body did without asking permission first. Quentin put the phone face-down and opened the book.

* * *

The records were organized by instrument type, then by date, then by a filing system that appeared to have been designed by someone who hated both order and humanity. Deeds were in the red-tabbed section. Quentin worked backward from the current year, pulling the chain link by link: Hume Development Group to Marisol's landlord entity — a shell called Larkspur Properties LLC, which was itself owned by another shell, which was owned by Hume Development Group again, a circle so tidy it was almost elegant. Then Larkspur Properties LLC from Alder Point Holdings, 2011. Then Alder Point Holdings from—

There. Book forty-one, page 214.

*Warranty Deed. Alder Point Holdings LLC, grantor. Corliss R. Hume, trustee, grantee. Dated March 12, 1998.*

Quentin read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way she used to read contracts back when she still believed contracts meant what they said. Her old mentor, Diane Okafor, had taught her that trick — *read it three times, sweetheart. Once for the deal, once for the words, once for what the words are hiding.* Diane had lost her firm to Hume too. Everyone had lost something to Hume.

The deed granted Hume, as trustee, the full waterfront parcel — the bakery block, the fish cannery building, the old chandlery, all of it — "together with all appurtenances." Signed by the grantor's authorized representative. Notarized by one D. Whitcomb, Notary Public. Two witness signatures beneath.

It looked right. It looked completely, boringly right, which was the first thing that bothered her, because nothing involving Corliss Hume had ever once been boring or right.

Quentin photographed every page with her phone, then went back to the shelf and pulled the index volume for 1998, cross-referencing the recording number stamped in the margin. And that was where the morning turned.

The index entry for instrument 98-4471 listed the document type as *Warranty Deed — 2 pp.* Two pages. But the document in book forty-one was a single page, followed immediately by a different instrument entirely — a mortgage satisfaction for a property on the other side of town, recorded three days later. No second page. No continuation sheet. Nothing describing the metes and bounds of the parcel, nothing itemizing the "appurtenances," nothing with the grantor's second signature block.

Deeds transferring parcels that size ran four to six pages minimum. She knew because she'd drafted enough of them during the brief, miserable year she'd spent doing real estate paralegal work before weddings swallowed her career whole. A one-page warranty deed for a multi-structure waterfront parcel wasn't just unusual. It was impossible.

Unless someone had removed the second page.

Quentin sat very still. The fluorescent tube above her flickered, steadied, flickered again. In the silence she could hear the radiator knocking upstairs, the clerk's pen scratching through his crossword, her own heartbeat doing something unprofessional in her ears.

*Okay,* she told herself. *One missing page is an anomaly. Anomalies happen. Pages get misfiled, books get rebound badly, clerks make mistakes. It proves nothing by itself.*

But anomalies, she had learned five years ago in the wreckage of her old life, came in patterns. And Hume's forgeries had always lived in the details — the signature that was almost right, the date that was almost plausible, the clause that was almost standard. He was a man who built his crimes out of ninety-seven percent truth, because he understood that people checked the dramatic things and trusted the boring ones.

So Quentin did what Diane Okafor had trained her to do. She stopped looking at the deal and started looking at the words.

She compared the signatures. The grantor's representative on the 1998 deed had signed as *"R. Castellanos, Auth. Rep., Alder Point Holdings LLC."* Fine. Except Quentin remembered — distantly, from the research she'd done on Hume years ago, back when she'd still been trying to prove what he'd done to her own firm — that Alder Point Holdings hadn't existed as an LLC until its articles of organization were filed in June of 1998. Three months *after* this deed was signed. Before that, the same principals had operated as a general partnership under a different name entirely.

An authorized representative signing for an entity that did not yet legally exist.

Her hands had gone cold. She pressed them flat against the tea-colored page and made herself keep going, because hope was a dangerous thing and she wanted to test every inch of it before she let it in the door.

The notary. *D. Whitcomb.* Quentin pulled the notary commission registers — the county kept them, bound in blue, indexed by name — and found Dolores Whitcomb, commissioned 1989, expired 2003. Legitimate. But the commission number written on the deed didn't match the number in the register. Off by two digits. Transposed, maybe. Or invented.

And the witnesses. Two names, signed in ink so similar in pressure and slant that they might have been written by the same hand within the same minute. Quentin photographed them, zoomed in, held the image beside the notary's signature. The *R*s matched. The terminal strokes matched. The way the pen lifted at the end of each word — identical.

Three signatures. One hand.

Quentin leaned back in the chair and let out a breath she felt like she'd been holding since chapter one of her life, the bad chapter, the one that started with a forged contract and ended with Daniel packing his things and her mother asking, gently, terribly, *are you sure you're remembering it correctly?*

She wasn't sure. She had never been sure. That had been the cruelest part — not the loss itself but the doubt, the way Hume's forgery had been good enough, plausible enough, that everyone around her had eventually chosen the simpler story: that Quentin Kavanagh was careless, or vengeful, or broken. That she'd misread the documents. That she'd ruined her own engagement and blamed a powerful man because blaming him hurt less than the truth.

But this — this wasn't memory. This wasn't her word against his. This was a bound public record, sitting in a basement, waiting twenty-six years for someone to count the pages.

*Hope with sharp edges,* she thought. Because even now, even with her pulse hammering, she could hear the counterarguments lining up like opposing counsel. *A missing page isn't proof of fraud. Signatures degrade. Clerks err. And Hume has had twenty-six years and unlimited money to make any challenge disappear. You tried this once before, Quentin. You know how it ends.*

Except last time she'd been alone, and broke, and twenty-eight, and fighting for a firm that was already dead. This time she had a lawyer who billed small-town rates and cared anyway. She had Edda's ledger and half the waterfront's buyout letters. She had Father Bram, who had gone quiet and strange ever since the night at the bakery when Quentin had shown him the deed copy and asked, casually, if he remembered anything about the 1998 sale — and Bram had looked at her with an expression she couldn't name and said only, *Come see me Sunday.*

This time she had a boy in a storeroom who could find anything on the internet, and a woman with flour on her forearms who believed her — instantly, without evidence, without hesitation — and that, more than any document, was the thing that kept her in the chair instead of running for the door.

Her phone buzzed again. Marisol: **Ganache situation escalating. Also Priya called. Sounds upset? Said she needs to talk to you today.**

Quentin stared at the message. Priya, upset, was a problem for the version of Quentin who still had a functioning heart and a schedule. Right now she had neither. She typed: **Tell her I'll call tonight. Promise.** Then she turned back to the book, because the sharp-edged hope had teeth now, and it wanted more.

* * *

By noon she had filled six pages of the legal pad and photographed forty-one documents. She'd traced the transposed notary commission through two subsequent Hume transactions — 2004, 2016 — finding the same wrong number each time, which meant it wasn't a clerical slip; it was a habit. Forgers have habits. They repeat their comfortable lies the way anyone repeats a comfortable lie, until the lie becomes a fingerprint.

She'd also found the recording stamp anomaly: the 1998 deed bore a received stamp dated March 9th, but the notary acknowledgment was executed March 12th. The document had been stamped into the record three days before it was legally signed. Impossible — unless the stamp was applied later and backdated, or unless whoever prepared the package simply wasn't careful, because why would they need to be? Who in Port Alder, in 1998, was going to audit Corliss Hume?

Nobody. That was the answer. Nobody had. For twenty-six years, nobody had.

The clerk wandered over around one o'clock, drawn perhaps by the smell of obsession, and peered at her table with mild interest. "You want copies? Copier takes quarters."

"I'll take everything," Quentin said. "Every page I've flagged. Certified copies, if you can do it."

"Certified's a dollar a page and a form." He squinted at the open deed book. "Property dispute?"

"Something like that." She hesitated, then heard herself ask, "Has anyone else pulled this book recently? Book forty-one?"

He scratched his jaw. "Hume's people come through every few years. Big fella, gray suit, always acts like the place smells bad. Last time was—" He counted on his fingers. "Must've been two, three years back. Why?"

"Did he take copies?"

"They don't take copies. They bring their own scanner. Little wand thing." He mimed it, unimpressed. "Why folks can't just use the copier like normal people, I don't know."

*A scanner.* Of course. Hume's team would have digitized the record long ago — which meant they knew exactly what was in book forty-one, including whatever state the second page had been in when they found it. Which raised a question Quentin didn't like at all: had the page been missing for twenty-six years, or had it gone missing more recently, after someone in a gray suit decided it was inconvenient?

Either way, the absence was now itself evidence. You couldn't un-ring a bell, and you couldn't un-miss a page once a certified record showed the index promising two pages and the book delivering one.

She paid for her copies — thirty-eight dollars in quarters, counted out of the change purse Marisol had pressed on her that morning with a look that said *don't argue* — and carried the warm stack out into the gray afternoon light. The municipal building steps overlooked the harbor, and from the top step she could see it all laid out: the fishing boats knocking against the pier, the cannery building with its boarded windows, and there, third from the corner, the bakery — golden light in the windows, the little chalkboard sign, the silhouette of a woman moving behind the glass with the unhurried confidence of someone kneading dough she'd kneaded ten thousand times.

Marisol stood in the doorway for a moment, talking to Edda, who was gesturing with a rolled-up newspaper the way she gestured when she was winning an argument. Theo appeared behind them, ganache evidently survived, holding a tray of something. Ordinary Tuesday. Found family, mid-shift.

Quentin looked down at the certified copies in her hands and felt the sharp edges of the hope dig in properly for the first time. This was the part nobody warned you about — that vindication, if it ever came, would arrive not as thunder but as paperwork. As a transposed number. As a page that should exist and didn't. As three signatures wearing one hand's handwriting, sitting quietly in a basement for a quarter century, waiting for a woman with nothing left to lose and a legal pad full of proof.

She took out her phone and photographed the top page one more time, then sent the whole set to her lawyer with a single line: **Found problems in the 1998 deed. Call me tonight. Bring your skeptical face.**

Then she walked down toward Larkspur Lane, and the harbor wind pushed at her back the whole way, like the town itself had opinions about which direction she ought to be moving.

She was half a block from the bakery when her phone rang. Not the lawyer. Priya.

Quentin watched the name flash on the screen — *Priya (bride)*, saved weeks ago in a fit of professional optimism — and remembered Marisol's text. *Sounds upset.* She thought about letting it ring, about the copies under her arm and the argument she was already composing in her head, about how there was only so much of Quentin Kavanagh to go around these days.

Then she thought about the woman she'd been becoming lately — the one who'd talked a jaded bride off a ledge once already, the one who was starting to remember why she'd loved this work before it broke her — and she answered on the fourth ring.

"Priya. What's wrong?"

There was a pause on the line, long enough that Quentin could hear the ocean behind Priya's breathing, and when Priya finally spoke, her voice was small in a way Quentin had never heard from her.

"I found something in Dev's desk," she said. "I think — Quentin, I think I need to tell you something, and I think it might mean the wedding's off."
