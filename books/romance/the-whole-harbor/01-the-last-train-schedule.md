## 1. The Last Train Schedule

The 8:47 out of Ashcombe Junction was late again, and Jonah Eastwick counted the delay the way other men counted money—by habit, by need, by a private arithmetic no one else could see. He stood at the end of his garden with a mug of tea going cold in his hands, listening to the rails sing their long, low note through the fog that came off the river every October evening like something poured.

Two minutes late. Then four. The schedule taped inside his kitchen cupboard said the freight would pass at 9:02 and the last passenger train at 9:14, and after that the valley would belong to him alone until morning. He had lived by this timetable for ten years. Not the railway's timetable—the other one, the one he'd written himself in pencil on the back of an old program, the one that governed when he taught, when he ate, when he allowed himself to touch the instruments in his workshop, and when, at nine o'clock exactly, he put them all down as if they were sleeping children who might wake if handled wrong.

The train came at last, sighing into the dark, its windows lit gold against the black hills. He watched it go. Somewhere in those carriages were people going home to dinners and arguments and televisions left murmuring, people whose evenings would simply continue past nine o'clock without ceremony or fear. He envied them the way you envy weather—distantly, without bitterness, mostly.

His hands were steady. That was the first thing he checked each day, before coffee, before the kettle, before the small rituals of a life built deliberately small: *hands steady?* Yes. They trembled only now, at this hour, in this light, and only because of the cold. He told himself it was only the cold.

He went inside.

* * *

The house had belonged to his grandmother, and it still smelled faintly of her—lavender water and beeswax and the ghost of coal smoke in the chimney flues, though the fires had been electric for thirty years. Jonah had moved back after everything happened, after the city spat him out, and he had never quite decided whether living here was retreat or exile. Some mornings it felt like sanctuary. Most mornings it felt like a sentence he was serving in comfortable conditions.

Upstairs, the workshop occupied what had been his grandfather's study: north-facing windows, good light for seeing the truth of wood. Eleven instruments hung on the wall in soft cloth bags, and two lay open on the bench in various states of attention—a student cello with a seam opening along its lower bout, and a viola da gamba that had come to him from a widow in Leeds who could not bear to look at it anymore but could not bear to sell it either. He understood her completely.

He worked with his sleeves rolled, hide glue warming in the double boiler, the smell of it sweet and animal and older than any grief. This was the part of the craft no audience ever saw: the mending. The world wanted performance, wanted brilliance, wanted the shining hour—but instruments spent most of their lives broken in small ways, waiting for someone patient enough to hold them together again. Jonah had gotten very good at waiting.

At 6:40 he closed the workshop. This too was on the schedule. At 7:00 he ate—tonight, soup and bread, eaten standing at the counter like a man expecting to be called away. At 7:30 his first evening student arrived: Priya, eleven years old, ferociously serious, whose mother waited in the car outside because the mother still found Jonah Eastwick slightly frightening, though she would never have said so. Everyone in the valley knew the story in some version. The cellist who fell apart. The scandal. Whatever it was that had happened ten years ago at the festival hall, the thing that made the newspapers for a fortnight and then made nothing at all, because there had been no explanation, no interview, no memoir. Just a man mid-phrase, mid-bow-stroke, stopping—and then the sound he made instead of music, which several reviewers described with a cruelty that has never left the language of criticism since.

Priya didn't know any of that. To her he was just Mr. Eastwick, who was strict about intonation and generous about everything else.

"Again," he said, when she rushed the passage. "The note doesn't exist yet. You're playing where it *was*. Play where it is."

She frowned at him, adjusted, played it true. Something in his chest turned over—not pride exactly, more like the ache of watching someone walk freely through a country he could no longer enter.

"You're better than last week," he told her, and she glowed, and her mother's headlights swept the hedge as they left at 8:50, and he stood in the doorway breathing the cold air and checking, without meaning to, whether his hands were steady.

They were. Nine o'clock came. He put nothing down, because he had picked nothing up—that was the trick of it, the discipline within the discipline. You cannot abandon what you never begin. In ten years he had not played a single note past nine in the evening. The rule had no logic anyone else would accept. But the collapse had happened at 9:17 p.m., second movement, and somewhere in his damaged architecture a wall had been built at that hour, and he had learned not to test it. You do not knock on a wall to see if it is still there. You simply live on your side of it.

He washed his mug. He wound the clock. He wrote tomorrow's tasks on the slate by the door: *glue seam, tune Priya's instrument, collect rosin from Halloway's.* Ordinary words. A life spelled out in ordinary words, held together like the instruments on his bench—cracked, repaired, holding.

He did not know, standing there in his lamplit kitchen with the fog pressing at the windows, that the schedule was about to break.

* * *

It broke the next morning, in the form of a moving van.

He heard it before he saw it—the diesel grumble laboring up Mill Lane, too heavy for the lane's narrow crown, pausing at the bend where the hawthorn grew wild. Jonah was carrying a crate of tools from the shed, and he stopped, and watched the enormous white vehicle ease past his gate with perhaps six inches to spare on either side, its mirrors folded in like a creature making itself small.

It stopped at the house next door. The Hollis house. Empty for three years, since old Mrs. Hollis went to live with her daughter in Carlisle; the estate agent's sign had faded from blue to the color of weak tea, and Jonah had grown used to the view of its blank windows the way you grow used to a missing tooth—aware of the gap only when the tongue, wandering, finds it.

Now there was a van backed up to the gate, and a young man in a gray hoodie was unloading furniture with the brisk indifference of someone paid by the hour, and a woman was directing him—not shouting, just gesturing, precise, economical, a conductor's economy of motion, though Jonah thought this and then thought *don't*, and put the thought down like a hot pan.

She was tallish, dark-haired, wearing a coat the color of wet slate. She carried things herself whenever the movers fumbled—boxes marked in a handwriting he couldn't see from this distance, a lamp, a rolled rug over her shoulder with a strength that surprised him. There was a piano among the effects. An upright, old, its case scarred. The movers wrestled it up the path while she steadied the corner, and Jonah caught himself holding his breath, the way he always did when anything precious passed near a doorway.

He made himself turn away. He made himself go inside and warm the glue and sit at the bench and work the seam of the student cello, drawing the clamps tight, wiping the squeeze-out with a damp cloth. The window of the workshop faced the lane, and he did not look through it. Mostly.

Twice, he looked.

The second time, she was standing at her new gate, looking directly at his house—or at him, though the glass was old and wavy and he doubted she could see in. She stood there longer than a person stands when they're merely looking at a building. Then she turned and went inside, and Jonah sat with his hands flat on the bench, feeling something he hadn't felt in years and couldn't immediately name.

It took him the length of a kettle boiling to name it. Curiosity. Plain, dangerous, human curiosity—the kind that asks questions, the kind that starts conversations, the kind that leads a man to stand at his own gate at odd hours hoping for a glimpse of a neighbor. He had spent ten years sanding himself smooth of wanting. Wanting was how the fall began. Wanting the phrase to be perfect, wanting the hall to love him, wanting his career to be the thing that finally made his father say the word *proud* out loud. Wanting had carried him up onto that stage at 9:04 p.m. with the concerto half-flown beneath his bow, and wanting had dropped him.

So he did not want. He scheduled. He mended. He taught Priya her scales and Mrs. Adeyemi her arpeggios and collected his modest fee and kept his evenings short and his nights quiet and his hands, God willing, steady.

But curiosity was already in the house. It had come in on the diesel wind of the moving van and settled in the corners like dust, and no amount of routine would sweep it out.

* * *

Over the following week, he learned her by fragments, the way you learn a piece of music from the next room.

She left early and returned late—city hours, London hours, the 7:12 down and the 8:31 back, sometimes later. She had a way of walking that was fast without hurrying, as if speed were simply her natural temperature. On Saturday she spent four hours in the garden cutting back the hawthorn that had gone leggy and wild, and the sound of the shears carried across the hedge in clean, rhythmic snips, and Jonah found himself keeping time with it while he rehaired a bow, his wrist moving in sympathy, and he had to stop and laugh at himself, alone, quietly, a dry sound with no humor in it.

On Sunday, music.

He was in the workshop when it started—piano, through her open windows, the upright he'd watched come off the van. Not a performance. Scales, first, methodical, then a Chopin nocturne attempted and abandoned, attempted and abandoned, the player stopping each time at the same bar with a small frustrated emphasis, as if shutting a door on it. She wasn't bad. She was rusty, and impatient with her rust, which was somehow more telling than skill would have been. People who play for others polish. People who play for themselves fight.

Jonah stood in his workshop with a brush full of varnish suspended in the air, listening, and the varnish began to run, and he let it.

Because here was the thing he had not said aloud to anyone, ever: he missed it. Not the fame—he'd been young enough to distrust the fame even while it fed him. Not the applause, which had always sounded slightly like weather happening to someone else. He missed the *work*. The four a.m. struggle with a passage. The slow, daily, invisible carpentry of interpretation, the way a phrase could be rebuilt a hundred times and still surprise you on the hundred-and-first. He missed being inside music the way you miss a language you once dreamed in. Ten years of repairing other people's instruments, of hearing music only at one remove—students' wavering scales, radio through the kitchen window, the trains' long iron note—and some sealed chamber in him had begun, without his permission, to fill with sound again.

He set down the brush. He cleaned it. He did not play anything, of course. It was barely afternoon, but the rule was the rule, and besides, the cello hanging nearest the bench—his own, the good one, the one that had not been out of its bag in three years—seemed to watch him with the patience of all instruments, which is the patience of things that know they will outlast us.

That night, the piano next door stopped at 9:03 p.m. He noticed the exact minute. He noticed that he noticed. And lying awake afterward in the dark with the freight train passing at its appointed time, Jonah admitted the truth to the ceiling: the schedule was cracking. Not because she had done anything—she had done nothing but move in, cut a hedge, play Chopin badly and stop too soon. Because she existed at the edge of his vision, and edges are where order fails.

* * *

On the ninth day, she spoke to him.

He was at his gate, collecting a parcel—a shipment of ebony fittings he'd been waiting on for weeks—when she came down her path with a recycling bin in each hand, saw him, and paused. Up close she was younger than he'd guessed, or looked younger in the flat gray light; early thirties, maybe, with tired eyes that seemed older than the rest of her face, eyes that had seen something and filed it away where it wouldn't show.

"You're the instrument repairer," she said. Not a question.

"Word travels."

"It does in villages." She set down the bins. "I've got a piano that needs looking at. It was my mother's. It's been in storage six years and it sounds like it's underwater."

"I can come by. Tune it, regulate the action if it needs it." He heard his own voice, level, professional, and marveled at it from a slight distance, the way you marvel at a hand that stays steady on a rope bridge.

"Today?" A beat, and then, softer, almost careless in the way that isn't careless at all: "Or whenever suits. I'm Mara, by the way. Mara Voss."

"Jonah Eastwick."

Something crossed her face when he said his name. It was small—smaller than a flicker, more like a change in air pressure—and it was gone before he could decide whether he'd imagined it. Later, much later, he would take that moment out and examine it under stronger light. For now he only registered that she knew his name already, which was unsurprising in a village, and that knowing it had cost her something, which was not unsurprising at all.

"Eastwick," she repeated, carefully, as if setting the word down on a high shelf. "Right. Today would be wonderful, actually. If you're free."

"I'm free after five."

"Come at six," she said. "I'll make tea."

He carried his parcel inside and stood in the hallway with the box of ebony in his arms and his heart doing something arrhythmic and unwelcome, and he told himself: *it's a piano. It's a tuning. It's forty minutes of work and a cup of tea and nothing else.* The schedule allowed for it. Barely. Six o'clock, done by seven, evening intact, walls unbreeched.

But when he looked at the slate by the door, at tomorrow's tidy list of ordinary words, he found himself picking up the chalk and adding a line beneath them, in his own handwriting, as if the day ahead were a piece of music requiring a marking:

*Mara Voss — 6:00.*

And underneath, before he could stop himself, smaller:

*who is she?*

The chalk dust drifted down through the lamplight. Outside, far off down the valley, the 9:14 was pulling out of Ashcombe Junction, right on time, carrying everyone home but him.
