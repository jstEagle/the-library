## 1. Debts Instead of Grapes

The first thing Ada Okafor did every morning of harvest was lie to herself.

*Today will be manageable,* she'd say, standing at the kitchen window with coffee going cold in her hand, watching the light come up over the eastern rows like a slow gold tide. *Today the crew shows up on time, the press doesn't jam, and nobody calls in sick.*

Twenty years of mornings, and the vineyard had never once agreed with her.

This morning it was Marta calling before six, her voice crackling through the truck's bad speaker: the tractor on the north block had thrown a belt, and the picking bins were still stacked where they'd been since spring because Tomás had quit—again—and the forecast said rain by Thursday.

"Rain by Thursday," Ada repeated, as if saying it twice might change the sky's mind.

"Or Wednesday night," Marta said cheerfully. "The app can't decide."

Of course it couldn't. Nothing could decide anything this time of year except the grapes themselves, and they had decided, with the mute authority of fruit everywhere, that they were ready now.

Ada set down her cup. She pulled on her boots—the left one with the split seam she kept meaning to fix, the right one that whistled faintly when she walked fast, which she always did during harvest—and went out into the gray-gold morning to begin the annual negotiation between what she wanted and what the land would allow.

By seven she had the belt situation half-solved with a spare from the shed and a length of baling wire that would have made her grandfather weep. By eight the crew arrived, eleven strong instead of fourteen, and she redistributed rows with the quick arithmetic of someone who had been doing sums like these since she was twenty-five and knew nothing except that her father was dead and the bank owned most of what he'd loved.

That was the year everything had landed on her at once. The inheritance papers still damp, the debts itemized in neat columns that seemed almost mocking in their tidiness—equipment loans, a line of credit, back taxes, the funeral itself. She remembered sitting at this same kitchen table with a yellow legal pad, writing numbers until they stopped meaning anything, until they were just shapes that spelled out *you will lose this place.*

She hadn't lost it. That was the miracle no one ever asked about. People asked how the wine was doing, how the tasting room was doing, whether the drought had hurt yields. Nobody ever asked how she'd done it, because the answer was a secret she kept so carefully it had become less like a possession and more like an organ—something inside her, working quietly, that she would die without.

At noon, under the tin awning by the barn, she ate a sandwich standing up and answered forty letters.

Not real letters. Not anymore. The Songbook lived online now, had for years, but the name had stuck from the early days when readers mailed actual paper to a post office box in town, and Ada liked the word *letters*, liked the weight of it. Letters were what had saved her. One desperate night in that first terrible autumn—crushing grapes alone in the dark because she couldn't afford help, crying into the must because no one could hear her over the press—she had written something. Not a letter. A column. She'd sent it to a small regional lifestyle magazine on a whim so reckless she barely recognized herself in it afterward, signed only *The Songbook*, because her father used to say a vineyard was just a song written slowly, in dirt.

The editor published it. Readers wrote back. And Ada discovered that she could say out loud, under a false name, all the things she couldn't say as Ada Okafor, who was competent and tireless and fine, thank you, always fine. As the Songbook she could admit fear. She could admit loneliness. She could tell a stranger whose marriage was ending that grief was not a failure of love but its continuation, and mean it, and be meant-for in return.

Ten years now. Ten years of weekly columns, of readers who felt like friends, of one reader above all.

Wren.

Ada smiled despite herself, thumbing past the inbox to the message she'd read three times already and would read thirty more before she answered it, because answering Wren required care, required her best self, the self that only existed on the page.

*I've been reading you for ten years,* Wren had written last week, *and I've never told you the whole truth either, which seems unfair given how much of yours I've absorbed like weather. So here is mine: my career ended the way careers do—not with a crash but with a long quiet erosion, and then one day there was nothing left to erode. I'm moving somewhere new. Somewhere with hills, I think. I want silence that isn't empty. Do you think silence can be full? You've taught me it can. I'll write again when I arrive, wherever it is. —W*

Somewhere with hills. Ada had read that line standing right here, sandwich in hand, and felt an odd pang she'd filed away as tiredness.

She typed her reply in fragments between crew questions and a phone call from the co-op about sulfur orders. *Dear Wren. Silence can absolutely be full. Mine is full of tractors.* She told him about harvest, the way she always did, in general terms—a life sketched lightly enough that no one could find her in it, though God knew she'd slipped details over the years, little confessions she hoped were too scattered to triangulate. The hills. The old cottage next door, empty so long the realtor had stopped listing it. Her father's saying about songs written in dirt.

Wren knew her better than anyone alive. He also knew nothing true about her at all. She had made her peace with that paradox long ago, or thought she had.

The afternoon went the way afternoons go in harvest: fast and slightly wrong. A bin was mislabeled. Marta's nephew drove the quad into a ditch and emerged, miraculously, laughing. Ada tasted berries from the south block and found them two days riper than the schedule allowed, which meant rearranging everything, which meant calling people, apologizing, persuading. She was good at this. She was so good at this that sometimes she forgot she had ever been anything else, that there had been a girl who wanted things she couldn't name, who wrote letters to no one in the margins of accounting ledgers.

It was nearly dark when the mail came—actual mail, the postman running late, his headlights sweeping the yard as Ada stood hosing sticky grape residue off her forearms. He handed her a small stack through the window of her truck: a seed catalog, a bill, and a thick cream-colored envelope with a return address she knew by heart.

Meridian House. Her editor's office. But not the usual thin envelope of proofs or contracts. This one had weight. This one had been *prepared*.

Ada dried her hands on a rag and opened it leaning against the truck bed, the yard going blue around her, the first crickets starting up in the drainage ditch.

Marguerite's handwriting on the note inside was brisk as always, but the sentences underneath were not.

*Ada—*

*We need to talk, and I'd rather say it in ink than wait for a call you'll dodge. There's interest. Real interest. National syndication. The kind of offer that comes once for a column like ours, and it comes with conditions: a public profile, a feature interview, a photograph. They want a face for the voice. They want a name.*

*I know what I'm asking. I've watched you build this thing brick by careful brick, and I know the anonymity isn't a gimmick—it's load-bearing. But I also know what your readers say. They don't love a mystery. They love you. And I think some part of you has been waiting ten years to stop hiding.*

*Call me when you're ready. Don't call me when you're scared; call me when you're honest. There's a difference, and you know it better than anyone.*

*—M.*

Ada read it twice. Then a third time, standing very still while the evening assembled itself around her—the smell of crushed fruit drifting up from the winery, the crew's taillights dwindling down the county road, the hills going soft and dark the way Wren had said he wanted them.

National syndication. Her words, in a hundred newspapers, in a million kitchens. The thing any columnist would kill for.

And a photograph. A face. A name.

She thought of the yellow legal pad, twenty years gone, the numbers that spelled ruin. She thought of the night she'd written her first column with grape-stained hands, anonymous and therefore free. Every good thing in her life had grown from that one act of concealment—the vineyard saved, the friendship with Wren built letter by letter on ground no one else could see. Anonymity wasn't a wall she'd built around herself. It was the trellis. Everything she loved climbed it.

Pull it out, Marguerite was asking, and see what falls.

Ada folded the letter along its creases, precisely, and put it in the glovebox as if storing it there might contain it. She walked back toward the house through the dark rows, boots whistling, and caught herself doing the thing she did when she couldn't afford to feel something yet: making lists. Belt for the tractor. Call the co-op. Fix the boot. Answer Wren.

Answer Wren.

She stopped between the third and fourth rows, the vines black around her, and realized her hands were shaking—not with fear exactly, and not with hope either, but with that awful trembling that comes when the ground under a safe life shifts by a single degree and you feel, before you understand, that everything growing on it is about to move.

Because here was the thought she couldn't list away: if her name went national, if her face ran beside her words in a million kitchens, then Wren would know. Wren, who had told her he was moving somewhere with hills. Wren, who was arriving—when? soon? now?—into a silence he hoped wouldn't be empty.

*Don't call me when you're scared,* Marguerite had written. *Call me when you're honest.*

Ada stood a moment longer in the dark among the vines, listening to the wind move through leaves that sounded, as her father had promised they would, almost like singing.

Then she went inside, sat down at the kitchen table—the same table, the same chair—and began, with unsteady care, to write back to Wren.

* * *

Three counties east, in a rented truck trailing a borrowed cello case and eleven years of packed boxes, Elias Wren crossed the county line as the sun went down, following a road sign toward hills he had chosen from a photograph, for reasons he could not have explained to anyone—including, especially, himself.
