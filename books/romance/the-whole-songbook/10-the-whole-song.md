## 10. The Whole Song

**Elias**

The cello had not left its case in eleven months.

He told himself it was the humidity, the dry rot of grief, the ordinary excuses a man makes when his hands have forgotten how to be brave. But standing at the edge of Ada's vineyard as the sun went down on harvest festival night, case open at his feet, bow resting across his knee like something surrendered—he knew the truth. He hadn't played because playing meant feeling, and feeling meant her.

The town had come out in force. Strings of paper lanterns swung between the trellis posts, gilding the rows of vines in amber and rose. Long tables groaned under Ada's wine and Mrs. Ferreira's empanadas and someone's grandmother's honey cake. Children chased each other down the aisles between vines, their laughter scattering like startled birds. It was, by every measure, a good night—a night Ada Okafor had built with ten years of stubborn hands.

And she stood at the far end of the clearing, laughing at something the mayor said, wearing a dress the color of crushed blackberries, and she would not look at him.

She had every right not to. He had detonated their whole world in front of a photographer and half the county, and then he had done the unforgivable thing: he had gone silent. Three days of silence while she wrote her way back to him in the only language she'd ever trusted—print, ink, her name at the bottom of a column that had run this morning under a headline he'd read four times before his eyes stopped stinging.

*My name is Ada Okafor,* she had written. *I am The Songbook. I have been Wren's friend for ten years, and I broke his heart by hiding, because hiding was the first thing I ever learned how to do. This is my last column. Everything after this, I want to say out loud.*

His name was in it too. Not just Wren—*Elias*. She had written about the storm cellar, about the lantern light, about the almost-kiss she'd pulled away from, and she had written: *I chose the column over him once, in that cellar, without even knowing I was choosing. I won't make that mistake twice.*

So here he was, in the last light, with a cello he hadn't touched since the orchestra collapsed and a piece of music he'd been composing in his head since the night she whispered *I'm not who you think I am* and he'd believed, foolishly, that it was a confession of something small.

It was a sarabande, slow and aching, built on the melody of the song she hummed while pruning—the one she claimed she'd made up, the one he'd recognized from her letters years ago, described note by note in a paragraph that had made him weep into his coffee in a hotel room in Vienna. Her song. Their song. The whole songbook, distilled into eight bars.

He picked up the bow.

His hand shook. The first note came out thin and wavering, and a few heads turned, and Elias Wren—who had played Carnegie Hall, who had played through a ruptured tendon and a dying marriage and the worst review of his life—discovered that his hands were steadier in front of two thousand strangers than they were in front of forty neighbors and one woman.

Then he found the second note, and the third, and the melody opened like a row of vines in morning light, and the crowd went quiet the way crowds only go quiet when they recognize something true.

He did not look at her. He kept his eyes closed and let the music do what words had failed to do for three days: say *I heard you. I read every word. Come here.*

* * *

**Ada**

She knew the melody before she knew what was happening.

It moved through the lantern-lit air and into her chest like water finding an old channel, and for a moment she simply stood there with her glass halfway to her lips, the mayor still talking, the festival still turning around her, while inside her everything went still.

That was her song. The one she'd invented at nineteen, alone in the vineyard office with the debts spread across the desk like a diagnosis, humming to keep despair at bay. She had never written it down. She had only ever described it—in a letter, nine years ago, to a stranger named Wren who had written back: *I can hear it. Play it for me someday?*

He had learned it. From nothing but her words, he had built it into something with bones and breath, and now he was playing it in her vineyard, in front of everyone, eyes closed, bow trembling, giving her back the sound of her own survival.

The mayor's voice faded. Someone shushed someone else. The whole town turned toward the man with the cello, and Ada set down her glass very carefully, because if she didn't she would drop it.

Her legs carried her before her courage caught up. Down the aisle between the tables, past Mrs. Ferreira, past the children gone suddenly solemn, past the photographer—*her* photographer, the one from the magazine, who had stayed for the festival and was now lowering her camera because some moments are not for capturing—and out onto the bare earth where Elias stood playing.

He felt her coming. She saw it in the way his shoulders changed, the way the vibrato deepened, the way his breathing stuttered without breaking the phrase. Ten years of letters had taught them both this: how to hear each other arriving.

She reached him at the bridge of the melody, the part that climbed, the part that refused to resolve, and instead of stopping—instead of letting him finish it alone, the way she had finished so many things alone—she began to hum.

His eyes opened.

For one suspended moment they looked at each other over the body of the cello, her voice threading through his strings, the whole town watching, and there was nowhere left to hide and no reason left to try. She took his free hand. He didn't stop playing—couldn't, wouldn't—but his fingers laced through hers, callused and cold and shaking, and held on.

They finished the song together, in front of God and the mayor and the syndication photographer and every person in the valley who had ever read her column without knowing her name. When the last note faded, no one applauded right away. There was just a long, ringing silence, and then Mrs. Ferreira started crying loudly enough for everyone, and the applause came down like the rain they'd all been praying wouldn't come during harvest.

Elias lowered the cello. "You're humming flat," he said, and his voice cracked on *flat*, and Ada laughed, and then she was crying too, and he pulled her against him, cello and all, and kissed her hair while the town roared.

"I published my name," she said into his collar. "You were supposed to hate me for another week at least."

"I tried." His arms tightened. "I got through most of a morning."

Above them, the first stars were coming out over the vineyard, and the lanterns swayed, and somewhere near the honey cake the band struck up something joyful and wrong-tempoed, and neither of them moved to join it. They had spent ten years writing to each other across a distance neither could name. The distance was gone now. All that remained was the closeness, terrifying and plain, like morning.

"Play it again sometime?" she asked.

"Every harvest," he said. "Every year. Until our hands give out."

* * *

**Elias**

Spring came up the valley like a rumor, then like a fact.

By April the vines wore their first pale green, and the frost danger had mostly passed, and Elias had developed the farmer's habit of scanning the sky at dawn with an expression of profound suspicion. His cello lived in the corner of Ada's kitchen now, next to the wood stove, because the cottage next door had become less a residence than an annex—his mail went there, his clothes migrated steadily eastward, and most nights he slept in the big drafty house where the columns used to be written, in the bed where Ada dreamed, or didn't, depending on deadlines.

There were no more anonymous deadlines. That was the strangest and sweetest change. Her final column had run in March, signed, and the response had been a flood—letters by the hundred, readers who felt betrayed and readers who felt liberated and readers, the majority, who simply wrote *thank you for telling us who you are.* And at the bottom of the flood, one letter forwarded by her old editor, from a young woman in Ohio who had inherited her father's hardware store and nearly lost it, who wrote: *I read your column the way other people pray. Please don't stop entirely.*

Which was why, on a bright Tuesday morning in the middle of planting week, Ada sat at the kitchen table with a legal pad and a look on her face that Elias had learned to fear and adore in equal measure.

"We're not starting a column," he said, before she could speak.

"We're starting a column."

"Ada."

"*We're* starting a column," she repeated, tapping the pad. "Two names. Look—I already drafted the header." She turned it toward him, and there it was in her slanted, unrepentant handwriting:

***The Whole Songbook — letters on love, loss, and second chances, from Ada Okafor & Elias Wren.***

"You play," she said. "I write. You know things about music and grief and starting over that I don't, and I know things about dirt and debt and staying that you don't. Ten years I answered strangers' letters alone in this kitchen. I'm not doing it alone anymore. And neither—" she leveled the pen at him, "—are you. Don't think I haven't noticed you reading them over my shoulder."

He opened his mouth to deny it. Closed it. Picked up his coffee.

"The Ohio woman," he said finally. "The hardware store. What would you tell her?"

Ada smiled—the smile that had undone him in a storm cellar, in a vineyard, in a thousand letters he'd read aloud to an empty room. "That's your letter," she said. "First one's yours. I'll edit."

Outside, the morning was warming, and the new plants waited in their long green rows, and somewhere down the valley a tractor coughed awake. Elias took the pen from her hand, pulled the legal pad toward himself, and after a moment—after the pause where a whole old life used to live—he began to write.

*Dear reader,* he wrote. *I spent ten years writing letters to a woman whose name I didn't know, and she turned out to be the neighbor I was falling in love with, which should tell you everything about how little any of us understands the plots of our own lives. So let's begin there...*

At the top of the page, beneath the header, two names. Side by side. Signed.
