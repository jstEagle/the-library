## 1. The Broken String

There is a moment before every solo when the hall holds its breath for you. Marguerite Nystrom had lived inside that moment for twenty years — the conductor's baton lifting, the second violins settling, two thousand strangers becoming one held breath — and she had never once been afraid of it. The silence was hers. She owned it the way other people owned houses.

Tonight the silence was the Royal Festival Hall, sold out, the program printed with her name in letters large enough to read from the back row. Elgar's Cello Concerto. The piece she had been playing since she was nine years old, when her mother had set the sheet music on the stand and said, *This one is yours, Marguerite. It will wait for you as long as it takes.*

It had waited. She had arrived. At thirty-four she was the principal cellist of the Aurora Symphony, the soloist critics had begun to call *inevitable*, and tonight the artistic director of the Vienna Philharmonic was in the fourth row with a program folded on his knee. Everyone knew what that meant. Everyone had been saying it to her all week with their eyebrows.

The adagio opened and she went into it the way she always did, which was to say she disappeared. Her left hand climbed the fingerboard and her bow breathed across the strings and the hall fell away until there was nothing in the world but the line of the melody, that long grieving fall of notes that Elgar had written in the wreckage of a war. She was not performing it. She was inside it, the way you are inside a house you grew up in, knowing every creak of every stair.

Then, in the ninth bar of the second movement, her left hand stopped.

Not trembled. Not fumbled. *Stopped* — the way a clock stops, all at once, with a small interior click she felt rather than heard. Her third and fourth fingers locked flat against the string, and the note she was playing curdled, sagged, died. The bow kept moving for half a second without her permission, dragging a sound out of the instrument that no cellist should ever have to hear herself make, and then her right arm seized too, and the hall — which had been leaning toward her like a field of wheat toward the sun — went absolutely still.

She heard her own breath. She heard the ventilation system. Somewhere in the balcony a cough, quickly strangled.

*Play*, she told her hands. They did not answer. It was as if they belonged to someone else now, someone standing behind her, someone who had taken them without asking. She looked down at the fingerboard as if the problem might be visible — a splinter, a broken string, something she could name and fix — and the audience saw her look, and the silence changed texture. It stopped being anticipation and became pity. She could feel it move through the hall like a change in barometric pressure, two thousand people deciding, in the same half-second, to feel sorry for her.

The conductor gave her a bar. Then another. His baton hovered, patient, terrible.

She tried to lift her fingers. They came, finally, stiff and stupid, and she got four notes out — four ugly, pinched notes, like a child's first lesson — and then the hand closed again, a fist around the neck of the cello, and she understood with a clarity that felt almost peaceful that it was not going to come back. Not tonight. Not in this hall, in this silence, with Vienna in the fourth row.

She set the bow on the rest. She stood up. She turned to the audience, and she bowed — a full, formal bow, the bow you give at the end of something — and she walked off the stage with the cello held against her body like a child, like a shield, like the only thing in the building that still knew who she was.

The wings were dark and smelled of dust and hot stage lights. She kept walking, past the stage manager with his headset and his open mouth, past the callboard, into the corridor, and she did not stop until she reached the green room, where she set the cello down in its open case with exaggerated care, the way you set down something breakable when what you actually want to do is throw it through a window.

Then she sat on the couch and looked at her hands.

They looked like her hands. Long fingers, the calluses on the tips like small amber beads, the faint scar on her right thumb from a broken string at fourteen. They lay in her lap, pale and quiet, and she stared at them the way you stare at a stranger who has just said something unforgivable.

The door opened. She didn't look up. She knew the cadence of the footsteps — Dev Anand, her agent, fifteen years, three orchestras, one near-death contract negotiation — and she knew that he would come in quietly, close the door behind him, and not say anything for at least ten seconds, because he had learned that about her long ago.

He made it eight seconds.

"Marge."

"Don't." Her voice came out flat and unfamiliar. "Don't do the gentle voice. I can hear the gentle voice from here and I will put you through that wall."

He sat down across from her instead. He was holding two cups of tea he had clearly acquired from somewhere, and he set them both on the table, one turned toward her, and said nothing else. Outside, muffled by two doors and a corridor, the orchestra was playing on without her. She could hear the horn solo. It sounded obscene, somehow — the music continuing, the world continuing, as if a thing had not just ended.

"They're doing the intermission early," Dev said. "Tomasz is covering. He's playing it well."

"Tomasz is playing it like a competition."

"He's playing it like a man who knows this is his audition. Yes." Dev took off his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose, which was what he did when he was about to say something she didn't want to hear. "Marge. How long?"

"How long what?"

"Don't." He said it gently, which was worse. "Don't do that. You know what. The hand. How long has it been happening?"

She looked at the tea. The tea was doing that thing tea does in paper cups, going lukewarm immediately, becoming an object of no comfort whatsoever.

"Three weeks," she said.

Dev closed his eyes.

"It was fine," she said, and heard herself defending it, and hated that. "It was fine in rehearsal. It was fine yesterday. It's — it happens for a second and then it's gone. I thought it was nerves. I thought it was the schedule. I thought if I got through tonight—"

"You thought if you got through tonight, it would be over."

"I thought if I got through tonight, I'd have the Vienna conversation, and then I'd have the whole summer to fix whatever this is. Rest. Physio. Whatever they do for—" She gestured at her hands, at the word she couldn't make herself say. *Dystonia* had been sitting in the back of her throat for two weeks, ever since a colleague had mentioned it at a dinner party in the careful voice people use for illnesses that end careers. She had gone home and read about it at two in the morning with the screen brightness turned all the way down, as if the condition were something shameful she might be caught looking at.

Dev was quiet for a long moment. Through the walls, the orchestra arrived at the movement's end. Applause, thin and distant.

"Here's what's going to happen," he said. "The second half goes on. You're not in it, obviously. I'll go out front and be visible and make jokes about the program change, and by tomorrow the story is *soloist withdraws from concert, minor injury* — I can get the hall's press office to say wrist strain, everyone loves wrist strain, wrist strain gets better. And then—"

"And then?"

He put his glasses back on. When he looked at her, his face had done the thing it did — the thing where the agent fell away and the friend was underneath, and the friend looked tired.

"And then you take a leave," he said. "A real one. Not two weeks. A season, maybe. We frame it as artistic sabbatical, we get the orchestra to co-announce it, we make it sound like a choice—"

"A leave." She said the word as if tasting it. "You mean a leave of absence."

"Marge—"

"From what? From the chair I've held for nine years? From the season we programmed around me? From the recording we signed for in March?" Her voice was rising and she could hear it rising and she could not stop it, the same way she had not been able to stop her hand, and the parallel was so exact and so cruel that she laughed — one hard syllable with nothing in it. "You're firing me in the passive voice, Dev."

"I'm keeping you alive." He leaned forward. "Listen to me. If you stay, they will be kind to you for six months and then they will quietly re-audition the chair, and you will be thirty-five years old auditioning against twenty-six-year-olds with a hand that seizes, and that is the ending. This—" he spread his hands, "—this is the version where you get to choose how it looks. It's the only version where you choose anything."

The green room was very quiet. On the table, the tea had stopped pretending to be warm. Somewhere beyond the walls the second half was beginning; she could hear the tuning, that long ambiguous chord of an orchestra finding itself, the sound she had loved since she was a child, the sound that had always meant *home* to her more than any house ever had.

She thought: *I am thirty-four years old and I have spent my entire life in rooms like this one, and I do not know how to be anywhere else.*

She thought: *My mother would have known what to say.*

Her mother had been dead four years, and still, in the worst moments, Marguerite reached for her the way an amputee reaches with a missing hand. It had been her mother who had put the cello in her arms at seven. Her mother who had driven her to lessons through Ohio winters, who had sat in every drafty youth-orchestra rehearsal hall with a thermos and a paperback, who had said, when Marguerite at seventeen had wanted to quit for a boy, *You can have both, sweetheart, but you will never forgive yourself if you let one of them make the decision for you.* Her mother, who had died on an ordinary Tuesday while Marguerite was on tour in Asia, so that the last thing they'd said to each other had been a text about hotel coffee.

And her mother's mother — her grandmother, the other one, the fierce one — had left her an orchard on an island in Puget Sound that Marguerite had visited exactly three times in her adult life and had not thought about in years. That was the shape of her inheritance: a career and a piece of paper about apple trees, and until tonight she had never doubted which one she was.

"Where would I even go?" she said. It came out smaller than she intended.

Dev shrugged, gently. "Anywhere. That's the point of a leave. You go somewhere and you remember that you're a person who plays the cello, instead of a cello that occasionally gets to be a person."

"That's a terrible line."

"It's a terrible night." He stood, and straightened his jacket, and became the agent again, the armor going back on piece by piece. "I'll draft the statement tonight. You don't sign anything until you've slept. And Marge—" He paused at the door. "The hand is not the end of you. Whatever it is. I've seen people come back from worse."

"Have you?" she asked. "Honestly."

He didn't answer, which was the answer, and then he was gone, and she was alone with the cello and the two cups of tea and the sound of an orchestra playing her concerto's second half without her, note after note after note, the music going on and on like a tide going out.

She opened the case and looked at the instrument. Two hundred years old, Italian, on loan from a foundation that would now have questions. In its varnish she could see the distorted reflection of the room, of the couch, of a woman sitting very still with her hands in her lap.

*Who am I if I don't play?* The question arrived without drama, the way the seizure had arrived — one moment present, the next simply there. It was not rhetorical. That was the terror of it. She had built a life with no rooms in it except this one. No husband, no children, no garden, no second language, no hobbies, nothing that would hold still and be hers if the music left. She had a career the way other people had a heartbeat, and tonight, in front of two thousand people and a man from Vienna, the heartbeat had skipped, and everyone had heard it.

Her phone buzzed on the table. She looked at it without picking it up. Forty-one notifications. She could imagine them all: the colleague with the careful voice, the critic already typing, her stand partner, her ex, her father in Ohio who never quite understood what she did but understood that tonight something had gone wrong because the notifications were arriving at the wrong hour.

She picked up the phone, and before she could stop herself — before the sensible part of her could intervene — she searched one thing. Not *dystonia*. Not *focal hand*. She searched the name of the island, the one from the deed in the fireproof box in her closet, the one her grandmother had left her alongside a letter Marguerite had read once, four years ago, standing in a lawyer's office with her mother's funeral flowers still in her arms.

*San Piedro Island. Ferry schedule.*

The ferry ran three times a day in summer, twice in the shoulder season, once in winter. The last crossing of the season was in late September. It was, at this moment, the second week of June.

She looked at the search results for a long time. Then she closed the phone, and she sat in the green room of the Royal Festival Hall, in the smell of dust and hot lights, and listened to the orchestra play Elgar without her, and she did not cry, because it seemed there was a part of her that had already decided something — a part that lived lower than grief, down where the hands and the heart make their real decisions — and that part had gone very quiet and very still, the way water goes still before it freezes, or before it moves.

The cello waited in its case. The tea went cold. Somewhere across the city, Dev was drafting a statement that would make her disappearance sound like a choice.

And somewhere across the country, on an island she had not seen in fifteen years, an orchard she did not know she no longer owned alone stood in the June dark with its rows full of storm-blown branches and its old barn holding its breath — waiting, as her mother had once promised her a concerto would wait, for as long as it took.

* * *

The statement ran the next morning: *Ms. Nystrom has withdrawn from the remainder of the season to address a minor injury. She is grateful for the orchestra's support and looks forward to returning to the stage.* It was, Dev told her, the best version of a bad story. Wrist strain. Rest. The passive voice, doing its work.

She read it in the ferry terminal at Anacortes, four days later, with the cello case between her knees and a duffel bag at her feet and no plan beyond a one-way ticket and a set of keys on a brass ring shaped like an apple. She had not told Dev where she was going. She had told him *away*, and he had looked at her for a long moment and then said, "Call me when you're a person again," which was the kindest thing anyone had said to her in a week.

The morning boat was loading. Gulls argued over the parking lot. She stood at the rail as the ferry pulled out, watching the mainland recede — the skyline of her life going small and gray behind her — and she flexed her left hand against the rail, once, twice, testing it, the way you test ice.

The fingers answered. For now, they answered.

She did not see, then, that the deed in the fireproof box held a second name. She did not know that the orchard had been waiting for her with company. She only knew that the water was cold and bright and going somewhere, and that for the first time since the silence fell in that hall, she was moving toward something instead of away from it — and that the something, whatever it turned out to be, had thorns.
