## 4. Terms of Engagement

The letter from Wren arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day the tide charts always seemed to lie to me — promising a gentle morning low and delivering instead a churning gray mess that turned my survey transects into soup. Wren's handwriting on the envelope had the same quality: familiar, and then not.

*Jude,* it said, in block capitals pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. *Tuesday. Ten a.m. The orchard. Come alone or don't come.*

No question mark at the end of that last sentence. Wren had never used question marks when she wanted something; she'd simply stated it as though it had already happened. When we were kids she'd announced, "You're carrying my bike home," and I'd carried her bike home, four miles, uphill past the cannery, because arguing with Wren was like arguing with weather.

I put the letter on my kitchen table and looked at it for a long time. Then I looked at the deed, which I had now read eleven times, as though the twelfth reading might reveal a fourth name, someone sane, someone with no history with me whatsoever. No such luck. Three names. Pemberton, Pemberton, Voss. My mother's posthumous idea of a joke, if my mother had ever joked, which she hadn't. She'd been a woman who expressed affection through pruning schedules and disapproval through silence, and apparently she'd saved her final act of both for me.

Ten a.m. Tuesday. The orchard.

I went, of course. I told myself it was about due diligence, about protecting my interest in a property I hadn't asked for. I told myself a lot of things on the forty-minute drive up the coast road, past the turnoff for the marine station, past the little diner where Dev and I ate breakfast every Thursday, past all the landmarks of a life I had built with the precision of a tide table. None of the things I told myself survived contact with the sight of the orchard gate.

It was still there. That was the first shock. The gate my father built before he left, sagging on its hinges exactly the way it had sagged twenty years ago, painted the same stubborn blue that Wren must have repainted herself, because nobody else would have. And beyond it, the rows. The vines climbing their wires in long green sentences, the old farmhouse with its crooked chimney, the packing shed with its rusted roof. Everything exactly where memory had filed it, which felt like an ambush. I had spent two decades constructing a life in which this place did not exist, and here it was, existing all over everything.

Wren was waiting by the gate, arms crossed, wearing canvas gloves and an expression I recognized from our childhood: the look of a person who has already won the argument and is merely waiting for you to arrive at your defeat.

"You came," she said.

"You said come alone or don't come."

"I gave you an out." She shrugged. "You didn't take it."

She was fifty-one and looked like our mother, which I could see would be a problem going forward. Same square jaw, same deep-set eyes, same way of standing with her weight forward like the ground might try something. But her hair had gone silver at the temples, and her hands — I noticed her hands, because I notice hands, it's a fieldwork habit — were cracked and stained with vine sap, working hands, hands that had clearly been doing something every day for twenty years while mine counted turtles.

"Nice gate," I said, because I am a coward.

"We should talk inside."

"We can talk right here."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, maybe, or respect, quickly suppressed. She uncrossed her arms. "Fine. Here. You got the lawyer's letter?"

"I got the deed. I got the certified letter. I got a lot of letters lately." I kept my voice level. Twenty years of practice keeping my voice level. "What I haven't gotten is an explanation for why my name is on a piece of property I last saw when I was twenty-six."

"Because Mom put it there."

"That's not an explanation, Wren, that's a mechanism."

"Well, excuse me, Dr. Pemberton." The title landed like a slap, and I realized she'd done it on purpose. "The explanation is that Mom was dying and she asked me what to do with the place and I said put all three names on it, and she said, and I quote, 'Wren, that girl will never come back unless you make it legally inconvenient.' So. Legally inconvenient."

The words went through me sideways. "You told her to put my name on it?"

"I told her to do whatever would get you back here." Wren's voice didn't rise, but it sharpened, the way a blade sharpens on a strop. "Because somebody in this family had to have a strategy, Jude, and you weren't available for consultation. You were busy. Twenty years of busy."

"I was building a career."

"You were hiding."

The word hit some load-bearing wall inside me. I heard myself say, too fast, "I don't hide. I work. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Wren took a step closer. Up close she smelled of sulfur spray and coffee, the exact smell of our childhood summers. "Because from where I stand, you took one bad year — one bad, awful, criminal year, and I'm not saying it wasn't — and you used it as an exit. You left me here. You left Mom here. You left *this*—" she gestured at the whole valley of vines with one furious arm, "—and then you sent birthday cards with generic messages like you were a dentist's office confirming an appointment."

"I sent cards—"

"With *generic messages*, Jude."

"Because I didn't know what to say!" It came out louder than I intended, loud enough that a crow lifted off the fence post. "What was I supposed to say? *Sorry I abandoned you, sorry I let Mom down, sorry I couldn't walk into a room without wondering who'd heard about the scandal?* Every time I picked up the phone I rehearsed something and every rehearsal sounded pathetic, so I mailed a card instead. That's not hiding. That's cowardice with postage."

For a moment neither of us spoke. Somewhere down the rows a wind machine ticked over, testing itself against a sky that didn't need it yet.

"Cowardice with postage," Wren repeated, and to my horror she laughed — a short, cracked sound that was half sob. "God. You always could turn a phrase. Mom used to say you got the words and I got the dirt."

"She said that?"

"She said lots of things. You wouldn't know. You weren't here." And just like that the laugh was gone and the blade was back. "So let's talk terms, since you're here. I want to harvest. This is the first year the Pinot Noir block is ready for anything serious, first year the market's right, first year in a decade I've had the crew to do it properly. I've put everything into these vines. Everything. I want one real harvest, and then we talk about the future."

"And if the future is selling?"

"Then we sell after. Together. But not before. Not now."

I made myself breathe. "Wren, I looked at the numbers. The orchard loses money. It has lost money for years — I saw the tax filings. Every season you keep it running, the debt gets deeper and the three of us get more entangled with each other, which, forgive me, does not appear to be good for anyone's health."

"The tax filings show what I let them show." Her chin came up. "I've been carrying this place on credit cards and stubbornness, yes. Because it was Mom's. Because it was ours. Because somebody had to believe it was worth saving even when everyone else—" she stopped herself, but I heard the end of the sentence anyway. *Even when everyone else left.*

"That's not a business plan," I said gently. "That's grief with a budget."

"Oh, that's *good*." She nodded slowly, dangerously. "Grief with a budget. Write that down, why don't you, in one of your little notebooks. You always did think feelings were data you could file away."

"I think feelings are real and they're also terrible reasons to bankrupt three people!"

"Then sell me your share!" she burst out. "If it's such a disaster, sign it over, take whatever pittance the accountant says it's worth, and go back to your turtles! At least then you'd be consistent!"

And there it was — the door she'd been walking me toward since I got out of the truck. Sell. Leave. Make the leaving official, notarized, permanent. And the terrible thing, the thing I sat with later that night, was how much of me wanted to sign. How easy it would be. One signature and the orchard stops existing again, the past goes back in its drawer, and I return to my measured life where nothing bleeds.

But something in me — some small, stubborn, unscientific thing — looked at her cracked hands and thought: *she stayed. Through Mom's dying, through the frost years, through all of it, she stayed, and she thinks I'm about to leave twice.*

"No," I said.

Wren blinked. "No?"

"No, I'm not selling. And I'm not signing anything over. If this place is going to be decided, I get a vote. That's presumably why you put my name on the deed."

"I put your name on the deed to bring you *home*, not to give you veto power over the only thing I care about!"

"Well, congratulations, you got both."

We stood there breathing hard at each other like two weather systems refusing to merge. A hawk circled over the far block, indifferent. I became aware, distantly, that we were shouting in a driveway, that the neighbor's kid was watching from the fence line with the open delight of a child witnessing adult collapse, and that none of it mattered, because underneath the shouting we were saying older things. *You left. You stayed. You left. You stayed.* Twenty years compressed into a driveway argument about agriculture.

"This is pointless," Wren said finally, dragging her glove off finger by finger. "This is exactly what Mom predicted, you know. She said, 'Those two will either kill each other or save each other, and either way it'll be loud.'"

"She said that?"

"She said a lot of things at the end. People do." Something moved behind her eyes — grief, surfacing and diving again like something big under a boat. "Go home, Jude. Think about the numbers. Think about whether you actually want to be here or whether you just want to win."

"I don't want to win."

"Everyone wants to win." She turned toward the rows, then stopped, her back to me. For a second I thought she might say something else — something true, something soft. Instead she said, "Watch the gate on your way out. It sticks."

* * *

I drove home with my hands at ten and two and my jaw aching from clenching, and I did not cry until I reached the stretch of coast road where the ocean opens up wide and blue and indifferent, and then I cried the way I do everything — efficiently, with an eye on the clock.

Dev was waiting at the station when I got in, which he usually was, sitting on the lab bench eating an apple with the calm of a man who has never once been legally entangled with his family.

"So?" he said. "How was the reunion?"

"It was a natural disaster with dialogue."

He waited. Dev is very good at waiting; it's most of what he does. He's the station's tech manager, a broad, unhurried man who fixes the boats and the computers and, apparently, me, though he'd deny the last one. He handed me the apple. I took it, which surprised us both.

"There was shouting," I said. "There was a metaphor about grief having a budget. There may have been a moment where I almost signed away my inheritance out of spite, and then didn't, out of a different spite."

"Sounds healthy."

"It was *awful*. It was like being fifteen again except now we have lawyers." I bit the apple. It was mealy. I ate it anyway. "And then, as if the day needed garnish, when I got back to my truck there was an envelope under the wiper. Hand-delivered. Cardboard stock. Very expensive."

Dev raised his eyebrows. I pulled the envelope from my bag and held it up. Heavy cream paper, a law firm's letterhead embossed in the corner: *Halloway & Finch, Attorneys at Law, Portland.*

"Elias's people," I said.

"You opened it?"

"I opened it in the truck, which was a mistake, because then I had to drive forty minutes knowing what was in it." I smoothed the letter flat on the bench between us. "He's offering to buy us out. Both of us. Full price, appraised, plus — I quote — 'a settlement reflecting Mr. Voss's desire to resolve this matter generously and move forward with goodwill.' He wants the whole orchard. He wants it clean."

Dev read the letter over my shoulder, which was unnecessary, since I'd memorized it somewhere around mile twelve. "'Generously,'" he said. "'Goodwill.' Huh."

"What?"

"Nothing. Just — you met him, right? Voss. Back in the day."

"I worked for him for six years. He taught me everything I know about population modeling and nothing at all about loyalty."

"So does generosity sound like him?"

I started to say yes, because Elias was charming, Elias was generous, Elias bought the whole lab dinner after every grant cycle and remembered everyone's children's names. But the apple was sour in my mouth and the question sat there, patient, and I heard myself answer honestly instead.

"No," I said slowly. "Elias doesn't give. Elias invests. There's a difference, and I spent six years learning it too late." I looked down at the letter, at the smooth corporate language wrapped around a man I had once admired more than my own reflection. "He doesn't want an orchard. Elias hasn't wanted an orchard in his life. He wants something else, and he's using my mother's vines to buy it."

"Like what?"

"I don't know yet." I folded the letter along its creases, once, twice, until it was a small hard rectangle I could close my hand around. "But he taught me that too. Read the data before you trust the conclusion."

Dev smiled. "There's hope for you yet."

Outside, the tide was coming in, steady and total, erasing the morning's footprints off the flats one measured inch at a time. I watched it through the window and thought about Wren's face when she'd said *you were hiding*, and about the offer in my fist, and about how Elias Voss had spent ten years being generous to people who didn't know what he'd done.

Monday morning, I wrote two letters. One to Halloway & Finch, declining the offer.

One to Elias himself, asking for a meeting.

I addressed the second one three times before I got the name right.
