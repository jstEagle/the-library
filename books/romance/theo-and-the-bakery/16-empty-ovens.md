## 16. Empty Ovens

The boardroom on the forty-second floor had a view of the whole city, and for eleven years Theo Marchetti had loved it — the grid of streets going gold at dusk, the river stitching light through the middle of everything, the sense that he stood above it all and could reach down and touch any piece of it he wanted.

Today the view looked like a map of everything he'd failed to hold.

"—a clear conflict of interest," Halvorsen was saying, his voice dry as old paper. "A silent acquisition of a property in which the CEO has a personal entanglement. Funds routed through a shell entity. Mr. Marchetti, did you honestly believe this wouldn't surface?"

Theo sat at the long table with his hands flat on the wood, the way his father had taught him to sit when the numbers were bad. *Own the table,* Enzo Marchetti used to say. *Even when the table's burning.*

"The acquisition was sound," Theo said. "The block's value—"

"The block." Halvorsen almost smiled. "You mean the café."

Around the table, twelve faces watched him with the particular pity of people who have already decided. Theo felt the word land where they intended it to: *entanglement.* As if Astrid were a liability line item. As if ten years of wanting her were a regulatory problem.

"I believed," Theo said quietly, "that what I did with my own money was my own business."

"Your own money is our money. That was the first thing your father ever taught this board." Halvorsen folded his hands. "The vote is a formality at this point, but we'll take it. All in favor of removing Theodore Marchetti as chief executive, effective immediately?"

Eleven hands rose. One abstained — Mrs. Okafor, who had known his father, who caught Theo's eye for half a second with something that might have been sorrow before she looked down at her papers.

Twelve years old, Theo thought absurdly. He'd been twelve when he first sat outside this room on a leather bench, waiting for his father, eating a cannoli from the bakery two blocks down. He had thought then that power looked like this: tall windows, quiet voices, men whose suits never wrinkled. He had spent thirty years climbing toward the bench.

He should have stayed at the bakery counter. The cannoli had been better there anyway.

* * *

He resigned in writing because they asked him to, and because some part of him — the part still standing guard over the family name, the part that had walked out of a bakery kitchen ten years ago without saying goodbye — insisted on doing even surrender neatly. He signed. He shook Halvorsen's hand. He took the elevator down forty-two floors with a cardboard box that held, in the end, almost nothing: a framed photo of his parents, a paperweight Julian had given him when Voss Development closed its first deal, a coffee mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST BAKER, a joke gift from Astrid, a lifetime ago, that he had never once let out of his sight.

The doorman at his building didn't ask questions. The elevator rose thirty-one floors without meeting anyone. And then the doors opened onto the penthouse, and Theo stood in the entryway with his box under his arm and understood, with the slow certainty of ice forming, that he had nowhere left to go that wasn't here.

The apartment was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Floor-to-ceiling glass, the city laid out below like a circuit board catching fire with evening. Italian marble that remembered the warmth of feet. A kitchen worth more than the entire block he'd tried to buy — a kitchen with two ovens that had never once been used, because Theo Marchetti ate standing up over the sink like a man in hiding, and because baking alone in a penthouse was a kind of grief he couldn't afford to feel.

He set the box on the island. He poured himself three fingers of the good scotch he kept for celebrations and drank it standing at the window while the sky went from copper to bruise.

Everything, he thought. I have everything.

The apartment. The watches in their case. The car in the garage he hadn't driven in a month because driving meant arriving somewhere, and every somewhere he wanted to arrive was either lost or furious with him. He had the family name, polished and hollowed out like a trophy cup. He had a bank account that could buy Astrid's building four times over — had, in fact, tried exactly that, through a shell company, like a coward, like his father, and the check was sitting in her café right now or maybe already torn into pieces small enough to swallow.

She'd found the transfer receipt. Of course she had. He'd imagined telling her himself, imagined her face softening — *you bought back the bakery, Theo, you came back for me* — and instead she'd read the truth off a bank slip: that he'd done it again. Spent money where words should have been. Chosen her future for her, silently, the way he'd chosen to leave her, silently, ten years ago.

*Pieces, not the whole.* Her voice, from the doorway of the café, cold enough to frost the glass between them. *I've spent ten years lending pieces of my heart and taking them back intact. If you can't risk being refused, you haven't changed.*

He'd stood there holding the returned check and felt something structural give way inside him, some beam he'd assumed was load-bearing.

Now he refilled the scotch and carried it to the couch and sat down in the middle of all that beauty like a man sitting in an airport at 3 a.m., and let the silence come down around him like snow.

* * *

The knock came at nine-forty. Three sharp raps, no hesitation. Only one person in the world knocked like that — like the door owed him money.

Theo opened it and Julian Voss filled the frame, coat still buttoned wrong, snow melting in his hair, eyes red-rimmed and blazing. He looked, for the first time in twenty years, unmanaged. Ungoverned. Like a man who had driven across the city in a storm because the alternative was sitting still with what he knew.

"You leaked it," Theo said. It wasn't a question. The headline had run that morning — MARCHETTI HEIR BUYING BACK LOST LOVE? — and there was only one person alive who had access to the acquisition file and a reason to burn it down.

Julian stepped past him into the apartment without being invited, the way he'd been walking into Theo's spaces since they were fourteen, since the bakery on Halsey Street, since two boys had split a stolen loaf behind the ovens and sworn nothing would ever come between them except maybe flour futures.

"Sit down," Julian said.

"It's my apartment."

"Then sit down in it." Julian shrugged off his wet coat and dropped it over a chair that cost more than a car, careless, and Theo realized with a distant shock that Julian was shaking. Not with rage. Or not only rage. With the specific tremor of a man holding something together by hand.

Theo sat. Julian didn't. He stood over the couch like a prosecutor, and the city glittered behind him through the glass, indifferent.

"The file," Julian said. "Page forty. Go on, say it. Say what you made me."

"Julian—"

"*Say it.*" His voice cracked on the second word, and that crack was worse than any shout. "Because I've been trying to say it to myself all day and I keep getting stuck on page forty. Page forty, Theo. Your name as silent buyer. And next to it—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "Next to it, her building. Marked *retain.* Not develop. Not flip. Retain. You were never going to build anything. You were buying her a future she didn't ask you for, with my signature on the paperwork, and you let me walk into that café and offer her a deal — you let me be the villain in her story so you could be the ghost in the machine."

"I was going to tell you."

"When?" Julian laughed, and it was an awful sound, stripped of everything that had ever made him funny. "After closing? At the ribbon cutting? Were you going to stand there with scissors and explain why your oldest friend spent six months doing your dirty work?" He turned away, braced both hands on the back of a chair, head bowed. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone rough. "Twenty-two years, Theo. You taught me to drive in your father's delivery van. I held your hair back when your mother died — no, wait, that's backwards, you held mine when my mother died, same winter, remember? Same funeral home. We were seventeen and we swore—" He stopped again. Swallowed. "And you handed me a shell company and a lie and let me be the man who nearly put Astrid Ravensworth on the street."

"She was never going to lose the building."

"That's not the point and you know it's not the point!" Julian spun around. "God, you've always done this. You fix things with money and call it love and nobody's allowed to be angry because look, everything worked out, the math is clean. Your father did it. You watched him do it your whole childhood — buy Mom flowers after he missed her recital, buy the whole team dinner after he missed your championship game. Money as apology. Money as presence. Money instead of—" He gestured at Theo, at all of him. "*Instead of showing up.*"

"I showed up." Theo heard how thin it sounded even as he said it. "I flew back in a storm. I walked five miles through a whiteout to get to that café."

"And then what did you do? You arranged a payment. You found the receipt before she found it and you still didn't say a word." Julian shook his head slowly. "You were in the room with her, Theo. Snowed in. Days. You baked bread with her by firelight — don't look surprised, she told me, after, when she was deciding whether to hate us both — and you *still* reached for the checkbook."

The scotch sat untouched on the table. Theo stared at it. Somewhere below, the city hummed, millions of lives, none of them his business anymore.

"What do you want from me, Julian?" he said finally, and his voice came out low and wrecked. "You want me to say I'm sorry? Fine. I'm sorry. I'm sorry I used you. I'm sorry about the headline. I'm sorry about all of it. Is that what you drove through a storm for?"

"No." Julian came around the chair and sat down across from him, elbows on knees, and for a moment he just looked at Theo — really looked, the way only someone who had known you at nineteen could look, seeing straight through the suit and the name and the forty-second-floor view to whatever was underneath. "I want you to say the true thing. The one under the sorry."

"There isn't one."

"There's always one. There's one under every sorry you've ever given me." Julian leaned forward. "Why didn't you tell her? Ten years ago, when your father went under — why disappear instead of telling her? Why pay her debts in secret now? Why buy the building instead of asking her to dinner like a human being? What are you actually afraid of, Theo? Because it isn't rejection. Rejection you could survive; you're arrogant enough. It's something else."

The silence stretched. The ice in Theo's glass ticked as it melted. He thought about lying — he was good at lying, he'd been lying elegantly for a decade, to boards and buyers and himself — and he looked at Julian's face, ruined and loyal and waiting, and found that the machinery wouldn't start.

"Say it," Julian whispered. "I'll carry it. Whatever it is, I'll carry it. That's what I came for. Not the apology. The weight."

And Theo Marchetti, who had owned tables and companies and skylines, who had never once in his adult life said a true sentence at full volume, heard himself speak.

"I'm terrified," he said, and his voice broke open on the second word like bread tearing, "of being loved without my money."

It hung in the air. He couldn't take it back. He hadn't known he was going to say it until it was already out, and once it was out, more followed, thirty years of it, tumbling like a shelf collapsing.

"My father loved us with invoices. That was the model. You need something, I provide it, we call that devotion and nobody has to be *known.* And Astrid—" He pressed his fists against his mouth for a moment, then lowered them. "Astrid knew me when I had nothing. When I was a kid with flour burns on my arms sleeping above a failing bakery. She loved that version. And I left her, and I got rich, and somewhere in ten years I started believing — God, I actually started believing — that the money was the only reason anyone would stay. That if I showed up empty-handed she'd finally see what I really am."

"Which is what?"

"A man who doesn't know how to be loved for free." He laughed, and it hurt coming out. "I bought her building because if I gave her the building, then even if she chose me, I'd know — I'd *know* — it was partly the building. Partly the rescue. Some insurance policy against the terror of just being Theo. Just a guy. Standing in her kitchen with nothing to offer but showing up."

Julian was quiet for a long time. Outside, the snow came down through the light of the windows, fat and slow, erasing the city floor by floor.

"You know what's pathetic?" Julian said at last, and his voice had gone gentle, which was somehow worse than the fury. "She would have taken you broke. She told me once — years ago, at that community night, before any of this — she said, 'The worst thing Theo ever did wasn't leaving. It was assuming I needed him to be impressive.' She figured that out about you a decade ago, Theo. You're the last one holding the secret."

Theo bent forward and put his face in his hands. He was aware, distantly, that he was crying — not dramatically, not the way executives cried in films, one beautiful tear — just leaking, steadily, like a cracked pipe, in front of his oldest friend, in an apartment that suddenly felt less like a home than a very expensive waiting room.

"I lost the vote today," he said into his hands. "I resigned. I have a box. There's a mug in it."

"I know the mug," Julian said. "She gave you that mug."

"Nineteen. My birthday. She said, 'Now you can't pretend to be great at this.'"

"And you kept it for fifteen years and never used a single oven in this place." Julian looked around at the dead marble kitchen, the gleaming unused range, and something in his face shifted — grief and anger making room, grudgingly, for something almost like hope. "You know what I did today, after the story ran? After I torched my best friend out of spite and heartbreak?"

"What?"

"I called my lawyer and pulled the deed history on Halsey Street. The whole block. Every parcel." Julian reached into his jacket and pulled out a folded printout, creased and damp, and set it on the table between them. "There's a clause, Theo. An old one. From the founding days — the neighborhood charter. Continuous operation by public competition. It's how the original bakers settled boundary disputes. Any tenant who wins a public bake-off holds priority claim to their lease. It's never been tested in a hundred years, but it's in the deed, and Astrid's lawyer will find it if mine did."

Theo lifted his head. "What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you the auction is in nine days, and there's a Winter Fair, and Voss Development hired a champion pastry chef to enter it and bury the clause forever." Julian stood, picked up his ruined coat, and looked down at Theo with red eyes and a jaw set like their fathers' generation. "I'm telling you I'm not doing your dirty work anymore. I'm not fixing anything for you, and I'm not paying for anything, and I am *not* forgiving you yet — that's going to take longer than nine days. But she's going to walk into that tent alone against a professional, betting everything, and I can't watch that happen and neither can you."

"So what do we do?"

"We don't do anything." Julian pulled the door open. Snow swirled in past his shoulders. "You're going to show up with nothing. No money, no name, no plan. No checkbook. You're going to stand in front of her empty-handed and ask her — out loud, with words, like a person — if she'll let you learn. And then, Theo—" He paused in the doorway, silhouetted against the corridor light, and his voice roughened. "Then you're going to let her say no. If she says no. You're going to survive it. Because that's the whole test. That's the only one there ever was."

The door closed behind him.

Theo stood alone in the enormous quiet apartment, the city burning gold below, the melted snow darkening the marble at his feet. On the table lay Julian's folded printout, and beside it, in the cardboard box, the mug.

He picked up the mug. He carried it into the dead kitchen, filled it with water, and set it down on the counter beside the ovens that had never been lit.

Nine days. Then he would go to her with empty hands, and he would finally, finally risk the only thing he'd never gambled.

Himself.
