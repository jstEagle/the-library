## 2. Dear Stranger

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, wedged between an invoice from the linen company and a postcard from a vineyard that wanted him to sell its rosé as "an experience."

Theo Marchetti stood in the doorway of his office, mail fanned in one hand, coffee going cold in the other, and read the envelope twice. *Marchetti Events, Attn: Festival Pen-Pal Exchange.* The handwriting was round and cheerful, the kind of handwriting that belonged to someone who owned a label maker and enjoyed using it.

He knew what it was. Dot Kowalski had cornered him at the chamber of commerce meeting three weeks ago, waving a clipboard like a traffic cop, explaining that every business in Alder Creek was being enrolled — *enrolled*, her word, with the gentle authoritarianism of a woman who had run the harvest festival for thirty years and considered voluntary participation a technicality — in an anonymous letter exchange. "Builds community," she'd said. "Bridges divides." She'd looked at him a little too meaningfully when she said it, but Dot looked at everyone meaningfully; it was how she got people to donate porta-potties.

Theo had said what he always said to Dot, which was *sure, whatever you need,* because saying no to Dot Kowalski cost more energy than it saved.

Now the letter sat on his desk like a small obligation.

He should have thrown it out. He had a wedding to plan — the Delgado wedding, two hundred guests, a bride who had changed the color palette four times and a groom who kept asking if they could just do it at a brewery — and a corporate retreat in three weeks, and a venue dispute he could already feel coming on like weather.

Instead, he sat down, slit the envelope open, and read:

*Dear Stranger,*

*Welcome! You've been paired with a fellow member of our wonderful community. Over the next eight weeks, exchange letters weekly. Share your thoughts, your week, your favorite things about Alder Creek. Identities remain confidential until the festival's final night, when pen pals meet at the reveal bonfire! (Participation optional but encouraged. — Dot)*

*To get you started: What brought you to your line of work?*

*Warmly, Your Pen Pal*

There was no name, of course. Just a number assigned by the committee — he was Seven, apparently, which felt less like an identity and more like a diagnosis.

Theo read it again. *What brought you to your line of work.*

He thought about the honest answer. He'd gotten into event planning because his mother had died in February and weddings were the only place grief and joy shared a room without apologizing to each other, and he'd needed to stand in rooms like that. He thought about how that would sound on paper to a stranger. He thought about how he hadn't said any of it out loud, ever, not even to his sister Priya, who asked the way sisters ask — sideways, patiently, leaving doors open he never walked through.

He got out a legal pad, because writing by hand made jokes come easier than typing did, and began.

*Dear Stranger,*

*You first. What brought me to my line of work? A client this morning informed me that she wants live swans at her engagement party. Not swan imagery. Not a swan motif. Live swans. On a lake that does not have swans. She wants me to source them, transport them, and — I quote — "make sure they feel included in the celebration."*

*I have been doing this for eleven years. I have hung chandeliers from trees in a windstorm. I have talked a father of the bride down from a balcony. I once spent four hours of a reception quietly following a drunk groomsman around a golf course making sure he didn't wander into the pond. But I have never been asked to make waterfowl feel emotionally invested in a marriage.*

*So that's what brought me here. Sunk cost, mostly, and a suspicious talent for smiling while dying inside.*

*Your turn. Tell me something worse than swans. I need to know there's competition.*

*— Seven*

He read it over, felt the brief hot embarrassment of a man who has just confessed to a stranger, and then decided he didn't care. It wasn't like anyone would know it was him. That was the whole architecture of the thing — anonymity as a fire door. You could say anything through one of those.

He mailed it Wednesday morning on his way to a site visit, dropping it into the blue box outside the hardware store without slowing down, the way you'd toss away gum.

* * *

The reply came the following Tuesday, and Theo almost missed it, because it arrived disguised as junk mail — a plain white envelope with his office address written in small, precise script, the ink slightly smudged as though the writer had been left-handed or impatient or both.

He opened it standing at his desk, half-listening to Priya leave a voicemail about Sunday dinner, and then stopped listening entirely.

*Dear Seven,*

*You asked for something worse than swans. I regret to inform you that I can do better.*

*Last month I ran a vow renewal for a couple celebrating twenty-five years. Lovely people. The husband, however, insisted on a "surprise element" that his wife knew nothing about, and refused to tell me what it was, on the grounds that I might "ruin the spontaneity." The surprise turned out to be a live owl. In a cage. Released during the vows. The owl did not understand the assignment. It flew directly into the tent, panicked, took out a floral archway, and perched, trembling, on the head table for forty minutes while we evacuated the elderly and the caterer defended the crab cakes. The wife loved it. The owl is fine. I am not.*

*But here is the thing about swans: they mate for life, which means if one dies mid-ceremony you are legally obligated to cancel the wedding. Check your contracts. I'm serious. Add a swan clause.*

*As for what brought me to this work — I'll trade you. You tell me why a man who clearly hates his clients keeps doing it, and I'll tell you why a woman who invoices for joy keeps smiling.*

*— Four*

Theo read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you reread a sentence in a contract that changes everything downstream.

*Add a swan clause.* He laughed — actually laughed, alone in his office at nine in the morning, a sound that startled him enough that he glanced at the door to make sure no one had heard. When was the last time a piece of mail had done that? When was the last time anything had?

And underneath the laugh, something sharper: *a woman who invoices for joy.* Whoever Four was, she'd handed him a coin with two faces and dared him to call it. He recognized the move. It was the move of someone who'd been underestimated before and had learned to hand people the knife handle-first, just to watch what they did with it.

He liked it. That was the alarming part. He liked it the way you like a sparring partner who doesn't flinch.

He wrote back that night, after the Delgado tasting, sitting at his kitchen table with a beer he didn't finish, and found — to his mild horror — that the letter came out of him fast, unguarded, like water finding a crack.

*Dear Four,*

*An owl. During the vows. I need you to understand that I have told this story to three people today and taken full credit for it. By Friday it will have happened to me personally. This is how the industry works — misery is communal property, authorship is negotiable.*

*You want to know why I keep doing it. Fine. Here's the part I don't put in brochures: I'm good at it. Not competent — good. I can walk into a room where two families hate each other and leave with everyone slow-dancing. I can look at a floor plan and see exactly where the fight will break out and station the bartender there. There is a version of control available in other people's happiest moments that I have never once managed in my own life, and I have built an entire career renting it out by the hour.*

*That's either very honest or very sad. Possibly both. Don't answer that.*

*Your turn. Why does a woman who invoices for joy keep smiling? And don't give me the brochure version. I can smell brochure copy through an envelope.*

*— Seven*

He hesitated over the mailbox slot the next morning — actually hesitated, envelope in hand, aware that he'd just told a stranger something he'd never told Priya, something he'd barely admitted to himself in the shower at six a.m. when the defenses were still down. Then he let it go and heard it hit the bottom of the box, and drove to work feeling lighter and more exposed than he had any right to feel over a piece of paper.

* * *

The week that followed was the kind that usually ground him down to nubs. The Delgado bride cried at the tasting — happy tears, mercifully — but the groom's mother declared the chicken "aggressive." A vendor doubled a quote overnight. The venue dispute he'd been dreading materialized right on schedule: a June Saturday, double-booked inquiry, and when he pulled the file he saw the competing planner's name and felt his jaw set before he'd consciously registered it.

Voss. Marguerite Voss, of Voss & Vine Events. Eleven years in the same town, the same market, the same clients, and in the last two years she'd taken three bookings out from under him — or he'd taken them from her, depending on which version of events you subscribed to, and Theo subscribed firmly to his. She was meticulous and cold and she priced like someone who'd never once worried about making rent, and every interaction with her left him feeling like he'd been handled.

He drafted a pointed email about the June date, deleted the word *again* twice, and sent something professionally neutral that still managed, he hoped, to radiate grievance.

Then Tuesday came, and the white envelope with the smudged ink, and he forgot he was angry anywhere at all.

*Dear Seven,*

*You're right, that was both. I'm choosing to frame it as brave.*

*Why do I keep smiling? Because the smile is load-bearing. Take it out and the whole structure comes down. You of all people should understand — you've built a career on being the calmest person in the room. I've built mine on being the most pleasant one. Between us we could probably fake an entire functional human being.*

*Here's what nobody tells you about planning joy for a living: you get very good at the mechanics of happiness and very bad at the experience of it. I can tell you the exact thread count of linens that photograph best at golden hour. I cannot remember the last time I was at a party where I wasn't working. I attended my cousin's birthday dinner last month and caught myself mentally repositioning the candles. There is something wrong with us, Seven. Professionally speaking.*

*Tell me something true that has nothing to do with work. I'll go first: I keep a list of every meal that has ever made me cry, and there are eleven entries, and I am not embarrassed.*

*— Four*

*Between us we could probably fake an entire functional human being.* Theo sat with that line for a long time, long enough that the office went dim around him and the parking lot emptied and the streetlights came on down Main Street. It was a joke. It was also, unmistakably, not a joke, the way the truest things never quite are.

Eleven meals that made her cry. He tried to imagine the list. He tried to imagine the woman who kept it — precise handwriting, dry as gin, funny in a way that landed like a tap on the shoulder rather than a punch — and found that he couldn't stop trying.

He wrote back the next night.

*Dear Four,*

*Something true, nothing to do with work. All right.*

*I haven't cooked a real meal for another person in four years. I own good knives. I own a Dutch oven that has never once held anything but doubt. Every dinner party I've thrown in that time has been catered, which my sister says is a personality disorder, and she's not wrong. I think somewhere along the way I decided that feeding people was too much — too intimate, too easy to get wrong — so I outsourced it, the way I outsource most things that matter. I plan the single most intimate day of strangers' lives and I won't cook pasta for a friend. Make of that what you will.*

*Also: I rescheduled a site visit today to be home for the mail. I'm telling you this against my better judgment because I suspect you'd find it funny, and because I'm starting to suspect that's the whole point of this arrangement.*

*What's on the list? Give me the top three crying meals. I need data.*

*— Seven*

He dropped it in the box Thursday instead of waiting for the weekend route, and hated himself a little for the eagerness, and did it anyway.

* * *

Her reply arrived Monday — early, which meant she'd written back fast too, which meant he wasn't the only one checking, and he chose to find that comforting rather than terrifying.

*Dear Seven,*

*Top three crying meals, in ascending order of devastation:*

*3. My grandmother's lemon rice soup, which I have not been able to recreate since she died, though I have tried eleven times, and yes, the number matters.*

*2. A bowl of pho in Portland on the worst night of my life, eaten alone at 11 p.m. in a restaurant full of strangers who had the decency to pretend not to notice me falling apart over noodles.*

*1. Toast. Buttered. Made by someone else, once, when I was too sad to make it myself. I have chased that toast for years and nothing has ever come close, and I believe this is because the primary ingredient was being known.*

*You outsourced feeding people. I outsourced being fed. Look at us. Two fully grown professionals, starving at our own tables.*

*As for the mail thing — I checked my box twice yesterday. Once on my way in and once on my way out, which is inefficient and undignified, and I regret nothing. I'm telling you this so you don't feel alone in your small humiliations. Consider it a professional courtesy.*

*— Four*

*The primary ingredient was being known.*

Theo read that sentence standing in his empty office, and something in his chest did a thing he didn't have vocabulary for — not pain, exactly, and not relief. More like a lock he'd forgotten was a lock, turning over.

He thought about the toast. He thought about whether he could make toast like that, for someone, someday, and whether the knives in his drawer would forgive him. He thought about a stranger across town — a woman, he was nearly sure, sharp and funny and hungry in the specific way of people who feed everyone else — checking her mailbox twice and admitting it.

He didn't know who she was. He found he didn't care, and then found, more unsettlingly, that he hoped he never had to care — that the festival's final night, the reveal bonfire, Dot's great design, could somehow be postponed indefinitely, because whoever Four was, she existed right now in the one form he could trust: words on paper, no history, no rivalry, no face to read and misread.

He wrote back five times that week. He was supposed to write once.

On Friday, sealing the fifth envelope, he caught himself smiling at nothing, and the smile felt strange on his face, like a suit that fit better than he remembered.

*Dear Four,* he'd written. *Tell me something you've never told anyone. I'll hold it. I'm surprisingly good at holding things — it's the letting go I never learned.*

He mailed it and walked back to his car under a sky going pink over the ridge, and behind him the blue box sat full of other people's letters, and he thought: *eight weeks. Dot said eight weeks.*

For the first time in years, Theo Marchetti wished a deadline would never come.
