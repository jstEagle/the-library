## 5. Swans Are a Liability

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was already the worst day of Theo Marchetti's week because Tuesdays were when his accountant called, and by the time he got home on Tuesday evenings he had usually been reminded of three separate ways his business was bleeding money and one way it might be committing tax fraud without meaning to.

He almost didn't open it. It sat on the hall table between a flyer for gutter cleaning and an invoice from the linen company, and it looked like all the other mail that wanted something from him. Cream envelope, no return address, his name written in handwriting that slanted left like it was in a hurry to get away from itself.

Then he remembered: the festival committee. The pen-pal thing. Dot Beaumont had cornered him at the hardware store six weeks ago with a clipboard and the unstoppable cheer of a woman who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered, and he'd signed the form mostly to make her stop talking. *Anonymous correspondence between local businesses! Building community!* He'd put down a fake-ish alias — not fake enough to be fraud, just fake enough to be funny — and forgotten about it entirely.

He opened it standing up in the hallway with his coat still on.

*Dear Stranger,* it began, and then, without any preamble whatsoever:

*I have been informed that I am now your pen pal, which I assume is either a punishment or a clerical error, and having met our festival committee, I know better than to rule out either. Before we begin, some ground rules. One: I am not interested in your horoscope. Two: if you are one of those people who describes themselves as "a foodie," I will report you to Dot and request reassignment. Three: tell me something true. Anything. I find that most people can't manage even one true sentence before lunch, and I'd like to know early what I'm working with.*

Theo read it twice. Then he laughed — actually laughed, alone in his own hallway, which hadn't happened since roughly the second Obama administration — and went into the kitchen and found a pen.

He wrote back that same night, mostly as a joke, entirely as a joke:

*Dear Stranger. Something true: this week a client asked me for live swans at her garden party. Swans. In July. In a venue with a koi pond and a bride's mother who is allergic to "anything with feathers or opinions." I explained that swans are, historically, a liability. She said she liked that they were dramatic. I said so is a gas leak. We are no longer speaking, but her deposit cleared, so who really won? Your turn. Tell me something true, since apparently we're doing that. I'll warn you now: mine get worse.*

He dropped it in the box outside the post office on Wednesday morning without thinking much about it, the way you'd toss a note into a well, and went about his week.

* * *

The reply came Saturday. He checked the mailbox out of habit — he always checked the mailbox, it was where invoices went to die — and there it was, cream envelope, left-slanting handwriting, and something in his chest did a small unauthorized thing that he chose not to examine.

*Dear Stranger. Something true: I once planned a wedding where the groom fainted during the vows, the best man caught the rings in his teeth because his hands were full of champagne, and the flower girl announced, at volume, that the bride was "already showing." The couple is still married. Happily, as far as I know. My point is that disasters are often load-bearing. Also: what do you do, exactly, that puts you in the path of swan enthusiasts? Don't answer if it's boring. Actually — do answer. Boring people are rarely this funny on paper.*

*P.S. Swans ARE a liability. They mate for life and then bite the hand that feeds them. There's a metaphor in there and I refuse to dig it out for free.*

He read it at his kitchen table with coffee going cold beside him, and then he read it again, slower, the way you'd reread a good paragraph in a novel to figure out how the trick worked. *Disasters are often load-bearing.* Who wrote like that? Who thought like that?

He should mention — because he was an honest man within reason — that he spent forty minutes that Sunday drafting his response. Not writing. Drafting. Crossing out sentences that sounded too eager, adding back sentences that sounded too cool, arriving finally at a tone he could only describe as *wry but available.* He had not put this much effort into a communication since a proposal to the Whitmore account in 2019, and the Whitmore account was worth eleven thousand dollars.

This was worth nothing. This was worth a stranger with good punctuation.

*Dear Stranger. What I do: events. Weddings, mostly, corporate things when the wedding well runs dry. I am very good at it, which sounds like bragging until you realize it means I spend my life making other people's perfect days while my own apartment contains one chair and a smoke detector I keep meaning to test. That was two truths. You're getting ahead of me. Something true from my week: I watched a father-of-the-bride cry during the toast and had to pretend I had something in my eye, and the something in my eye was real, and I've decided not to look too closely at why. Your move. And a question: does everyone lie to you, or just clients? You write like someone who gets lied to professionally.*

He mailed it Monday morning. He told himself the small lift he felt dropping it through the slot was about exercise, or sunlight, or the coffee.

* * *

By the third exchange, Theo had noticed something alarming, which was that he had started structuring his week around the mailbox.

Not consciously. Consciously, he was a grown man with a business to run. But when the Hendricks site visit — a vineyard two towns over, forty minutes each way — came up for scheduling, he heard himself say, "Thursday afternoon doesn't work, can we do Friday?" Thursday was a delivery day. He knew the mail came around eleven. He rescheduled a paying client to be home for a letter from someone whose name he didn't know and whose gender he'd stopped trying to guess after the third letter made the question feel irrelevant.

*Dear Stranger,* she — he'd settled on she, privately, based on nothing he'd admit to — wrote that week. *Something true: I don't trust people who are nice to waitstaff only when the client is watching. I once fired a vendor mid-event for snapping at a teenager carrying a tray. The client was furious. The teenager cried. I gave the kid my jacket, which sounds noble until you learn it was a rental and I paid for it. Worth it. Question for you: what's the worst thing you've ever done for a client? The real worst. Not the funny-worst.*

And Theo, sitting at his sister Priya's kitchen table on a Sunday morning while her kids built a fort out of his invoice folders, wrote back the truth, which surprised him:

*Real worst: I let a bride believe her late grandmother's recipe cards had been "safely archived" when in fact I lost them. A caterer misplaced the box. I replaced them with printed copies and never told her. She stood up at the reception and said the dessert tasted "just like Nana's" and I stood there and let her say it. That was four years ago and I still think about it more than I think about most actual crimes. So. Now you know I'm a fraud with good penmanship. Still writing?*

Priya looked over his shoulder as he sealed the envelope. "Who's that?"

"Nobody."

"You're smiling at nobody."

"I'm smiling at the kids' fort."

"The fort is attacking your leg." She plucked the envelope from his hand before he could react and read the front. "'Stranger.' Theo. Is this the festival pen-pal thing? Dot's project?" Her face did something complicated and delighted. "You signed up for Dot's project?"

"It was sign up or hear about it forever."

"And now you're writing love letters to a stranger."

"They're not love letters. They're correspondence. It's community building."

"Mm." Priya handed the envelope back with the expression of a woman filing evidence. "Community building. On cream stationery. With a fountain pen, by the look of it. You bought a fountain pen for community building?"

"I already owned a fountain pen."

"You did not. You write contracts in ballpoint like a psychopath."

His niece, aged seven, looked up from the fort. "Uncle Theo has a girlfriend."

"I don't have a—" He stopped, recalibrated. "It's anonymous. Nobody knows who anybody is. That's the whole point."

"That's the whole point of dating apps too," Priya said, "and look how well that turned out for you."

He left earlier than usual that day, and mailed the letter on the way home, and hated how good it felt.

* * *

The trouble — the actual trouble, the trouble that would have consequences — started on a Wednesday, in the conference room of the Hollis Barn, with Marguerite Voss sitting across the table from him looking like winter had been tailored specifically for her.

They were co-managing the festival's main stage logistics, which meant they were legally required to be in the same room for ninety minutes every other week, a fact Theo suspected Dot had engineered with malice aforethought. Marguerite ran Voss & Co., premier event planning, and had spent three years being the thorn in the side of everything Theo built. She'd taken the Delacroix wedding out from under him. She'd taken the Ashford gala. She'd once, allegedly, described his work as "competent, in the way a fire extinguisher is competent" to a room of people who repeated it to him within the hour.

"Marquee supplier," she said now, not looking up from her folder. "The committee needs a commitment by Friday. Whitfield Tents quoted us four thousand two hundred."

"I know. I negotiated it."

"I know you negotiated it. I also know Whitfield gave me a better number this morning."

Theo felt the familiar heat climb the back of his neck. "You went around me to my supplier."

"I went *to* a supplier. Suppliers aren't yours, Marchetti. They're independent businesses making rational choices." She finally looked up, and her eyes were gray and level and completely without mercy. "Three thousand eight. Same spec, delivery included. I sent the committee the quote an hour ago."

"You undercut me to a committee I'm volunteering for."

"You volunteered. I was enrolled." A flicker of something crossed her face when she said *enrolled* — fast, gone before he could name it — and then it was business again. "If you'd rather match the number, match the number. If you'd rather sulk, sulk efficiently. We have an agenda."

He matched the number. He matched it out of spite and then spent the rest of the meeting not hearing a word about generator placement because he was replaying the moment she'd said *enrolled*, because it had sounded — and this was insane, he knew it was insane — like something. Like a word with weight behind it. Like a woman describing a cage.

That night his pen pal's letter was waiting, and it was a good one, sharp and strange and warm underneath the armor, and he read it twice and laughed once and felt the day's anger drain out of him like air from a valve.

*Dear Stranger. Something true: today I won something I didn't want to win, and the winning felt like biting tinfoil. Have you ever done that? Beaten someone and then stood there holding the trophy thinking, well, this is ugly? I'm asking for a friend. The friend is me. The trophy is a tent.*

He sat with that one for a long time. *Beaten someone and then stood there holding the trophy thinking, well, this is ugly.* He'd done it that afternoon. He'd matched her price out of pure ego and then driven home gripping the wheel, thinking: *what is wrong with me, what is this even for, three years of this, over what?*

He wrote back:

*Dear Stranger. Yes. More times than I'll admit. Here's what I've noticed: the fights I win cleanest are the ones I understand least. Somebody crosses me and I cross back and it takes me years to ask what the crossing was actually about. Usually it's about something older than the person I'm fighting. Usually it's not about them at all. Something true from me: I have a rival. Professional. We've been circling each other so long I genuinely couldn't tell you who threw the first punch, and last week I realized I plan parts of my week around running into her, which is either strategy or something my therapist would bill me for. Anyway. Tell me about the tent. Tell me whether you kept it. Some trophies you should throw away.*

He read that over before sealing it and felt exposed in a way that had nothing to do with anonymity. Anonymity, he was beginning to understand, wasn't a wall. It was a window with the glass painted out on one side only. She couldn't see him. He could see himself perfectly.

* * *

Friday, the committee confirmed Whitfield at three thousand eight hundred, Marguerite's number, and Theo signed off on the email with a professionalism so polished it was practically hostile. She replied within the hour: *Noted. Thank you.* Four words. He read them eleven times looking for the knife in them and couldn't decide if finding none was better or worse.

Saturday's letter was waiting when he got home, and he made tea first — he never made tea first, tea was a during activity — and took the envelope to the good chair by the window, and opened it slowly, like a man rationing something.

*Dear Stranger. Kept the tent. Of course I kept it. Throwing away a trophy feels like admitting the fight was stupid, and I'm not ready for that conversation with myself. Ask me again in ten years. Something true: I keep a list. Mental. Of things I'll do "when things slow down." Learn the cello. Go to Lisbon. Call people back properly instead of texting. The list is long and nothing on it has ever happened, because things don't slow down, they just change shape. What's on your list? And don't say nothing. Everyone has a list. The sad ones are the people who've stopped keeping it.*

Outside his window, the streetlights came on. Theo sat in the good chair with his tea going cold and thought about Lisbon, and the cello, and a woman across town — he'd started imagining her across town, close, reachable — who kept trophies she hated and lists she ignored and wrote sentences that landed in his chest like darts thrown by someone who didn't know she was throwing.

He got out the fountain pen. He wrote:

*Mine: fix the smoke detector. Learn to cook one thing well instead of five things adequately. Say the thing I've been not-saying to my rival for three years, whatever it is — I honestly don't know anymore, which might be the truest thing I've ever written you. And go back to a certain barn on a certain road outside town, for reasons I also can't explain. Your turn, Stranger. And here's a new rule, since we're collecting them: next letter, tell me something you've never told anyone. I'll trade you one of mine. Fair warning — I've been saving one up, and it's heavy, and I think it's time somebody else held it for a while.*

He sealed it, addressed it, and set it on the hall table for morning. Then he stood there a moment in his quiet apartment with its one chair and its untested smoke detector, aware of a feeling he couldn't file anywhere: that the mailbox had become the most honest room in his house, and that somewhere across town, a woman with gray eyes and a left-slanting hand was about to hold a piece of him no one else had ever touched.

He just hoped, when the heavy thing came out, that she'd recognize it for what it was.

He had no way of knowing she already did.
