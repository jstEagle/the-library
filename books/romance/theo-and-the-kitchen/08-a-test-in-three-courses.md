## 8. A Test in Three Courses

Theo Marchetti had built a career on knowing things before other people knew them.

He knew which venues were about to lose their liquor licenses. He knew which florists padded their invoices and which caterers watered the champagne. He knew, within one handshake, whether a groom was going to cry at the altar and whether a bride's mother was going to make that crying expensive. This knowledge had never made him beloved, but it had made him good — and being good at something was the only kind of love Theo had ever trusted.

So when he sat down at his sister Priya's kitchen table on a Tuesday night with his pen pal's latest letter and felt the floor of his certainty tilt beneath him, he did what he always did with a problem he couldn't solve directly.

He set a trap.

* * *

It started, as most of his worst ideas did, with a glass of wine and an accusation from his sister.

"You're smiling at paper," Priya said, sliding into the chair across from him with her own glass and the particular expression of a woman who had been waiting all week to say something. "You know that, right? You've been smiling at paper for a month."

"I'm reading correspondence."

"You're *giggling*. I heard you giggle through the wall last Thursday. I thought someone had broken in and was tickling you."

"It was a funny letter." He turned it face-down on the table, which was a mistake, because it made it look like contraband, which it essentially was. "There's a woman in this town who thinks swans should be classified alongside open flames and trampolines as event liabilities, and she's right, and she's funnier about it than anyone I've ever hired."

"The swan thing." Priya sipped. "You mentioned the swan thing. You've mentioned the swan thing four times."

"Because it's a load-bearing detail of my emotional life now."

"Mm." She studied him over the rim of her glass with the patience of an older sister who had watched him burn through friendships like kindling for thirty-eight years. "And do you know who she is?"

"No idea. That's the point of the program. Anonymous. Dot's rules. You don't ask, you don't guess, you just write."

"And you've never once been tempted to guess?"

Theo looked at the letter, face-down, its handwriting hidden but not forgotten — the sharp leftward slant of it, the way the writer pressed hard enough to emboss the next page, the little blade of a crossbar on every *t*, like each word had been signed with a small knife. He'd caught himself, twice now, reading vendor emails and wondering. He'd stood in the parking lot of the Voss Events office for a full ninety seconds last week holding a contract he didn't need to deliver in person, telling himself he was there because the light was good and the coffee across the street was better.

Marguerite Voss had come out of that office with her blazer buttoned like armor and her chin up like a woman walking into weather, and she had not smiled at him, because she never smiled at him, and he had driven away feeling like a man who'd been caught doing something he hadn't done yet.

"No," he said.

"Liar," said Priya, fondly.

* * *

Here was the problem, laid out plainly on his kitchen table between the olive oil stain and the fruit bowl: he liked her.

Not Marguerite — or not only Marguerite, though the not-only part was becoming harder to ignore. He liked the stranger. The stranger wrote to him every Sunday night like clockwork, letters that arrived smelling faintly of bergamot and typed on actual paper because, she'd explained in her second letter, *email is where sincerity goes to be forwarded.* She complained about clients with a precision that read like affection. She noticed things — the way a tent pole leans before it fails, the way a mother-of-the-groom's smile goes brittle at the third hour of a reception — and she described them so exactly that Theo had started seeing the world through her eyes during his site visits, cataloguing details to send back to her like a man picking flowers.

Last week she had written: *I have decided that my entire profession is grief management with better lighting. We sell people one perfect day because we cannot sell them the thousand ordinary ones after.*

He had read that sentence eleven times. He had written back three drafts of a reply and hated all of them, because none of them were honest enough, and honesty with this stranger had begun to feel less like a risk and more like a debt.

And somewhere in the middle of all that — in the middle of Sunday letters and Wednesday replies and the strange new habit of checking his mailbox before he checked his phone — a thought had arrived that he didn't want and couldn't evict:

*What if I already know her?*

The town wasn't that big. The pool of people who wrote like this — surgical, wry, wounded in a place they kept carefully out of frame — was smaller still. And there was exactly one person in Cedar Hollow whose professional voice sounded, in his memory, like these letters read: clipped, elegant, every sentence ironed flat, with heat showing only at the seams.

But wanting it to be her was not evidence. Wanting it to be her was, in fact, the single best reason not to trust his own judgment. So Theo did what he did with venue contracts and unreliable vendors and every other thing in his life that could hurt him if he believed it too fast.

He verified.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, took shape over the next three days, and he hated himself a little more with each stage of it.

He told himself it was harmless. A controlled experiment. One false detail, planted in Sunday's letter, specific enough to trace and trivial enough to mean nothing. If the detail surfaced anywhere else — in gossip, in a vendor meeting, in an email — then his correspondent was someone with access to his business circle, and the field narrowed. If it never surfaced, fine. No harm. No foul. Just a man who'd lied to a stranger for science.

He chose the detail the way he'd choose a decoy centerpiece: plausible, forgettable, distinctive. In his Sunday letter, wedged into a paragraph complaining about a client's tasting menu, he wrote:

*The caterer has proposed a first course of chilled cucumber soup with dill oil, which sounds like something you'd serve to punish a man. I'm pushing back. If I have to eat one more spoonful of cold green disappointment I'm going to start planning my own funeral instead of other people's weddings.*

None of it was true. There was no cucumber soup. There was no dill oil. The tasting menu he'd attended that week had been braised short rib and a lemon tart he'd thought about for two days afterward. But the detail was textured, dated, specific — the kind of thing a person might repeat without realizing they were repeating it, the way you catch a cold.

Then he sealed the envelope, walked it to the box on Alder Street in the blue dark, and stood there for a moment with his hand still on the flap, feeling like a man who had slipped a note into a bottle and poisoned the water inside.

"You're a coward," he told the mailbox.

The mailbox declined to argue.

* * *

Wednesday morning brought two things: rain, and an email from Voss Events with the subject line **RE: Hollowbrook Barn — revised timeline**.

He opened it standing at his counter, coffee untouched, because opening Marguerite Voss's emails had become, since the letters began, a small daily sport — a fencing match conducted in CC fields and bolded deadlines. Her emails were masterpieces of hostile professionalism. *Per my previous message.* *As discussed.* *Happy to accommodate, schedule permitting.* Every one of them a glove thrown down with immaculate manners.

This one began normally. Timeline attached. Load-in moved to Thursday. Then, in the third paragraph, in the middle of a bullet point about service stations:

*— Please confirm your caterer's final menu by Friday. (And for the record, if anyone attempts to serve chilled cucumber soup with dill oil at a October wedding, I will personally escort them off the property. Some things are liabilities.)*

Theo read it four times.

The kitchen around him went very quiet, the way a room does when a floorboard settles somewhere upstairs — nothing changed, everything changed. Rain ticked against the window. The refrigerator hummed. His coffee cooled beside his hand, forgotten, while he stared at nine words that could not possibly be there.

*Cucumber soup. Dill oil. Liabilities.*

She hadn't just repeated the detail. She'd repeated the *joke*. Not the fact — facts travel; a caterer talks, a planner overhears, information moves through this town like groundwater. But the framing, the *liability*, the dry contempt aimed at a menu that did not exist outside one envelope on Alder Street — that was not groundwater. That was a fingerprint.

He sat down slowly on the stool at the counter.

There were other explanations. There were always other explanations; he'd built a career on offering them to skeptical clients. Coincidence. Parallel invention. Someone else in the program leaking. Dot herself, gossiping, though Dot guarded her pen-pal pairings with the ferocity of a woman protecting state secrets, mostly because they *were* her state secrets, her one unimpeachable claim to having done something good for this town.

But Theo Marchetti had spent twenty years learning to tell the difference between a coincidence and a pattern, and this was a pattern wearing a trench coat and trying to look casual.

He opened the email again. Read her sign-off. *Regards, M. Voss.* Two words, ironed flat, no warmth offered and none requested. The same two words she'd been signing for three years of professional warfare.

Then he got up, retrieved the shoebox from the closet where he kept the letters — he'd told himself the shoebox was temporary, that he'd buy a proper file box eventually, that keeping them in order by date was just good record-keeping — and laid the correspondence out across his kitchen table like evidence at a trial.

Because that's what it was, wasn't it. A trial. Defendant: Marguerite Voss. Charges: being the person on the other end of the truest thing in his life. Evidence: everything.

* * *

He read them backward first — a trick he'd learned from a fraud examiner years ago, the idea being that lies are constructed forward and fall apart backward. Start at the end and walk toward the beginning, and see if the voice holds.

It held. God help him, it held.

*My entire profession is grief management with better lighting.* He heard it in her voice now, that low even register she used in negotiations, the one that made clients lean in and rivals reach for their contracts. *I lend pieces of myself and invoice for the privilege.* He'd been in the room when she'd said a version of that — the Hollowbrook bid, eight months ago, when she'd dismantled his proposal in front of the committee with a smile like a scalpel and he'd gone home and punched a pillow about it. He'd thought she was cruel. He'd thought she was made entirely of edges.

Now he read the same sentences in ink, in her slanted, knife-crossed handwriting, and saw what he'd refused to see in person: the edges were load-bearing. Something under there was holding the whole structure up, and whatever it was, it was tired.

*You strike me as a man who schedules his feelings the way you schedule deliveries — efficiently, and never on weekends,* she'd written in September, and he had laughed alone in his kitchen like an idiot, and it had taken him until tonight, reading it as *her*, to feel the sting underneath the joke. She'd been describing him accurately. She'd also been describing a man she had no reason to be gentle with. Except she had been gentle, in the way of a person handling something breakable while insisting they don't care if it breaks.

He put his head in his hands.

Outside, the rain kept on. Inside, Theo Marchetti conducted the trial, and the verdict came back fast and unanimous, and there was no appeal, because he had designed the experiment himself and the experiment had worked perfectly, and working perfectly was the problem.

Marguerite Voss was his pen pal. Marguerite Voss — who outbid him on suppliers out of what he'd assumed was pure spite, who fought him over timelines and parking allocations and the moral status of swans, who had not smiled at him in three years — had been writing to him every Sunday night. Telling him things. Real things, or real-adjacent things, things with the armor off, things she would die before saying to his face.

And here was the part he couldn't get past, the part that made him ashamed in a way that sat low in his stomach like swallowed stone: his first feeling wasn't triumph. It wasn't even surprise.

It was *relief* — vast, shameful relief, the relief of a man who finds out the beautiful stranger has been in the room the whole time — followed immediately by its twin, uglier brother: *now I can't unknow it, and I don't want to stop.*

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't balance. If he told her he knew, the letters ended. Whatever this was — this Sunday-night honesty, this slow unwrapping, this thing more intimate than any relationship he'd ever managed in person — it ended the moment she learned he'd tested her, trapped her, confirmed her identity like verifying a suspect. She would feel surveilled. She would feel *handled*. And a woman who lent pieces of herself and invoiced for the privilege would understand exactly what it meant that he'd run a sting operation on her heart and then kept the findings to himself.

But if he said nothing —

If he said nothing, he got to keep it. Every Sunday. The whole unfolding. Her, unguarded, delivered weekly to a mailbox on Alder Street, addressed to a man who didn't exist anymore because Theo had stopped being able to pretend he didn't know.

He was going to say nothing. He knew it even as he told himself he'd decide in the morning. He was going to keep reading, keep writing, keep receiving this woman's trust under false pretenses, because he wanted it more than he wanted to deserve it, and that want — that specific, greedy, human want — was the most honest thing about him, and he hid it too.

He gathered the letters back into the shoebox with hands that weren't quite steady. At the bottom of the stack, the newest one, Sunday's, the one he hadn't fully answered yet. Her closing line, which he'd read a dozen times and which detonated differently now:

*I'll tell you something I've never told anyone, if you promise to do the same. Fair trade. Next letter. Don't be a coward about it.*

He laughed — one short, helpless sound, there in his kitchen with the rain coming down and the evidence boxed and buried.

She was going to confess something. To him. To the fiction of him. And he was going to sit there and take it, knowing her name, knowing her handwriting, knowing the exact expression she wore when she was pretending not to bleed — and give her nothing back but a stranger's face.

"Dot," he said aloud, to no one, "if you're behind this, I hope you're proud of yourself."

* * *

Friday he sent the menu confirmation. *Final menu attached — braised short rib, lemon tart. No cucumber products will approach this wedding. Regards, T.M.*

Her reply came in forty minutes, which was fast for her, which meant she'd been waiting, which meant — he made himself stop analyzing. *Adequate,* she wrote. *See you at the Hollowbrook walkthrough Monday. Bring your timeline and your humility.*

He was still staring at it when the festival committee email landed in his inbox, Dot's cheerful all-caps subject line announcing the storm-prep schedule for next week's tent build, and a line buried in the logistics that made his chest go tight for reasons that had nothing to do with weather:

*NOTE: Forecast looks ugly midweek. Marguerite and Theo — you two are co-managing the main marquee, so coordinate load-out plans. Together. I mean it. I will check.*

Together. In a storm. With everything he knew and she didn't know he knew sitting between them like a loaded tray.

Theo closed his laptop, looked out at the gray sky rolling in over the ridge, and poured himself the coffee he'd let go cold.

Monday, he thought. Four days. Four days to figure out how to stand next to her in the wind and act like a man who hadn't just read her soul by flashlight and stolen it.

Four days to learn how to lie with his whole face to the only person whose letters he kept in a shoebox by the bed.
