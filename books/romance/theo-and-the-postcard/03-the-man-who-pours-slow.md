## 3. The Man Who Pours Slow

The thing nobody tells you about hiring a fake boyfriend is that it's basically a job interview where you're the one being evaluated for the position of liar-in-chief.

I'd spent two days drafting the posting in my head. *Wanted: attractive man, mid-thirties, capable of sustained eye contact with my grandmother without flinching. Must convincingly claim to love me. Improvisation skills essential. Pay negotiable. Benefits include one (1) weekend of free dumplings and the permanent knowledge that I am the kind of person who rents affection.*

I wasn't going to post it anywhere, obviously. I have a career built on discretion; advertising my personal collapse would be like a locksmith picking his own front door in public. But I turned the problem over the way I turn over plot problems — what does this character want, what will she do to get it — until the answer walked up to me on its own, carrying a bottle of rye and a rag over his shoulder.

Theo Marsh had been pouring drinks at the Anchor Line for eight months by then. I know because I'd been sitting at the end of his bar for most of them, nursing two fingers of whatever was cheap and writing other people's sentences in a battered notebook while he pretended not to read over my shoulder. He never did read over my shoulder. That was the first thing I noticed about him, back when noticing things was still something I allowed myself: every bartender I'd ever known made it their business to know your business. Theo made it his business to know exactly how much you'd drunk, whether you were okay, and nothing else unless you offered it.

He poured slow. That sounds like nothing, but watch a man work a bar sometime. Most of them pour like they're putting out fires — fast, sloppy, generous with everyone's money but their own attention. Theo poured slow, level, precise, like each drink was a small contract he intended to honor. It was weirdly calming. It was probably why I kept coming back to a dive with sticky floors and a jukebox that only played the first half of any song before giving up.

So on Thursday night, when the bar emptied out around eleven and it was just me, him, and a guy asleep with his forehead on the pool table, I closed my notebook and said, "I have a business proposition."

Theo didn't look up from restacking glasses. "I don't buy anything before midnight."

"It's not that kind of proposition."

"Shame." He set the glass down and finally looked at me, and I remembered the second thing I'd noticed about him, which is that his face did this thing where it went from closed to open in stages, like a house turning its lights on floor by floor. "Okay. Business proposition."

"I need you to be my boyfriend."

I'd like to report that I said it smoothly. I'd rehearsed versions of it all day — casual, jokey, framed as a joke so I could retract it if he laughed. What came out instead was flat and abrupt, the way confessions come out when you've been holding them too long. Like ripping off a bandage and finding out the wound underneath has opinions.

Theo's eyebrows did something complicated. To his credit, he didn't laugh. He picked up the rye, poured two fingers into each of two glasses, slid one across the bar, and said, "Start from the beginning."

* * *

Here's what I told him, over the next hour, in the particular honesty that only exists between strangers after closing time:

My mother called Sunday. My mother calls maybe four times a year, always on a delay, always as though resuming a conversation we'd paused mid-sentence in 2019. This time the sentence she resumed was: "—so your aunt Mei is hosting this year, and she wants a headcount by Friday." The Zhou family reunion. Every three years. A hundred and forty relatives descending on my aunt's house in Flushing, a hundred and forty pairs of eyes calibrated to detect failure at a range of fifty feet.

"And you need a boyfriend because," Theo said. Not a question. A prompt. He had this way of prompting that felt less like impatience and more like handing you the next line of a duet.

"Because the last reunion, I told them I was 'working on something big.' Which was true, technically. I was ghostwriting a crypto CEO's memoir about integrity." I took a sip of the rye. It was better than what he charged for it, which told me he'd been undercharging me for months, which was its own small humiliation. "And now it's three years later and the natural follow-up question is *how did the big thing go*, and the answer is that I've spent three years writing books that other people put their names on, and my savings account looks like a cautionary tale, and if I walk in there alone again my grandmother will stage an intervention involving a man named Gerald who sells insurance and owns a condominium."

"Gerald," Theo repeated.

"He has a boat. The boat is named *Gerald's Girl*. I don't know what that says about Gerald, but I know I don't want to find out at close range."

"So you invented a boyfriend."

"On the phone. In real time. While my mother was talking." I turned the glass on the bar. "Which is ironic, professionally speaking, since inventing people is literally my job. Apparently the skill transfers."

"What's his name?"

"Excuse me?"

"The boyfriend you invented. On the phone. He must have a name."

And here's the part I still can't fully explain. I could have said anything. I could have said *Marcus* or *David* or *a very nice man named Gerald's Girl*. Instead I looked at the man pouring slow behind the bar and heard myself say, "Theo."

Silence. The jukebox gave up halfway through a song, as usual, leaving us in the hum of the beer fridge.

"You named him after me," Theo said. His voice didn't change much, but something behind it shifted, a current under still water.

"That seems like a design flaw now, yes."

"Why me?"

"Because you were there. Because you're—" I gestured at him, the whole of him, the rolled sleeves and the forearms and the way he stood like someone who'd learned to take up exactly as much space as necessary and not one inch more. "You're plausible. You look like a person a mother would approve of and a grandmother would interrogate and survive. And because hiring a stranger off the internet is how people end up as a true-crime podcast, whereas you—" I stopped, recalibrated. "I've watched you for eight months. You don't lie well. That's a compliment."

"It isn't."

"It's the highest one I give."

He studied me for a long moment. Then he said, "What's in it for me?" — and I understood, with a lurch, that I'd been so busy constructing my pitch that I hadn't actually finished it. There was no salary. There was no anything. I'd walked up to a man and asked him to spend a weekend lying to my family for free, on the strength of eight months of me drinking alone at the end of his bar.

"Money," I said quickly. "Obviously money. Whatever your rate is. Plus room and board for the weekend, plus my eternal gratitude, plus—" I ran out of currency. "Look, I'm a writer. I'm good at making things believable. I'll build us a whole history. How we met, our first date, why you love me. You just have to deliver the lines."

Something crossed his face at *why you love me* — fast, gone before I could name it. He picked up my empty glass, considered it, refilled it without asking. When he spoke, he was looking at the glass, not at me.

"One condition."

"Name it."

"We do it properly. Rehearsal. If I'm going to lie to a hundred and forty people, I'm not doing it half-cocked." He set the bottle down. "Your place or mine?"

"Neither. Neutral ground. A bar, ideally. Preferably this one, after hours, when the sleeping man has gone home."

"Fine." He wiped his hands on the rag, and for a second I thought that was it, deal closed, and I felt absurdly relieved and absurdly disappointed in equal measure, which should have been my first clue about the whole enterprise. But then he said, "One question."

"Shoot."

"Why me? Really. You could hire anyone. There are websites for this. You clearly thought about it." He leaned on the bar, and his eyes were doing the thing they did when he was actually paying attention, which was worse than when he wasn't, because it felt like being read. "Eight months of watching you sit right there. You don't talk to anyone. You don't laugh at anyone's jokes. You write in that notebook like it owes you money. And then tonight you look at me and decide I'm the guy who gets to be your boyfriend for a weekend. So. Why?"

I should have lied. I'm excellent at lying; it's my trade. But there's something about a man who pours slow that makes lying feel like spilling something.

"Because you never ask what I'm writing," I said. "Everyone asks. Everyone who finds out what I do asks what I'm working on, whose book it is, whether they've heard of them. You never once asked. You just—" I shrugged, hating how exposed the shrug made me. "You leave the light over that end of the bar on later than the rest. I noticed. I assumed you didn't notice me noticing."

"I notice everything," Theo said quietly. "That's sort of my whole problem."

It was past midnight. The sleeping man had been roused and dispatched into a cab. Theo flipped the chairs onto the tables with the efficiency of a man closing a chapter, and I gathered my notebook and my coat and my ridiculous, reckless plan, and at the door I turned around and asked the question I'd been sitting on all night:

"Why did you say yes? You didn't even haggle. Everyone haggles. I budgeted for haggling."

Theo stood behind the bar with the lights half-off, and for a moment his face did the opposite of its usual trick — went from open to closed, floor by floor, like a house shutting down for the season.

"Maybe I needed a weekend away from here," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't." He pulled the door open for me, and cold air came in off the street, and he added, almost lightly, almost carelessly, in a tone I'd later think about far more than I wanted to: "Saturday. Seven o'clock. Bring the backstory. I'll bring questions."

I walked home through the wet streets with my collar up, telling myself the flutter in my chest was just the rye and the cold. I was a professional. I manufactured believable love stories for a living; I knew exactly how they worked, beat by beat, and I knew — I *knew* — that the flutter was a production artifact, a special effect, nothing.

But somewhere between the bar and my apartment, I realized I'd forgotten to ask him the most basic question of all. I knew how he poured a drink and how he stacked a glass and how he watched a room. I knew he never asked what I was writing.

I didn't know the first real thing about Theo Marsh. And he'd said yes anyway, for reasons he wouldn't explain, to a woman he had no reason to help — and when I replayed the evening in my head, looking for the flaw in my own construction, the thing that snagged wasn't his answer.

It was the way he'd said *I notice everything*. Like a confession. Like a warning.

Like a man who recognized something about me that I hadn't told him yet.
