## 5. Arrivals

The Zhou family reunion convened, as all catastrophes do, at my aunt Mei's house in Flushing — a brick semi-detached with a garden the size of a bath mat and a dining table that had been extended so many times it now required a building permit. I stood on the front steps with Theo Marsh beside me, holding a paper bag of lychees I'd bought as a prop, and felt the particular nausea of a liar about to perform for her most demanding audience.

"Last chance to run," I said. "There's a subway stop two blocks that way. You could be back pulling pints in forty minutes."

"And miss meeting your family?" He shifted the bag of fruit to his other hand and looked at the house like he was casing it, which, given his former profession, he probably was. "Tamsin. I've interviewed warlords. I'll be fine."

"You've never met my grandmother."

He paused. "Point her out."

"You can't miss her. She's the one who looks like she's already disappointed in you."

"That's most grandmothers."

"Not like this one. My grandmother once made a bride cry at her own wedding by asking, loudly, whether the groom's family had *actually* paid for the banquet or just promised to." I pushed open the gate. "She has ended three engagements. Two of them weren't even hers."

Theo smiled — that slow, unhurried smile he used when he was about to do something I hadn't authorized. "Good. I like a challenge with rules."

"There are no rules. That's the problem."

Inside, the house smelled of fried shallots and jasmine tea and thirty years of the same reunion happening over and over, only the faces aging in place. My mother spotted us from the kitchen doorway before we'd fully cleared the hall, and I watched her do the thing she always did: a full-body inventory, top to bottom, cataloguing everything wrong.

Then she saw Theo, and the inventory stopped.

He was wearing the grey shirt — the one I'd told him made him look trustworthy, because apparently I had opinions about costume design for fraud — and he did the thing I'd coached him not to do, which was to bow slightly, offer both hands to shake, and say, "Mrs. Zhou, thank you for having me. Tamsin talks about you constantly."

My mother melted like a dumpling left in steam.

"She does *not*," she said, glowing, gripping his hands. "She never tells us anything."

"Well," Theo said, glancing at me with something warm and terrible in his eyes, "she's modest. But she called me after she hung up with you that day, and she was so nervous about today that she rehearsed introducing me in the bathroom mirror."

*I had done no such thing.* I had, in fact, rehearsed it twice, but there was no possible way he knew that unless he'd been watching me through my own window like a Victorian ghost, and I decided not to pursue the question.

"Auntie!" someone shouted, and then we were swallowed — cousins materializing, uncles materializing, small children materializing underfoot like weather. Theo absorbed it all with the calm of a man who had spent years getting people to tell him things they didn't want to tell him, which is essentially the same skill set as surviving a Chinese family reunion, except journalists get hazard pay.

I lost him within ten minutes. That was the plan — divide and conquer, less surface area for scrutiny — but losing him turned out to be a tactical error, because every five minutes a cousin would find me and report, breathlessly, on what Theo was doing elsewhere in the house.

"He asked Uncle Wei about his koi pond," said Cousin Lily. "For like twenty minutes. Uncle Wei hasn't talked that long since his divorce."

"He fixed the wobbly chair," reported Cousin Daniel — yes, another one; we ran to names — "without being asked. Just picked it up, looked at it, tightened a screw. Who *does* that?"

A man who grew up in a house where things broke and nobody came to fix them, I thought, and then hated myself for knowing that, because I wasn't supposed to know him yet. We'd had three conversations and one rehearsal. The file I had on Theo Marsh was thin and mostly invented. And still my brain kept supplying real answers, the way it does when you're falling for someone and haven't filed the paperwork yet.

* * *

My grandmother held court in the good armchair by the window, a small, immaculate woman of eighty-seven who communicated primarily through silence and the strategic deployment of a teacup. Her name was Po-Po, and she had survived war, famine, emigration, and my grandfather, and she regarded the modern world with the weary tolerance of someone who had seen it all before and been unimpressed the first time.

Theo approached her the way you'd approach a source who might be armed.

"Po-Po," I said, doing introductions with the enthusiasm of a hostage reading a statement. "This is Theo. My—"

"Boyfriend," Theo said. "It's very nice to meet you, ma'am." And then, instead of sitting when offered a seat, he crouched down beside her chair, lowering himself to her level, and added, "Your granddaughter says you make the best salted plum in three counties. She wouldn't shut up about it."

"I said no such thing."

"She said it with her face."

Po-Po looked at him for a long moment. The room, I swear, got quieter. Somewhere an uncle stopped mid-sentence.

"You," my grandmother said finally, in Cantonese-accented English, "are either very smart or very stupid."

"Everyone in my life is waiting to find out which."

The teacup lowered. This was, historically, the equivalent of a knighthood. "You eat?" she demanded.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You eat *enough*? Tamsin eats like bird. Look at her. Bird."

"I've been trying to feed her for months. She resists."

Po-Po turned to me and delivered a verdict in rapid Cantonese that I will translate here for posterity: *"Keep this one. He lies well, but he lies kindly, and that is rarer than honesty."*

I laughed, too loudly, and steered Theo away before my grandmother could conduct whatever further vetting she had planned, possibly including blood tests.

"What did she say?" he asked.

"Nothing. She said you seem nice."

"Liar."

"We're all liars here. It's a family value."

But my hands weren't steady, and I shoved them into my pockets and pretended to be looking at the wall of framed family photos, decades of reunions, everyone younger each year going backward until the photos went sepia and then black-and-white and then there was just my grandmother as a girl, squinting into a camera in a world that no longer existed. Theo came to stand beside me. He didn't say anything. He just stood there, shoulder almost touching mine, letting the silence be a shared thing instead of an accusation.

That was the moment it started to sting, I think. Not any single line he delivered, but the accumulation — the way he fit. The way he remembered that my mother's name was Grace without checking, the way he asked Lily about her nursing exams because I'd mentioned them once, offhand, weeks ago, in a conversation I barely remembered having. He was doing the job. He was doing the job better than I'd briefed him to, using materials I hadn't provided, and every accurate detail landed like a small, warm knife.

Because here's what nobody tells you about hiring a fake boyfriend: the performance only works if there's something underneath it to perform. And Theo Marsh, pouring slow and watching everything, had apparently found the something underneath, and was building on it, brick by brick, in front of my entire family, while I stood there holding a bag of lychees and my composure, watching a fiction become load-bearing.

* * *

Lunch was a siege. Fourteen dishes, rotating lazy Susan, and a seating arrangement that put Theo directly across from my mother — the worst seat in the house, the hot seat, the interrogation bench — and he handled it like a man enjoying himself.

"So," my mother said, in the tone she used for tax audits, "you are bartender."

"I am."

"Tamsin says you are... how she says... 'between projects.'"

"Mom."

"I'm curious! A grown man, healthy, smart-looking—" she gestured at him as though presenting evidence "—and he pours drinks. In Queens."

Theo set down his chopsticks. He gave her his whole attention, unhurried, the way he did behind the bar when someone ordered their grief neat. "Your daughter found me at a low point, actually," he said. "Not hers. Mine. I used to be a journalist. Investigative work. I lost a story that mattered — lost more than the story — and I ended up bartending because it was the only job I could do with my hands while my head caught up." He shrugged. "Tamsin kept coming in, ordering the same thing, writing in a notebook like the notebook owed her money. One night she corrected my grammar. I figured if a woman corrects your grammar at midnight, you should probably keep her."

The table laughed. My mother's eyes had gone soft and dangerous.

"And now?" she pressed. "You go back to journalism?"

"I don't know. I'm learning that some stories you don't chase. Some stories you stay for." He looked at me when he said it, briefly, and I had to pick up the tea I didn't want and drink it anyway.

*"Some stories you stay for."* Where did he get this stuff? We had rehearsed exactly none of this. Our cover story was that we met at the bar, which was true, and that it developed slowly, which was false, and that he found my stubbornness charming, which — fine, allegedly. Everything else he was laying down across this table like mortar was improvised, sourced from somewhere inside him I hadn't authorized, and it was all so *good*. Warm without being slick. Specific without being showy. He defended my career gaps as "a writer between books, which is different from a writer with nothing to write." He told Uncle Wei that Tamsin was the most disciplined person he knew, and Uncle Wei, who had once called my career "typing," nodded gravely.

By the time dessert came out — Aunt Mei's almond cake, dense and pale and legendary — I understood that I was in trouble. Not exposure trouble. The other kind.

* * *

I escaped to the porch with a slice of cake I had no intention of eating, and stood in the late-afternoon light watching a squirrel audit the bird feeder, and let myself have sixty seconds of honest terror.

My phone buzzed.

**CELESTE:** *Draft of Part One by end of month. Publisher moved up the timeline. Also — Marcus chapter needs to sing. Call me.*

No greeting. No question mark about my life. Eleven years of friendship, rivalry, theft, and reconciliation-that-wasn't, compressed into a text message shaped like an invoice.

I stared at it. *The Marcus chapter.* Marcus Vane, Celeste's publisher, dead these ten years, whose memoir chapters she dictated in that honeyed voice that turned every memory into upholstery. There was something wrong in those transcripts — I'd felt it for days without being able to name it, a smoothness where a scar should be, a summer mentioned once and never again. But that was Monday's problem. Today's problem was standing behind me, having followed me onto the porch without my noticing, which was becoming a habit I'd need to break or surrender to.

"You okay?" Theo asked.

"Work email."

"At a family reunion?"

"Celeste doesn't respect borders." I locked the phone. "Deadline moved up. End of the month."

"The memoir?"

"The memoir." I cut a piece of cake with the side of my fork and didn't eat it. "You know what the worst part is? She gets to dictate her life to me. Every lie pre-approved, every ugly thing sanded off, and I make it beautiful. That's the job. I make people's lives beautiful so they don't have to have lived them honestly."

Theo leaned against the railing beside me. For a while he said nothing, which was its own kind of reply.

"My brother used to say," he offered eventually, "that everybody's memoir is a crime scene they've redecorated."

I turned to look at him. "That's grim."

"He was quoting someone. Maybe me. It's been a long decade." Something crossed his face then — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. "Sorry. Inside voice. Today I'm just the boyfriend."

"You're very good at it," I said, and meant it as a deflection, and it came out sounding like an accusation.

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's a complaint filed with the management."

He smiled, and looked back toward the house, where through the window I could see my mother telling the cake story — already improved, already gaining details — and Po-Po watching us through the glass with the expression of a woman keeping receipts.

"For what it's worth," Theo said quietly, "the part about the notebook owing you money? True. I thought it every time you came in. Before you ever asked me anything."

"Before—" I stopped. Recalculated. "We agreed you'd say you noticed me months ago, but—"

"I did notice you months ago, Tamsin. That part isn't work." He said it easily, plainly, the way he said true things, which was the most disorienting thing about him. Then he took the fork out of my hand, cut a proper piece of almond cake, and handed it back. "Eat. Your grandmother's watching, and she counts bites. I checked."

And I ate the cake, because I was outnumbered — by him, by my family, by the whole warm apparatus of the lie we'd built, which was functioning so beautifully that it had begun to generate its own truth, and I stood there on my aunt's porch tasting almond and dread in equal measure, thinking:

If this is fake, I would hate to see the real thing.

My phone buzzed again against my palm. Celeste again. I almost didn't look.

**CELESTE:** *Also — odd question. Did you ever finish that old novel of yours? Someone's been asking about it. Old publishing circles. Probably nothing. Call me.*

I read it twice. The porch light buzzed on above us as the afternoon dimmed, and somewhere behind the glass my family laughed at a story that was only half true, told by a man I'd hired six days ago, and I stood very still with the phone in my hand, feeling the ground tilt beneath the first sentence of a message that shouldn't have existed.

Nobody knew about that novel. Nobody living.

Except whoever had sent me the postcard.
