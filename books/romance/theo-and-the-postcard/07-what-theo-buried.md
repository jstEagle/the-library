## 7. What Theo Buried

The bar was closed, the chairs were up, and I was doing what I always did after closing on a Sunday: standing in a room that smelled of bleach and spilled lager, pretending my life was a choice.

Most nights the pretense held. Most nights I could wipe down the taps, count the drawer, and let the ritual sand off whatever edges the day had left. But tonight there was a post-it note stuck to the register with Tamsin Zhou's handwriting on it — *you owe me one convincing story about how we met, Marsh* — and underneath it, in her smaller, more honest hand, a phone number she'd already given me twice and still felt the need to write down, like part of her didn't trust the arrangement to survive contact with technology.

I peeled the note off and stuck it in my shirt pocket, which was a stupid place for it, right over the heart, like I was in a war movie.

Then I went upstairs and got the shoebox out from under the bed.

Everyone has a shoebox. Mine just happens to be literal.

* * *

The clipping was ten years old and had gone the color of weak tea. I'd kept it in a zip-top bag inside the box, because at some point in the last decade I'd learned that paper decays and grief doesn't, and one of those things you can do something about.

**PUBLISHER DEAD IN BLAZE; BARTENDER'S BROTHER HELD**, said the headline, because even the *Post*, in its mercy, couldn't resist the bartender angle. My father had tended bar for thirty years. It made for a tidy paragraph: *the suspect's brother, Theo Marsh, 24, works at the Copper Lantern.* As if pouring drinks were a character flaw. As if it explained anything except that our family had never once been interesting enough for the newspaper until it was ruined.

Below the fold, a photo of Daniel at twenty-six, handcuffed, jaw set, looking directly into the camera like he dared it to blink first. He'd lost that dare, eventually. Everyone does.

Marcus Vane. Fifty-eight. Publisher, philanthropist, collector of first editions and, allegedly, enemies. Died when his lake house went up in the middle of the night with him asleep inside it. The fire marshals found accelerant. The prosecutors found Daniel's prints on a gas can Daniel swore he'd never touched — found them, in fact, eleven days after the fire, in a search that had already happened once and turned up nothing. Found a motive, too: Daniel had done contracting work on Vane's properties and been stiffed on the final invoice. Eleven thousand dollars. That was the sum my brother's whole life had supposedly been worth. Not even twelve grand. The universe didn't even round up.

I read the clipping the way you touch a bruise. Not because it tells you anything new. Because the hurt confirms the thing is still there, still yours, still real.

Ten years ago I'd believed the system would work. I'd sat in that courtroom every day in my one good blazer, certain that juries could tell the difference between a man who burned a building down and a man who'd once fixed its gutters. I watched twelve strangers listen to nine days of evidence and then take four hours to decide my brother was a murderer, and I remember thinking, with a clarity I've never managed since: *oh. It's not broken. It works exactly as designed. It just wasn't designed for us.*

My mother lasted three years after the verdict. The doctors called it cardiac failure. Our family called it what it was, privately, in the voice you use for things too heavy to say out loud: the trial ate her, and the sentence finished the plate.

Daniel got twenty-five to life. He's eleven years in. He writes to me every month, short letters, no self-pity, mostly jokes about prison food and requests for crossword books. I write back. That's the part nobody warns you about — how ordinary it becomes. Grief doesn't stay a storm. It becomes furniture.

* * *

Here's what I hadn't told Tamsin Zhou, sitting across from her in my empty bar while we rehearsed how we'd met — here's what I'd buried under the shoebox, under the bleach smell, under ten years of pouring slow and keeping my head down:

Three weeks ago, a woman came into the Copper Lantern I'd never seen before, ordered something expensive, and talked too loudly on her phone about "the Vane estate" and "the memoir deadline." I wasn't eavesdropping. Bartenders don't eavesdrop; we absorb. It's an occupational hazard, like carpal tunnel.

The name Marcus Vane hit me the way a dropped glass hits tile — sharp, sudden, everywhere at once. I kept wiping the counter. I kept my face doing nothing, which is the one skill my father actually taught me on purpose.

When she hung up, I asked what she was drinking and she said, "Whatever the sad writers drink," and I gave her a Manhattan and she tipped badly and left, and I stood there holding a rag over a spot that was already clean.

Vane. Dead ten years, and the name was still moving through the world, still attached to words like *estate* and *memoir*. Dead men don't hire ghostwriters. Which meant someone alive was writing the official version of Marcus Vane's life, and I wanted to know who, and why now, and what exactly needed saying that hadn't been said at trial.

It took me two days of nursing a beer next to a chatty paralegal to learn the rest: the memoir was Celeste Marchand's. The literary darling. The woman whose debut novel had won everything and whose face was on the side of a bus I took home twice a week.

And then, a week later, Tamsin Zhou sat down at my bar, ordered a whiskey neat, and looked at me like I was a form she was trying to fill out correctly, and said the word *ghostwriter* the way other people say *indentured servant*, and mentioned — casually, the way you mention weather — that her biggest client was someone whose name she didn't want printed.

I didn't ask. Asking is what journalists do, and I'd retired from journalism the way you retire from boxing: not by decision but by concussion.

But I'm not a journalist anymore. I'm a bartender. And bartenders notice things. It's the whole job. You notice the guy drinking doubles who's usually a singles man. You notice the couple at table six where her laugh arrives half a second before his smile. You notice that the quiet woman in the corner booth who claims to write "corporate materials" has ink stains on her right middle finger and flinches whenever anyone says the word *novel*.

You notice that your fake girlfriend's client list might have a dead publisher on it.

* * *

I laid it all out on my kitchen table that night, which is to say I laid out two items: the tea-colored clipping and a cocktail napkin with three names on it, because apparently I'd become the kind of man who investigates with bar supplies.

*Daniel Marsh. Convicted.*
*Marcus Vane. Dead.*
*Tamsin Zhou. Ghostwriting for — ?*

The napkin math was simple and terrible. If Celeste Marchand's memoir covered Vane — if Vane had been her publisher, her champion, the man whose imprint launched her — then somewhere in that manuscript there was going to be a chapter about him. A polished, glowing, carefully edited chapter. And polished chapters have a way of being polished for reasons.

I knew what the fire marshals' report said, because I'd read it so many times the pages went soft: origin point, exterior wall, north side, below the study window. Accelerant pattern consistent with pour. And I knew what the report didn't say, what it couldn't say, what I'd spent a decade refusing to build theories around because theories are what you make when you can't afford facts:

Daniel had told the police, from the very first interview, that Vane had been nervous in the months before the fire. That Vane had asked him odd questions about the property. About insurance appraisals. About whether the old wiring was as bad as it looked. Daniel thought it was small talk. Daniel was a contractor; contractors think everything is small talk.

Nobody wanted to hear it. Why would they? The state had a gas can with fingerprints and a defendant with debts, and a story that fit on one page. The truth, whatever it was, would have required a second page, and second pages cost money.

I put my head back against the kitchen chair and stared at the ceiling water stain shaped like Ohio.

Here was the problem, laid out neater than the napkin: I had spent ten years learning to be invisible. Invisible men don't get answers. Invisible men get to keep their jobs and their low blood pressure and their careful, airless lives. And now, for the first time since the verdict, I had a thread — a thin, ridiculous thread made of cocktail-napkin logic and a loud woman's phone call — and pulling it required exactly the kind of person I had spent a decade failing to be.

Or it required someone who already had access.

Someone who was already inside the manuscript.

Someone who owed me one convincing story about how we met.

* * *

I want to be honest about the part that shames me, because honesty is the only currency I've got left that isn't counterfeit.

When I agreed to be Tamsin Zhou's fake boyfriend, I told myself it was for the money. Two hundred dollars and all the free peanuts she could watch me not eat. Then I told myself it was curiosity — she was interesting, guarded in a way I recognized, a locked room I wouldn't mind picking. Both true. Neither was the truth.

The truth was that when she said *ghostwriter* and *client I can't name*, some animal part of me — the part that had sat in that courtroom in the good blazer — stood up and pointed at the door and said: *there. That's the way in.*

So yes. I took the job knowing I might use her. I sat across from her while she briefed me on her mother's expectations and her grandmother's interrogation techniques, and I memorized her the way you'd case a building — the way she held her glass by the bowl instead of the stem, the way she said *it's fine* in a tone that meant nothing in her life had ever once been fine, the way she laughed at her own lies a half-beat late, like they had to travel a long distance to reach her mouth.

And then somewhere between the briefing and the reunion, the casing stopped feeling like casing. Somewhere between *pretend to know my coffee order* and watching her grandmother try to dismantle me with one raised eyebrow, I stopped taking notes and started just — noticing. The way you notice when it's real. Her hand shaking slightly when her aunt asked about her novel. The specific loneliness of a person surrounded by family and starving anyway.

That's the trap of this kind of work, if you let it be work. You rehearse intimacy until intimacy shows up uninvited and stays.

So now I had a problem with two layers, like bad lasagna. Underneath everything: I might be falling for a woman I'd originally approached as a lead. And on top of that: the lead itself. Because if her client's memoir touched Marcus Vane, then Tamsin Zhou was going to be transcribing the official story of the fire that broke my family — typing it up, polishing it, making it shine — without ever knowing there was a boy in the photo below the fold who grew into the man mixing her drinks.

She deserved to know that. Probably. Eventually. When I was sure.

When was I ever going to be sure? I'd known Daniel for twenty-six years before the state told me I'd misread him completely. Certainty was a luxury brand I couldn't afford.

* * *

I picked up the clipping one more time. Daniel's eyes, daring the camera. My own name in the third paragraph, age twenty-four, employed at the Copper Lantern, defined entirely by proximity — to the crime, to the suspect, to the story. Ten years later I was still defined by proximity. I just worked closer to it now: forty feet of mahogany between me and everyone else's tragedies, served neat.

On the back of the clipping, in my mother's handwriting — she'd written on everything, receipts, envelopes, the backs of photographs, like paper might run out — was a single line. She'd written it the week after the sentencing, and I'd found it years later and never moved it, because moving it felt like agreeing she was gone.

*Ask who benefits,* it said. She'd been a bookkeeper, not a detective. She'd also been right.

Who benefited from Marcus Vane's fire? The insurance company paid out — I'd looked, once, years ago, and made myself stop. Who benefited from the conviction? The state got its closure, the town got its villain, and somebody got to stop worrying about whatever Vane had been nervous about in his last months. Somebody slept better starting the night of the fire.

Somebody maybe even wrote about it, eventually, in a memoir, in a glowing chapter, in a polished paragraph that a ghostwriter was typing up right now in a rent-controlled apartment eating instant noodles.

I took the post-it note out of my shirt pocket. *One convincing story about how we met, Marsh.* Her handwriting slanted left, like it was leaning away from something.

Tomorrow I'd call her. We'd rehearse. We'd hold hands in front of her relatives and invent a history, and I'd be warm and believable and full of shit, and none of that was the plan anymore, or maybe it had been the plan all along, and the difference between those two things was a distinction I was no longer sober enough — literally or otherwise — to draw.

But tonight, I did one small thing. I opened my laptop, which I use for payroll and sports scores and nothing else, and I searched two words together for the first time in ten years:

*Celeste. Vane.*

The results loaded. There were photos of them at galas, his hand on her shoulder, her laugh frozen mid-air. There was an old interview where she called him *"the man who taught me everything about publishing — and most of what I know about survival."*

And there, third link down, dated eight months ago, a trade announcement I'd somehow never seen:

***Marchand memoir to be co-published with the Vane Family Trust. "Marcus believed in me before anyone," Marchand said. "This book is my thank-you letter."***

A thank-you letter. To a dead man. Dictated to a ghost.

I closed the laptop slowly, the way you close a door on a room you're not ready to enter but can't stop standing outside of.

Then I texted Tamsin: *Rehearsal tomorrow? I have questions about your client.*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

*Careful, Marsh,* she wrote back. *That almost sounded like journalism.*

I looked at the clipping. I looked at the napkin. I looked at the phone in my hand, warm as a live coal.

*Old habit,* I typed. *Buried deep. But it digs.*
