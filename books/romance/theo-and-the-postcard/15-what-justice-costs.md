## 15. What Justice Costs

The file sat between us on my bar like a bomb with a paper clip for a trigger.

It was two in the morning, four days after the archive, and I'd spent those four days doing what I do best, which is nothing. Pouring drinks. Wiping the counter. Smiling at strangers while a manila folder burned a hole through the floorboards of my apartment upstairs, directly above the taps, as if it wanted to be closer to the alcohol. Daniel's name was in that folder. Marcus Vane's handwriting was in that folder. A fire marshal's report that had been "lost" twenty years before I was old enough to know what a fire marshal was — all of it real, all of it there, all of it heavy enough to bend a table.

Tamsin had barely touched her drink. That should have been my first clue. Tamsin Zhou did not leave drinks untouched unless she was planning something.

"So here's what happens next," I said, and reached for the folder.

"Here's what happens next," she said, and put her hand flat on top of it.

We looked at each other across the file like two people playing tug-of-war with a rope made of someone's life. Hers or mine, depending on who won.

"I've been thinking," I said carefully.

"Terrifying."

"Tamsin."

"Sorry." She didn't sound sorry. She sounded like a woman holding a grenade and enjoying the weight of it. "Go on. You've been thinking."

"We take it to a lawyer. A good one. Wrongful conviction unit, somebody who's done this before. We hand them everything — Vane's letters, the insurance payout, the fire marshal's original findings — and we let them build the case properly. Motion for a new trial. Exoneration. The whole slow, boring, legal machine." I heard myself say *we* and felt something turn over in my chest that I decided not to examine. "Daniel walks out the front door instead of through a loophole. It holds. Nobody can take it away from him later because some judge got nervous."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "How long?"

"How long what?"

"How long does the machine take? Ballpark. You were an investigative journalist, you must know how fast the wheels grind when the wheels are being asked to admit they ran over somebody."

I knew exactly how fast. That was the problem.

"Two years," I said. "If we're lucky. Three if the state fights it, which they will, because admitting Daniel Marsh was framed means admitting a prosecutor buried evidence, which means lawsuits, which means every lawyer in that building develops a sudden passion for procedure."

"Three years." She said it the way you'd read a weather report for a hurricane. "He's already done eleven."

"He can survive three more."

"Can he?" Her voice went quiet, and quiet from Tamsin was worse than loud. "Theo. Have you actually looked at him lately? Not the visitation-room version where he performs okay-for-you. The real one. Eleven years, Theo. He's thirty-four and he talks like a man reading his own obituary and hoping it'll be flattering."

I didn't answer, because the honest answer was that I had looked, and I had seen it, and I'd driven home afterward and sat in the parked car for forty minutes with the engine off.

"And meanwhile," she went on, tapping the folder once, "Celeste Ashford is dictating a memoir to me in which this entire story gets one paragraph. One. 'That summer was shadowed by tragedy.' That's the sentence, Theo. I transcribed it. *Shadowed by tragedy.* Like the sun went behind a cloud and not like a man burned alive and another man buried for it." Her eyes had gone bright in the way they do when she's furious in a language she doesn't let herself speak. "Vane framed your brother to keep him quiet about an arson-for-insurance job, and the woman who helped bury it is going to be on every bestseller list by fall, and you want to hand this to a lawyer and wait three years while she does her book tour?"

"Yes," I said. "Because the alternative is—"

"The alternative is it comes out *now*. All of it. The file, the letters, the whole rotten architecture. We give it to a reporter — not me, someone with a masthead behind them — and it runs, and the podcast people pick it up, and suddenly there's public pressure, and pressure moves faster than motions. Daniel's lawyers get a gift-wrapped narrative. The state can't sit on it when the whole city knows his name."

"That's not justice," I said. "That's a detonation."

"It's both."

"It's revenge wearing justice's coat, and you know it, because half of this isn't about Daniel. Half of this is about Celeste, and the novel, and ten years of you swallowing—" I stopped, because I could hear where the sentence was going and it was a place I didn't have the right to go yet.

But Tamsin, being Tamsin, went there for me.

"Ten years of me swallowing what?" she said, very evenly. "Say it."

"You want her destroyed," I said. "Not exposed. *Destroyed.* And I understand why. God help me, after what I found in that box — the manuscript, Vane's note, *the one Celeste will publish* — I understand it better than you think. But if we light this fuse, it doesn't burn in a straight line. It burns everything it touches. Including Daniel, if we get it wrong. Including you. Celeste has lawyers, Tamsin. Celeste has a publisher with a legal department the size of my building. If we come out swinging and miss, she doesn't just survive, she gets to play the victim, and then nobody touches this file again for another decade."

The bar hummed around us — the ice machine, the dying neon in the window, the refrigerator's long asthmatic sigh. I'd closed an hour ago. Neither of us had turned on more than the lamp behind the register, so we were lit the way bars are lit at that hour: warm from above, dark from below, everyone looking better than they are.

Tamsin pulled her hand off the folder. For a second I thought I'd won.

Then she said, "Tell me about your mother."

The words landed like a dropped glass.

"What?"

"Your mother. You've mentioned her twice in two months, both times in past tense, both times like you were changing the subject from yourself. Your father I've heard plenty about — the coat, the postcards, the man who taught you to notice things. But her—" She shrugged, and it wasn't casual at all. "You want to talk about what justice costs. So cost it out loud. What did it cost her?"

I should have refused. There was a version of me, ten years younger, who would have refused — who would have put up the bartender smile and poured her another drink and changed the channel. But I'd spent weeks learning this woman's tells, and she'd apparently spent the same time learning mine, and she knew — she had to know — that the only way to win this fight was to make me fight it inside my own history first.

"She stopped writing letters," I said.

Tamsin waited.

"After the conviction. My mom used to write everybody. Christmas cards, thank-you notes, letters to the editor about potholes. She believed correspondence was a civic duty. Then Daniel got convicted and she wrote one letter a week for six months — to the governor, to the DA, to the *Sentinel*, to anyone with a mailbox — and every single one came back either ignored or form-lettered, and one day she just... stopped. Stopped writing. Stopped answering hers. She'd sit at the kitchen table with a pen in her hand and nothing would happen." I turned my glass a quarter-turn on the bar. "The doctors called it depression, which is like calling a house fire 'warmth.' She died four years in. Heart, officially. But I've always filed it under the verdict. The state killed her slowly and billed us for the appeals."

Tamsin's face did something complicated and stayed very still around it.

"So yes," I said. "I know what it costs to go outside the law. I watched it cost my mother everything, because the system told her the truth didn't matter, and she believed the system, and the believing is what killed her. Which is exactly why I'm not handing this to the court of public opinion either. Because that court killed her too — it just did it by *not caring*. Public opinion is a jury that's already left the building, Tamsin. You can't detonate your way into caring. You can only detonate."

"And you can't lawyer your way into speed," she shot back. "You keep talking like the legal system is a machine that works if you feed it correctly. Theo, the machine ate your family. The machine took a fire marshal's actual findings and lost them in a drawer for twenty years. The machine convicted your brother on the word of a publisher who was torching his own warehouse. Why — *why* — do you trust it now?"

"Because I don't trust anything else to last!" It came out louder than I meant, bouncing off the bottle shelves. I lowered my voice. "Because a headline is a match strike. Bright, fast, gone by Thursday. And Daniel has to live in whatever's left after the light goes out. A vacated conviction is forever. A scandal is a news cycle. I've watched scandals eat people, Tamsin. I used to *make* them. I know exactly how much wreckage they leave and how little of it lands on the guilty."

"And I've watched the legal process eat people too," she said, "from the inside, quietly, with paperwork. You know what my whole career is? Process. Doing things the correct way, the approved way, the way that protects everyone except the person doing the work. I ghosted a memoir for the woman who stole my novel because that was the *proper channel*, Theo. I followed procedure for ten years and it got me instant noodles and no name. So forgive me if 'let's do it properly' sounds less like justice and more like the thing that's been killing us both politely for a decade."

There it was. Laid out on the bar between us alongside the file: her wound and mine, matching set, same manufacturer.

And underneath the anger — I could see it, and I think she could see me seeing it — was the thing we were actually fighting about, which was not lawyers or headlines at all. It was fear. She was afraid that if we did this slowly, someone would take it away from us, the way everything had always been taken away from her, quietly, through channels. I was afraid that if we did it loudly, it would blow apart in our hands, the way everything I'd ever loved had blown apart, publicly, in flames. We were both just guarding our own scar tissue and calling it strategy.

"Tamsin." I said her name and made myself keep going. "If we do it your way and it goes wrong, Celeste doesn't just walk. She gets sympathy. She gets to stand at a podium with her chin up and her eyes wet and talk about being harassed by bitter people with axes to grind, and half the country will believe her, because that's what half the country does. And Daniel goes back to being a footnote in somebody else's redemption arc. I can't — " My voice snagged on something. I pushed through it. "I can't give her that. I can't hand that woman one more inch of my brother's story. She's had enough of it."

For a long moment Tamsin looked at me, and the fury drained out of her face by degrees, the way tide goes out, leaving all this exposed ground underneath that I hadn't known was there.

"You really think I don't know that," she said softly. "You think I haven't thought about her podium? Theo, I've been imagining that woman's downfall since I was twenty-three. I've directed it. Full production values. I know what dress she wears when it happens." She laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "That's the problem. That's exactly the problem. I want it so badly I can't trust myself to aim."

"Okay," I said. "Then we don't aim alone."

She looked up.

"We do both," I said. "Lawyer first. Quietly. We get the case moving, we protect Daniel, we make sure whatever happens in public sits on top of something solid. And then — once there's a filing, once it's on the record and can't be disappeared — then it goes loud. Reporter, records request, all of it. Not as a detonation. As a spotlight on something that's already standing. You get your reckoning. She still ends up destroyed, if destruction's what the truth does to her. But it destroys her *after* Daniel's safe, not instead of him."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. She picked up her untouched drink, considered it, and set it down again.

"A month," she said finally. "The lawyer files within a month, or I start making calls. And Theo — there's something else. Something I haven't told you yet, because until tonight I thought it was paranoia."

My stomach did the thing it does. "Which is?"

"Celeste wants the final chapters delivered in person. Next week. Just me, at her place." She met my eyes. "And she said — her exact words — 'I hear you've been asking about Marcus.'"

The ice machine chose that moment to shudder and go silent, and the bar felt suddenly very large and very cold.

"She knows," Tamsin said. "Or she suspects. Either way, Theo — I'm walking into that house carrying everything we just fought over, and she's going to be watching me carry it."

* * *

I walked her out at three. The street was empty in that washed way streets get after rain, and she stood on the curb hailing nothing, waiting for a car that the app insisted existed.

"For what it's worth," I said, "you were right about part of it."

"Which part?"

"The wanting-her-destroyed part. I've got a version of that too. Been feeding it for eleven years. It's just shaped like a courtroom instead of a bonfire."

She almost smiled. "We're a matched set of grudges."

"Romantic."

"Devastatingly." The car slid up to the curb. She opened the door, then paused, half in, half out, streetlight catching the edge of her jaw. "Theo. When I'm in that room with her — if it goes sideways —"

"It won't."

"If it does," she said, "don't do anything stupid and public. Wait for me."

And then she was gone, taillights dissolving down the wet avenue, and I stood there realizing I had just agreed to spend the coming week doing the hardest thing I'd ever attempted, harder than prison visits, harder than the archive:

Waiting.
