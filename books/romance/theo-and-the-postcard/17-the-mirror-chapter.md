## 17. The Mirror Chapter

The chapter was called "Owning My Mistakes," which was a title only someone who had never owned a single mistake could dictate with a straight face.

Celeste had recorded it in two sittings, her voice doing that thing it did on podcasts and panels — warm, rueful, faintly amused at her own humanity, as if she were a gracious host showing you around the gift shop of her remorse. *I was young. I was ambitious. I made choices I'm not proud of.* Fourteen minutes of audio that managed to say nothing at all, delivered in the tone of a woman being very brave about a hangnail.

My job was to make it true. Or rather — my job was to make it *sound* true, which is a different profession entirely, and one I had been practicing for eleven years without ever once saying the word *fraud* out loud.

I sat in my apartment at eleven p.m. with the transcript glowing on my laptop and a cup of tea I'd forgotten existed, and I stared at the sentence that had been giving me trouble since Tuesday:

*I have always tried to be honest about who I am.*

And I thought: *you haven't. I know for a fact you haven't, because I am the person you hired to make it look like you have.*

Here is what nobody tells you about ghostwriting a confession: you become the confessor. The words leave your hands wearing someone else's name, but they pass through your chest on the way out, and some of them stick. For eleven years I had been writing other people's apologies, other people's grief, other people's hard-won wisdom, and I had gotten very good at a kind of emotional money-laundering — taking the raw ore of my own experience, smelting it down, and casting it into shapes with Celeste's face on them.

This chapter was supposed to be the centerpiece. The redemption beat. The part the reviewers would quote. Celeste, humbled. Celeste, grown. Celeste, *accountable* — that word she loved, that word she used the way other people use perfume, a light application over everything.

And I couldn't write it. Not because I didn't know how. Because every sentence I drafted came out sounding like a lie, and I could no longer tell whose lie it was.

* * *

Theo had started staying over some nights. Not in any way we'd named — he'd come by after closing with a bag of groceries and an expression of elaborate casualness, and cook something in my kitchen that required actual ingredients, and then we'd end up on the couch with our feet tangled together like it was the most obvious thing in the world. We hadn't discussed it. We had, between us, an entire architecture of things not discussed, and we'd built it so well it was almost a home.

That night he found me at two a.m. still in the same chair, the cursor blinking in the middle of a paragraph I'd deleted six times.

"Still fighting with Celeste's soul?" he asked, setting a glass of water next to my cold tea.

"I'm trying to write her confession and all I've got is a press release."

"Maybe she doesn't have a confession in her."

"Everyone has a confession in them." I said it with more certainty than I felt. "It's just that most people hire someone else to dig it out."

He sat on the arm of the couch, close enough that his knee touched my shoulder. "What would yours be?"

And there it was — the question I'd been circling for weeks, delivered in his quiet bartender's voice, the one he used on drunks and grieving regulars and me. I could have deflected. I was excellent at deflecting; it was practically my mother tongue. But it was two a.m., and he was warm, and somewhere in the last month the fake boyfriend and I had stopped keeping books on what was real.

"I disappeared," I said.

He waited. He was good at waiting. It was, I suspected, the same skill that had made him a journalist and the same one that kept him from pushing.

"Eleven years ago, Celeste published my novel under her name. And I let her." I watched his face in the laptop light, looking for pity and finding only attention. "No — that's not even true. I didn't let her. I *made* her. I handed her the manuscript and told myself it was a test, and when she failed it, I told myself I'd known she would, and then I spent a decade writing her books from the inside while telling everyone — my mother, my aunts, myself — that I was a successful writer." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I ghost my own life, Theo. I've been ghosting it so long I don't know where the byline goes anymore."

The cursor kept blinking. *I have always tried to be honest about who I am.*

"Why?" he asked. Not *how could you*. Not *that's insane*. Just *why*, like the answer might be reasonable, like I might be reasonable.

"Because surviving was the priority." The words came out of me with the strange weight of things long stored. "I was twenty-six and broke and she was offering me a career — her career, her name, her platform — and I told myself I'd take it for a year, two years, and then come back for what was mine. And every year it got harder to come back, because coming back meant admitting what I'd done, and admitting it meant losing everything I'd built on top of the lie. So I built higher. That's the thing about disappearing, Theo — it's not one decision. It's a thousand small ones, and each one is survivable, and the sum of them is a person who isn't there."

He was quiet for a long moment. Then: "You know that's what I did too. After Daniel."

I turned to look at him.

"Ten years of not writing to my brother. Not because I believed he was guilty — never that. But because writing to him meant the case was still open, and the case being open meant I could still lose, and I'd already lost my mother to it. So I stopped. I poured drinks and I kept my head down and I called it moving on." He looked at his hands. "You disappear by writing other people's books. I disappeared by not writing one letter. Same country, different address."

I don't know why that undid me more than anything else he'd ever said. Maybe because forgiveness is easier to accept when it arrives disguised as company.

* * *

The next morning I sat down to the chapter again, and something had shifted — not in Celeste, who remained exactly as polished and as hollow as a showroom piano, but in me.

Because here's what I finally understood, sitting there with her transcript and my tea: I couldn't write her confession because she hadn't made one, and I couldn't make one *for* her anymore, because I'd spent my whole career doing exactly that — manufacturing accountability for people who felt none — and the manufacturing was the thing I needed to confess.

*Forgive Celeste*, I'd been telling myself for weeks, as if forgiveness were a door I could unlock with the right key. But you can't forgive someone else's theft while you're still hiding your own. Every time I looked at her, I saw the woman who stole my novel, and she saw — nothing, because I had made myself invisible, and you can't be betrayed by a ghost.

The rage I'd been carrying wasn't really about her. It was about the version of me who'd stood in her kitchen at twenty-six, holding a manuscript and a grudge and a mortgage's worth of fear, and chosen the safe road and called it strategy. I had been waiting eleven years for Celeste to apologize so I could stop being angry at myself. She was never going to do it. The apology was mine to write, and I was the only one who'd ever accept the terms.

So I did the thing I had never once done in eleven years of professional confession-writing.

I wrote the truth.

Not Celeste's truth. Not a chapter about her mistakes, softened and structured and sanded smooth. I opened a new document — not the memoir file, not the ghost file, a third thing, unnamed — and I wrote:

*There is a version of this story where I am only the victim, and I have been telling it for eleven years, and it is a lie of omission so large I could live inside it, and I have.*

*I let her take my name because taking it back meant admitting I'd given it away. I built her career because building my own meant failing in public, and I had already failed once, in the only way that mattered to me, and I could not afford to do it twice. So I disappeared into other people's lives the way you disappear into a crowd — one shoulder at a time — and I told myself invisibility was a superpower, and it was, and it cost me everything.*

*This is the confession Celeste never dictated. She was never brave enough to make it, and I was never brave enough to stop making it for her.*

I wrote for four hours. I didn't check the transcript once. I didn't think about voice or structure or what the reviewers would quote. I wrote the way I had at twenty-three, before I'd learned that truth was a commodity with a market rate — fast and unguarded and a little afraid of itself, sentences arriving like postcards from someone I used to be.

When I finally surfaced, the document was nine pages long and it was the truest thing I had ever written, and I knew — the way you know a fire is real from three streets away — that it could not stay in a folder. That it was going to cost me the memoir, the clients, the whole careful invisible architecture of my life. And that I was going to send it anyway.

Theo found me like that, tear-streaked and terrifyingly calm, and read it standing up, and when he finished he didn't say it was good. He said, "There she is," like he'd been waiting for me the whole time.

I was still holding the printed pages when my phone buzzed on the table.

Celeste. *Need the final chapters in person. Friday. And Tamsin — Marcus's assistant tells me you've been making calls. We should talk about what you think you know.*

I looked at the nine pages in my hand, the truth still warm off the printer, and then at the phone, and I understood that the two conversations were about to become one.
