## 3. What the Tide Brought Back

The boxes came off the van looking like everything else Wren owned: unremarkable. Cardboard, tape gone soft at the seams, her name written on the sides in a hand she barely recognized as her own anymore. *W. Halloway*, in block capitals, because cursive was evidence. Everything about her life had been edited for legibility by people who wanted to read it.

She'd told the driver to leave them stacked in the front room of the cottage, and now they sat there in the November light like a jury already seated. Six boxes. That was what ten years reduced to when you were running — six boxes, a laptop with the battery removed, and a set of documents that had ruined her once and might, if she was careful, ruin the right person this time.

She locked the door. She checked it twice, which wasn't paranoia, it was procedure. Then she knelt on the cold flagstones and opened the first box.

* * *

People imagined that fugitives traveled light because they were free. Nobody understood that you packed light because you never knew which night would be the night, and a woman who couldn't be out the door in ninety seconds was a woman who got caught. Wren had rehearsed the ninety seconds so many times she could do it blind. Shoes by the bed. Cash in the left coat pocket, not the right, because in the panic of the real thing your hands always went left. Documents in one bag, never two. The bag lived under the kitchen table in every place she'd rented since, and tonight she put it under the cottage's scrubbed pine table, where it fit exactly, as if the furniture had been waiting for her.

But there were things you couldn't run with and couldn't burn either. That was the cruelty of it. The ledger pages were the only proof that Marcus Vane had moved £340,000 through Halvern & Croft's client accounts into a shell company with his brother-in-law's name on it, and then walked the shortfall onto his junior associate's signature. Her signature. Four years ago, in a courtroom in Leeds, a man in a good suit had explained to twelve strangers how the metadata proved nothing, how access logs were routinely corrupted, how a young woman with debts and a grudge was exactly the profile fraud always wore. Twelve strangers had nodded. One of them had looked at her like he was sorry about it.

The pages were copies — she'd learned that much from the grandfather she'd never met, whose letters she'd answered for reasons she still couldn't fully explain — but copies were all she'd ever needed. Copies, plus time, plus someone who would listen without a judge's permission.

She unwrapped them from the tea towels she'd swaddled them in and laid them out on the floorboards in order. Page one. Page forty-one. The reconciliation sheets with the dates that didn't match the dates in the official audit, the little arithmetic of theft, patient as tide. She hadn't looked at them in eight months. Looking at them was like pressing a bruise to check whether it still hurt. It did.

Outside, through the cottage's small window, she could see the garden next door going gold and grey in the late afternoon, and beyond it the lighthouse, white and blind, its lamp room dark for fifty years. A decommissioned light. She'd chosen Brackenlow partly for that — a village with no reason to watch itself, no cameras on the high street except the one outside the chippy that everyone said hadn't worked since 2019. She'd chosen it partly because it was the kind of place where a letting agent asked no questions if you paid six months up front.

And partly — though she'd admitted this to no one, least of all herself — because the return address on ten years of letters had been here. *Silas Underhill, Keeper's Cottage, Brackenlow.* She had known the shape of those words before she ever saw the town. She had, in some sense, been moving toward them for a decade.

That was the part she'd have called romantic, back when she was a person who used words like that. Now it just felt like exposure.

* * *

Here is what Wren knew about Silas Underhill, compiled across ten years of correspondence, filed in the part of her memory she kept behind three locks:

That her grandfather had been the last keeper of the Brackenlow light, and that she'd inherited the cottage and the garden and a grief she wrote about with a precision that made Wren ache. That she was writing a novel, or trying to, and described her progress the way other people described weather — hopefully, inaccurately. That she took her tea too strong and apologized for it. That she had a habit of ending letters mid-thought and picking them up three lines later with *anyway*. That she was funny in a dry, sideways way that arrived most often when she was clearly trying not to say something else.

That she signed her name *S.U.*, and once, in the fourth year, had added a postscript: *You're the only person who writes to me who isn't obliged to.*

Wren had read that line maybe four hundred times. She had never once let herself wonder what it would cost to be the person standing in that garden.

Because here was the thing about the letters, the thing she'd built her whole architecture of caution around: Wren Halloway did not exist in them. The woman who wrote back was *W* — no surname, no city, no employer, no crime. In the first letter, sent at seventeen from a library in Sheffield, she'd invented almost nothing; she'd simply subtracted. Grief was true — her mother had died that spring, and she'd told Silas so. Loneliness was true. The rest was silence, and silence, she'd reasoned, was not a lie. It was a wall, and walls were legal.

Then the trial happened, and the subtraction became survival, and by then the letters were the only room in her life with no camera in it. She couldn't stop writing. She also couldn't risk being found. For ten years those two facts had coexisted the way a lit match and an open gas valve can coexist — perfectly, until they don't.

She had known, abstractly, that Silas lived in Brackenlow. Everyone knows where their own heart is buried; nobody expects to move in next door to it.

When the letting agent had handed over the keys and mentioned that the neighbor in the keeper's cottage kept a lovely garden, Wren had felt the floor tilt and blamed the long drive. Then she'd seen the woman herself, yesterday, kneeling among the dead rose canes with dirt to the wrists and hair escaping a knot, and Wren had stood in her own doorway holding a box labeled KITCHEN and thought, with total clarity: *that's her handwriting made flesh.*

She'd recognized her. Not from photographs — there were none, Silas had never sent one, another mercy of the rules — but from the letters themselves. From the way she moved like someone talking to plants because they listened better than people. From the particular stubborn gentleness of her, which Wren had spent a decade reading between lines.

Thirty feet. Thirty feet of garden wall between Wren Halloway, convicted embezzler, and the one person alive who would know her handwriting on sight.

* * *

She worked fast after that. Unpacking became triage. Books into shelves — the innocuous ones, the ones any woman might own. Clothes into the wardrobe. And the ledger pages, wrapped again, went not under the floorboards (first place anyone looks) and not in the loft (second place) but into the one hiding spot she'd scouted within an hour of arriving: inside the cottage's old bread oven, sealed behind a loose brick, dry as a bone and smelling faintly of a hundred years of loaves. If someone searched this cottage, they'd find a woman starting over. Quiet, tidy, forgettable. That was the performance, and Wren had gotten very good at performances she hated.

It was full dark when she finished, and she realized she hadn't eaten, and that eating meant going out, and going out meant the village shop, and the village shop meant being seen, and being seen was fine, actually, that was the point, invisible people drew more attention than ordinary ones. She recited the logic like a rosary while she pulled on her coat. Ordinary. Forgettable. A woman buying eggs.

The bell over the shop door did its cheerful work and Wren flinched, hating herself for it. Fluorescent light, shelves of tinned things, a radio murmuring the shipping forecast. Normal. She gathered eggs, bread, tea — strong tea, she caught herself, remembering *I take it strong enough to stand the spoon up, sorry* — and brought it all to the counter, where a woman in her fifties with reading glasses on a chain rang it up and studied her with frank, friendly interest.

"You'll be the new one at the keeper's cottage," the woman said. "Postmistress as well as shopkeeper, so I'm doubly pleased to meet you. I'm Enid Marsh."

"Wren." Just the name. No surname offered; surnames invited questions.

"Unusual." Enid smiled. "Pretty, though. You'll want to watch the post — deliveries come odd hours round here, tide-dependent sometimes, don't ask me why. Anything important, I'd send it recorded."

Something in Wren's face must have moved, because Enid laughed. "Everyone asks eventually. Mail's serious business in a village like this. Half our history's in envelopes."

Wren paid, thanked her, and walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and salt, telling herself the cold in her stomach was just hunger.

* * *

It wasn't hunger. It was the letter.

There was one letter she'd never mailed, the way there was one sentence Silas had apparently never sent — though of course Wren didn't know that yet, wouldn't know it for weeks, standing in firelight. What Wren knew was her own unsent thing: a reply drafted two years ago, after Silas had written about the anniversary of her grandfather's death and the empty chair at the kitchen table, a reply that had begun *If I lived nearer I would sit in that chair* and had gone on, dangerously, for three pages, into territory no careful person enters. She'd sealed it, addressed it, carried it to a postbox in a town whose name she no longer remembered, and stood there for eleven minutes. Then she'd torn it up and dropped the pieces in a bin, piece by piece, like burying something alive.

Because the truth was this, and she finally let herself think it, sitting at the pine table with cold tea going bitter beside her: she loved her. Ten years of ink, and somewhere in the middle of it the correspondence had stopped being a lifeline and become the actual life, and Wren had fallen in love with a voice, a mind, a way of noticing the world — fallen in love precisely as hard as you can fall when you've made sure you'll never have to be known.

And now the known and the unknown were thirty feet apart, separated by a wall and a garden, and tomorrow morning Silas Underhill would probably come over with cuttings, because that was what she did, that was in the letters, *I propagate shamelessly and give the babies away* — and Wren would have to stand in a doorway and be a stranger to the person who knew her best.

Unless she ran. There was always running. Ninety seconds, shoes by the bed, cash in the left pocket. She could be gone before the kettle boiled. She'd done it before; she'd done it in places she'd sworn she'd stay.

But she looked out the window instead, at the dark shape of the lighthouse against a sky just beginning to bruise with dawn, and she thought about page forty-one of the ledger, and about the fact that she was tired, so tired, of being a woman defined by leaving. Ten years ago a stranger had written back to a frightened girl and asked nothing, demanded nothing, required no explanation — had simply kept writing, season after season, like a light kept burning for ships that might never come in.

Wren Halloway had spent a decade being careful. She was beginning to suspect that care, unchecked, was just fear wearing a sensible coat.

She washed the cup. She dried it. She put it away in a cupboard that still smelled of someone else's life, and she lay down in the narrow bed and did not sleep, and around six the light came up grey over the water, and she heard — unmistakable, unhurried, thirty feet away — the sound of a gate hinge, and footsteps on gravel, coming toward her door.

Through the window she watched Silas Underhill cross the frost-silvered grass carrying a terracotta pot of green cuttings, and Wren's heart performed the entire catastrophe at once: terror and longing, braided so tight she couldn't tell which strand was shaking.

She had perhaps twenty seconds to decide who she was going to be.

She smoothed her hair, arranged her face into polite blankness, and opened the door before the knock — because a woman with nothing to hide doesn't wait to be knocked for.
