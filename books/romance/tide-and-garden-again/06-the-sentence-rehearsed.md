## 6. The Sentence Rehearsed

The tape recorder still worked.

Silas found it in the third box of her grandfather's papers, wedged between tide tables and a bundle of receipts from a hardware store that had closed before she was born. A silver Sony, its plastic gone amber with age, the cassette door held shut with a strip of electrical tape that had fossilized into something like commitment. She pressed PLAY out of habit more than hope, and the machine whirred, and then — impossibly, horribly — her own voice came out of it, nineteen years old and shaking.

*"Okay. Okay. Say it now. Just say it."*

A long silence on the tape. The sound of the sea through an open window, the same sea outside her window now, ten years later and no different.

*"I love—"*

She hit STOP so fast she nearly cracked the button.

She sat on the floor of the lighthouse keeper's cottage with the machine warm in her lap and her heart going like a bird trapped in a room, and she thought: *of all the boxes, of all the tapes, it had to be this one.*

There were three cassettes in the box. She knew without looking what was on them. She had recorded them over one week in October, ten years ago, in the bedroom of a flat in Falmouth that smelled of damp and other people's cigarettes, while the letter she'd received that morning lay open on her desk like a wound that wouldn't close.

She should burn them. That would be the sane thing. Feed them to the fire pit in the garden where she burned the unsent confession last month, let the plastic curl and stink and be done with it.

Instead she set the recorder on the kitchen table, poured herself tea she didn't drink, and let the past come up the stairs like a tide.

* * *

It began, as most ruinous things do, innocently.

Her grandfather had been dead eleven days when she walked into the Falmouth library because it was raining and her flat was unbearable and grief needed somewhere to go that wasn't inside her own skull. She remembered the exact shape of that afternoon: the green carpet, the radiator ticking, the smell of old paper and someone's microwaved soup. She wasn't looking for anything. That was the point of libraries after a death — you could stand among thousands of books and not be looking for anything, and no one minded.

She found *The Shipping News* by accident, or it found her, which is what she believed then and believes now. Her grandfather had read it twice. She opened it in the corner armchair by the window and read forty pages without absorbing a word, and then, somewhere around page fifty, she started crying — not elegantly, not the way people cry at funerals, but the ugly silent kind, tears dropping onto the library's property and warping the pages.

And because she couldn't say any of it out loud, not to her mother on the phone, not to the friends who kept sending casseroles, she did the only thing her body knew how to do, which was write.

She tore a page from the notebook in her bag — she always carried a notebook, even then, even before she was anyone — and she wrote:

*To whoever reads this book next: my grandfather died eleven days ago and I don't know how to be a person in a world that doesn't have him in it. He taught me the names of tides. He grew roses in salt wind, which everyone said was impossible, and he did it anyway for thirty years, which tells you everything about him. If you're reading this, please just know that someone loved him very much and that love doesn't have anywhere to live now, so it's living here, in this book, in this stupid note. Don't throw it away. That's all. That's the whole request.*

She signed it *S.* She folded it into the book at page 112, where the protagonist's life was also coming apart, and she put the book back on the shelf, and she walked home in the rain feeling marginally less like a ghost.

She forgot about it within a week. Grief is generous that way; it fills every available space.

Then, in February, an envelope arrived at her flat. No return address. Inside, a single sheet of paper, typed — actually typed, on a machine that left the letters slightly uneven, the *e*s riding high.

*Dear S.,*

*I found your note in The Shipping News. I'm sorry about your grandfather. I never met him but I feel like I have, because you described him the way people usually only describe weather — like something too big to argue with.*

*I don't know the proper etiquette for answering letters left in library books. There probably isn't any. So I'll just say: the roses aren't impossible. Salt kills most things, but not everything, and the things it can't kill are worth knowing about. He sounds like he was one of them.*

*If you ever want to write again, I'll answer. If you don't, I'll understand, and I'll leave your note where I found it for the next grieving person.*

*— W*

Silas read it four times standing in the hallway with her coat still on. Then she sat down on the stairs and cried again, but differently this time — the way you cry when something has come back to you that you didn't know you'd lost.

She wrote back that night. It took her three hours. She told W about the garden, about the lighthouse, about the way her grandfather used to taste the air in the morning and announce the day's weather like a man reading scripture. She didn't tell W her full name. Something about the arrangement — stranger to stranger, ink to ink — felt sacred precisely because it had no faces in it. Faces complicated things. Faces came with expectations, with the risk of being seen badly, known wrongly, dropped.

W wrote back in nine days. Then seven. Then five. Then they stopped counting.

* * *

By the third year, Silas had a shoebox. By the fifth, two. She kept them under her bed like contraband, and when she moved flats — twice, three times — the letters moved first, wrapped in a jumper, carried by hand rather than trusted to a van.

They wrote about everything except themselves. Or rather: they wrote about themselves constantly, endlessly, in the sideways manner of people who have agreed not to look directly at each other. W described a job she hated without naming it — *"I spend my days making numbers agree with each other, and lately the numbers have been lying"* — and cities she lived in without naming those either, though Silas pieced together Bristol, then somewhere further north, then, oddly, nothing at all for a stretch of months in year six, when the letters thinned to postcards and then thickened again as if nothing had happened. Silas told W about the novel she was failing to write, and then the novel she was writing badly, and then, in year eight, the novel that finally worked. W was the first person to read the ending. W said: *"You've written grief the way it actually is — not a wave but a weather system. I read the last page three times and then went outside and stood in my street like an idiot."*

Silas had read that line so many times she could recite it in W's cadence, though she had never heard W's voice, didn't know if it was high or low, accented or plain. That was the strange arithmetic of it: she knew W completely and not at all. She knew that W hated coriander and loved thunderstorms and had once, per a letter from year four, swum in the North Sea in February *"to prove a point to myself, though I've since forgotten what the point was."* She knew W's handwriting better than her own — the way it slanted harder when W was angry, loosened when W was happy, went small and dense during the bad months, of which there were more than Silas liked.

Because there were bad months. Letters that arrived with the ink pressed hard enough to tear the paper. *"Something has happened. I can't write about it yet. Don't ask. Please don't ask. Just keep writing to me, it's the only thing keeping me level."* And Silas had kept writing, every week, through the months when W's replies came late and short and wrong-seeming, and then through the worse months when they stopped coming at all for six weeks, and Silas had stood at her mailbox like a woman watching a horizon for a sail.

That was the year Silas bought the tape recorder. Not her grandfather's — a cheap dictaphone from an electronics shop, because some sentences were too dangerous to write down, and she had learned by nineteen-and-a-half that her hands would betray her, would write things her mouth would never agree to.

She had fallen in love with W sometime in year two and had known it by year three and had told absolutely no one, including W, including herself on most days.

Hence the tapes.

* * *

Present-day Silas looked at the three cassettes lined up on the kitchen table and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at inconvenient hours, that she had been rehearsing the same sentence for a decade.

She pressed PLAY on the second tape.

*"Right,"* said her nineteen-year-old voice, brittle with false cheer. *"Take four. This is ridiculous. People say this to actual humans, in actual rooms, with their actual mouths. It's four words. Three words. However many words it is."*

A breath. The sea.

*"W. I need to tell you something and I need you to not make it weird. I think I lo—"*

STOP.

Third tape. This one began mid-sentence, as if she'd given up on preamble entirely.

*"—and I know we've never met, I know I don't know what you look like, I know this is insane, but when your letters come I read them standing up because sitting down makes it too real, and when they're late I check the mail twice a day like a lunatic, and last Tuesday I wrote you a letter and then read it over and realized half of it was flirting and I couldn't decide whether to send it, and the fact that I couldn't decide means something, doesn't it? It means—"*

STOP.

Silas turned off the machine. The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the November light was doing its thin silver thing over the harbor, and the lighthouse stood white and blind above the garden, decommissioned, useless, still somehow the tallest thing for miles.

She had deleted every one of those recordings back then — well, not deleted, she wasn't a monster, she'd taped over the worst parts and hidden the rest — and she had never sent the sentence. Not in year three, when W wrote something that sounded almost like an opening. *"Sometimes I think if you lived closer I'd tell you things I can't tell anyone."* Not in year five, when Silas drafted an actual letter — *the* letter, the one she'd burned in the fire pit weeks ago, the confession written at nineteen and sealed and stamped and carried to the postbox and brought home again because her nerve failed at the slot. Not in year nine, when W's letters had grown strange and frightened and Silas had wanted, more than anything, to say *whatever it is, I'm yours, whatever you've done, whoever you are, I'm yours* — and instead had written something careful and useless about rose cuttings.

Ten years. Ten years of drafting the same sentence and filing it under *not yet*, and the punchline — the cosmic, humiliating punchline — was that W had lived thirty feet away for two months. Had taken cuttings-refusals and candlelight and storm-night conversation from Silas's hands. Had stood in Silas's kitchen eating soup while the sentence sat right there behind Silas's teeth, fully formed, ten years mature, and Silas had served her soup.

Unless she'd known. Unless she'd known the whole time.

No. Silas pushed that thought down. It was too large to hold and too sharp to carry. Wren flinched at cameras and asked odd questions about the post and watched Silas with an attention that could have been recognition or could have been suspicion or could simply have been loneliness wearing a disguise. There was no evidence. There was only the phrasing — *that night, candles, the storm* — Wren saying *"you keep writing the way other people talk,"* and Silas's spine going cold, and Wren changing the subject so smoothly it might have been accident.

Might have been.

Silas got a fresh sheet of paper. This was ritual now, older than the correspondence itself: when she couldn't speak, she drafted. Her grandfather had done the same with his logs — she'd found entries crossed out and rewritten, the truth attempted three times before it was allowed to stand.

She wrote:

*Dear W —*

*Here is the sentence. I've been carrying it for ten years and I'm tired of carrying it and I'm done pretending it weighs nothing. I love you. Not the abstraction — not the voice on paper, not the idea I built out of your handwriting. Well. Yes, that too, shamelessly. But also the woman next door who won't take my cuttings and looks at me like I'm a question she's afraid to answer. If that's you — if it's been you this whole time — then I am furious with you, and I don't care, and I want you to stay. Stay. That's the whole letter. That's the whole decade.*

*— S*

She read it twice. Her hands were steady, which surprised her.

Then she folded it, and did not stamp it, and did not walk it to the postbox, and did not carry it thirty feet to the cottage next door. She put it in the drawer with her grandfather's tide tables, underneath the tape recorder, in the place where unsent things went to wait.

*Not yet,* she told herself. *Not until I know.*

But the drawer stuck slightly when she closed it, and the sound it made was exactly the sound of a door being decided upon, and upstairs the phone rang — the landline, which almost nobody used anymore, which rang perhaps twice a year — and Silas stood in her kitchen with her heart suddenly loud, listening to it ring and ring, and thought: *nobody calls this number except people who don't know it's dead.*

She let it ring out.

Outside, down the lane, a car she didn't recognize slowed past the garden wall. Slowed. Paused. Moved on.

Silas stood at the window a long time, watching the road, one hand flat against the drawer that held the sentence, and the light in the cottage next door stayed dark until well past dark.
