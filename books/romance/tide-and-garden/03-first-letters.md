## 3. First Letters

### Edmund

The letter sat on my kitchen table for three days before I opened it.

This is not an exaggeration for dramatic effect, though I've learned that most things worth saying are exaggerations for dramatic effect. It sat there under the fruit bowl like something venomous that had wandered in from the garden, and I walked around it the way you walk around a wasp on a windowsill — carefully, respectfully, with the full knowledge that one of us was going to have to do something about it eventually.

It had come in Tuesday's post, on library stationery, with Mrs. Okafor's unmistakable handwriting on the envelope. *Mr. E. Coldharbour. You have been matched.* Four words and already the exclamation point of destiny hanging over me.

I should explain. The Greywater Bay public library runs a pen-pal program every winter, ostensibly to combat loneliness among the town's shut-ins and eccentrics, actually because Mrs. Okafor believes with religious fervor that the postal service is the last honest institution left in Britain and must be defended accordingly. You write your first letter anonymously. The library forwards it. Your correspondent never learns your name unless both parties agree, in writing, after no fewer than six exchanges. There are rules. There are laminated rules. I have seen the lamination.

I had signed up in October, if memory serves, in a moment of weakness brought on by rain, a failed batch of damson jam, and the particular silence of a house that used to belong to someone who talked. My aunt had been dead eleven months by then, and her absence had developed a texture — velvety, suffocating, faintly sweet, like fruit going over. Signing up for a pen pal seemed harmless. A letter a month. Someone to lie to politely about how I was getting on.

What I had not accounted for was the terror of the blank page.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about anonymity: it strips away the performance, and then you discover how much of yourself was performance. With a name attached, a letter is a costume. You write the version of yourself you'd like the recipient to meet — the witty one, the stoic one, the one whose garden is doing better than it is. Anonymous, there's nowhere to hide. Whoever reads it meets the actual man, the one who talks to bean plants and rehearses arguments with his dead aunt in the greenhouse.

So for three days the letter sat there, and I made tea, and I weeded the brassica bed in weather no sensible person weeds in, and I listened to the sea doing its one song over and over like a cellist who only knows open strings.

On the third evening I opened it, poured a glass of the cooking sherry I pretend is for cooking, and wrote back in one sitting, the way you rip off a plaster or confess to a crime.

---

*Dear Correspondent,*

*Mrs. Okafor tells me I'm to address you as nothing at all, which suits me. Names are heavy things. I've spent twelve years trying to put mine down.*

*You asked what I do with my days. I'll tell you, since you asked so directly — you have the directness of someone who interrogates people professionally, or wishes they did. I keep a garden that belonged to my aunt, who kept it for forty years and would be appalled by my stewardship. I teach music to children, eight of them, none of whom chose the instrument and all of whom hold it the way you'd hold a dead fish. On Tuesdays I buy groceries. On Wednesdays I regret the groceries.*

*You asked what I'm afraid of. That's not a question strangers ask, but then we're not exactly strangers, are we — we're whatever the opposite is. Two people who know everything except each other.*

*I'll answer honestly, because I suspect you'll know if I don't. I'm afraid of my own hands.*

*That sounds mad written down. Let me try again. Twelve years ago I did a thing I was good at — genuinely good, the kind of good where people go quiet — and in front of several hundred people, my hands stopped working. Not dramatically. No blood, no broken glass. They simply declined. Like staff who've decided to resign mid-shift. And everything I was ended in the space between one note and the next.*

*I haven't played since. I teach, which is like being a chef who can only describe food. Some nights I take the instrument out of its case just to feel the weight of it, the way you might visit a grave. Then I put it away and water something, because watering things is what I do instead of grieving.*

*There. Now you know the worst of me, and you don't even know my name. Isn't the post a marvelous invention.*

*Yours,*
*— C.*

*P.S. You didn't ask a second question, which I respect enormously. Ask it next time. I find I'm in the alarming habit of answering you.*

---

I read it over twice, mortified, and did not burn it. That felt significant, though I couldn't have said why. I sealed it, addressed it care of the library, and walked down to the postbox in the dark like a man disposing of evidence, and stood there a full minute with the letter in the slot, my hand still holding it through the metal, as though the box and I were having a tug-of-war nobody could see.

Then I let go. The flap clanged. Somewhere out in the dark, the tide was coming in whether I approved or not.

Walking home, I caught myself humming. I hadn't hummed in years. I decided not to read anything into it, and then read everything into it, all the way up the lane.

### Vivienne

Let us establish something immediately: I did not want a pen pal. I wanted ammunition.

The pen-pal program was Mrs. Okafor's idea, pressed upon me in the library's back room while I waited for the solicitor, and I said yes for three reasons. One: she had that relentless kindness that makes refusal feel like kicking a spaniel. Two: I needed somewhere to put the fury, and fury needs a mailbox. Three — and I'll admit this under seal of anonymity, which is rather the point — I thought it might be useful. A local voice. Someone who knew the town's soft spots. I'd spent fifteen years in London learning that every person is a door, and doors are for opening.

My father had died nine days earlier, and I had come home to collect.

I want to be precise about "home," because the word has been doing fraudulent work in this town for decades. Greywater Bay was not my home. It was the place my father kept his house, his boats, his grudges, and his second family — the town itself, which he courted more attentively than he ever courted my mother or me. He was generous here the way men are generous with money that costs them nothing emotionally. Every bench had a little plaque. Every plaque had his name. I grew up understanding that love, in our household, was a thing you endowed.

The will, I'd been told, was sealed pending some condition. Lawyers love conditions the way cats love boxes. Fine. I could wait. I'd waited twenty years to be acknowledged; I could wait a season more. Meanwhile, I wrote my first letter on hotel stationery at one in the morning, slightly drunk on the good scotch I'd found in my father's study, and I meant it to be a weapon.

It came out as something else entirely. These things happen when you're tired and grief has loosened the screws.

---

*Dear Correspondent,*

*You should know at the outset that I am not a nice person. I say this not as false modesty but as consumer protection. People meet me and expect either the ice queen or the wounded bird, and I am neither, or possibly both, and either way they leave disappointed. Consider yourself warned.*

*You asked what I'm afraid of. Bold. Most people lead with the weather.*

*Here is my answer, and I'll thank you to lose it immediately: I don't know if I loved my father. He died nine days ago and I have cried exactly once, and that was at something unrelated — a dog in a raincoat, if you must know, a small dog in a yellow raincoat outside the chippy, and I sobbed into my scarf like a woman in a Russian novel, and then went dry again when I got to the church.*

*What does that make me? Everyone at the funeral had theories. The town loved him — loves him, present tense, he's more alive dead than most people manage living. They looked at me, the daughter who left, the daughter who stayed away, and I could hear the arithmetic running behind their eyes. Cold. Ungrateful. Her mother's child.*

*Maybe. Or maybe you cannot love a man who treated affection like a subscription service — generous tiers available, premium features locked. Maybe some children aren't unloving, just unaudited. Nobody ever checked the books on what he owed me, and now he's dead, and the audit is closed, and I'm standing here with a yellow-raincoat-dog amount of grief and no idea whose fault that is.*

*You told me your secret. I could hear it straining against your sentences — the hands, the music, the thing that ended. You think you disguised it. You didn't. You wrapped it in gardening prose like a pill in cheese, and I swallowed it whole anyway.*

*So here's mine, unwrapped: I came back to this town to take what I'm owed, and I will burn down anything in my way, and the fact that I just admitted that to a stranger with a postage stamp says something damning about my current state.*

*Don't write back something kind. I'll know it's a technique.*

*— V.*

*P.S. The dog's name was Biscuit. I asked. Obviously I asked.*

---

I sealed it before I could reconsider, which is the only way I ever do anything worth doing.

Then I sat in my father's study — my study, allegedly, pending the lawyers — surrounded by his things. His decanters. His photographs of himself with people more important than himself. His model ships, which he'd built with those careful surgeon's hands and never once offered to teach me to build, though I'd asked, aged nine, aged twelve, aged sixteen, a record of asking preserved in amber somewhere in my chest cavity.

And I thought about the letter, and about the stranger on the other end of it, whoever they were. Some gardener, probably. Some local. Someone who'd known my father the way everyone here had known him — as the benefactor, the name on the plaques — and who would be horrified to learn who they were writing to.

I almost laughed. Imagine it. Imagine being paired, out of all the lonely souls in Greywater Bay, with someone whose father you were currently preparing to sue.

Imagine.

### Edmund

Her reply arrived in nine days, which Mrs. Okafor informed me was "remarkably prompt, considering." Considering what, she declined to specify, with the air of a woman guarding state secrets.

I took it up to the greenhouse to read, because the greenhouse is where I do my feeling — it's warm, it smells of tomato vines, and nothing in there requires anything of me except attention. I slit the envelope with the pruning knife. Small blue handwriting, slanted forward like it was in a hurry to get somewhere.

She called me bold. She called my disguise transparent. She told me about her father, and the dog, and the funeral, and then she said the thing I've been turning over ever since, the way you turn a stone over in a stream to see what's underneath:

*"Nobody ever checked the books on what he owed me."*

I read that line four times. Because I knew that arithmetic. I had done that exact sum across a different ledger, twelve years ago, standing in a green room with a contract in front of me and a man's generosity curdling into ownership, and I had understood suddenly and completely that what I'd taken for patronage was purchase. That the quiet checks, the introductions, the instrument — all of it had been a subscription service, and I was expected to renew.

I never told anyone that. Not my aunt, not the doctors, not the children's parents. The official story — the story the town half-knows, the story I let stand — is that my nerves failed, that performing is a young man's game, that bodies betray artists sometimes and there's no villain in it.

And here was a stranger, some furious anonymous woman with a pruning-knife wit, writing the truest sentence about my life without knowing she'd written it.

I sat on the potting bench until my legs went numb, and then I wrote back.

---

*Dear V.,*

*You instructed me not to write back something kind. I'm going to ignore you, but only briefly, and then I'm going to do something worse, which is write back something true.*

*Your father sounds like a man who kept accounts. I knew a man like that once. Everything he gave came with invisible stitching — you'd wear the gift for years before you noticed the thread ran back to him, and by then pulling it unraveled the whole garment. I signed nothing, in the end. It cost me nearly everything. Some nights I think the signing would have cost less. Most nights I know it wouldn't have.*

*Here's what I believe about your dog and your dry eyes: grief doesn't run on schedule, and it doesn't always attach to the correct recipient. Sometimes you cry at the dog because the dog is small and honest and wearing a raincoat, and your father was none of those things, and the feeling has to go somewhere. I don't think that makes you cold. I think it makes you someone whose feelings are waiting for a safe place to land.*

*God knows I know about waiting.*

*You said you came back to take what you're owed. I won't argue with that — I don't know what you're owed, and it isn't my business, and I've learned the hard way that other people's inheritances are minefields best admired from a distance. But I'll tell you what I tend, since you seem to collect confessions: a garden that isn't mine, on land that was never really mine, for a woman who's gone. And I've started to suspect that's the only inheritance worth having. The kind you keep alive rather than the kind you keep.*

*Forgive the sermon. I spend my days with eight-year-olds; occasionally the preaching escapes.*

*Ask me anything. I appear to have lost the ability to lie to you, and I'd like to get some use out of it before I recover.*

*— C.*

*P.S. Biscuit sounds like a very good dog. The raincoat was clearly earned.*

### Vivienne

He wrote back in ten days, and I read his letter standing in the hallway of my father's house in my coat, because I'd only come inside to escape the wind and had no intention of staying, and then I stayed for two hours.

I'd like to report that I remained armored throughout. That I read his confession about the contract and the invisible stitching with cool professional detachment, filing it away. But the truth is I read the line *"the signing would have cost less"* and had to put the letter down on the hall table and look at the coat hooks for a while, because something in my chest had gone strange and hot.

He'd refused to sign. This stranger who described vegetables and dead fish instruments had stood where I have spent my whole life refusing to stand — in front of a powerful man holding out paper — and said no, and been destroyed for it, and rebuilt himself into someone who waters things.

And I had signed everything, always. Every compromise, every silence, every Christmas phone call where I performed gratitude for gifts that were really invoices. I'd told myself it was strategy. Survival. The smart move. Standing in that hallway with his letter shaking slightly in my hand, I heard my own voice from the night of the scotch: *I will burn down anything in my way,* and for the first time it sounded less like armor and more like inheritance. His. My father's. The account-keeping voice, installed in me so long ago I'd mistaken it for my own.

I wrote my reply that evening. It took four attempts. The first three were barbed and brilliant and completely dishonest, and I burned them in the study fireplace, which felt ceremonial, which felt like practice.

---

*Dear C.,*

*Attempt number four. The others are ash. You should feel honored; I've never burned drafts for anyone.*

*You asked what I'm owed. I've been thinking about it, and the honest answer is: I don't know. That's the humiliating truth at the center of me. I built a whole life on the certainty that I was owed something — by my father, by this town, by the universe generally — and now he's dead and the estate is locked behind some lawyer's riddle, and I'm sitting in his house realizing I don't actually know what I wanted from him. An apology? Acknowledgment? For him to have been, once, the man in the photographs before the photographs got expensive?*

*None of it's collectible now. That's the joke. I waited twenty years to present the bill and the debtor died insolvent.*

*You said other people's inheritances are minefields. Yours especially, maybe. Don't ask me why I say that. I know things about this town's land dealings I wish I didn't, and I'm not ready to say them, and possibly never will be, and you deserve better than a redacted confession, so consider this a placeholder.*

*Tell me about the children. Not the teaching — the children. What do they play like? Do any of them love it yet? I ask because I'm trying to remember if I ever loved anything before I learned to want. It occurs to me I may have skipped a step.*

*Also: your aunt. Tell me about her. Anyone who leaves a garden to a man who talks to plants must have been formidable.*

*— V.*

*P.S. I looked up whether it's legal to cry at a stranger's letter. The research was inconclusive. Make of that what you will.*

---

I sent it the next morning, walking to the postbox in a wind that smelled of salt and coming change, and I felt lighter and more terrified than I had since the funeral.

Because here is what I hadn't told him, couldn't tell him, the redaction sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone: the will was to be read Friday. And the solicitor's letter, which I'd read eleven times, had mentioned — casually, in that way lawyers mention landmines — that the disposition of the estate would rest on the judgment of a town council.

And one of the names on that council, printed there in ordinary black type, was **E. Coldharbour**.

I didn't know who he was. Not yet. But the name snagged in me like a hook, and all the way back up the hill from the postbox, the sea keeping its one dull time below, I found myself wondering what kind of man holds a stranger's fate in one hand and writes letters like that with the other.

Friday would tell me.

I had no idea, then, how much.
