## 3. The Cottage Next Door

The moving truck came on a Tuesday in late March, when the harbor was still gray with winter and the town had not yet remembered it was beautiful.

Silas heard it first—the diesel groan of an engine laboring up the hill from the causeway, a sound rare enough in Harbor Light that she lifted her head from her manuscript like a deer catching wind of something wrong. She was sitting at the kitchen table in her sweater and wool socks, a mug of tea gone cold at her elbow, working on the scene where her heroine finally admits she cannot forgive her father. The words had been coming slowly, the way they always did in March, when the light was thin and the sea gave nothing back.

She watched the truck through the window over the sink. It slowed at the fork in the road, hesitated—everyone hesitated at the fork, there was no signage, there had never been signage—and then took the left branch, the one that led past her house to the only other dwelling on this stretch of the point: the Hollis cottage, empty for six years, its shingles weathered the color of driftwood, its windows holding that particular blind shine of glass that no one has looked through in a long time.

Silas set down her pen.

She had known, of course. Ruthie Pell at the post office had told her three weeks ago, in the way Ruthie told everyone everything, wrapping the news in tissue paper of speculation. *Sold to a fellow from away. Young, they say. Some kind of settlement money, they say. You'll never guess why he came here, Silas. You'll never guess.* And Silas, who had guessed instantly, who had known the moment the words *settlement money* left Ruthie's mouth, had said, *I'm sure I won't,* and bought her stamps, and walked home with her heart going like a gull in a storm.

Ten years of letters. Ten years of writing to a man she had never met, in a prison three hundred miles away, signing herself only *A Friend of the Truth.* Ten years of telling him about the tide, about the terns coming back in April, about the smell of the cannery when they ran the herring through, because she had decided early on that if she could not give him her name, she could at least give him the world he'd lost—the world he'd been taken from at nineteen years old, convicted of a robbery and a beating he had not committed, while a fourteen-year-old girl stood behind a stack of lobster traps and said nothing.

The truck backed up the cottage drive with a series of beeps that scattered the crows from the Hollises' dead apple tree. A man got out.

Even at this distance—even through glass fogged at the corners with the breath of the house—she knew him. Not from memory; she had never seen him before the trial, and at the trial he had been a boy, hollow-cheeked, in a borrowed suit that hung off his shoulders like the skin of something larger. She knew him from the photographs. The ones the *Boston Globe* had run when the Innocence Project took his case. The ones Ruthie had clipped and saved, because Harbor Light remembered its scandals the way the tide remembered the shore. Jonah Reyes, thirty-one years old now, taller than the boy had been, with the same dark hair and a way of standing that was very still, the way of a man who had spent a decade learning that sudden movement could be misread.

He stood looking at the cottage for a long time. He did not go inside right away. He walked the perimeter instead, slow, touching the shingles, and Silas understood that he was checking whether the world was real. She had read enough letters back—his handwriting had gone from desperate to careful to something almost serene over the years—to know the shape of what he was doing. *Sometimes I stand outside and just look at a building,* he had written once, in his fifth year. *Any building. A garage. I think: that was built by someone, and it's still standing, and nobody took it away. It helps.*

She should not have known that about him. That was the thing she could never say aloud, the thing that lived in her chest like a fishhook: she knew him intimately, and he did not know her at all.

The movers brought out boxes. Jonah directed them with small gestures. And Silas stood at her sink with cold tea and did the arithmetic she had been doing for three weeks: the cottage was forty yards away. Forty yards of salt grass and one leaning fence. She could not take her mail without passing his door. She could not walk her beach without crossing his view.

She had ten years to prepare for this and she had prepared nothing.

* * *

That evening she did what she always did when she could not write and could not sleep: she took out the letters. Not his—hers. She kept carbons, had kept them from the beginning, a habit born of a lawyer's warning that she should document nothing and her own stubborn refusal to stop. The box lived in the bottom of her closet, under the winter coats, and she knelt on the floorboards and lifted the lid and the smell of old paper rose up, faintly sweet, like something drying in the sun.

The first letter, dated eleven years ago last January, began: *Dear Mr. Reyes, You do not know me, and I will not tell you who I am, but I know you are innocent, and I thought someone should say so in writing.*

She had been fifteen then. The trial had ended in December, the verdict read in a courtroom that smelled of floor wax and her mother's perfume, and Silas had sat in the second row with her hands pressed flat against her knees and watched a boy be sentenced to twenty years for a crime she had watched another man commit. She had been fourteen the night of it—October, the fish market on Commercial Street, out past curfew because her father had been drinking and home was a place you circled like a dog circles a trap. She had seen the whole thing from behind the lobster traps: the man with the tire iron, the way the shopkeeper went down, the face of the man with the tire iron—she had known that face, he bought bait from her father, he'd once given her a quarter for the gum machine—and she had seen Jonah Reyes too, younger than she was, walking past the market with his cello in its case, and she had seen him stop, and she had seen him run toward it, and she had seen the police arrive and decide, in the space of four minutes, that the boy with the record of a broken window was the one who did it.

She could have said something. That was the sentence she had carried ever since, worn smooth as sea glass from handling: *I could have said something.*

She had been afraid of her father finding out she'd been out. She had been afraid of the man with the tire iron, who had looked down the row of traps as the police led Jonah away, and looked at her, and she had understood that look the way animals understand a raised hand. She had been fourteen. And then the years passed, and the fear did not pass with them, it only changed shape, and the longer she waited the more the silence itself became the crime—a second crime, committed daily, against the same man.

So: the letters. Penance by correspondence. She told him about the town he'd been stolen from. She told him when the herring ran, when the lighthouse got its new lamp, when Mrs. Hollis died and the cottage went empty. She never told him who she was. She never told him she had been there. In the first year she almost did, a dozen times; she would get as far as *I know you are innocent because* and then stop, the pen hovering, and cross it out, and write instead about the weather.

And he had written back. That was the miracle she had never earned. The prison forwarded the letters to some program, or he had simply answered the return address—a post office box she'd rented under her pen name—and for ten years, a man in a cell in Concord had written to a stranger about hope, and a woman in a cottage in Harbor Light had written back about the tide, and between them they had built something that Silas could not name without lying.

*You are the only person,* he wrote in his ninth year, *who has never asked me what I was convicted of. Everyone else wants the story. You just give me the harbor.*

She had sat with that letter for a whole day, unable to touch it.

Now the box was closed and the man himself was forty yards away, and every letter was suddenly evidence.

* * *

She did not go over that first week. She told herself it was tact, giving him room to land, and it was, partly. But it was also cowardice, the oldest kind, and she knew the difference even as she chose it.

The town had no such scruples. Ruthie Pell appeared at Silas's door on Thursday with a lemon loaf and the full report: the truck from Boston, the boxes, the man himself buying a shovel and a rake at the hardware store and asking Mr. Tavares, in a voice Ruthie described as *careful, like he was handling each word before he set it down,* whether the soil on the point grew anything. Silas held the lemon loaf and made the appropriate sounds and felt like a spy in her own kitchen.

"He seems sweet," Ruthie said, in the tone that meant the opposite of finished. "Quiet. You'd like him, Silas, you're both quiet ones. And can you imagine—" she lowered her voice as though the cottage could hear—"ten years, and then to come home to *this.* I cried, I don't mind saying. The whole town cried when they read the article. That detective, too, the one who—"

"I read the article," Silas said.

Ruthie looked at her, and Silas arranged her face into the mild, closed expression she had been perfecting since she was fourteen, the face that said *nothing here, nothing to see, move along.* It worked. It always worked. That was the tragedy of it; it had always worked.

"Well," Ruthie said, taking the hint the way the tide takes the sand, gradually and completely. "You'll introduce yourself, I expect. Being neighbors."

"Of course," Silas said, and closed the door, and stood with her forehead against it.

* * *

She went over on Sunday, because Sunday was the day she walked the point, and the walk took her past his gate, and it would be stranger not to speak. That was the story she told herself. She put on the gray coat, the one that made her feel armored, and she walked down through the salt grass with the wind pushing at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades, and she rehearsed—God help her, she rehearsed—*Welcome to Harbor Light, I'm Silas Dunmore, I live next door, I write novels,* as though she were a normal woman making normal conversation and not the architect of a decade of correspondence.

He was in the garden. Of course he was; the shovel and the rake. He had cleared a strip of the old Hollis bed and was turning the soil with the concentration of a man performing surgery, and he did not hear her at the gate, and she stood there for a moment with the wind in her face and looked at him properly for the first time.

The photographs had not lied, but they had flattened him. In person he was taller and thinner, with lines around his eyes that thirty-one was too young for, and he moved with a deliberateness that she recognized from her letters—he had written about learning to move slowly, in his sixth year, *because in here everything fast is dangerous, so I taught my body to be slow and now it won't unlearn it.* His hands were remarkable. She had thought so even at the trial, that terrible detail she'd never confessed to anyone: even at fourteen, even in the middle of the horror, she had noticed the boy's hands, long-fingered and careful, cradling the cello case like it was a sleeping thing.

"Hello," she said.

He turned. And she watched it happen—the flicker of assessment, the half-second where a man who had spent ten years among strangers measured a new one—and then his face opened, cautiously, like a fist uncurling.

"You're the neighbor." His voice was lower than she'd imagined, and roughened, as if it had been used sparingly for a decade. "The writer. Ruthie told me. She told me a lot of things." A pause, and then, with something that was almost a smile: "I think she told me your entire bibliography."

"She has the complete works," Silas said. "Including the ones that don't exist yet."

"I'm Jonah." He wiped his hand on his thigh, and then, as if remembering something, wiped it again, more thoroughly, and offered it. "Jonah Reyes."

His hand was warm and calloused at the pads of the fingers. She had known it would be. She hated herself for knowing it.

"Silas Dunmore."

"Silas." He tried the name the way he tried everything, carefully, setting it down and looking at it. "That's an unusual name. Family name?"

"My father's idea. He wanted a boy." The truth, or a piece of it, offered like small change.

"It's a good name," he said. "It sounds like it belongs here." And then, gesturing at the ruined bed with the rake: "I'm told things grow on this point. I'm told that's what it's for."

"Mrs. Hollis grew roses," Silas said. "And beans. She said the secret was to talk to them, but I suspect the secret was that she never shut up and the plants had no choice."

Jonah laughed. It came out of him unexpectedly, a short startled sound, as though laughter were a language he was relearning and had just surprised himself with a correct word. And Silas stood in the wind with her heart going and thought: *I know that laugh. I have been given that laugh, in ink, forty times, at the ends of letters, after the jokes I told him about the terns and the tourists. I have earned nothing and I know everything.*

"The article said you write about the coast," he said. "The reporter asked what brought you here, and I read your answer, and I thought—" he stopped, and she watched him decide whether to say the thing, and then he said it: "I thought, that's where I'm going too. Harbor Light. I used to live two towns over, before. My mother moved us to Boston when I was sixteen. But I spent summers here. There was a dock off the point, the old one. Did you know it?"

"I did," Silas said. "It burned when I was little."

"Everything burns or falls down," Jonah said, not bitterly, just factually, a man reporting weather. "But it's still here, the point. That's what I kept thinking on the drive up. The point's still here."

The wind came off the water and flattened the grass and Silas heard, beneath his words, the sentence he wasn't saying: *I'm still here. They took ten years and I'm still here.*

"There's a good bakery in town," she heard herself say. "The woman who runs it knew your mother, I think. Ada Reyes. She used to sell her bread at the Saturday market."

Something moved through his face, quick as a fish under a boat. "You know my mother's name?"

"Small town," Silas said. "Everyone knows everyone's business. You'll hate it and then you'll never leave."

He looked at her for a long moment then, longer than the conversation required, and she felt the look go through her like a probe through ice, testing the thickness. What he found there she could not have said. Whatever it was, he nodded, once, and said, "Well. Thank you. For coming by. People keep—people have been very kind, and I keep waiting for the catch, and there isn't one, and that's its own kind of strange."

"There's no catch," Silas said, and the words tasted like ash and salt, and she smiled, and turned back down the path, and did not let her hands shake until the fence was between them.

* * *

That night she wrote to him.

She sat at the kitchen table with the lamp on and the tide going out with a long hissing sigh, and she took a fresh sheet of paper, and she wrote: *Dear Mr. Reyes,* and stopped. Because he was not *Mr. Reyes* anymore. He was the man with the rake and the startled laugh and the hands, forty yards away, and the letter she had been writing for ten years had just become a letter to a neighbor, to a friend, to—she would not let herself think the word yet, but it stood in the doorway of her mind anyway, patient as winter—possibly more.

She wrote: *The herring will run in April. You'll know it before you see it—the gulls go mad first, the whole sky turns white and screaming over the channel. I thought you might want to know, since you're here for it this year. You're here for it.*

She read it back. It was a good letter. It was the kind of letter she had written a hundred times, warm and observant and utterly evasive, and for the first time in ten years the evasiveness made her sick. He was going to be in her life. He was going to borrow her rake and stand with her at the mailboxes and ask her about her books, and every kindness between them would be built on the same foundation as everything else: the thing she saw, and the thing she didn't say.

She signed it *A Friend of the Truth,* as she always did, and then she looked at the signature for a long time.

Then she folded the letter, and put it in an envelope, and did not seal it. She set it on the corner of the desk, where she could see it from the bed, and she lay down in the dark and listened to the tide and thought about the look he'd given her at the gate—*the probe through ice*—and wondered what he'd felt under there, under all that stillness. Wondered if a man who had spent ten years learning to read people for danger had read anything true in her face.

In the morning, the letter was still on the desk, unsealed, unsent.

And when she went out to get the paper, she found a box on her porch step—no note, just a box, wrapped in the brown paper the hardware store used, and inside, wrapped in a dish towel, a jar of Mrs. Hollis's old rose jam, salvaged from the pantry, with a scrap of paper in careful, deliberate handwriting: *Thought you should have this. It was hers, and you knew her. — J.*

Silas stood on her porch in the early light holding the jar, and down the point, faintly, she could hear the sound of a rake against stone, steady as a heartbeat, and she understood that the tide had turned, that there was no more waiting, no more letters across a distance, that the man himself had arrived in her life with his careful hands and his startled laugh, and that every day from now on would be a choice.

She carried the jam inside and set it on the windowsill, where the light came through it like something burning, and she did not cry, and she did not seal the letter, and the herring were coming in April, and she had perhaps a month to decide who she was going to be when the whole sky went white and screaming.
