## 10. First Kiss, Second Secret

The summer turned the color of ripe things. Harbor Light in August was a town made of light and salt—the marsh grass gone gold at the edges, the harbor so full of sailboats it looked like a flock of white birds that had decided to rest on the water. Tourists came on weekends and left their money and their noise behind them by Sunday night, and then the town exhaled and became itself again: the fish market hosing down its slabs, the church bell counting out the hours, the long slow slap of water against pilings like a heart that had nowhere to be.

Silas had lived through thirty-one summers here, or near enough to here, and she could not remember one that had felt like this one. It was not that anything had happened. That was the strange part. Nothing had happened, and everything had changed.

Jonah Reyes fixed her fence on a Tuesday.

She hadn't asked him to. She'd mentioned it once, in passing, the way you mention weather—a slat loose by the gate, leaning into the wind off the water—and three days later she looked out her kitchen window and there he was, shirt dark at the back with sweat, a hammer in his hand, fitting the slat back into place with the same unhurried precision he brought to everything. He worked the way he must have learned to work in prison, she thought, and hated herself for thinking it: carefully, without waste, as if tools were a privilege.

She carried out two glasses of iced tea and set them on the porch rail, and he came up the steps wiping his hands on a rag, and they stood there drinking tea in the late-morning heat while the harbor glittered below them like something scattered on purpose.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

"I know." He looked out at the water. He did that a lot—looked at the water like a man reading a letter from someone he wasn't sure he forgave. "I wanted to."

It was such a small sentence. I wanted to. But she heard everything underneath it, all the years of wanting to and being unable to, all the doors that opened for other people when they stayed shut for him, and she had to look down into her glass so he wouldn't see what crossed her face.

They had fallen into a life together without ever naming it. Mornings, sometimes, they walked the tideline before the beach filled up, picking through what the tide had left—kelp ropes, moon snail shells, once the ribs of a skiff that had broken loose from someone's mooring and been beaten against the jetty until it gave up. Afternoons he read on her porch because his cottage was still half boxes; he read whatever she was reading, so they could argue about it, and they argued about books the way other people argued about politics, seriously, joyfully, over cold dinners that went cold because neither of them wanted to stop talking. Evenings he cooked, badly and with tremendous confidence, and she ate every over-salted bite.

And always, underneath it, unspoken, the knowledge that sat between them like a third person at the table: *ten years.* Ten years of letters. Ten years of a voice that had kept him alive in a concrete room, arriving week after week like weather from a kinder country. He had told her once, in June, turning his teacup slowly on its saucer, that he used to read her letters aloud in his cell, quietly, just to hear a human cadence that wasn't a guard's or a lawyer's or another man's grief. He said it lightly. She had nearly come apart at the table.

He didn't know she was the one who wrote them. He knew only that a stranger named S.—no last name, no return address beyond a post office box in Harbor Light—had written him four hundred and eighty-one letters, and that when he got out, the first thing he'd done, before the press conference, before his mother's arms, was go to the Harbor Light post office and ask, and been told that box 114 had been closed two weeks earlier, paid up, emptied, gone.

"Whoever it was," he'd said, "didn't want to be found."

*No,* Silas had thought, her heart going flat and hard in her chest. *Whoever it was couldn't bear to be.*

* * *

The kiss happened on a Thursday, which surprised her later, because she would have expected it to happen on some occasion, some hinge of a day, and instead it happened on a day exactly like every other day, which is how most true things happen.

They had walked out to Gull Point at low tide, past the mussel beds, to the spit of sand and stone where the harbor opens onto the open sound. The light was doing what it does there in the hour before sunset—turning everything to bronze, laying a road of hammered gold across the water straight out to the horizon, as if you could walk it if you were brave enough or foolish enough.

Jonah crouched at the tideline and picked up a piece of sea glass, green, frosted smooth, and turned it in his fingers.

"My mother used to collect this," he said. "Back before. We'd come to Revere Beach and she'd fill her pockets. She kept a jar of it on the windowsill. When I—" He stopped. Started again. "When I went away, she moved apartments. The jar got lost somewhere between one life and the next. She still talks about it. Isn't that something. Thirty years of a life, and what she mourns is a jar of broken glass."

"It's not the glass," Silas said.

"No," he agreed softly. "It's not the glass."

He held the piece out to her, and she took it, and it was warm from his hand, and she closed her fingers around it and thought: *this is the moment.* Not because of any grand feeling but because of the smallness of it, the terrible tenderness of a man handing you a piece of the sea and telling you about his mother's windowsill. Because she knew, in the way you know a wave has begun its long gathering far offshore, that if she did not do something now, she would spend the rest of her life standing on beaches holding pieces of other people's lost things.

"Jonah," she said.

He looked up. The light caught the gray coming in at his temples—he was forty-three and it showed at his temples and around his eyes, ten years of a cell written on him in fine print—and his eyes were patient and questioning and so open that it hurt.

She kissed him.

Or he kissed her—afterward they would disagree about this, amiably, for years, each insisting the other had leaned in first, and the disagreement itself became one of the sweetest things they owned. What she remembered was the salt on his lips, and the sound of the tide going out behind them, long and low like a held breath finally released, and the way his hand came up to the side of her face as though she were something breakable, which she was, though not in the way he thought.

When they broke apart, the sun was nearly down and the gold road on the water had gone to copper, then ember, then ash.

"Well," Jonah said. His voice was rough. He laughed, a little helplessly, and pressed his forehead to hers. "Ten years I imagined getting out. And nobody told me about this part."

"Which part?"

"The part where it starts again." He drew back and looked at her, really looked, and there was something in his face she couldn't read—wonder braided with something older, warier, a man who had learned not to trust good news too quickly. "Everything," he said. "The whole world starting again."

They walked back along the darkening spit with their shoulders touching, and the lighthouse at the point swept its white arm across the water, once, twice, patient as it had been for a hundred and forty years, and Silas held the piece of sea glass in her fist the whole way home like a talisman, like evidence, like a promise she intended to keep.

* * *

But that was the thing about happiness, she thought later, lying awake in the blue dark of her bedroom with the window open and the tide talking to itself below: happiness made you careless. Happiness was a lamp lit in a window, and lamps could be seen from a long way off.

Because there was a second secret now. There had been one for six days, since the morning she'd checked the mail and found the envelope.

It had come the same way the others always came—cream paper, her name typed, no return address—but this one was different, and she had known it was different before she opened it, known it the way animals know weather. Inside was a single sheet, and on it, in the same neat typewriter font:

*Ms. Sandoval — I'm a freelance journalist working on a piece about wrongful convictions in New England. I understand you corresponded with Mr. Jonah Reyes during his incarceration. I'd very much like to talk with you. I'll be in Harbor Light through the end of the month. — R. Calloway*

She had stood at her mailbox at the end of the gravel drive with the gulls screaming overhead and read it three times, and each time the words rearranged themselves into something worse. *I understand you corresponded.* How? The letters had no name on them. Four hundred and eighty-one letters, unsigned, sent from a post office box under the initial S., and even the initial had been a gamble, a vanity she'd never been able to explain to herself—she could have signed them nothing at all, and instead she'd signed *S*, over and over, year after year, like a woman scratching a mark on the wall of her own cell.

Maybe that was it. Maybe someone at the prison had noticed. Mailroom clerks saw everything; guards talked; a man in a cell for a crime he didn't commit becomes famous within the system, and famous men's mail gets noticed. Maybe Calloway had pulled the file, seen the letters, traced the post office box, and found it closed two weeks after the exoneration—which was its own kind of confession, wasn't it? Who closes a box the week the prisoner walks free, unless the letters were never charity at all?

She should have burned them. She had thought about burning them, a hundred times, standing over the woodstove in winter with the bundle in her hands, and a hundred times she hadn't, because they were the only honest thing she had ever done and destroying them felt like a second silence.

Now they might surface anyway, dragged up by someone else's hand, and the story they would tell without her permission was a story she needed to tell herself, on her own terms, in her own words—or lose Jonah forever.

She had not answered Calloway. She had put the letter in the drawer of her desk, beneath the manuscript pages of her novel, beneath the photograph of her father, and told herself she had time. Through the end of the month, Calloway said. It was August 9th.

Six days ago. And she had spent those six days kissing Jonah on a sandbar at sunset and laughing over burnt fish and walking the tideline with their shoulders touching, and every hour of it had been the best hours of her life, and every hour of it had been built on top of the envelope in the drawer like a house built on a grave.

Tonight she got up and padded barefoot to the desk and opened the drawer and took out the letter and read it a fourth time, and this time she noticed what exhaustion and happiness had let her miss: the faint crease in the paper, as if it had been folded around something before it was mailed. She held it up to the lamp. Nothing there now. But folded around something, once. A photograph, maybe. A copy of a letter.

Her own handwriting, reproduced in some archive, coming back to her like a tide returning what it borrowed.

Down the coast, a foghorn sounded, one long note, the way it did when the fog was moving in. She looked out the window toward Jonah's cottage, where a single light still burned—he read late, he'd told her, because sleep had never come easily to a man who'd spent ten years listening to a cell block breathe—and she thought: *he trusts me. He kisses me with the mouth that read my letters aloud to an empty room, and he doesn't know, and if he learns it from a stranger's article instead of from me, I will lose him in a way that no apology will ever reach.*

The foghorn sounded again. Somewhere out in the dark, the fog was already sliding through the harbor mouth, soft and total, erasing the boats, the point, the lighthouse beam, everything.

In the morning, she decided. Tomorrow she would tell him. She put the letter back in the drawer, and lay down, and did not sleep, and outside the fog came on and on, and the light in the cottage next door burned until nearly three.

She did not know—could not have known—that three miles up the coast road, in a rented room above the hardware store, a woman named Ruth Calloway was sitting at a card table with a manila folder open in front of her, and that the folder contained a photocopied letter dated March 3rd, eleven years ago, postmarked Harbor Light, and that she had already made an appointment to see the district attorney on Monday morning.

Monday was four days away.
