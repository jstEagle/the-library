## 4. What Theo Kept

The glovebox of Theo Marchetti's car had held the same weight for ten years, and he had carried it up forty-one steps of a lighthouse without once thinking to put it down.

Now it sat on his knees in the keeper's room, wrapped in a wool scarf that smelled faintly of petrol and old winters, and Sebastian Brightwater was across from him with a candle between them, and Julian Ashe was somewhere above them on the lamp gallery, pretending to check storm shutters that did not need checking. The wind had found its voice again — a long, low note against the glass, like the sea clearing its throat before saying something unforgivable.

"You've been quiet," Sebastian said. "For you."

"I'm rationing myself. Julian got the first hour; I get the second. House rules."

"That's not a house rule. You made that up."

"I make excellent rules under duress." He turned the bundle over in his hands. The scarf was coming loose at one corner, and beneath it he could see the rubber band, stretched pale and tired, holding together what it had held for a decade. "Actually, there is something. I've been carrying it since Cornwall decided to bury us alive, and if the roof comes off tonight, I'd rather not have regrets rattling around in my pockets."

Her face did the thing it always did — the small, controlled stillness, the drawing-in, like a tide deciding whether to come in or go out. He had spent his twenties learning that face and his thirties failing to forget it.

"Is it a weapon?" she asked. "Because if you're going to kill me, I should warn you I'm very difficult to finish. Ask my editor."

"It's worse than a weapon."

He unwound the scarf.

* * *

There were thirty-one letters.

He knew the number the way other men knew their golf handicaps. Thirty-one letters written across ten years, posted to an address in London that stopped being hers after the third year, forwarded to a publisher, returned unopened — every single one — in envelopes that grew soft at the corners from handling, because he took them out sometimes. On birthdays. On the anniversary of the night she left. On ordinary Tuesdays, when the missing of her arrived without cause, like weather.

Sebastian looked at the bundle the way people look at a dog they're afraid has been mistreated.

"What are they?"

"They're yours." He set them on the table between them, and the candle flame leaned toward them as if it too wanted to read. "Or they were meant to be. Letter one, I wrote the week after you left. Letter two, the week after that. Then it became a discipline. Every month, whether I had anything to say or not. Especially when I didn't."

"Why would you—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Theo. They came back?"

"Every one. Unopened. Your forwarding address expired, your publisher's office either lost you or lied for you — I never found out which — and eventually I stopped looking for a new one and just kept writing to the last address I had. It felt less like giving up than... keeping the channel open. Like a frequency nobody answers but you broadcast anyway." He smiled, and hated how much effort the smile cost. "I know how it sounds. I've had ten years to hear how it sounds."

She didn't touch them. Her hands stayed flat on her knees, fingers pressed white, as though the letters were hot metal and contact meant admission.

"The night I left," she said slowly, "you told me—"

"I told you Julian had already chosen. Yes. I know what I told you." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, and let himself look at her properly — the grey threading her dark hair now, the lines around her mouth that hadn't been there at twenty-six, the way she still held her chin like someone braced against a wave. "And I believed it, Sebastian. That's the part I need you to understand. I didn't lie to you. I lied to myself first and then handed you the result like it was news. Julian told me he was going to ask you to choose him. I saw the letter he wrote you — I found it, I read it, I know, that makes me the villain in my own story — and I thought: she'll say yes, because Julian asks for things like he's already been granted them, and I will be the friend who steps aside gracefully, and everyone will call me noble, and I will die quietly inside for about forty years."

The wind hit the tower and the whole structure hummed, a low organ note through the stone. Neither of them moved.

"So I told you he'd chosen," Theo said, "because I couldn't bear to stand in front of you and lose out loud. And then you left before either of us could find out anything at all, and I have spent ten years wondering which of us you were running from."

"Both," she said, too fast, and then, wrecked by her own honesty, "Neither. I don't—"

"No." He raised a hand, gentle. "You don't have to answer tonight. That's the point of the letters. They don't ask anything. That was the rule I set myself — no questions, no demands, no guilt. Just... here's what happened today. Here's what I built. Here's a joke that would've made you laugh. Here's the fact that I still can't order coffee without thinking of that café in Falmouth where you put salt in your own hot chocolate by accident and drank it anyway out of sheer stubbornness."

Something moved across her face — almost a laugh, immediately grieved. "I remember the salt."

"Of course you do. It's the only thing we both remember identically. Everything else we remember differently, which is probably why we're in this mess."

* * *

He told her about the years in order, because the letters were in order and it seemed dishonest to skip.

The first year, he'd written from a rented flat in Bristol with a leaking ceiling and a job designing harbours — actual harbours, working coastlines, "which is what happens when a man tries to build his way toward a lighthouse without admitting why." The fifth year, he'd written from Lisbon, having taken a contract he hoped would burn her out of his head, and reported instead that the light off Cabo da Roca looked exactly like hers and that homesickness, he'd discovered, could be specific to a person rather than a place. The ninth year, the letters got shorter. Not from less love — he watched her realize this in real time, watched her flinch — but from practice. Grief, well-maintained, becomes efficient.

"Thirty-one," she said at last. Her voice had gone rough. "You wrote thirty-one letters and kept every rejection."

"I kept the rejections because throwing them away felt like agreeing with them."

"And you carried them here? Tonight? In your car? For ten years they've lived in a glovebox?"

"A glovebox, a desk drawer, a rucksack, a glovebox again. They travel with me. My sister calls it my ballast." He shrugged. "A man needs ballast. Otherwise he lists to one side forever."

Outside, the snow had thickened until the window showed nothing but white noise, the world beyond the glass erased. The candle guttered, steadied. Somewhere above them a door closed — Julian, descending, or pretending not to descend.

"Why now?" Sebastian asked. "Why tell me this tonight?"

"Because tomorrow the roads might clear, and we'll all drive away and rebuild our careful lives, and I will have missed my tenth annual opportunity to be honest with you." He tapped the bundle once, lightly, and withdrew his hand. "These are yours. All of them. Read them or burn them — I mean that. Burn them and I'll write letter thirty-two tomorrow, because apparently I'm incapable of stopping, but at least you'll have chosen the burning instead of the silence choosing for you. That's all I ever wanted. For something to actually be chosen. By anyone. About anything."

She was crying. Quietly, without drama, the way she did everything — tears that seemed furious at themselves for existing. She wiped them with the heel of her hand like an inconvenience.

"You absolute idiot," she said. "Ten years. Ten years, Theo."

"Yes."

"You could have found me. People find people. There are entire professions—"

"I did look. Year three, I hired someone. She found a Sebastian Brightwater who taught riding lessons in Yorkshire. Wrong woman. I took it as an omen, which was cowardice dressed as mysticism." He laughed, and it hurt on the way out. "Also — and this is the shameful heart of it — some part of me believed you'd left because you'd chosen him. So every returned envelope confirmed it. The silence wasn't empty to me, Sebastian. It was an answer. A very loud one."

"That's not—" She pressed her palms against her eyes. "That is not what happened. None of that is what happened."

"Then what did happen?"

And she looked at him — really looked, the way she must have looked at blank pages for ten years, hunting for the sentence that wouldn't come — and upstairs, deliberately loud, a floorboard creaked. Julian Ashe, who had been listening at the top of the stairs for God knew how long, announced his descent like a man walking into a room he intended to rearrange.

Sebastian stood so fast the candle nearly died.

"There's a tin box," she said, to neither of them, to both of them, her voice cracking down the middle. "In my grandfather's room. If we're doing this — if we're doing *this* tonight — then you may as well see what I kept."
