## 6. Whiteout

**Sebastian**

The storm stopped being weather and became a fact of geology sometime in the night. Sebastian woke to a silence so total it had texture, the kind of hush that meant the world outside had been erased. She went to the lantern room with her tea going cold in her hand and looked out at nothing — no horizon, no sea, no road. Just white, moving, alive, pressing against the glass like something that wanted in.

The coast road had closed at noon yesterday. The radio said what she already knew: drifts over the wall at Gull Point, visibility nil, travel not advised, then travel prohibited, then nothing at all but static and a woman's voice repeating emergency numbers as though numbers could reach them.

Three people. One lighthouse. A week's worth of food if she rationed it honestly, which she intended to do.

She found them both in the kitchen when she came down — Julian standing at the window with his arms crossed like a man auditing the storm for structural failures, Theo sitting at the table turning her grandfather's brass compass over in his fingers with an ease that made her want to snatch it back. They'd been there four days. It felt like four years. It felt like four minutes.

"Roads are gone," she said. "All of them."

Julian didn't turn around. "I know. I called the council line at six. Automated message." A pause. "I've been checking every hour."

"Of course you have," Theo said lightly, and set the compass down with a small click that sounded, in that kitchen, enormous.

Sebastian poured herself more tea she didn't want. This was the arithmetic she couldn't solve: two men, one storm, and her own heart locked in its tin box upstairs like contraband. Four days of rationed candles and careful sentences. Four days of Julian watching her the way he watched blueprints, and Theo watching her the way he watched the sea — as if she might change without warning and he'd need to be ready.

"You should both understand," she said, "that this could be weeks. The lane won't be plowed first. We're the last house on the earth, effectively."

"Good," Theo said.

She looked at him. He had the grace to look slightly ashamed of it, but only slightly.

"It is *not* good," she said. "It's a logistical problem."

"Everything's a logistical problem with you," Theo said, and there was no cruelty in it, which was somehow worse. "That's not an insult, Bash. It's a diagnosis."

* * *

**Julian**

By midday the wind turned and came at the tower from the north, and the whole structure sang — a low note in the ironwork that Julian felt in his back teeth. He'd been an architect for fifteen years; he knew buildings the way other men knew faces. The lighthouse was sound. It was everything inside it that was failing.

He found Theo in the lower gallery, wrapped in a blanket, watching the white world try to kill them.

"We should talk about it," Julian said.

"Should we?"

"Before it detonates on its own. It will. You know it will."

Theo smiled at the snow. "Four days of civil behavior, Ashe. That must have cost you."

"It cost me nothing. I'm not the one performing."

That landed. Julian watched it land — saw the smile flicker, saw the real face come up underneath like a fish rising. Ten years ago he would have pressed the advantage. Ten years ago he'd catalogued every one of Theo's softnesses and used them like load-bearing walls. He wasn't proud of the man he'd been at twenty-four, the man who'd treated love as a competition with rules he'd written himself.

But God help him, looking at Theo now — the returned letters, the decade of devotion worn like a scar he didn't hide — Julian felt something uglier than guilt rise in his throat anyway. Because Theo had *stayed*. Had written into the void for ten years while Julian had built a career and a marriage and a divorce and a life with no room in it for wanting anything he couldn't control. And some petty, freezing corner of Julian's heart insisted: *he got to keep loving her. I had to stop. Why does he get the moral high ground when I'm the one who did the harder thing?*

"Theo," he said quietly. "Whatever happens when this storm breaks—"

"Don't." Theo stood. The blanket slid off his shoulders. "Don't negotiate terms, Julian. Not with me. Not about her."

"I'm not negotiating. I'm telling you that when the roads open, we both leave, and whatever this is—" he gestured at the tower, the storm, the charged air between all three of them, "—ends politely."

"And if it doesn't end politely?"

"Then one of us makes it worse than it needs to be. And it's usually you."

Theo laughed — a short, sharp sound with no humor in it. "You always did think you were the reasonable one. The disciplined one. You know what your discipline is, Julian? It's just fear wearing a very good coat."

* * *

**Sebastian**

She heard them before she saw them — voices climbing through the stairwell, stripped of the careful civility they'd been rationing for four days. By the time she reached the base of the tower, they were already outside, already in the snow, and the sight of it was so absurd it took her a full second to process: two grown men, forty-something years old, standing chest-deep in a whiteout in the lee of the lighthouse wall, shouting at each other over a wind that ate half their words.

"—ten YEARS, Julian, you don't get to—"

"—I don't get to what? Want her? You had a monopoly on wanting? At least I never made my longing her problem—"

"You made your *indifference* her problem! You stood in that garden and let her believe—"

And then Julian shoved him.

It wasn't much of a shove. The snow stole most of it. But Theo came back up out of the drift with his collar full of white and his eyes lit like flares, and Sebastian was moving before she'd decided to move, boots punching through the crust, wind screaming sideways.

They were grappling — graceless, stupid, fists finding coats rather than faces, snow spraying — and she waded straight into the middle of it and put herself between them, hands flat on both their chests, and shouted with a voice she hadn't used since her grandfather died:

"*STOP.*"

The word cut through the wind because she threw it like a blade. They froze. Three people breathing hard in a white world, snow filling the tracks of their struggle almost as fast as they'd made them.

She pushed them apart — actually pushed, palms shoving, and they staggered back and let themselves be staggered.

"Look at yourselves," she said. "Look at this. Two intelligent men fighting over who *deserves* me. In a snowdrift. Like I'm a mooring neither of you wants to leave vacant."

"Bash—" Theo started.

"No. You don't get to *Bash* me right now." Her breath tore out of her in clouds. "I am not a prize. I am not a lighthouse you're both trying to dock at. I am a person standing in a storm, and the two of you have managed, in four days, to make me feel like territory. Do you have any idea how familiar that feels? How exactly like twenty-six years old it feels?"

The wind filled the silence. Neither of them spoke.

"Ten years ago I ran," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked, and she kept going anyway. "I ran because you two had turned my heart into a chess match and I couldn't tell anymore whether anything either of you felt was real or strategic. And now I stand here in the same storm, literally, and it's happening *again*, and the difference is I'm thirty-eight now and I am done. Done running, done being run after, done being the board you play on."

"Sebastian." Julian's voice was low, stripped of everything architectural. "We're sorry."

"I know you are. That's not the point." She wiped her face with the back of her glove — snow or something else, she didn't check. "The point is that I've spent ten years writing one sentence and never sending it, because sending it means admitting my heart isn't trainable. That it's inherited, like this tower. That it does what it does and I just keep the lamp lit. And I would rather freeze in this storm than spend another decade pretending otherwise."

Something shifted in all three of them at once. She felt it — the fury going out of the air and something rawer coming in behind it, dangerous and warm.

Theo sank down where he stood, right into the drift, sat in the snow like a boy, and started laughing — broken, wet-edged laughter. "One sentence," he said. "Ten years. God, Bash. I wrote you forty letters."

"I know," she said. "They're in your glovebox. I saw them the night you arrived, when you emptied your pockets."

His laugh stopped dead. "You *knew*?"

"I knew." Her voice gentled despite itself. "I've known for three days, Theo. I just couldn't—"

"She read mine too," Julian said quietly. "The manuscript. She put me in the book. Chapter nine. I'm the villain who calculates."

The wind screamed. Nobody moved.

And Sebastian stood between them in the ruins of the storm's noise and understood that the whiteout wasn't outside anymore. It was here, in the open space where all their secrets had been, and there was nothing left to hide behind.

"Inside," she said finally. "All three of us. Before we freeze. And tonight—" her heart slammed against the tin box in her chest, "—tonight we read them. All of them. Every letter. Out loud. No more rehearsed versions. No more fiction."

Theo looked up at her from the snow, eyes red-rimmed and terrified and hopeful all at once.

"Even yours?" he asked.

Sebastian looked at the lighthouse — her inheritance, her fortress, her ten-year sentence — and heard her grandfather's voice in the wind: *a keeper who won't light the lamp is just a landlord.*

"Even mine," she said.
