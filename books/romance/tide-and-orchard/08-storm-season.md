## 8. Storm Season

**Ada**

The storm arrived the way bad news does — first a rumor on the wind, then all at once, everywhere.

Ada watched it come in from her kitchen window while the kettle screamed itself hoarse behind her. The sea had turned the color of hammered pewter, and the gulls had gone silent, which she'd learned in ten years of island winters was the ocean's version of holding its breath. By noon the rain came sideways. By two, the power flickered and died, and the island folded itself inward like a hand closing.

She lit the old oil lamp she'd bought as a joke her first autumn here — *for atmosphere*, she'd told herself, though she'd never once needed it until now — and set it on the table beside her laptop, its battery down to eleven percent and falling. The screen glowed blue-white against the dark, and on it, half-finished, sat a letter that had taken her three days to begin:

*Dear Mr. Adeyemi,* it said. *Please accept this letter as formal notice of my withdrawal from all current and future projects—*

She deleted *Mr. Adeyemi* and typed *To whom it may concern* and hated herself for it. Even her resignation wanted to be anonymous.

The celebrity chef's lawyer had called twice before the lines went down. Both times Ada had let it ring through to voicemail, both times listening to the message without picking up: *We need an answer by Friday, Ms. Okafor. Either you're on the book or you're off it, and if you're off it, we'll need to discuss the non-disparagement language.* Friday was tomorrow. The ferry wasn't coming back this season after tonight's last crossing, which meant the choice would be made here, in the dark, with no bridge back to the mainland and no way to outrun it.

Ten years of other people's names on her work. Ten years of writing *I* in someone else's voice, of pouring her own grief into a footballer's redemption arc and her own mother's death into a chef's chapter about Sunday dinners, until she sometimes woke not knowing which memories were hers and which were inventory. And now Elias's memoir sat half-built on her hard drive beside the chef's, two books breathing in the same small machine, and she could keep only one — or neither.

Or, said a voice that sounded suspiciously like her sister's, you could stop hiding altogether.

Ada looked out the window. Through the rain-streaked glass she could see the neighboring cottage, one window lit amber against the storm. He was awake, then. Of course he was. She wondered what he did with his nights now that their sessions had stopped — whether he wrote, whether he reread, whether he thought of her with anger or something worse, something gentler that would be harder to survive.

She thought about the envelope he must have found. She'd thrown it away carelessly, carelessly being the one luxury she allowed herself, and now it sat somewhere in his house like a lit match. *Elias Mensah* in her handwriting, addressed to an address that hadn't existed in a decade. There was no innocent explanation. She'd stopped trying to build one days ago.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the wind took the roof of the woodshed and carried it somewhere into the dark like a giant plucking a leaf.

Ada opened a blank document. Not the resignation. Something else. Her fingers hovered, then moved, almost against her will:

*I have spent ten years teaching other people to say true things beautifully. This is me saying one badly.*

She stared at it. Eleven percent. Nine.

She kept typing anyway.

**Elias**

The notebooks covered the floor of his living room like fallen leaves.

Elias sat cross-legged among them, lamp low, rain hammering the roof so hard it sounded like applause from a very large, very indifferent audience. He had pulled every reply out of the box he'd carried across three moves and an ocean — every envelope postmarked from towns he'd never visited, every page written in that steady, unhurried hand that had once been the only fixed point in a winter that wanted him dead.

He'd read them all before, of course. In those first years he'd read them the way drowning men read anything: desperately, repeatedly, looking for rope. But tonight he read them differently. Tonight he read them like a writer.

It was the details that undid him.

He smoothed one open on his knee — the third letter, from February of that terrible year, when he'd written to the PO box asking how anyone survived a thing like this and expected nothing back. The reply had said: *You won't survive it the way you're trying to, all at once, like diving into cold water. You'll survive it the way tides do — a little at a time, going out further than you meant to and coming back shorter. Some mornings you'll only manage to notice that the light on the water is beautiful, and that will have to be enough, and some days it will be.*

He had underlined that passage years ago. Now he read it aloud to the empty room and heard something he should have heard a decade ago.

The light on the water. *This* water. He'd assumed she meant some sea she knew, some coast of her own. But he'd lived on this island eight months now, and there was only one way the light came off this channel in February — low and silver and slanting through the gap in the headlands, the exact view from the cottage next door.

He set the letter down. Picked up another. The fifth one, where he'd mentioned — obliquely, because he'd mentioned everything obliquely then — that his sister used to hum while she cooked, tunelessly, and that the silence in the kitchen was the worst part. The reply: *Leave the radio on even when you can't bear it. Silence isn't empty; it's just listening for her. Give it less room than you want to.*

His sister. A detail he'd never published, never spoken aloud except once, drunk, at a funeral reception, to people who'd forgotten it by morning. How had a stranger in a distant city known to answer that? How had she known about the orchard — *plant something in spring; grief needs somewhere to put its hands* — before he'd ever told her the orchard existed?

Because she hadn't been answering letters. He saw it now, the whole architecture of it rising out of the dark. She'd been answering *him*. Someone who knew him. Someone close enough to know the kitchen, the humming, the orchard — someone who had chosen distance anyway, who had loved him at arm's length for reasons he couldn't fathom, and who was now sitting thirty feet away through a wall of rain, transcribing his life back to him with her own words threaded through it like a hidden seam.

The woman who finished sentences. Who had completed that line in the orchard — *grief needs somewhere to put its hands* — before catching herself, eyes going wide as a startled bird. He'd told himself it was coincidence. Writers knew each other's rhythms; the phrase had circulated; any number of explanations.

There was one explanation.

Elias got up and stood at the window. The storm threw rain against the glass in fistfuls. Next door, the amber light still burned, and behind it a shadow moved — pacing, pausing, pacing. Writing, maybe. Or packing.

The last ferry. He'd seen the notice pinned at the dock that morning, the schedule amended in red ink: *FINAL CROSSING OF THE SEASON — 6:40 PM, WEATHER PERMITTING.* Weather was not permitting. The crossing might already be cancelled, or it might happen in the teeth of the gale, and either way tomorrow the island would hold exactly the people it held tonight, no more, no fewer, until spring.

He thought about letting it go. About the dignity of never asking, of preserving whatever strange, wounded thing they'd built from the wreckage of the interviews. He thought about the chapter she'd written under his name — the final one, the confession scene he'd read three times and wept over once, certain the voice in it belonged to someone else, someone who had once saved his life.

*Certain.* What a word. He'd built a decade on certainty and it had all been wrong.

The shadow next door stopped moving. Stood still. As if it, too, had arrived somewhere.

Elias went to the shelf and took down the original letter — the first one, the one returned undelivered, the one he'd carried to her door weeks ago meaning to ask and losing his nerve. He held it a long moment, feeling the paper's age, the ghost-weight of ten years inside it.

Then he pulled on his coat.

**Ada**

Eight percent. Seven.

The resignation letter had become something else entirely, and Ada let it. The words came up like groundwater, unstoppable, unsanctioned: about the chef, about the threats, about ten years of bylines that weren't hers. About a man on an island who had answered her letters without knowing it, whose handwriting she'd recognized through a cardboard box, whose orchard she'd weeded with her hands shaking. About a confession she'd buried in his final chapter because it was the only place she trusted herself to say it — under his name, where no one would look for her.

*If you are reading this, Elias, I wrote the letters. I have always written the letters. I am done writing them anonymously.*

Five percent. The lamp flame bent in a draft from the window seal, and outside the wind made a sound like the whole island exhaling.

Then, through the rain, she saw him.

A figure coming down the path between the cottages, coat whipping, one hand raised against the weather, the other clutching something white to his chest. Coming toward her door through the worst storm in a decade, as if the last ferry of the season were the least important thing leaving this island.

Ada stood up so fast her chair went over.

Four percent.

She went to the door and opened it before he could knock, and the wind rushed in carrying rain and salt and ten years of unsaid things, and Elias Mensah stood on her step holding the letter she'd thought was lost forever, and said, over the roar of it:

"I know it was you."
