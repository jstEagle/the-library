## 1. The Last Bouquet

The orchids arrived at nine o'clock precisely, forty-two of them, white as a held breath, and Rafael Ashgrove counted them twice because counting was the only part of the morning that still required her whole heart.

"Mrs. Whitcombe-Hale wants them cascading," said Priya, her assistant for six years now, holding the clipboard the way one holds a shield. "Not arranged. Cascading."

"They'll cascade." Rafael lifted one stem, turned it in the gray coastal light pouring through the tent's service entrance, and set it back in its bucket with the tenderness she no longer felt anywhere else. "Tell the florist I want the stems wired at an angle of thirty degrees. If they're vertical they'll look like a funeral spray by the time the vows are done."

Priya wrote it down. She always wrote everything down; Rafael had trained her to, in the years when Rafael herself had still needed notes, before the machinery of her own competence had grown so smooth and self-lubricating that she could run a two-hundred-guest wedding the way other people drove to the grocery store—on instinct, on rails, on a kind of sleep.

The Delacourt-Whitcombe-Hale wedding was the kind of wedding that appeared in the society pages under photographs of tents like white cathedrals pitched on lawns that sloped down toward the sea. The bride was twenty-six and lovely and had cried at her final fitting, which Rafael had noted without comment. The groom was thirty-four and had asked, during their planning session last spring, whether Rafael could "make sure nobody checks their phone during the ceremony," as though joy were a matter of signal jamming. She had assured him it would be handled. Everything would be handled. That was the entire architecture of her reputation: things, placed into Rafael Ashgrove's hands, did not fall.

By eleven the tent was up. By noon the chairs were aligned to a string line she had insisted on, because crooked rows were the first thing a grieving mother noticed and the last thing anyone forgave. By two the string quartet had been fed and placated after the cellist threatened to leave over a shade issue involving the canopy. By four Rafael had stood in the exact spot where the sun would strike the aisle at 5:40 p.m., closed her eyes, and confirmed what she already knew: perfect. It would be perfect. It was always perfect.

"You should eat something," Priya said, appearing at her elbow with a plate of the canapés meant for the cocktail hour. "You've had coffee since dawn."

"I'll eat at the reception."

"You said that at the Fenwick wedding. You ate one shrimp standing behind the ice sculpture and then you disappeared for forty minutes."

Rafael took a canapé to end the conversation. It tasted like nothing, which was not the canapé's fault. Nothing had tasted like much of anything for a long time now—not the sea air, not the champagne she sipped from clients' glasses when they pressed them on her, not even the particular salt-and-cedar smell of this stretch of coast that had once, she remembered distantly, the way one remembers a language studied in childhood, made her feel lucky just to breathe it.

She looked out past the tent flaps at the water. October light lay flat on it, silvered and patient. Somewhere down the shoreline, hidden behind the headland, the old seaside chapel stood in its chain-link fence with its demolition notice curling off the gate. She had driven past it that morning, as she did most mornings, and had not looked. She was very good at not looking. A decade of practice.

"Ms. Ashgrove?" One of the venue staff, a young man with a headset and the anxious brightness of someone delivering news he hoped wasn't his fault. "The bride's grandmother is asking for you. She says—" he consulted nothing, remembered everything, "—she says you're the only one who'll tell her the truth about the seating."

*The truth about the seating.* Rafael almost smiled. There had been a time when people brought her their truths—all of them, the whole tangled inventory of a family's grievances—and trusted her to sort them like silverware. Divorced parents who couldn't share a pew. Best men in love with bridesmaids. Grooms who got cold feet in parking lots and needed talking down by a woman in a headset who was not, technically, paid to do any such thing. She had been a priest of a religion with no god, only ritual, and she had been very, very good at it.

"Where is she?"

***

Grandmother Hale turned out to be installed in the front row, ten minutes before processional, gripping a program rolled into a baton and radiating the specific fury of the elderly and unheard.

"She's put my sister next to the band," the woman announced, without preamble. "Dolores is eighty-seven. She has a pacemaker. That trumpet section is going to stop her heart mid-hymn and everyone will remember this wedding as the one where old Dolores died."

"Then we move Dolores," Rafael said.

"It's not that simple. Beatrice did it *on purpose.*"

"Nothing is ever that simple," Rafael agreed, "which is why we don't have to make it simple. We just have to make it quiet." She scanned the seating chart in her head—the whole thing lived there now, laminated and permanent—and plucked two names from the third row, a childless couple who'd arrived late to every decision and would notice nothing. "Mr. and Mrs. Okafor move to the back near the band—they're hard of hearing and will love being close to the music. Dolores takes their seats. Beatrice sees a solved problem, not a defeat. Nobody dies. Everybody dances."

Grandmother Hale looked up at her for a long moment with wet, fierce eyes. "How do you do that?"

"Do what?"

"Make it look easy. My Harold used to say that about me. Said I made fifty years of marriage look easy, right up until the year it wasn't." Her grip tightened on the program. "It wasn't easy, you know. None of it. But we stayed. We stayed until staying was just what our hands did."

The quartet began tuning somewhere behind the hedge, a scatter of broken scales gathering itself toward order. Rafael heard herself say, gently, "I'll check on Dolores myself," and moved away down the row, straightening chair covers as she went, touching each one lightly, the way a doctor palpates a patient she already knows is fine.

*We stayed until staying was just what our hands did.*

She filed the sentence away the way she filed everything now—in the vast, well-lit warehouse of other people's marriages, where she kept thousands of such artifacts and used none of them. Other people's weddings were her museum. She walked its halls daily. She had stopped, some years ago, being able to remember why she'd started collecting.

The ceremony itself unfolded like a film she'd seen two hundred times but never starred in. The sun struck the aisle at 5:40 exactly. The bride did not cry, then did, beautifully, at the vowels of *I do*. The phones stayed dark—Rafael had stationed two discreet attendants at the tent mouth whose entire job was charm and confiscation. When the officiant said *what God has joined*, the wind came off the water and lifted the veil for one full second, a white flag, a sail, and four hundred guests made a sound like the sea itself approving, and Rafael stood at the back with her clipboard against her chest and felt—

Nothing. Not cynicism, even. Cynicism would have been something. Just a clean, professional absence, a room in her chest swept and prepared for a feeling that had checked out years ago and left no forwarding address.

She caught her own reflection in the glass of the tent door: a woman in a charcoal suit, immaculate, forty-one years old, holding a clipboard like a shield of her own. Behind her shoulder, reflected smaller, the bride turned in her groom's arms for the first dance, lit gold.

*Once,* Rafael thought, watching the small bright ghost of them turn and turn, *I knew what that felt like from the inside.*

She turned away before the memory could finish arriving.

***

The client came to her office on a Tuesday, which Rafael privately considered the worst possible day for hope—too far from Sunday's mercy, too far from Friday's escape. Her office occupied the ground floor of a converted boathouse above the harbor, all exposed beams and good light, designed a decade ago by a woman who had believed in love stories and decorated accordingly. Rafael had never redecorated. Taking down the framed photographs of her most beautiful weddings would have been an admission of something, and she preferred not to know of what.

Lena Marsh sat across the desk with her hands wrapped around a mug of tea she hadn't touched, and her fiancé, Dev, sat beside her wearing the expression of a man who had suggested this meeting and was already regretting it.

"We want something small," Lena said. "Fifty people, maybe. On the beach, if the permits allow. And we—" She glanced at Dev, gathered herself, and said the rest in one breath, like diving into cold water. "Everyone keeps telling us what our wedding should look like. Pinterest, his mother, the internet. And every option feels like a performance of a marriage instead of the start of one. So we found you, because people say you're the best, and we thought—if anyone could make it feel real, it would be you." Her eyes came up, hopeful and terrified. "We want you to make us believe again. In weddings. In ours. Is that—is that a thing you can do?"

The harbor light moved on the water behind her. Somewhere below, a gull argued with another gull about something neither would win.

Rafael heard herself begin the sentence she had begun ten thousand times—I'd be delighted to discuss—and stopped. It hung there, unfinished, in the boathouse quiet. Lena's face did something complicated. Dev sat forward slightly.

"I'm going to tell you the truth," Rafael said, "because you came here asking for real, and it would be a poor return to give you anything else." She laid her pen down parallel to the desk's edge, aligning it, because her hands wanted occupation. "I am very good at weddings. You've heard correctly. I can build you a day so beautiful your guests will speak of it for years. I can manage your families, your weather, your timelines, your griefs and grudges, and on the night itself I can stand in the dark with a headset and make magic look like logistics. What I cannot do—" and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, something quieter, "—is believe for you. Belief isn't a vendor. It doesn't take bookings. Whatever you lost faith in, you'll have to find it yourselves, or decide you don't need it, and both of those are honest choices. But I won't sell you a feeling I don't have in stock."

The silence that followed had texture. Dev reached over and took his fiancée's hand.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to us in eight months of planning," Lena said finally. Her voice wobbled and steadied. "Can I ask—did you used to? Believe?"

Rafael looked at the window, at the harbor, at the far gray line where the sea met a sky the color of unmarked paper.

"Once," she said. "A long time ago. Before I learned the difference between a wedding and a marriage is the difference between a photograph and a life." She rose, extended her hand, and produced, from the drawer that held her referral network, a card for a celebrant in the next town—a woman named Odile who did barefoot ceremonies and charged by the hour and believed, loudly, in everything. "She'll serve you better than I would. And Lena—for what it's worth, the fact that you asked the question means you're already halfway to the answer."

They left holding hands. Rafael watched them go down the lane, two small figures against the bright water, and stood a long while in her beautiful office with her hand still half-extended, as though the handshake had been with something else entirely—with the woman she might have been, if a certain June day ten years ago had gone differently. Then she squared the pen on the desk, squared the card drawer, squared herself, and locked up early for the first time in living memory.

***

Her house sat on the cliff road, a narrow cedar-shingled thing she'd bought with her first big success, back when success had still felt like a word worth saying aloud. She built a fire because the evenings had turned cold, made tea she let go tepid, and ended up—as she ended up perhaps four times a year, on the bad anniversaries she never marked on any calendar—kneeling at the bottom shelf of her bookcase, behind the novels she no longer read, where a tin box sat with a faded label reading POST in her own younger handwriting.

Inside: a handful of postcards, a ribbon, a dried sprig of something that had once been flowers. She didn't take those out. She only ever took out the one.

It was a library postcard, cream-colored, pre-printed with the seal of the Point Merrow Free Library and the words THE DEAD LETTERS DESK: WRITE TO A STRANGER. SOMEONE NEEDS TO HEAR FROM YOU. Someone had typed onto it, long ago, in letters slightly too large:

*The tide doesn't keep anything. That's its whole job—to carry things in and let them go again. I think people who live by the water learn this twice: once with their heads, and once, much later, with their chests. — J*

Beneath it, in different ink, in handwriting she had once known better than her own—slanted, quick, the *g*'s looping like waves breaking—someone had written a reply. Her own reply. Her own hand, ten years younger, confident, unhurt:

*Then I suppose the trick is learning to hold things loosely enough that the tide can take them without taking you. Working on it. — R*

She read it the way she always read it: fast, then slow, then word by word, hunting the paper for something new, some clause she'd missed, some evidence of who they had both been before. The fire ticked. Rain had started against the windows, soft and indifferent.

Ten years. Ten years since she had written that card and dropped it into the wooden box at the library's dead-letter desk, back when the program was new and she had been newly engaged and giddy enough to write to a stranger about tides. Ten years since the answer had come back signed only *J*, and she had written again, and again, a summer's worth of cards traded through the library's misfiling system like contraband tenderness—until June, until the chapel, until the forged contract and the ruined wedding and the name Julian Vane spoken in rooms she'd been escorted out of, and she had never gone back to the desk, never mailed another card, never learned whether *J* had waited at the box for a reply that the tide, in its whole job of carrying things in and letting them go, had simply carried away.

She had told herself for a decade that the correspondence had died of natural causes. Sitting on the floor with the fire dying and the rain coming harder off the water, Rafael admitted, in the privacy of her own swept-clean chest, that this had never quite been true. Letters didn't die of natural causes. They were abandoned. There was a difference, and the difference had a name, and she had spent ten years not saying it.

She put the card back in the tin. She did not put the tin back on the shelf.

Instead she carried it to the table, set it beneath the lamp, and stood looking down at it while the rain wrote its own long letter against the glass—until her eye caught, pinned to the corkboard by the window where she dumped mail she meant to deal with later, a flyer she'd been not-reading for a week. Cream-colored stock. The seal of the Point Merrow Free Library.

THE DEAD LETTERS DESK RETURNS, it said. RECONNECT WITH A STRANGER. CARDS AVAILABLE AT THE FRONT DESK THROUGH NOVEMBER.

Rafael stood very still. Outside, far down the dark coastline, past the headland, the old chapel would be black against the blacker sea, waiting under its curling notice for the machines to come.

She reached out and took the flyer down.
