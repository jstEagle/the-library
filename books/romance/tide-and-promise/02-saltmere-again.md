## 2. Saltmere, Again

The city let her go the way a tide lets go of a hull — reluctantly, with a long sucking drag she felt all the way down the coast road.

Odile had told herself the drive would take four hours and feel like nothing. She had packed the night before with the ruthless efficiency of a woman who had spent eleven years practicing departures: one suitcase, one garment bag, a box of files she told herself were for the shop's accounts and not, in any way, an escape kit. She had left a note for the cleaning service, forwarded her calls, watered nothing because there was nothing to water. Her apartment — *her* apartment, three years of mortgage statements proving it was hers — had watched her go with the blank patience of a place that had never quite believed she lived in it.

By the second hour the highway had thinned to two lanes, then to one, and the landscape began doing something to her chest she hadn't authorized. The pines came first, dark and close, their shadows striping the road like old film. Then the smell — salt and spruce and something mineral underneath, the cold stone breath of the coast itself — seeped through the vents of the car before she'd even rolled down a window, as if Saltmere Bay had sent its scent out ahead like a scout.

She rolled the window down anyway. The air that came in was September-soft, briny, threaded with woodsmoke from somewhere inland, and it undid her so thoroughly that she had to pull over at the overlook above Miller's Narrows just to breathe.

Below her, the bay opened out in that long crescent she had once known the way she knew her own handwriting: the gray-green water scalloped with white where the tide ran against the point, the harbor clustered at its elbow like a handful of toys spilled from a child's fist, the church spire, the water tower with its faded mural of a leaping salmon. Gulls hung motionless on the wind. A lobster boat crawled toward the moorings trailing its wake in a long silver seam.

It was beautiful. It was unbearable. It was exactly as she had left it, which was the cruelest thing about it, because she had not stayed exactly as she'd left it. She had become someone who wore heels to boardrooms and said things like *circle back* and *let's pressure-test that assumption*, someone whose hands were soft and whose sleep was shallow, and this place had no idea what to do with any of that.

"You're only going for the summer," she said aloud, to the windshield, to the gulls, to herself. Her voice sounded strange in the car, unused to declarations. "You're reopening the shop, you're sorting out Aunt Viv's estate, and you're coming home in October."

The words sat there, thin as tissue paper over a wound.

She drove on.

* * *

Saltmere Bay announced itself the way it always had — not with a sign but with a shift in the road's character, the pavement giving way to brick-paved Main Street, the trees opening to reveal the harbor in full view, sudden and blue-silver between the buildings. Odile came down the hill past the fire station — her stomach clenched and unclenched around that building like a fist — and turned onto Harbor Street at ten miles an hour, the way you do when you're afraid of what you'll see and more afraid of looking away.

What she saw was a town mid-metamorphosis, caught between two skins.

The Cannery Row end had been gentrified almost beyond recognition. Where the sardine plant had stood — the one that had employed half her graduating class's fathers — there was now a development called The Saltery, all cedar shingles and black-framed glass, a boutique hotel with a restaurant whose menu, visible through the window, offered *foraged coastal greens* and *day-boat scallops, 38*. A wine bar occupied the old chandlery. A gallery displayed driftwood sculptures priced with five digits. Tourists in expensive rain shells wandered the sidewalk with the unhurried confidence of people on vacation from lives larger than this one.

But the far end of Harbor Street, past the bookshop, still belonged to the other Saltmere. The bait shack was still there, listing slightly, its paint gone the color of driftwood. The net-drying racks still stood empty along the seawall. The Rusty Anchor tavern still had its hand-lettered sign and a Harley out front and a dog asleep in a patch of sun, and when Odile passed it she saw, through the window, men at the bar who might have been the fathers of her classmates, grown old in place, holding their coffee mugs like lifelines.

Two towns, stitched together down one street, pretending not to notice each other. She recognized the condition. She was living it herself.

Galloway Books sat at the hinge point between the two worlds, and God, it looked small.

She had remembered it as vast — the whole universe of her childhood, three rooms deep with ladders and rolling shelves and the smell of paper and paste, the green awning with the gold lettering, the window display she and Aunt Viv had built together every month like an altar. What stood in front of her now was a narrow storefront with a sagging awning, the gold letters flaked to ghosts — GAL OWA Y BOOK — and plywood screwed over the windows. Someone had chained the door. A realtor's flyer, sun-bleached to translucence, was taped beside the bell: *Prime Harborfront Commercial — Inquire Within.*

*Inquire within.* There was no within. There was only the shuttered shell of everything she had loved, wearing a FOR SALE sign like a hospital band.

Odile stood on the sidewalk with her keys in her hand and did not move for a long moment. A woman pushing a stroller gave her a polite, assessing look — tourist? buyer? — and moved on. Down the street, a man in a harbormaster's jacket stood talking to a fisherman by the fuel dock, and something about the set of his shoulders made her heart lurch stupidly before she registered that it wasn't him, couldn't be, was too tall or too broad or simply not Cormac Hale, and she hated herself for the checking, for the reflex, eleven years stale and still working.

She unlocked the chain with the padlock key from the estate folder — Aunt Viv's lawyer had mailed it with the rest of the documents, a small brass thing worn smooth — and the door swung open onto darkness and dust and the smell.

The smell stopped her on the threshold. Paper, yes, and dust, and the faint sweet rot of old glue — but underneath it all, still, unmistakably, the shop itself: cedar shelving, beeswax polish, the ghost of Aunt Viv's lavender hand cream. Eleven years closed up and the room still smelled like being eight years old, sitting cross-legged in the biography section reading about Amelia Earhart while the radiator ticked and her aunt's pen scratched in the margins of some poor paperback upstairs.

"Hello," Odile said softly, to the dark, feeling foolish and not caring. "I'm back."

No one answered. Dust motes turned in the light from the doorway. Somewhere in the back, water dripped — a slow, patient drip, the sound of a roof losing an argument with the weather.

She found the breaker box, flipped the main, and heard the old refrigerator in the back room shudder and groan awake like something raised from the deep. Half the track lights worked. The register — the ancient manual one, brass and glass, older than she was — sat under a sheet on the counter like a sleeping animal. Books still lined the shelves, thousands of them, spines faded unevenly where the sun had come through the window all those years, a tide line of light and shadow across the whole collection. Aunt Viv had never sold the inventory. Had never sold anything. The estate papers said the shop had been operating at a loss for six years before her death, propped up by savings that ran out eighteen months ago, and Odile understood suddenly, standing in the middle of it, that her aunt had kept every book because selling them would have meant admitting the story was over.

Odile had inherited a museum of a life that had ended without her permission.

She worked until the light went gold and then gray. She swept. She unscrewed the plywood from the windows and let the harbor back in. She found the leak — a cracked flashing over the poetry section, naturally — and put a bucket under it and wrote ROOF on the growing list on the back of the realtor's flyer. She carried the sheet off the register and stood looking at the till drawer, remembering the sound it made, the *cha-ching* that had been the soundtrack of her adolescence, and when she pulled the drawer open it stuck, and she laughed, actually laughed, alone in the dusty shop, at the sheer stubbornness of the object.

It was nearly six when the knock came at the door — two sharp raps, businesslike.

A man in his fifties stood on the step, silver-haired, tanned, wearing a fleece vest over a collared shirt and an expression of practiced warmth. He held a leather portfolio the way other men held fishing rods.

"Ms. Galloway? Dale Pruitt, Pruitt & Associates, coastal properties." He extended a hand across the threshold. "Your aunt's attorney mentioned you might be coming by. I have to say, it's wonderful to see these doors open. Wonderful."

Odile shook his hand. His grip was dry and firm and entirely without risk. "Mr. Pruitt."

"I'll be honest with you" — he beamed, entering without waiting to be asked, his shoes leaving clean prints in her dust — "when word got out that the Galloway property might come available, my phone rang off the hook. Off the hook. There are parties — serious parties — who see this stretch of Harbor Street as the last piece of the puzzle." He gestured at the shelves with an airy sweep, as though the books were scenery. "Of course, that was before anyone thought a Galloway would want it back."

"It's not for sale," Odile said. "I'm reopening it."

Something flickered behind the tan and the teeth — disappointment, quickly repainted as enthusiasm. "Reopening! Well. That's a commitment." He produced a card and laid it on the counter with two fingers, precisely, like a man dealing himself into a game. "I'll leave this anyway. No harm in knowing your options, is there? Because I'll tell you something for free, Ms. Galloway: the harborfront's changing fast. The consortium that did The Saltery has its eye on the whole east end. They buy, they build, values go up — everybody wins." His eyes traveled over the sagging awning, the bucket under the drip, the dust. "Or a person can hold out, and hold out, and watch the tide come in around her. Tide doesn't negotiate. That's the thing about tides."

"Thank you," Odile said. "I know how tides work. I grew up here."

"Did you?" He smiled again, wider, and she realized he didn't believe her — or rather, he believed it the way you believe a stranger's claim to a famous ancestor, technically possible, practically irrelevant. Whatever she had been, whatever Saltmere had been to her, it didn't register on his instruments. To him she was a checkbook with a local surname. "Well. Welcome back, either way. Door's always open." He tapped the card once and let himself out.

Odile stood in the middle of her ruined, beloved shop, listening to his footsteps fade down Harbor Street, and felt the shape of the summer rearrange itself around her. *The consortium.* She'd seen the name in the due diligence files her own firm circulated — Meridian Coastal Group, or one of its vehicles; the harborfront deals moved through her office like weather systems, and she had approved memos about this very peninsula without ever once thinking of the bait shack, the net racks, the green awning. She had read the parcel numbers. She had not read the town.

Her phone buzzed. Marcus, her managing partner, with the breezy cruelty of the truly busy: *How's the funeral tour? Don't get sentimental — Q4 planning starts Oct 1. We need you sharp.*

She typed back: *Just settling the estate. Back before you miss me.*

Then she stood there holding the phone, aware of how the lie fit her hand like a tool she'd used so long she'd forgotten it was a tool.

* * *

That night she slept in the flat above the shop, on an air mattress she'd brought in the trunk, under sheets that smelled of the cedar closet and eleven years of waiting. The flat was exactly as Aunt Viv had left it — mug in the sink, reading glasses folded on top of a paperback of *Jane Eyre* with the cover held on by tape, a cardigan over the chair by the window. Odile lay in the dark listening to the tide come in below the window, that long hush-and-hiss she had not heard since she was twenty-two, and her body knew it instantly, deeply, the way you know a language you spoke as a child, and she wept without drama, without decision, the tears simply arriving like weather, until she slept.

In the morning she signed the papers.

The lawyer's office was above the hardware store, in a room that smelled of toner and old carpet, and the young associate — a girl who could not have been born when Odile left — slid the lease across the desk and explained, kindly, the terms of the commercial renewal, the seasonal option, the harborfront zoning contingency. Odile read every clause. She was good at contracts; it was, in fact, the thing the city paid her extravagantly to be good at, and there was a bitter comedy in using those skills to bind herself to a leaking bookstore for the price of a dinner at The Saltery.

At the signature line she paused, pen hovering.

Here was the door, wide open, back onto her whole other life. Sign nothing, drive north, be in the office Monday, let the shop pass to the consortium and the past stay safely past. Nobody would blame her. Everybody would understand. It was the sensible thing, the professional thing, the thing the Odile Galloway of the last eleven years would do without breaking stride.

And here was the other thing: the smell of paper and lavender rising through the floorboards from the shop below, faint but insistent, like a voice calling up a stairwell.

She signed. *Odile E. Galloway.* Her hand wrote the name in the careful cursive she'd been taught at this town's little school, and watching it happen she felt like a stranger witnessing her own history from outside — a woman signing a document in a dead woman's office, in a town that had learned to live without her, claiming a right she had forfeited fair and square.

"There," the associate said brightly. "Welcome home."

Home. Odile gathered the copies, thanked her, and went down the stairs into the bright cold morning.

The harbor was full of light. On the dock below the seawall, a man in a harbormaster's jacket was coiling rope, and this time — closer, in daylight, with no time to look away — she knew him at once, the way you know a scar. Broader than he'd been at twenty-four, grayer at the temples, but the same economy of movement, the same way of setting his feet like a man who trusted the deck under him. Cormac Hale. Alive, solid, forty yards away, and utterly unaware of her.

He laughed at something the fisherman said, and the sound crossed the water and went through her like a harpoon.

Odile stood very still on the sidewalk with her lease papers pressed to her chest, telling herself the oldest and most useful lie of her life, the one she'd been rehearsing since the overlook:

*Only for the summer.*

Above the fuel dock, the harbormaster looked up — not at her, at the gulls wheeling over the moorings — and for one suspended second his face was turned full toward the shop, toward the naked windows, toward her standing in the doorway of her own history. Then he turned back to his rope, and Odile stepped inside and began, with shaking hands, to sweep the steps.
