## 4. First Words

The bell above the door had not rung in eleven years, and when it rang now — a bright, corroded jangle that Odile had polished herself just that morning with a rag and a stubbornness she couldn't explain to anyone, least of all herself — it sounded less like a welcome than an alarm.

She was on the stepladder, arms full of paperbacks, reshelving the last of the coastal mysteries her aunt had loved so indiscriminately, when the bell went off. She didn't look down right away. She knew who it would be before she saw him. She had known since yesterday, when she'd signed the lease at the harbormaster's office and watched the clerk's eyes flick toward the back room, where a man's voice had been low and clipped through frosted glass. She'd known the way you know weather is coming: pressure behind the ears, a taste like pennies.

"Shop's open," she said to the shelf in front of her, sliding *The Smuggler's Daughter* into its gap. "If you're looking for bait or diesel, that's two doors down."

"I know where the bait shop is."

His voice hadn't changed. That was the first cruelty of it. Eleven years should have roughened it, aged it, made it a stranger's voice, but it came up through the dusty light exactly as it had come across the dunes the summer they were both nineteen — low, unhurried, with that faint gravel underneath like sand in a shell. Odile's hands stilled on the books. She made herself finish slotting the last one, spine flush with its neighbors, because if she stopped moving she would have to turn around, and if she turned around she would have to look at him, and she was not ready. She would never be ready. Readiness was a luxury the past did not sell.

She descended the ladder anyway.

Cormac Hale stood in the middle of her aunt's shop with his hands jammed in the pockets of a canvas jacket, and he looked — God help her — he looked like the harbor itself, salt-bleached and storm-tested, broader through the shoulders than the boy she'd left, with lines carved deep around his mouth that hadn't been there at twenty-two. His hair was still dark but for a thread of gray at the temple, and his eyes were the same impossible color, that green-gray of the water over the shoals that could look gentle from a distance and kill you up close.

He was looking at her the way he used to look at incoming storms. Not with hatred. With calculation.

"You're shelving," he said.

"It's a bookshop."

"It's been boarded up for three years." He turned slowly, taking inventory the way he must take inventory of boats, of moorings, of damage after a blow. "Since your aunt died. The windows were papered over. Kids kept breaking in. Half the town figured the building would go the way of the cannery." His gaze came back to her and settled, heavy as an anchor. "Then you show up out of nowhere with a lease agreement and a paint scraper."

"The lease is legal. I checked."

"That's not what I asked."

"You didn't ask anything," Odile said. "You made a speech."

Something moved through his face — not quite a smile, more the ghost of one, the muscle memory of a boy who had once laughed easily and often, drowned somewhere along the way. "You always did talk like a lawyer."

"I am a lawyer."

"Yeah." He said it flatly, and the flatness carried everything: the scholarship, the city, the eleven years of silence, all of it folded into that single syllable like a letter into an envelope. "I heard."

*From whom? What else did they say?* She wanted to ask, and hated herself for wanting it. Instead she set the empty carton down on the counter and busied her hands with the tape gun, because Cormac Hale had always been able to read her face like large print, and she could not afford to be legible today.

He drifted along the front shelves, and she watched him do it — watched him trail one finger along the spines the way he used to trail it along the gunwale of his father's skiff, and the gesture hit her somewhere under the ribs, an old bruise pressed by an old hand. He stopped at the local history section, which she'd rebuilt from the boxes in the cellar: tide tables going back sixty years, a water-stained history of the herring fleet, her aunt's own slim volume of essays about the bay, self-published and never sold more than forty copies.

"Margaret would've liked this," he said quietly, and then seemed furious at himself for saying it.

"She would have hated the dust," Odile said. "Everything else she'd have tolerated."

"How long are you staying?"

"There it is." She set down the tape gun. "Day four, and the town sends its emissary."

"I'm not the town's emissary." He turned to face her fully, and the light from the front window caught him edge-on, gilding one side of his jaw and leaving the other in shadow, so that he seemed to be two men at once — the one she'd loved and the one she'd wronged, standing in the same body, neither willing to yield the ground. "I'm the harbormaster. This building sits on the harborfront. Anything that happens to it is my business."

"Is that in the charter? Bookshops?"

"Fires started in buildings like this one," he said, and the words dropped between them like a stone into still water.

Odile felt it go all the way down. She kept her face still — years of boardrooms and depositions had taught her that — but inside, something old and charred shifted and groaned, a beam settling in a burned house. "That fire was eleven years ago, Cormac."

"I know how long ago it was." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I was there. I pulled Mrs. Trelawny's cat off the roof of the chandlery at two in the morning while your family's whole life smoked behind me. So yes. I remember the date."

"I wasn't suggesting you'd forgotten."

"No?" He took a step closer. The shop was small; one step closed most of the distance, and suddenly she could smell him — salt and engine oil and something underneath that was only him, warm skin and cedar, unchanged, unbearable. "Because from where I stand, you forgot everything else. You forgot the town. You forgot your aunt sat alone in this shop every Christmas after you left. You forgot—" He stopped himself. Jaw working. She watched him swallow whatever the next thing was, watch it go down hard, and she knew — she *knew* — what it had been. *You forgot me.* He wouldn't say it. He'd rather chew glass.

"Cormac—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, and there was a scar across the palm she didn't recognize, white and ridged, a whole story she hadn't been told. "I didn't come here for an apology. I came to tell you that the harborfront association meets Thursday, and if you're reopening a business on this street, you'll want to be there. People have questions."

"People have questions," she repeated. "Or people have opinions."

"In Saltmere Bay those are the same thing." He moved toward the door, and relief and disappointment arrived in her chest simultaneously, twins, indistinguishable. His hand was on the bell rope when he paused, half-turned, not quite looking at her. "Why here, Odile? Of all the towns you could've bought a bookshop in. You've got a career. You've got a life that doesn't smell like fish. Why come back to the one place you ran from like the tide was chasing you?"

And there it was — the question, the real one, the one with teeth — and behind it, unspoken but loud as a foghorn, the question under the question: *why did you leave me?*

Her hand found the counter's edge behind her and gripped it. She thought of the parcel sitting in her apartment nine hundred miles away, the water-stained envelope with her own handwriting on it, the letter she had written at nineteen with shaking hands and never mailed, returned to her at last like a message in a bottle from a girl who no longer existed. She thought: *he doesn't know. He can't know. If he reads those words—*

"I needed a change," she said.

It was a terrible answer and she saw him receive it as such. Something in his face closed, a shutter drawn down over a window, and he nodded once, briskly, a man accepting a bad weather report.

"A change," he said. "Right."

The bell shrieked as he pushed the door — and shrieked again, differently, as a small body slipped through the gap before it could shut.

"Dad! You said you'd get me at the— oh."

A girl. Ten, maybe eleven, dark-haired, with a canvas satchel slung across her chest and a library book clutched against it like a shield. She skidded to a stop on the worn floorboards, looked up at her father, read his expression with the fluency of a child raised by a quiet man, and then — with the shameless pivot only children can execute — turned her attention entirely to Odile.

"You're opening the bookshop," the girl said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation of hope.

"I'm opening the bookshop," Odile confirmed.

"Are you Margaret Galloway's niece? Dad said someone bought the lease but he wouldn't say who." She shot her father a look of pure betrayal. "Mrs. Quill told everyone at the bake sale anyway. She says you're a big lawyer in the city."

"Nell." Cormac's voice had changed completely — gone soft, gone careful, a different instrument entirely. "We're leaving."

"But it's a *bookshop*, Dad. An actual one. The school library only has forty books per grade and half of them are missing their last chapters." She was already drifting toward the shelves, satchel swinging, and Odile watched her go with a sensation like vertigo — because the girl moved through the stacks the way Odile had moved through them at that age, trailing fingers, head tipped back, reading spines the way other children read faces. "Do you have *The Wreckers*? Everyone says it's too scary but it isn't, it's just true."

"It's in the back room," Odile heard herself say. "Still boxed. But I have *Moonfleet* on the shelf, and if you want scary-and-true, that one's older and meaner."

Nell spun around. Her whole face lit — and it was like watching a lamp come on in a window Odile had believed dark for years. "Dad, did you hear that? She knows books."

"I heard it." Cormac stood by the door with his arms crossed, and the look he gave Odile over his daughter's head was complicated in a way that hurt to decode: warning braided with something that might, in a better world, have been gratitude.

Nell found *Moonfleet* in under a minute, pulled it out, opened it to a random page, and began to read standing up, lips moving slightly, utterly transported. Odile knew that trance. She remembered being ten and discovering that a book could pick you up and carry you out of your own life, and she remembered, too, that the shop had been the only place that ever did it without a cover price.

"You can borrow it," Odile said. "Grand opening policy. First customer rides free."

"Really?" Nell hugged the book to her chest. Then, with the devastating directness of childhood: "Are you staying? Like, staying staying? Because the last person who leased this place lasted six weeks and then put in a smoothie bar, and nobody wanted a smoothie bar, we wanted books."

"Nell." Cormac again, sharper.

But Odile answered the girl, not him. "I don't know yet," she said honestly, and watched Nell absorb the honesty, weigh it, and apparently find it acceptable — a small nod, solemn as a treaty.

"Okay," Nell said. "But you should stay. This town needs stuff that isn't condos." She glanced up at her father with artless savagery. "Dad hates the condos."

"*Nell.*"

"What? You do."

Cormac pinched the bridge of his nose, and for one unguarded second he looked so much like the boy Odile had known — exasperated, outnumbered, secretly pleased — that her chest ached with it. Then he recovered, took his daughter's shoulder, and steered her toward the door.

"Thursday," he said to Odile. "Harborfront association. Seven o'clock, town hall." A beat. "Come or don't. But if you're going to be here, be *here*. Saltmere doesn't do halfway."

The bell rang them out. Through the window Odile watched them go down the street — the tall man and the small girl, the girl talking up at him, waving the book, him listening with his head tilted down toward her — and she stood in the dust-gold light of her aunt's shop with her heart doing something arrhythmic and unwelcome.

Because the pull was still there. That was the thing she'd flown nine hundred miles to escape and it had simply waited for her, patient as the tide, coiled in the smell of old paper and salt. One conversation and her body had already relearned him: the cadence of his anger, the softness he switched on for his daughter like a lamp, the way he'd almost smiled. Eleven years of building a life in the city, and Cormac Hale walked into a room for four minutes and the whole structure listed like a boat taking water.

"Be here," she muttered to the empty shop. "Easy for you to say."

On the shelf where Nell had pulled *Moonfleet*, a gap remained, a thin stripe of bare wood among the crowded spines. Odile straightened it absently, then stopped. There, wedged behind the neighboring books, was something that hadn't come from her boxes — a scrap of paper, folded once, tucked into the shelf's shadow. Old. Yellowed. In her aunt's handwriting, the looping script Odile knew better than her own:

*O — if you ever come back, look in the ledgers. Some things about the fire were never written down where people could see.*

Odile stood very still. Outside, the gulls cried over the harbor, and the tide, faithful and indifferent, began to turn.
