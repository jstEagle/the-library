## 14. The Fire, Reconsidered

The rain had been falling on Saltmere Bay for three days when Odile finally opened the flat file drawer at the back of the shop.

She told herself it was inventory. That was the word she'd been using for everything lately — *inventory* for the boxes of her aunt's papers she hadn't dared touch, *inventory* for the ledgers Mildred Galloway had kept in a hand so small and precise it looked like typesetting, *inventory* for the eleven years of correspondence her aunt had saved in bundles tied with butcher's twine. It was a good word. It sounded administrative. It sounded like a woman doing her job instead of a woman standing at the edge of something and hearing the ice groan beneath her.

But it was past eleven on a Thursday night, the shop was closed, the storm rattled the shutters Cormac had helped her repair, and there was no one left to perform for. Not even herself.

She made tea she didn't drink and carried the lamp to the back room, where the drawer waited under a film of dust like something sleeping.

Her aunt's handwriting was the first thing that undid her. Mildred Galloway had died two years ago, and Odile had grieved from a distance, efficiently, the way she did everything — flowers ordered online, a card signed by her whole department, a donation to a literacy charity in lieu of attendance because the trial schedule wouldn't bend. She had not come back for the funeral. She had told herself the shop was only a shop, that grief was a country you could visit by post. Now here was her aunt's hand again, slanted and certain, filling the margins of insurance documents Odile had never known existed, and the grief arrived all at once, retroactive and enormous, like water finding a crack.

The file was thick. The top layer was ordinary: policy renewals, premium notices, the mundane paper skeleton of a small business. But beneath those, in a folder labeled in her aunt's block capitals — *FIRE — DO NOT DISCARD* — the documents changed character.

The fire report itself came first. Saltmere Bay Volunteer Fire Department, incident number 0417. Eleven years old. The paper had gone soft and yellow at the edges, and someone — her aunt — had pressed so hard with her pen in the margins that the writing had embossed through to the next page.

Odile read the report twice. The first time as a stranger would: cause undetermined, electrical fault suspected in the wiring of the rear stockroom, no evidence of accelerant, no injuries. The second time she read it the way her aunt had clearly wanted someone to read it eventually — slowly, line by line, with a finger under each sentence and a cold feeling climbing the spine.

Because the report didn't add up, and her aunt had noticed, and her aunt had written it down.

*No evidence of accelerant* — but beside it, in Mildred's hand: *Fire started in stockroom where nothing electrical runs. I asked them about the junction box. There is no junction box on that wall. Never was.*

*Electrical fault suspected* — *Who suspected? Name is unsigned. Investigator listed as R. Calloway & Associates, Portland. Why is a Portland firm investigating a bay fire? Our own chief signed off as "concurring." He'd retired six weeks before this date.*

Odile sat down on the old office chair, the one with the split vinyl seat she'd sat in as a girl doing homework among the paperbacks, and read the name again. *R. Calloway & Associates.* A coincidence of surname. She remembered thinking, distantly, that the world was full of Calloways.

Then she turned to the next document, and stopped being able to breathe evenly.

It was a letterhead she knew better than her own signature. Ashworth & Vane. Her firm. The firm that had taken her in at twenty-three as a junior associate, that had paid for her bar exam study course, that had made her the woman who now negotiated nine-figure contracts over lunch. The letterhead was dated four months before the fire.

It was an engagement letter. *Re: Preliminary site assessment, Saltmere Bay harborfront parcels.* And there, in the list of parcels under consideration, parcel fourteen: Galloway Books, 12 Harbor Road.

Her aunt had circled it. Had drawn a line from the circle to the margin, and in the margin had written, in letters that dug deep enough to tear the page:

*They were looking at us before the fire. Four months before. Ask yourself why a city firm cares what a bookshop is worth.*

Odile's hands were steady. That was the strange part. She watched them turn the pages — the preliminary assessment, the comparable sales, the projections of harborfront value once the marina expanded — and her hands were perfectly steady, and somewhere underneath them her entire life was quietly coming apart.

She knew this assessment. Not this one specifically, but its species. She had written dozens of them. She knew the language, the careful hedging, the way a firm could make a piece of land look like a problem or an opportunity depending on which client was paying. She knew how a consortium worked: identify distressed assets, wait for distress to deepen, acquire cheap. She knew — God help her, she had *taught* juniors this — that the most efficient acquisition was not the one you negotiated but the one the seller was forced into.

Forced into by what?

By fire. By an insurance claim denied. By a business too broken to rebuild and a family too broke to fight.

She found the denial letter next. It was brief, brutal, and beautiful in the way corporate documents are beautiful when they are doing exactly what they were engineered to do: citing the investigator's finding of *undetermined cause*, invoking a clause about failure to maintain wiring, declining coverage on grounds of *pre-existing condition*. Pre-existing condition. As if the shop were a heart, as if the fire were a disease it had been carrying all along, waiting to happen.

And beneath the denial, the chain became visible, link by link, the way her aunt must have seen it, alone in this back room, year after year:

R. Calloway & Associates produced the investigation. Ashworth & Vane held the preliminary assessment showing the property's value depressed by damage. The insurer — whose outside counsel, Odile saw with a sensation like stepping off a stair that wasn't there, was also Ashworth & Vane — denied the claim. The Galloways, uninsured and unappealed, sold parcel fourteen within eight months to a holding company whose registered agent, she confirmed with trembling fingers and her laptop's dying battery, shared a suite in Portland with — she checked twice, three times — R. Calloway himself.

And the sale price. She did the math on the back of a bookmark, the way she used to do homework sums on the backs of library slips. The sale covered the debts. The sale funded the scholarship. The scholarship bought the train ticket, the dormitory, the law degree, the career, the apartment with the view, the woman she had become.

Her aunt had known. Not all of it — maybe not even most of it — but enough. Enough to keep a folder marked *DO NOT DISCARD*. Enough to write, on the last page, in a hand that had begun to shake:

*I am an old woman and they are a large firm and I have no proof, only arithmetic. But if anyone ever reads this: my niece did not abandon us. We sold her future to buy our own, and we let her believe she won it. Forgive us, Odile. Forgive me. The money was clean when it reached you. It was not clean before.*

Odile put the folder down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The rain filled the silence. Down the street, the harbor lights smeared in the wet dark, and beyond them the black water moved the way it always moved, indifferent, keeping its own accounts.

She thought: *I built everything on this.* The scholarship. The degree. The partnership track. Every case won, every contract signed, every dinner where colleagues said *Odile Galloway, self-made* — all of it resting on a fire that was lit, or allowed, or arranged, by the people who then handed her the proceeds and called it merit. She had spent eleven years believing she had escaped Saltmere Bay by outworking it. She had not escaped. She had been *spent*. She was the return on an investment made by men who burned a bookshop down and called the ashes a bargain.

And Cormac. Cormac, who had stood on the pier the night she left and asked her to stay, who had spent eleven years believing she chose a scholarship over him — and the scholarship was real, the choice was real, but the choice itself had been manufactured, priced, and executed by a firm whose letterhead she still carried in her bag. He had grieved her like a betrayal. It had been a transaction. Somehow that was worse. Somehow it made every cruel thing he'd ever said to her feel like a wound she'd earned.

She thought of Nell's margin notes in the copy of *The Secret of the Gull Cliffs*, tucked in the front cabinet, and had to press her fist against her mouth.

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere in the walls the old wiring ticked as it cooled, and she flinched at the sound — flinched at the sound of electricity in these walls, of all things — and then laughed, once, a hard small laugh with nothing in it.

There was a knock at the front door.

She went cold. Then she saw, through the glass, a figure under an umbrella, and the shape of the shoulders resolved, and her heart did the unwelcome thing it always did, lifting like a gull before it dove.

Cormac. At nearly midnight, in the rain, standing outside her shop with his hat dripping and his jaw set in the particular way it set when he had driven somewhere on anger alone.

She unlocked the door. Rain swept in around him; he stepped inside and stood dripping on the mat, and he looked at her face, whatever it showed, and something in his expression shifted from fury to alarm.

"Odile." His voice was rough. "I've been calling your cell. There's a man been asking questions at the Anchor all week — city type, nice coat, taking notes on the harborfront parcels. Tonight he asked about you by name. Asked whether the bookshop owner was *the* Odile Galloway of Ashworth & Vane."

The folder lay open on the desk behind her, her aunt's torn page facing the ceiling.

"He said," Cormac went on, and now his voice dropped, went quiet and dangerous, "that his firm was sending someone to discuss the harborfront project. Someone senior. Someone who'd be arriving Monday."

He searched her face.

"Odile. What's going on? What aren't you telling me?"

Behind her, the rain ticked against the dark windows, and the folder with her aunt's shaking handwriting lay open between them like a confession neither of them had read yet.
