## 6. Small Safe Steps

The package arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for inventory, for counting what was left, and Theo had gotten very good at counting.

She stood behind the counter of Salt & Rise with the box in her hands before she'd even opened it, because her hands already knew. The weight of it. The particular give of cardboard gone soft at one corner where something inside had pressed against it for years. She set down the box cutter she'd reached for and used her thumbnail instead, slow, the way you open a letter you're afraid of.

The recipe book smelled like smoke that had never fully left it. Charred at the edges, water-swollen in the middle from whatever hose or storm or grief had found it after the fire, but whole. Her mother's handwriting marched across the first page in that slanted, impatient cursive—*Honey buns, double the glaze, never listen to your father about the cinnamon*—and Theo sat down right there on the flour sack behind the counter because her legs decided the standing part was over.

Twelve years. Twelve years since the fire took the bakery on Harbor Street and her parents with it, and somewhere in those twelve years this book had walked out of the ruins and into the world and come back to her like a tide thing, deposited on her doorstep by no one.

No return address. No note. Just the book, wrapped in brown paper and twine tied in a knot a sailor would have approved of, and a single line typed on a strip of paper: *It seemed like it should be home.*

Theo read that line maybe forty times. She turned the paper over. She held it up to the window light looking for a watermark, a clue, anything. Nothing. Someone out there knew what this book was and had chosen not to be known, and she couldn't decide if that was mercy or cowardice, and then she hated herself a little for judging, because hadn't she spent twelve years doing exactly the same thing? Sending pieces of herself into the world unsigned?

Her pen pal's latest letter was still in her apron pocket. She'd read it four times that morning between customers, and each time the same sentence had snagged her like a nail in a sweater:

*You keep describing your life like it's a room you're sitting in. I want to know what you'd do if the door were open.*

She'd written back three drafts and thrown all three away. Now, with the recipe book open on her knees and her mother's voice rising off every page—*add the salt late, let it bite*—she pulled out fresh paper and wrote without stopping:

*I have a book that came back to me today. It belonged to my mother. I thought it was gone forever, and now it's on my lap smelling like 2009, and I don't know who sent it, and I think that might be the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me, and also the loneliest, because I can't thank them.*

*You asked what I'd do if the door were open. Here is the truth: I don't know where the door is. I've been in this room so long I've wallpapered over it.*

Then, because the book was open to the honey bun page and her hands wanted to do something truer than talking, she got up, tied her apron tight, and baked.

*

The dough came together the way it always does when you stop thinking—by feel, by smell, by the memory in the wrists. Her mother's recipe didn't survive translation into cups and grams; it survived in the hands. Theo proofed the buns in the warm corner by the oven and while they rose she did something she hadn't done in eleven months: she opened the manuscript file on the old laptop behind the register.

Forty pages. Forty pages of a novel about a lighthouse keeper named—she'd never been able to name her—who keeps a light burning for a ship that isn't coming. Theo had abandoned it at the point where the keeper has to decide whether the light matters if nobody sees it, because Theo had not known the answer, and she refused to write a lie.

Now she scrolled to the last written line—*The lamp turned in its long patience, asking nothing*—and beneath it, for the first time in nearly a year, she typed:

*But the keeper had begun to wonder whether patience was just fear wearing a uniform.*

She stared at it. It wasn't good. It might be true, though, and she'd learned the hard way that true comes first and good catches up later, if you feed it.

She wrote for two hours. Customers came and went; she sold out of sourdough by noon and didn't notice until someone knocked on the counter. When she finally surfaced, the honey buns had risen too high and collapsed slightly, and they were still the best thing she'd made in years, because they were hers, because they were her mother's, because both.

That night she wrote to her pen pal again, longer, braver:

*Today I wrote fiction for the first time in almost a year. One sentence. Then forty more. They're probably terrible. I don't care. Something came back to me today and I think it was me.*

*You said invisible isn't safe. I've been turning that over like a stone. Here's what I keep finding underneath: invisible is safe. Invisible is the whole point. Nobody can take from you what they can't see. I lost everything once, all at once, in one night, and I swore I'd never be findable again.*

*And yet I mailed you this letter. So maybe the door isn't locked. Maybe I just keep forgetting which way it opens.*

She signed it with the program's number—Keeper, they'd assigned her, which the librarian had clearly done with a straight face and private delight—and dropped it in the box outside the library after dark, hood up, like a criminal, like a woman smuggling her own heart across a border.

*

Jonah Reyes came into her bakery on Thursday morning, and he did not buy a honey bun.

He stood at the counter in his coat with flour dust still on his forearms—he'd come straight from his own ovens, she realized, and hated that she noticed—and laid a folder on the counter between them like a man laying down cards he already knew beat hers.

"Galloway." He nodded at the folder. "You should look at that."

She looked at him instead. Snow-melt on his shoulders. A tiredness around his eyes that she recognized from her own mirror and refused to feel sympathy for. "If this is about the market incident—"

"It's not about the market."

She opened the folder. Numbers. His numbers, neat as a confession: an offer to buy out her lease. A figure that was almost fair, which was worse than a lowball, because almost fair meant he'd done the math on what she needed, which meant he knew what she needed, which meant—

"You want my lease."

"I want you out of a bad situation," Jonah said, and his voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man reading terms aloud. "Whitlock is going to consolidate the units. You know that. One of us goes. If it's you, you go with money in your pocket and options. If it's me—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "This is a good offer, Galloway. It's generous."

"Generous." She tasted the word like spoiled milk.

"For your own good," he said, and something in the phrasing—the *for*, the *own*, the *good*, stacked like bricks—made her want to put her fist through the display case.

She closed the folder. Slid it back across the counter. Under her palms, the wood was warm from the ovens below, her grandmother's ovens, her mother's ovens, and she thought about the recipe book on the shelf behind her, smelling like smoke and coming home.

"My grandmother kept this counter through two depressions and one hurricane," Theo said quietly. "My mother died for the building it sits in. And you're standing here offering me charity like I'm a stray you're trying to rehome before winter."

"That's not—"

"You don't see me, Reyes. That's the problem. You see a failing lease. You see a problem to solve. You walk in here with your folder and your almost-fair number and you think you're being kind." She picked up the folder and handed it back to him, handle first, like returning a weapon. "Kindness would have asked me what I wanted."

His jaw worked. For a second—one unguarded second—something moved behind his eyes that looked almost like hurt, and she catalogued it coldly and moved on, because she was very good at moving on, she'd had practice.

"The offer stands," he said.

"The offer can stand out in the rain."

He left. The bell over the door shivered for a long time after it settled, the way bells do in cold air, and Theo stood with her hands flat on the counter breathing like she'd run somewhere, though she hadn't moved at all.

*

That night the lighthouse lamp turned its long patience above the harbor, automated now but still hers to check, hers to climb toward, and Theo sat in the keeper's room at the top with the recipe book and the laptop and wrote to her pen pal with shaking hands—not from cold.

*Someone offered to buy me out today. Not maliciously—that's the strange part. Coldly. Efficiently. Like I'm a leak he's patching. 'For your own good.' Do you know what that phrase does to a person? It takes your whole life and files it under PROBLEM.*

*Here's what he doesn't see: I'm not failing. I'm rebuilding. There's a difference and the difference is everything. Failing is falling down. Rebuilding is choosing, every morning, to lay another brick.*

*You said invisible isn't the same as safe. I'm starting to understand what you meant. Because I did everything right—I stayed small, I stayed quiet, I stayed behind my counter and my light and my unfinished book—and it didn't save me. He found me anyway. The world finds you anyway. So maybe safety was never the reward for invisibility. Maybe it was just the invoice.*

*So here's my answer to your question, the one about the door. Tomorrow I'm going to bake my mother's honey buns and sell them at full price and tell anyone who asks that they're my mother's recipe, out loud, in front of people. Small steps. Safe steps. But mine.*

*Tell me yours. What did you almost do today and didn't?*

She sealed the envelope. Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, and far below, the harbor lights burned in their long rows, and somewhere down there, presumably, Jonah Reyes was also awake, also writing, also keeping some small stubborn flame she knew nothing about.

She didn't post the letter right away. She held it a moment, weighing it, this new heavier self, then tucked it into her coat and started down the spiral stairs into the dark, taking the steps one at a time—small ones, safe ones, but downward, toward the town, toward morning, toward whatever was coming for her lease next.

Because Whitlock's consolidation notice would arrive eventually. She could feel it gathering offshore like weather.

And when it came, she intended to be standing in the doorway when it did.
