## 7. Two Kinds of Bread

**Theo**

The flyer went up on the harbor boardwalk on a Monday, in the librarian's unmistakable handwriting—loops like gulls, letters that leaned into the wind.

*HARBORLINE WINTER BAKE-OFF. Saturday, 2 p.m., Fisherman's Hall. Open to all. Judged by the Rotary. Proceeds to the roof fund.*

Theo read it twice, standing in the cold with a crate of flour balanced on her hip, and felt the trap close around her before she'd even agreed to walk into it.

Because she couldn't not enter. That was the thing about Harborline. A bake-off in a town with two bakeries was not a competition; it was a referendum. If she skipped it, the town would say Galloway's was scared. If she entered and lost, the town would say Galloway's was finished—which, given the lease, was a rumor she could not afford to have confirmed. And if Reyes entered—

Reyes would enter. Of course he would. The man had a jawline built for press photos and a father's name to redeem.

"You're doing it," said Dot Chen from the bait shop, appearing beside her the way Dot always did, materializing out of salt air and gossip. "I can see you doing it."

"I'm reading a flyer."

"You're doing it with your face." Dot peered at the entry list already taped beneath the announcement. Three names so far. *Jonah Reyes* sat at the top in confident block capitals. "He signed up first thing this morning. Walked down here at seven a.m. in the rain to put his name on a piece of paper."

"Maybe he wanted to be first."

"Maybe he wanted you to see it."

Theo shifted the flour crate higher. "Dot."

"I'm just saying. Seven a.m. In the rain."

"It's a bake-off, not a duel."

"Everything's a duel with you two," Dot said cheerfully, and wandered off toward her nets, leaving Theo standing in front of the flyer with her heart doing something inconvenient against her ribs.

She signed up that afternoon. Not because of him. Because of the roof fund, and the lease, and because Galloway's had won the harbor market's bread table eleven years running and she would not be the one who let it lapse.

That was the story she told herself, anyway, while she pulled her grandmother's cardamom knot recipe out of the tin box and laid it on the counter under the kitchen light. Her grandmother had won things with this dough. County fairs. A ribbon that still hung, faded rose-pink, above the lighthouse stairs.

The recipe called for patience Theo wasn't sure she had anymore: two rises, one long cold proof, hands that knew when the dough was ready without being told. She pressed her palms into the first batch at midnight and thought, absurdly, of the letter she'd mailed that morning. Her pen pal had asked what she was afraid of, and she'd written *nothing*, which was a lie, and then crossed it out and written *everything, but only in daylight*, which was closer.

Saturday, then. Daylight. Everything.

* * *

**Jonah**

Jonah practiced the bolillos four times that week, which his father found hilarious.

"Four times," Marisol Reyes said, leaning in the doorway of the apartment above the bakery, watching Jonah score dough at two in the morning. "For a town bake-off. What are you going to do when it's regionals?"

"There are no regionals, Papá."

"So it's worse than regionals." His father came in, tied an apron over his pajamas, and stood beside him at the bench—not helping, just present, the way he'd been since Jonah was small enough to stand on a milk crate to reach the counter. "You know why you're really doing this."

"To support the roof fund."

"To be seen beating her."

Jonah's scoring knife paused mid-stroke. "That's not—"

"It's fine." His father took a piece of dough, rolled it between his palms with the absent grace of fifty years, set it down. "But know what you're doing. A man who doesn't know why he competes loses to men who do."

Except Jonah was starting to suspect he didn't know why he competed. Or rather, he knew the reason he'd told himself—the lease, the landlord, the family name, the town that had watched them struggle and said nothing—and he knew it was true, and he also knew it wasn't the whole truth, because if it were, he wouldn't have spent Tuesday night writing to a stranger about his father's hands.

*Dear Lighthouse,* he'd written. (Her pen name; she'd chosen it, or the librarian had assigned it, and he tried not to read anything into the fact that it made his chest ache every time he wrote it.) *You asked what my father taught me that school couldn't. Here it is: how to tell when dough is done proofing by pressing one finger into it. If it springs back slow, it's ready. If it springs back fast, wait. If it doesn't spring back at all, you've waited too long and you start over. He says most of life is knowing the difference. I think he means more than dough when he says it, but we don't talk about that.*

He hadn't mentioned the bake-off. He didn't know why. Maybe because the letter-self and the bakery-self were different men, and the letter-self had no interest in winning anything.

* * *

**Theo**

Fisherman's Hall smelled like every winter fair of Theo's childhood: coffee urns, wet wool, cinnamon from someone's illegal contribution, the particular industrial tang of folding tables. Someone had strung lights along the stage. Someone else had arranged the judging table with a seriousness usually reserved for city council.

Contestants got a six-foot table, access to the hall's ancient ovens, and three hours. Theo arrived early enough to claim the oven nearest the wall—hers by right of seniority, she decided, having used it every bake-off since she was nineteen—and was arranging her station with military precision when the door banged open and Jonah Reyes walked in carrying a sheet pan of shaped dough covered in cloth, like a man bringing a sleeping child indoors.

Their eyes met across the hall. The temperature dropped four degrees.

"Galloway."

"Reyes."

"You're in my light."

"The sun is behind you."

"Then move."

She did not move. He set up beside her anyway, close enough that their elbows nearly touched when they worked, which was either territorial aggression or something neither of them wanted to name, and for the next hour they baked in a silence so loud that people began drifting over from the judging table just to watch it.

Because here was the thing about two people who hated each other properly: it looked, from the outside, like nothing else in the world.

It looked like the way Jonah reached past her for the salt and she tilted her head exactly out of his way without looking, a choreography neither of them had rehearsed. It looked like the way she caught her proving basket sliding off the table edge and his hand shot out and steadied it before hers arrived, and neither of them thanked the other, and both of them remembered it. It looked like the way they argued about oven temperature—"Four-twenty-five will split them." "They're supposed to split." "That's not splitting, that's structural failure"—in low, rapid voices, faces close, eyes locked, while the whole hall pretended to judge pies.

"They're at it again," Dot whispered loudly to the librarian.

"At what?"

"Whatever it is."

By two o'clock the tables were dressed: Theo's cardamom knots glazed and gleaming, arranged on her grandmother's blue-rimmed platter; Jonah's bolillos stacked in a basket lined with cloth his mother had sewn. The judges—three Rotary men and the school principal, who was the only one of them who'd ever baked anything—worked their way down the line.

The principal bit into a cardamom knot and closed her eyes. One of the Rotary men did the same with a bolillo, tore it open, and held it up to the light like a jeweler assessing a stone.

The deliberation took twenty minutes, which everyone understood to mean it was close.

"First place," announced the Rotary president, holding up a cardamom knot as though presenting evidence, "goes to Theo Galloway, Galloway's Bakery."

Applause. Theo stepped forward, accepted the ribbon—a real one, satin, the kind her grandmother used to win—and felt Jonah's eyes on her profile.

"And second place," the president continued, "to Jonah Reyes, Reyes Bakery. The judges note—" he squinted at the card, "—that the margin was, quote, 'one crumb.'"

Laughter rippled through the hall. And then someone—Dot, definitely Dot—called out, "Rematch next year!" and someone else called "Make it monthly!" and before Theo fully understood what was happening, the hall had decided, by acclamation, that the two of them were a fixture. An institution. *Those two.* The way people said it made her sound half of something.

Jonah leaned over as the crowd closed in, voice pitched under the noise. "One crumb."

"My grandmother's recipe."

"One crumb, Galloway. Enjoy it."

"I intend to."

And he smiled—not the cold smile from the market, something quicker, realer, gone before she could decide what it meant—and the crowd carried them apart, and Theo stood holding a satin ribbon and thinking about how her hand had shaken slightly when she'd taken it, and how it hadn't been from nerves.

* * *

**Jonah**

That night Jonah sat at the kitchen table above the bakery with the second-place certificate propped against the sugar jar where his father had installed it, and wrote to his pen pal.

*Dear Lighthouse,*

*I won second place today at a bake-off. You should know I'm competitive, so you understand the full catastrophe of what I'm about to admit: I'm not sure I minded losing.*

*Here's why. There's a woman—I've mentioned her, maybe, the rival—and today we were stationed side by side and the entire town watched us argue about oven temperatures like it was theater. Which it was. They loved it. At one point she caught her proving basket falling and I caught it instead and neither of us said thank you and I have been thinking about that basket for six hours.*

*Here's the part I keep circling. When they announced she'd won, my first feeling wasn't losing. It was pride. Like I'd watched someone do a thing perfectly and I got to be near it. And then I thought—that's a strange thing to feel about your competitor. And then I thought, is she my competitor? About bread, yes. About everything else, I genuinely don't know anymore.*

*My father says most of life is knowing when the dough is ready. I think I'm proofing. Don't tell anyone I wrote that.*

*—Anchor*

He sealed it before he could reread it. Some letters you had to mail the way you pulled bread from the oven—before you had time to talk yourself out of the shape of it.

* * *

**Theo**

The lighthouse was cold that night, the wind coming off the water in long shoves, and Theo climbed to the lamp room with her thermos and her letter box and wrote by lamplight with her coat on.

*Dear Anchor,*

*I won a bake-off today and I feel terrible about it, which makes no sense, so I'll explain.*

*There's a man. My rival. We were stationed next to each other and the whole town watched us like we were entertainment, and maybe we were, and I hate that, except during the actual baking I forgot anyone was watching, and it was—it was easy. Working next to him was easier than working alone. Do you understand how infuriating that is? To find that the person you're supposed to be fighting is the person you work best beside?*

*He caught my basket when it fell. He didn't make a thing of it. He just—caught it, like it was obvious, like of course he would. And then he said "one crumb" like an insult and smiled at me with a completely different face than his market face, and now I don't know which face is the real one, and I hate not knowing, because not knowing is how you get hurt.*

*Everyone in that hall thinks there's something between us. They were calling us "those two" like we're a weather pattern. And the worst part—the genuinely humiliating worst part—is that for one second, hearing them, I didn't want to correct them.*

*I'm telling you this because you're nobody. Because you don't know him, and you don't know me, and you can't look at me differently tomorrow across the market stall. Whatever you write back, I'll believe it, because you have no reason to lie to me. Isn't that a strange kind of intimacy? Being honest with someone precisely because they can't see you?*

*Sometimes I wonder if you're someone I pass every day. The librarian swears the matching is random. But this town is small. Statistically, you probably know my name.*

*Write back soon. Tell me something true.*

*—Lighthouse*

She sealed it, weighed it in her hand, and set it on the table by the door for morning.

Below her, through the lantern glass, Harborline lay dark except for two windows still lit—one above the bakery on Marsh Street, one above the bakery on the harbor road. Two people, miles apart by street and inches apart by everything else, each writing to a stranger about the other, each certain the stranger was safe precisely because the stranger was the other.

The tide came in. The light turned. And on the hall table of the library, in the sorting tray marked with a red star, two envelopes lay face-down, waiting for morning, addressed in two hands that the librarian—who had started this whole program, and matched the pairs herself, and was currently enjoying a cup of tea with an expression of profound satisfaction—recognized instantly.

She'd known since week one.

She'd said nothing.

But identities were due to be revealed at the winter festival, and the festival was three weeks away, and the librarian believed—with the serene confidence of a woman who had watched two people fall in love through her mail slot—that some things simply needed a deadline.
