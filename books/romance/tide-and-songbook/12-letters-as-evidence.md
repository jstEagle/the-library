## 12. Letters as Evidence

The supply order book lived on the shelf between the two bakeries' back doors, a fat ledger with a cracked spine that had outlived three landlords and at least one hurricane. It was the only piece of paperwork in Harborline that both Reyes and Nakamura touched without flinching—flour by the fifty-pound sack, yeast by the case, salt by the drum. My grandmother had started it decades ago, back when the two ovens on this street belonged to friends instead of rivals. There was a time, I'd been told, when the book was passed back and forth like a conversation.

By December it was mostly a weapon. Whoever ordered first set the delivery day, and whoever ordered second paid the split-delivery surcharge. Small arithmetic. The kind of arithmetic you learn to count in your sleep when your lease has four months left on it and every one of them is a Tuesday.

I went to the book on a gray morning because we were low on bread flour and I wanted to order before Jonah did. Petty? Absolutely. But the harbor market confrontation was two weeks behind us and the eviction papers were nine days old, and pettiness was the only muscle I still trusted.

The book fell open to the current page. And there it was.

Jonah's handwriting.

I knew it the way you know a voice through a wall—without wanting to, without trying. *Rye flour, 2 sacks. Butter, unsalted, 40 lbs. Sea salt — the good stuff, not the coarse.* A little joke to himself, probably. A note to the supplier. Ordinary.

Except.

I stood there with my finger hovering over the page and felt the morning tilt sideways.

*Not the coarse.* The letters leaned forward, eager, the *t*s crossed with a single impatient stroke that sometimes caught the next letter and dragged it along. The *g*s looped low and came back up like something surfacing. I had spent three months reading that handwriting twice a week by lamplight. I knew its moods. I knew how it slanted harder when he was angry—the letter about his father's hands, the one where the ink had nearly torn the paper—and how it softened, went almost shy, when he wrote about bread rising in the dark before dawn.

*Sea salt — the good stuff.*

My pen pal wrote *the good stuff* about everything. About the coffee at the harbor stand. About the librarian's terrible holiday decorations. About the way the light off the water looked at five a.m., which he could only know if he was up baking, which meant—

No. Stop. Half the town baked. Half the town drank bad coffee and complained about tinsel. Handwriting wasn't evidence. Handwriting was a coincidence wearing a trench coat.

But my hand was already turning back through the ledger, page by page, month by month, and there it was again and again: the forward lean, the impatient crosses, the *g*s coming up for air. An order from October. An order from September, written the same week my pen pal had told me he'd had a bad day and couldn't say why, and the handwriting on that entry was jagged enough to draw blood.

I closed the book. Opened it again. Closed it.

The kitchen behind me smelled like cardamom buns proofing for the afternoon, warm and patient, and I stood in the cold doorway between warmth and suspicion and did the thing I do best, the thing I have done since I was nineteen years old standing in the ashes of my parents' bakery: I made myself small and quiet and very, very still, and I let the arithmetic finish.

Jonah Reyes writes with the same hand as my pen pal.

There were exactly two explanations. One: coincidence, cruel and meaningless. Two: the man who served me eviction papers—who had offered to buy me out "for my own good" with a face like a closed door—had also spent an entire autumn telling me I was brave, telling me *invisible isn't the same as safe*, telling me things no stranger should know how to say.

And if it was the second one, then every letter was a lie with good posture. Every kindness was reconnaissance. He'd known about the novel. He'd known about the fire. He'd known about my mother's last phone call, the one I'd never told a living soul in Harborline—and if he was Jonah, then he'd known while he sat across from me at the bake-off judging table, while he stood in my doorway with a buyout offer, while he signed his name as witness on the papers that said *get out*.

He would have known everything. And I would have known nothing.

That was the part that made my hands go cold. Not the deception itself—people deceive each other constantly, cheerfully, in Harborline and everywhere else. It was the asymmetry. I had handed him the unfinished rooms of myself and he had walked through them holding a flashlight he never told me he had.

*

I didn't confront him. That surprised even me. I am, historically, a person who confronts—I confronted him at the market in front of half the town, after all, over a folding table of cinnamon rolls and wounded pride. Confrontation is loud and it resolves things, one way or another, and resolution is a luxury I've learned not to trust.

Instead I did something worse. I waited.

I waited three days, actually, during which I proofed dough and iced buns and answered Mrs. Okonkwo's questions about whether the cardamom recipe was for sale (it is not) and watched, through my front window, across the wet gray street, as Jonah Reyes carried sacks of flour into his bakery with his collar up against the wind. On the third day he smiled at me. Across the street, in the rain, no particular reason—a small, tired, real-looking smile, the kind you give someone whose day you've been quietly wondering about.

And I found myself wondering: does he smile like that at the page too? Did he write *you are not hard to read, Theo, whoever you are* with this same mouth?

Because here was the detail I hadn't let myself say yet, the detail sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone: whoever my pen pal was, he had called me Theo. Not in the letters—we were careful, no names—but once, in the third month, a slip. *You remind me of someone named Theo,* he'd written, and then, the next letter, *forget I said that.* I had thought it was sweet. A near-miss. Fate clearing its throat.

Now it sounded like a man who knew exactly who he was writing to and enjoyed the game.

I stopped writing on a Thursday.

I want to be honest about what that Thursday looked like, because from the outside it was nothing. I opened the bakery at six. I sold out of the cardamom buns by ten. I climbed the lighthouse path at dusk because the light doesn't care about my feelings—it gets lit or it doesn't, and twelve years of keeping it have taught me that showing up is the whole job. I sat in the lamp room with my thermos and my notebook and I wrote my pen pal's address on an envelope, and then I sat there until the seal glue dried unused, and I walked home in the dark.

Inside the envelope, unwritten, was everything. That was the problem. Once I suspected him, I couldn't write a single sentence that didn't have two meanings, didn't need to survive being read twice—once by the stranger I loved a little, and once by the rival who knew my name. Every *dear friend* became a test. Every confession became bait I might be laying for myself.

So I wrote nothing. And nothing, it turns out, is a very loud thing to send.

*

He noticed by the second week.

Of course he noticed. We had written every Sunday since October, rain or storm or bake-off humiliation, a rhythm so fixed that missing it felt like a missed heartbeat. I imagined him on that first silent Sunday—sitting wherever he sat, lamp or kitchen table, envelope ready—and then the next Sunday, checking the mail twice. Then going to the librarian, casual, asking if the program was still running, if maybe some letters got lost this time of year. Ruth Ann Delacroix can keep a face like a locked drawer, but she told me later—much later, when it was safe to laugh about it—that he came in three times in eleven days and bought a bookmark each time so the visits would look incidental. She had four bookmarks by the end. She said he held the fourth one like it was evidence in a trial he was losing.

I knew all this only in outline, then. What I knew directly was smaller and worse: the little signals. He started finding reasons to cross the street. Deliveries that could have gone to either bakery arriving at mine. A tin of his father's anise cookies left on my counter with a card that said only *For the recipe book. —J.R.*, in handwriting I now recognized the way you recognize a voice in the dark, and which I put in the drawer with the eviction papers because I could not decide which pile it belonged to.

Once—this is the one I keep returning to—he stood in my doorway in the late afternoon, snow starting behind him, and asked, not looking at me, fiddling with the strap of his bag: "Are you okay? You seem—" and then he stopped himself, visibly recalibrated, and finished with "—quiet lately," in a tone carefully stripped of anything.

"I'm fine," I said. "Busy."

"Right." He nodded. He looked at me for one second too long, and I watched him decide not to ask whatever the real question was, and he said, "The rye flour order comes Tuesday. Split delivery. I already called it in," and left before I could answer.

He'd called it in. Meaning he'd written in the book again, meaning his handwriting was sitting right there on the shared page like a confession he kept making and I kept refusing to read aloud. I went to the ledger after he left. At the bottom of the order, under the supplier notes, he'd added: *Tell N. the Tuesday slot works better for her oven schedule.* Tell N. He couldn't write my name. Or he wouldn't. Or writing it felt like stepping onto ice he'd already heard crack.

I stood in the doorway between our two kitchens holding the ledger and understood, with total clarity, that I could end all of it with one sentence. *Is your handwriting in this book the same hand that writes me letters?* Sixteen words. One question. Either he lied—which would tell me everything—or he told the truth, and then we'd be in the open air together, exposed and ridiculous and possibly fine.

I put the book back on the shelf.

Here is why I didn't ask, and I've turned it over enough times now to say it plainly: because if I asked and the answer was yes, I would have to grieve him while looking at him. And I wasn't strong enough for that. Grief needs a place to go, and my place, for twelve years, has been paper. If I confronted him, I'd lose the letters too—the one room in my life where I had ever said true things at full volume. Even poisoned letters, I thought, were better than no letters. Even a kindness that was strategy had taught me I could speak.

That was the lie I told myself, anyway. The truth is that silence isn't keeping a room; it's bricking up the door.

*

The last letter I ever wrote him—and I didn't know it was the last, which is the mercy of endings, they never announce themselves—was written the night before I stopped, and I never sent it. I found it weeks later tucked in the notebook, and reading it was like reading a letter from a woman I barely remembered being: hopeful, specific, reckless in the small ways that matter. She had written about the storm coming, about how the barometer had dropped so fast her ears popped on the tower stairs. She had written: *Something is different this winter and I think it might be me. I keep almost telling you things in daylight. There's a man I work against—you'd hate him, or you'd understand him completely, I honestly can't tell which—and lately when he looks at me I feel read. Isn't that a strange word for it? Read. Like I'm a page someone finally bothered to turn.*

*I think I might be falling for my own rival,* she had written, *and I think I might be falling for you, and I don't know what I'll do if those turn out to be the same mistake.*

She never sent it. I never sent it. And the version of me who wrote it—the one who believed being read might be a gift instead of a trap—she stayed in that notebook, sealed, unmailed, while the woman who actually walked around Harborline learned to keep her eyes down and her envelopes empty.

*

The silence devastated us both. I can say "us" now, though I couldn't say it then.

What I saw, then, was only my side: the empty Sundays, the bookmarks accumulating at the library, the way Jonah's smile across the street got more careful each week, then less frequent, then gone entirely, replaced by a flatness I recognized because I'd invented it. He stopped leaving things on my counter. He stopped finding deliveries. By the third week he'd gone back to the version of himself from the harbor market—all edges, jaw set, ordering his flour like a man building a wall one sack at a time.

What I didn't see—what I only pieced together much later, from Ruth Ann, from his father, from the letters themselves—was his side of it. How he reread every letter I'd sent, hunting for the sentence where he'd gone wrong. How he drafted replies and burned them in the sink, one after another, until his father asked why the kitchen smelled like matches. How he finally wrote one letter that said everything—*if I hurt you, tell me how, I'll fix it, I don't know what I did but I know it was something, the silence is killing me*—and then, at the last moment, understanding that any letter from him was now suspect, any apology could be read as strategy, he folded it into his coat pocket instead of an envelope and carried it around for days like a stone, waiting for a honesty that had nowhere safe to land.

Two people, one street apart, each holding a truth the other needed, each convinced that speaking it would cost more than silence. That's not a misunderstanding. That's a machine, and we built it together, and it ran beautifully.

On the twenty-third night of the silence, I climbed the tower and found myself doing something I hadn't done since the night after the eviction papers: I stood in the lamp room and imagined the light going dark. Not dramatically. Just—off. My thumb on the switch. The town below getting on with its lit windows and its lit streets, needing nothing from me, me needing nothing from anyone. It frightened me how easy the imagination was. How worn the path of it already was.

I went down the stairs two at a time, heart hammering, and checked the lamp twice, and stood outside in the cold wind watching it burn until my fingers went numb—as if I could prove something to myself about staying lit.

The next morning, Ruth Ann Delacroix appeared in my bakery before I'd even flipped the sign to OPEN, snow melting on her shoulders, wearing the expression of a woman carrying news she'd promised herself she wouldn't deliver and was delivering anyway.

"Theo," she said. "We need to talk about the festival."
