## 22. Keepers

The lighthouse went dark on a Tuesday in October, and nobody died.

Theo stood on the gallery deck with her hands in her coat pockets and watched the technician flip the switch that handed the light over to a machine—a sealed unit the size of a bread oven, self-rotating, self-monitoring, guaranteed for twenty years. It had taken him eleven minutes. Twelve years of her grandmother's logbooks, of climbing these stairs at all hours, of checking seals and polishing lenses and lying awake listening to the foghorn count out its patient arithmetic—all of it, retired in eleven minutes.

"You okay?" the technician asked. He was maybe twenty-five, with a thermos of coffee and the cheerful indifference of someone who did this forty times a season.

"I'm fine," Theo said, and found she meant it.

The new lamp clicked on at dusk, right on schedule, throwing its beam across the water without anyone's hand on it. It would burn whether she climbed the stairs or not. It would burn while she slept, while she baked, while she forgot about it entirely for whole weeks at a time.

Her grandmother would have hated it. Her grandmother would also have been the first one down the hill to see it, notebook already open, writing *Automated this day, and the light held* in her small sure hand.

Theo stayed until full dark anyway. Some things you don't rush just because they're finished.

* * *

The bakery opened at five-thirty now, which was Jonah's doing, because Jonah believed—had always believed, she'd learned, back when believing it nearly killed him—that the first ferry crowd deserved warm bread like anyone else. Theo had fought it for two months on principle. Then she'd tasted what his morning loaves looked like when he wasn't rushing them at seven, and surrendered without terms.

The sign above the door said **TIDE & SONGBOOK**, which had been her idea, and underneath it, smaller, in letters Jonah had painted himself: *Bread from two ovens since the winter we stopped pretending.*

The town had opinions about the name. Mrs. Okonkwo from the library board thought it sounded like a law firm. Dev from the chandlery thought it sounded like a band. But everyone agreed the bread was better than either bakery had managed alone, and everyone knew why, and nobody said so out loud anymore, which in Harborline was as close to tact as the town ever got.

Theo unlocked the door at five-fifteen and stood a moment in the cold, looking at the paper taped inside the glass.

It had been there eleven months now. Two hands, two inks. Jonah's blocky confident capitals: **OPEN UNTIL THE LAST TRAIN LEAVES—** and then hers, smaller, slanting, added the night after the festival when the ink on their shared lease was barely dry: **—AND AFTER.**

The last train of the season ran through Harborline at 9:40 p.m., mid-April through mid-November. In winter there were no trains at all; the line closed past the junction, and the town kept its own hours. So the sign was technically a lie for four months of the year, and neither of them had ever corrected it.

Some promises you keep by keeping the door open even when there's nothing to stay open against.

She flipped the sign to OPEN and went in to start the dough.

* * *

By seven the counter was three deep and running on the rhythm they'd built without ever discussing it: Jonah on the ovens and the savory side, Theo on pastry and the register, the space between them worn smooth as a tide pool. He didn't look up when she passed behind him anymore; he just shifted left, an inch, the way you make room for something you've decided is permanent.

"Mrs. Ferreira called," Jonah said, sliding a tray of rolls onto the rack. "Wants the seed loaf tomorrow. Also she wants to know if we're doing the festival buns again."

"We're doing the festival buns again."

"She said if we're not, she's organizing a petition."

"Tell her I'll personally deliver them to her door."

"See, that's why she likes you." He glanced over, flour to the elbow, hair escaping its tie. "That and the thing where you undercharge her."

"The thing where I charge her what seed costs in this economy."

"The thing," Jonah said, "where you undercharge her."

There was no heat in it. There hadn't been heat in any of their arguments for months—not since the night he'd read her letter standing on the empty platform, sleet in his hair, and understood that the woman who'd written it had already forgiven him more than he'd earned. Whatever anger survived between them now was the ordinary kind, the kind that lived in a house, the kind that made room.

The bell over the door rang. Dev came in for his usual, plus two croissants "for reasons I'm not discussing," and behind him a cluster of ferry workers who took their coffee standing at the window bar Jonah had built himself, badly, in February, and rebuilt in March, competently.

Theo worked the register and watched the town come through her door and felt the thing she still couldn't say casually, even to herself: *this is mine.* Ours. The lease hung framed by the kitchen door—both names, both signatures, the landlord's grudging third—and some mornings she caught herself reading it like a poem.

At eight-fifteen, between customers, Jonah set a plate beside her elbow. One roll, torn warm from the tray, butter already melting into it.

"Eat," he said. "You've been running on coffee since five."

"Pot, kettle."

"I eat. You witness me eating."

She ate. Outside, the harbor did its slow silver business, and the automated light on the headland swept its beam across the water in broad daylight, invisible, faithful, needing no one.

* * *

The librarian came at ten.

This was not unusual—Ruth Okonkwo took her coffee at ten every Tuesday and had, since the merger, appointed herself unofficial quality inspector of the almond croissants—but today she carried a canvas bag with the particular set of a woman carrying paperwork, and she had the expression of someone about to make it someone else's problem.

"Theo." She set the bag on the counter. "I need to talk to you about the pen-pal program."

"Renewal forms? I have them somewhere—"

"No." Ruth lowered her voice, which in Ruth's case meant only that she enunciated more clearly. "I need to talk to you about taking it over."

Theo wiped her hands on her apron. "Taking it over?"

"Retiring. End of the winter season, I'm handing the program to whoever wants it, and I've made a list of candidates, and your name is at the top because—" she ticked fingers— "you were in it, you benefited from it, you understand what it's actually for, and unlike the other candidates you will not use it to settle personal grudges." A pause. "Marcus Delgado is second on the list, but Marcus thinks anonymous correspondence is 'a gateway to catfishing,' so."

Theo laughed before she could stop herself. Then the laugh caught on something.

Because here was the truth she'd been circling for weeks, the way you circle a word you can't quite spell: she'd been thinking about it too. Not the paperwork—the *work*. The mechanics of pairing strangers, of choosing whose loneliness might recognize whose. All autumn she'd caught herself studying people at the market, at the counter, on the ferry ramp, wondering. *Who needs a letter. Who has something they can't say out loud. Who would write to a stranger what they'd never tell a friend.*

It was, she realized, exactly how her grandmother had looked at the harbor. Reading the water. Keeping the light.

"I'd want to change some things," Theo said slowly. "Not the anonymity—that stays, that's the whole engine of it. But the matching. I'd want to actually read the intake letters myself. Pair people on what they're afraid of, not what they list as hobbies."

Ruth smiled the smile of a fisherman watching a fish jump into the boat. "So that's a yes."

"That's a—" Theo exhaled. "Yes. Yes, I'll do it."

"Wonderful." Ruth produced the folder with the timing of a woman who had never once in her life entered a negotiation unprepared. "First order of business. This year's intake. Forty-one letters so far, and the season starts in three weeks, and I have read exactly none of them, because I am retiring, which I mentioned."

"Three *weeks*?"

"Mm." Ruth accepted her croissant, paid exact change, and paused at the door. "One piece of advice, keeper to keeper."

"I'm not a keeper anymore. It's automated."

"Nobody told the light that." Ruth nodded toward the headland, where the beam was sweeping its patient arc. "You'll see. Things keep whether or not anyone's hands are on them. That's not the same as being kept well." She pulled her scarf up. "Read the letters slowly. That's all. The pairing isn't the work. The reading is."

The bell chimed her out. Theo stood holding the folder, forty-one strangers' folded lives inside it, and felt the weight of it settle into her hands like something she'd been carrying for years and had only just been handed.

* * *

She read the first letter that night, after closing, at the kitchen table while Jonah did inventory aloud in the other room—flour counts, salt counts, the litany of their small empire—and she understood within two paragraphs what Ruth had meant.

The letter was from someone who signed only *M*, and it said: *I moved here in June and I know eleven people by name and none of them know anything true about me. I work at the ferry office. I am good at my job. I have not told anyone here that I used to paint, or that I stopped, or why.*

Theo read it twice. Set it down. Picked it up again.

Somewhere in the stack were thirty-nine other people saying versions of the same sentence in different handwriting. And somewhere in the stack, presumably, was the person M's words might land in—the stranger whose particular silence matched M's particular silence, the way a certain kind of loneliness had once matched hers to a rival baker with flour in his hair and a confession he couldn't finish.

She got up, found a pencil, and wrote in the margin of M's intake form, in her grandmother's habit of mind if not her hand: *Careful with this one. Wants to be seen, terrified of it. Don't pair with anyone too eager.*

Jonah appeared in the doorway, clipboard under his arm. "We're low on rye flour. Also you've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you get when you're solving somebody's problem they haven't given you yet." He came over, read upside-down over her shoulder the way he always did, and went quiet. Then: "Is that the pen-pal stuff?"

"Forty-one letters. Season starts in three weeks. I'm organizing it now, apparently."

"Apparently." He pulled out a chair, sat, and looked at the stack with an expression she'd learned to read the hard way—the look of a man revisiting a country he'd emigrated from. "You know what I'd do, if I were running it?"

"Screen for cowards?"

He laughed. "No. Well. Yes. But—" He tapped the folder. "I'd put a question on the intake form. One question. Something like—" he thought— "*What would you write if you knew no one would ever know it was you?* Because that's the real application. Everything else is demographics."

Theo looked at him.

A year ago that sentence would have been a knife. *If you knew no one would ever know it was you*—that had been the whole architecture of their almost-destruction, the shelter he'd hidden in, the distance that let him be honest at her expense. He'd served her eviction papers with one hand and written her tender anonymous letters with the other, and it had taken her a burned recipe book, a canceled train, and one long letter taped to a lighthouse door to understand that the cowardice and the courage had been fighting each other inside him the whole time.

Now he offered the question freely, as craft. As the thing he'd learned from having gotten it wrong.

"Add it," she said. "To next year's form. This year's already in."

"This year's forty-one are going to keep you up till Christmas."

"They might." She squared the stack. "That's sort of the job."

He leaned over and kissed her temple, flour transferring from his forearm to her shoulder, and went back to inventory, and the house settled around them into its familiar evening sounds—the oven cooling, the sea working the seawall, the light on the headland sweeping and sweeping, keeping watch over a town that no longer needed watching but slept better for it anyway.

* * *

On Sunday, Theo climbed the tower.

She still did this sometimes—not to check anything, there was nothing to check, but because her legs remembered the stairs and her eyes remembered the view and some inheritances you honor by repetition. Two hundred and six steps. At the top, the lamp room hummed its machine hum, and the lens turned its unhurried circle, and beyond the glass the whole coast lay out in November gray: the harbor, the town, the roof of the bakery with its new shingles, the rail line running north into the hills where the trains would stop running in another week.

Her grandmother had kept this light for thirty-one years. Before her, a series of men whose names filled the front of the logbook, and then, in the back pages, in different ink, the entries that mattered: weather, wrecks, weddings, the fire year, the year Theo's mother's voice went off the phone and never came back. *Light held*, her grandmother had written every single night of those years, including the nights it must have been a lie, including the nights the light was the only thing in the world still burning on schedule.

Theo took out her phone, considered, and instead took out the small notebook she'd started carrying in September—her own logbook, though she hadn't admitted that's what it was yet. She uncapped her pen.

*Automated since October,* she wrote. *Light holds without me. Bakery holds with both of us. Forty-one letters on my desk waiting to become forty-one pairs of strangers learning what Jonah and I learned the hard way: that you can be known by accident, but you can only be seen on purpose.*

She hesitated. Then, beneath it:

*Signing up for the season. First intake closes Friday. If you're lonely in Harborline this winter, write to the library and ask for the pen-pal program. Tell the truth. No one will know it's you—until, if you're lucky, someone does.*

She closed the book and stood a while longer, watching the beam go out across the water and come around again, patient as tide, faithful as bread.

Down the hill, the bakery lights were on. Through the glass, she could see Jonah moving between the cases, turning the sign, getting ready for Monday. On the door, in two hands, two inks, the promise held:

**OPEN UNTIL THE LAST TRAIN LEAVES—AND AFTER.**

Theo started down the stairs, two hundred and six steps, toward the light she'd chosen, the name she'd claimed, the home she'd signed for—and in her coat pocket, the first letter of the new season waited to be answered.
