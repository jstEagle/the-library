## 2. Waterlogged

The hurricane's name was Odette, and like most things that arrive with great fanfare and leave without apologizing, she took more than she gave back. She stripped shingles off the bait shop, drowned the marina parking lot to its second stripe, and turned the old post office on Cypress Lane into something between a swimming pool and a very sad aquarium.

Isandro Eastwick heard about it from Marisol, who heard it from her cousin Delfina, who heard it from the man who owned the sump pump, which on this island was how all news traveled: one mouth at a time, slightly damp.

"The roof failed," Marisol said, dropping into the chair across from Isandro's desk at the marine station, peeling a tangerine in one long unbroken spiral, which was a skill she refused to teach anyone out of pure spite. "Three feet of water inside. They're hauling out mail sacks that have been sitting down there since before you had crow's feet."

"I don't have crow's feet."

"You have the *beginnings* of crow's feet. Very tasteful ones. Anyway—" she flicked a segment of tangerine at her, "—they're distributing what they can salvage. Undelivered mail. Decades of it. Some of it's going back fifty years. Can you imagine? Some poor widow finally getting a birthday card from 1987."

Isandro made a noncommittal sound and kept her eyes on the nesting spreadsheet, where nest 14 had logged an emergence window of July ninth through eleventh and she intended to be there for every hour of it, because being somewhere for every hour of it was, in fact, her entire personality.

She did not think about the post office. That was the trick of it—there were whole rooms in her life she simply didn't enter anymore, and she'd gotten so good at not entering them that she sometimes forgot the doors were there.

* * *

The distribution happened on a Saturday, in the community center gymnasium, under fluorescent lights that buzzed like trapped insects. Folding tables in rows. Cardboard boxes labeled by decade. Volunteers in rubber gloves sorting water-stained envelopes into piles, calling out names like bingo numbers.

"Okafor! Any Okafor?"

"Boudreaux, two pieces!"

"Eastwick—is there an Eastwick? Isandro Eastwick?"

Isandro had come, she told herself, out of civic interest. The island's history lived in its paper, and paper was drowning, and someone ought to witness it. She told herself this while standing at the edge of the gym with her arms crossed, watching a volunteer hold up a wedding invitation from 1996 to general applause.

The volunteer—a teenager with a ponytail and the boundless cheer of someone who had never once read a spreadsheet—waved her over. "You're Isandro Eastwick? There's a piece for you. Well—for your mom, but it says care of you? It's weird." The girl frowned at it. "It's weird," she repeated, and handed it over.

Isandro knew it was weird before she touched it.

She knew it the way you know a voice through a wall—by its shape, its weight, the particular yellow of the envelope, which wasn't water damage at all but age, eleven years of age, the yellow of something that had been waiting in the dark and getting good at it.

Her own handwriting looked back up at her.

*Mrs. Rosa Eastwick,* the front said, in her younger self's careful cursive—the cursive she'd practiced in Lark's old notebooks, trying to make her letters as pretty as her sister's, never quite managing the tails. *Care of I. Eastwick, Pelican Road.*

She stood very still while the gymnasium went on around her, the bingo-call of names, the applause for the wedding invitation, somebody laughing near the punch table. Her hands held the envelope the way you hold a bird that might be dead or might just be stunned—you don't squeeze, you don't let go.

"You okay?" the teenager asked. "You look like you saw a ghost."

"I'm fine," Isandro said, which was the sentence she'd been practicing longest.

* * *

She didn't open it that day. Or that night. She put it in the drawer.

Not the drawer—that would have been too honest. A different drawer, the kitchen one, third from the top, where the takeout menus lived. She slid it beneath a stack of menus from restaurants that had closed years ago, which felt symbolically appropriate, and shut the drawer with the soft click of a decision deferred.

Then she made tea she didn't drink and sat at her kitchen table looking at the drawer.

Here is what Isandro knew about herself, known in the way you know the tide schedule: she was a woman who counted things. Turtles, nests, eggs, emergences, hatchlings lost to gulls, hatchlings lost to lights, hatchlings lost to nothing anyone could name. Counting was how she loved the world—by keeping track of it, by refusing to lose even one. Ten years on this island and she could tell you within four percent how many loggerhead crawls the beach had seen each summer, and she had never once, not once, added up the other column. The column with no numbers. The one that started with a rainy night and a road and her sister's name.

Lark.

The envelope sat three inches of pine and laminate away from her elbow, and it hummed.

That was the only word for it. Not literally—she was a scientist, she did not believe envelopes hummed—but there it was anyway, a frequency under the quiet, the way a storm offshore changes the sound of the waves before you can see anything wrong.

Because here was the thing she had not said aloud in eleven years, the thing she had buried so deep she'd nearly convinced herself it was sediment: she remembered writing that letter. She remembered the pen—blue, chewed, Lark's pen, God help her, she'd stolen it off her sister's desk the week before—and she remembered the kitchen table of the little rental on the mainland, and she remembered her mother propped up in the hospice bed asking her, in the last lucid week, *Did they ever find who did it, mija?* And Isandro had said *no*—had said it smoothly, had said it like a woman counting turtles—and then she had gone home and written the truth to the one person she owed it to, addressed it, stamped it, walked it to the mailbox, and stopped.

Stopped with her hand on the door of the blue collection box. Stopped because if her mother read it, her mother would die knowing her daughter had lied to her face for months. Stopped because the letter named no one, but it admitted everything else: *I saw him, Mama. I saw his face, and I said nothing.* Stopped because love, at twenty-three, had seemed like a load-bearing wall, and she could not yet imagine the house without it.

So she'd brought it home instead. And then her mother died, and the letter went into a box, and the box went into a closet, and the closet went into a storage unit, and the storage unit—when she moved to the island for good, when she built her small deliberate life out of tide charts and turtle counts—was cleared out, or she thought it was cleared out, or some version of her had paid a shipping company to send her mother's things to the island and then never opened any of it, and apparently, apparently, at some point in those fogged-over years, she had mailed it. Or tried to. Had stood in the old post office on Cypress Lane with the envelope in her hand and dropped it in the slot—or meant to drop it in the slot—or dropped it *near* the slot, into some purgatorial bin of misdirected mail, and walked away and let eleven years pass over it like water.

She couldn't remember which. That was the worst part. She had done the thing and then amputated the memory of doing it, and now the thing had washed back up on shore like everything does eventually, everything, the sea returns all debts—

"Okay," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the drawer, to nobody. "Okay."

She got up. She opened the drawer. She picked up the envelope, and her thumb found the flap, unsealed—she'd never sealed it either, of course she hadn't sealed it, sealing it would have meant finishing it—and she lifted the flap and saw the first line of her own handwriting, the blue ink faded to the color of shallow water:

*Mama, I need to tell you something and I'm afraid if I don't write it I'll spend my whole life—*

She closed the flap.

Her heart was going like a hatchling's pulse, fast and useless. She set the envelope on the table, sat down across from it, and regarded it across the expanse of wood like a colleague she suspected of fraud.

"Not tonight," she informed it.

The envelope did not argue. Envelopes never do; that's their whole strategy. They just wait. They are patient in a way people aren't, patient the way the sea is patient, and Isandro Eastwick, who had spent ten years learning the sea's patience from the inside, recognized the technique immediately and respected it and hated it.

* * *

Sunday morning she took it to the beach, which was either avoidance or confrontation depending on how you squinted, and honestly she'd stopped being able to tell the difference in her own behavior sometime around year six.

Dawn on the nesting beach was the one church she still attended. The light came up gray-gold over the water, the sand held the cool of the night, and she walked her survey line with the envelope zipped into the breast pocket of her field jacket, against her heart, which she told herself was for safekeeping and knew perfectly well was for something else.

Nest 9 showed a depression the evening before—a collapse in the sand above the egg chamber, the telltale sign. Emergence was close. She knelt and checked the tracks around it: ghost crab ladders, a raccoon investigation from two nights past, otherwise clean. Good sand. Good depth. Somewhere below her knees, ninety-some eggs that had been quietly becoming themselves in the dark for fifty-four days, and now they were ready, and when they came up they would all come up together, boiling out of the sand in a single mad rush toward the brightest horizon, because that was the deal hatchlings made with the world: you go at once, you go together, you go toward the light, and you don't stop to check whether the light is the moon or a porch lamp or a truck's high beams sweeping the road.

Isandro knelt there longer than the survey required.

"You wait fifty days," she said to the nest, conversationally, because talking to nests was normal and everyone on her team did it and also because Marisol wasn't there to raise an eyebrow. "Fifty days in the dark, and then one morning you just—go. All at once. You don't get to pick the morning."

The tide came in around her answer, which was no answer, which was the sea's whole communication style.

She thought about her mother's hands. That was what surfaced, kneeling in the sand—her mother's hands, thin at the end, spotted, turning a coffee cup in circles on the hospice blanket, always turning it, and her mother's voice, gentle even when it was asking the question that had teeth in it: *Did they ever find who did it, mija?*

And Isandro, twenty-three years old, holding her mother's hand, saying *no, Mama, not yet,* and watching her mother nod and turn the cup, turn the cup, believing her daughter the way mothers believe daughters, completely, all the way to the end.

The gulls started up somewhere down the beach, arguing over something. The sun cleared the dune line and lit the whole flat world gold, and Isandro stood, brushed the sand from her knees, and pressed her hand flat against the pocket where the letter rode.

Tonight, she told herself. Tonight I'll read it.

She had told herself that Friday, too.

* * *

Monday, Marisol cornered her in the sample room with the tangerine spiral again, which was how Isandro knew she was about to be interrogated, because Marisol peeled fruit when she was thinking and ate it when she was winning.

"You went to the mail thing Saturday," Marisol said. Not a question. On an island, everything is eventually not a question.

"I did. Civic duty."

"Mm." A segment of tangerine, offered. Isandro took it, which was a tactical error, because accepting food mid-interrogation established rapport. "Delfina says you got a piece. Old one. Addressed to your mama."

"Delfina should run the NSA."

"Delfina runs the church bulletin, which is the same job with better attendance." Marisol leaned against the counter, studying her with the frank brown gaze that had dismantled stronger defenses than Isandro's. "You've been weird since Saturday. Weirder. You reorganized the calipers. Nobody reorganizes the calipers, Isandro. The calipers live where they live."

"They were disorganized."

"They were *fine.*" Marisol popped a segment in her mouth and chewed with the deliberation of a woman buying time. Then, gentler, in the voice she saved for stranded animals and stubborn colleagues: "Whatever it is. You know you can—"

"It's nothing," Isandro said. "Old mail. Water damage. Probably a utility bill."

"Addressed to your dead mother in your own handwriting."

The sample room went very quiet. Somewhere a freezer compressor kicked on, humming its one note.

Isandro looked at her—at Marisol, five years her junior, sharp as a fishhook, loyal as tide—who had apparently heard the whole story from Delfina, who had heard it from the ponytailed volunteer, because the island gave you privacy the way Odette gave back shingles.

"A utility bill," Isandro said again, without conviction.

"Sure." Marisol nodded slowly. "A utility bill you carry around in your jacket pocket and talk to nests about."

"I don't—I was conducting a survey."

"Uh-huh." Marisol pushed off the counter and headed for the door, then paused, hand on the frame, and delivered the parting shot with terrible kindness: "You know, in my experience, the stuff we won't open isn't the stuff we're curious about. It's the stuff we already know." She tapped the doorframe twice. "Calipers, Isandro. Think about it."

* * *

That night the letter sat on her kitchen table under the good lamp, centered, squared to the table's edge, because if she was going to fail to open it she was going to fail neatly.

She made tea. Drank it. Made more. The house ticked and settled around her, the small sounds of a small life, and outside the window the Gulf breathed in and out, in and out, patient as correspondence.

She picked up the envelope. Put it down. Picked it up again and held it to the lamp, and through the thin old paper she could almost see the shadow of the pages folded inside—three pages, she remembered, three pages written in the handwriting she'd practiced in her sister's notebooks—and the shadow of them was somehow worse than the words themselves, the words being merely the shape of a thing she had carried for eleven years while telling herself she was carrying nothing at all.

Her thumb rested on the flap.

*Mama, I need to tell you something and I'm afraid if I don't write it I'll spend my whole life—*

Spend my whole life what? She knew. Of course she knew. She'd spent it. Eleven years of it, spent like coins from a jar, on tide charts and turtle counts and a life so deliberately small it fit in one drawer, third from the top, beneath the takeout menus.

But knowing the beginning wasn't the same as reading the ending. The girl who wrote those pages had been twenty-three and drowning and in love with the exact wrong mercy. What had she promised her mother in the end? What had she confessed, exactly, in the dark of that rented kitchen? Isandro found, to her genuine surprise, that she didn't know—not all of it. Memory had sanded the edges smooth. There were lines in that letter she had not read in eleven years, maybe lines she had never read at all, written by a version of her that no longer existed, and they were coming to her now the way everything comes to this island eventually: by water, by weather, by the slow patient return of whatever the current takes.

The clock on the stove read 11:47. Outside, far off, thunder muttered—Odette's leftovers, some remnant band dragging itself north, the season not finished with them yet.

Isandro set the letter down. Squared it to the table's edge. Turned off the lamp, and lay in the dark listening to the Gulf breathe, and told herself tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow, the way she'd been telling herself for eleven years, fluent in it, native-speaker fluent.

And in the morning, when she checked the emergence log, there it was in Marisol's blocky handwriting, circled twice:

**NEST 9 — EMERGENCE IMMINENT. FIRST BOIL EXPECTED TONIGHT OR TOMORROW PM. ALL HANDS.**

All hands. Even hers. Especially hers.

She stood in the doorway of the station with the letter in her jacket pocket and the whole bright day waiting, and she understood—with the clarity that comes only at the exact moment it's least useful—that she could keep deferring the letter forever, easily, the way she'd been doing it, one tomorrow at a time.

But the island had other ideas about buried things. The flood had already proven that: Cypress Lane fills, the doors give way, and everything you sent into the dark comes back to you, watermarked, yellowed, addressed in your own hand.

She just didn't know yet that the mail wasn't finished arriving.
