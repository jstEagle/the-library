## 6. Small Talk, Large Silences

Theo Vance had given a hundred presentations in his career—hotel developers in Dubai, county boards in three states, one memorably hostile crowd of oystermen in Apalachicola who had thrown shrimp at him. He could read a room the way other men read tide charts. So he knew, standing at the front of the commission chamber with his laser pointer and his renderings of Vance Point Marina, exactly when Isandro Eastwick walked in.

He felt it before he saw her. A change in the air pressure of the room, some old animal instinct that had nothing to do with business and everything to do with being nineteen years old and terrified on a wet road.

He didn't look up. That was the first discipline. He kept talking about dredge volumes and mooring capacity, kept the laser dot steady on the rendering, and only when he'd counted to thirty did he let his eyes travel the room naturally, the way a presenter's eyes do—and there she was.

Third row back. Hair pulled back the way she'd always worn it, a little looser now, sun-streaked at the ends. She'd gotten a crease between her brows that hadn't been there at nineteen, the kind that comes from squinting into weather for a living. She was wearing a canvas jacket over a faded T-shirt, and she looked—God help him—she looked like every summer he'd ever loved, folded up into a person and seated politely among the zoning public.

He did not drop the laser pointer. He considered it a professional triumph.

* * *

Afterward, in the hallway outside the chamber, he let the crowd carry him, shaking hands, answering questions about stormwater runoff, all while tracking her the way you track a bird you're afraid will fly off if you move too fast. She was talking to a woman with a clipboard—a colleague, maybe, someone from the marine station—and she was not looking at him. She was very deliberately not looking at him, which was its own kind of looking.

He knew what she'd seen when she'd recognized him. He knew because he'd spent eleven years imagining it: the flicker, the lockup, the whole body going still the way hers had gone still in the passenger seat of his father's Buick while rain hammered the roof and neither of them said anything true ever again.

He should have gone home after the hearing. That would have been the smart play, the lawyer-approved play. Let her sit with it. Let recognition do its slow work.

But Theo Vance had never once in his life taken the smart play when the stupid, honest-feeling one was standing right there wearing a canvas jacket.

"Ms. Eastwick." He crossed the hallway like a man who had just remembered something pleasant. "I don't think we've officially met. Theo Vance."

The pause before she took his hand lasted approximately one geological era.

"Isandro," she said. "I know who you are."

"You do?" He smiled the smile he used on county commissioners—warm, self-deprecating, slightly apologetic for being tall and well-dressed. "Small island."

"Very small."

Her hand was cool and calloused at the palm. Rope work, net work, ten years of hauling gear off boats. He catalogued that the way he used to catalogue everything about her—the chipped nail polish, the way she bit her lower lip when she was doing math in her head—and then made himself stop, because cataloguing her was how they'd ended up here, two adults performing a handshake like it was their first.

"I'm told you run the marine station," he said. "The survey work. I'll be honest, I've been dreading meeting you. I hear you're the reason half my permits are going to take twice as long."

Something moved behind her eyes. Not anger. Something older and more tired than anger.

"Environmental review isn't a delay," she said. "It's information."

"Spoken like a scientist."

"Spoken like someone whose job is telling people things they don't want to hear."

And there it was—that edge under the courtesy, thin as fishing line but sharp enough to draw blood if you pulled. He almost laughed out loud, not because anything was funny, but because eleven years ago this exact voice had said *Theo, get in the car, just get in the car,* and he had never stopped hearing it.

"Well," he said, "I hope we can be civil adversaries. Or adversarial civilians. Whichever sounds better in the minutes."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, a muscle twitch at the corner of her mouth, quickly arrested. But he caught it, and he carried it out of that building like contraband.

* * *

The problem with engineering a chance meeting, Theo discovered over the following week, was that it required an enormous amount of un-engineering. You couldn't simply show up at the marine station; that was stalking, or worse, sincerity. You needed infrastructure. Pretexts. A paper trail of coincidence so dense it looked like fate.

Fortunately, he worked for a development company, and development companies generated paperwork the way jellyfish generate spawn.

So: the marina's permit application required consultation with the station's ongoing sea turtle survey. Perfectly legitimate. He drafted a request for a site walk-through of the eastern shoreline, cc'd her director, cited the relevant section of the coastal code, and signed it with a flourish he immediately regretted, because flourishes were a thing he did when he was nervous, and he had been nervous since roughly 2014.

Then he waited four days—not three, three was eager; not five, five was a statement—to hear whether she'd respond herself or delegate him to some junior biologist with a clipboard and no history.

She responded herself. Two lines. *Thursday works. Meet at the station. Bring boots.*

No punctuation at the end. He read the email eleven times, which he was aware was insane, and then he went out and bought boots, because his were in storage in Jacksonville along with the rest of a life he'd been meaning to unpack for a decade.

* * *

Thursday morning arrived gray and mild, the Gulf lying flat and pewter-colored beyond the dune line. The marine station turned out to be a converted bait shop at the end of a shell road—corrugated roof, a hand-painted sign reading EASTWICK STATION, EST. 2015, a rack of kayaks listing gently toward ruin. There was a cat asleep on the welcome mat, orange and enormous, who opened one eye at Theo with the expression of a customs official.

"You must be the marina," the cat seemed to say.

Isandro came around the side of the building carrying two thermoses and a rolled-up chart, and Theo's heart did something embarrassing and adolescent that he chose not to examine.

"Coffee?" she said. "It's station coffee. Fair warning—it's been described as punitive."

"Punitive coffee is the only kind I trust."

"That," she said, handing him a cup, "is the most honest thing a developer has ever said to me."

They walked east along the tideline, her chart between them, and Theo performed normalcy the way he'd once performed confidence at his father's dinner table: fluently, and with total internal catastrophe.

"So the survey," he said, nodding at the beach. "Walk me through it. Pretend I'm smart but ignorant."

"That's usually accurate for your industry."

"Ouch."

"Sorry." She glanced sideways, and there it was again—the almost-smile, fighting for its life. "Habit. You're the fourth developer this year."

"What happened to the other three?"

"One cried. One tried to bribe Marisol with a boat. One actually listened and redesigned his project, and now he sends us a Christmas card."

"And which one am I going to be?"

She stopped walking. The question hung there between them, heavier than either of them had loaded it, and for a second—one long, salt-tinged second—he thought she might say something real. Her eyes came up to his face and stayed there, searching, and Theo understood with sick clarity that she wasn't looking at a developer at all. She was looking at a boy in the rain.

"The survey," she said finally, turning back to the chart, "runs May through October. Nesting season. We mark every crawl, cage every nest, count hatchlings at emergence. Your proposed channel cuts directly through the primary nesting beach—" her finger traced a line on the chart, and her voice went flat and professional, "—which means dredging during the season, lighting that would disorient hatchlings—they orient to the moon, not to floodlights—and boat traffic through the nearshore corridor where the juveniles feed."

"Okay," he said quietly. "Show me."

And she did. For two hours she walked him down the beach and showed him everything: a fresh crawl stitched across the sand like tire tracks from something ancient and determined; a caged nest with its little laminated tag; the wrack line where the ghost crabs stood sentry. She talked with her hands. She forgot, briefly, to be guarded, and when she forgot, she lit up like the station sign at dusk, and Theo stood in the sand holding a thermos he wasn't drinking and understood that he was in more trouble than any permit process could fix.

Because here was the thing he couldn't say: he hadn't come back for the marina. The marina was his father's last project, inherited like a debt, and Theo had taken it for one reason only, though he'd dressed the reason up in professional language even in his own head. He'd come back because Lark Eastwick had died on Route 12 eleven years ago, and Isandro Eastwick had stood in the rain beside him and lied to a state trooper to protect him, and he had driven away the next morning and kept driving for a decade, and there is only so far a man can drive before the road turns out to be a circle.

"There," Isandro said suddenly, pointing. "See that?"

He followed her finger. Down the beach, a dark shape was moving in the surf line—no, several shapes, small and frantic, tumbling in the wash.

"Hatchlings?" he said. "This time of day?"

"Washbacks." She was already moving, kicking off her sandals, shrugging out of her jacket. "Storm swell pushed them back ashore. They're exhausted—if they stay on the sand the gulls will get them." She looked back at him, and her expression was pure command, no performance left in it. "You wanted to see what we do. Start picking them up. Gently. Cup them in both hands."

Theo Vance, vice president of coastal development, heir to the Vance name and its considerable sins, knelt in the surf in his good jeans and began rescuing baby turtles with a woman who had every reason to hate him.

They worked without speaking. She carried a bucket from the station; he waded in to his knees and cupped the little swimmers out of the foam, one, two, five, each one no bigger than a silver dollar and beating its flippers against his palms like tiny furious hearts. Every time he brought one to the bucket, Isandro took it from him with both hands, careful as communion, and their fingers touched, and neither of them acknowledged it, and both of them noticed.

"This one's got a barnacle," he said, holding up a straggler.

"We'll clean him at the station. They rehab a few days and we release them at night." She peered into the bucket, counting. "Fourteen. Good haul." Then, grudgingly, like it cost her: "You're not bad at this."

"My mother raised me to rescue things," he said. "It was the one lesson that stuck."

She looked at him then—really looked, a long beat too long for small talk—and whatever she saw in his face made her turn away fast and busier than necessary with the bucket.

* * *

At the station, they washed the barnacle off the straggler in a shallow tub while the orange cat supervised from the windowsill. Isandro named the turtle, of all things, "Commissioner," and Theo laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and she looked startled by the sound, as if she'd forgotten he could make it.

"Thank you," she said, at the door, when the afternoon was spent and there was nothing left between them but the drive home. "For today. The hands-on part. Most of them send lawyers."

"Most of them are idiots."

"Most of them are busy." She hesitated, one hand on the doorframe, and he watched her decide something. "Theo. Why are you really here?"

The question landed like a dropped anchor. He could feel the truth sitting in his chest, eleven years heavy, pressing against his ribs: *because of you, because of her, because of the rain, because I have been running down a road shaped like a circle.* And he heard himself answer instead:

"For the project. It's a good project. I believe in it."

The lie was smooth. It was practiced. It was, he realized as he said it, the same lie he'd told the trooper on Route 12, polished by a decade of use, and watching her face he saw that she knew—knew the way she'd known things at nineteen, completely and without proof—and that she was going to let him tell it anyway.

"Right," she said softly. "The project."

She closed the door with a click that sounded, to his ears, exactly like a cell locking.

* * *

That night he sat on the rented cottage's porch steps with a beer he didn't drink and called up, for the thousandth time, the memory he'd been rationing like wartime bread: Route 12, rain coming down in ropes, the world lit strobe-white by lightning, and Lark's bicycle in the road, and Isandro's voice saying *get in the car* — and the terrible, holy silence afterward, two children holding one secret between them like something hot.

His phone buzzed. An email. From the marine station.

He opened it expecting a schedule, a follow-up, another round of professional courtesy.

Instead there was a single photograph: the bucket of hatchlings from that afternoon, Commissioner prominent in the foreground, and beneath it one line from Isandro:

*All fourteen released safely. They orient by the moon. Some things always find their way back.*

Theo set the phone face-down on the step and put his head in his hands, and above the Gulf the clouds were gathering, far offshore, in a slow dark spiral that the forecasters had not yet bothered to name.
