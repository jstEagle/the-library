## 16. Hatchlings in the Rain

The storm made landfall at 9:47 p.m., and by 10:15 the marine station's generator had surrendered with a sound like a man clearing his throat before a long apology.

Isandro worked by headlamp. She had taped the spare batteries to the underside of the supply shelf years ago, after Marisol lost a whole box of them to a drawer that ate things, and now she pried the tape loose with numb fingers and told herself the shaking was from cold. It was not entirely from cold.

Ophelia had been supposed to veer east. Ophelia had been supposed to be a nothing storm, a rain event, a footnote. Instead it had sat offshore all afternoon like a cat deciding whether to knock something off a table, and then it had turned, and now the island was taking the worst of it sideways, and somewhere out on the eastern spit, a nest laid sixty-two days ago was either fine or drowning.

She had checked the tide charts three times. King tide plus storm surge plus a nest that sat lower than she liked even in calm weather. The math was ugly. The math said water over the nest by midnight, maybe sooner, and loggerhead eggs can take a soaking but not a drowning, not six hours of it, not when they're this close to pip.

Marisol had left at noon with the evacuation order, taking the interns and the truck and a great deal of loud opinion about Isandro's judgment. "It's eggs," Marisol had said, standing in the doorway with her duffel bag, "not children," and Isandro had said, "I know what they are," and Marisol had looked at her for a long moment and said, softer, "That's exactly my worry," and gone.

So: alone, except for the storm, except for the thing she was not thinking about, which was standing in her kitchen at eight o'clock soaked through, saying *We need to talk about Lark*, and her saying *Not tonight*, and him nodding like a man who had waited eleven years and could wait one more night, which was either the most decent thing she'd ever seen or the most strategic, and she genuinely could not tell, and did not want to find out in the middle of a hurricane.

The radio crackled. She'd left it on the NOAA channel for company. The voice read coordinates and wind speeds in its flat, tireless way, and Isandro pulled on her rain jacket, filled a dry bag with gloves and towels and the little foam coolers they used for relocations, and went out into the dark.

* * *

The beach at night in a tropical storm is not a place. It's an argument the ocean is having with the land, loudly, and you are a comma in the transcript.

Isandro walked the high berm with her head down and her light off—light disorients hatchlings, and anyway the wind would have eaten the beam—and counted her steps the way she always did, because counting was the oldest trick she had, the first wall she ever built. Two hundred and ten steps to the marker post. The post was gone. Not broken—gone, swallowed, along with four feet of dune and the ghost crab burrows and the sea oats she'd watched turn gold in September.

The nest site was under water.

Not ankle-deep. Under. Waves rolling clean over the spot where a female had labored at 2 a.m. in June while Isandro crouched in the dark taking notes, loving her a little, the way she loved every one of them, the way she never let herself love anything else that couldn't be counted.

She stood in the surge to her knees and felt for it with her feet, which was stupid, because the sand beneath had already gone to liquid, and then a wave shoved her hard enough that she sat down in the surf like a toddler, and that was when the other headlamp came on.

She hadn't heard him. You couldn't hear anything but the storm. But there he was, ten feet away, chest-deep in his own yellow circle of light, holding a shovel and a dry bag, water streaming off him, and he didn't say hello or *how did you find me* or *you shouldn't be out here*. He just waded closer and shouted, close to her ear, over the wind:

"Which end's the deep end?"

And God help her, some hysterical part of her wanted to laugh. Because it was a real question—the nest sloped, they both knew it sloped, she'd measured it in June—and because he asked it like they were two people who had done this together a hundred times, like the last eleven years were a commercial break.

She pointed. He nodded. They started digging.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about emergency nest relocation: it is not delicate. The brochures show gloved hands cradling eggs like communion wafers. In practice, at night, in a storm, it is two people on their knees in a hole, scooping wet sand with their hands because shovels are too blunt, passing eggs back blind, trusting the other person's grip.

Theo dug the first chamber wrong and she corrected him with a shove of her shoulder and a gesture, no words possible, and he redug it without complaint, and something about that—about being corrected and simply *correcting*, no flinch, no argument—cracked something open in her chest that she immediately pretended hadn't happened.

They worked by feel. His hand would come into her light and she'd pass him an egg, and somewhere around the thirtieth egg she stopped flinching when their fingers touched, and somewhere around the fortieth she realized she had stopped, and hated herself a little, and kept working.

Forty-one eggs. Forty-two. The hole was filling with seawater faster than they could dig, so they moved fast, sloppy by protocol, perfect by necessity. Fifty-three. Fifty-eight. At sixty-one her fingers found something soft and wrong, and she lifted it into the light: a pipped egg, hatching mid-catastrophe, a tiny face pressed against the membrane, furious, ancient, alive.

She held it up so he could see. Theo leaned in, rain hammering his shoulders, and looked at the hatchling the way people look at things they don't believe in anymore and have just been shown proof of.

"Sixty-two days," he shouted. "That's today? That's *today*?"

"Today!" she shouted back, and heard her own voice crack on the word, and saw him hear it, and saw him decide—not quickly, deliberately, the way he seemed to decide everything—to say nothing about it.

The last egg came up at 1:20 a.m. Sixty-two total. She packed them into the foam coolers with damp sand, and they carried the coolers up the collapsing berm together, one each, leaning into the wind like drunks, and made it to the station door as a gust hit them from behind like a helping hand, or a shove, depending on how you wanted to read your life.

* * *

Inside, the station smelled of salt and old coffee and the particular mildew of Gulf-coast institutional buildings. Theo got the camp stove lit on the second try. Isandro set up the incubation tubs in the interior room—the windowless one, the one they called the bunker, where the walls held posters of turtle anatomy from a decade ago and someone's ancient cartoon of a crab labeled DO NOT TRUST.

They worked in silence for a while. There was a rhythm to it that didn't need words: she measured sand temperature, he wrote numbers on the waterproof slate; he cut ventilation holes in cooler lids, she held the lids steady. Their elbows bumped. Neither moved away.

At some point she became aware that he was wetter than she was—he'd given her the better rain jacket without her noticing, sometime on the beach, some sleight of hand she'd missed entirely—and she took the spare towel off the shelf and threw it at his head without ceremony. He caught it. Dried his hair. Said, "Thanks," and nothing else, and she thought: *eleven years ago you couldn't even look at me, and now you can't stop looking, and you've learned to do it quietly.*

"Forty-five minutes to the mainland dock," she said finally, because silence with him had a weight limit. "In this. If you're going."

"I'm not going."

"You should evacuate."

"So should you."

"I live here. This is my job." She smoothed sand over the top tub, gentling it, and did not look at him. "You're the development director. Your job is in a building that has a generator and a bar."

A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh. "Fair."

"Also," she said, and here it was, the thing she'd been circling since the beach, arriving now because exhaustion makes honest people of us all, "if you leave, I'll spend the whole storm wondering if you're going to disappear again. And I don't want to wonder. I want—" She stopped. Started over, smaller. "Stay where I can see you. That's all. It's not forgiveness. It's logistics."

"Logistics," Theo repeated, and something in his face did a complicated thing, grief and gratitude fighting over one small territory. "Okay. Logistics."

He unrolled the emergency cot against the far wall, as far from her as the room allowed, and lay down on top of his clothes, and within minutes—she checked, she was ashamed of how precisely she noted it—his breathing went long and even. Sleep came easy to him, apparently. Or he was very good at performing it.

Isandro sat with the tubs. Every twenty minutes she checked the temperature. Between checks she listened to the storm lean on the building and thought about hands. His hands passing her eggs in the dark. Her hands, eleven years ago, doing something else in the dark. How a person is just a pair of hands, really, and everything else is story.

Around 3 a.m., half-dreaming in her chair, she heard him say her name. Just once, quiet, from the cot. Not asleep, then. Or asleep and honest.

"Yeah," she said.

"The sixty-second egg," he said. "The one that was already pipping. What happens to it?"

"It goes in the release tub with its siblings. We let it finish on its own time."

"And if it can't?"

"Then we help a little. But only when it's done trying on its own." She paused. "You don't get to skip the trying part. That's the rule."

In the dark, the cot creaked. "I remember," Theo said, "that you always knew the rules."

"I invented most of them."

"I know," he said. "That was the thing I loved—"

He stopped. The word *loved* hung in the bunker air between the anatomy posters and the treacherous crab, enormous, unkillable, and neither of them reached for it and neither of them knocked it down. After a while his breathing lengthened again, real this time, and Isandro sat in the lamplight watching sixty-two eggs breathe sand, and let the storm say the things none of them were ready to.

* * *

Dawn came gray and rinsed-out, the storm's back edge dragging across the island like a skirt hem. Isandro stepped outside into wreckage and shine—shingles in the sea oats, a kayak wrapped around the flagpole, the air scrubbed new—and found Theo already awake, already dressed, standing at the tideline with his hands in his pockets, looking at the beach the way surveyors look and also the way penitents look, and she still couldn't separate the two, and was beginning to suspect she never fully would.

"We lost the berm," he said when she joined him. "Fifteen feet, maybe. Right through the primary nesting zone."

"I know. I'll survey it when the water drops."

"The county will call it an opportunity," he said bitterly. "'Natural redevelopment.' There's already language in the marina proposal about post-storm reconstruction exemptions." He turned to her, and his eyes were bloodshot and level. "I'm going to fight it. From inside. I wanted you to hear that from me before you hear it from anyone else."

"Why?"

"Because last night I handed you sixty-two eggs and you trusted my grip every single time, and you never once looked to check." His voice caught and steadied. "I'm going to spend the rest of my life trying to deserve that, and I'm starting with the marina."

She looked at him a long moment. Then she said, "The bunker needs restocking. And somebody has to drive to the mainland for supplies, and my truck's flooded."

"I'll drive."

"You'll need the ferry schedule."

"I'll figure it out." He hesitated, and then, carefully, like setting down something fragile: "There's a box. In my father's boathouse. Files he kept on—" He stopped. Started again. "On everything. When I get back, I'm bringing it to you. Unopened, if you want. Or opened. Your call. But it comes to you either way."

The wind had gone soft. Down the beach, exposed by the retreating surge, something pale caught the light—a scatter of shells, a tangle of dune grass, and, half-buried at the old nest site, one single egg the surge had stolen back in the night, sitting in the open sand like a word someone had forgotten to erase.

Isandro walked to it and knelt. Cold. Too long in the water. She lifted it anyway, gently, and carried it back up the beach, because that was the job, because you accounted for every one of them, even the ones the sea took back.

When she turned, Theo was watching her, and she held the egg up between them like a small moon.

"Sixty-three," she said. "We count all of them. That's the rule."

He nodded slowly, and she saw him understand—not the egg, the sentence underneath it—and he said, quietly, "Then I'd better go get that box," and turned toward the road, and Isandro stood in the washed morning holding the sea's receipt, knowing that whatever was in his father's files, by nightfall, the past would be in her hands too—and there would be no putting it back in any drawer.
