## 22. Tide and Summer

The morning of the first release, Isandro woke before her alarm, which was nothing new, and lay still for a moment listening to the sound of someone else in her kitchen, which was everything new.

Three years ago the only sounds at five a.m. had been the coffee maker's gurgle and the wind worrying the screen door. Now there was a second set of footsteps, careful on the old floorboards because Theo had learned within his first week back that the third board from the door complained if you stepped on it wrong, and he had taken this knowledge with the seriousness of a man being entrusted with state secrets.

"You're hovering over the eggs," she called through the wall. "I can hear it."

"I'm not hovering. I'm making oatmeal."

"Oatmeal requires hovering?"

"Theo Vance oatmeal does," Marisol said from somewhere near the stove, and Isandro smiled into the pillow. Marisol had appointed herself supervisor of the entire arrangement—of Theo's probationary presence, of his volunteer hours, of the way he looked at Isandro when he thought no one was counting—and she performed the role with the diligence of a woman who had once told Isandro, *You've been mid-sentence for ten years*, and considered herself therefore permanently invested in how the sentence ended.

Isandro got up. She dressed in the dark out of habit, though there was no need for darkness anymore; the station had windows now, real ones, installed during the reserve renovation, and the light came in early and generous across the desk where her survey sat in its published form, her full name printed beneath the title like a signature she'd finally learned to write.

*Isandro Eastwick.* It had taken twenty-nine years to put it on something without flinching.

In the kitchen, Theo stood at the stove in the faded volunteer shirt that said ASK ME ABOUT NESTS, which Marisol had ordered as a joke and which he wore anyway, every shift, with what Isandro could only describe as penitential cheerfulness. His hair was going gray at the temples. He'd come back from those three years with gray hair and a steadiness he hadn't earned so much as built, board by board, the way you build a dock: one plank at a time, checking each one to see if it would hold.

"Thermos is packed," he said. "Marisol made me stir counterclockwise. She says clockwise oatmeal is arrogant."

"Marisol is correct." Isandro took the bowl he offered. Their fingers touched over it, briefly, the way they always did now—not the desperate contact of the storm night, not the careful distance of the early visits, but something in between, a touch that assumed tomorrow. "Big crowd tonight?"

"Busload from the mainland school. Plus the Hendersons. Plus, I'm told, a woman who read your paper and drove four hours."

"My paper," Isandro said, tasting the phrase. She'd published under her own name fourteen months ago—the survey, then the follow-up study, then a piece in a coastal ecology journal about community-led nest protection that had somehow been picked up by three newspapers. Lark Eastwick's sister, cited. Lark Eastwick's sister, *read*. "Still strange."

"It'll be strange for exactly as long as you let it be," Marisol said, shouldering her field bag. "Which knowing you will be another decade. Move. The tide doesn't wait for your feelings."

* * *

The beach at dawn was the color of hammered tin, and the tide was going out, leaving the sand smooth and dark and printed with the night's traffic: ghost crab runs like calligraphy, a gull's argument with itself, and the long deliberate drag-marks where a loggerhead had come ashore and gone back to the sea, heavy and unhurried, doing the oldest work in the world.

The nest sat below the dune line, marked with stakes and ribbon and the small laminated sign Marisol had made years ago that read, in cheerful block letters, DO NOT DISTURB — THEY'RE HAVING A BIG NIGHT. Sixty-two days ago Isandro had found this crawl herself, walking the tideline alone in the blue hour, and had knelt in the sand and pressed her palm flat above the chamber and felt, or imagined she felt, the faint seismic promise of what was coming.

Tonight it came. Eighty-nine hatchlings, when they counted them out of the sand—eighty-nine small furious lives, each one no bigger than a bottle cap, each one programmed by a hundred million years of patience to do exactly one thing.

The crowd gathered behind the rope line. The schoolchildren were vibrating. The four-hour woman had brought binoculars and a folding chair and an expression of profound readiness. Isandro gave her short talk—she'd given it a hundred times now, and it never got shorter, because some things deserved their full length—about why they didn't carry the hatchlings to the water, about how the crawl itself was the point, how the sand remembered them, how the females would come back to this exact beach in thirty years because the beach writes its address into their bones.

Then she knelt at the top of the nest pit and lifted the first hatchling into the light.

It went still for one heartbeat—one held breath that the whole beach seemed to share—and then its flippers began to row against the air, urgent and absurd and magnificent, and Isandro set it down facing the water, and it went.

They all went. Eighty-nine small engines churning down the slope of the world toward the foam line, and the crowd went quiet the way crowds only go quiet when they've forgotten to perform, and Isandro crouched at the edge of the rope with her tally counter in her hand and counted them into the sea.

She had counted turtles for eleven years the way other people pray. One click per life, one small mechanical assurance that she was paying attention, that she was good, that her attention could stand in for all the things she couldn't say. Tonight she clicked eighty-nine times, and somewhere around sixty she became aware of Theo beside her, also crouched, also watching, not touching her, not needing to, and she realized her hand had stopped shaking somewhere in the last three years and she hadn't even noticed it stop.

"There goes number seventy," he murmured. "He's taking the scenic route."

"He's thorough."

"He's lost."

"He's *thorough*, Theo. There's a difference."

A wave took the stragglers—all of them, the whole silver-dark scatter of them, folded into the foam and gone—and the crowd exhaled, and the children cheered, and the four-hour woman wiped her eyes with a sleeve and pretended she hadn't. Isandro stayed crouched a moment longer, looking at the wet sand where eighty-nine sets of tracks ran down and vanished, separate lines, same direction.

She knew the image meant something. She'd known it for three years. She was, as Marisol frequently observed, slow on purpose.

* * *

They walked the tideline after the crowd drained away, the way they did most evenings now, though this evening the light was doing something extravagant—turning the water to pewter and rose gold, gilding the wrack line, making even the washed-up horseshoe crabs look like ceremonial objects.

Their footprints ran parallel down the beach. Not together, not apart. Two lines of prints with a hand's width of dry sand between them, holding that width the whole way, as if the sand itself had agreed to the terms.

"I got a letter today," Theo said.

Isandro's stomach did the old drop, the letter-reflex, eleven years deep. Then she saw his face and unclenched. "Good letter or bad letter?"

"Strange letter. The development company—the one I worked for before all this—they're doing some kind of legacy review. Compliance thing. They want former employees to certify they were never asked to suppress environmental findings." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "I wrote back two pages. Marisol helped me edit it down to one."

"What happened to the other page?"

"It contained the word 'vultures' eleven times."

"Eleven times is probably excessive."

"Nine was defensible. Eleven was spite." He kicked gently at a shell, sent it spinning. "Anyway. That's done. Filed. Certified." He glanced at her sideways. "How's the grant application?"

"Submitted. All forty pages. With my name on it and everything."

"Braggart."

"Forty pages, Theo. I earned bragging rights."

They walked. The lighthouse at the point blinked on, punctual as ever, sweeping its slow white arm across the water, and Isandro thought—as she often did now, in this hour, on this stretch of sand—about counting.

She had spent half her life counting things as a way of not counting others. Turtles instead of losses. Nests instead of nights. Clicks of a tally counter instead of the words *I saw him and I said nothing*, or later, the harder ones: *I loved him and I stayed silent*, or later still, the hardest of all, the ones she'd finally said aloud in a courtroom with her hands flat on the rail: *My sister wanted to be a lawyer, and I broke the law to protect the man who killed her, and silence was the only inheritance our family ever finished paying off.*

Her father had died eighteen months after the sentencing, in the lucid hour, holding her hand and saying *Lark would be proud of you* and then, ten minutes later, asking when Lark was coming home, and Isandro had answered honestly for the first time: *She's not coming home, Daddy. But she's everywhere else.* He'd seemed satisfied with that. She chose to believe he understood more than the doctors thought possible. Grief permitted certain fictions; love required them.

And Theo—Theo had served his sentence and served it clean, no appeals, no complaints, letters home that were honest to the point of discomfort, the kind of honesty that arrives like cold water and is somehow a gift. He had written to her twice a year, brief and unsentimental: *Still here. Still working in the library. Still not asking for anything.* And when he got out, he had come back to the island and stood at the edge of the station parking lot with a duffel bag and no speech prepared, and he had said, "I'd like to volunteer. If you'll have me. I understand if you won't. I understand if you will and it means nothing. I understand if it takes ten years."

It had taken three. Not because forgiveness arrived on schedule but because Isandro had learned—slowly, the way you learn tide charts—that love wasn't a debt being repaid. Debts end. Love was more like the tide itself: it didn't finish, it renewed. It went out and it came back and it went out again, and you didn't stand on the sand demanding it justify the recession. You just learned its rhythm, and you built your life where the water could reach you.

"Tell me the number," Theo said.

She smiled. He asked every year, on release night. "Eighty-nine."

"Eighty-nine." He nodded slowly, as if approving a verdict. "And how many made it to the water?"

"All of them that we saw."

"And how many will come back?"

"One in a thousand, maybe. Maybe fewer."

"So we did all this—" he gestured at the darkening beach, the empty nest, the whole patient apparatus of stakes and screens and predawn walks "—for maybe zero-point-one turtles."

"For maybe zero-point-one turtles," Isandro agreed. "That's the job. You don't get to know which one. You just make the beach ready and trust the ocean to do its part."

Theo was quiet for a few steps. When he spoke, his voice had that register it found sometimes, low and careful, the voice of a man who had learned that words were load-bearing. "You know that's what you did for me. Don't you. All these years. Made the beach ready."

Isandro stopped walking.

She looked out at the water, where the last light was going copper and then gray, and she felt the old reflex rise—the reflex to deflect, to joke, to click the tally counter and move along—and she let it rise and kept walking right through it, because that was the practice now, that was the whole discipline of her life: feeling the thing and staying in the room with it.

"I know," she said.

Theo exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down something heavy he'd been carrying politely for miles.

They reached the point where the wrack line curved and the dunes leaned close, their usual turning place, and Isandro did something she almost never did, which was stop first and turn around first and look at him first. The lighthouse swept past behind him, white, then dark, then white.

"Marry me," she said.

Theo blinked. "What?"

"You heard me. I'm not saying it twice. I rehearsed it for three years and I'm not doing encores."

"You—" He put a hand over his mouth. Above it, his eyes were doing something complicated and shining. "Isandro Eastwick. Are you proposing on a work beach during a monitored nesting event?"

"The event concluded. We're off the clock."

"There are protocols—"

"There are *no* protocols, you made those up to feel useful—"

"—and Marisol is absolutely going to say she called it—"

"Marisol *did* call it. In writing. There's a bet pool. I intend to collect my winnings either way."

Theo laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh of a man with nothing left to hide, which was the only laugh of his she'd ever trusted—and then he crossed the hand's width of sand between their footprints and took her face in both hands, sand-dusted and salt-stiff and trembling slightly, and said, "Yes. Obviously yes. Though I want it noted that I had a plan involving dinner."

"Noted and rejected. Dinner plans are how people end up mid-sentence for ten years."

"That was one time."

"It was eleven years, Theo. I have the receipts."

He kissed her then, and the lighthouse swept past, white, then dark, then white, and the tide came in over their footprints and erased the boundary between them, and Isandro thought: *there*, that's the trick of it, that's what nobody tells you—you don't get absolution, you don't get the past back, you get this. A person who stays. A beach that holds. A tally counter clicking in the dark for lives too small to see the point of any of it, going down to the water anyway, every single one of them, because going down to the water is what they're for.

* * *

Later—much later, the stars fully out, the station dark except for the porch light—they sat on the steps with the thermos between them and split the last of the oatmeal, which had, through some thermal miracle, remained edible.

"Next season," Theo said, "I want to take the north beach survey. Marisol thinks I'm ready."

"Marisol thinks you're ready because Marisol wants her mornings back."

"Both things can be true."

"They usually are." Isandro leaned her head on his shoulder. Below them, the tide worked its endless arithmetic, adding and subtracting the same water, keeping perfect books. "One in a thousand," she said quietly. "We send out eighty-nine and maybe none of them come back. And we do it again next year anyway."

"That's the job," Theo said.

"That's the job." She turned the tally counter over in her hand, its little brass wheel worn bright from years of her thumb. Eighty-nine clicks tonight. Eleven years of clicks before that. And underneath all of them, uncounted until recently, the other numbers: the mornings she'd gotten up anyway. The truths she'd said out loud. The years she'd spent learning that a life could be rebuilt not despite what broke it but on the very spot where it broke, the way a dune rebuilds seaward of the old one, farther out, closer to the weather, braver.

She clicked the counter once more—an eighty-ninth-plus-one, an unofficial turtle, a private census—and Theo felt her do it and didn't ask, because he was a man who had learned which silences were wounds and which ones were just a woman counting herself among the living.

Above them the stars came on in their multitudes, patient as eggs in warm sand. Down at the foam line, invisible in the dark, some small fraction of eighty-nine swam east into the Gulf, carrying the beach's address in their bones.

Thirty years, give or take, and they'd find their way home.

Isandro intended to be here when they did.
