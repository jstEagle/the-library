## 4. Stranded

### Isolde

The snow started as a rumor and became a verdict.

I'd been driving north along the coast for two hours, watching the forecast on my phone degrade from *flurries possible* to *winter storm warning* to a red banner that looked less like weather advice and more like a restraining order against the entire peninsula. My manager, Dev, had texted four times in the last hour, each message escalating from professional concern to something approaching prayer.

*Are you off the coast road yet?*

*Isolde. The pass closes at five.*

*Please tell me you're not being romantic about this.*

I was, actually. I was being extremely romantic about it, though not in the way he feared. There was nothing romantic about fleeing your own life; it's one of the least glamorous activities available to a woman of thirty-four. But there was something — I'll call it gravity — pulling me toward this particular stretch of gray coastline, toward the town where the winter festival had booked me to headline, toward the venue I'd insisted on scouting personally when any sane artist would have sent an assistant with a camera phone.

The town was called Pelham's Reach, which sounded like either a fishing village or a law firm, and it was both, historically. It was also, though I had never once said this aloud, the place where Rafael Whitlock grew up. Where he learned to row before he learned to bow. Where he used to talk about the water like other men talk about God.

Ten years, and I still knew his childhood geography by heart. That's the kind of fact you keep in a drawer you don't open, but the drawer doesn't lock, apparently, because here I was, driving toward it in a rented Audi with the heat on full blast and my career humming pleasantly in the back seat like a well-fed cat.

The cat was about to be drowned.

The road narrowed where the pines closed in, and the snow — which had been decorative, storybook, almost charming — went suddenly horizontal. The wipers lost their argument with it. The world outside the windshield turned into static, white noise made visible, and I felt the car's tires go quiet in that specific way that means they've stopped negotiating with the road and started merely corresponding with it.

"Fine," I said, to no one. "Fine. I'll turn around."

There was nowhere to turn around. The shoulder had dissolved into a ditch that the county presumably pretended didn't exist eleven months of the year. I eased off the gas, and the car did what cars do when they're frightened: it decided for itself. The rear end swung out with a gracelessness that would have embarrassed me even without witnesses, and then the whole vehicle slid sideways down an embankment with a sound like a piano being moved by amateurs, and stopped.

I sat there. Snow ticked against the glass. Somewhere under the hood, the engine ticked back, offended.

I checked myself — wrists, neck, pride. All intact, more or less. I put the car in reverse and pressed the pedal, and the wheels spun with the cheerful futility of a dog chasing its tail. Forward: same result, different direction of futility. I got out to assess, and the wind took my breath, my hood, and most of my dignity in one motion. The front end was buried in a drift. The rear wheels hung over the ditch like a woman fainting onto a chaise.

No signal. Of course no signal. The storm had eaten the cell tower along with everything else.

I stood in the falling white and considered my options, which were: freeze elegantly beside a luxury automobile, or walk. Behind me, the road ran empty back toward the highway. Ahead, through the thickening curtain, I could just make out a light — a single yellow smudge, maybe half a mile on, bobbing slightly. Moving. Attached, I hoped, to a human being.

I walked toward it. The snow came down harder, as if it had been waiting for exactly this dramatic cue, and I thought, absurdly, of the Tchaikovsky finals, of walking out onstage into lights that hot and total, and how every stage since had been a smaller version of that first blinding moment, and how lately even the biggest halls had felt like walking into weather.

The light resolved into a truck. An old truck, dark green gone chalky with salt, parked at the roadside with its hazards blinking amber through the white. And standing at its tailgate, wrestling a coil of chain into some kind of order, was a man in a canvas jacket who moved — even hunched against the wind, even at forty meters through a snowfall — with a posture I would have known blindfolded, upside down, dead.

Tall. Left-handed. Shoulders that carried a cello for twenty years and hadn't forgotten the shape of it.

My body knew before my mind consented to the information. My heart did something unprofessional.

"No," I said, out loud, to the snow. "Absolutely not."

But the universe, which has always had my number, chose that exact moment to clear a seam in the wind, and the man turned around, and ten years collapsed like a bad scaffold.

Rafael Whitlock looked at me across a snowstorm, holding a tow chain, and his face did three things in quick succession: blankness, recognition, and something I can only describe as the expression of a man finding a receipt for a purchase he'd sworn he'd returned.

"Marchetti," he said.

"Whitlock." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "You look—"

*Good*, I was going to say. He did look good, which was infuriating. Older, yes, lines around his eyes that hadn't been earned gently, but the same face underneath, the one I'd spent a decade not thinking about at a frequency that suggested constant thought.

"You look stranded," he said.

### Rafael

Of all the driveways in all the towns in all the world.

I want it on record that I was having a perfectly ordinary evening. Maren had called the boatyard at noon to say the Harborlight's pipes were acting up again and could I take a look before the storm buried everything, and I'd said yes, because saying yes to Maren is not really a choice, it's a reflex, like breathing or losing competitions to Isolde Marchetti.

Sorry. Old habit. I don't lose to her anymore. Nobody loses to her anymore; she wins everything, always has, always will, and I have built a life — a small, salt-bleached, entirely adequate life — on the foundation of not caring about that.

So there I was, chains in the truck bed, headed home ahead of the worst of it, when I saw the taillights of a car pointing in a direction cars are not supposed to point, down an embankment that eats one tourist per winter like clockwork. I pulled over, muttering about rental plates and city tires, and got out to play the role of Good Samaritan, which is a role I perform competently and without emotional investment.

Then she stepped out of the snow.

Here is what nobody tells you about the past: it doesn't knock. It doesn't send a letter announcing its visit. It waits until you're standing in a storm with cold hands and a tow chain, looking like exactly the man you became instead of the man you were supposed to be, and then it steps out of the white wearing a wool coat that costs more than my truck, and it says your name like a diagnosis.

"Whitlock."

"Marchetti." I kept my voice level. Inside, a hall full of people was applauding, and I was standing onstage with my left hand seizing into a claw, and the Elgar was dying note by note, and everyone was watching. Ten years old, that memory, and it still played at full volume whenever she was near. She'd been in the audience that night. Third row. I'd seen her face do something complicated right before everything went wrong, and I'd spent a decade telling myself it had been pity.

"You look—" she began.

"You look stranded," I said, because I am a coward with excellent timing.

She laughed. One short note, sharp as a downbow stroke. "Charming as ever."

"I have a chain," I said. "And a truck. Beyond that, my hospitality is limited."

"Wouldn't dream of expecting more."

We stood there in the falling snow, two adults, internationally credentialed, conducting a conversation composed entirely of fencing moves we'd learned at nineteen. The wind shoved between us like a chaperone with opinions. Her hair was coming loose from whatever architecture usually held it, and there was snow melting on her cheekbones, and I noticed — hated myself for noticing — that she still tilted her head slightly left when she was deciding whether to be angry. She'd done it in masterclasses. She'd done it the night she told me she was taking the Tchaikovsky audition instead of coming away with me, head tilted left, mouth already forming the word *someday*.

"Keys?" I said.

She handed them over like a queen delegating an unpleasant chore. I waded down to the car, hooked the chain, and worked while she stood at the top of the embankment pretending not to watch. The winch groaned. The Audi complained. The storm, sensing an audience, escalated.

It took twenty minutes. Twenty minutes of rope and mud and the two of us shouting coordinates at each other over the wind — *left, harder, ease off* — and somewhere in the middle of it I realized we had fallen back into a rhythm so familiar it was obscene. Ten years of silence, and our voices still fit together like a duo that had rehearsed.

The car came free all at once, lurching up onto the road like a breaching thing, and she exhaled, and I killed the winch, and the sudden quiet was enormous.

"There," I said. "Your chariot awaits."

"Thank you." She said it stiffly, the way people thank surgeons. Then, because she has never once been able to leave a score unresolved: "What are you doing here? In Pelham's Reach. You used to talk about Vienna like it was oxygen."

"I breathe fine at sea level."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get for free."

Her chin came up. Snow caught in her lashes. For a second neither of us said anything, and the storm filled the silence with its own white noise, and I was aware of her the way you're aware of a struck bell — not the sound anymore, just the ringing after.

"The festival," she said finally. "I'm headlining the winter festival. I came to scout the venue."

Of course she was. Of course she was headlining it. The universe has a sense of humor, and it is cruel, and it is thorough.

"Congratulations," I said.

"Rafael—"

"Don't." I picked up the chain and coiled it, needing my hands busy, needing them to be good for something. "Road's closing behind you. There's nothing open between here and the highway except the Harborlight, and Maren's rooms are cold but her brandy isn't. Take the truck or take your chances with the ditch. Your call, Marchetti. I'm neutral."

"You are many things," she said, "but you have never once been neutral."

### Isolde

He drove. I followed in the Audi, which felt symbolic in a way I refused to examine. The last half mile to the inn took ten minutes, the wipers fighting a war they'd already lost, and by the time we pulled into the Harborlight's lot the snow was coming down like the sky had a grudge.

Maren Voss opened the door before we knocked, a small silver-haired woman in a cardigan the color of strong tea, and looked at the two of us standing on her step — separately, pointedly, a full arm's length apart — with the expression of someone reading a very short book and knowing exactly how it ends.

"Power'll go by midnight," she said. "I've got two rooms and a sitting room, and the kitchen's yours if you cook. No refunds, no exceptions, and no drama in my parlor."

"No danger of that," Rafael said.

"None whatsoever," I said.

Maren looked between us for a long moment, the way you'd look at a weather system. "Hm," she said, which contained multitudes.

The rooms were narrow and clean and smelled of cedar and cold radiators. I unpacked nothing. I sat on the edge of the bed in my coat and listened to the storm lean on the building, and I thought about the fact that Rafael Whitlock was thirty feet away, sanding hulls in a coastal town, talking about sea level like a man who had amputated something and called it a lifestyle change.

*I breathe fine at sea level.*

Liar. I know liars. I've been one professionally for a decade.

Through the wall, faintly, I heard him moving around his room. A drawer opening. Something set down carefully — too carefully, the way you handle a thing that matters. Then nothing.

I should sleep. Tomorrow the plows come, the road opens, I scout the venue, I sign whatever Dev puts in front of me, and Rafael Whitlock goes back to being a name in a drawer.

The radiator clanked. The wind howled its long single note.

I did not sleep.

* * *

### Rafael

The cello case was under my bed where it has lived for six years, and I hadn't meant to touch it. I'd meant to find the spare blanket Maren keeps in the bottom drawer. But the room was dark and the storm was loud and my hands, traitorous things, went straight past the blanket to the handle of the case, and I knelt there on the floor like a man at a shrine, not opening it, just resting my palm on the cool composite lid.

Thirty feet away, through one wall, Isolde Marchetti was not sleeping either. I'd bet the boatyard on it. I could feel her awake the way you feel a rest in a piece of music — the whole structure leaning on the silence, waiting for the downbeat.

Tomorrow the road opens. Tomorrow she leaves, and the drawer closes, and I go back to hulls and varnish and a life I chose, damn it, a life I *chose*.

Downstairs, a floorboard creaked. Maren, doing her rounds, or the house settling under the weight of all this weather. Or fate, clearing its throat.

I let go of the case. I lay down on top of the blankets, boots off, coat still on, and stared at the ceiling while the storm wrote its long sentence across the roof.

One night, I told my hands. One night, and then we all go back to pretending.

My hands, which have betrayed me once before, said nothing at all.
