## 7. What the Storm Brought In

The power died at 6:47 in the evening, and I know this because Rafael Whitlock was watching the clock when it happened.

I know *that* because I was watching him watch the clock — a habit I'd apparently never broken, even after a decade of not thinking about him at all. Which was a lie I told myself the way other people tell themselves they'll only have one glass of wine.

The lights flickered twice, apologetic, and then the whole inn went dark except for the blue glow of the storm outside and the small orange pilot light on the kitchen stove. Somewhere upstairs, Maren's radio crackled through static: *coastal route closed until further notice, folks, so settle in.*

"Settle in," Rafael repeated, to no one. "That's one way to put it."

I lit the candle I'd found in the pantry drawer — a stub of beeswax that smelled like honey and dust — and held it up between us like a peace offering or an accusation. With him it was hard to tell the difference.

"Well," I said. "This is cozy."

"It's a blackout, Isolde. Cozy requires consent."

"Everything with you requires paperwork. No wonder you went into boat repair."

He almost smiled. I saw it happen — the corner of his mouth making a break for freedom before he wrestled it back down. Ten years ago I could have made him laugh in under three seconds. Now I had to work for the almosts. It was infuriating. It was also, God help me, the most engaged I'd felt in months.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about a career at the top: it's very quiet up there. Not silent — there's always a manager's voice in your ear, always another city, another hall, another review that says *flawless* in a tone that somehow makes flawless sound like a diagnosis. But quiet in the way a snowfield is quiet. Beautiful, and empty, and if you sat down in the middle of it you might never get up.

I had been sitting down in the middle of it for about two years now.

Which is why, when the gas burner caught with a soft blue *whump* and Rafael started pulling things out of Maren's refrigerator with the unselfconscious competence of a man who actually knew how to feed himself, I didn't retreat to my room to answer emails. I stayed in the kitchen. I told myself it was because the candle was in here. The candle. Sure.

"What are you doing?" I asked, as he set down eggs, a block of cheese, half an onion, and something that might once have been chives.

"Cooking dinner. You're welcome to starve performatively in the sitting room if you'd rather."

"I don't starve performatively. I starve professionally."

"There she is," he said quietly, and cracked an egg one-handed.

There she is. As if I were a weather pattern he recognized. As if ten years hadn't happened, or had happened only to him, leaving me exactly where he'd left me — nineteen years old and insufferable, arguing about fingering positions in a practice room that smelled like resin and radiator heat.

I sat down at the kitchen table because standing suddenly required more of me than I had. The candle threw our shadows huge against the wall, two giants hunched over a stove.

"You cook," I said. It came out as an accusation. Most things did, with him.

"Boats need feeding too." He sliced the onion without weeping, which struck me as deeply unfair. "Maren keeps a decent kitchen. She left instructions. There's a note taped inside the cupboard that says *feed them, they're too proud to ask.*"

"She wrote that about us?"

"She wrote that about *me.* You she described as 'the famous one, mind the good china.'"

I laughed. It escaped before I could vet it — a real laugh, ugly and sudden, the kind I hadn't produced in front of another human being since approximately the Obama administration. Rafael turned around with the frying pan in his hand and looked at me like I'd done a magic trick.

"What?"

"Nothing," he said. "You just — laughed."

"People laugh all the time, Whitlock. It's in every city. Check the tour schedule."

"Not like that."

The pan hissed. The storm pressed its whole weight against the windows. And I sat in a stranger's kitchen in a town I'd driven into on a whim, watching my oldest rival scramble eggs by gaslight, and thought: *this is the most honest hour of my year.* That was either very sad or very promising, and I genuinely couldn't tell which.

* * *

Dinner was eggs and toast and tea, eaten off mismatched plates while the wind performed its full program — allegro con fuoco, with dramatic pauses. Rafael ate the way he did everything now: carefully, economically, hands steady in a way that seemed less like ease than discipline. I remembered those hands differently. Restless. Always moving, always talking, conducting some private orchestra against his knee during other people's performances because he couldn't bear to just listen.

"Do you remember," I said, before my better judgment could tackle me, "the time you fell asleep during my masterclass recital?"

His fork stopped. "I didn't fall asleep."

"You snored. In the third row. Frau Lindqvist thought the building had a plumbing problem."

"That was you. You fell asleep during *my* recital, and when Lindqvist woke you, you said—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it from whatever vault he kept the past in, "'—you said, *I wasn't sleeping, I was listening with my eyes closed,*' and she believed you, because you could sell anything."

"*Anything*," I agreed, and then heard what I'd said and wished I hadn't, because selling things was literally my profession now, and the word landed between us like a dropped glass.

Rafael looked at his plate. "It was a good performance, though. That Tchaikovsky. Even asleep, I could hear it was good."

"You hated that piece."

"I hated that *you* played it better than I did."

And there it was — the old scoreboard, still hanging in the air between us after all these years, both of us glancing up at it out of habit. I waited for the familiar sting. It didn't come. What came instead was something worse: fondness. A warm, treacherous flood of it, rising around my ankles like water through a hull.

"The judges' comments," I said, "called your Elgar 'devastatingly mature.' I framed mine. Did I ever tell you that? I framed the comment sheet where they ranked me second and hung it over my desk."

"Why would you do that?"

"So I'd stay angry enough to practice."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, something heavy — a branch, maybe, or the whole concept of mercy — hit the side of the inn.

"Did it work?" he asked.

I should have said yes. The correct answer, the polished answer, the answer my publicist would have approved, was yes, obviously, look at everything. Instead I heard myself say:

"I'm not sure I've stopped practicing angry since. It turns out you can build an entire life on a grudge. It holds weight. It's just — cold in the rooms."

Rafael set down his fork. For a second I thought he was going to say something enormous, something that would require structural repairs to both of us. Then the wind screamed, the candle guttered, and the moment passed like a ferry disappearing into fog.

"We should check on Maren," he said instead, standing. "And then — I want to show you something. If you can stand it."

"Stand what?"

"The hallway," he said, and something in his voice made my chest go strange. "The photo wall."

* * *

I should explain about the photo wall, except I can't fully explain it, because I didn't know it existed until that night, and by the time I understood what I was looking at, it was already too late to look away.

The Harborlight Inn's main hallway ran from the kitchen to the stairs, and one wall of it — the north wall, the one that never got direct sun — was covered in photographs. Dozens of them. Old ones, salt-faded, pinned and taped and framed in frames that had clearly been chosen by three different generations with three different ideas of aesthetics. Maren's family, presumably. Maren's harbor. Maren's whole long life arranged in a grid.

Except that Rafael walked straight to one particular cluster near the stairs, and I followed him because following him was apparently still a thing my body did without consulting me, and I saw—

"Oh," I said.

A boy of maybe eight, gap-toothed, sitting in a dinghy far too big for him, holding an oar like a cello bow. A teenager on the dock, squinting into the sun, a battered instrument case beside him. A young man in a tuxedo, holding a certificate, scowling at the camera as though the certificate had personally offended him.

"You kept these," I said stupidly. "Your mother—"

"My mother kept them. When she died, Maren took the boxes. Said she wasn't going to let me throw away my own childhood just because I'd resigned from it." He touched the edge of one frame, careful, the way he touched everything now. "I keep meaning to take them down."

"You've been here how long?"

"Three years."

"And you haven't taken them down."

"They grow on you," he said. "Like barnacles."

I moved along the wall because I couldn't not. There was the harbor in summer, packed with boats. There was a woman with kind eyes and Rafael's jaw — his mother, unmistakably. There was a fishing crew laughing on a deck. There was—

My own face.

Nineteen years old, sunburned, hair escaping its braid, laughing so hard the picture had blurred slightly at the edges, and next to me, equally ruined with laughter, Rafael — Rafael at nineteen, before the tuxedos and the certificates and the silence, his arm slung around my shoulders like it belonged there, like it had been issued to him at birth and he was simply wearing it home.

We were standing on this dock. This actual dock, outside this actual window, in a photograph taken by someone who must have called out something ridiculous to make us laugh like that. We were holding ice cream cones. Mine was dripping. Neither of us cared.

"Where did this come from?" My voice came out wrong. Smaller.

"Maren took it. She used to run the snack shack summers. She said we were—" He stopped. Started again. "She said we were the loudest thing on the waterfront and the only thing louder was us pretending we weren't in love."

The storm filled the silence for both of us.

"We weren't," I said.

"No," he agreed.

"We were *going* to be. That's different."

"Is it?" He was looking at the photograph, not at me, which was the only reason I could breathe. "I've spent ten years trying to figure out if it's different."

I reached out and touched the frame. My fingertips left prints in the dust — the photograph had been handled, recently, often. I looked at Rafael. He looked at the floor with the dignity of a man who has been caught red-handed at something unforgivable, namely hope.

"You dust it," I said.

"I live here. Dust happens."

"You *dust it*, Rafael."

"Somebody has to keep the place from falling apart," he muttered, and it was such a transparent deflection, such an appallingly bad lie, that the laugh came up out of me again — the real one, the abandoned one — and this time he joined, helpless, both of us standing in a dark hallway giggling like the teenagers in the photograph, and God, it was awful, it was wonderful, it was the first time in a decade I had laughed at something true.

"Frau Lindqvist," I wheezed, "thought the building had plumbing problems—"

"—and you sold her *eyes closed listening*—"

"—she gave me a *prize* for it—"

"She gave you the prize I was going to win!"

"You were ASLEEP—"

"I was NINETEEN, everything was boring at nineteen except you—"

He stopped. So did I. The sentence hung there, unplanned, unpolished, entirely unsold — the most honest thing either of us had said in ten years, delivered by accident in a blackout hallway between a photo of two ice cream cones and a dead woman's kindness.

Neither of us moved. The candle I'd carried from the kitchen had burned low; the light was going amber and thin. Down the hall, the storm threw itself against the glass again, and again, like something demanding to be let in.

"I should—" I began.

"Yeah," he said.

Neither of us went anywhere. We stood there in the amber dark, two adults orbiting a photograph of who we'd been, and I understood with terrible clarity that the truce we'd drafted yesterday — *no past, no festival, no cello* — had just drowned somewhere off the coast, quietly, without witnesses.

From the top of the stairs, Maren's voice floated down, dry as driftwood: "If you two are going to stand in my hallway all night, at least bring the brandy up. Top shelf, behind the flour. And take your time finding it."

Rafael closed his eyes. "She's been listening the whole time."

"Of course she has. She dusts that photo." I turned toward the kitchen, toward the brandy, toward whatever was coming next, and found I wasn't afraid of it — which frightened me more than anything else had all week. "Well, Whitlock? You heard the landlady. Top shelf. Behind the flour."

He followed me into the dark, and neither of us pretended it was about the brandy.
