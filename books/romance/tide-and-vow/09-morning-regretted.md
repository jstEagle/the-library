## 9. Morning, Regretted

I woke to gulls and the smell of coffee and the immediate, animal certainty that I had made a catastrophic error.

Not the kiss. The kiss I would get to later, in the privacy of a shower stall or a long drive, where it could be filed under *anomalies* and dealt with by a version of me with better resources. No — the error was simpler and more damning: I had slept. Actually slept, deeply, bonelessly, for what felt like the first time in three years, in a strange bed in a shuttered inn on a coast I had no professional reason to visit, and when I opened my eyes the light through the salt-frosted window was the flat gold of mid-morning, which meant Rafael Whitlock was awake somewhere in this house, possibly dressed, definitely aware of everything that had happened between us last night, and I had no script.

I always have a script. That is not vanity; it is infrastructure. Twenty years of competitions teach you to walk onstage knowing exactly what your face will do at every bar of music, how you'll bow, how you'll receive the verdict, how you'll smile at the person who beat you — especially how you'll smile at the person who beat you. My entire career is a load-bearing structure of rehearsed expressions, and last night I had walked out onto a bare stage with nothing prepared, kissed my oldest rival in a kitchen that smelled of plum brandy and woodsmoke, and then gone to sleep like an amateur.

The ceiling above me was old plaster, water-stained in one corner like a map of somewhere I'd never been. Somewhere below, floorboards creaked under a man's weight. Maren's radio murmured — weather, probably, or the shipping report, the liturgy of small coastal towns.

Right, I thought, sitting up. Inventory.

I had: one phone at forty percent battery, one tour beginning in eleven days (Vienna, Prague, Zurich, a stretch of German cities whose names my manager said like drumbeats), one manager who called twice daily regardless of time zones, one reputation built over a decade on the premise that Isolde Marchetti did not do anything unplanned, unreviewed, or unprofitable. I had a dress rehearsal schedule taped inside my mind like a rosary. I had a life.

What I did not have was any defensible explanation for why I had let Rafael Whitlock put his hands in my hair while a storm dismantled the world outside, or why, when he'd pulled back and looked at me with that terrible open face — the one he'd had at nineteen, before we taught each other to armor up — I hadn't run.

I should have run. Running was the move. Running was, historically, *my* move, executed flawlessly since 2014.

I got up and dressed in yesterday's clothes because my bag was still in the car, which was still in the ditch, which was still buried under a foot of snow at the end of a closed road, and there was something almost funny about that — the great Isolde Marchetti, stranded without so much as a change of underwear, her entire armored life reduced to what she could carry in her pockets. I did not laugh. Laughing would have been the first crack, and I'd already made enough of those.

In the mirror over the washstand, a woman looked back at me with a mouth that had clearly been kissed and eyes that hadn't decided what to do about it. I told her, firmly, *We are professionals*, and she had the audacity to look skeptical.

* * *

The kitchen was empty. The coffee pot was full and still hot, two mugs set out — two, the traitor — and a note in Maren's looping hand: *Gone to check on the Hendersons' pipes. Behave.* Underlined twice, which from Maren was less a warning than a dare.

So it was just me, the coffee, and the sitting room where last night's photo wall still waited. I drank standing up, because sitting down felt like settling in, and I was not settling in. I was waiting out a road closure. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

Through the window, the harbor lay white and stunned, boats hauled up and shrink-wrapped, the water beyond them the color of hammered tin. It was beautiful in the way places are beautiful when they don't know you're watching — and that was the problem with this town, I realized. It kept catching me off guard. It had caught Rafael off guard too, once, apparently, long enough to make him stay.

And there he was, actually — crossing the yard toward the inn with an armful of split firewood, snow in his dark hair, wearing a flannel shirt and work gloves like a man in an advertisement for a life I couldn't afford. He moved differently than he had at twenty-five. Slower through the shoulders. Careful, the way people are careful when something in them has broken and healed crooked. But his stride was still his stride, and some traitorous part of my chest recognized it from across a frozen yard and *leaned toward it*, the way plants lean at windows.

No, I told myself. Absolutely not. We are going to go inside, be civil, be brief, and construct a narrative in which last night was a storm-induced anomaly attributable to brandy, nostalgia, and low blood sugar. I have done harder things than this. I once played the Shostakovich concerto with a fractured rib. I once smiled through an entire awards ceremony while the man I loved stood in the audience not looking at me.

That last one, I noted, had taken considerably more out of me than the rib.

He came in stamping snow off his boots, and the warmth of him arrived before he did — woodsmoke and cold air and something underneath that was just *Rafael*, unchanged, which was the most dangerous thing in the room.

"Morning," he said. Carefully casual. A man handling glassware.

"Morning." Bright. Too bright. I heard myself do it and winced internally. "Maren left coffee."

"I see that." He hung his coat. Stacked wood by the stove with the competence of someone who'd done it ten thousand times. "Sleep okay?"

"Fine." A beat. "The bed's fine. The room. Everything's fine."

Three fines. Excellent. Very convincing. Somewhere in Vienna, my publicist felt a disturbance and didn't know why.

He straightened, and for a moment neither of us moved, and the kitchen held all the weight of last night — the brandy bottle with its neck gone, the photo of us at nineteen with our arms slung around each other like the word *rival* hadn't been invented yet, the kiss itself, which lived now in the air between us like heat off the stove. His eyes went to my mouth for half a second. I watched him decide not to act on it, and I hated him a little for being the disciplined one, because discipline was supposed to be *my* instrument.

"So," I said, and reached for the script. "About last night—"

"You don't have to—"

"It was a mistake." It came out fast and clean, a downbow stroke, and I watched it land. Watched something behind his eyes close, politely, the way a shopkeeper closes a door at closing time. "I want to be clear about that. Not — it wasn't *unpleasant*, obviously, but it was a mistake, categorically. Storm logic. Cabin fever. Brandy." I gestured vaguely at the bottle as if it might take responsibility. "None of it means anything about — the present situation."

"The present situation," he repeated.

"I have a life, Rafael." And here it came, the whole rehearsed aria, the one I'd been drafting since I woke, and God help me it sounded even worse aloud than it had in my head, faster, more defensive, the tempo of panic. "I have a tour starting in eleven days. I have a manager who will be calling within the hour demanding to know why I'm not answering emails. I have students, a recording contract, a season planned out to next June. None of that—" I stopped. Restarted. "None of that includes this town. Or this inn. Or—"

"You," he supplied quietly.

"Or you," I agreed, because denying it outright would have been a lie too large even for me, and lying badly is the one unforgivable sin in my profession. "It was ten years ago. People aren't who they were at nineteen. You're not. I'm certainly not. Last night was two people getting drunk on an old woman's plum brandy and confusing memory with—"

"Don't." Just the one word, but it stopped me like a hand on the wrist. He wasn't angry. That was the awful part. He looked tired, in a way that had nothing to do with sleep, and he said, gently, "You've been rehearsing that. Since you woke up. How long — an hour? Two?"

Heat climbed my throat. "I don't know what you mean."

"You do your best lying at speed. You always did. At the Tchaikovsky semifinals, when they asked if you were nervous, you talked for four minutes straight." He picked up his mug. "I'm not asking you to explain it, Isolde. I just wish you'd skip the speech. We both know it by heart. You gave me most of it already, ten years ago, from a payphone outside the competition hall."

The payphone. He had to bring up the payphone. I had spent a decade not thinking about that payphone — the rain, the coin I didn't have, the way his voice had gone carefully blank when I told him the finals were the same week as the trip we'd planned, the train tickets we'd bought together to a town not unlike this one, and how I'd said *after, I promise, after the prize* — and how we had both known, even then, that *after* was a country I never intended to visit.

"I meant it," I said. "At the time, I meant it."

"I know you did. That was the problem." He drank his coffee, looking out the window at the ruined white harbor. "You always meant it right up until the moment choosing cost you something."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Fairness was never really our thing. We were better at scores."

And there it was — the old fluency between us, the shorthand of two people who had spent their formative years keeping accounts. It would have been easier if he'd shouted. Shouting I could answer. But he just stood there being reasonable and wounded and *familiar*, and the worst part, the part I would never say aloud, was that every word of my speech was true. I did have a life. It was a good life, an enviable life, a life other cellists described with a particular tightness around the eyes. It simply had no room in it for a man who repaired boats in a dying coastal town and wouldn't touch a cello, and I could no more shrink myself into that room than I could—

Than I could what? Play from memory without fear? Love someone without keeping an exit mapped?

"You're doing it again," Rafael said.

"Doing what."

"The thing where you argue with yourself behind your eyes and lose." He set down the mug. "Look. Forget the speech. I only came in to ask if you wanted to walk up to the road and see if the plows have been through. Maren thinks tomorrow, maybe. If they clear it, you can be in Vienna by Thursday and none of this ever has to survive contact with daylight."

The offer was kind. That was the unbearable thing — he was offering me the exit, wrapped neatly, no recriminations, and some starved animal part of me wanted to refuse it so violently that my hands shook around the mug.

"Yes," I said. "Good. That's — yes. Sensible."

"Sensible," he echoed, and almost smiled, and the almost was worse than the real thing.

* * *

We didn't end up walking to the road. The wind came around to the north while we laced our boots and turned the snow to horizontal shrapnel, and Maren, returning with news of burst pipes and a face like a satisfied prophet, pronounced the coast road "shut till Thursday, if the good Lord's willing, and He's been sulky all winter." So the exit stayed shut, and the day stretched out ahead of us like a sentence neither of us knew how to serve.

I hid in my room for an hour with my phone pressed to my ear, taking the call I'd known was coming. Daniela's voice came through crisp and faintly distorted, all agenda, no weather.

"There you are. Finally. The festival people confirmed your dates, and I need you to look at the exclusivity rider before you sign anything — two years, minimum, touring season plus festival commitments, it's the best package you've ever been offered, Isolde, this is the summit, this is *it*. Where are you even? Your location says coastal Maine, which is either a typo or a cry for help."

"A scouting trip," I said. "For the festival venue."

"Mm. And the storm?"

"Scouting it as well."

She laughed her corporate laugh and moved on to rehearsal schedules, and I stood at the window holding the phone and looking down at the yard, where Rafael was chopping wood with steady, economical strokes, breath steaming, and I thought: *two years*. Two years of trains and hotels and green rooms, of playing beautifully to halls full of strangers, of being exactly the woman I had built at such cost. It sounded, for the first time in my life, less like a summit and more like a very well-appointed exile.

"—Isolde? Are you hearing any of this?"

"Every word," I lied, at speed, my best register.

After the call I found the sitting room again because there was nowhere else to be, and he was there, of course — Rafael, at the window seat, doing something with his hands. Not idle. Never idle; his hands had always needed work the way mine needed music. He was carving, I realized. A small shape emerging from pale wood under a knife that moved with the same care he used to give to bow strokes.

"What is it?" I asked, before I could stop myself.

"A boat. Eventually. Right now it's mostly optimism." He glanced up. "There's tea. Maren restocked before the storm, God knows why, she must have known we'd need fortifying."

I poured tea I didn't want and sat across the room from him, at the maximum distance the furniture allowed, and we performed civility at each other for the better part of an hour — weather, Maren, the structural integrity of old inns — two virtuosi playing the easiest piece in the repertoire, *Small Talk for Two Survivors*, andante, con dolore.

But the silence between phrases kept filling up with last night. Every time our eyes met and skated away, I felt the kiss again, ghost-pressure, a note ringing after the string has been damped. And I caught him watching my hands once — my hands, wrapped around the teacup, moving the way they do when I'm thinking in music, conducting some private phrase against the air — and something crossed his face that I recognized with a sick lurch, because I'd seen it in mirrors for years.

Longing. The specific, hopeless longing of a musician looking at an instrument he can't play.

"Your hands," I said, before wisdom could stop me. "Do they still—"

"They're fine," he said, too quickly, and stood, and began stacking firewood that didn't need stacking. "Fine for boat work, anyway. Different job. Different requirements."

The lie was bad and we both knew it, and I understood suddenly that whatever had happened to him — whatever had ended the career that was supposed to be *his* summit, the career I'd spent a decade telling myself I'd won fairly — was still in the room with us, dust-sheeted, like the furniture in the closed wing of this inn. Like something in his own closet, maybe. Something shaped like a cello.

I should have let it lie. The truce we'd drafted said no past, no festival, no cello, and I had signed it as readily as I'd ever signed anything.

But I had woken up this morning and lied at speed to a man who could hear it, and defended a life that had started sounding, somewhere between the payphone and the plum brandy, like a very long performance of a piece I no longer believed in — and I was tired, tired in the marrow, tired the way you get tired of your own playing when you can no longer hear whether it's true.

"Rafael," I said. "Last night, when you said I always meant it until it cost me something. You're right. And I hate that you're right, and I'm going to spend the rest of the day pretending otherwise, but I need you to know that I know."

He went still, knife resting on the half-carved hull.

"And," I continued, because momentum was the only thing keeping me honest, "you do it too. Whatever happened to your hands — whatever you're not saying — you've been rehearsing *your* speech for ten years. So maybe we're even. Maybe we're just two liars with excellent technique, stuck in an inn until Thursday."

A long moment. Snow ticked against the glass. Then he laughed — short, unwilling, real — and it did something to my chest that no review had ever managed.

"Two liars with excellent technique," he said. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in years."

He sat back down. Closer, this time. Not touching. The distance of a decision not yet made.

And that was when I saw it — through the door of his room, left ajar behind him, at the edge of my vision: a tall shape under a dust sheet, standing in the corner like a guest who'd been asked to wait. The right height. The wrong silence.

I looked away too late. He saw me looking.
