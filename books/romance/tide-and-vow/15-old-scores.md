## 15. Old Scores

There are three kinds of torture I know personally: the first is a seized hand in front of two thousand people, the second is watching the woman you love walk away twice, and the third — which I discovered on a Saturday night in December, standing in a ballroom full of people who used to know my name — is being pitied by a man in a good suit.

The festival had decanted itself from the church hall into the Harborview Hotel's function room for its opening-night reception. Someone had strung fairy lights along the windows in a valiant attempt to make the place look festive rather than municipal. There was a string quartet playing Vivaldi at conversational volume, which is the correct volume for Vivaldi if you ask me, and there were canapés on trays, and there was Isolde Marchetti across the room in a dress the color of deep water, laughing at something the mayor's wife had said.

She'd asked me to come. That was the thing. She hadn't tricked me or maneuvered me; she'd stood in Maren's sitting room with her coat already on and said, plainly, *I don't want to walk into that room alone*, and I had looked at her — this woman who could fill any hall on earth without breaking stride — and understood that she meant it. So I put on the blazer I use for funerals and boat inspections, and I came.

For forty minutes it was almost fine. People nodded at me with the careful neutrality of those who have been briefed. A cellist from the youth orchestra asked me about rosin and I gave an answer that was actually about rosin. Isolde's hand found mine once behind a potted ficus, brief and warm, a semaphore only we could read: *still here, still us.*

Then Anton Reval spotted me.

You have to understand who Anton Reval is. He's a conductor of the second tier who has spent thirty years convincing himself he's first, and his particular genius is cruelty delivered as charm — a compliment with a fishhook in it, a question shaped like a knife. He conducted the youth orchestra when Isolde and I were nineteen. He judged competitions. He once told me, after I won the Sibelius, that my Elgar had "real promise, if the hands hold." Even then he said things like that. Even then, everyone laughed.

He crossed the room toward me with a glass of white wine and the smile of a man arriving at a buffet.

"Rafael Whitlock." He said my name like he was reading a headstone. "Good God. They let you out of the boatyard."

"Anton."

"I read about the festival. Thought, now there's a town with loyalty." He sipped. "How are the hands? Still —" and here he made a small gesture, fingers curling and uncurling like something dying, "— temperamental?"

The room did not go silent. That's the detail I keep returning to. The quartet kept playing, the canapés kept circulating, maybe six people nearby went very carefully still, and the rest of the world continued, indifferent, because my ruin was not actually news anymore. It had stopped being an event years ago and become a fact, the way weather becomes climate.

"They work," I said. "Boats need them."

"Mm. Practical." His eyes did a slow tour of me — the blazer, the calluses, the whole biography written on my body — and I felt every year of the last decade stack up between us like sandbags. "You know, I always wondered what happened that night. The Elgar. One minute you're the future of the instrument, the next —" he snapped his fingers softly, "— nothing. Careers have died of less. Most die of exactly that, actually." He smiled wider. "But then, you always did have a certain fragility. Beautiful tone, no spine. I said so at the time."

"You said I played like someone apologizing," I said. "I remember."

"Do you? How flattering. Memory is the musician's curse." He leaned in slightly, lowering his voice to a register that carried anyway, the way it always had. "Tell me honestly — do you ever sit in the audience and watch Isolde play and think, *that should have been me*? Because everyone else thinks it. We all do. It's the saddest story of our generation, Rafael. The great Whitlock hands, ruined before they —"

"Anton."

Isolde's voice arrived before she did, but not by much. She came through the crowd like weather changing — I watched people physically reorient around her — and she planted herself between us with the calm of someone who has faced down worse than a conductor with a grudge. She had, in fact. She'd faced down me.

"I'm sorry," she said pleasantly, "were you mid-sentence? Please finish. I'd love to hear how a man who hasn't premiered anything since 2011 diagnoses spines."

A flicker of genuine surprise crossed Reval's face, quickly converted to delight. "Isolde. Magnificent as ever. I was just telling Rafael how much we all —"

"You were telling him his career's death was the most interesting thing about him," she said. "Yes. I heard. Here's what I remember, Anton, since memory is the musician's curse: I remember you telling a nineteen-year-old girl before the Tchaikovsky final that her temperament was 'almost masculine enough' for the concerto. I remember you telling Rafael his Elgar had promise *if the hands held* — planting that sentence in him three weeks before he walked onstage, so it would be sitting there waiting. You didn't judge competitions. You haunted them." Her voice never rose. That was the terrifying part. It got quieter and more precise, like water finding the crack. "And I remember that the finest cellist of our generation is standing in front of you repairing boats because men like you made a stage feel like a trap, and you're calling *him* fragile."

The quartet, God help them, had drifted into something slow and mournful, underscoring her like a film score. Sixteen people were now pretending not to listen and failing completely.

Reval recovered with a conductor's instinct for the dramatic pause. "Passionate," he said. "Well. The festival chose its headliner wisely." He raised his glass to me — to *me*, the toast of a dead man — and moved off into the crowd, leaving a wake of murmurs.

And I stood there in the silence he left behind, and I did the worst thing I have done in ten years, which is saying something, because I've done plenty.

What I felt, in that moment, was not gratitude. I want to be honest about that, even though honesty costs me. What I felt was the specific horror of being seen — of having my damage held up under the ballroom lights by the one person whose eyes I couldn't bear it in. Everyone else could pity me; pity from strangers slides off. But Isolde had just told sixteen people, in a voice like cut glass, that I was broken. She'd defended me the way you defend something wounded, and wounded things don't get to be mysterious anymore. Wounded things get looked at.

Ten years ago, in a train station I still dream about, I told her I couldn't run away with her because I had a final to win. What I actually meant — what I've never said out loud until this storm, until this winter — was that I couldn't bear for her to watch me fail at becoming great. And here she was, ten years later, watching me fail in public, defending the failure, loving the failed man, and every eye in that room was on both of us, and I could feel the story assembling itself in their heads: *the tragic cellist and the woman who stayed.*

I would rather have been invisible.

"That wasn't necessary," I said.

She turned. The fight was still in her face, the bright furious shine of it, and it faltered when she heard my tone. "What?"

"That speech. It wasn't necessary."

"It was entirely necessary. He was —"

"He was saying what everyone thinks. Fine. Let him." I picked up a glass of wine from a passing tray mostly to have something to do with my hands. My hands. Always my hands. "You stood up in front of half the festival and announced I'm damaged goods. Thank you very much."

Something closed in her expression — not hurt yet, but the door swinging toward it. "That is not what I did."

"It's what happened. Now everyone in this room knows the great Isolde Marchetti is traveling with the cautionary tale." I heard myself doing it, heard the old machinery engage, the part of me that pushes first so I can't be pushed. "You should be in there talking to presenters, not fencing with Anton Reval over my corpse."

"Your corpse," she repeated. "Rafael. Look at me."

"I am looking at you."

"No, you're performing looking at me. There's a difference, and I know it intimately, because I invented it." She stepped closer, lowered her voice. "Whatever just happened in your head, whatever story you're telling yourself about what my defense cost you — it isn't true. I defended you because he was cruel and because I was angry, and because nobody gets to talk about your hands like they're a punchline. Not him. Not anyone."

"And that's the problem," I said. "You think they're yours to defend."

The words landed. I watched them land. Her face went very still, the way a sea goes still before it decides what it's going to do.

"Say that again," she said quietly.

"Isolde —"

"*Say it again.* Say it slowly, so we both hear it."

I should have apologized. Any version of me worth anything would have apologized. Instead I doubled down, because shame is a coward and cowards attack. "You spent ten years outrunning me. You built a career on beating a ghost. And now that the ghost lives in your guest room, you want to stand guard over him in ballrooms. I don't need a defender, Isolde. I especially don't need one who —"

"Who what?" Her voice cracked, finally, the precision failing. "Finish the sentence. Who loves you? Who sat on a frozen dock and told you the truth about the Tchaikovsky final while you held my hands? Who tore up a contract clause for you?" Her eyes were wet and furious and I deserved every degree of it. "You know what the difference between us is, Rafael? When I'm afraid, I run toward. When you're afraid, you burn the bridge and call it architecture."

The quartet finished their piece. The applause was thin and polite. Somewhere near the window, Reval was laughing at something, and the sound reached me like a verdict.

"I need some air," I said.

"Of course you do," she said. "Go. I'll be here, being seen with you. Apparently that's the crime."

I went. Past the fairy lights, past the ficus, past the mayor's wife who wouldn't meet my eye, out through the double doors into the hotel lobby and then through the side exit into the night, where the cold hit my face like a slap I'd been asking for.

* * *

The harbor in December is honest in a way ballrooms aren't. Wind off the water, masts knocking against each other like nervous teeth, the lighthouse sweeping its slow white arm across everything. I walked to the end of the dock — our dock, the planning-to-run-away dock, though I couldn't think about that tonight without bleeding — and I stood there hating myself with real craftsmanship.

Because here's what I knew, standing in the dark with my hands jammed in my pockets: she had been magnificent. She had taken the cruelest man in my history apart in front of witnesses, for me, without hesitation, and my response had been to punish her for it. Not because she was wrong. Because she was right, and rightness aimed at me feels like exposure, and exposure feels like the Elgar, and the Elgar feels like dying.

Reval had said *fragile*. And the unforgivable thing, the thing I'll carry, is that for one hot second in that ballroom I'd agreed with him — agreed so hard that I'd proven it, performed it, handed him the demonstration. *Beautiful tone, no spine.* I'd shown Isolde exactly which parts of me were still rotten.

My phone buzzed. A text from Maren: *Saw the whole thing from the coat check. Come home when you're done being an idiot. There's soup.*

I almost laughed. Almost.

Behind me, footsteps on the dock boards. I knew the rhythm before I turned — I'd have known it in a crowd of thousands, I'd have known it deaf. Isolde came down the dock with her coat unbuttoned and her heels in her hand, barefoot on frozen planks in December because she'd apparently decided dignity was a problem for spring, and she stopped an arm's length away, and the lighthouse swept over us and lit her face for one full second: tear-streaked, furious, and more stubborn than the sea.

"The inn is locked," she said. "Maren gave me the spare key and told me to tell you there's soup, and also that you're an idiot, though I suspect she already texted you that part herself."

"She did."

"Good. Then I don't have to." She set her heels down on the planks with a click. "I'm not going to apologize for defending you. Ever. If you want an apology for that, you'll wait past the end of the world."

"I don't want an apology."

"What do you want, Rafael? Actually. Name it. Because I've watched you want everything except the thing in front of you for two weeks now, and I'm running out of ways to stand still while you do it."

The lighthouse arm came around again. In the sweep of light I saw my own hands, hanging useless at my sides, and hers, clenched at hers, and the water black and moving beneath us both.

What I wanted was to say *you*. What I said was:

"I want to not be the saddest story of our generation."

It came out rawer than I intended, stripped of all the sarcasm I usually wrap these things in, and she flinched like I'd struck her.

"So you push away the one person who doesn't see you that way," she said. "Brilliant. Truly, the strategy of a genius." She bent, picked up her shoes, straightened. "I'm going back to the inn. Come home or don't. But decide soon, Rafael, because the ferry leaves tomorrow, and I am done choosing pieces of you. All of you or none. Last offer."

She walked back up the dock, barefoot, unhurried, and did not look back, which is how I knew she meant it.

I stood alone at the end of the pier with the wind coming in off the black water, and somewhere behind me in the hotel, Anton Reval was probably still laughing, and somewhere ahead of me the ferry terminal sat dark and waiting, and in my chest the oldest fear of all — the one older than the Elgar, older than her — turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

Tomorrow the last ferry of the season departed at noon.

And I had until then to decide whether I was a man who repaired boats, or a man who could still repair himself.
