## 2. A Stranger with Good Posture

The rain found Ulyana Marsh eleven minutes after she arrived in Alder Bay, as if it had been waiting for her specifically, holding its breath behind a cloud.

She stood on the porch of her grandmother's house with the key in her hand and her suitcase going dark with water, and she thought: *Well. That's about right.*

The house smelled the way it always had—cedar and lavender and something faintly like old paper—which was remarkable, considering Margaret Marsh had been dead for six weeks and the house shut up for four of them. Ulyana set her suitcase in the hall, dripped onto the welcome mat (WELCOME, in needlepoint, frayed at the S), and felt the particular loneliness of a person standing inside a dead woman's kitchen.

There were notes everywhere. Her grandmother had been a note-leaver. Notes on the fridge: *Buy more of the good honey, the clear kind.* Notes tucked into books. A note on the bathroom mirror that said only: *Stand up straight.*

Ulyana caught her own reflection doing exactly that—spine straight, chin level, the posture of a woman who had been corrected too many times as a child to ever fully relax—and laughed once, a short, unamused sound.

"Six weeks," she told the empty house. "You couldn't have waited six more weeks to die? I had a life."

Her aunt Denise would say that was a terrible thing to joke about. Aunt Denise had opinions about everything, which was why Ulyana had flown across two time zones partly to settle the estate and partly to be somewhere Denise wasn't. Unfortunately, Denise had announced—by email, three exclamation points—that she would be arriving Thursday to "help," by which she meant supervise, critique, and eventually suggest that the house be sold to a developer who would "do something useful with the lot."

Ulyana had four days of freedom left.

She spent the first one cataloguing the linen closet, because grief is strange and grief does what it wants, and by late afternoon she needed out of that house the way a swimmer needs air. She grabbed an umbrella that turned inside out within thirty seconds, surrendered to the rain, and walked down toward the water because her feet seemed to remember the town even if the rest of her didn't.

That was how she came to be standing in front of Halloran's Books, dripping, at 4:47 on a Tuesday afternoon.

* * *

The shop was narrow and deep and smelled like every good decision Ulyana's grandmother had ever made. Shelves ran floor to ceiling, slightly bowed under the weight of their own convictions. A cat—or possibly a large opinionated dust mop—was asleep in the window display, curled around a stack of poetry collections as though guarding them from theft.

Behind the counter stood a man with dark hair going silver at the temples, reading. He looked up when the door chimed, took in her drowned state, and said, without any discernible emotion:

"There's a towel on the radiator. The blue one. It's clean."

"You keep towels for drowning customers?"

"I keep towels for Poppy. She considers puddles a personal invitation." He nodded toward the radiator. "Blue one."

Ulyana retrieved it. It was small and soft from washing and smelled faintly of something floral—bubble bath, maybe, or a child's shampoo—and she dried her hair with it while the man watched her with the wary expression of someone who had learned that strangers in his shop usually wanted something.

"I'm not buying anything," she warned him. "I'm hiding."

"From the rain?"

"From my aunt. The rain was incidental." She looked around. "This place used to be bigger."

"It used to be a hardware store next door. My grandfather bought both in '62 and knocked through. Then the hardware half became a laundromat, then a phone repair place, then nothing." He said this the way other people recite weather. "Now it's just us."

"Us being you and the cat."

"The cat's called Dostoevsky. He doesn't pull his weight."

Despite herself—the long flight, the dead grandmother, the wet shoes squelching—Ulyana felt something in her chest loosen by a single degree. She drifted along the nearest shelf, running a finger over spines, and stopped when she found a section labeled LOCAL INTEREST in her grandmother's handwriting. Of course it was. Of course Margaret Marsh had known this man, or his father, or someone, because her grandmother had known everyone in this town and had apparently kept meticulous records of it in other people's shops.

"Do you remember a Margaret Marsh?" Ulyana asked. "Lived on Gullwing Lane. Tall. Terrifying posture. Wrote letters like they were going out of style, because they were."

Something crossed the man's face—quick, unreadable, gone. "Everyone knew Margaret."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." He came around the counter, and up close she could see he was younger than the gray suggested, maybe early forties, with tired eyes and the general bearing of a man who had once been handsome and had decided to stop keeping up with it. "Wren Halloran. I own the shop."

"Own," Ulyana repeated, tasting the word. "Bold claim in this economy."

"My mother asks me the same question weekly. Usually louder." He glanced at the window, where the rain had committed fully to its bit. "You'll want to wait it out. It does this for hours sometimes. Comes off the bay sideways."

"So I'm trapped here."

"Trapped," he agreed, "in a bookshop. Tragic. There's tea, if you don't mind it being slightly terrible."

"I mind all tea that isn't slightly terrible. The good stuff makes me suspicious."

He almost smiled. It was a near thing—she watched him get most of the way there and then think better of it—but it counted, and she found herself absurdly pleased to have caused it.

* * *

They drank the terrible tea at a small table near the back, beneath a shelf of maritime disasters, which Ulyana pointed out was poor feng shui. Wren said the shipwrecks were load-bearing. Poppy, he explained when she asked, was his daughter, eight years old, currently at a friend's house, currently the entire organizing principle of his existence. His wife, he did not explain, but the absence sat beside him at the table like a third guest, and Ulyana—who had grown up watching her grandmother carry her own silences around like luggage—recognized it and didn't poke at it.

Instead she asked about the shop, and he talked about it the way some men talk about a lost love: inventory that wouldn't sell, inventory that sold too fast, the school visit he did monthly, the order form he still filled out by hand because the computer and he had "an understanding" that mostly involved the computer winning.

It was nearly six when he said it.

Later, Ulyana would replay the moment many times, searching for the exact mechanism of it, and never find one. There was no segue. One minute they were arguing about whether *Jane Eyre* was a romance or a hostage situation, and the next, Wren Halloran set down his cup, went slightly pink, and said:

"This is going to sound insane."

"Those are usually my favorite sentences."

"The Halloran reunion is Saturday. My family. All of them. My mother's announcing—" He stopped, restarted. "She's selling the building. This building. Out from under me, basically, and she wants everyone there to watch me smile about it, and I can't face them alone. Not this year." He exhaled. "So I need a date. A fake one. Someone who will stand next to me, look convincing, and leave before dessert."

Ulyana stared at him.

To be fair to Wren, he stared back, looking increasingly like a man who wished the tide would come in and take him.

"You're proposing," she said slowly, "that I—a woman you met ninety minutes ago, whom you suspected of shoplifting until roughly the tea—attend your family reunion as your girlfriend."

"When you put it like that—"

"How else would I put it?"

"A mutually beneficial arrangement?" He rubbed his jaw. "Look. You said you're hiding from your aunt. If you're with me Saturday, you're unavailable. Family obligation. Very respectable. Nobody can argue with it. And I get—" He gestured vaguely at himself. "A witness. Someone who makes me look like a functioning adult who has moved forward with his life."

"And what do I get besides escape from my aunt?"

"Free books."

Ulyana raised an eyebrow. "I'm settling my grandmother's estate. I could buy this building's worth of books."

"Fine. Terrible tea, indefinitely. And my undying gratitude, which I'm told is worthless, but it's what I've got."

Here is the thing Ulyana could not explain, then or later: every sensible part of her—the part that had built a careful life in another city, the part that filed things and followed rules and stood up straight—said *no, absolutely not, this man is grieving and desperate and you are a stranger with good posture and no business playing pretend in a dead woman's town.*

But another part of her, a part she'd quieted for years, looked at this rumpled, honest, catastrophically awkward man and thought: *he needs help lying to his family, and I need help lying to mine, and we are clearly two people who take honesty very seriously indeed.*

"One condition," she heard herself say.

"Name it."

"We agree on a story beforehand. How we met, how long, all of it. I refuse to improvise in front of hostile relatives. I've done dinner theater; I know how it ends."

"Done." He stuck out his hand, then hesitated. "Wait. What do I call you?"

"Ulyana Marsh. Margaret's granddaughter."

His hand paused midair for half a heartbeat—she saw it, filed it away, said nothing—and then they shook, his grip warm and dry and slightly too formal, like a treaty being signed.

"Pleasure," he said. "Fake girlfriend."

"Temporary acquaintance," she corrected. "Let's not get ahead of ourselves."

* * *

She walked home after eight, the rain finished, the streets shining. At the corner of Gullwing Lane she stopped and realized she was smiling—not the polite, postured smile she wore for aunts and airline staff, but a real one, crooked and unauthorized.

Four days until Denise arrived. Two days until the reunion. Somewhere in between, she'd have to invent a love story with a stranger, and somehow the prospect felt less like a chore than like permission.

Inside, the house smelled of cedar and lavender. On the bathroom mirror, her grandmother's note waited: *Stand up straight.*

"Not tonight, Grandma," Ulyana murmured, kicking off her wet shoes. "Tonight I'm hunching."

She fell asleep thinking about the way Wren's hand had paused at her name—half a heartbeat, no more—and woke at dawn with the odd certainty that there was something in this town her grandmother hadn't told her.

She went downstairs to start on the linen closet.
