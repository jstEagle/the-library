## 5. Poppy's Library Card

The trouble with eight-year-olds, Wren had long ago concluded, was that they had been alive just long enough to develop opinions and not nearly long enough to learn when to keep them.

Case in point: Tuesday afternoon, four o'clock, the dead hour between school pickup and dinner, when Alder Bay's only bookshop held exactly one customer—a retired fisherman browsing the nautical histories he never bought—and Poppy Halloran sat cross-legged on the counter doing her homework, which was technically arithmetic and was actually surveillance.

"Dad."

"Mm." He was re-shelving a returned copy of *Anne of Green Gables* that some monster had dog-eared. He took dog-earing personally.

"Miss Marsh came in again today."

"She did."

"That's three times this week."

"Four," said Mr. Okonkwo from the history section, without looking up, because retired fishermen apparently kept score too. "She bought the poetry on Monday. Came back Tuesday for the tea you don't sell. Today she just stood by the window reading for an hour and then left."

Wren slid Anne back into place with perhaps more force than necessary. "Thank you, Mr. Okonkwo."

"Happy to help."

Poppy set down her pencil with the deliberate care of a lawyer producing evidence. "Dad. If it's all pretend—your words, by the way, I heard you tell Uncle Dev it was 'a business arrangement'—then why does she keep coming back? Business arrangements have, like, meetings. This is just her being here."

He considered several answers. *Because we're still rehearsing*, which was thin. *Because she likes the shop*, which was true but led down roads he wasn't ready to map. *Because I asked her to come back and she said yes before I finished asking*, which was the actual truth and therefore unusable.

"Adults are complicated," he said, which was the universal dad answer, the Swiss Army knife of deflection.

Poppy looked at him the way his mother looked at quarterly statements. "You're blushing."

"I am not."

"You're doing the thing where your ears go red. Mom used to say—" She stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, and something crossed her face that was too old for eight, too quick to catch. Then she shrugged, eight again. "Can I get a library card?"

The pivot was so smooth he almost missed it. Almost.

"A library card," he repeated.

"For the actual library. In Port Mercer. Everyone at school has one. I want to read things you don't have."

Wren pressed a hand to his chest as though she'd shot him. "Betrayed. In my own shop."

"You have three copies of *Charlotte's Web* and they're all yours from when you were little and one has a juice stain shaped like Australia."

"That stain is part of its provenance."

"Dad."

He laughed, and the laugh did what laughs do—it papered over the crack she'd opened with that unfinished sentence about her mother. But the crack stayed open after she went back to her arithmetic, and Wren stood there among his shelves feeling the particular ache of a father who knows his daughter is carrying questions she's decided not to ask him yet.

She'd stopped mid-sentence about Claire. His wife. Poppy almost never mentioned her mother unprompted anymore—not since the year of the quiet, when grief had made the house so heavy they'd both learned to walk softly through it. And now here she was, testing the water with one toe: *Mom used to say.* Watching him to see if the water was safe.

It should be safe. He wanted it to be safe. He just didn't know how to make it safe while his ears were going red over a woman who'd been in town eleven days.

* * *

That night, after Poppy was asleep and the shop was locked and the kettle had gone cold beside him untouched, Wren climbed to the attic.

He told himself it was for the Halloween boxes. It was October; the lie was seasonally appropriate. But his hands went past the Halloween boxes, past the Christmas lights coiled like sleeping snakes, to the cedar chest in the corner where he kept the things he couldn't look at and couldn't throw away.

Claire's journals. Seven of them. He'd read them all in the first terrible year, hunting for her voice anywhere he could find it, and then he'd put them away because her voice in ink turned out to hurt differently than her voice in memory—more honest, less forgiving of the version of him he'd built.

He hadn't opened them in four years.

He took out the third one—the one from their second year together, when everything had been new and possible—and carried it downstairs to the kitchen, telling himself he'd read a page or two and feel close to her and sleep fine.

He read forty pages. She'd been funny in a way he'd forgotten, sharp-edged and silly, writing about his terrible parking and his worse singing and the way he talked to books while shelving them ("he apologizes to the ones he puts spine-in; I married a man who apologizes to books"). He laughed out loud alone in his kitchen, and then the laugh caught on something, the way a sleeve catches on a nail.

A name.

Not his name. Not any name he knew from her stories of college or childhood or the years before him. Just a first name, dropped into an entry dated the spring before they met:

*Saw E tonight. Of course I did. I always know exactly which street corner to avoid and then go stand on it anyway. Some loves you get over. Some you just get better at hiding. I'm very good now. Nobody can tell.*

E.

He turned pages backward, forward, looking for more. There were two other mentions, months apart, both equally oblique—one about a letter she'd written and burned ("better in the fire than in my head"), one just a line that stopped his heart cold in the quiet kitchen:

*I chose this. I choose him. That has to be enough, and most days it is.*

Most days.

Wren sat in his kitchen with his dead wife's handwriting swimming in front of him and felt the floor tilt gently under everything he'd believed. For seven years he had built his guilt on a simple architecture: she died, and he hadn't been enough, and if he'd been more—if he'd noticed the tiredness sooner, argued less about money, been a bigger man—she would still be here. The guilt had a shape. It fit him like an old coat.

But this—*some loves you just get better at hiding*—this didn't fit anywhere. This suggested that Claire had carried something into their marriage and set it down carefully in a room he'd never been shown, and had lived beside it, and chosen him anyway, *most days*, and he had never once thought to wonder what she might be hiding, because he'd been too busy assuming he was the problem.

Grief loosened its grip just enough to hurt differently. Not the old wound—the raw, screaming absence—but a newer, stranger pain, the pain of meeting someone you loved after they're gone and realizing you didn't know everything about them. That there had been rooms in Claire Voss—Claire Halloran—that he'd never entered, and now the doors were closed forever, and somewhere in the dark behind them was a person named E.

He thought absurdly of Ulyana standing by his front window for an hour, reading, saying nothing. Of Poppy's unfinished sentence. Of all the things people carry and don't say.

He got up, found a pen, and wrote on a scrap of paper—the kind of note you leave yourself when you're afraid morning will talk you out of it:

*E. Find out who E was. Not to pry. To know her whole.*

Then he looked at the note and understood, with a tired clarity, that it wasn't prying he was afraid of. It was what he might find. Because if Claire had loved someone else and chosen him anyway, then the story he'd told himself for seven years—I wasn't enough—was wrong. And he didn't know who he was without that story. It had held up the whole sad architecture of his quiet life.

Outside, rain started against the window, soft and steady, the same rain that had driven a stranger with good posture into his shop two weeks ago. He thought of her grandmother's house on Marsh Lane, dark now, full of another dead woman's unsaid things.

Everyone, he was beginning to suspect, was a bundle of letters they never sent.

Upstairs, Poppy slept with her library application form half-filled on her desk—she'd filled in every field except one, and in the morning she'd ask him for help with it, and he'd realize she'd left blank the box marked *Parent/Guardian Signature*, waiting, in her sharp-eyed way, to see if he was paying attention.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight, Wren Halloran sat in his kitchen until two in the morning, holding a journal he'd hidden from himself, learning that his wife had once stood on a street corner she could have avoided—and wondering, with a chill that had nothing to do with October, why the name Ulyana Marsh had sounded familiar the very first time he'd heard it.
