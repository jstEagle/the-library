## 1. The Muscle of the Heart

The bar at the Corvus Hotel had been designed by someone who believed that wealth should feel like weather — all low amber light and soft pressure, the kind of room where voices dropped without being asked. Rosalind Voss had been coming to rooms like this for eleven years, long enough to know that the lighting was never accidental, that the prices were a form of etiquette, that everyone in it was performing some version of themselves they'd rehearsed in a mirror or, in her case, on behalf of someone else.

She sat at the far end of the bar with a glass of Sancerre she wasn't drinking, and watched Auguste Lindqvist make a publisher fall in love with him.

It was, she thought, like watching a pianist perform a piece you'd written. There was the familiar opening — the self-deprecating laugh, the hand pressed briefly to his chest as if the charm cost him something. *I was a terrible father, Marguerite will tell you herself, if you can get her on the phone, which no one can.* Then the pivot, the one she'd built for him over three drafts: the story of the soup kitchen in Marseille, told not as charity but as appetite. *I went in to eat, I swear to God, I was broke and arrogant and twenty-three, and I stayed because an old woman told me my hands were wasted on nothing.* The publisher — a compact woman named Deborah Fenn with reading glasses pushed up into her hair like a crown she kept forgetting — was leaning forward now, both elbows on the table, doing the thing publishers did when they'd already decided and were simply waiting for the paperwork to justify them.

Rosalind took a sip of wine to give her mouth something to do besides smile.

*An old woman told me my hands were wasted on nothing.* She had invented that woman. Not out of malice — Auguste had actually eaten at the soup kitchen, that much was true, she'd verified it twice — but because *hunger* was a better word than *poverty*, and a specific old woman was better than a general kindness. The truth was a rough stone. Her job was cutting. People imagined ghostwriting was transcription. It wasn't. It was surgery. You opened a life up, removed what didn't serve, closed it neatly, and left no scar that showed under the lights.

Auguste caught her eye across the room — just for half a second, a flicker, the private signal they'd developed during the proposal tour — and then turned back to Deborah with his full attention, which was considerable. He was sixty-one and looked fifty, silver at the temples in a way that seemed less like aging than like seasoning. His hands, resting on the table, were enormous and scarred from thirty years of burners and blades, and he used them when he talked the way other men used their whole bodies.

He was saying the line about his mother now. Rosalind felt her own chest tighten slightly, the way it always did at that line, because it was the only thing in the entire proposal she hadn't touched. *My mother cooked for twelve children on a stove the size of a suitcase, and she never once said the word sacrifice, so I am not going to say it either.* That was pure Auguste, unedited, given to her in a taxi on the way to Heathrow while he ate a sandwich and watched the motorway. She'd typed it into her phone at a red light and never changed a word. Even ghosts knew when a door was load-bearing.

Deborah laughed — the real laugh, not the professional one — and Rosalind knew the meeting was over in every way that mattered. The contract was a formality. It had been a formality since the auction, since the number came back with six zeros and her agent called her at seven in the morning to say, in a voice carefully stripped of greed, *Rosie. We're going to need a bigger boat.*

She finished the wine. She did not go over. Not yet. This was part of the discipline too — letting Auguste have the room, letting the warmth he generated be entirely his own, because the moment people started wondering where the words came from was the moment everything got fragile. A ghost's first job was absence.

* * *

They met afterward in the lift, by arrangement, riding up together like strangers who happened to share a floor.

"You were magnificent," Rosalind said.

"I was quoting you." Auguste examined himself in the mirrored wall, tugging his cuffs straight. "Chapter two. The Marseille bit. You've put the old woman back in."

"She earns her keep."

"She's fictional."

"She's *structural*," Rosalind said. "There's a difference. Everything around her is true. She just holds the true things up so they can be seen."

Auguste smiled at her reflection rather than at her, which she preferred; it meant he was thinking. "You know what my mother would have said about that? She'd have said a lie that carries truth is still carrying, and God forgives the porter." He turned. "You should write your own book, Rosalind. You say things like that and then you hide them inside me where nobody can find them."

"My name doesn't sell."

"Your sentences do. They're just wearing my cologne." The lift doors opened onto the twelfth floor. He held one with a scarred hand. "Dinner tomorrow? Marguerite's calling at nine, I'll want armor."

"Send me notes tonight. Voice memos. Anything about the year in Copenhagen — we haven't cracked that yet."

"The year in Copenhagen," Auguste repeated, with the weariness of a man being asked to reopen a wound he'd been assured was decorative. "Fine. Memos. But Rosalind—" He stopped, and for a moment the performance dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was tired and shrewd and oddly kind. "Whatever it is you're not putting in these books. One day you're going to have to. It leaks. I can smell it. All my life I've smelled what's missing from a dish."

The lift doors began to close between them. She held his gaze steadily, giving him the small, composed smile she'd practiced until it was indistinguishable from sincerity.

"Goodnight, Auguste."

* * *

Her suite was on the fourteenth floor, all pale wood and grey upholstery, a room designed for people who needed to feel nothing in particular. Rosalind stood at the window with the city spread out below her — the river a dark seam, the lights of Southwark smeared gold by the glass — and let the day drain out of her shoulders one floor at a time.

On the desk lay the contract. Deborah's assistant had couriered it up before dinner: heavy paper, blue tabs, her name printed in the party block as *Rosalind Voss (ghostwriter)*, the word itself sitting there in parentheses like a diagnosis. Beside it, her phone showed forty-one voice memos from Auguste, timestamped across three months, each one a vein of ore waiting to be cut and polished into somebody else's permanent record.

She picked up the pen.

This was the part she loved, if she was honest — and she permitted herself honesty only in this exact posture, alone, vertical, after midnight, looking at something beautiful she'd earned. The signing. The moment where chaos became text. Somewhere out there was a man's whole unruly life — the marriages, the bankruptcy, the daughter who wouldn't take his calls, the year he'd spent drinking in Copenhagen that the memoir would call *the apprenticeship* — and here was a document that would fold all of it into order. Two hundred and eighty pages. An index. A redemption arc with a beginning, a middle, and a forgiveness. The heart, properly trained, could sit still for a photograph. The heart, properly contained, could be sold in hardcover.

Her mother had died believing debts were a moral condition. Eleven years ago, in a hospice in Croydon that smelled of lilies and disinfectant, Eileen Voss had gripped Rosalind's wrist and said, *Don't let them take the house, love,* and Rosalind — twenty-four years old, unpublished, unemployable except as a waitress — had said yes, absolutely, whatever it takes, and then gone home and found the letters.

That was how she thought of it. *Found.* As though they'd been lost. Three years of correspondence with a stranger, conducted through a pen-pal service her grandmother had signed her up for as a joke — *you talk to plants and dead people, Rosie, talk to someone alive for once* — a stranger known to her only as **E.**, who wrote about music and orchards and his father's silences, and to whom she had confessed, in ink, on paper, everything. Every fear. Every debt. Every night she'd sat in the hospital corridor doing sums instead of praying. He had been the only person alive who knew her entire interior, and then, in the worst month of her life, the letters had simply stopped, and she had learned the final lesson of that decade: that the heart is a muscle, and muscles can be trained, and anything trained can be contained.

She signed her name. The pen made its small scratching sound, and the signature came out clean and confident, a stranger's confident, and she watched it dry.

*R. Voss,* in parentheses, inside someone else's book.

Her phone buzzed against the desk. She expected her agent, or Auguste's assistant, or the editor with a note about chapter structure. Instead it was an email from a firm of solicitors in Kent — Halloway & Prew, a name she didn't recognize, on letterhead she'd never seen. The subject line read: **Estate of Ulyana Voss — Re: Provision 4(b)**.

Rosalind stared at it for a long moment. Her grandmother had been dead fourteen months, mourned efficiently from a distance, buried on a wet Tuesday Rosalind had flown back for and flown away from within seventy-two hours. Whatever the estate had needed to settle, it should have settled by now.

She opened it.

*Dear Ms. Voss,* it began, in the courteous, bloodless prose of the profession, *we write further to our attempts to reach you via your literary agent...* — and there, in the fourth paragraph, in language so plain it took a second to detonate:

*...your late grandmother has bequeathed to you a one-half interest in Hollowmere Orchard, subject to the condition precedent, set out in clause 4(b) of her Will, that you reside upon the property for a continuous period of one full year...*

Rosalind set the phone down on the desk, face up, beside the signed contract, and looked at the two documents together — the career and the orchard, the containment and the condition — and felt, beneath the exhaustion and the champagne and the eleven years of disciplined control, something shift in her chest like a floorboard under weight.

One full year. In the middle of the biggest book of her life.

Outside, London glittered on, indifferent, and somewhere down the river a church bell she couldn't hear was striking one.
