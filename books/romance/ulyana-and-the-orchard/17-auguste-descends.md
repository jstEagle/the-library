## 17. Auguste Descends

The car was wrong for the village, and Auguste Lindqvist knew it the moment the driver turned off the A-road and the hedgerows began closing in like a crowd with opinions. A Bentley Flying Spur, gunmetal grey, twenty-two-inch wheels. He had asked for something discreet — "a Volvo, anything Swedish" — but his assistant Priya had booked what she'd booked, and arguing with Priya about cars was like arguing with a soufflé about falling. It had already made up its mind.

"Beautiful country," said the driver, whose name was Dennis and who had been silent for forty minutes in the way that only men hired by management companies can be silent.

"It is," Auguste agreed, and meant it, which surprised him. He pressed his forehead lightly to the cool of the window. Rolling fields, wet stone walls, sheep arranged on a hillside with the casual artistry of a still life. Somewhere beyond those hills was an orchard. *His* orchard now, in the way that a man who has never planted a tree can come to own one — through paper, through narrative, through the particular alchemy of a good story told at the right moment to the right publisher over too much Barolo.

He was fifty-eight years old, three Michelin stars deep into a career that had begun with a burnt omelette in a Malmö boarding house, and he was tired in a way that sleep did not touch. The memoir was supposed to fix that. That was what he'd told himself, anyway: write it all down, the fraud and the fury and the redemption, put it between covers, and maybe the shape of a life would finally hold still long enough to be looked at.

Rosalind would make it beautiful. Rosalind always made things beautiful. She had a way of listening — head tilted, pen still, those gray eyes giving you the whole of their attention — that made you say more than you meant to, and then she gave it back to you better than you'd said it. He had never once caught her taking notes during their sessions. She just absorbed him, the way flour absorbs butter, and produced something laminated and flaky and worth eating.

*She's wasted on me,* he thought, watching a heron lift out of a drainage ditch with prehistoric indignation. *She should be writing her own books.*

The thought arrived and departed, as such thoughts did, like weather.

* * *

The village announced itself with a sign — LOWER HOLLOWMERE, POP. 412, PLEASE DRIVE SLOWLY, WE MEAN IT — and then a single street of honey-colored stone, a church with a leaning spire, a pub called the Graft & Crown with chalkboard letters advertising tonight's pie ("venison, or whatever Dennis shot"), and a shop that appeared to sell eggs, wool, and, inexplicably, marine paint.

"Stop here," Auguste said. "I'll walk."

"You sure? The cottage is another—"

"I'm sure. Leave the bags at the cottage. And Dennis—" he leaned forward, catching the driver's eye in the mirror, "—if anyone asks, you're my nephew."

"I don't think anyone will ask, sir."

"They will," Auguste said cheerfully. "Everyone always asks. It's how villages work."

He got out. The air hit him first — cold, green, faintly sweet with rotting apples somewhere nearby — and beneath it something else, something mineral and old, the smell of earth that had been worked for centuries by people who hadn't been in a hurry. He stood on the pavement of the high street with his cashmere coat and his Milanese shoes and felt, briefly, like a man who had wandered onto someone else's stage without knowing his lines.

Then a woman emerged from the egg-and-paint shop, saw him, stopped dead, and said, "You're him. Off the telly."

"I'm him," Auguste admitted.

"My daughter's got your book. The bread one. She does the focaccia." A pause, appraising. "It comes out like a brick."

"The oven's probably too cool," Auguste said. "Tell her to let it run hot and trust the steam. And tell her the brick stage is normal. Everyone's first hundred loaves are bricks. Mine were paving slabs."

The woman considered this, decided it was acceptable, and nodded once — the nod of a village granting provisional entry — before continuing down the street with her paint.

Auguste smiled after her. This, he thought. *This* was the texture the book needed. You couldn't invent a woman who criticized your bread and forgave you in the same breath. Well — you could, but it would read as invented, and readers could smell invention the way dogs smelled fear.

Priya had arranged everything, because Priya arranged everything: two weeks in the gamekeeper's cottage on the Hollowmere estate, "immersion access" to the orchard, and — this had taken some doing, she'd mentioned, in the tone that meant it had taken lawyers — a meeting with the orchard's co-owner, one R. Voss, who lived in the late grandmother's house and was, per the estate solicitor, "the other party to the tenancy matter."

Tenancy matter. Auguste had skimmed that email at thirty thousand feet somewhere over Greenland and filed it under *local color*. There was a dispute about a boundary, apparently. Old deeds, old grievances, a tenant family named Cooper. In his experience every village had exactly one of these simmering, and it was always about either a fence line or a widow's will, and it was always older and more complicated than any outsider could usefully understand, which was precisely why outsiders kept poking at it.

He intended to poke gently. He wanted the orchard for its chapters — the grafting, the pressing, the frost nights, the whole liturgy of trees — not for its feuds. Feuds were for lesser memoirs. His needed apples and silence and the smell of woodsmoke.

He walked on, following the lane as it bent away from the village, past a field of winter kale, past a stand of bare hazels, until the land opened and there it was.

Hollowmere Orchard.

Even leafless — especially leafless — it took his breath. Rows and rows of old trees marching up a gentle slope, their branches black and gnarled against a sky the color of pewter, each tree shaped by decades of hands into something between architecture and calligraphy. At the top of the slope, a stone press house with a slate roof. Below, a stream running fast with recent rain. And everywhere the quiet — not silence, but quiet, the layered quiet of wind in bare wood and water over stones and, far off, a dog remarking on nothing.

"Oh," Auguste said aloud, to no one. "Oh, you lovely thing."

He had cooked in palaces. He had foraged with Sami reindeer herders above the Arctic Circle. He had once eaten a meal in Kyoto that had made him cry into his napkin and pretend it was the wasabi. But there was a specific register of beauty that only working landscapes had — beauty with labor in it — and Hollowmere Orchard was singing in that register, even in February, even half-asleep.

He was so busy being moved that he nearly walked into the fence.

It was a low wire fence, sagging where a post had rotted, marking the boundary between the orchard proper and a small cottage garden to his left. Beyond the garden stood a cottage — stone, slate, roses gone woody and wild over the door — and beyond the cottage, another house, larger, older, with light in its windows.

And in the cottage garden, a man was carrying a cello.

Not a guitar. Not a double bass. A cello, in its case, carried with the particular care of a man transporting a sleeping child, and the sight of it stopped Auguste mid-stride, because he had spent thirty years in kitchens listening to opera and he knew what a cello case meant about a person before he knew anything else about them.

The man set the case down inside the gate, straightened, and looked at Auguste across the fence with the expression of someone who had already identified the Bentley parked in the village and had drawn his conclusions.

"You're the chef," the man said.

"I'm a chef," Auguste said. "There are thousands of us. It's practically a plague."

"You're *the* chef. The one writing the book." The man's voice was level, pleasant even, but there was iron under it. "Elias Thorne. I live here now."

"Auguste Lindqvist." He extended a hand through the gap in the fence, and Elias Thorne shook it — briefly, correctly, the handshake of a man closing a transaction rather than opening one. "And yes, I'm writing the book. Though I confess I'm mostly here to be useful and stay out of the way. Do you play?" He nodded at the case.

A flicker crossed Thorne's face — something old and guarded moving behind the calm. "I used to."

"And now?"

"Now I grow apples." He said it like a sentence from a trial. *Now I grow apples, Your Honor.*

"That," Auguste said, "is the best trade I've ever heard of. May I ask an enormous favor?"

Thorne's eyebrows invited him to try.

"When I was young — properly young, seventeen, useless — I worked a season in an orchard in Skåne. Picking, pruning, hauling. Worst year of my life and I've thought about it every day since. I want to do it again. Properly, here, for the book but also—" he gestured vaguely at the slope of dark trees, "—for me. Would the orchard have me? I'm told there's a co-owner I should speak to. A Ms. Voss?"

Something happened to Thorne's face then. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a fractional stillness, the kind of thing Auguste had spent a career reading across a pass — but it happened, unmistakably, at the name.

"She's next door," Thorne said. "The big house. But she's busy. There's a dispute on. About the boundary." He glanced down at the sagging fence between them, and something almost wry moved in his mouth. "This fence, actually."

"This fence?"

"Half of it's hers. Half of it's mine. Neither half of us agrees on which half is which." He picked up the cello case. "Welcome to Lower Hollowmere, Mr. Lindqvist. Mind the mud."

He went inside and shut the door, politely and completely, the way you close a book you've decided not to finish.

Auguste stood a moment in the lane, mud on his shoes, heart oddly full. *What a character,* he thought, with the shameless appetite of his profession. *The cellist turned orchardist. Priya will weep with joy.* Then he corrected himself, because he was trying, these days, to be a better man in increments: *What a person,* he amended. *The cellist turned orchardist is a person.*

The distinction mattered. He was fairly sure it mattered. He'd have to ask Rosalind.

* * *

Rosalind Voss opened the door of the big house before he knocked, as if she'd been watching him come up the path — and perhaps she had; villages had eyes the way orchards had roots, everywhere and invisible.

"Auguste." Her face did the thing it always did: warmth arriving a half-second after assessment. "You found us."

"Dennis found us. I merely occupied the vehicle." He kissed the air near both her cheeks. "Rosalind. You look well. London doesn't suit you and I mean that as the highest compliment."

"Careful. Flattery before noon."

"It's never flattery when it's true. Is it true? Are you well?"

"I'm—" and there it was, that fractional pause, that recalculation behind the gray eyes, so brief no one but a ghostwriter would catch it, "—well enough. Busy. Come in, the fire's lit."

The house smelled of woodsmoke and old paper and, faintly, of apples in storage. Books everywhere, stacked in the honest disorder of a working mind. A desk by the window with a laptop closed upon it like a hand folded in prayer. On the wall, photographs: a stern grandmother, unmistakably the source of Rosalind's jawline; a younger Rosalind squinting in this very garden; a woman Auguste didn't recognize, ill and smiling anyway.

"Tea?" Rosalind asked.

"Desperately."

While she made it he drifted, as he always drifted in her houses, along the shelves. Cookbooks, obviously. Poetry, surprisingly. A whole row of notebooks with dates on their spines going back fifteen years, and he didn't touch them, because a man who read another writer's notebooks deserved to be read back.

"So," he said, when tea was in his hands and the fire was talking to itself. "The famous orchard. I walked part of it. Rosalind, it's extraordinary. Even now, even bare. I met a neighbor — Thorne, Elias Thorne — a cellist turned apple-grower, if you can believe our luck—"

"He's not luck," Rosalind said. Too quickly. Too flatly. "He's a neighbor."

Auguste paused with his cup halfway to his mouth. Twenty years of reading people across steel counters had taught him the weight of a pause, and this one had mass.

"A neighbor," he repeated, mildly. "Of course. And the boundary business — the fence, the Coopers — I gather it's rather fraught?"

"It's legal," she said. "Boring, mostly. Deeds and surveyors. You shouldn't get near it."

"Wouldn't dream of it." He sipped his tea. It was excellent, which figured; everything Rosalind touched turned precise. "Though I'll admit the orchard itself is why I've come. Two weeks. I want to learn the work — grafting, pruning, whatever the season allows. I want to press cider with my own hands. I want to stand in that press house at dawn and find out what it does to a man."

"What does London do to you?" she asked, and it was a real question, asked softly, the way she asked the questions that ended up being the spine of chapters.

Auguste looked into the fire. He could give her the performance answer — *London is the restaurant, the reviews, the treadmill* — and she'd take it and shape it and it would be fine. Or he could give her the true thing, the thing he'd flown four hundred miles to be able to say:

"London makes me feel like a recipe that's been cooked too many times," he said. "Same dish, same table, everyone pleased, nobody fed. Out here—" he gestured at the window, the darkening slope, the black trees holding up the sky, "—out here I remember there was a boy who burnt an omelette and cried about it and kept cooking anyway. I'd like to meet him again before the book comes out. Before I forget he existed."

Silence. When he glanced over, Rosalind was looking at him with an expression he couldn't file — something raw around the edges, quickly covered, like a wound glimpsed under a sleeve.

"That's the chapter," she said quietly. Almost to herself. "That's the whole book, right there."

"Well," Auguste said, pleased and unsettled in equal measure, "don't spend it all on page one."

* * *

He stayed for supper — she cooked, of course, simply and perfectly, leeks and potatoes and a chicken from somewhere within walking distance — and it was only afterward, walking back down the lane under a sky rinsed suddenly clear and reckless with stars, that the pieces began arranging themselves into the shape he'd later recognize as the beginning of trouble.

Because he passed the cottage, and the kitchen window was lit, and through it he saw Elias Thorne with the cello out of its case, cradled upright against his chest, bow hovering — not playing, just hovering, a man remembering the shape of a gesture. And beside him at the table sat a girl of perhaps sixteen, homework spread out, watching him with the frank patience of the young, and when Thorne lowered the bow without playing a note, she said something that made him laugh — actually laugh, the sound carrying faintly through old glass — and Auguste stood in the frozen lane grinning like a fool, because *there* it was, the story under the story: the musician who wouldn't play, teaching a child who didn't know she was being taught.

And then, further up the lane, the big house, and the upstairs window where Rosalind's lamp still burned, and her silhouette at the desk, hunched over the glowing laptop, writing — writing him, presumably, writing the boy who burnt the omelette, giving his life back to him in sentences better than his own.

Two lit windows in a dark valley. The cellist who wouldn't play and the writer who wrote other people's lives instead of her own.

Auguste stood between them in the cold, breathing apple-sweet air, feeling the peculiar happiness of a man who believes he has stumbled into the middle of a wonderful story — and the complete, catastrophic innocence of a man who has not yet understood that he is standing in the middle of someone else's.

He smiled at the stars, patted his coat pockets for his phone, and composed a message to Priya as he walked:

*The village is perfect. The orchard is perfect. There's a cellist. There's a girl. There's a fence dispute with literary potential. Cancel my Monday — I'm staying till I understand this place.*

He hit send. Somewhere ahead of him, in the dark orchard, a branch cracked under the weight of wind, and a winter apple fell without anyone to hear it.
