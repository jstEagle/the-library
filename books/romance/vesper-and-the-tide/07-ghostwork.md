## 7. Ghostwork

The townhouse on Wetherell Row smelled the way it always did in October: woodsmoke and old paper and, beneath it, the faint medicinal sweetness of whatever Edmund Hale was taking now that he refused to call it medicine. Vesper let herself in with the key he'd given her nine years ago—brass, heavy, warm from her coat pocket—and stood a moment in the front hall listening to the house breathe.

It was a house that remembered being grand. The Persian runner had gone thin along its center line where forty years of visitors had worn their patience into it. Portraits of Edmund at various ages watched from the walls: young Edmund with his first book, middle-aged Edmund with his prize, old Edmund in a photograph that flattered him by half. Nowhere, of course, was there any portrait of the person who had written his last three books. Ghosts didn't hang on walls. That was rather the point of them.

"Is that you?" came the voice from the study, pitched to carry, pitched the way it always was when he suspected company. "Or have I finally started hearing things?"

"It's me," Vesper said, hanging her coat. "You're not hearing things yet."

"A tragedy postponed." He appeared in the study doorway, cardigan buttoned wrong—one button off, so the whole garment pulled slightly leftward, like a man leaning out of frame. His hair had gone fully white this year, which he claimed was distinguished and which Vesper privately knew frightened him. He looked at her for a beat too long, the way he sometimes did lately, as though retrieving her name from a drawer that stuck. "Vesper," he said, finding it. "Good. Sit. We're doing the marriage chapters today."

"We're doing the marriage chapters today," she agreed, and carried her bag into the study.

The room was exactly as it had been for a decade: the desk facing the window so that Edmund could watch the street and be watched writing; the two chairs before it, one leather and one not; the shelves of his own books in every translation, spines like a small nation's flag collection. On the blotter lay the manuscript pages she'd couriered over last week—chapter twenty-nine, the courtship—with his marginalia in fountain-pen blue. *Yes. Yes. This is right.* And beside one paragraph, circled twice: *I remember this differently.*

She had learned, over ten years, what that phrase meant. It meant: *this is true and I wish it weren't.* It never meant what it said.

Edmund lowered himself into his chair with the careful choreography of a man who had begun negotiating with his own body. Vesper took the other chair, opened her laptop, and waited for the ritual.

"Read me where we are," he said.

"You read it last Tuesday."

"I read it last Tuesday as a reader. Today I need to read it as the man who lived it." He said this without irony, without even the flicker of a glance that might have acknowledged the strange architecture of their arrangement—the man who lived it, reading the woman who wrote it, so that together they could produce the man who lived it properly. Ten years ago the sentence would have made her laugh. Now it simply made her tired in a place below tiredness, somewhere near the bone.

She read. She'd written the chapter in three nights, after the session where Edmund had told her, for the fourth time, the story of meeting his wife Claire at a wedding in Cornwall—a story she had already shaped, polished, and improved until it bore only a family resemblance to anything he'd actually said. In the manuscript version, Claire wore a blue dress. In life, according to the one photograph Vesper had found, she had probably worn green. But blue sang against the Cornish light, and blue was what the memoir needed, and so Claire had worn blue forever now, in every telling, including Edmund's own. That was the thing no one warned you about ghostwriting: eventually your inventions became the subject's memories, and then they became yours too, and there was no unmaking any of it. You built a man out of better material than God had used, and then you had to live inside the cathedral knowing every stone was yours and none of the windows showed you.

"—and I remember thinking," Edmund said, picking up from her reading, his eyes closed, performing recollection the way he performed everything, generously, at full volume, "that if I didn't speak to her in the next five minutes I would regret it for the rest of my life. Which turned out to be true, of course, just not in the way I expected."

Vesper's fingers hovered over the keyboard. He had taken the line from her. Not stolen it—he didn't think of it as theft, she was almost sure he didn't—but absorbed it, the way a river absorbs rain, so that within a week he would tell interviewers it was something he'd always felt, something he'd said to Claire himself once, on the cliff path, in the wind. She had heard him do it at dinner parties. *I told her, you know, that I'd regret it all my life if I didn't ask her to dance.* And the guests had leaned in, delighted, and Vesper had sat with her wine going warm in her hand, listening to her own sentence come back to her wearing his cologne.

"Put that in," Edmund said, opening his eyes. "That thought. It's good."

"It is good," Vester said quietly, and typed it.

* * *

The session went the way sessions went. He dictated fragments; she caught them, tested them, asked the questions that made him go deeper or made him deflect, depending on the hour. By eleven he was flagging, and by noon he was magnificent again, the second wind that had fooled three generations of interviewers into thinking Edmund Hale was inexhaustible. He was exhausted. Everyone close enough to see it up close knew he was exhausted, and everyone close enough also understood that naming it would end the working relationship, because Edmund Hale had built an entire identity on the premise that he was still the youngest person in any room.

They broke for lunch, and it was during lunch that the editor arrived.

Marguerite Osei had edited Edmund for fifteen years and Vesper had met her perhaps thirty times, always in this house, always in Edmund's presence, always introduced with some variation of "our research assistant" or "my archivist" or, once memorably, "the girl who keeps my papers in order," as though Vesper were a filing system with legs. Marguerite was sharp-eyed and kind and had, Vesper suspected, figured out the truth years ago and elected silence, the way the whole literary world elected silence about ghosts: everyone knew, no one said, and the books went on winning prizes under names that had done less and less of the work.

"Marguerite!" Edmund rose to greet her, buttoned-wrong cardigan and all, and kissed her cheeks. "You've come to tell me how brilliant I am."

"I've come to tell you the excerpt is moving," Marguerite said, settling into the chair across from Vesper with the ease of someone claiming familiar territory. "The publisher wants to lead with the marriage material. The Claire chapters." She looked down at the pages in her bag, and Vesper saw her thumb find the passage—the wedding, the blue dress, the five minutes. "This bit here. Where he says he'll regret it all his life if he doesn't speak to her. That's—" She stopped, and Vesper watched her choose her word with the precision of a woman who chose words for a living. "That's the best sentence in the book so far. It's the voice people fell in love with forty years ago. It's *him* at his most essential."

"Yes," Edmund said, pleased, expansive, pouring the sherry Marguerite hadn't asked for. "That one cost me something to write."

Vesper looked at the window. The garden beyond was going bronze and rust, the late roses holding on past all reason. *Cost me something,* she thought. It had cost her four drafts and a night of lying awake listening to the sea-wall wind, trying to find the exact weight of a man's regret for a marriage he had neglected for thirty years—a regret he had never once articulated to her in plain language, which was why she'd had to build it herself, plank by plank, out of his evasions. What it had cost *him* was nothing but the trouble of saying *yes* in blue ink.

"The rhythm of it," Marguerite was saying, tapping the page. "Short clause, long clause, then that turn at the end. 'Just not in the way I expected.' You can hear him thinking. Readers will feel they're inside his head."

Inside his head, Vesper thought. Inside the head I furnished.

"That's kind," Edmund said. "Though I'll confess I barely recognize myself in these pages sometimes. Vesper digs things up. Half of it surprises me." He laughed, and Marguerite laughed, and the joke landed exactly as such jokes always landed—as a charming acknowledgment of collaboration that was, in fact, a complete description of authorship delivered in the key of modesty. It was brilliant, really. It was the perfect crime committed openly, in front of witnesses, and everyone smiled.

"Keep surprising yourself," Marguerite said, gathering her pages. "Four months, Edmund. The publisher's firm on it. And—" she paused at the door, and for one moment her eyes moved to Vesper, held there a fraction longer than courtesy required, and something passed between them that neither of them would ever name aloud. Something like *I know,* and something like *I'm sorry,* and something like *what a strange trade we're all in.* "Whatever it takes," Marguerite finished, and was gone.

* * *

The afternoon session collapsed early. Edmund grew foggy around three—not dramatically, nothing anyone filming him would catch, but Vesper had spent a decade learning his weather, and she could feel the pressure dropping. He called Claire by name twice, which he did sometimes, speaking to a dead woman as though she'd merely stepped out to post a letter. Then he confused the decade, asked whether the prize committee had called, and when Vesper gently redirected him he looked at her with an expression she had never quite seen before—or rather, an expression she had seen and refused to catalogue: naked, brief, total terror. The look of a man standing in his own house unable to remember which door led out of it.

"Read me something," he said finally, small-voiced. "Anything. Read me the good part."

So Vesper read him the wedding scene. Her scene. His scene. The blue dress, the five minutes, the regret that would last a lifetime. And Edmund closed his eyes and listened to his own life read back to him in a stranger's cadence, and when she reached the final line he murmured, "God, yes. That's exactly how it was," and Vesper felt something in her chest go quiet and cold, like a room after the fire has been carried out of it.

Exactly how it was. It had never been how it was. She had invented the wind on the cliff path. She had invented the color of the dress. She had invented the pause before the last clause, the little hitch that Marguerite had called the sound of him thinking. There was no *how it was* anymore. There was only the book, and the book was hers, and the book was his, and the difference between those two facts had been dissolving for ten years like sugar in tea, invisible, irreversible, sweetening nothing she got to taste.

She packed up at five. At the door Edmund caught her hand—his grip still strong, that actor's grip—and said, "Same time Thursday?"

"Same time Thursday."

"You're very good to me, Vesper." And then, looking past her shoulder at nothing, at the hallway, at the long corridor of his own fading architecture: "I don't know what I'd write without you."

*What*, she almost said. *What would you write without me.* But she only squeezed his hand and let herself out, and the door closed behind her with the soft expensive click of a house that kept its secrets well insulated.

* * *

The walk home took twenty minutes along the coast road, and she walked it slowly, letting the October wind do what it did to her hair and her thoughts, which was tangle both. The tide was out. Far below the sea-wall the flats gleamed like hammered pewter, and a lone dog ran figure-eights through the shallows while its owner stood wrapped in scarves, watching the horizon as though expecting it to explain itself.

Ten years ago she had stood on a platform waiting for a train that had already left, and a stranger had stood beside her waiting for one that hadn't come yet, and they had talked about tide charts and second chances until the lamps buzzed on, and she had gone home and written him a letter she never sent to any address he gave her—because he hadn't given one, because she hadn't asked, because asking would have made it real and real things could be lost. So she'd mailed it blind, to the city printed on his luggage tag, care of nothing, addressed to a man whose full name she'd learned only because he'd said it aloud once, laughing: Jonah Reyes. A name like a wave breaking. One hundred and nineteen letters since. One hundred and nineteen replies. A correspondence conducted entirely outside her visible life, in the one country where she was a citizen: the country of signed-nothing, claimed-nothing, owned-nothing.

And tonight the country felt less like refuge than like exile.

Because here was the arithmetic she could no longer avoid, walking home in the dark with her collar up: she had spent ten years writing Edmund Hale's life so convincingly that he believed it was his, and she had spent ten years writing her own life so invisibly that no one—including, increasingly, herself—could find it. Two manuscripts. One famous. One missing. And the terrible suspicion, arriving now with the flat calm of a tide chart consulted too late, that the two projects were the same project. That she had ghostwritten herself. That somewhere inside her was a woman who talked to strangers on night trains and said bold things about second chances, and that Vesper Pemberton had buried her under chapters, deadlines, blue ink, and the gentle corrosive lie that invisibility was a discipline rather than a wound.

Her cottage was dark when she reached it. Next door, the new neighbor's windows glowed—warm, ordinary, occupied. She had not yet worked up the courage to do more than nod at the man with the cane who had arrived with boxes labeled in another city's handwriting, and she told herself the flutter this caused was indigestion, or weather, or anything other than what it was.

Inside, she did not turn on the main lights. She lit the lamp by the window, put the kettle on out of habit and let it go cold out of distraction, and sat down at the small desk where the letters lived—in the drawer, in their box, in their strict chronological order, one hundred nineteen received and one hundred nineteen answered, a complete archive of the only honest relationship of her adult life.

She took out fresh paper. Cream, heavy, the good stock she saved for him.

*Dear Jonah,* she wrote.

The pen hovered.

What followed should have been easy. Ten years of practice. She had written him through job losses and publishing triumphs that weren't hers to claim, through her mother's anniversary, through storms and springs and one memorable winter when the pipes froze and she'd described the ice flowers on the window glass so vividly he'd written back that he'd hung a blanket over his own window to match her. She could write him anything.

Except, apparently, the truth.

*I wrote a sentence today that a famous man will say for the rest of his life,* she began, and stopped. Crossed it out. Tried again: *Today someone praised my work to my face and called it his, and I smiled, and I want you to tell me why I smiled.* She stopped again. Because the answer was in the letters already, wasn't it? Because he knew her better than anyone alive—the her on paper, the her with the signature that was a pseudonym, the her who had confessed everything except her name—and because if she wrote this letter honestly, it would become a confession, and confessions required addresses, and addresses required faces, and faces could be lost.

She wrote instead: *The tide is far out tonight. The flats look like pewter. I keep thinking about how the sea takes back everything it lends, and how I have lent so much, and to whom, and whether any of it comes home.*

It was beautiful. It was evasive. It was, she realized with a clarity that felt almost physical, exactly the kind of sentence she would give Edmund—true in texture, false in substance, gorgeous as a cover story. Even her letters to the one person who deserved her plainness had learned the family trick: say the deep thing sideways, sign nothing, owe nothing, lose nothing.

She read it over twice. Then she folded it, and did not seal it, and did not address it, and placed it in the drawer beneath the box—unmailed, unfinished, the first letter in ten years she had chosen not to send. It sat there in the lamplight like a door left ajar.

Outside, the wind shifted and brought the smell of the incoming tide. Somewhere down the coast the sea was turning around, beginning its long patient return toward everything it had touched.

And next door, faintly, through the wall, she heard the new neighbor's radio playing something slow and old, and a man's low voice humming along—slightly off-key, entirely unguarded, alive—and Vesper sat very still in her pool of lamplight, pen uncapped, heart behaving strangely, telling herself she was not listening.

Telling herself, as she had told herself for ten years, that she was merely keeping watch on the platform.

The tide, meanwhile, was coming in.
