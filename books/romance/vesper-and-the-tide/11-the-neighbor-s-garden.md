## 11. The Neighbor's Garden

The first flower was a wallflower.

Vesper noticed it on a Tuesday in late April, when she went out to retrieve the morning paper and found, instead of newsprint, a small brown hand thrusting up from the churned earth of the neighboring garden bed. She stopped mid-step. The paper slid from under her arm and lay face-down on the flagstones, and she didn't pick it up, because the flower was blooming at the edge of Jonah Reyes's property—wallflower, *Erysimum cheiri*, the color of old honey and dusk—and there was no reason on earth for a man to plant wallflowers unless he had a reason, and she knew exactly what that reason was, because she had written it herself.

*You asked once why I love wallflowers,* she had written, in the fourth year, letter forty-one or thereabouts. *Because they grow in walls. In cracks. In places nothing is supposed to take root. They bloom where they were never invited, and they don't apologize for it.*

She stood on her own flagstones with her bare feet going cold and stared at that small defiant blossom as if it were a sentence she'd written appearing in someone else's mouth.

Then she picked up her paper, went inside, and told herself it was coincidence. Gardens needed early bloomers. Wallflowers were common. Any nursery in three counties would stock them.

She almost believed it until Thursday, when the sweet peas went in.

* * *

By the second week of May, the garden next door had become an argument she was having with herself across the property line.

Jonah worked in it every morning. She could see him from her kitchen window while the kettle heated—a tall man made slightly stooped by the cane, kneeling on a folded pad with his sleeves rolled, pressing seeds into dark soil with a patience that looked almost devotional. He moved slowly now; she'd noticed that before, catalogued it without letting herself interpret it. But his hands were sure. They knew what they were doing, and more unsettlingly, they seemed to know *why*.

Sweet peas along the back fence. Night-scented stock near the bench. A trellis going up for moonflowers, which bloomed only after dark and lasted a single night each. Honesty, planted deliberately, its papery silver seed pods like small moons. Heliotrope, whose scent she had once described in a letter as *vanilla and memory*. Sea thrift, though they were a full mile inland and sea thrift wanted salt. He'd planted it anyway, in a pot, near the gate, as if daring the geography to object.

Vesper stood at her window with her tea going cold and counted them like sins.

Every one of them had appeared in her letters. Not all at once—across ten years, scattered through a hundred and twenty envelopes like a code she hadn't known she was writing. She had mentioned flowers the way lonely people mention weather: as proof of attention, as small offerings, as ways of saying *I am here, I see things, this beauty has nowhere else to go.* She had never imagined anyone keeping the list.

But he had kept the list. He had kept everything. And now he was planting it, six feet from her kitchen window, in plain view, like a man laying out evidence and waiting to see who would come forward to claim it.

She should have felt exposed. That was the thing she kept returning to, standing there with the steam fading off her cup: she *should* have felt flayed open, burgled, seen without permission. Instead she felt something so close to joy that it frightened her worse than exposure would have. It was the feeling of walking into a room and finding your own handwriting on the walls—not defaced but framed, honored, lit properly for the first time.

He knows me, she thought. He has known me for ten years, and he has never once used it against me.

The kettle clicked off. She poured water over leaves she wouldn't taste and watched him tuck a nasturtium seed into the ground with two fingers, gentle as a man closing a sleeping child's fist around something precious.

Nasturtiums. Letter sixty-three. *They taste peppery and they climb anything you give them, even ruin.*

She turned away from the window. She had chapter thirty-two to draft, and Edmund expected pages by Friday, and there was no room in her disciplined, invisible life for a man who gardened her correspondence into bloom.

She wrote four hundred words that morning. Every one of them was about flowers.

* * *

It rained on the Saturday, a soft grey drizzle that made the whole street smell of wet stone and turned earth, and Vesper broke her rule about not watching him.

She told herself she was only taking a walk. She took walks; it was established; no one could read anything into it. But her walk curved, the way rivers curve, toward the low stone wall between their properties, and she found herself standing at the gate of his garden with rain beading on her hair and no honest explanation prepared.

He was there, of course. Under a canvas hat gone soft with age, kneeling by the sweet pea row, shielding seedlings from the drizzle with a length of cloche glass. He looked up when the gate hinge spoke, and something crossed his face—recognition, or the hope of it, quickly folded away and put somewhere safe.

"Morning," he said. "Or afternoon. The rain makes it hard to tell."

"It's nearly noon." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "You're out in this."

"Best time to plant. The ground's soft, the sun won't scorch them." He gestured at the bed with a trowel, an invitation shaped like information. "Though I'll admit I'm guessing at half of this. I've never grown most of it."

"You seem to know what you're doing."

"I'm following instructions." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on her a beat too long, weighing whether the words landed anywhere. "A very detailed list."

Vesper's heart performed a maneuver she chose not to examine. "From a book?"

"From letters." He said it plainly, watching her. Watching her hands, she realized. Watching her face. "Someone I used to write with. She had strong opinions about gardens."

The rain filled the silence between them, patient and indifferent. Vesper became aware of every part of her body separately—the cold tip of her nose, the pulse in her throat, the way her fingers had curled around the gate's iron latch as if it might hold her upright.

"She sounds particular," Vesper managed.

"Particular isn't the word." He smiled, and it was a strange smile, private and a little sad, aimed somewhere just past her shoulder. "She'd say *opinionated*. She said heliotrope smells like vanilla and memory. She said moonflowers are worth staying up for because they only live one night and they spend it being beautiful instead of afraid." He pulled off one glove, finger by finger. "I've never met anyone who described flowers like that. Like they were people she'd loved."

"And you believed her? About the moonflowers?"

"I've stayed up for them twice now." His voice did something quiet at the edges. "She was right both times."

Vesper looked at the trellis—the empty trellis, the seeds not yet sprouted—and saw the whole architecture of what he was doing. He wasn't building a garden. He was building a trap made entirely of tenderness, and the terrible truth, the truth she carried back up her path with rain soaking through her cardigan, was that she wanted to walk into it. She wanted to stand in the middle of that garden in high summer when everything bloomed at once and say: *I wrote all of it. I am the opinionated woman. I am the vanilla and memory.* She wanted it the way the thirsty want water—without dignity, without negotiation.

Instead she made tea and sat with her back to the window and did not look at the garden for the rest of the day, which fooled no one, least of all herself.

* * *

On Monday, Edmund Hale wanted to talk about lilacs.

"The scene," he said, pacing his townhouse study with a brandy he wouldn't drink, "where Charles brings her lilacs after the funeral. It needs weight. It needs—" he waved the glass, sloshing it, "—it needs *botany*, Vesper. Specificity. Readers can smell laziness."

Vesper sat with her notebook and let the familiar machinery turn. Ten years of this. Ten years of translating Edmund's vague appetites into sentences with bones. "What kind of lilacs?"

"How should I know? You're the one who reads."

She wrote: *Common purple, cut too short, the stems still dripping when he pressed them into her arms—because Charles never learned that you bring flowers already arranged or not at all, and grief makes everyone clumsy.*

Edmund read over her shoulder, as he always did, and nodded slowly. "Yes. That's it exactly. That's how it was." And there it was, the old sleight, performed so smoothly neither of them flinched anymore: *that's how it was.* He remembered nothing of the kind. There had been no funeral, no Charles, no lilacs. There had been a paragraph she invented twenty minutes ago, and now it belonged to him, the way everything belonged to him, the way her name belonged to the space beneath his.

But today the theft snagged on something. Today, driving home with the notebook on the passenger seat, Vesper found herself thinking about the garden next door—about a man who had received a hundred and twenty letters and preserved every flower named in them like relics, versus a man who had received ten years of her finest work and couldn't locate her in a single sentence of it.

Two men. Two archives. One kept her words alive and didn't know her face. The other knew her face and couldn't see her at all.

She parked outside her house as the light failed, and Jonah's porch lamp came on next door, and beyond it, faint in the dusk, the pale shapes of new stakes marking rows she could recite from memory. Sweet peas. Stock. Moonflowers. Honesty.

Honesty. She laughed aloud in the dark car, alone, a short startled sound. Of course he'd planted honesty. She'd written about it in letter ninety: *Lunaria annua—they call it honesty because the seed pods go transparent, and you can see straight through to the seed. Imagine being built like that. Imagine having nothing to hide behind.*

He had taken even that. Or rather—he had taken nothing, because it had always been his; she had mailed it to him, month after month, decade upon decade, a slow hemorrhage of self delivered in stamped envelopes. She had given herself away in installments and called it anonymity.

And God help her, sitting in the cooling car with her notebook full of Edmund's stolen lilacs, she wanted to give him the rest.

* * *

Wednesday brought the foxgloves.

She came home past seven to find him staking them—*Digitalis*, spires just breaking bud, purple-throated, dangerous and lovely—and she must have made a sound, some small involuntary thing, because he turned and found her standing at the fence with her bag still on her shoulder.

"You're staring," he said. Not unkindly. Almost hopefully.

"They're poisonous," Vesper said. "Foxgloves. Every part of them."

"So I read." He braced a stalk against its stake with strips of soft cloth. "Digitalis. They make heart medicine out of it. Something that could stop your heart, refined correctly, is the very thing that keeps it beating." He looked up at her then, and the evening light came through the foxglove spires and laid stripes across his face. "That was in one of the letters too. That some poisons are medicines if you get the dose right. Do you think that's true?"

The question hung between them, wearing its disguise. She could feel the real question underneath it, the one he'd been asking since the moving trucks arrived: *Are you her? Will you tell me? How long do I have to keep pretending I don't already know?*

"I think," Vesper said carefully, "that it depends on who's measuring the dose."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and gave up. The foxgloves swayed in a wind she couldn't feel, and Jonah Reyes smiled at her the way men smile at locked doors they intend to wait beside.

"That's exactly," he said softly, "what she would have said."

Vesper went inside. She set her bag down. She stood in her dark kitchen with her heart going like something caged, and through the window she watched him finish staking the foxgloves, and she understood—with the clarity that arrives only after it's too late to be useful—that the garden was not a trap at all.

It was a map. Drawn in flowers, planted in order, letter by letter, year by year. And maps, she knew better than anyone, were written by one person for another to follow home.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I will—

But the phone rang, and it was Edmund's editor, and the moment folded itself away like a letter into an envelope, sealed, addressed, and—for now—unsent.
