## 14. Chapter Thirty-One

The townhouse smelled of cold coffee and old paper, the way it always did by mid-afternoon, when the light through the tall windows went the color of weak tea and Edmund Hale began his slow descent from morning brilliance into evening vagueness. Vesper had learned to read him the way sailors read weather—by the set of his shoulders, the angle of his reading glasses, the particular quality of his silences. Today the weather was turning.

"Where were we?" he asked, though he'd asked it an hour ago, and an hour before that.

"Chapter thirty," she said gently. "The year in Lisbon."

"Lisbon." He said the word like a man tasting something he couldn't identify. "There was a woman in Lisbon."

"There was a wife in Lisbon." She kept her voice level, kept her eyes on her notebook. "Marguerite. You've been telling me about her for three weeks."

Edmund's face did something complicated—the flicker of a man reaching for a coat hook and finding nothing there. Then, as always, charm arrived to fill the vacancy. "Of course. Marguerite. The saint. The martyr. The woman I made into furniture." He laughed at himself, that rich, practiced laugh that had charmed four decades of interviewers, and Vesper felt the familiar small violence of hearing her own sentences come back to her in his mouth. He had said *furniture* first—or rather, she had written *I made her into furniture* six days ago, and he had read it, nodded, and now owned it. That was how it worked. Everything she gave him, he absorbed. Everything he absorbed, he believed had always been his.

"Dictate for me," he said. "You know how I like it. Start with the house."

She knew the house. She had built it three times over: the house on the hill above Funchal, the house with the long window facing the water, the house where Marguerite Hale had spent eleven months of every year arranging flowers for a husband who was never home to smell them. Vesper closed her eyes and let the chapter rise up through her, the way it always did—not remembered but composed, assembled out of Edmund's fragments and her own invention, the two so thoroughly married by now that no forensic stylist could ever separate them.

*The house had forty-one windows,* she wrote as he watched, *and Marguerite cleaned each one herself, because the cleaning woman came only on Thursdays and Marguerite could not bear to look at the sea through anyone else's smears.*

"Good," Edmund murmured. "God, that's good. I remember those windows."

He didn't. There were no windows; she had invented them Tuesday night, lying awake, thinking about glass and salt and the labor of keeping things clear for someone who never looks. But he would swear to them now until the day he died. He would tell the story of the forty-one windows at dinner parties, and some young biographer would write it down as fact, and Vesper Pemberton would sit somewhere in the back of the room with her hands folded, the author of a house that had never existed, the widow of a marriage she had written alone.

"Keep going," Edmund said. He had settled into the wingback chair across from her, his legs under a wool blanket even though it was June, his hands laced over his stomach like a man being read to. This was their liturgy. She spoke; he received. Somewhere in the transaction, the words stopped being hers.

*He sent postcards instead of letters,* she dictated. *Postcards were a form of absence you could hold. A letter required an answer; a postcard required nothing but a drawer.*

Edmund's eyes had drifted shut. "The drawers," he said softly. "She kept them all in the blue drawer of the desk in the hall. Hundreds of them. I found them after—" He stopped. His brow creased. "After what?"

Vesper waited. This was the dangerous hour, the hour when the scaffolding showed, when the architecture of the memoir revealed itself as exactly what it was: a building with one architect and one occupant who had moved in recently and kept rearranging the furniture.

"After she died?" she offered.

"No." He opened his eyes, and for a moment—she would think about this moment for days afterward—for a moment he looked at her with terrible clarity, the way a drowning man looks at the surface. "No, she didn't die. I just left. That's the thing, isn't it? That's the whole rotten thing. She's probably still there. Still cleaning windows for a ghost."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Downstairs, Mrs. Okafor, the day nurse, was running water in the kitchen sink. Outside, a bus sighed past on the wet street.

"That's chapter thirty-two," Vesper said quietly. "That's where we're going. The leaving. We're not there yet."

"We're not there yet," Edmund repeated, and the clarity drained out of him like tide off a flat, and he was smiling again, vague and pleasant, an old lion dozing in the sun. "Read it back to me. The part about the postcards. I want to hear how it sounds."

She read it back. Her voice in the high-ceilinged room, saying sentences she had made out of the shape of another man's guilt, and Edmund nodding along like a man listening to his own heartbeat on a monitor—reassured, absolved, present at the creation of himself.

"It sounds true," he said, wonderingly. "It sounds truer than anything I actually lived."

And there it was. The whole profession, the whole ten years, compressed into a single sentence spoken without irony by a man who had won prizes for a truth he had outsourced. Vesper wrote *end of session* in her notebook, packed her pens, kissed the papery cheek offered to her like a stamp on a document, and walked down the four flights of stairs past the framed covers of his celebrated novels—each one, if anyone had ever thought to check, carrying more of her hand than his—and stepped out into the street feeling like a woman who has just watched someone else eat a meal she cooked, and call it grown.

* * *

The walk home took twenty minutes if she took the hill road and thirty-five if she took the shore path, and she always took the shore path, and she always told herself it was because of the light.

The light was good that evening. Low and gold, laying a bright seam along the water, gulls hanging motionless over the breakwater like notes on a staff. And there—because the town was small and the path was narrow and the universe, apparently, had opinions—there was Jonah Reyes, standing at the rail where the path overlooked the sandbar, leaning on his cane with the careful economy of a man who had learned exactly how much standing his body would sell him per day.

"You're back late," he said, as though they were people who kept track of such things. As though they were neighbors in the ordinary sense, two lives adjacent rather than parallel.

"Working session ran long."

"The famous book." He turned his head, and the last light caught the lines of his face—the face she had spent ten years writing to, the face she had reconstructed from memory so many times that the memory and the man had begun to diverge in ways that unsettled her. He was thinner than the train. Older. There was a new gravity around his mouth. "How's it going?"

"He's remembering things wrong," she heard herself say, and then, appalled at her own honesty, added quickly, "which is normal. At his age. It's my job to arrange the remembering."

"Arrange the remembering." He tried the phrase out, rolling it like a stone. "That's a kind thing to call it."

"It's an honest thing to call it."

They stood together at the rail. Below them the tide was making, sliding up the channel in long silver sheets, beginning its patient work of erasing the sandbar, erasing the footprints, erasing everything that had been written on the flats since noon. She watched the water come and felt the strange vertigo she always felt lately in his presence—that sensation of standing in two places at once, of being the woman at the rail and also the woman in the letters, and knowing that only one of them existed for him, and it wasn't this one.

"I've been thinking," Jonah said, "about second chances."

Her heart performed a small, treacherous maneuver. "Have you."

"On the train, once, a woman told me they weren't given. They were taken. She said most people spend their whole lives waiting at the platform for a chance to arrive on schedule, and it never comes, because chances don't run on timetables. You have to board them while they're moving." He was looking at the water, not at her, which was either mercy or accident. "I thought that was nonsense when she said it. I was twenty-six and immortal. Now I'm not sure."

"What changed your mind?"

"Time," he said simply. "Time changed my mind."

The tide slid another yard up the flats. Somewhere behind them, in the dunes, a song thrush started its evening arithmetic, note after note after note. Vesper understood suddenly, with the force of a physical blow, that she was doing it again—right now, here, at the rail, in the gold light. She was composing. She was watching this moment happen and simultaneously drafting it, choosing the details worth keeping, discarding the rest, arranging the remembering. Even here. Even with him. Especially with him. Ten years of ghosting other people's lives had trained her so thoroughly that she could not stand inside her own without reaching for a pen.

*He said time changed his mind,* the inner narrator noted. *The thrush sang. The tide made. She said nothing, and the nothing was the truest sentence of the day, and she would never write it down.*

Stop, she told herself. Stop it. Be here. Just once, be here.

But the narrator was her, and the pen was her, and there was no one else to be.

"I should go," she said. "It's getting late."

"It usually is," Jonah said, and smiled, and let her go.

* * *

The house was dark when she got in. She didn't turn on the lights. She moved through the rooms by habit—hall, kitchen, the small study at the back with its single window facing the garden—and sat down at the desk where she had written 120 letters to a stranger, and pulled out fresh paper, and stared at it.

Chapter thirty-one was due Friday. *The marriage he neglected.* She had the shape of it already: the blue drawer full of postcards, the forty-one windows, Marguerite waiting in a house built for company that never came. It was good material. It was the best material in the book, maybe—Edmund's great failure rendered with all the tenderness he had never actually spent, the confession of a man who loved in absentia, who mistook sending for staying, who thought a marriage was something you could maintain by correspondence.

And tonight, for the first time in ten years, the material had turned around and looked at her.

Because that was the thing she had seen at the rail, the thing that sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone: she was Marguerite. Not the waiting part—she had chosen her solitude, or told herself she had—but the correspondence part. The love conducted entirely on paper. The devotion that never risked a door. For ten years she had written to Jonah Reyes every month, pouring out everything she could not say aloud—her mother, her loneliness, her terrible discipline, her hope that she refused to name even in her own head—and she had called it courage. She had called it intimacy. She had signed a name that wasn't quite hers, *Vesper*, the evening name, the name for the hour when you can see the stars but no one can see you, and she had believed that made it honest.

It made it a postcard. A form of absence you could hold. A letter required an answer; she had gotten answers, God knows she had gotten answers, a hundred and nineteen of them, and still she had stayed on her side of the desk, in her own house with its forty-one imaginary windows, keeping the glass clean for a man who was finally, impossibly, living next door—and she was still writing to him. In her head. All day. Every day. While he stood three feet away asking her about second chances.

She picked up the pen.

*Dear Jonah—*

The nib hovered. Then it broke loose, the way it always did at night, the way it had broken loose a hundred and twenty times before, and the letter poured out of her in the dark:

*I have been trying to write Edmund's marriage today, and I could not stop seeing mine. Not a marriage—a decade. Ten years of loving you in the conditional tense. If you knew me. If I were braver. If the circumstances were different, if the timing were better, if I were the sort of person who boards trains instead of missing them. I have built an entire self out of conditionals, and tonight I understand that the woman you're looking for—the letter-writer, the one from the train—is not hiding from you. She is hiding from me. She is the person I would have been if I had ever once done the brave thing in real time instead of writing it down afterward and calling the writing a life.*

*You told me today that time changed your mind. Time has been changing mine too, and I am afraid of what it's changing it into. I am afraid that one morning I will find your house empty and the garden unplanted and I will understand that I missed the last departure of my actual life, the real one, the one that doesn't run again tomorrow—and that I will deserve it, because I saw the timetable. I have always seen the timetable. Seeing is the one thing I'm good at.*

*Here is the truth, which I will burn, because I am who I am: I know why you came to this town. I know about the letters—I have always known about the letters, because they are mine, every word, and I have known since the day the moving truck came and I saw the box with my old address on it and my whole body went cold. I know what you're looking for. I am standing in front of you at the rail every evening, and I cannot make myself say it, and I hate myself in a way that is almost peaceful, the way a habit is peaceful, the way a locked room is peaceful.*

*Sign your name, the letter-writer would say. Whoever she is, wherever she is, she would tell you both to sign your names. She would say that outliving yourself is the only death.*

*Yours, in every tense except the present one,*

*Vesper*

She sat back. The clock in the hall said 1:40. The page was covered edge to edge in her slanted hand, the handwriting loosening toward the end the way it always did when the truth got ahead of the pen.

She read it twice. It was the truest thing she had written in ten years, including the hundred and twenty letters, including thirty chapters of Edmund Hale's celebrated life. It was the truest thing and it was useless, because it was addressed to a man who slept forty feet away through a garden wall, and it was signed by a woman who existed only at night, on paper, in this room, alone.

In the memoir, Marguerite kept the postcards in the blue drawer. Vesper had invented the drawer, but she understood it completely. You kept the evidence of love somewhere with a lock on it. You kept it where it couldn't hurt anyone, especially yourself.

She carried the letter to the kitchen. She struck a match against the box—the same cheap matches she'd used for ten years of candles and stove flames, ordinary domestic fire—and held the corner of the page to it.

The paper caught. Light bloomed up the wall of the dark kitchen, orange and trembling, and she watched her own handwriting curl and blacken, the conditionals going first, then the confessions, then the signature—*Vesper*, dissolving into ash in the sink, the evening name returning to the evening. It took less than a minute. Ten years of discipline, and the truest page of her life was gone in under a minute, and the smoke smelled like every other burned thing, and the kitchen was dark again, and she was exactly who she had been before she wrote it.

Except.

Except that her hands were shaking. Except that she stood at the sink a long time, watching the last flecks of ash go gray, and felt something shift in her that was not peace and was not shame but was perhaps the ground floor of decision—the bedrock under all the conditionals, the place where a foundation either holds or doesn't. Edmund had left Marguerite because leaving was easier than arriving. Vesper had stayed away from Jonah for ten years because staying away was easier than arriving. Same cowardice, different compass point. And tomorrow she would sit in the wingback chair across from a dying man and write his repentance for him, sentence by beautiful sentence, a redemption he hadn't earned and wouldn't recognize—and she would do it well, because she always did it well, and she would come home hollow, because she always came home hollow.

Unless.

The matchbox was still on the counter. One match left in it, bent, the last of the row. She picked it up and turned it in her fingers, this small wooden hinge, this entire future balanced on a splint of pine and a bead of sulfur, and she thought: tomorrow. Tomorrow at the session, when Edmund dictates the leaving, when he gives her the words for the man who walked out of the house with forty-one clean windows behind him—tomorrow she would ask him something she had never asked in ten years. She would ask him whether he regretted it. Whether the book was worth the woman. Whether, at the end, with his memory going soft and his time running short, the famous sentences had been worth the silence they were written in.

And whatever he answered—whatever the old lion said from inside his wool blanket—she would take it as a verdict. On him, and on herself.

Outside, far off toward the station, a train sounded its horn: the last departure, right on schedule, pulling out into the dark with its lit windows and its sleeping strangers and its second chances nobody boarded. Vesper stood in her black kitchen holding an unlit match, and listened to it go.
