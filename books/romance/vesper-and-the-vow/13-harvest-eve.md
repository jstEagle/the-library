## 13. Harvest Eve

The village arrived before the sun did.

Vesper heard them first — the low rumble of truck engines on the gravel road, doors slamming in cheerful sequence, a dog barking at nothing — and stood at the farmhouse window with her coffee going cold, watching headlights bloom and fade through the mist like paper lanterns being lit one by one down the rows.

"They're early," she said.

Ansel was already dressed, already flour-dusted, already holding out a second mug as if he'd known she'd be standing there deciding whether to wake before dawn or simply never sleep. He'd been up since four. She could tell by the trays cooling on every horizontal surface: galettes, hand pies, a plum cake that filled the kitchen with a smell so warm it felt like an argument against leaving.

"Farmers are always early," he said. "It's the rest of us who are late to everything."

She took the mug. Their fingers didn't touch, but they came close enough that she noticed, which was its own kind of touching now. Ninety days minus eleven. She had stopped counting out loud weeks ago; Ansel had stopped counting at all somewhere around the night of the frost, when they'd fallen asleep on the porch with the smudge pots smoking themselves out below them and the blossoms saved, every last one.

Now those blossoms were fruit. The vines hung heavy and dark, and today they would come off.

* * *

By seven the crush pad looked like something from a postcard Vesper would once have sneered at and now couldn't stop looking at. Long tables under strung lights that someone — Marta from the co-op, probably — had insisted on hanging despite the daylight. Baskets stacked in pyramids. Children weaving between the rows with more energy than purpose. And people, dozens of them, villagers who had spent a season buying their cider at market and correcting their pruning technique unasked, all of them showing up with gloves and clippers and opinions about tonnage.

"You'll want to pick before ten," said old Henrik, who had mistaken them for Marguerite's grandchildren back in week three and whom neither of them had ever corrected. "Heat's coming after lunch. Fruit'll soften."

"He's right," Vesper murmured, and then caught herself, because six months ago she would have said *the acid retention alone argues for a pre-dawn pick* and let everyone think what they liked. Somewhere between the frost and the market day she had stopped performing expertise and started sharing it, and the difference, she was discovering, was the difference between a wall and a door.

Ansel appeared beside her with a clipboard he clearly didn't know how to hold. "Marta says we need forty more baskets. I said we'd take thirty-five because I don't want anyone driving to town twice."

"Take the forty," Vesper said. "We'll need them for the second block. The Pinot's heavier than you think."

He looked at her sideways. "You've been walking that block at night."

"I've been walking both blocks at night." She shrugged, embarrassed to be caught caring this much about fruit. "I couldn't sleep. The vines were there. It seemed rude not to look."

Something moved across his face — not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "Rude," he repeated softly, and went to get the baskets.

The picking began with a sound Vesper felt in her sternum: the first snip, the first soft drop of grapes into a bin, and then a hundred variations of it up and down the rows until the whole hillside was whispering. She worked the Pinot block with a team of teenagers who asked her questions about wine the way children ask about the sea — endlessly, reverently, without any intention of retaining the answers — and she answered all of them, and found she meant the answers, and that surprised her most of all.

There had been a time, not long ago, when the sound of harvesting would have been unbearable to her. When the sight of her own name on a label had made her flinch. But these bins held no label yet. These grapes belonged to no scandal, no estate, no man in a good suit explaining to a room full of journalists why the counterfeits had to be someone's fault and why that someone should be the sommelier who noticed them too late. These grapes just hung there in the September light, honest as anything, waiting to become whatever they became.

"Vesper!" Ansel called from the far row, holding up a cluster like a trophy. "Taste!"

She crossed to him. He split a grape with his thumb and held it out, and she took it from his fingers because refusing would have required a reason and she was fresh out.

Sweet, yes — but underneath the sweetness, structure. Acid like a spine. She closed her eyes and let it sit on her tongue and thought, absurdly: *this is what I taste like now.* Not the polished thing. Not the ruined thing. Something still becoming.

"Well?" he said.

"It's good." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She cleared her throat. "It's actually — Ansel, it's really good. Better than the soil has any right to make."

"That's Marguerite's doing."

"That's Marguerite's doing," she agreed, and they stood there a moment in the aisle between rows, grape juice on their hands, surrounded by the whisper of the whole village working their land, and neither of them said the other thing, the large thing, the thing that had been standing patiently behind them since the porch since the market day since the frost — but it stood there anyway, patient as weather.

* * *

They worked until the light went bronze, then copper, then gone.

The last bin rolled onto the crush pad under the string lights, which had come alive at dusk and turned the whole yard into something out of a fairytale Vesper had never been invited to. Someone had set out Ansel's pastries on the long tables. Someone else had brought fiddles. Children chased each other between the empty baskets, and the air smelled of crushed fruit and woodsmoke and, faintly, of sugar.

"We did it," Marta said, gripping Vesper's arm. "First harvest. Your grandmother would have—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, squeezed harder instead of finishing.

"Thank you," Vesper managed. "All of you. For — I know you didn't owe us anything. We showed up strangers with a dead woman's deed and a pile of debt, and you—"

"You showed up," Marta said simply. "That's the whole test, love. Everyone else just drives past."

Ansel found her later, near midnight, when the crowd had thinned to embers — a few couples by the fire, the fiddlers packing up, children asleep across benches. He carried two glasses of the press-day juice, cloudy and sweet, and wordlessly handed her one, and they climbed together to the crush pad above the yard where the press sat silent and gleaming under the lights.

From up there the village looked like spilled gold. Beyond it, the vineyard ran black to the tree line, and beyond that the sky did its enormous indifferent work.

"To Marguerite," Ansel said.

"To Marguerite." They touched glasses. The juice was cold and tasted of the morning, of the whole day compressed into liquid — sun, sweat, snipped stems, laughter carrying up the rows.

"She'd have hated this party," Vesper said. "Too many people touching her fruit."

"She'd have loved it and pretended to hate it." Ansel settled onto the edge of the pad, elbows on knees, and after a moment she sat beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly met. "She'd have stood right here counting barrels and complaining about the noise, and then she'd have written about it in that recipe book like it was the best night of her life."

"You've been reading her marginalia again."

"I've been reading everything again." He turned the glass in his hands. "There's a note in the back, next to her tart recipe. Just one line. *Feed people like the season might end.*"

Vesper looked at him. In the string-light glow his face was half gold, half shadow, and there was something raw in the shadow half, something he usually kept turned away.

"The season's ending," she said quietly.

He didn't answer right away. A fiddle started up again down in the yard, slow, almost private. "Ninety days," he said finally. "Minus eleven. That was the deal. Restore it, sell it, split it, never speak again." He laughed, but it wasn't a laugh. "I wrote that contract. On butcher paper. Like I was drafting terms of surrender."

"Why did you?" she asked. "Write it that way?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he shifted, and his shoulder came to rest against hers — lightly, deliberately, the way you'd set down something fragile — and he said, "Because I'm better at endings than beginnings. I've had practice." And before she could ask — before she could say *the photograph, the other orchard, tell me, tell me, tell me* — he turned his head and looked at her, and the distance between them was suddenly very small and very loud.

"Vesper."

"Don't," she whispered. Not because she wanted him to stop. Because she wanted him to keep going, and some old scarred part of her believed that wanting things openly was how you lost them.

"I'm going to say it anyway."

"Ansel—"

"Tomorrow," he said. "Tomorrow, after the press. There are things I have to tell you first — about my family, about why I— why I left. You deserve the whole thing before you decide anything." His hand found hers on the cold concrete between them, not closing around it, just resting there, palm to palm, an offer rather than a claim. "But tonight I needed you to know that the contract is a lie. It's been a lie since roughly week two."

Her heart was doing something undignified. "Since week two?"

"Since you corrected my soil chemistry and didn't even gloat."

She laughed — a real one, helpless — and turned her hand over beneath his so their fingers laced, and leaned in, and he leaned in, and the whole world narrowed to the warmth of his breath and the string lights blurring gold—

Headlights swept the crush pad.

A car door slammed in the yard below, heavy and deliberate, and the fiddle faltered and died mid-phrase.

Callum Reeve came up the gravel path with a leather folder under his arm and his smile already deployed, immaculate as ever, his suit somehow unwrinkled despite the hour and the dust. Behind him the few remaining villagers had gone quiet in a way Vesper recognized from boardrooms and courtrooms — the silence of people smelling blood.

"Lovely evening," Reeve called up. "Nothing smells like money quite like a crush pad."

Ansel's hand tightened around hers. Then, slowly, he withdrew it, and stood, and stepped forward so that he stood between her and the path — an instinct, she realized, not a tactic.

"It's midnight, Reeve."

"And you're both awake, and my Friday deadline is Friday." He reached the top of the pad and opened the folder with a flourish. "So let's not waste each other's time. My final offer — raised, mind you, out of respect for what you've accomplished here. Enough to clear your café debt twice over, Mr. Oyelaran. Enough for Ms. Lindqvist to disappear anywhere in the world she likes." His eyes flicked to Vesper, and the smile sharpened into something with edges. "Which I imagine holds considerable appeal. Given the circumstances."

The cold went through her like well water. "You've had a drink too many."

"I've had no drinks at all. I'm very careful." He drew a document from the folder and held it out. "Here's how tomorrow goes. You sign, the sale closes by end of month, and certain correspondence stays where it belongs — in a drawer. Certain photographs. Certain testimony from a former colleague of yours at Château Verre who's been, shall we say, cooperative." He glanced at Ansel, almost kindly. "And you, Mr. Oyelaran — I know what happened at your family's orchard. I know what the fire report says and, more interestingly, what it doesn't. I haven't used it. I'd prefer not to. But sentiment doesn't pay my investors."

The world had gone very still. Down in the yard, Marta was watching from the firelight, frowning. The string lights buzzed. Vesper could hear her own pulse.

Reeve held the papers higher. "Sign tonight, sleep well, and everyone's secrets stay in the cellar where they belong. Or refuse—" he smiled, wide and white, "—and we find out what the village thinks of its new darlings come Monday morning."

Beside her, Ansel had gone rigid, jaw tight, staring at the offered pages as though they might burn. Vesper's hands were shaking — with fury, yes, but underneath the fury, horribly, with temptation. One signature. One clean exit. No exposure, no trial by gossip, no watching Ansel learn what she'd been accused of from a newspaper instead of her own mouth.

And then she saw it — the way Ansel's hand had drifted back toward hers without his seeming to notice, finding her fingers in the dark and holding on.

"No," she said.

Reeve's smile flickered. "Ms. Lindqvist—"

"I said no." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "You can keep your money and your folder and your friendly little threats. Come Monday, tell the village whatever you like. Tell them everything. Because here's what they'll hear when you do: that a woman was scapegoated by men who forged wine and forged documents, and that the man standing next to me chose her anyway. That's not leverage, Mr. Reeve. That's just Tuesday."

For a long moment nobody moved. The fiddle player, emboldened or oblivious, struck up a new tune down in the yard.

Reeve closed the folder with a snap. "Friday," he said quietly. "My offer expires Friday. After that, everything I know goes everywhere I can put it." He looked at each of them in turn, cataloguing, filing. "Enjoy your harvest."

His footsteps crunched away down the path. The car door slammed. Headlights swung across the vines and dwindled toward the road, and then the night was theirs again — except it wasn't, because the words were still hanging there, *everything I know*, settling over the crush pad like frost.

Ansel exhaled slowly. "What does he have on you? Really?"

Vesper looked at the man she had almost kissed, whose secrets she had almost earned, and understood with terrible clarity that the next sixty seconds would decide everything — that truth offered now might save them, and truth discovered later might not.

"More than I ever told you," she said. "And if we're doing this — if we're really doing this — you need to hear all of it. Tonight. Before Friday finds out for us."

Down in the yard, the fiddle played on, and the string lights burned, and somewhere in the dark rows the picked vines stood stripped and open, waiting to see what the winter would bring.
