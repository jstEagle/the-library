## 19. First Press

The press arrived on a Tuesday, hauled up the gravel drive on the back of Halvard's flatbed truck like a bride carried over a threshold — old, dented, and heavier than it had any right to be.

"Marguerite's," Halvard said, slapping the rust-freckled frame with an affection that bordered on irreverence. "Sat in my barn for eleven years. She told me if anyone ever came back to work these vines, I was to bring it home." He looked at Vesper then, and something in his weathered face made her look away. "Guess someone came back."

Vesper ran her palm along the wooden basket slats. They were scarred and sugar-dark with decades of must, and when she leaned close she could smell them still — fermented summers, whole vintages that existed now only as memory and oak stain. Her hands, which had once been insured for more than most people's houses, trembled against the wood.

*You used to judge wine other people made,* she thought. *Now you're going to make your own.*

The thought should have terrified her. Instead it landed somewhere beneath her ribs and glowed there, low and steady, like a pilot light.

Ansel came around the side of the farmhouse carrying two mugs of coffee and stopped dead when he saw the press. "Is that—"

"Marguerite's."

He set the mugs down on the tailgate and circled it slowly, the way he circled proofing dough — assessing, respectful, already planning. "The screw mechanism's intact. Baskets need re-staving but the wood's sound." He glanced up at her, and the morning light caught the flour still dusted at his hairline; he'd been baking since five, she knew, though he'd never once mentioned it and she'd never once admitted she could smell it through her window and that it had become, without her permission, the best part of waking up. "Have you ever pressed before?"

"I've watched it done. Badly, by men who wanted me to be impressed by them."

"So no."

"So no." She heard herself laugh — actually laugh — and watched the sound land on him like sunlight.

They spent the morning re-staving the baskets with oak staves Halvard had cut years ago and never needed until now. Ansel worked the bands while Vesper fitted the wood, their hands crossing and retreating in a rhythm that had developed over ninety-some days of shared labor and that neither of them had ever named. The contract still hung on the kitchen wall behind its glass, the deadline line struck through in Ansel's careful hand after Reeve's forged option had burned to ash in the lawyer's shredder. Neither of them had taken it down. It felt less like a document now than a scar — evidence of what they'd survived, not what they'd agreed to.

By noon the press stood assembled in the barn's open bay, facing the rows, and the fruit was waiting.

* * *

Harvest resumed the way a heartbeat resumes after a long held breath.

The village came again — Halvard and his sons, the Delacroix sisters who'd taught Vesper to drive the tractor without killing the roses, old Marta from the market stand who brought more food than forty pickers could eat and stayed to eat all of it. They moved down Row One in the gold light of late afternoon, and Vesper walked the rows with clippers and a crate and felt something in her chest unlock that had been locked so long she'd forgotten it was a lock.

She'd spent three years believing her hands were finished. That was the cruelty of the scandal — not the headlines, not the estate's lawyers circling like gulls, but the quiet certainty afterward that anything she touched would turn suspect. A counterfeit vintage had passed through her cellar wearing her name, and no one — not the investigators, not the trade press, not even she herself some nights — could fully untangle negligence from complicity. So she had stopped touching. Stopped pouring, stopped tasting, stopped trusting the instrument of her own ruin.

But here, under Marguerite's sky, her hands remembered everything. The weight of a ripe cluster. The exact give of skin that said *now or never*. She filled crate after crate and the fruit was small and concentrated and stubbornly alive — these old vines that had survived frost and neglect and two strangers' worst intentions — and every cluster felt less like harvest than like testimony.

Ansel worked beside her without talking much. He didn't need to. Somewhere in the last week — since the storm, since the letter, since he'd driven back through the rain and stood dripping in her doorway and told her about the fire, about his cousin, about the silence he'd bought his survival with — the space between words had stopped being a wall and become a kind of language itself. He handed her a crate. She took it. His fingers brushed hers and neither of them flinched, and that was its own sentence, its own paragraph, its own entire chapter.

"You're smiling at grapes," he said as the sun dropped toward the ridge.

"I'm smiling at *evidence*." She held up a cluster, dark and dusted with bloom. "This is going to be honest wine. First wine I've put my name near in three years that has nothing to hide."

"It doesn't have a name yet."

"It will." She looked down the row, at the pickers moving through the vines, at the farmhouse with its lit kitchen windows, at the man holding the crate. "I'm working on it."

* * *

They pressed at dusk.

Halvard showed her the mechanics once — the filling, the pressing, the slow turning of the screw, the first pale-pink rush of free-run juice spilling into the bucket — and then stepped back and folded his arms and let her do it alone.

Her hands shook on the crank. Not from the effort. From the weight of it: the first juice this land had given in years, running through her palms' work into a vessel she'd chosen, a fermentation she'd manage, a wine that would exist because she had decided to stay.

"Slower," Ansel said quietly, beside her. Not correcting — witnessing. "Let it come to you."

She slowed. The screw turned. Juice ran clear and cold and fragrant — green apple and wet stone and something floral underneath, something that tasted like the smell of this valley in early summer — and Vesper pressed her whole first barrel standing in the amber barn light with tears running silently down her face while everyone around her pretended very hard not to notice, except Ansel, who simply handed her a clean cloth and stood shoulder to shoulder with her until she could breathe again.

"First press," Halvard declared, raising a mug of Marta's cider. "To Marguerite."

"To Marguerite," the yard echoed.

Vesper touched her mug to the rim of the press, because it seemed right, and thought: *You chose us. Both of us. You knew exactly what you were doing, you impossible woman.*

* * *

While the last barrels settled, Ansel disappeared into the farmhouse kitchen, and the smell began.

Vesper found him there an hour later, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted white, bent over Marguerite's recipe book — the real one now, rescued from the attic, its spine cracked and its margins crowded with a dead woman's handwriting. He'd claimed a page near the front. She could see the heading from the door: *Tarte aux Fruits de la Première Pression.* Tart of the First Pressing.

"You're making her celebration tart."

"I'm attempting her celebration tart." He didn't look up. "There's a note in the margin. 'If the baker is nervous, the crust will know.'" He glanced at her then, flour on his cheekbone, and something in his expression made her breath catch. "I've made this crust four times today. The first three knew."

"And this one?"

"This one I made angry."

She laughed and came to lean against the counter beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched. Through the window the crush pad lamps burned over the barrels; the night smelled of yeast and oak and crushed fruit and, faintly, of rain coming. "Angry about what?"

"About how long it took me to get here." He said it simply, without drama, the way he'd said everything since the storm — a man done hiding inside himself. "To a kitchen where I'm not counting anything. Not payments, not days, not exits." He slid the tart into the oven and closed the door and left his hand on the handle a moment, as if sealing something in. "Ninety minutes. Then we take it to the cellar."

"The cellar?"

"Where else does a first vintage get tasted?"

* * *

The cellar held the year's new barrels in one corner and Marguerite's ghosts everywhere else — racks of dusty empties, a tasting table worn concave by decades of elbows, cobwebbed bottles of vintages older than either of them. Ansel had lit two lanterns instead of using the electric bulb, and the light moved across the stone walls like water.

He set the tart on the tasting table between two glasses of the day's free-run juice, still cloudy, still cold, still more promise than wine. Then he cut two slices with the paring knife he kept in his jacket pocket — a habit she'd teased him about for weeks — and pushed one across the wood toward her.

Vesper looked at the tart. Golden crust, fruit glazed dark and shining, the edges crimped in a pattern she recognized suddenly from the recipe book's margin sketch — Marguerite's own hands, translated through Ansel's.

She picked up her fork and took a bite and forgot, briefly, how to swallow.

It tasted like the orchard. Not metaphorically — literally, impossibly, like the whole season compressed into pastry: the frost night, the smudge pots, the blisters, the market day, the rain on the vines. Sweet and sharp and a little wild at the edges, imperfect in the way only true things are.

"Well?" Ansel asked. His voice wasn't steady.

Vesper swallowed. "She'd have hated it."

His face fell —

"—because she'd have wanted to eat the whole thing herself." Vesper took another bite, and another, and laughed with her mouth full, and Ansel's relief broke across his face like dawn, and then they were both laughing, standing in a dead woman's cellar eating a dead woman's tart, alive in every way that mattered.

Then the laughter faded, the way tide fades, and what remained underneath was the thing they had both been walking around for days — politely, carefully, the way you walk around a sleeping animal.

Ansel set down his fork.

"Vesper."

"I know."

"There's no contract anymore. No deadline. No—" He stopped. Started again. "I tore up my creed. You know that. I told my regulars, out loud, in public, that the heart isn't a muscle you train. It's a—" his jaw worked, "—it's a thing you risk. Whole. Marguerite wrote that. I just finally understood it."

"I read the letter too."

"I know you did." He exhaled. "So I'm not asking for terms. There are no terms. No exit clause, no split, no ninety days. Just—" he gestured helplessly at the cellar, the barrels, the lanterns, himself, "—this. Me. If you want it. If you want *me*, not the co-heir, not the baker, not the bargain. All of it, badly organized, prone to burning things, terrible at lying about photographs in wallets."

Vesper set down her fork very slowly.

She had rehearsed surrender her whole life — the graceful retreat, the strategic withdrawal, the elegant way to lose. She had sold pieces of herself so many times she'd kept a mental ledger of what remained. And here was this man offering her the one transaction with no ledger at all, no columns, no balance, nothing to protect because everything was already on the table, glazed in Marguerite's fruit and trembling slightly in the lantern light.

"Surrender without defeat," she said aloud, not meaning to say it aloud.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something my grandmother—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "*Our* grandmother might have said." She crossed the small distance between them. "Ansel. I spent three years being afraid of my own name. You're asking me to sign it to something again."

"I'm asking you to sign it to *us*."

"Yes," Vesper Lindqvist said, and her voice did not shake at all. "Yes."

And then, surrounded by oak and yeast and the slow breathing of new wine, she kissed him.

It was not the almost-kiss of harvest eve, interrupted, ambushed, stolen. It was unhurried. It was certain. His hand came up to cradle her jaw the way he handled ripe fruit — like something that had waited all season for exactly this moment and deserved gentleness — and she fisted her hands in his flour-dusted shirt and pulled him closer and felt the last compartment in her chest, the last locked room, swing open and flood with light.

When they broke apart, the lanterns had burned lower and the tart sat forgotten and somewhere above them the rain had begun, soft against the cellar's stone throat, and Ansel rested his forehead against hers and laughed — a low, undone sound, the laugh of a man whose containment strategy had failed completely and gloriously.

"For the record," he murmured, "the crust was perfect."

"The crust was perfect," she agreed. "Everything was."

His thumb traced her cheekbone. "So what happens now? We didn't draft terms."

Vesper looked around the cellar — at the barrels that would become their first vintage, at the dusty racks, at the tasting table where a dead woman's recipe had fed two living ones. She thought of the deed in the lawyer's office, two names that could be rewritten. She thought of the café in the city, dark three months, waiting. She thought of everything ahead — bottling, labeling, reopening, building — and felt not fear but appetite, vast and unashamed.

"Now," she said, "we make it official. Properly. In ink." She smiled against his mouth. "But this time, we write it ourselves."

Above them, through the floorboards, the farmhouse phone began to ring — insistent, unfamiliar, the ringtone of the lawyer's office line — and neither of them moved to answer it, not yet, not quite yet, holding on to the last unsupervised minute of the world they'd built in the dark.

The phone rang on. The rain came down. The wine slept in its oak, dreaming of spring.
