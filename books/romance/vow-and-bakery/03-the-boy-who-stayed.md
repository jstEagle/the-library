## 3. The Boy Who Stayed

The press woke before Elian did, the way it always did in October — a low mechanical groan rolling up through the floorboards of the cider house like something old turning over in its sleep. He'd learned to sleep through it the way sailors learn to sleep through weather. You didn't fight the sound. You let it hold you.

He lay for a moment in the loft above the pressing room, listening to the building breathe. The smell came next, as it did every morning: crushed apple and wet wood and the faint vinegar ghost of last year's batches, layered so deep into the beams that no amount of scrubbing would ever get it out. His mother had said once that you could tear this place down to the studs and it would still smell of apples. She'd said it laughing. That was before.

Elian swung his legs off the cot and pulled on the flannel shirt hanging from the nail by the ladder — the one with the burn hole near the cuff where he'd leaned too close to the pasteurizer two winters ago. The floorboards were cold. Everything in Salt Hollow was cold by mid-October, even the things that were supposed to be warm.

Downstairs, the press was already chewing through the first bins of Winesap, and Marcus Teague — nineteen, perpetually late, perpetually indispensable — was feeding fruit into the hopper with his headphones in, bobbing his head at some rhythm only he could hear.

"You're supposed to sort those first," Elian called over the noise. "There's rot in the east bins. I told you yesterday."

Marcus pulled out one earbud. "I sorted."

"You sorted *fast*."

"I sorted *enthusiastically*."

Elian almost smiled. It was the closest thing he had to a workforce: one teenager with good arms and bad attention, paid partly in cash and partly in cider, both currencies increasingly theoretical. The Voss Cider Company — a name that sounded grander on the label than it had any right to, given that the company currently consisted of one man, one boy, one press older than either of them, and eleven acres of orchard that had spent the last decade quietly deciding whether it wanted to keep living.

He took the sorting table himself, because if he wanted it done right he would do it while also doing six other things, and began pulling the bruised fruit from the conveyor. Outside, the fog was still burning off the rows, the pear trees emerging from it one by one like figures wading out of the sea. The Marchetti pears. Everyone still called them that, though there hadn't been a Marchetti standing on this land in twelve years. The trees didn't know that. Trees kept their own ledgers.

His hands moved without him. Brown spot, toss. Soft shoulder, toss. Good fruit, down the chute. He'd been doing this since he was eight years old, since his father used to lift him onto this same table and say, *Feel it, Elian. An apple tells you everything if your hands are honest.* His father's hands had not been honest. That was the whole story of Salt Hollow, more or less, told in the space between one sentence and the next.

The letter was sitting on the office desk when he went in at noon, propped against the coffee maker like it knew it was important.

He saw the law firm's name first — Hollis & Crane, Port Angeles — and felt the familiar tightening behind his sternum. Lawyers wrote to him exactly twice a year: once about the loan, once about the lease. Both letters said the same thing in different fonts. *Your time is running out.*

But this envelope was different. Thicker. Cream-colored instead of white. And the name typed in the address window was not Elian Voss.

It was *Estate of Rosalind Marchetti.*

He stood very still in the doorway of the little office, one hand still on the frame, and let the name settle over him the way fog settled over the rows. Rosalind Marchetti. Mrs. R., the whole town had called her, though nobody under forty remembered why. She had run the orchard office back when the orchard was an empire, back when the packing shed ran three shifts and the trucks went out heavy every morning and the name Marchetti meant something other than *ruin*. She had signed his father's first supply contract. She had, according to his mother, once driven twenty miles through an ice storm to bring them a ham when the Vosses' well froze and they had nothing but apples and pride.

She had died on a Tuesday, three weeks ago. The whole town had closed early for the funeral, which was strange, because half the town claimed to hate what her family had done and the other half claimed not to care, and yet somehow every shop on Harbor Street had gone dark between two and four o'clock that day.

Elian picked up the envelope. It was addressed to him. Inside the estate notification, he could feel other pages, folded, official, dense.

He opened it at the sorting table because his legs wouldn't carry him to a chair.

He read the first paragraph twice. Then he read it aloud, because the words refused to mean anything until they'd been given air.

"*Be advised that the Last Will and Testament of Rosalind Anne Marchetti conveys title to the real property known as the Marchetti Orchard, parcel 114-C, Salt Hollow County, jointly and in equal shares to August Wren Marchetti and Elian James Voss...*"

Marcus chose that moment to come crashing through the door with an empty bin on his shoulder. "Hey, boss, the east pump's making that noise again, the one like a — " He stopped. "You okay? You look like somebody died."

"Somebody did," Elian said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like it belonged to someone standing further away than he was. "Three weeks ago. And she just handed me her orchard."

Marcus set the bin down slowly, the way you set things down around a man holding something sharp. "Whose orchard?"

"The Marchetti orchard." Elian laughed, and the laugh came out wrong too, cracked down the middle. "Half of it. Half of it is mine. Legally. On paper. In ink." He held up the letter as evidence, as if Marcus might dispute it, as if anyone could dispute it. "And the other half belongs to August Marchetti."

Marcus frowned. "Who's that?"

"Nobody," Elian said. "Nobody who lives here. Nobody who's been here in twelve years." He looked down at the letter again, at the name in the second line, and felt twelve years compress into a single point, sharp as a nail. "She left."

* * *

That night he couldn't sleep, which was nothing new, but the shape of the sleeplessness was new — less like dread and more like vertigo. He climbed down from the loft at two in the morning and walked out into the rows with a flashlight he didn't turn on, because he knew this ground the way he knew the backs of his hands, better maybe, and because some part of him wanted to feel it blind.

The fog had come back in off the water. It lay in the aisles between the trees like spilled milk, and his boots made soft dark prints through it. The pears were nearly done — another week, maybe ten days, and then the harvest would be whatever it was going to be, which this year looked like barely enough to cover the loan payment and not nearly enough to cover the equipment note behind it. He'd stopped saying that number out loud, even to himself. Numbers said out loud became true.

He stopped at the oldest tree, the Bartlett his grandmother's grandmother had planted, the one everybody called the Matriarch, its trunk split and scarred and still stubbornly alive, still putting out fruit that tasted like nothing grown anywhere else. He put his palm flat against the bark. It was damp with fog. Under his hand the tree felt less like wood than like skin, like something waiting.

"Twelve years," he said to it. He talked to the trees sometimes. Marcus thought it was a joke; it wasn't. "Twelve years she stays away, and now she owns me."

Except she didn't own him. That was the thing he couldn't stop turning over. They owned each other, or they owned the same thing, or they were owned by the same dead woman who had sat in that office above the packing shed for fifty years and apparently, in her final act, decided to play God with two people's lives. Joint tenancy. Equal shares. Right of survivorship. He'd read the legal language four times now, lying in the dark with the flashlight under the blanket like a kid reading contraband. If one of them died, the other got everything. If they agreed, they could sell. If they disagreed —

If they disagreed, they were stuck. Glued together. Two names on one deed, tied to eleven acres of dying trees and a town that remembered everything.

He should have felt angry. Some part of him noted, distantly, that anger was the appropriate response, the expected response, the response his father would have had, roaring about Marchettis and thieves and blood money. But the anger wasn't coming. What was coming instead was memory, unbidden, in pieces, the way wreckage comes ashore.

August at fourteen, standing on a cider barrel in the barn, hair escaping its braid, declaring that she was going to be a marine biologist and live on a boat and never wear shoes again. He'd been fifteen and had believed every word, mostly because he wanted to be on the boat too, though he'd have died before saying so.

August teaching him to whistle through a blade of grass behind the packing shed, both of them laughing so hard they couldn't hear the trucks.

August the last time he ever saw her, seventeen and gray-faced, standing in the driveway while men carried boxes out of the big house, her father's hand clamped on her shoulder like a clamp on a hose. She had looked at him across forty feet of gravel and he had looked away first. That was the detail that lived in him like a splinter. He had looked away first. He had been eighteen years old and his family was drowning and the Marchetti name was the water coming in, and he had looked away from the only person he had ever — 

He made himself stop there. That was as far as the memory was allowed to go. Past that point lay the rest of it: the audit, the fraud charges, the foreclosure notices stapled to the packing shed door in the rain, his father drinking himself silent in the cider house, his mother working doubles at the diner in Port Angeles and coming home with her feet wrapped in ace bandages, smiling anyway, always smiling, saying *we'll be fine, we've got the land, we've got the trees.* The land had saved them, technically. The bank had let the Vosses keep the cottage and the cider operation in exchange for everything else, a mercy arranged by lawyers, and his father had called it mercy right up until the night he drove his truck into the quarry pond, and after that nobody called it anything at all.

And all of it — every bit of it — had flowed downstream from one signature that wasn't real. Everyone in town knew the story by heart: Gerald Marchetti, the great benefactor, caught forging supplier contracts, cooking the books, skimming his own orchard into ruin. Except Elian had spent enough nights lately going through his father's old ledgers — looking for anything the bank might accept as collateral, finding instead columns of numbers that didn't add up in ways that made his skin prickle — and the numbers were starting to whisper something else. Something about payments. Something about who benefited when the Marchetti empire fell, and who bought the parcels afterward, cheap and quiet, through holding companies with bland names.

He wasn't ready to follow that thread all the way down. Not yet. Tonight the thread was simply this: a cream-colored envelope, a dead woman's will, and a girl on a cider barrel who had promised never to wear shoes again.

The fog thickened. Somewhere out toward the road, a car passed, headlights sweeping through the mist like a lantern dragged along the bottom of a lake. Elian stood among the trees with his hands in his pockets and tried to imagine her arriving. Because she would arrive. He understood that now, with the certainty of a man who has read a contract twice. Rosalind Marchetti had not written that deed carelessly. Whatever else the old woman had been — and the town told a hundred stories, saint and tyrant and witch — she had not done this by accident. She had put two names on that paper the way you graft two scions onto one rootstock: deliberately, precisely, knowing exactly what kind of tree she wanted to grow.

Which meant August Marchetti was coming home.

He found, to his considerable irritation, that the thought produced not dread but something quicker, lighter, lodged high in his chest like the first swallow of a bright cider. Curiosity. Twelve years of resentment and debt and grief, and underneath all of it, alive as a seed under snow: *what does she look like now? Does she still talk fast when she's nervous? Did she ever get her boat?*

"Damn it," he said quietly, to the Matriarch, to the fog, to the dead woman who had engineered all of this from beyond the reach of anyone's argument. "Damn it, Mrs. R."

The tree said nothing. Trees kept their own counsel. But when he turned back toward the cider house, its windows glowing amber in the dark, he caught the scent of the first fermenting batch riding the cold air — sweet beginning to turn sharp, sugar becoming something stronger — and he thought: *that's what this is.* Sweetness with a edge coming. Something that would either become wonderful or go to vinegar, and no way to know which until it was far too late to stop it.

Inside, he pinned the lawyer's letter to the corkboard above the desk, next to the loan notice and the photograph of his parents on the beach at Copper Cove, young and sunburned and unaware. Two names on one deed. He stared at the second name until the letters blurred.

Then he got out the ledger again, and the flashlight, and started, at last, to follow the thread.
