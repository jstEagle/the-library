## 17. Reeve's Second Offer

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Elian would remember later because Tuesdays were the days he drove the truck to the co-op in Corvallis and back, four hours of highway with nothing but the radio's static-voiced weatherman for company. The envelope was thick cream stock, heavier than anything that had ever come to the cider house before, and it sat on the workbench like something dropped from another world — which, in a way, it was.

MERIDIAN DEVELOPMENT GROUP was embossed across the flap in silver letters that caught the low winter light and threw it back cold.

He stood there a long time with his hands still smelling of apple pomace, the press behind him ticking as it cooled, and he did not open it. He knew what it was. He'd known since the courthouse steps, since Callum Reeve had shaken his hand with a grip like a man testing fruit for ripeness — squeezing just enough to learn what gave.

* * *

He opened it at the kitchen table in the cottage, under the lamp with the crooked shade that had been his mother's, though the cottage itself had never been hers. That was the strange arithmetic of this place: he lived in the Marchetti house now, slept beneath the quilted coverlet some Marchetti grandmother had stitched, drank his morning coffee from a mug with a faded pear painted on its side, and every day the walls reminded him whose home this had been first. August had not asked him to leave. He kept waiting for her to ask him to leave.

The letter was two pages, single-spaced, on paper so fine it felt like skin. He read it once standing, then sat down and read it again slowly, letting each sentence land where it wanted to land.

*Mr. Voss,* it began. *Meridian Development Group wishes to extend to you, personally and privately, an opportunity that we believe aligns with your interests and your family's legacy.*

There it was. *Your family's legacy.* As if Reeve hadn't spent three weeks making calls, buying debts, and letting the word *foreclosure* drift through Salt Hollow like smoke through a cracked window. As if the legacy weren't the orchard itself — forty acres of pear trees his father had pruned and his grandfather had planted, the rows Elian had walked since before he could walk, the cider house his great-uncle had raised beam by beam in 1931.

The offer was simple. Meridian would pay Elian Voss, individually, one point nine million dollars for his half of the deed. Not the land — his *signature*. His name alone, released from the joint tenancy, leaving August Marchetti holding her half of a property Meridian would then acquire through her, or through the bank, or through whatever mechanism proved necessary. The letter did not spell out what would happen to August. It didn't need to. One point nine million dollars was not a price for land. It was a price for a betrayal, itemized and notarized.

And it was enough. God help him, it was enough. It would erase the loan — the loan his father had taken out the year the scandal broke, the loan that had metastasized through the years like rot moving up a trunk, quietly, invisibly, until last month when the bank's letter arrived with its polite language of *remediation* and *default proceedings*. It would buy new presses, stainless tanks, a bottling line. He could start over somewhere the name Voss didn't taste of ash. He could walk away from Salt Hollow the way August had walked away, clean-shaven and unburdened, and let the town keep its memories without him in them.

He set the letter down. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He'd expected them to shake.

Outside, the wind had come up off the water, the way it did in the late afternoons, carrying the smell of brine and cold mud and, faintly, impossibly, the green ghost of the trees themselves — dormant now, leafless, but alive underneath their bark the way a sleeping animal is alive. He stood at the window and looked down the long slope of the orchard, row after row of bare limbs against a sky the color of pewter, and he thought about his father.

His father had died believing the Marchettis destroyed him. That belief had been the last warm thing he owned, and he'd held it the way a man holds a coal in winter, cupping it, feeding it, passing it to his son in fragments over years of dinners and drives and slow evenings on the porch. *They ruined us, Elian. Remember that.* And Elian had remembered. He had built a life on the remembering.

But lately the ledger — his father's old ledger, the one he'd found in the packing shed with its careful columns and its one strange entry — had been sitting in the bottom drawer of the workbench like a stone in a shoe. *Payment received. M. Co.* Three words in his father's handwriting that didn't fit any story Elian had ever been told. He hadn't shown August yet. He wasn't sure why. Maybe because showing her meant admitting the ground might be shifting, and if the ground shifted, then everything he'd hated her for might turn out to be something else entirely, and he didn't know who he'd be without the hating.

Now this letter. Now this number.

* * *

Callum Reeve came to the orchard himself on Thursday.

Elian heard the car before he saw it — a low German engine note climbing the gravel drive, too smooth for Salt Hollow, too expensive for the potholes. Reeve stepped out in a charcoal coat that probably cost more than the cider house roof, no hat despite the wind, his gray hair swept back and his smile already in place, the way other men put on gloves.

"Mr. Voss." He extended his hand. "Thank you for seeing me."

"I don't remember agreeing to."

"You will." Reeve's smile deepened by a millimeter. "May I?"

He didn't wait for permission. He walked toward the cider house with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once been told no by a building, and Elian followed because following was better than watching him wander.

Inside, Reeve stopped in the middle of the floor and turned a slow circle, taking in the old press, the barrels stacked along the north wall, the light falling dusty and gold through the high windows. "Remarkable," he said. "You know, most people see decay when they look at a place like this. I see potential. That's the difference between me and the bank, Mr. Voss. The bank sees collateral. I see what this valley could become."

"What it could become," Elian said, "or what you could take from it?"

Reeve laughed — a real laugh, warm, delighted, the laugh of a man genuinely enjoying himself. "Oh, I like you. August said you were stubborn. She undersold it." He pulled a leather folio from inside his coat and laid it on the pressing table, opening it with two fingers like a man dealing cards. "Let's skip the part where I pretend this is a courtesy call. You've read my letter."

"I have."

"And?"

"And nothing. My answer is the same as it was. We're not selling."

"We," Reeve repeated softly. He picked up a barrel stave shaving from the table and turned it over, examining it as though it were a rare coin. "That's an interesting word, *we*. Two weeks ago you couldn't stand in the same row as that woman. Now you're a *we*. Love works fast in cold weather." He set the shaving down. "Here's what I know about you, Elian. I know your loan comes due in full on the fifteenth of March. I know the bank has already spoken to us about the note. I know that if this harvest fails — and the soil tests say it will fail, the root damage alone guarantees a thin crop — this property goes to auction, and I buy it for pennies, and you get nothing. Nothing except the memory of being stubborn."

"The soil tests—"

"Were falsified." Reeve said it mildly, the way a man says *it's raining*, and Elian felt the floor tilt under him. "Or rather, they were *arranged*. But that's neither here nor there. What matters is the arithmetic. Auction: you get nothing. Sale to me, jointly: you get half of a fair price, maybe eight hundred thousand apiece after the bank takes its cut. Or—" he tapped the folio, "—you sign alone, today, and you walk out of here with one point nine million, debt-free, tomorrow morning. No auction. No waiting. No August."

The wind pressed against the cider house walls, and somewhere up in the rafters an old swallow's nest shifted and settled.

"Why her?" Elian asked. "Why go around her? If you want the land, make her the same offer."

Something flickered across Reeve's face — quick, quickly buried, but Elian saw it. A tightening at the eyes. The smile staying on while something behind it recalculated.

"Ms. Marchetti is a more complicated negotiation," Reeve said. "She has certain... attachments. Sentimental history. You, Mr. Voss, have a debt. Debts are simpler. Debts are honest." He closed the folio and slid it across the table until it rested an inch from Elian's hand. "I'll leave this with you. The offer stands for seventy-two hours. After that, the number changes — and not in your favor."

Elian looked down at the papers. His own name was printed there, *Elian James Voss*, in a font chosen by someone who had never met him, above a signature line as blank and white as fresh snow.

"She trusted you," Reeve added, almost gently, gathering his coat. "That's the tragedy of people like August. They trust. And trust, in my experience, is just leverage that hasn't been priced yet." At the door he paused, silhouetted against the gray light, and looked back at the press, the barrels, the whole patient architecture of a dying trade. "One point nine million, son. Think about what your father would have done."

"That," Elian said quietly, "is exactly what I'm afraid of."

Reeve's smile didn't waver. "Seventy-two hours."

Then the car took him away down the drive, gravel popping under the tires, and the orchard swallowed the sound, and Elian stood alone in the cider house with the folio lying on the table between his hands like a wound that had learned to dress itself.

* * *

He didn't sleep that night.

He sat in the cottage kitchen until the lamp burned low, the folio open in front of him, and he did the math the way he did everything — slowly, honestly, hating every honest answer. One point nine million. The loan: gone. The interest that had been bleeding him since October: gone. New equipment, a real bottling line, maybe even the coastal parcel he'd dreamed about once, years ago, before everything contracted to survival. He could be free. Free was a word he hadn't allowed himself to think in years, and now it sat across the table from him wearing a charcoal coat and offering its hand.

And August.

He thought about her hands in the soil two weeks ago, both of them kneeling in the third row with the pruning journal open between them, her finger tracing their grandmother's cramped handwriting about cut roots and poisoned tests, her face going through grief and anger and something fiercer than either. He thought about how she'd looked at him when they found the entry — not as an enemy, not as a stranger, but as the only other person alive who understood what it meant to inherit a wound. He thought about the way she'd said *we* without flinching, as if the word had always belonged to them and they'd only just gotten it back.

If he signed, she'd lose the orchard. Not gradually, not through some fair fight she could contest — instantly, structurally, the moment his name left the deed. Meridian would close around her half like water closing over a stone. And she would know — she would *know* — that the boy her father ruined had finished the job, and she would carry that the way he had carried his father's hatred, faithfully, for decades, handing it down to whoever came after.

Unless she never had to know. Unless he signed quietly, took the money, disappeared, and let her believe the bank did it, the auction did it, fate did it. People believed what was easiest. He could be a rumor instead of a signature.

He got up and poured the last of the season's cider, the batch he'd pressed in November from the few trees that had still borne, tart and thin and honest. He drank it looking at the dark window, at his own reflection ghosted over the black shapes of the trees, and his reflection looked like his father. That was the thing no one warned you about — that the older you got, the more the dead moved into your face.

*Think about what your father would have done.*

His father would have signed. His father, who had taught him that a Voss keeps score, that a Voss never forgets, that forgiveness was a luxury for people who hadn't been robbed — his father would have taken the money and called it justice and drunk to it every night for the rest of his life.

But his father had also written *payment received, M. Co.* in a ledger he'd hidden in the packing shed, and Elian still didn't know what those words meant, and until he did, every lesson his father ever taught him was built on ground that might be sand.

Dawn came up gray and slow, the way it did in February, and with it came the sound of boots on frost-hardened gravel — August, early, a thermos under her arm and the pruning journal tucked against her ribs, coming up the path from the direction of the oldest trees. Through the window Elian watched her stop at the edge of the dooryard and look up at the cottage, at the light in his window, and even from here he could see her breath hanging in the cold air, could see her hesitate the way she always hesitated, one beat, before committing to crossing his threshold.

The folio lay on the table behind him. Seventy hours left on the clock.

He opened the door before she could knock, and the cold rushed in around him, and she looked up at him with her chapped cheeks and her wary eyes and said, "I found something else in the journal. About the west field. You're going to want to see it."

And behind him, on the kitchen table, the blank signature line waited — white as snow, patient as debt — for a name that hadn't decided yet whose it was.
