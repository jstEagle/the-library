## 18. The Almost-Betrayal

The call came at six in the morning, when the light through the cottage kitchen window was still the color of weak tea and the kettle had only just begun its low complaint.

August was standing at the counter with her grandmother's pruning journal open beside a bowl of oatmeal she'd forgotten to eat, tracing with one finger the entry from October 1979 that read, in Rosalind Marchetti's small, furious hand: *Soil test came back wrong again. Third time. I don't believe in coincidence three times running.* She'd slept badly, dreaming of turtles — of hatchlings turning the wrong way down the beach, drawn toward headlights instead of moonlight — and she woke with the particular heaviness that came from carrying a secret too large for one body. The journal lay between her and Elian like a lit fuse now. They were supposed to go through the rest of it together today. He'd said so last night on the porch, his breath fogging in the cold, his voice careful and warm at once: *Tomorrow. We'll start at the beginning.*

Then the phone rang.

"Ms. Marchetti? Dana Okafor, from Whitfield and Associates." A stranger's voice, brisk as frost. "I'm calling because we received an inquiry this week regarding the Marchetti-Voss parcel. A purchase inquiry. I'm obligated to inform all parties of record before any documentation moves forward."

August's hand went still on the page. "I'm sorry — what kind of inquiry?"

"A formal offer of purchase. From Meridian Development Group. It appears to have been initiated by your co-owner, Mr. Voss. There was a meeting scheduled for yesterday afternoon at their regional office."

The kettle screamed. August didn't move toward it. The sound filled the kitchen like something dying.

"That can't be right," she heard herself say, and her voice sounded far away, underwater, the voice of a woman watching a nest wash out to sea. "He wouldn't — we agreed. We agreed we'd wait until we understood what happened to the soil tests. We *agreed*."

"I can only tell you what's in our file, Ms. Marchetti. Mr. Voss met with a Callum Reeve yesterday at two o'clock. Our records show he left with preliminary documents." A pause, professional and pitying. "You're under no obligation to sign anything. But if both parties don't sign, nothing proceeds. I wanted you to hear it from us first rather than—"

"From him." August set the journal down very carefully, the way you set down something fragile when your hands want to throw it. "Yes. Thank you."

She hung up. The kettle boiled itself hoarse and kept screaming until she crossed the kitchen and lifted it off the flame without thinking, and then she just stood there holding it, steam rising past her wrists, scalding-adjacent heat blooming against her skin, and she thought: *of course*. Of course. Of course he did.

Because hadn't some quiet, buried part of her been waiting for exactly this since the day she'd come back to Salt Hollow? Since the day she'd stood in her grandmother's doorway and found him living there, wearing the house like a coat he'd stolen fair and square? Some part of her that had counted turtles for eleven years precisely so she would never have to count on anyone — that part had whispered, all along, *he will leave too, they always leave, or worse, they stay and take everything*.

Her father had taught her that lesson with the whole town as his classroom. Her mother had taught it by driving away before the scandal even broke, before the worst of it, leaving August to answer the door for reporters. And now Elian — Elian, who had held her hand over the pruning journal two nights ago like it was a holy thing, who had said *we'll figure out what they did to this land together* — Elian had walked into Meridian's glass office and sat down across from Callum Reeve and listened to numbers.

She put the kettle down. She did not cry. Crying was a luxury of people who still believed in being caught when they fell.

* * *

By seven she had dressed in work clothes and walked the orchard rows alone, because walking was what she did instead of breaking. The frost had come hard overnight; every blade of cover crop wore a rime of white, and the bare pear trees stood in their long ranks like a congregation waiting for news. Her breath hung around her in clouds. Somewhere downslope, the cider house was dark, no smoke from the chimney, no sound of Elian moving through his mornings — and she hated how quickly she catalogued his absence, how attuned she'd become to the geography of him without ever meaning to be.

Two o'clock yesterday. While she had been in the packing shed cataloguing damaged irrigation lines, he had been shaking hands or not shaking hands, listening or negotiating, and he hadn't said one word about it. Not one. He'd sat across from her at dinner — soup, bread, the lamp between them — and asked her about the journal, about the 1979 entries, and let her talk, and the whole time he must have had Reeve's offer folded in his pocket like a knife.

Or maybe not a knife. Maybe a lifeline. That was the thing about drowning men; they grabbed whatever floated past, and Elian was drowning — she knew the shape of his debt the way you know the shape of a bruise you keep accidentally pressing. The bank letters came addressed in red. She'd seen them on the cider house table, squared into neat anxious stacks.

But knowing why a man betrays you doesn't un-betray you. Knowing the wound was made carelessly rather than cruelly doesn't make it bleed less.

At the top of the hill, where the oldest pear tree stood — the one her grandmother had marked in the journal with a small pressed flower and the words *planted by Nonna's own hands, 1948* — August stopped and pressed her palm against the bark. It was cold and furrowed and alive underneath, faintly, in the way of things sleeping but not dead.

"You knew them both," she said to the tree. "You knew what they were. Why didn't you warn me?"

The tree said nothing, being a tree, and being wiser than her.

When she got back to the cottage, Elian's truck was in the drive. He was on the porch steps with two mugs of coffee, and when he saw her coming up the lane his whole face changed — opened, brightened, the way it had been doing more and more lately, in ways that had started to feel like a language she was almost ready to speak.

"Morning," he called. "I made yours with the honey. I found actual honey at Hollis's store, I don't know how long it's been sitting there, but—"

"Did you meet with Callum Reeve?"

The mug stopped halfway to his mouth. She watched the brightness drain out of his face in real time, watched him understand, and some cold clinical part of her noted that he didn't deny it fast enough. That was the detail that mattered. That was the detail she would carry.

"August." He set the mug down on the step. "Okay. Yes. Let me explain—"

"There's nothing to explain." She climbed the porch steps because the cottage was hers too, half hers, and she refused to stand in the yard like a visitor being turned away. "Dana Okafor called me at six this morning. Meridian filed a formal purchase inquiry. Initiated by you. Yesterday, two o'clock. You sat across a table from the man who is sabotaging this orchard — who has probably been sabotaging it since before either of us could drive — and you didn't think I deserved to know?"

"I went to tell him no." His voice was steady but his hands weren't; she saw them curl and uncurl at his sides. "I swear to you, I went to tell him no. He called me, he said he had information about my father, and I thought — I thought if I went, I could find out what he knows. What his firm knows. About the ledgers, about the scandal. I thought I could get something useful out of him."

"And did you?" She heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used on colleagues who mishandled nesting sites, the voice that meant the conversation was over. "What did you get, Elian? Because from where I'm standing, what you got was a meeting on record. A paper trail. You gave him exactly what he wanted — proof that one of us is willing to deal."

"I took nothing." He stepped toward her and she stepped back, and she saw the hurt land on him like weather. "August, I took *nothing*. I told him no. I walked out. I came straight home and I was going to tell you last night, I tried to, and then you were talking about your grandmother's handwriting and you looked — God, you looked happy, actually happy, for the first time since you got here, and I couldn't—"

"You couldn't." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You couldn't find a way to mention that you'd spent the afternoon with the enemy. So instead you let me plan our day around going through evidence together. You let me believe we were—" She stopped. The word *partners* sat in her mouth like a stone. She swallowed it.

"We are," he said quietly. "Whatever else is true, we are."

"You don't get to decide that anymore." She pulled off her gloves, finger by finger, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. "Here's what happens now. I'm signing nothing. No offer, no option, no document with Meridian's name anywhere near it, and I'd advise you, legally and personally, to do the same. Dana Okafor will confirm in writing that I refuse consent. If you want to sell your half of something that can't be sold, that's your funeral. But you will do it without me, and you will do it after telling me the truth the first time instead of the second."

"August—"

"And don't follow me." She was inside the doorway now, the old cottage smelling of woodsmoke and dried lavender and eleven years of a dead woman's loneliness, and her eyes were burning and she would not let them. "That's the one thing, Elian. Don't follow me around this property apologizing. I've watched men apologize their whole lives and mean none of it. My father apologized to the entire town from the courthouse steps and he was lying through his teeth. I have no way of knowing which kind you are. So give me room to find out."

She closed the door. Gently. That was the worst part, she thought, leaning against it with her heart slamming — gently, with a soft click, like ending something that had barely begun.

Through the window she watched him stand on the porch a long moment, coffee going cold in both mugs, and then pick hers up and carry it inside anyway and set it on the counter by the journal, and walk out to the orchard alone.

* * *

The days that followed arranged themselves into a cold geometry.

They worked the same land and avoided each other's orbits with the precision of planets. He took the east rows; she took the west. They passed notes about irrigation schedules taped to the packing shed door, terse as telegrams: *Pump #2 needs a gasket. Frost forecast Thursday. Cover the young trees.* Once, she found her wheelbarrow moved to the exact spot where she'd need it and her gloves, which she'd left damp, hung dry and warmed by the cider house stove, and she stood looking at them for a long moment with her throat tight before putting them on and saying nothing to anyone.

The town noticed. Towns always notice. At Hollis's store, Mrs. Adeyemi rang up her purchases and said, delicately, "He's over at the cider house till midnight most nights, you know. Man looks like he's aging in those barrels himself." August paid and smiled and said the orchard was busy this time of year, and drove home with the accusation sitting in her chest like a swallowed hook.

Because here was the thing she wouldn't say out loud, the thing that made the anger worse instead of better: she missed him. Eleven days of silence and she missed him with a specificity that frightened her — the way he hummed off-key when the press was running, the way he said *look, look* when he found something in the soil, the way he'd started saying *we* without flinching. She had built her whole adult life on the principle that missing people was optional, a discipline, a muscle you simply declined to exercise. And this man had gotten under that in six weeks flat, and then handed her the perfect reason to build the wall back higher.

Old wounds are efficient that way. They tell you the new pain is proof of the old pattern, and they never mention that the pattern might be broken this time, that the man on the porch might have been telling the truth, that *I went to tell him no* might be the plain unvarnished fact. Doubt doesn't need evidence. It needs only a door left open, and grief holds every door.

On the twelfth night she sat up late with the pruning journal, reading entries she'd already read, because reading them felt like company. And there — in the margin of a page dated March 1981, in ink faded to the color of weak tea — she found a line she was certain she'd never seen before, though she must have skimmed past it a dozen times:

*R. Voss came by tonight. He knows who is buying up the debt. He wants to meet me at the tree. Says he has papers. God forgive us both for what we let them do to each other's children.*

August read it four times. The fire ticked. Outside, the wind moved through the bare orchard with a sound like the sea, like the beaches she'd fled to, like everything that had ever carried her away from this place.

*R. Voss.* Robert Voss. Elian's father — the ruined man, the ruined family, the whole architecture of blame she'd inherited along with the deed. And her grandmother meeting him secretly beneath the oldest pear tree, with papers, with words like *God forgive us both*, twenty years before either August or Elian was born.

She looked at the clock. It was past eleven. Across the dark field, the cider house window glowed — he was awake, he was always awake now, aging in his barrels — and every instinct she owned said *wait, verify, protect yourself, you've only just built the wall back up.*

But her grandmother had spent her whole life writing in margins, hiding truths in places her granddaughter would only find when she was ready. And Rosalind Marchetti had not written *go alone*.

August pulled on her coat. She copied the entry into her notebook, tucked the journal under her arm like a shield, and stepped out into the cold, crossing the frozen ground toward the one lit window, rehearsing nothing, planning nothing, knowing only that the wall had a door in it after all, and that she was the one who had to open it.

The cider house door was unlocked. Inside, Elian looked up from a ledger older than both of them, his face exhausted and hopeful and afraid, and before she could decide whether she'd come to fight or to listen, he said:

"I found something too. About my father. And August — I think you need to sit down."
