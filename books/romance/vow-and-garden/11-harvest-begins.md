## 11. Harvest Begins

### Lena

The first morning of harvest, Lena woke before her alarm and lay in the dark of the farmhouse guest room listening to the orchard breathe.

That was the only word for it. Seven years she'd lived in the city, where dawn arrived as a sound—the groan of delivery trucks, the hiss of a steam wand, her own name called over the clatter of cups—and she had forgotten that places could make noise without making demands. The orchard exhaled. Wind moved through ten thousand leaves with a sound like slow water, and somewhere down the slope a crow said one dry thing and thought better of it.

She got up, dressed in the dark, and stood at the window watching Elias already crossing the yard with two ladders on his shoulder, moving through the blue half-light like a man who had done this every morning of his life. Which, she supposed, he had. Before everything. Before her mother's signature had cut his family out of their own story and sent him walking down her grandmother's drive without looking back.

*Without looking back.* She'd told herself that version so many times it had worn smooth as river stone. But she'd seen him look back now—she'd caught it twice this week, that flicker of his eyes toward the house when he thought no one was watching—and the smoothness of the old story was starting to feel less like truth than like polish.

Downstairs, the kitchen smelled of coffee and bruised apples. He'd left a mug on the counter for her, which was either courtesy or strategy; after six weeks of pruning season they had developed a whole silent language of small considerations, each one deniable.

"Pickers arrive at seven," he said when she came out to the barn, not turning from the crate-stacking. "Hallorans' people tried to poach two of them last week. Offered double."

"Did they take it?"

"No." Now he turned, and there was something in his face she was still learning to read—not softness exactly, but the absence of armor, which on Elias Marchetti looked almost the same. "Marta Reyes says the Halloran man smelled like cologne and lies. Her words."

"Marta Reyes has never been wrong about anything except her pie crust."

"She puts vodka in it now. Fixed it." He handed her a clipboard. Rows, crews, bins. His handwriting was small and exact, an accountant's hand on a farmer's list. "You take the east slope with the Reyes crew. I'll start west and meet you at the top by noon."

It was such a simple plan. It was the first plan in seven years they had made together without arguing about it first, and Lena felt the strangeness of that settle into her chest like a swallowed coin.

"Elias," she said, because the coin wanted to come back up. "If we get even sixty percent of what the trees are holding—"

"Then we pay off the line of credit. Both lines." He shouldered a picking bag. "And if we get eighty, we're the first orchard in three generations to end a season owing nobody."

He said it flatly, the way he said all hopeful things, as if hope were a debt he refused to incur until it cleared. But his eyes stayed on hers a beat too long, and then he walked out into the gray-gold morning, and Lena stood in the empty barn with her heart doing something she refused to name, because naming it would mean trusting it, and trust was the one crop she'd never managed to grow.

* * *

By nine the east slope was loud with voices.

The pickers came from everywhere—half the town, it seemed. Marta Reyes and two of her daughters. Old Tom Weller, who had supposedly retired from everything in 2011 and who climbed a ladder like a man settling an old score. The Vasquez boys from the feed store, teenagers with earbuds and astonishing hands. Even Father Brennan showed up in rolled sleeves and said God was in the ladders today and then spent four hours proving it.

Lena worked beside Marta in row fourteen, and the talk came easy the way it does among people whose hands are busy. Marta had picked this orchard as a girl, before the Marchetti collapse, before the Voss name became a thing people said carefully.

"You know," Marta said, tipping a ripe Jonagold into her bag, "your grandmother used to stand right about there—" she nodded at the head of the row "—every first day of harvest. Rain or shine. Said a grower who didn't greet the first apple had no business eating any of them."

"I didn't know that."

"There's a lot you don't know, mija. That's not an insult. That's just what it is to be young." Marta's eyes were shrewd and kind at once. "Elias used to run these rows barefoot. His mother would holler at him. Whole town could hear it."

Lena laughed despite herself, and the laugh surprised her—it came out whole, unguarded, the first one in weeks that wasn't deployed for a customer or a creditor. Around her the orchard went on being loud: crates knocking, ladders creaking, Tom Weller telling some story about 1987 that everyone had heard forty times and no one ever interrupted.

At noon she reached the top of the slope and found Elias waiting with sandwiches and two bottles of cider from last year's pressing—their pressing, the small test batch they'd argued over for a week. He'd spread a tarp under the biggest tree, and the light came down through the leaves in coins.

They ate without talking much. Below them the orchard moved with people, dozens of them, the whole slope dotted with color and motion, and Lena felt something loosen in her that she hadn't known was tight.

"It's working," she said. "Look at it. It's actually—"

"Don't say it."

"—working."

"You'll jinx the press."

"You don't believe in jinxes. You believe in soil chemistry and cold storage."

"I believe in not tempting a September hailstorm." But he was almost smiling, and the almost was worth more than most men's entire smiles. He passed her the second bottle. Their fingers touched around the glass and neither of them moved away, and the touch lasted one second too long to be accidental and one second too short to be a decision.

Lena drank. The cider was imperfect—too sharp, unfinished, honest—and it tasted like the whole summer: like pruning cuts and arguments and the night in the cider barn when the rain had come down like a held breath finally released and he had leaned toward her and then said *I can't*, and she had pretended for both their sakes that nothing had happened.

"Elena," he said quietly. He almost never used her full name. When he did, it landed like a hand on her shoulder. "Whatever happens with the press, with the money, with—" he gestured vaguely, at the orchard, at the past, at the unsayable thing between them "—today is good. I want you to know I know that."

"I know you know that," she said, and heard how careful they both were, how they built sentences like load-bearing walls, everything structural, nothing exposed.

Below them, someone started singing—one of the Vasquez boys, tuneless and happy—and other voices caught it, and the song rose up the slope thin and bright as pollen.

Elias looked at her. Not past her, not around her, not through her the way he had for the first weeks. At her. And Lena thought: *he's going to kiss me,* and the thought arrived not as fear but as fact, the way you know rain is coming by the smell of it.

He leaned in. She didn't move. The world narrowed to the space between them, warm and apple-scented and trembling—

"Halloran's car!" Tom Weller's voice cracked up the hillside like a whip. "Somebody's driving up the lane!"

### Elias

The moment broke the way ice breaks—all at once, along a fault you'd been pretending wasn't there.

Elias sat back. Lena sat back. Neither of them looked at the other, and both of them looked everywhere else, and the silver sedan ground up the gravel lane below trailing dust and entitlement.

"That's the third time this month," Lena said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, setting down the bottle, were not quite.

"He's not here for the pickers this time. Pickers are settled." Elias stood, brushing off his knees, grateful for the interruption and furious at himself for being grateful. "He's here for us."

They walked down together, and Elias was aware of her beside him the way he was aware of weather—constant, consequential, impossible to ignore. Six weeks ago he'd have sworn he hated her. Four weeks ago he'd have sworn he was indifferent. Somewhere in the long evenings of grafting and ledger-work, indifference had died quietly and he'd neglected to report it, and now here he was, twenty feet from kissing her, interrupted by the one man alive who wanted this orchard dead.

Whitfield Halloran Jr. got out of the sedan in linen the color of oatmeal and surveyed the harvest like a man appraising a garage sale.

"Quite the operation," he said. "Community turnout. Very charming. Very temporary."

"Mr. Halloran." Elias kept his voice level. "We're working."

"So I see. And I'm here to work too." He produced a folder from the car, thick as a Bible. "Final offers. Both of you. Individually numbered, individually signed. My father asked me to convey his personal respect for your grandmother—she was, whatever our differences, a formidable woman." His gaze slid to Lena. "Ms. Voss. Your number has gone up since June. Considerably."

"I'm not interested in my number."

"Everyone's interested in their number. Some people just need help admitting it." Halloran smiled with excellent teeth. "Your café alone is carrying what, three liens? This offer clears all of them by Christmas. You could reopen anywhere you liked. Paris, even. Pastry chefs do well in Paris."

Elias watched her face while Halloran talked, watched the temptation move through it like weather—he'd seen it before, in June, and he didn't blame her for it. Wanting rescue wasn't weakness. But watching it now, with the taste of the almost-kiss still on his mouth, he found he couldn't breathe evenly until she spoke.

"We have a harvest to bring in," Lena said. "Come back in November if you still want to waste gas."

Halloran's smile thinned by a degree. "Mr. Marchetti?"

"You know my answer. You've heard it four times. Ask me a fifth and I'll charge you for the consultation."

"Stubbornness is expensive," Halloran said pleasantly, closing the folder. "But I'll leave these. Offers expire October fifteenth. After that, my father's patience expires with them—and he has other means. The county has been very cooperative lately. Very cooperative indeed." He let that sit in the air like exhaust, tipped an imaginary hat, and drove back down the lane.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Tom Weller spat with tremendous precision and said, "Well. Back to work," and the orchard resumed its noise, and the song started up again somewhere in row nine, defiantly off-key.

Elias and Lena stood in the lane in the settling dust.

"October fifteenth," she said.

"He's bluffing about the county."

"Is he?" She looked at him, and he saw the question underneath the question—the real one, the one they'd been circling since June. *Can I trust you? Will you tell me things? Are you going to leave?*

"The county clerk owes Halloran money," Elias said slowly. "I've heard it rumored. I haven't confirmed it." He hesitated, and something in him—the same something that had kept his mouth shut for seven years, the family loyalty that had cost him everything—tightened like a fist. There was a thing he'd found in the county records in August. A signature. He had said nothing. He was still saying nothing.

"But you'll confirm it," Lena said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'll look into it," he said, and hated how much that sounded like a door closing.

* * *

### Lena

The afternoon went long and golden. By five the bins stood ranked at the bottom of the slope like a wooden army, and the light turned the whole orchard the color of the inside of a peach.

Lena worked the last rows alone as the crews drifted home, and she let herself feel it—the good day, the whole day, the town in the trees and the cider shared and the almost-kiss that was still sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal. Joy, she thought, was a thing she'd stopped budgeting for. It kept arriving anyway, uninvited, and she kept refusing to sign for it.

She was reaching for the last high apples when she saw the truck.

Not Halloran's sedan—a battered pickup, city plates, parked crooked at the gate. And leaning against it, arms crossed, wearing sunglasses at dusk and an expression Lena knew better than her own heartbeat, was her mother.

Diana Voss did not visit. Diana *arrived*, the way weather systems arrive, and Lena's stomach dropped through the ladder rungs as her mother pushed off the truck and started up the row, heels sinking into orchard dirt, smiling the smile she wore when she had already decided how everything was going to go.

"Lena, sweetheart," Diana called. "We need to talk before October fifteenth. All of us."

Behind her, at the edge of the rows, Elias had gone very still—still the way a man goes still when he recognizes a shape from an old nightmare—and Lena, halfway up the ladder with an apple in her hand, understood that the harvest had just gotten much more complicated than fruit.

Her mother smiled again, wider, and took off her sunglasses.

Her eyes were frightened.
