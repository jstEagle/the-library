## 14. The Accomplice Next Door

*Xanthe*

The name had been sitting in the ledger for twenty years, and I had looked at it a hundred times without seeing it.

That was the thing I couldn't stop thinking about, three days after the judge sent me home with a photocopy and a warning — *don't spook anyone, don't say a word until we know what we have* — the thing that kept me awake at two in the morning with the ceiling fan turning slow circles above me like a hand winding a clock: I had looked at it. Many times. I had run my thumb over the ink.

**Received of A. Halloway — 4,000. Re: settlement of outstanding matters.**

A. Halloway. August Halloway. The man who had stood at my father's funeral with his hand on my shoulder and said, *Your old man was the best of us, Xanthe. The harbor won't see his like again.* The man whose face was on the plaque outside the library. The man who had lent my father money in the lean years — or so I'd always believed — and whose generosity the town still told stories about at the Legion hall, at the diner counter, at every harvest festival where they gave out the Halloway Prize for the best preserve in the county.

I had read the entry as charity. A friend settling accounts. That was what twenty years of willful blindness could do to a line of handwriting: it could turn evidence into kindness, because kindness asked nothing of you.

Marisol saw it in ten seconds.

She was sitting at my kitchen table — the same table where my father had eaten breakfast every morning of my childhood — with the ledger open under the window light, and she didn't gasp, didn't go pale. She just went very still, the way she did when she was reading something she already knew the shape of.

"August Halloway," she said.

"You knew."

"I knew he was there that night." She said it the way you'd say *I knew it would rain*. "I told you. I told the judge. There were two cars on the service road. I only ever saw one driver. But I heard the voice through the window, before the glass broke, before the fire. Someone arguing with your father about the insurance payout — about the *split*." She looked up at me. "I never saw his face. But I knew the voice. Everyone in this town knows that voice. He was the one who read the announcements at the festival. He had a way of projecting, like a man who'd spent his life being listened to."

"Why didn't you say his name at trial?"

"Because I wasn't sure." Her hands were flat on the table, steadying it, or steadying herself. "Because I was nineteen and terrified and my lawyer told me a guilty plea with good behavior meant seven years instead of twenty. Because if I named August Halloway from a prison cell, I'd have been a liar *and* a convict, and nobody — not you, not this town, not God — would ever have listened to me again." She closed the ledger gently, like it was something that could bruise. "I needed to be sure. Now I'm sure."

"How?"

She reached into her bag — the canvas bag she carried everywhere, the one that had crossed two states and a prison fence and a probation office — and took out an envelope, old and soft at the corners.

"Your father wrote me a letter. Before he died. It never got sent — I'll tell you that story another time. But in it, he didn't name him either. He was a coward to the end, your father. He wrote around it." She slid a single page across the table, and I read the sentence she'd underlined in pencil, the graphite worn faint now from handling:

*The other man's conscience is heavier than mine, because he kept the money and I only kept the silence.*

"Kept the money," I said. "Halloway kept the money."

"Four thousand in the ledger was the first payment. The insurance on the winery was sixty." She said the number flatly, and I did the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do since the night in her doorway: sixty thousand in 1998, when the vineyard was drowning, when my father's debts were eating an acre a year — it wouldn't have saved us, but it would have bought time. Instead it bought August Halloway a building on Front Street, and my family twenty years of slow bleeding.

"And now," Marisol said, "he's backing Corliss."

The word landed between us like a dropped glass. Corliss. The developer with the soft voice and the hard numbers, who'd offered to erase my debt if I signed away the bakery block, who'd smiled at me across a table with the patience of a man who always, eventually, got the signature. I'd refused him. I'd thought I was refusing a land deal.

"I saw them at the Legion hall," Marisol said. "Last week. Corliss had the plans spread out and Halloway was standing over his shoulder, pointing at things. Everyone was clapping him on the back. *August's finally bringing the harbor into the next century.*" Her mouth twisted. "He's not backing the development, Xanthe. He's *finishing* it. The winery fire was the first move. Your father's silence was the second. The bakery block is the last piece, and if it goes, there's nothing left of the Thornbury name on this harbor but a plaque nobody reads."

I sat with that. The kitchen was quiet except for the fan and the gulls and the far-off halyard clinking against the harbor flagpole, a sound I'd heard every morning of my life and never once listened to.

"He was at my father's funeral," I said. It came out stupid, but it was the truest thing I had. "He carried the wreath."

"He's carried a lot of things for this town." Marisol's voice softened, not with pity — with something more dangerous, which was understanding. "That's how a lie like this survives, Xanthe. Not by hiding. By *carrying*. He's carried the memorial fund, the scholarship, the festival. He's carried your family's story for twenty years — the story where your father was the honest man ruined by a bad fire and a bad woman. He built that story with his own hands. And this town helped him build it, because the story was comfortable and the truth would have cost everyone something."

I thought of the trial transcripts in the box under my bed. I thought of how many times I'd heard *that Vega girl* said in the diner, in the feed store, in the pews of St. Brendan's, always in the same tone, the tone reserved for weather that had ruined a harvest. I thought of how I'd let them say it. How I'd said it myself, once, to a tourist who asked about the burned winery, before I caught myself.

The town's memory wasn't just built on a lie. It was built on a lie *we'd all helped pay for* — with our silence, our nods, our small safe steps around the truth. Halloway hadn't done it alone. He'd never needed to.

"We have to take this to the judge," I said.

"We already did."

"No. I mean — everything. The letter, the ledger, the voice. All of it, named. No more *the other man*. His name goes in the record." I heard my own voice harden, and I let it. It felt like life. It felt like the first breath after twenty years underwater. "August Halloway."

Marisol watched me for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and there was something in her face I hadn't earned yet and maybe never would — not gratitude, not forgiveness. Recognition, maybe. Two people standing on the same burning ground at last.

"There's a problem," she said. "The hearing is in eleven days. And August Halloway has been the most beloved man in this harbor for forty years. You're going to stand up in a courtroom and tell this town that its saint set the fire." She gathered the ledger, the letter, the envelope, and stood. "You'd better be ready for what that costs. Because he will be."

*Marisol*

I hadn't wanted to be the one who said his name out loud. Not because I doubted it — doubt had left me somewhere between the ledger and the letter, the way fog leaves a harbor at first light, all at once and without apology. I said it because it had to be said by someone, and Xanthe's mouth had gone stiff around it, and I understood why. Saying *August Halloway* in that kitchen was like saying the harbor itself had a crack in it.

But there was a second reason, one I didn't say, and it walked home with me along the fence line in the last of the afternoon light.

I had heard that voice before the fire. But I had also heard it *after*. Three days after, to be exact, when I was still free, still awaiting trial, still the girl from the cannery family nobody quite believed could have struck a match. August Halloway had come to my mother's house. He'd sat in the parlor with his hat on his knee — I remember the hat, the way he turned it, a man performing humility — and he'd told my mother the town would look after us. He'd told her that a plea would be *kindest for everyone*. And when my mother went to the kitchen to make coffee, he'd looked at me across the parlor — nineteen years old, hands shaking in my lap — and he'd said, very quietly, in that projected voice that carried like a man who'd spent his life being listened to:

*"You seem like a sensible girl. Sensible girls have long lives."*

I had spent ten years in a cell turning that sentence over, and for most of them I'd told myself I'd imagined the threat, that grief and fear had bent it into something it wasn't. But I hadn't imagined the hat turning in his hands. I hadn't imagined the way the parlor got small around him.

And I hadn't imagined, two weeks ago, the blackened ring in my yard where someone had burned a warning into the grass while I slept. I'd told myself it was kids. I'd told myself it was Corliss's people, hired muscle, anonymous money. But standing at my fence now, looking across the stubble field at the light on Front Street, at the Halloway Building with its brass letters polished by forty years of civic pride, I let myself think the thought I'd been refusing:

He knows I'm here. He's known since I rented the cottage. And the fire in my yard wasn't a warning to leave.

It was a reminder that he'd won the last time.

I made myself go inside. I made myself eat. I made myself write the statement the judge had asked for — the parlor, the hat, the sentence, the date — in handwriting that only shook a little. And then, because the walls were too close and the night was too long, I did what I'd done for ten years through bars: I wrote a letter I wasn't sure I'd send.

*Xanthe,* I wrote. *There's something I didn't tell you, because it makes me a witness and not just a survivor, and witnesses can be broken. But you're going to hear it at the hearing anyway, and I'd rather you hear it from me...*

I was three pages in when the truck went past.

It slowed. It always slowed on this stretch — the road was bad, the cottage sat low, and locals eased down here out of habit. But this truck didn't ease. It crawled. And in the wash of its headlights, before it turned onto the harbor road, I saw the bumper sticker, faded but legible, the one that was on a third of the trucks in this town because the man who ordered them handed them out free at the festival every year:

**HALLOWAY — THE HARBOR'S HEART.**

The truck didn't stop. It didn't need to. It was a message the way the burned ring had been a message, and I understood that I had spent ten years learning to survive a cell and had learned nothing at all about surviving a town.

I finished the letter. I sealed it. And in the morning, before the diner shift, I walked it next door and put it in Xanthe's hands, and watched his face change as he read, and said the thing I'd been carrying since the parlor twenty years ago, out loud, in daylight, where it finally belonged:

"He threatened me, Xanthe. Before the trial. And everyone in this town who ever shook his hand — they've been shaking the hand that did it."

Xanthe looked at the letter for a long time. Then he looked past me, down the hill, at the harbor, at the Halloway Building catching the morning sun like it was made of something holy.

"Eleven days," he said. "We'd better move fast. Because I just remembered where I've seen that truck before."

"Where?"

"Corliss's office," he said. "Parked out front. Every single time I've been there."
