## 7. Julian Calls

The call came on a Tuesday, which Elodie would later think was fitting. Tuesdays were the days Julian Voss liked to remind people he existed.

She was in the farmhouse kitchen, elbows deep in a bowl of apples Theo had left on the counter with a note that said *these are yours, I'm not doing your chores anymore, we agreed* — a note she suspected was less about chores and more about the fact that she'd spent three nights in the attic with Maren's papers and had begun to look, in his words, "like a woman being haunted by a very small ghost." The phone buzzed against the table, screen lit with a name that made her stomach perform a small, practiced drop: **JULIAN VOSS**.

Twelve years of ghostwriting had taught her that certain names were weather systems. You didn't argue with them. You dressed for them.

"Julian," she said, wiping her hands on a dish towel embroidered with something Maren had probably stitched decades ago — a wren, she thought, or possibly an ambitious chicken.

"Elodie." His voice was warm the way polished brass is warm — beautiful, and only if you don't think about what it's for. "I've missed the sound of you working. There's a particular silence you make when you're writing well. Very restful."

"I'm hanging up now."

"Don't." A laugh, low and easy. "I need my voice. Chapter fourteen is due Friday, and it reads like a man describing his own funeral. You've made me *contemplative*, Elodie. Readers don't pay forty dollars to watch me contemplate. They pay to watch me win."

"You haven't won anything yet," she said. "The book isn't finished."

"*There* she is." She could hear him smiling, that audible smile he did, the one that had sold half a million hardcovers under other people's names before anyone thought to wonder whose voice it actually was. "Friday. And I want the orchard chapter moved up. The Maren chapter."

Elodie went very still. Outside the window, a crow was dismantling the scarecrow with the patience of a professional. "There is no Maren chapter in your outline."

"There is now. My people loved the angle. The great man remembers the small places. A eulogy for the woman who taught him everything about honest work." He said *honest work* the way some men say *charity* — gently, and from a great height. "Send me something by Sunday. Anecdotes. Texture. You're good at texture."

"I never met her," Elodie lied, and heard how easily it came, and hated it.

"Mm," Julian said, which was not agreement but was also not accusation. Julian never accused; he simply filed things away where they might later prove useful. "Actually — that reminds me. Come to the city this week. Thursday. We'll do it over lunch, my treat, and you can tell me all about your little sabbatical in the country. How *is* the countryside? I hear you've come into property."

The dish towel stopped moving in her hands.

"I don't discuss personal matters with clients."

"You don't discuss anything with clients, darling, that's why I employ you." A pause, silk over wire. "Thursday. Le Marchand, one o'clock. Wear something that isn't apple-scented."

He hung up before she could refuse, which was, she understood, the entire point.

* * *

She told Theo nothing, which felt like the honest thing to do, since honesty between them currently operated on a strict need-to-know basis and neither of them needed to know much of anything.

But Theo noticed things. That was the trouble with a man who'd spent twelve years learning an orchard — he read weather in bark, mood in light, and apparently guilt in the way a woman packed a bag.

"You're going somewhere," he said Friday morning — no, Thursday morning; she'd lost a day somewhere between the attic and the ledger pages she'd hidden inside a hollowed-out copy of *The Complete Poems of Emily Dickinson*, because even her paranoia had literary standards.

"To the city. Work."

"At the orchard?"

"My work," she said, "isn't here."

Something crossed his face — quick, old, familiar — and was gone before she could name it. "Right," he said. "Well. Take the truck. The bus schedule out here was designed by someone who hates buses."

"The truck has a dent—"

"The truck had a dent when Maren got it, and Maren got it used. It's a tradition. Dents are how you know it's loved." He handed her the keys and then, as she turned away, added, so casually it wasn't casual at all: "Maren hated the city too. Went twice a year, came back each time looking like she'd been wrung out. Said there were people there who smiled with their teeth."

Elodie paused on the step. "Did she ever say who?"

Theo looked at her for a long moment, wind moving through the bare rows behind him, and she saw him decide — deliberately, expensively — not to ask why she wanted to know.

"No," he said. "But she kept every letter they sent her. In case, she said. In case of what, she never told me either."

* * *

Le Marchand at one o'clock was everything Bramble Hollow was not: white linen, low light, waiters who materialized like well-dressed ghosts. Julian was already seated, already charming the maître d' into laughing at something that was probably about the maître d', already wearing the kind of sweater that cost more than the truck's remaining value. He rose when he saw her and kissed the air near both her cheeks, a ritual that managed to be intimate and entirely bloodless at once.

"Elodie Halloran," he announced, holding her at arm's length. "You look like a person who's been climbing ladders. Good for the arms. Terrible for the mystique."

"You look like a person who's been photographed recently."

"Constantly." He sat, waved away the menu on her behalf — "she'll have the trout, she always has the trout" — and leaned back with the ease of a man in a room he owned, whatever the deed said. "Now. Tell me about the dead woman."

The word landed like a dropped glass. "I beg your pardon?"

"Maren Marsh. Your co-inheritor's sister. Your benefactor, if the gossip columns are to be believed, though they rarely are, which is why I employ better sources." He smiled. "You've inherited a piece of an orchard, Elodie. With a man. A man with a history with you, if my sources are worth their retainer. This is *material*. A ghostwriter with a gothic subplot of her own. I'm almost jealous."

"How do you know any of this?"

"I know everything. It's my best quality and my worst habit." He poured her water himself, unasked, a small intimacy performed like a magic trick. "What interests me is what doesn't interest you. Everyone else wants to know about the man. I want to know about the land. Tell me — what shape is the place in? Financially. Be specific. I adore specifics."

"Why?"

"Because I'm writing a chapter about her," he said smoothly, "and I like my details true."

It was a good answer. It was, in fact, exactly the kind of answer she would have written for him. And that was precisely what made her skin prickle — hearing her own craft deployed back at her, the machinery of believability running without a writer behind it.

"Maren Marsh ran a failing orchard," Elodie said carefully. "That's not a detail, it's a headline."

"Failing," Julian repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. She was always bad with money. Generous to a fault — faults being, in my experience, where the money goes." He speared an olive, ate it, continued with the serenity of a man reciting his own alibi. "I knew her, you know. Years ago. Before your time."

The room seemed to tilt a degree and right itself.

"You knew Maren?"

"Intimately." He let the word sit there, fat and deliberate, watching her face the way a fisherman watches a line. "Oh, don't look scandalized, it was long ago and entirely consensual and mostly about money, which is the most honest kind of intimacy there is. She had land and no liquidity. I had liquidity and an appetite. For a few years it worked beautifully." Another olive. "Then it didn't. People are so sentimental about orchards. All that romantic nonsense about roots. Roots are just the part of the tree nobody sees rotting."

"What happened?"

"What always happens. She wanted to keep the place. I wanted—" he gestured elegantly, encompassing the linen, the light, the entire city beyond the windows, "—*this*. We parted as friends. Well. She parted as a friend. I parted as an investor." His eyes came up, bright and amused and utterly without warmth. "Why do you ask? Has she left you something? Papers, perhaps? Old letters? Dead women love leaving letters. It's their last chance to control the narrative, and Lord knows I respect that."

"No," Elodie said. "Nothing."

The lie came out level. Twelve years of practice held it steady. But Julian tilted his head slightly, the way he did in interviews when he smelled something usable, and she understood with cold clarity that he hadn't believed her — that he had perhaps never once believed anyone, that disbelief was simply the operating temperature of the man.

"Pity," he said lightly. "Well. Business. Chapter fourteen by Friday, the Maren chapter by Sunday — make her vivid, make her *quaint*, the town will eat it up, quaint sells. And Elodie—" he reached across and touched her wrist, two fingers, brief and proprietary, "—do be careful in the country. Rural estates attract lawyers. Probate is such a slow, hungry process. One wouldn't want to get tangled in old accounts while grieving. Grief makes people sloppy with documents."

"Is that a threat?"

"Goodness, no." He laughed, delighted, genuine for the first time all afternoon. "It's advice. Threats are for people who can't afford lawyers. I can afford *choirs* of them." He stood, pressed bills into the check folder without looking at it, kissed the air beside her cheek again. "Sunday. Make her wonderful. She deserves it — whoever she really was."

And then he was gone, trailing cologne and waiter-smiles, leaving Elodie alone at the table with her untouched trout and a single thought arriving with terrible, glacial slowness:

*Maren died nearly penniless. Julian had liquidity and an appetite.*

She sat there long after the plates were cleared, watching the city glitter through the glass like frost on a window she couldn't open, and understood that she had just had lunch with the answer to a question she hadn't known she was asking.

* * *

The drive back took three hours, most of them spent replaying the conversation like a manuscript she was line-editing for evidence. By the second hour she had pulled onto a gravel shoulder, dug her notebook from her bag, and written down everything she could remember, verbatim, in the shorthand she used for interviews:

*Intimately. Mostly about money.*
*Bad with money. Generous to a fault.*
*Roots are just the part nobody sees rotting.*
*Old accounts. Probate. Sloppy with documents.*

She stared at the last line until the streetlight above her flickered on.

He'd asked about papers. He'd asked about ledgers. He'd warned her off accounts before she'd ever mentioned accounts. And underneath all of it, threaded through the charm like wire through wax fruit: *he needed to know what she had.* Which meant he feared what she had. Which meant—

Which meant the shell company initials she'd glimpsed in Maren's ledger pages, the ones she'd been telling herself were coincidence, were not coincidence.

Her phone buzzed. Julian again. Her thumb hovered.

**JULIAN VOSS:** *Forgot to mention — lovely touch, the wren towel in your kitchen. Maren had one just like it. Small world.*

She looked up sharply, as if the road itself might explain it, but there was only gravel and dark and her own face ghosted in the windshield. He'd never been to the farmhouse. He'd never seen the kitchen.

Someone had told him. Someone was watching. Or — colder, quieter — someone had been inside.

She drove the last hour with both hands on the wheel, and when the orchard finally rose out of the dark around her, bare trees standing in rows like patient witnesses, she saw a light burning in the attic window. Theo's silhouette moved behind it, and she realized with a jolt that he was up there — in her space, near the trunk, near the floorboard where she'd hidden the pages.

She parked the truck and sat in the cold, engine ticking down, and made herself say it plainly, the way she'd say it in print, under a name that was actually hers:

*Julian Voss knew Maren. Julian Voss profited from Maren. And Julian Voss wants to know exactly what she left behind.*

Tomorrow she would have to decide whether to tell Theo. Tonight, she climbed the attic stairs to find out what he was doing with her secrets — and found, spread across the floorboards in the lamplight, the ledger pages fanned open, and Theo standing over them with a look on his face she had never seen before.

Not anger.

Recognition.

"Elodie," he said quietly, without turning around, "who is J.V.?"
