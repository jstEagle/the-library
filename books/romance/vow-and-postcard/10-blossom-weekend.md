## 10. Blossom Weekend

Elodie woke to the sound of a truck she didn't recognize, and then another, and then a low murmur of voices drifting up through the floorboards like weather. For one disoriented moment she lay in Maren's old bed—she'd moved into it in week three, when the guest room's mattress springs had declared war on her spine—and listened to Bramble Hollow filling up with people.

It was blossom weekend. She'd known that, of course. Theo had mentioned it eleven times, each mention carrying the tonal weight of a man reading a verdict. But knowing a thing and hearing it arrive were different. She dressed quickly, pulled her hair back, and went downstairs to find the kitchen occupied by a woman in her seventies arranging pies on every available surface with the authority of a field general.

"You'll be the writer, then," the woman said, not looking up. "Maren's girl. I'm Dot Prewitt. I've brought nine pies and I expect all nine sold by dark."

"Dot runs the church kitchen," Theo said from the doorway, and there was something in his voice Elodie had never heard before—a loosening, almost like relief. "She also runs the town, the school board, and the fate of anyone who's ever been rude to her at a bake sale."

"Someone has to," Dot said. She straightened, finally looked at Elodie properly, and her eyes were sharp as paring knives. "Maren Marsh was my oldest friend. She talked about you. Said you could make a sentence sit up and beg." A pause, weighted. "She also said you left. So I'll be watching how you behave this weekend, and so will everyone else. The orchard needs a good day. Cider sales carry us through winter. You understand?"

"I understand," Elodie said.

"Good." Dot turned back to her pies. "Press barn's through there. The old press sticks if you don't talk sweet to it. Maren used to sing to it. Badly."

By nine o'clock the lane was a river of cars, and the orchard had transformed. Bunting appeared—Elodie never learned from where—strung between the oldest apple trees in faded red and cream. Folding tables sprouted along the rows. Children were everywhere, running under the boughs where five-petaled white-and-pink blossoms drifted down like a snowfall that had gotten confused about the season. The air smelled of grass and sugar and, underneath it, the green sharpness of apples still months from ready.

And the neighbors came. That was the thing that undid her a little. They came in their good jackets and their Sunday shoes, and they looked at her—openly, frankly, the way people look at a stranger in a small town—and Elodie understood that she was not merely helping at a festival. She was on trial.

* * *

The press barn was dim and cool and smelled of a decade of crushed fruit soaked into the boards. Theo was already there, wrestling the great wooden screw of the press, his sleeves rolled, a smear of pomace on his forearm that he hadn't noticed.

"Dot says you have to talk sweet to it," Elodie said.

"Dot says a lot of things." He grunted; the screw resisted. "The press and I have an understanding. It sticks. I threaten it. It sticks anyway."

"Have you tried singing?"

He looked at her over his shoulder. "I don't sing."

"I know. I remember. Twelve years and I still remember that you don't sing." She hadn't meant to say it that way—hadn't meant to say it at all—but it was out now, warm and dangerous, and she covered it by stepping forward. "Show me. Maren taught me the basics. I was eleven."

Something shifted in his face. He stepped aside, and she took the handle, and her hands found the old rhythm without asking her permission—the lean, the pull, the turn, the lean again. The screw groaned and gave.

"There," she said, absurdly pleased with herself. "She'd have said I was a natural."

"She'd have said you were showing off." But he was almost smiling, and the morning light through the barn slats fell across both of them, and for a moment the twelve missing years were just a gap in a conversation, not a canyon.

Then the first customers came, and the moment was trampled by commerce.

The day found its shape quickly. Elodie worked the cider stand because she could talk—talking was, after all, her trade—and Theo worked the press and the barrels and the endless logistics of keeping a hundred people fed and watered in a field. She sold Dot's pies with descriptions that made people laugh. She told the story of the orchard to anyone who'd stand still: how Maren's grandfather planted the Bramley rows in 1931, how the Winesap block survived the frost of '85 because Maren, then nineteen, had stayed up three nights feeding smudge fires with her father.

"You knew her well?" a man asked—gray-bearded, weathered, the kind of face that had watched a lot of seasons from a tractor seat.

"Since I was a child," Elodie said. "She was the only person who ever told me the truth about myself."

The man studied her. "Huh," he said, in the tone of a jury reserving judgment. But he bought two gallons.

* * *

By mid-afternoon, the trial was going better than she'd dared hope.

It turned on an accident, the way these things do. A boy of maybe seven—somebody's grandson, all elbows and sticky hands—climbed the rail fence by the Winesap block to get a better look at the blossoms, and the top rail, rotten at one end, gave way under him. He didn't fall far. But he fell into the mud between the rows, and he came up howling, and his mother came running from the pie table with her face white.

Elodie got there first. She didn't think about it—she just knelt in the mud, gathered the boy in, and started talking, low and fast, the way you'd talk to a frightened character in a story you loved. "Hey, hey, look at me. You're fine. Look at your hands—see? Not a scratch. You know what you just did? You tested that fence for the whole town. That's a job. That's a very important job and you did it perfectly."

The boy hiccuped. "I did?"

"Absolutely. That rail was rotten clear through. Theo!" she called. "We've got a fence inspector here and he found a bad one!"

And Theo—Theo, who did not perform for anyone—came over, crouched down, and examined the broken rail with complete solemnity. "Good catch," he said to the boy. "That could've been bad. You want to help me fix it? I've got a hammer and everything."

The boy helped fix the fence. His mother, watching, put her hand briefly on Elodie's shoulder and said, "Maren would've liked that," and the words went through Elodie like warm water through cold ground.

Word traveled. It always does. By four o'clock, people were coming to the cider stand specifically to talk to her—asking about the memoir, no, not the famous ones, the Maren one, had she really found Maren's papers, what was Maren like when she was young? And Elodie answered what she could and deflected the rest, and something loosened in her chest that had been tight since the solicitor's letter arrived.

They were winning. Not the whole town—there was a knot of older men by the press barn who watched her all afternoon with folded arms and unmoved faces, and Dot Prewitt's approval, Elodie sensed, was provisional and revocable at any time. But the day was tipping their way.

At five, Theo appeared beside her at the stand, handed her a paper cup of the fresh-pressed first run, and said, quietly, "You're good at this."

"I'm good at selling things. It's a slightly shameful skill."

"No." He shook his head. "You're good at *this*. The trees. The story of the trees. You always were. You used to name the grafts."

"I named the grafts," she agreed. "You said it was ridiculous."

"It was ridiculous." He was looking at the orchard, not at her, at the long rows going gold in the lowering sun, blossom-heavy, trembling a little in the breeze. "The one on the third Winesap you named Ferdinand. It's still there. Still the best graft in the block."

Elodie looked at the third Winesap, at the swollen, healed scar of the old graft, and had to press her lips together before anything escaped them.

* * *

The festival wound down the way good days do—gradually, reluctantly, with stragglers and last sales and Dot Prewitt counting pie money with the satisfaction of a woman whose forecasts had come true. Nine pies, sold. The bunting came down. Cars thinned along the lane until the orchard was theirs again, and the quiet that followed was the pleasant kind, the earned kind.

They counted the till together at the cider stand, elbows almost touching on the small table. The number, when Theo finished adding it, made him go still.

"That's—" He cleared his throat. "That's more than we took last year. In the whole weekend. By a fair margin."

"Dot's pies."

"Dot's pies," he agreed, and then, because the day had worn his armor thin: "And you. They came to look at you, and they stayed. I heard them. Half the town hasn't said ten words to me since Maren's funeral, and today they were—"

"Judging me," Elodie said.

"Deciding about you. There's a difference. And I think—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way a man walks on ice he's fallen through before. "I think they decided well."

The light was doing something to him. The last of the sun came low through the blossom rows and turned everything the color of cider, and he was standing close, closer than the till required, and Elodie found that she had stopped counting anything at all.

"Theo," she said.

"Mm."

"I keep waiting for this to feel like a mistake. Being here. And it keeps not feeling like one."

He turned to her then. Up close, in that light, she could see the years on him—the lines at his eyes, the gray coming in at his temples—but underneath them was the boy who'd taught her to drive the tractor and never once laughed when she stalled it, and her heart did something unpractical and entirely predictable.

"Elodie," he said, and her name in his mouth was a different word than it was in anyone else's.

The blossom above them shifted in the breeze. A few petals came loose and drifted between their faces, slow as snow, and she was aware of everything at once—the smell of pressed apples, the warmth coming off his arm, the fact that she was going to kiss him, that she had already decided it somewhere below the level of thought, that twelve years of careful distance was about to end under a tree Maren's grandfather had planted—

His phone rang.

They both froze. The ringtone cut through the orchard air, bright and insistent, and Theo exhaled through his nose and stepped back, and the space between them refilled with ordinary air.

"That'll be the supplier," he said, not quite steadily. "I should—"

"Go," Elodie said. "Business."

He answered as he walked toward the farmhouse, his voice fading. Elodie stood alone under the Winesap, hand pressed flat against her own sternum as if to hold something in place, and watched the petals keep falling.

* * *

She was stacking cups when she heard him stop walking.

It was a small thing—the rhythm of his boots on the grass simply ceasing—but she'd spent twelve years reading people for a living, and she knew the sound of a man going rigid. She turned. Theo stood halfway to the house, phone at his ear, and even at forty feet she could see the change: the shoulders squared, the jaw set, the whole body becoming a wall.

He didn't say much. She watched him listen. Whoever was on the other end talked for a long time, and Theo's face did not move at all, which was somehow worse than if it had.

When he finally lowered the phone, he stood looking at it for a moment. Then he looked at her.

"Who was it?" Elodie asked.

He crossed the grass slowly. "A man named Julian Voss," he said. "Says he's your employer. Says he's been trying to reach you." His voice was flat, controlled, and underneath the control something was moving like water under ice. "He wanted to know if the orchard was for sale yet."

The evening had gone cool around them. Somewhere in the rows, a bird made one last complaint about the day.

"He offered," Theo went on, "generously. For both our shares. He said—" and here Theo's mouth twisted, "—he said he wanted to preserve Maren's legacy. Those words. *Preserve Maren's legacy.*" He looked at her, and the question in his eyes was the oldest one in the orchard, older than the trees. "Elodie. Why does Julian Voss know this orchard exists?"

The petals kept falling between them, unhurried, indifferent. Elodie thought of the ledger pages in the attic trunk, the initials in the margins, the shell company she hadn't yet told him about—and of the man in the city with the charming teeth, who had known Maren, and who now knew, somehow, exactly where she was standing.

"Because," she said, "I think he's been stealing from it for years."

Theo didn't move. The last light drained out of the western sky, and under the blossoming trees, with the till money still open on the table between them, Elodie reached for the truth she had been carrying alone—and found, for the first time, that she wanted to set it down.
