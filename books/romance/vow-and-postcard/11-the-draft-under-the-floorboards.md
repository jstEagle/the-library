## 11. The Draft Under the Floorboards

Theo Marsh had never been a suspicious man by nature. Suspicion, he'd always believed, was what you did instead of work, and work was what he did instead of thinking. For eleven weeks he had kept his side of their brittle treaty with the diligence of a man defusing something explosive: he stayed on the north end of the farmhouse, she stayed on the south; he asked no questions about London or her career or why she flinched at the sound of her own phone; she, in return, had stopped looking at him like he was a debt she couldn't remember taking out.

It was not peace. But it was architecture, and Theo trusted architecture.

The trouble began, as most trouble at Bramble Hollow did, with the floorboards.

He was fixing them because they needed fixing — the third board from the window in the old sewing room had gone soft as overripe pear, and with blossom weekend coming and half the county expected to tramp through the cidery barn, he wanted the farmhouse presentable. Maren would have been mortified to have guests stepping through her floors into the cellar. So on a grey Tuesday morning, with rain ticking against the glass and Elodie off in town buying sugar for cider testing, Theo pried up the board.

And found the loose tin box beneath it.

His first thought was practical: mice, probably, or water damage, or one of Maren's endless caches of things she meant to sort later. His second thought arrived when he lifted the lid and saw paper — dozens of pages, typed, neat, stacked with the kind of care that meant someone had been working at this for weeks. Months, maybe. Since before the frost, since before the storm, since before that night under the smudge pots when everything between them had finally cracked open like ice on the mill pond.

He should have put the lid back on. He knew that even then, some honest corner of himself knowing it. But his hands were already lifting out the first page, and his eyes were already reading, and the words went through him like a nail through soft wood:

*Chapter One. For the girl who owes me nothing.*

Maren's voice. Unmistakably Maren's voice — dry as August, sharp as a grafting knife — except that Maren had never written a book in her life. She'd barely written letters. And this wasn't Maren's typing. This was clean, professional, shaped prose, prose with a rhythm Theo recognized from somewhere he couldn't place until he could, and then he wished he couldn't.

Because he'd read that rhythm before. Twelve years ago, in a magazine, in an article about the rising young ghostwriter who could give anyone a voice — *the woman who writes other people's souls*, the profile had called her, with a photograph of Elodie Halloran looking elegant and slightly amused, as though being invisible were a clever joke only she got.

*For the girl who owes me nothing.* He turned the page. Another. The attic light of memory came on: Elodie's long absences, the hours she claimed to spend walking the orchard rows, the way she locked the sewing room when she thought no one noticed, the ink stain on her right middle finger she never explained. The way she'd gone quiet once, months ago, when he'd mentioned Maren's ledgers, and changed the subject with a skill he'd mistaken for grief.

She was writing a book. In his house. Under his floorboards. About Maren.

And she hadn't said a word.

* * *

By the time her car crunched up the drive at four o'clock, Theo had read forty pages, replaced the floorboard badly, and arranged himself in the kitchen with the studied calm of a man who has rehearsed a speech too many times. He'd made tea. That was the trouble with him — even furious, he made tea. Two cups. He hated himself for the second cup and left it on the counter anyway, steam rising like an accusation.

Elodie came in flushed from the cold, hair escaping its clip, a paper sack of sugar under one arm and something almost bright in her face — the brightness he'd spent two months learning to trust again, the brightness that had started showing itself after the storm, after the dawn, after the words they'd finally said among the smoking smudge pots.

"Hello," she said, and smiled at him, and it was the smile that undid him worse than anything. Because it was real. Because she meant it. Because she stood there meaning it while a manuscript about his sister rotted the floorboards above his head.

"You've been busy," Theo said.

"Oh — the sugar took forever, Mrs. Petch held me hostage talking about her dahlias—"

"Not the sugar." He set the top page of the manuscript on the table between them, squared it, the way you'd square a document in court. "*For the girl who owes me nothing.*"

He watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out. First the flush, then the pink, then the last warm undertone, until she was standing in his kitchen the color of the February sky.

"Where did you get that."

"The sewing room. Third board from the window." He heard his own voice and didn't recognize it — flat, careful, the voice he used with buyers who tried to cheat him at the farm gate. "Loose floorboard. Tin box. Funny thing, the things people hide in this house."

"It's not—" She stopped. Started again. "Theo."

"Go on," he said. "I'm curious how this one goes. I've heard the first draft already, you see. Years ago. You were very good at it then too."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "Not much is."

* * *

Here is what Elodie Halloran had learned about fear in twelve years of professional life: fear was manageable if you named it quickly. Fear was just information arriving ahead of schedule. She had faced down editors, lawyers, Julian Voss himself across a table strewn with contracts, and she had never once let them see the machinery behind her face.

But this was Theo, and the machinery failed her completely.

"I can explain," she said, which was the worst possible opening, and she knew it even as it left her mouth, because it was what guilty people said, and she was not guilty, except that from where he was standing she must look exactly like every lie she'd ever told professionally stitched into one.

"Take your time." He sat down across from her, arms folded, and the distance in him was worse than shouting. Shouting she could have worked with. This was the door closing. This was twelve years ago all over again, except this time she hadn't even left yet and he was already gone.

"Maren wrote to me," Elodie said. "Before she died. Letters. Did you know that?"

Something flickered in his face. "No."

"Eleven letters over two years. I never answered most of them. I couldn't — I told myself I didn't deserve—" She pressed her palms flat against the table to steady herself. "In the last one she said she was writing her memoirs and she couldn't do it alone. She said she needed someone who owed her nothing, because everyone else in this valley either worshipped her or resented her, and neither makes an honest biographer. She asked me to come. Not for the orchard. For the book."

"And you came," Theo said slowly, "for the book."

"I came for the book and found the orchard and found you, and yes, I kept writing it, because it was hers, Theo, it was the last thing she asked of me, and I was going to tell you — I was going to tell you so many times—"

"When?" The word came out quiet and terrible. "After harvest? After the deed? On your way back to the city, a postcard maybe — *sorry about the memoir, hope the trees do well*?" He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "You know what the worst part is, Elodie? It's not the book. Write the bloody book. It's that I found it under a floorboard. You slept down the hall from me. You ate breakfast with me. You kissed me in the smoke and frost and told me—" His voice caught, and he hated that more than anything, and pushed through it anyway. "Told me you were done running. Done hiding. And the whole time there was a manuscript buried six inches from where I sleep."

"It wasn't about you—"

"Everything's about you, Elodie! Don't you see that? It always has been!" He was on his feet now without remembering standing. "Your father, your shame, your career, your guilt — you carry it all around like luggage and you never once set it down long enough to ask what anyone else is carrying. Maren carried you, God help her, for years, and you repaid her by writing her secrets in secret. And now you're doing it to me. Again. In my own house."

"In *her* house," Elodie said, and the moment it was out she wished it unsaid, because it was true and cruel and precisely the wrong truth.

The kitchen went very still. Rain ticked against the window. The second cup of tea had gone cold between them, steam long dead, and somehow that small dead thing hurt more than the shouting.

"Yes," Theo said finally, quietly. "Her house. Which is why I suppose it makes sense you'd treat it like a hotel. You always did know which rooms were yours and which ones you were just passing through."

* * *

Later — much later, after the door had slammed and the truck had gone and come back twice without him getting out, after Elodie had sat alone at the kitchen table until the dark came down — she climbed the stairs to the sewing room and knelt on the cold boards and pried up the third one from the window.

The tin box looked smaller than it had that morning. Everything did, at night.

She understood him. That was the unbearable part. She understood him so well it felt like punishment: she knew exactly what he'd seen when he lifted that lid — not a manuscript but a pattern, proof that the woman he loved still lived by concealment, still kept whole worlds in hidden places, still chose the locked room over the open hand. She knew because she'd spent twelve years seeing the same pattern in herself and calling it professionalism.

And she knew, too, what he'd missed, because he'd stopped reading at the point where the pain started. He hadn't reached chapter nine. He hadn't read what Maren had written about *him* — the boy who came every spring to learn grafting, the brother she'd failed, the man she'd loved with the fierce helpless love of someone who knew she'd run out of time to say it properly. He hadn't read the passage Elodie had copied out three times trying to get it right, the one that ended: *Tell my brother the trees were never the inheritance. He was.*

He hadn't read any of it, because she'd hidden it, and hiding was the one sin this family never forgave, and she had committed it in the house built on it.

Elodie closed the tin. She did not put it back under the floorboard. Instead she carried it downstairs and set it on the kitchen table, dead center, where no one could miss it — a flag planted, a door left open, whatever he wanted to call it.

Then she went to the south end of the farmhouse, the end that had been hers since March, and packed a bag. Not to leave. She made herself say it aloud to the empty room, to make it true: *not to leave.* But the guest room at the Petches' had a bed going spare, and Mrs. Petch had been dropping hints about company since the dahlias came in, and one night apart might be the difference between a wound and a scar.

At the bottom of the stairs she paused. Through the window, down the lane, headlights swung in off the county road — early for visitors, wrong for neighbors, too sleek for this valley. A long dark car rolled to a stop in the yard, wipers still going, and even before the door opened and the tall figure stepped out into the porch light, smoothing his coat with that gesture she knew from a hundred hotel lobbies, Elodie's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the frozen ground itself.

Julian Voss stood in the yard of Bramble Hollow, smiling up at the farmhouse windows.

And he was holding flowers.
