## 14. Storm Over the Hollow

The frost warning came at four in the afternoon, riding in on Theo's phone with the cheerful menace of all bad news delivered by machines. *Hard freeze advisory. Lows near 24°F. Clear skies, calm winds — ideal radiative cooling conditions.*

"Radiative cooling," Elodie read over his shoulder. "That sounds almost elegant."

"It means the sky is going to suck the heat out of everything we love tonight." Theo was already moving, pulling his coat off its hook, grabbing keys from the bowl by the door. "Every bud on every tree is a small green promise right now, and by morning they'll all be black if we don't fight."

"Fight how? You can't argue with weather."

"You can smother it." He was counting under his breath — she could see him doing arithmetic behind his eyes, trees against pots against hours. "Smudge pots. Maren kept two hundred of them in the lower barn. Old diesel burners, filthy, cantankerous, and the only reason this orchard survived '96. We light them at sundown and keep them fed till dawn."

Elodie looked out the kitchen window at the orchard, bare-branched and silvered with new blossom just beginning to crack open, thousands of trees standing patient as an audience waiting for a play. "Two hundred pots across forty acres."

"Which is why," Theo said, shrugging into his coat, "I'm about to do something I swore I'd never do."

"What's that?"

He paused at the door, and there was something rueful and terrified in his face. "Ask Bramble Hollow for help."

* * *

They had not asked for help in twelve years. That was the unspoken law of the place, written into the deed alongside the co-ownership clause: *the Marshes manage alone.* Maren had been proud that way, and Theo had inherited the pride along with the land, and the town had learned to leave them be.

So when Theo drove the truck slowly down Hollow Road at five o'clock, stopping at the feed store, the church hall, the cidery, and the diner, saying the same six words at each stop — *"Frost tonight. We're lighting the pots."* — he did it with the expression of a man volunteering for a root canal.

And the town said yes before he finished asking.

Dot Kowalski closed the diner early and showed up in her husband's hunting coveralls with three teenagers she'd apparently deputized on the spot. Pastor Ellen arrived with a van full of choir members and a thermos the size of a fire hydrant. The Hollis brothers came down from their own orchard two miles east — their trees were higher on the slope, less exposed — carrying extra gloves and, inexplicably, a bag of oranges. By seven, there were thirty people in the lower barn, and Dot was organizing them with the crisp authority of a woman who had run a lunch rush for forty years and considered a frost emergency a modest change of menu.

"We'll work the rows in pairs," she announced. "One lights, one feeds. Nobody wanders off alone — these old rows have roots that'll trip a goat. And somebody find those Marsh kids matches."

"We're not—" Theo started.

"Hush. I've known you since you were in rompers, Theodore Marsh. You're both kids to me." She pressed a box of long kitchen matches into Elodie's hands and looked at her for one beat longer than necessary, with an expression Elodie couldn't quite read — something between assessment and blessing. "You light the north forty with him. He knows where the pots sit; you know how to pay attention. Go on."

The first pot was stubborn. It always was, Theo said — the first one of the season sulked like a cat being put out in rain. He knelt beside it in the blue dusk, struck match after match while Elodie held the flashlight and the wind found every gap in her coat.

"Fuel valve," she said finally. "It's closed."

Theo looked at the valve, then at her. "How do you know that?"

"I don't. I watched your hands. You checked the fuel line twice but never touched the valve, so either you'd already done it or you'd forgotten." She shrugged. "I write characters who lie to themselves for a living. I've gotten good at noticing what people skip."

He turned the valve. The pot caught on the next match with a soft *whump*, and orange light bloomed up between them, warming their faces and throwing their shadows huge against the first row of trees.

"There," he said quietly. "One down. A hundred and ninety-nine to go."

They worked the north forty together, and something happened that neither of them named: their bodies remembered each other. Not romantically — not yet, not exactly — but in the older, deeper way of people who had once moved through the same days. When Elodie crouched at a pot, Theo was already holding the matchbox steady for her without being asked. When he hauled a fuel can down a row, she had the next pot's lid off before he arrived. They fell into a rhythm like a song they'd both pretended to forget, each note landing where the other expected it.

"You used to hate the smell of diesel," he said somewhere around the ninetieth pot, conversationally, as though they hadn't spent twelve years not speaking.

"I used to hate a lot of things I got over." She fed the flame, watched it take. "Turns out most of what I hated was being cold and poor. The diesel was innocent."

That earned her a laugh — short, surprised out of him, quickly swallowed, but real. She hoarded it.

By ten o'clock the whole hollow glowed. From the ridge road it must have looked like the orchard had caught fire gently, hundreds of small flames breathing smoke into the dark, holding a warm ceiling of air over the blossoms. The temperature on Theo's phone read thirty-one degrees and falling. They had maybe four hours before the killing cold reached the buds.

At some point Dot appeared out of the smoke with sandwiches and coffee, looked at the two of them working in tandem, and said nothing at all, which from Dot was a paragraph.

* * *

It went wrong around midnight.

The wind — the calm, cooperative, nonexistent wind the forecast had promised — arrived anyway, a thin treacherous breath sliding down from the ridge, and it found the weakest thing in the north forty: the old wooden ladder leaning against the Henderson block, where Elodie stood feeding a pot set up on the hillside terrace. The ladder shifted. Not much. Just enough.

She heard Theo shout her name, and then the world tilted, and then his hands were on her — one catching her arm, one slamming flat against the ladder rail, arresting the fall with a violence that sent them both staggering backward into the soft dirt between the rows. They landed tangled, her back against his chest, his arms locked around her, both of them gasping in the smoke-sweet air.

For a moment neither moved. The pot hissed beside them. Somewhere down the slope, voices called — *you okay? what happened?* — and Theo managed to lift one hand and give a thumbs-up, which apparently satisfied everyone, because the calls faded and the night resumed its work.

Elodie became aware, gradually and then all at once, of several facts. That his heart was hammering against her shoulder blade. That his breath was warm on her hair. That she was clutching his forearm with both hands like a drowning woman, and had been for some time after any drowning was possible.

"The ladder," she said, because someone had to say something and her brain had filed everything useful under *later*.

"Rotten rung. I told Maren we should replace that ladder in—" He stopped. His voice did something complicated. "Twelve years ago. I told her that twelve years ago."

"Then it's been planning this for a decade. Very patient ladder."

"Elodie." Her name in his mouth, low and rough, and she felt it in her spine. "Are you hurt?"

"No." True. Mostly true. Nothing was broken except, possibly, her policy of keeping a careful distance. She made no move to get up. Neither did he. The pot crackled. Smoke drifted through the branches overhead, and above the smoke, absurdly, the stars were coming out — hard and bright, the kind of sky that meant deep cold coming.

"We should keep working," she said.

"Yes," he agreed, and didn't move.

A minute passed. Two. Down the slope, another pot caught with a distant whump, and someone laughed at something, and the ordinary world carried on while the two of them lay in the dirt of the orchard they co-owned, holding on to each other under the pretense of recovery.

Finally Theo exhaled, long and controlled, and helped her up — hands lingering a half-second past necessity, which they both pretended not to notice, which was getting harder to sustain by the hour.

* * *

Three in the morning was the hour when the night stopped being dramatic and became simply long. The temperature bottomed out at twenty-six degrees. The pots needed feeding every forty minutes, and the fuel cans got heavier with each round, and the coffee went from hot to terrible to a concept rather than a beverage. People worked in silence now, the camaraderie of the early evening worn down to the bare wire of endurance.

Elodie's hands were black with soot. Her hair smelled like a truck stop. Somewhere around the fourth circuit of the north forty she realized she had stopped thinking entirely and become pure motion — strike, feed, close, move — and that this was the closest thing to peace she had felt in years. No drafts due. No voice to erase herself from. Just flame and cold and the man moving beside her, matching her pace without effort or negotiation.

Around four, they ended up at the top of the hillside, at the oldest tree in the orchard — the Gravenstein Maren had planted the year she took over, gnarled as a fist, still bearing. Its pot sat beneath it like a votive offering. They fed it together and then, instead of moving on, sat down against its trunk, side by side, because their legs simply refused further terms.

The hollow spread below them, glowing row by row, dotted with the small figures of neighbors moving through the smoke. Dot's laugh carried up faintly. Someone was singing — badly, tunelessly, one of the choir members too tired to care.

"They came," Elodie said. "Nobody asked them twice. They just... came."

"Maren never asked either. They came for her frosts too." Theo's head tipped back against the bark. "I think I forgot that. Twelve years running this place like it was a fortress. Turns out it was never a fortress. It was a table, and I was the only one sitting at it."

Elodie turned her soot-blackened hands over in the firelight. "I know something about fortresses," she said. "I built a very good one. Locked doors, professional distance, a career made out of being nobody." She paused. "You want to know the stupidest part? Tonight is the best night I've had in twelve years, and it smells like diesel and my back is ruined and I'm covered in filth."

"That's not stupid."

"It's a little stupid."

"It's not." He said it simply, without performance, and the simplicity undid her more than any eloquence would have. He was quiet a moment, watching the fires below. Then, in the tone of a man stepping off a ledge: "The night you left, I stood at the end of your drive for two hours. I had a speech. It was a good speech. I'd rehearsed it in the truck."

Elodie went very still.

"And then I decided you deserved better than a speech, that if you wanted to go I shouldn't make it harder, and I drove home, and I have regretted the politeness of that decision every single day since." He laughed, short and joyless. "Twelve years of being furious with you, and half of it, if I'm honest, was fury at myself for letting you go without a fight."

"Theo—"

"I'm not finished, because if I stop I won't start again." His jaw worked. "I don't need the whole story tonight. I don't need why. But I need to say my part, because we might die of cold on this hill and I refuse to do it politely again." He turned to look at her, firelight and exhaustion stripping away every last piece of armor. "I loved you at nineteen and I love you now, and there was no gap in between. Not one day. And I am so tired, Elodie. I am so tired of loving you in the past tense."

The words hung in the smoke. Below them the fires breathed. Elodie discovered she was crying, which was inconvenient, and that she didn't care, which was unprecedented.

"I took money to disappear," she said. The truth came out cracked and plain, no craft in it at all. "After the trial — after I testified against the man who ruined my father and lost, and made everything worse — someone offered me money to go away and I took it, because I thought I was poison, because everything I touched broke, and leaving you was the cruelest kindest thing I thought I could do." Her voice shook. "It wasn't kind. It was cowardice wearing kindness's coat. I've been writing other people's truths because I couldn't survive my own."

Theo reached over and took her soot-black hand in his soot-blacker one.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. That's the why. Thank you for the why." He squeezed her hand. "Now here's what happens next. We finish the north forty. We feed the pots till dawn. And then—" his voice caught, steadied— "and then you let me love you in the present tense. If you can stand it. No speeches. I already gave mine."

Elodie laughed through her tears, a wet, ridiculous sound. "Your speech was fine."

"It was adequate."

"It was the best thing anyone's ever said to me on a hillside at four in the morning."

"Low bar. The competition's thin at four in the morning on hillsides." But he was smiling — really smiling, the old smile, the one that had made nineteen-year-old her reckless — and he lifted her hand and pressed his lips to her sooty knuckles, and neither of them said anything else for a while, because nothing needed saying, and the frost was coming, and the fires were burning, and they were done pretending.

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold and merciful.

The thermometer on the barn wall read twenty-seven — cold enough to kill, and would have, if not for the ceiling of warmth the town had held over the blossoms all night. Theo walked the rows as the light strengthened, checking buds, cracking a few open between thumbnail and forefinger with the tenderness of a surgeon. Green. Green. Green. Center intact, pistil unharmed.

He straightened up at the end of the Henderson block and looked back at the sight the storm had left behind: thirty neighbors asleep in cars and truck beds and Dot's diner booths, soot-faced and stiff-necked, having saved an orchard that wasn't theirs for people who had spent twelve years insisting they needed no one.

Elodie came down the hill toward him, gray with smoke, wrecked, radiant. She stopped in front of him and looked up.

"So," she said. "Present tense."

"Present tense," he agreed.

And they kissed among the trees Maren planted, in the smoke-smelling dawn, while the first thaw dripped from ten thousand branches like slow applause — and neither of them saw the black SUV turn off Hollow Road below, slowing, its driver taking a long look at the glowing, waking orchard before continuing on toward the farmhouse.

Voss had arrived early.
