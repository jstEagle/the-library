## 15. Terms of Surrender

*Elodie*

The week began with a list.

Theo wrote it on the back of a feed invoice, in the blunt carpenter's hand that hadn't changed since he was seventeen and labeling his lunch so his brothers wouldn't steal it: *press check. jars. call Hollis re: crates. fix the cidery door before it falls on someone (Elodie).*

"I resent being a parenthetical," she said, reading over his shoulder at the kitchen table with coffee steaming between them.

"You're the someone the door would fall on. You walk through it like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money. I'm ghostwriting this orchard back to life; I should be paid in cider."

He almost smiled — the full version now, not the guarded half he'd rationed out during pruning season — and went back to the list, and Elodie sat there in the morning light of Maren's kitchen and thought: *this is what I gave up for twelve years.* Not him specifically, though yes, him, God, him — but this. The domestic music of it. Two people making a list about jars.

The week had started Sunday night, after the storm, after they'd said the things drenched people say at dawn when pretense has been washed off them like mud. They had agreed, without ever formally agreeing, to stop performing. Which meant that by Monday morning Theo had stopped knocking on her door as if it were a diplomatic embassy, and Elodie had stopped writing in her room with the door locked like a teenager hiding a diary.

Well. Almost stopped.

* * *

Monday was harvest planning. They spread Maren's old orchard maps across the kitchen table — hand-drawn, annotated in Maren's fierce cursive, with comments like *Hollis is wrong about this row, Hollis has always been wrong* — and Elodie discovered that planning a harvest was, structurally, exactly like plotting a novel. Same architecture. Same need to know which characters ripen early and which will break your heart in October.

"This block goes first," Theo said, tapping the map. "Maren always pressed the early apples separate. Said they made an honest cider — nothing to hide behind."

"Did Maren say everything in aphorisms?"

"Maren said everything twice. Once normally and once as a lesson." He looked up. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you write down what people say." He nodded at her notebook, where she'd indeed written *honest cider — nothing to hide behind.* "I'm not a character, Elodie."

"No," she agreed, closing the notebook. "Characters are easier."

He laughed — actually laughed, a sound that seemed to surprise him on its way out — and she felt it land somewhere under her ribs and stay there.

Tuesday they drove to Hollis's farm supply in town, and Elodie experienced the particular pleasure of watching Theo Marsh be known. The man behind the counter greeted him with the weary affection of long acquaintance, tried to sell him a crate sorter Theo didn't need, and then turned to Elodie with open curiosity.

"So you're the one who came back."

"That's me," Elodie said. "The prodigal ghostwriter."

"Huh." He weighed this. "Maren used to talk about you. Said you could make anything true if you wrote it down enough times."

Elodie kept her face pleasant while something cold walked through her chest. *Make anything true.* That had been the compliment, once. Julian Voss had said nearly the same thing, the first time he'd hired her, and she'd taken it as praise for years before she understood it was an instruction.

In the truck on the way home, Theo glanced at her. "You went somewhere just now."

"Grocery store of the mind," she said lightly. "We needed eggs."

He let it go. That was the bargain they'd made in the wreckage of the storm — no more running, but also, not yet, no prying. She told herself the file in her room was a temporary exception. Everyone's honesty has a load-bearing wall or two.

* * *

Wednesday, Theo taught her grafting.

*Theo*

She came out to the nursery rows in the gray-gold morning wearing one of Maren's old cardigans, sleeves pushed up, hair tied back with what appeared to be a pencil, and Theo had to spend a full thirty seconds looking at the trees instead because some sights still shorted him out.

"All right, professor," she said. "Teach me."

"Maren taught me when I was fourteen. I broke six scions before I got one to take." He handed her the grafting knife, handle first, the way Maren had handed it to him. "Rule one: the knife does the work. You're not carving a pumpkin."

"Understood. Rule two?"

"There is no rule two. There's only rule one, repeated until your hands believe it."

She made her first cut too deep, of course, and swore with a fluency that would have made Maren proud, and Theo found himself grinning like an idiot in front of a row of rootstock.

"Say it," she said dangerously.

"I said nothing."

"You're vibrating with it."

"You cut like you're mad at it. The wood didn't do anything to you. Match the cambium layers — green to green — and hold them still. A graft is just two wounds agreeing to heal together."

She went quiet at that. He watched her turn the phrase over, watched her writer's habit of taking every sentence apart to see what else it might mean, and he understood — not for the first time — that he had married his life to a woman who lived in subtext the way other people lived in houses.

By the fourth scion her hands remembered. He saw it happen: the small correction of grip, the angle easing, the cut coming clean in one motion instead of three. Twelve years ago she'd grafted beside him in these same rows, quick and laughing, sap to the elbows. Her hands had not forgotten. It undid him a little, watching proof that the past wasn't dead, just dormant — waiting on weather.

"There," he said, when she set the last one. "That one might live."

"They all might live."

"One in three takes, even for good hands. Maren said grafting was faith with a knife."

Elodie looked down at the row of taped, splinted little hopes and said, softly, "She really did say everything twice."

They stood there a moment in the smell of cut wood and wet grass, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and Theo thought: *say it now. Say the whole thing.* But the words were big and clumsy and he'd never been good at carrying big clumsy things, so instead he said, "Lunch?" and she said, "God, yes," and it was enough. It was, for that hour, everything.

* * *

Thursday she cooked. This was a development.

*Elodie*

The truth was Elodie Halloran could write a five-course tasting menu in prose that made food editors weep, and could produce, in an actual kitchen, approximately four dishes, one of which was toast. But the farmhouse kitchen had Maren's recipe tin on the shelf — index cards in that same fierce cursive, grease-spotted, annotated with verdicts (*too sweet. Halved the sugar. Theo ate three.*) — and Elodie wanted, with an ache that surprised her, to cook one of them correctly.

She chose the apple-stuffed pork loin because the card said *Theo's favorite* in the margin, which felt like cheating and also like strategy, and both were fine.

It took two hours, one minor smoke incident, and a phone call to Dot Fenwick at the farm stand for clarification on what "a knob of butter" meant in units larger than grief. When Theo came in from mending the cidery roof and stopped dead in the doorway, sniffing like a suspicious dog, Elodie felt a satisfaction entirely disproportionate to a pork loin.

"You cooked."

"I contain multitudes."

"Maren's recipe?"

"Maren's recipe."

He sat down slowly, the way a man sits when he's not sure the chair is real. He ate. He was quiet for a while, and Elodie recognized the quiet — it was the quiet of someone meeting a ghost at their own dinner table.

"She used to make this in October," he finally said. "First press day. Whole crew, everyone filthy, and this on the table by dark."

"I know. There's a postcard in the trunk about it. To someone named Wren. 'Made the loin. Set a plate anyway.'" Elodie paused. "Who's Wren?"

Theo's fork stopped. "You read that?"

"It fell open. I wasn't—" She stopped, because she had been. She had been reading the trunk papers steadily every night, sorting, transcribing, building — but that part she couldn't say. "I've been going through Maren's papers. For the memoir. Our memoir. I told you I wanted to finish it."

"You did tell me." He resumed eating, but slower. "Wren was her person. Before my uncle. Before any of it. Town ran Wren off in '98 and Maren never forgave Bramble Hollow, not really, she just stayed and outlived everyone who'd done it." He looked up. "Why? What did the postcard say?"

Elodie hesitated. Some sentences were landmines and she'd learned to map them. "'Some plates you set knowing no one's coming. It's how you keep setting them.'"

Theo put his fork down. "Yeah," he said quietly. "That's Maren." And then, after a moment, reaching across the table to take her hand like it was the most ordinary thing in the world: "The loin's good. She'd have halved the sugar, but the loin's good."

Elodie laughed and squeezed his hand and did not think about the folder hidden under the false bottom of her suitcase, the one growing fatter every night. She failed completely at not thinking about it, actually. But she smiled through it, and dinner held, and afterward they washed dishes together in a rhythm so easy it frightened her — because things this easy, in her experience, were always built over something.

* * *

Friday night she worked late. She heard Theo come up the stairs, pause outside her door — a new courtesy, or maybe just old habit returning — and continue on. She waited until his footsteps faded, then pulled the folder from its hiding place.

*Ledger pages. Photocopies of wire transfers. The shell company's registration — Voss Meridian Holdings, LLC, initials like a signature he thought no one would notice. Eleven years of payments routed out of Bramble Hollow Orchard into a company that owned, as far as she could trace, nothing but paper.*

And beneath it all, the thing she hadn't shown anyone: the draft offer letter from Voss's publisher, dated three weeks ago, confirming delivery terms for *his* memoir. Her name nowhere on it. Her fingerprints nowhere on it. Twelve years of her sentences, signed away, and if she burned Voss now, the industry would ask why his book died mid-manuscript, and the answer would lead straight to her, and ghostwriters who burned clients didn't get hired — they got blacklisted, quietly, permanently, the way Maren's Wren had gotten run out of town.

The career that raised her. That had fed her when her father's ruin left nothing. That had taught her that a name was a liability and invisibility was safety.

She looked at the folder. The folder looked back, the way folders do.

"Just one more week," she told it. "Let me have the week."

The folder, being paper, said nothing, which was somehow worse.

* * *

Saturday it rained, soft and steady, and they spent it in the barn cataloging the cellar stock, and it was the best day Elodie had spent in twelve years, and she hated herself a little for the *and*, for the way joy and guilt had learned to share a sentence in her head.

*Theo*

Sunday evening Theo found her asleep on the kitchen table.

Not at it — on it, cheek on Maren's atlas of rootstocks, pen still in hand, lamplight pooling around her. There was a page under her hand covered in her slanted handwriting, and Theo, being a gentleman, did not read it. Being a man hopelessly in love, he read exactly one line before his conscience caught him:

*Maren deserved better than to be a footnote in his book.*

He stood in the doorway a long time. The rain had come back, ticking against the glass. Elodie murmured something in her sleep — his name, he was almost sure, though she'd deny it — and Theo Marsh, who had spent twelve years learning to want nothing, stood in his mother's kitchen wanting everything, and noticed, for the first time, that the woman he loved had been carrying something heavy all week. He'd seen the signs before. In himself, in soldiers he'd served with, in Maren in her last year: the careful smile, the laugh arriving a half-second late, the way a person holds their body around a weight and calls it posture.

He didn't ask. Their truce said no prying, and he was a man who honored terms.

But Monday morning, when the mail came, and among the seed catalogs and the electric bill there was a thick cream-colored envelope with a city postmark and a return address Theo recognized from the news — *Voss Media Group* — he watched Elodie go white to the lips, take the envelope, and carry it upstairs without opening it, like a woman carrying something with teeth.

And Theo Marsh stood in the hallway below, listening to her door close, and understood that whatever she was carrying, it had just changed weight.
