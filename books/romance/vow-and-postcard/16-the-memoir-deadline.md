## 16. The Memoir Deadline

The call came on a Tuesday morning, which Elodie would later think was fitting. Tuesdays were the days Voss liked to ruin things — he'd once announced the cancellation of a whole publishing imprint on a Tuesday, over eggs Benedict, with the serene expression of a man describing weather.

She was at the kitchen table with Maren's ledger pages spread out before her like a spread of tarot cards, each one dealing some new small catastrophe. Theo was outside somewhere doing something violent to a fence post. The kettle had just gone quiet. And her phone lit up with a name she'd spent twelve years training herself not to flinch at.

*Julian Voss.*

She let it ring four times. Long enough to seem busy. Not so long as to seem afraid.

"Elodie." His voice was warm the way a heated knife is warm. "I hope I'm not interrupting anything important."

"Only breakfast," she said. "And the slow financial death of an orchard."

"Ah, yes. The orchard." A pause, delicately weighted. "How is rural life treating you? Finding enough to write about?"

Her hand went cold around the phone. *Finding enough to write about.* She looked down at the ledger pages, at the neat columns of Maren's handwriting, and felt the words rearrange themselves into something else entirely.

"I'm sure you didn't call to ask after my hobbies, Julian."

"No. I called because my book is due." Paper rustled on his end; she could picture him in his glass office high above the city, one ankle crossed over his knee, watching his own reflection approve of him. "My editor wants the final manuscript in ten days. Ten, Elodie. And I'm told the last three chapters are still... gestating."

"They're drafted. They need polish."

"Good. Excellent. You've always been quick." A sip of something — coffee, or the sound of a man drinking his own importance. "Because here's the thing. I've been thinking about your little sabbatical in the country, and it occurs to me that you've been there rather longer than grief requires."

Elodie stood up. It wasn't a decision; her body simply refused to have this conversation sitting down.

"Maren died in February," she said evenly. "It's October."

"And yet you're still there. Still sorting through her things, I'd imagine. Her papers. Her—" he paused, and she could hear him smiling, actually hear it, the way you can hear a trap close even over a bad line, "—her *correspondence*."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, faintly, came the rhythmic thud of Theo's mallet, steady as a heartbeat.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." He said it kindly, which was worse. "Elodie, let me save us both the theater. A mutual acquaintance of ours handles estate matters for a number of families in that part of the county. He mentioned — casually, over golf, where all the world's secrets go to die — that Maren Marsh kept an attic full of papers. Unsent letters. Ledgers. The beginnings of some sort of... memoir project."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree. Elodie gripped the back of a chair.

"A memoir," Voss continued, "addressed, charmingly, to 'the girl who owes me nothing.' Which I assume is you. It's always you, isn't it? You collect the unfinished stories of dead women like other people collect spoons."

"What do you want, Julian?"

"*Finally*, a direct question. I want my book. Finished, polished, brilliant, in ten days. In exchange, whatever you found in that attic stays in that attic. You'll notice I haven't asked what you found. That's generosity. That's trust." Another sip. "Because here's what happens if you don't deliver. If you go wandering off down some sentimental lane about ledgers and shell companies — and do be careful, Elodie, initials are such dangerous things, anyone can have the wrong ones — then I will be forced to have a very public conversation about your professional practices. About how many books you've written under other people's names. About the clauses in those contracts. About how every word of praise ever heaped on 'Julian Voss, author' belongs, contractually and morally, to a woman hiding in an orchard."

"You can't void my inheritance," she said. "That's not how deeds work."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But I can make sure the town of Bramble Hollow learns exactly who their new co-owner is. The woman who writes lies for a living. The woman who took money to disappear once before — oh yes, I know about that too, did you think I hired you by accident? — and who's now squatting in a dead woman's house reading her private papers for material." He sighed, a rich, regretful sound. "Small towns are so unforgiving of ghosts, Elodie. You of all people should know."

She sat down slowly, because her knees had opinions.

"The manuscript," she said. "Ten days."

"Ten days. Delivered clean. And then we never speak of attics again." Warmth flooded back into his voice, sunlight returning after a cloud. "You're the best in the business. It's why I chose you. Finish my story, and yours stays exactly where you want it: nowhere."

He hung up before she could answer, which was also like him. Julian Voss ended conversations the way he ended marriages and publishing houses — abruptly, and always before the other person had said their final word.

Elodie set the phone face-down on the table.

Then she looked at the ledger pages, at Maren's small fierce handwriting marching across columns of numbers that added up to theft, and understood, with terrible clarity, that every identity she owned had just been taken hostage. The ghostwriter. The heiress. The woman Theo was slowly learning to look at again. All of them, locked in a trunk, and Julian Voss holding the only key.

*Ten days.*

Outside, the mallet struck again. Steady. Patient. Building something instead of tearing it down.

She put her head in her hands and, for the first time since she'd arrived at Bramble Hollow, allowed herself to consider the possibility that she might not be clever enough to get out of this one.

* * *

Theo found her an hour later, still at the table, the ledger pages squared into a neat stack that fooled no one. He came in smelling of cut wood and cold air, hung his coat on its peg, and stopped when he saw her face.

"What happened."

Not *are you all right*. Not *you look pale*. Just the question itself, flat and immediate, as if bad news arriving were simply another chore on the list, and he was ready to pick up his half of it.

"Julian called."

Theo went very still. She watched him decide, visibly, not to say the first three things that occurred to him.

"And?"

"He wants his memoir finished in ten days. Final manuscript. Or—" She turned the top ledger page toward him, the column of payments, the shell company's name with its tidy initials. "Or he makes very sure everyone learns what I am. What I've done. Who I've written for. He says he can't touch the deed, but he can poison this town against me, and Theo—" her voice caught, and she hated it, "—he knew about the attic. Someone told him about the attic."

Theo pulled out the chair across from her and sat, forearms on the table, close enough that she could see the wood dust still caught in the creases of his knuckles.

"Who else knows about the attic?" he asked.

"Maren. Me. And whoever handled her estate business." She laughed, a thin, airless sound. "Which apparently plays golf with Julian Voss. This whole county is two people in a trench coat."

"He threatened you."

"He offered me a deal dressed up as a threat. It's his best suit." She pressed her palms flat against the table, against the solid grain of it, as if she might anchor herself there. "Finish his book, bury Maren's truth, keep my name hidden where it's always been. Ten days, and everything goes back to normal."

"And if you don't?"

"Then I lose the career that fed me for fifteen years, and this town learns I'm a professional liar living off a dead woman's land. He'll take the orchard fight without throwing a punch, because no one will stand beside me long enough to try."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and even, but underneath it ran something she hadn't heard from him before — not anger at her, but a banked fury aimed elsewhere, precise and cold.

"He thinks you'll do it."

"He has good reason to think that. I always have before."

"Yeah." Theo looked at her, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it harder to bear. "But he doesn't know what I know."

"Which is?"

"That you read those ledger pages for six hours last night and didn't sleep. That you've got them cross-referenced with dates he doesn't even know matter yet. That you sat at this table looking like a woman doing arithmetic she hates." He leaned back. "A person planning to bury the truth doesn't organize it first, Elodie. Believe me. I've buried plenty, and I never once made a filing system."

Despite everything — despite the phone still lying face-down between them like a dead thing — she felt her mouth twitch. "That's the least romantic compliment anyone's ever given me."

"I'm out of practice." But he was almost smiling too, and for a moment the kitchen held something warm and fragile, a candle flame cupped in both hands.

Then Elodie looked back down at the ledger, and the moment passed through her fingers.

"Ten days," she said quietly. "I can finish his book in ten days. I've done it faster. That's the horror of it, Theo — I could sit down tonight and give Julian Voss three flawless chapters of a life he didn't live, and he'd win awards for it, and no one would ever know. That's the one thing I'm genuinely gifted at. Making lies beautiful."

"And the other thing?"

She looked up.

"The true thing," Theo said. "Maren's book. The one addressed to you. How long would that take?"

"I don't know. She left it unfinished. There are gaps — chapters she sketched, chapters she only titled. I'd have to write them, and they'd have to be *right*, because they're hers, and—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "It wouldn't be ten days. And it couldn't be published under a ghost's name. That's the entire point of it. That's the entire point of *me*, if I ever manage to become one."

"So it's not really a choice between two deadlines." Theo's voice was gentle now, and terribly clear. "It's a choice between the life you've had and the one you keep almost starting."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a crow argued with the fence posts. Elodie thought about the page she'd written in the attic — her own name at the top of it, the first time in fifteen years — and how it had felt less like writing and more like setting a bone.

"There's something else," she said. "Something I haven't told you. When he said he knew about the attic, he said something stranger. He said — 'initials are such dangerous things, anyone can have the wrong ones.'" She slid the ledger page across the table, her finger stopping on the shell company's name. "He was telling me he knows I've seen this. And he was daring me to prove it's him. As if proof were something he's already handled."

Theo studied the page. Then he stood, went to the dresser, and came back with a battered tin box that Elodie recognized from the cidery office — the one that held the orchard's older records, the ones Maren had kept before her hands got too stiff for handwriting.

"If he's already handled the proof," Theo said, setting the tin down between them with a click that sounded like a decision, "then we've got ten days to find the proof he missed. Because I've been going through these since August, and there's a pattern in the payment dates. They don't match invoices. They match *harvests*. Every year, right after the press run, money moved. Whoever built that shell company didn't just steal from Maren, Elodie. They stole the orchard's best years, one season at a time. And seasons leave receipts."

Elodie stared at him. "You've known about the dates?"

"I've known something was wrong since spring. I didn't know what." He opened the tin, and the smell of old paper rose up between them, apples and ink and time. "Now I know who to thank for teaching me to look."

She reached across the table and touched the edge of the tin, as if sealing a pact.

"Ten days," she said.

"Ten days," Theo agreed. Then, with the ghost of Maren's own dry humor: "Better get started. Dead women don't write fast, and neither do liars — but you're done being either one."

Elodie pulled the nearest folder toward her, opened it, and began.

Above the farmhouse, in the attic, Maren's unfinished manuscript waited in its trunk, patient as winter. Below it, at the kitchen table, the girl it was addressed to finally picked up her pen — not to erase herself this time, but to begin.
