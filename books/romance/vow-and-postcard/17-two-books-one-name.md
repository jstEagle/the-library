## 17. Two Books, One Name

The deadline sat on the kitchen table like a stone.

Ten days. Voss's voice still lived in the phone's silence after the call — that velvet patience of his, the way he said *Elodie* as though she were a chapter he'd commissioned and could revise at will. *I know you have her papers. I know what you've been writing up there. Deliver my book, and we'll all pretend the orchard is just an orchard.*

She had made coffee she didn't drink. She had sat with Maren's trunk open in front of her, ledger pages fanned out like a hand of cards she'd been afraid to play. And somewhere between the second sleepless hour and the third, something in her had gone very quiet and very clear, the way water does when it finally stops fighting the shape of its container.

*Two books,* she thought. *One name.*

Not his. Not a ghost's. Hers — and Maren's, side by side on a title page, the way they should have been from the beginning. Elodie Halloran and Maren Ashgrove. She could see it already: the spine, the small type, the dedication Maren had drafted herself years ago and never sent anywhere. *For the girl who owes me nothing.* Well. The girl owed her everything now, and intended to pay it in the only currency she'd ever actually possessed — the truth, set down properly, under a name that would have to stand behind it.

And Voss's book? Voss's book could die unfinished, gasping, a manuscript-shaped hole where a man's reputation used to be. Let him explain to his publisher why his memoir ended mid-sentence. Let him hire another ghost and discover that no one else could make him sound like a better man than he was. That had always been her real talent, hadn't it — not the sentences, but the mercy. She had spent twelve years lending her voice to people so they could sound innocent.

She was done lending.

The decision, once made, was almost embarrassingly easy. What came after was not. Because finishing Maren's book meant assembling the evidence — every ledger page, every photocopied invoice, every postcard and dated receipt — into something a journalist could hold, a lawyer could threaten, a town could read aloud. And assembling it meant carrying it. And carrying it meant, at some point, someone seeing her carry it.

Which is how Theo Marsh found her at six in the morning, kneeling in the attic with a canvas duffel bag open beside her, wrapping Maren's ledgers in dish towels like they were heirloom china.

He didn't say anything at first. She heard him on the stairs — the third step still groaned; she'd have to fix that or stop noticing it — and then his boots stopped in the attic doorway, and the whole space seemed to hold its breath along with her.

"You're leaving," he said.

It wasn't an accusation. That was somehow worse. It was just tired, the way a man gets tired who has been braced for a blow for twelve years and has learned to recognize the wind-up.

"No." She kept her hands moving, because if she stopped she'd have to look at him. "No, I'm packing."

"Same thing, historically."

"Theo—"

"I'm not angry." He came into the attic properly, ducking under the low beam, and crouched down across the trunk from her. Up close she could see he hadn't slept either. "I want to be clear about that before you build a whole argument I have to lose. I'm not angry."

"Then what are you?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything — fence posts, frost warnings, her. "Scared," he said finally. "Last time you packed, you were gone before the kettle cooled. So my body doesn't quite believe the word 'no' yet. But my brain does. My brain watched you read Maren's postcards until three in the morning and then get up and prune the Cortlands like a woman with somewhere to be. People who are running don't prune."

Despite everything — the stone on the table, the ten days, the duffel bag — Elodie laughed. It came out cracked but real. "That's your evidence? Pruning?"

"It's the best evidence I've got." He reached over and lifted one of the wrapped ledgers out of the bag, turning it in his hands. "What's all this, then? If you're not running."

And there it was. The moment. She could feel it standing in the room with them, patient as weather. She could tell him the safe version — *research, logistics, nothing to worry about* — the version that kept the walls up and the truce intact and cost her nothing except, apparently, everything, because she had done safe for twelve years and it had gotten her a career built on other people's names and a heart that flinched at mail.

Or she could do the thing Maren's book had been teaching her, chapter by chapter, in a dead woman's careful handwriting: *say the true thing, even badly.*

"I'm building a case," Elodie said. "Against Julian Voss. Everything Maren kept — the shell company, the payments, the invoices with his initials hidden in them like a child's signature. I'm putting it in order. And then I'm going to finish her memoir, and publish it, and put my own name on it, and let his book rot on his desk." She exhaled, shaky. "There. That's what's in the bag. Not a suitcase. An arsenal."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a crow argued with something about the state of the north field.

"He threatened you," he said. Not a question.

"He knows I have the papers. He gave me ten days to deliver his manuscript or he'll expose the ghostwriting and try to void the inheritance." She shrugged, aiming for lightness and missing by a mile. "So really, the bag is also a resignation letter. From my entire life."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"I'm doing it anyway."

He set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way he handled anything older than himself. Then he did something she didn't expect, which was to reach past her and pull a second dish towel off the shelf, and begin wrapping the next ledger himself.

"What are you doing?"

"Packing," he said. "You said you're not leaving. I'm choosing to believe you. Out loud. With dish towels." His ears had gone faintly red, which after all these weeks she knew meant he was more moved than he wanted anyone to notice. "You don't get to carry all of it alone. That was the old arrangement. The old arrangement was garbage."

Elodie sat back on her heels. Something in her chest — some load-bearing wall she'd been leaning against since she was twenty-six — quietly developed a crack, and through the crack came warmth, which was inconvenient and wonderful.

"Theo Marsh," she said, "are you offering to help me destroy a literary empire?"

"I'm offering to photocopy things. I'm good at machines with buttons. And I'm offering—" he paused, and looked at her straight on, no armor anywhere in his face, "—to stand next to you when it gets loud. Because it will get loud. Voss doesn't fight fair, and lawyers are weather to people like us. But there are more of us than there are of him. There always were. Maren just never knew it."

"She knew," Elodie said softly. "That's what the postcards were. She knew exactly how many of us there were. She just couldn't say it out loud anymore." She pressed her palm flat against the trunk's open lid. "So we'll say it for her."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and it was the strangest, gentlest labor of her life.

They spread Maren's papers across the cidery barn's long table because the light was better there, and because — Theo pointed out, deadpan — if Voss was going to have her followed, the least they could do was give the poor man a view worth photographing. They sorted by year, then by season, then by the color of ink Maren had used, which turned out to be its own kind of calendar: black for ordinary days, blue for worries, red for the years she'd stopped trusting anyone.

"There," Theo said, tapping a red-ink entry from 2011. "That's when the payments started going through Halloway Ventures. H-V. He didn't even hide it well. He hid it *confidently.*"

"That's his whole method," Elodie said. "Confidence is just theft wearing a nice coat."

They photocopied until the machine overheated and Theo stood fanning it with his hat like a nurse with a fainting patient. They drank tea that went cold because neither wanted to leave the table. And twice — twice — Elodie caught herself explaining a document and heard her own voice change: not the smoothed, invisible ghost-voice she used for clients, but her actual voice, quick and sharp and occasionally funny, and each time Theo looked at her like a man watching a window being unboarded.

At noon she found the last piece. It had been tucked inside Maren's copy of the orchard's original deed — a single sheet, undated, in blue ink:

*I have decided to write down what happened to me. Not for the courts. Courts want receipts and I have those too, God help me. This is for whoever comes after. If you're reading this, you're probably the girl. You owe me nothing, but if you're reading this, maybe we owe each other the truth. Start here: I trusted a charming man because I was lonely, and loneliness is the most expensive thing I ever bought.*

Elodie read it aloud, because some words needed air. When she finished, the barn was silent except for the photocopier ticking as it cooled.

"She wrote her own opening," Theo said. "Twelve years ago. She was waiting for you."

"No," Elodie said, and her throat was tight and her eyes were hot and she was smiling anyway. "She was *trusting* me. There's a difference. I just took a decade to deserve it."

* * *

By evening the evidence lived in three places — a folder in the farmhouse, a box at the solicitor's office, and a memory card in a tin of grafting wax where no one in the city would ever think to look. Redundancy, Theo called it. Insurance against fire, flood, and publishers.

Elodie sat at the kitchen table with a fresh legal pad and wrote two lines at the top of the first page:

*MAREN ASHGROVE: A MEMOIR*
*completed by Elodie Halloran*

Her hand shook on the second line. Twelve years of signing other people's names, and her own felt foreign in her grip, oversized, like shoes she'd outgrown and forgotten she owned. She wrote it again. Steadier. Then a third time, until it simply looked like what it was: hers.

Theo came in smelling of woodsmoke and evening, set two mugs down, and read the pad upside down without asking permission, which was its own small revolution.

"When does it go out?" he asked.

"Thursday. There's a journalist — Dana Okafor, she runs an independent press out of Kestrel Falls. I emailed her tonight. She wrote back in nine minutes." Elodie turned the pad toward him. "But Thursday means Voss finds out Wednesday. Maybe sooner. He'll come here, or he'll come at me through lawyers, or he'll do something worse and smile while he does it."

"Then we've got Wednesday," Theo said. He turned his mug slowly on the table. "You know what Maren taught me about grafting? You don't do it in calm weather. Everyone thinks you should wait for perfect conditions. But the cut heals faster when the tree's a little stressed. Scared sap runs sweet." He glanced up. "We're scared. Good. Means it'll take."

Elodie reached across the table and took his hand — deliberately, the way you lay a foundation stone, checking that it sat level before you built on it. Trust, rebuilt brick by brick, mortar still wet.

"Together, then," she said.

"Together." He held on. "Though I reserve the right to be terrified in private."

"Denied. You terrified in private for twelve years. Look what it got you — a sign, a hammer, and frozen ground."

"A very good sign," he protested. "*NOT FOR SALE.* Best sentence I ever wrote."

"Second best," Elodie said, and tapped the legal pad, where her own name sat drying in the lamplight like a promise.

Outside, the orchard stood dark and patient, ten thousand trees holding their blossoms like held breath. Somewhere down the valley, a car was already winding its way toward Bramble Hollow — too early for neighbors, too late for deliveries — and neither of them saw it yet, headlights sweeping the hedgerows like a searchlight finding its mark.
