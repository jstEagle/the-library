## 2. Proof and Yeast

The sourdough starter had been alive longer than Marisol Vega's marriage prospects, and it was the only thing in her life she'd never once considered killing.

She fed it at five a.m., as her mother had done, as her grandmother before that—a cup of flour, a splash of warm water, a whispered word in Spanish that was less prayer than habit. *Levadura*, yeast. Proof. The word meant two things in English, which Marisol had always found quietly hilarious: proof was what you demanded of a claim, and proof was what you waited for when you wanted dough to rise. Evidence and faith, wearing the same coat.

Her bakery needed both.

At 5:14, with the ovens ticking toward temperature and the town of Alder Creek still dark outside the windows, she stood at the counter with the lease spread flat beneath a flour-dusted palm. Thirty days. The paper didn't look like a death sentence; it looked like every other piece of paper that had ever tried to ruin her—thin, officious, printed on both sides to save money. But she read it the way she read everything now, twice, slowly, hunting for the loophole the way other people hunted for truffles.

*Landlord reserves the right to non-renew upon expiration of current term.*

No loophole. There never was one. Loopholes were for people with lawyers, and Marisol had an accountant named Deb who did her best and a dead mother whose recipe box sat in the back office like a reliquary nobody opened.

Thirty days.

She tied her apron—the same canvas apron her mother had worn, faded from rust-red to the color of old brick—and began the work. This was the mercy of bread: it did not care about your feelings. The dough wanted exactly what it wanted, warmth and time and salt, and if you gave it those things honestly, it rose. If you lied to it, rushed it, cheated the bulk ferment by even twenty minutes, it punished you in the crumb. Her mother used to say the oven was the only honest judge left in the world. Marisol believed her. She'd watched enough people lie their way through enough years to know how rare honesty was, even in flour form.

By six, the first loaves were in, and the smell began its slow colonization of the room—toasted grain, caramelizing crust, the deep brown warmth that made strangers on the sidewalk stop mid-stride like they'd walked into a wall made of childhood. By seven, the lights were on and the case was filling: pain au chocolat with laminated layers she'd learned from a Frenchwoman in Napa who'd told her, *your hands are too good for this town*, and Marisol had smiled and said nothing, because her hands were not too good for this town. Her hands were this town's hands. They had kneaded it into shape since 1987.

At 7:40, her phone buzzed against the steel counter.

**DANIELLE:** *Board call moved up. 9am. Bring the Thornbury numbers.*

Marisol looked at the message for a long moment, then set the phone face-down beside the proofer, where the croissants were rising in their warm, humid dark, patient as anything that knows it will be eaten eventually.

Thornbury.

She picked the phone back up, put it down again. Picked it up. Typed *on my way after morning rush* and deleted it, because Danielle hated hedging, then retyped it, because lying to Danielle was like lying to a customs officer—pointless and later expensive.

*On my way after morning rush.*

There. Definitive. Professional. A woman with thirty days left on a lease and no sentimentality about vineyards or the men who owned them.

---

The morning rush came and went the way it always did, a tide of familiar faces and familiar orders. Ruth Ellery, two almond croissants, paid in exact change counted out from a coin purse older than Marisol. The Herrera boys, sent by their mother for a loaf of the seeded rye and instructions to *not eat it on the way home*, which they violated within sight of the door. Old Tom Vasquez, who ordered a cortado she wasn't licensed to sell and got a small coffee with steamed milk anyway, because some rules existed to be broken gently.

"You look tired, mija," Tom said, accepting his cup.

"I'm radiant."

"Mm." He sipped. "Your mother used to make that face. Right before she reorganized the whole shop at midnight."

"She reorganized the whole shop at midnight because she was avoiding my father's letters."

"And you're avoiding what?"

Marisol wiped down the counter with more force than the counter deserved. "I'm avoiding nothing. I'm running a business."

Tom looked at her over the rim of his cup with the mild, terrible patience of a man who had watched her grow up and had therefore seen everything. "The Thornbury boy," he said. Not a question. A diagnosis.

"Gideon Thornbury is a line item," Marisol said. "A parcel number. He's forty acres of river-bottom land with water rights and a tasting room that smells like dust and regret, and in three weeks he'll either sign or he won't, and either way I will have a cappuccino and go home."

Tom smiled into his coffee. "Forty acres," he repeated softly. "That's a lot of land to describe a person in."

"Get out of my shop, Tom."

He went, leaving exact change and a tip that was too big, the way he always did, the way her mother's customers still did sometimes, as though the bakery were a church and generosity were the collection plate.

* * *

Vega Cellars occupied a converted fruit-packing house on the east side of town, all exposed beams and reclaimed wood and the particular aggressive casualness of money trying to look relaxed. Marisol parked her ancient Subaru between Danielle's Tesla and Marcus's Audi and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, doing the breathing exercise her therapist had taught her, the one for board meetings and funerals.

They were the same category now, apparently.

Inside, the conference room smelled like someone else's ambition—cold brew and laser printer toner. Danielle Okafor stood at the head of the table in a blazer the color of a storm front, and Marcus Lee sat to her right with his sleeves rolled and his laptop open like a shield. On the screen behind them, a map of the valley glowed, and there it was: the Thornbury parcel, outlined in blue, sitting along the river like a held breath.

"Marisol." Danielle gestured to a chair. "Sit. Tell me where we are."

"We're where we were last week." Marisol sat. "Gideon Thornbury won't sell. He's refused two offers and the bank's extension. He's going to ride it into foreclosure rather than hand it to us."

"He can't ride anywhere," Marcus said. "The debt service alone—" He turned his laptop so she could see the spreadsheet, columns of red marching downward like casualties. "He's leveraged to the teeth. One bad frost and the whole thing comes down. We just have to be standing there when it does."

"That's not a strategy, that's vulturing."

"That's *timing*," Marcus said, unbothered. "There's a difference, and the difference is the return profile."

Danielle held up a hand. "Here's why we're moving the timeline." She clicked, and the map zoomed, and a second parcel appeared adjacent to the Thornbury land, this one in green. "The Hollis farm. Forty acres, south-facing slope, river access. It hit the market yesterday. We're not the only ones looking at it. If we don't lock the Thornbury parcel in the next three weeks, we lose the contiguous acreage, and the whole project becomes a hobby instead of a winery."

Three weeks. Thirty days. The numbers aligned with the cruelty of things that are aligned on purpose.

"So close it," Marcus said, looking at Marisol. "You're the relationship. You grew up here. He knows you. Get in the room, get the signature."

"I've been in the room. He doesn't want to sell."

"Then make him want to." Marcus leaned back. "Or find the pressure point. Everybody has one. His is probably pride. Or debt. Usually they're the same thing."

Marisol looked at the map. At the blue outline of Thornbury Vineyards, the place where she had spent one entire summer at twenty-five learning the difference between a vine and a grapevine, learning what a man's hands looked like when they were gentle with something living, learning the taste of wine pressed at midnight by two people who thought they were inventing love.

She looked away.

"The guesthouse is on the parcel," she said, her voice level, professional, a knife laid flat on a table. "Historic structure. Any demolition or conversion would need county review. That slows everything down. If we're serious about timeline, we should know what we're walking into."

Something flickered across Danielle's face—not quite suspicion, something adjacent to it. "You've been to the guesthouse?"

"I've been to the property."

"When?"

"A long time ago."

Danielle let the silence sit for exactly as long as it took to become a question, then chose, visibly, not to ask it. "Fine. Site survey this week. You do it—you know the ground. Walk the parcel, assess the structures, get me a condition report and a realistic acquisition path. And Marisol—" Danielle's voice softened by a single degree, which from Danielle was practically tenderness. "Whatever history is in that valley, leave it in the valley. We're buying dirt and vines. Nothing else ships."

"Understood," Marisol said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did, go smooth and closed, a door shutting politely.

Nothing else ships. As if feeling were freight. As if she hadn't spent ten years perfecting the art of shipping nothing, of packing her grief in bubble wrap and labeling it *FRAGILE: DO NOT OPEN UNTIL NEVER*.

* * *

She drove back through town with the windows down despite the cold, because the cold kept her honest. Main Street slid past: the hardware store, the diner, the empty storefront that had been a bookstore and before that a video rental and before that, briefly, optimistically, a candle shop. Towns like Alder Creek didn't die dramatically. They lost a store every few years, the way old men lose hearing, one frequency at a time, until suddenly the world was quiet and nobody could say exactly when it happened.

Her mother had watched that quiet coming for years and fought it with flour. *As long as the ovens run,* she used to say, wiping down the case at closing, *the street has a heartbeat.* Then she'd gotten sick, and the sickness ran its own schedule, indifferent as weather, and Marisol had come home from Napa to hold her hand through eight months of diminishing and had inherited, at thirty-one, a bakery, a recipe box, a grief with no bottom shelf to put it on, and a lease.

Now the lease was dying too.

She parked behind the shop and stood in the alley for a moment, looking up at the fire escape where, at nineteen, she'd sat with a boy from the Thornbury place who'd driven into town on a tractor-road excuse and shared a stolen bottle of his father's reserve with her, both of them giddy and illegal and certain. He'd said, *someday this whole valley will know our names.* She'd said, *that's the stupidest thing anyone ever said to me,* and kissed him, and meant, *say it again.*

Ancient history. That was the phrase. She'd been using it for ten years, the way you use a good hinge—so often you forget it's machinery.

Inside, the afternoon light came slanting through the front windows, catching the flour motes suspended in the air like a slow snowfall of small bright things. The case gleamed. The ovens ticked as they cooled. Everything in the shop was exactly where her mother had left it, plus ten years of Marisol's careful additions, and in thirty days it would all be inventory or landfill.

She went to the office—the closet-sized room with the safe and the filing cabinets and the recipe box on the high shelf, tin, dented, painted with fading roses. She did not take it down. She never took it down. Opening it meant reading her mother's handwriting, and reading her mother's handwriting meant the eight months of hospital chairs and vending machine coffee and the last afternoon, when Rosa Vega had gripped her daughter's wrist with sudden ferocious strength and said, *mija, promise me you won't let that place die,* and Marisol had said yes, yes, whatever you need, meaning the bakery, meaning all of it, meaning she would carry the whole burning building on her back if her mother asked, and her mother had smiled and closed her eyes and that was that.

Promise given. Promise kept. Ten years of keeping it, and now the keeping was expiring in thirty days, same as the lease.

Marisol sat down at the desk and pulled the Thornbury file toward her. Parcel maps. Soil assays. Water rights documentation. Gideon's refusal letters, each one polite as a locked door. She knew his handwriting from these letters—she'd studied it, hating herself for studying it—the same narrow, controlled script he'd had at twenty-five, when he'd written the daily measurements in the vineyard log in that same careful hand: *Brix 22.4. Acid holding. Rain expected Thursday.*

Rain expected Thursday. He'd written the weather like a man who trusted it.

She flipped to the appraisal summary and stopped. The guesthouse. There it was in the assessor's language: *historic stone structure, circa 1890, condition fair-to-poor, significant deferred maintenance.* Fair-to-poor. She could see it perfectly, of course—everyone who'd grown up in the valley could—the long low building at the edge of the vine rows, its stone walls gone gold in evening light, the iron bedstead, the fireplace big enough to stand in. She'd slept there once, in another life, in a bed that smelled of cedar and him, while a storm came down off the mountain and the whole valley disappeared into white and neither of them had wanted it to end.

She shut the file.

Enough. She was thirty-five years old, she owned a third of a growing winery, she made croissants that people drove forty miles for, and she did not have time to be haunted by a summer that ended the way summers end, which is to say badly, which is to say with a boy choosing his inheritance over her and a girl learning that promises are just sentences people say out loud when they want something.

The phone buzzed. Danielle again:

**DANIELLE:** *Survey Thursday. Forecast says snow moving in Friday. Beat the weather.*

Thursday. She'd walk the Thornbury parcel Thursday. She'd put on her boots and her field jacket and her most neutral expression, and she'd catalogue that land like the asset it was—soil types, drainage, structural deficiencies, demolition costs—and she would feel precisely nothing, because Gideon Thornbury was a line item, a parcel number, forty acres of river-bottom dirt, and the past was a country she had emigrated from without papers and could never go back to.

She texted back: *Confirmed. Thursday AM.*

Then she stood, tied her apron back on, and went out to shape tomorrow's dough, because the dough didn't care about her history, and the oven was honest, and proof—real proof, rising proof, the kind you could hold in your hands—was the only kind she trusted anymore.

Outside, the first clouds were already coming down off the mountains, earlier than forecast, gray and patient, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it.
