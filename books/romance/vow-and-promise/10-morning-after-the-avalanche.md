## 10. Morning After the Avalanche

Gideon woke before dawn, as he always did, but this morning his body knew something had changed before his mind consented to it. The fire in the wood stove had burned down to a red memory, and the guesthouse was cold enough that his breath ghosted silver above him. He was on one end of the couch. Marisol was on the other, curled beneath the wool blanket they'd divided like a treaty line, and between them lay six inches of cushioned no-man's-land where, at some hour he could not account for, his hand had crossed.

He remembered the kiss the way you remember a fall—suddenly, completely, with the ground still rising up in you.

It had happened after midnight, after her mother, after the bakery that was supposed to be theirs, after she'd said *you chose the vines over me* and he had not been able to deny it. After ten years of silence had cracked open like an ice sheet and neither of them had known how to stop the water coming through. She had been crying, or nearly crying, or fighting it with that set of her jaw he knew better than his own signature. And then she'd turned toward him in the candlelight and the distance between them—the six inches, the ten years, the whole frozen geography of it—had simply ceased to exist.

He had kissed her. Or she had kissed him. He honestly did not know, and the not-knowing was its own kind of torment, because if he had started it then the shame was his alone, and if she had started it then he had taken something from her that grief had loosened and morning would want back.

Her hair was loose across the blanket. In the gray pre-light she looked younger than thirty-five, younger even than twenty-five, unguarded in sleep in a way she had never once permitted herself awake. There was flour still, impossibly, under one fingernail—a baker's ghost, a residue of the life she'd built without him. He found himself memorizing it. The fingernail. The chapped lip. The small scar at her hairline from the night she'd fallen off his father's tractor at nineteen and laughed harder than she'd cried.

*Stop*, he told himself. *Inventory it and put it away.*

That was the creed. That was the discipline he had built like trellis wire around his own chest: love is trainable, containable, a vine that can be pruned back to stub every winter so it doesn't take the whole row down with it. He had run the vineyard that way for a decade. He had run himself that way. And last night, in a cold room lit by candles because the storm had eaten the power lines, the whole system had failed spectacularly, and now here he lay in the wreckage of it, cataloguing damage.

Outside, the snow had stopped. He could tell by the silence—not the muffled, pressing silence of falling snow, but the emptied-out quiet that comes after, when the world is buried and holding its breath. The window beside the couch was furred white at the edges, clear only in a small circle where the stove's residual heat had fought back. Through it: blue. Actual blue, high and hard and early, the first sky in two days that wasn't the color of surrender.

The pass would open today. Plows, maybe, by midmorning. And when the roads opened, this room would close again, permanently, and they would both walk out of it and pretend—

*Pretend what?* He didn't finish the thought. He got up instead, quietly, easing the blanket higher over her shoulder because his hands committed the betrayal before his judgment could stop them, and went to stand at the window with his arms crossed like a man guarding a border.

* * *

The guesthouse kitchen was a museum of his neglect. He found the percolator on the second shelf behind a stack of his father's old tasting notes and filled it from the jug of melted snow they'd set by the door yesterday, because the pipes were frozen solid and would stay that way until the thaw. The stove burner caught on the third try. He stood watching the flame the way he used to watch frost warnings—hypnotized by the thing that could ruin him.

Last night, in fragments, came back whether he wanted it or not. Her voice saying *I sat in that bakery for eleven years waiting for you to walk in.* His own voice, rough and unfamiliar, saying things about his father he had never said aloud to anyone—the creditors at the funeral, the ledger open on the desk, the way he'd understood at twenty-five that Thornbury wasn't a home anymore but a debt wearing the shape of one. Her hand against his jaw. The taste of salt and woodsmoke. The way she had said his name afterward—not *Gideon* the way the bank said it, not *Mr. Thornbury* the way the appraisers said it, but the low private version, *Gid*, that only she had ever used, that he had not heard since the summer they were twenty-five and had believed, stupidly, completely, that the future was a place they were already standing in.

And then she had pulled back. He remembered that too, with terrible clarity. She had pulled back maybe four inches, her forehead almost touching his, and said, "This is the storm."

Not a question. A verdict. She had handed him the excuse herself, gift-wrapped, and he had taken it the way a drowning man takes a rope, and hated himself for taking it, and said nothing.

He could have said, *No. It isn't the storm. It's ten years and I never stopped—*

He poured the coffee instead. Two cups, because his hands did it before his mind intervened, and then he stood looking at the two cups like evidence at a crime scene, and drank his own black at the counter while hers went lukewarm on the windowsill.

* * *

She woke at first light proper, the way bakers do, surfacing all at once with no groggy intermediate state. He heard the blanket shift, heard the small pause—the held breath of a person checking the geography of a room and finding it altered—and then her footsteps, bare feet on cold boards, stopping in the kitchen doorway.

"Coffee," she said. Not a question either. They were trading in statements this morning, both of them, everything flattened into the declarative so nothing could be mistaken for a feeling.

"There's a cup on the sill. It's probably cold."

"I've survived worse." She took it, wrapped both hands around it, and leaned against the doorframe opposite him, and for a moment they just looked at each other across twelve feet of linoleum and twenty years of history, and the air between them was so charged he half expected the dead bulb overhead to flicker.

"We should talk about—" he began.

"The plows," she said, at exactly the same moment, and something almost like a laugh escaped them both, brittle and startled, and broke the tension the way ice breaks—cleanly, along a line that was already there.

"You first," he said.

"The plows." She sipped the cold coffee without wincing. "Once the pass opens I need to get back to town. Rosa can't hold the bakery alone, and there's inventory going bad in the cooler, and my partners will have called approximately nine hundred times." She said it briskly, practically, ticking items off like a manifest, and he recognized the performance because he'd invented it. Defiance dressed as pragmatism. Grief filed under logistics.

"Right," he said. "Of course."

"And we should probably agree on—" she gestured vaguely with the cup, a motion that encompassed the couch, the candles, the entire catastrophic night, "—the version."

"The version."

"What we tell people. If anyone asks. Which no one will, because no one knows we were stranded together, but still. For our own—" she searched, "—housekeeping."

Housekeeping. As if the kiss were dust on a shelf.

"The version," Gideon said slowly, testing each word like a man walking onto ice he didn't trust, "where the storm made us both say more than we meant."

Something flickered across her face—there and gone, fast as a bird's shadow—and he watched her decide to let it stand. "Yes. Exactly. Cabin fever. Bad coffee. No power." She smiled, and it was the worst smile he had ever seen on her, because it was flawless, and Marisol Vega had never once in her life been flawless when it mattered; she was brilliant precisely when she was messy. This polished, reasonable, weatherproof smile was her armor, and she had put it on for him, and he understood suddenly that she was offering him the exit the same way she'd offered it last night: *this is the storm, take it, we'll both survive.*

All he had to do was nod.

He nodded.

"Good," she said softly. "Good." And she looked down into her cup, and for three full seconds—long enough for him to see it, long enough for it to burn into him forever—the mask slipped, and what was underneath wasn't regret exactly, and wasn't relief, but some raw compound of both that had no name in any language he spoke. Then it was gone, sealed over, and she was brisk again, rinsing her cup in the melted-snow water, asking where he kept the matches so she could relight the stove.

He let her have the version. He stood there and let her have it, and every cell in his body screamed at him to tear it up, and he let her have it anyway, because what right did he have to insist on the truth? He was the one who had vanished. He was the one who had chosen the ledger over her, the debts over her, the pruned and containable heart over the wild one she'd offered him at twenty-five. If she wanted to blame the weather, the weather at least was innocent. He was not.

So he hated himself quietly, thoroughly, with the practiced efficiency of a man doing a task he was good at, and carried the stove wood in from the porch, and did not say one true thing all morning.

* * *

They passed the hours until the road opened in a strange, careful choreography—two people who had kissed at midnight performing the manners of strangers. She found a broom and swept the hearth. He checked the generator and reported, uselessly, that it was flooded. She discovered his father's old record player in the back bedroom and held up a warped LP of Bolivian folk music with one raised eyebrow, and he said, "My mother's," and she said, "I know," and neither of them said anything else, but the room got warmer by several degrees that had nothing to do with the stove.

At some point she stood at the window looking out at the vineyards, white to the tree line, the rows like ribs under a blanket of snow, and said, "It looks peaceful. It always photographs peaceful. Nobody photographs the debt."

"No," he agreed. "Nobody photographs the debt."

"My mother used to say that about the bakery, too. The window display looked like a dream. Behind it was my father working eighteen-hour days and my mother counting quarters for the flour order." She paused. "I keep doing that. Building the beautiful front and letting the back room go to hell."

Gideon looked at her profile against the bright window—at the woman who had once promised to put her bread in his tasting room, whose handwriting was on the margin of his father's oldest business plan, *M.V. — pastries? ask her* —and felt the sentence rise in him again, the one they'd drafted at twenty-five on the stone bench, the one he'd rehearsed ten thousand times in the dark cab of his truck and never once delivered.

*I choose you. Not the land, not the name, not the debt. You.*

He swallowed it. Again. The muscle of containment flexing one more time, though he could feel now—had been able to feel since midnight—that the muscle was torn, that something in it had given way and would not knit back to what it was.

"Marisol," he said, and she turned, and whatever she saw in his face made her go very still.

But before he could speak—or fail to speak, which was more likely—they both heard it.

Distant. Faint. Almost subliminal under the wind. A low diesel growl, rhythmic, methodical, coming from the direction of the pass.

The plows.

Marisol closed her eyes. Opened them. "There's our ride," she said lightly, and her voice did not shake, and that was somehow the most heartbreaking thing he had witnessed in ten years.

* * *

By the time they'd gathered her things—the wet boots drying by the stove, her coat, her phone with its dead battery and its nine hundred missed calls waiting to resurrect—the sound had grown to a definite, approaching fact. Orange blade flashing far down the valley road, throwing up twin roosters of powder. Freedom, arriving on schedule, exactly when he no longer wanted it.

They stood together on the guesthouse porch in the shocking brightness, snow-blind light bouncing up off four feet of accumulation, breath steaming. The world outside had resumed. The world outside had opinions, deadlines, banks, partners, leases, appraisers—all the machinery that had been so conveniently buried for two days was grinding back to life, and it would demand they return to their corners.

"So," she said.

"So."

She stuck out her hand. Actually stuck out her hand, like a closing meeting, like a term sheet, and he looked at it and felt something crack behind his sternum, and he shook it. Her grip was warm and brief and businesslike and it was the worst handshake of his life.

"Thank you," she said. "For coming out on the road. For the stove. For—" a fractional hesitation, smoothed instantly, "—everything."

"Drive slow," he said. "The county does a miserable job on the switchbacks."

"I know. I grew up here too, remember?"

He remembered. Remembering was the one thing he had never managed to stop doing.

She walked to her car—it had been towed free of the drift that morning by nothing more heroic than Gideon and a shovel and forty minutes of profanity—and the engine, miraculously, caught on the second try. She rolled down the window against the cold, and for a moment she just looked at him across the snow, and he saw her mouth open slightly, saw the beginning of some word forming, some real word, the word, maybe—

"Gideon," she said. "The acquisition. We'll still need to talk terms."

And she drove away, taillights shrinking down the freshly cut canyon of the road, and he stood on the porch until the sound of her engine faded into the ordinary noise of the waking world, and then a while longer than that.

Inside, the guesthouse smelled like woodsmoke and cold coffee and her. On the windowsill sat the second cup, untouched, still faintly warm. He picked it up and washed it and dried it and put it back on the shelf, and told himself the tightness in his chest was just the altitude, just the shoveling, just the season.

Then his phone buzzed on the table—power restored somewhere up the line, signal crawling back into the valley. One message. The bank. An appraiser had been scheduled. Thursday. To assess the property for *alternative disposition*, the email said, including, in a phrase that landed like a fist, *demolition evaluation of the guesthouse structure.*

Gideon read it twice, standing in the room where he had finally, catastrophically, felt something.

Thursday. Three days. And somewhere down that road, in a bakery with thirty days left on its lease, the woman who had just shaken his hand goodbye was about to learn that her firm was the fallback buyer—and that saving herself meant razing the only place he had ever come back to life.

He set the phone face-down on the table, and began, with steady hands, to pack up the candles.
