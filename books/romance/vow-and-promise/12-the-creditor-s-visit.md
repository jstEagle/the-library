## 12. The Creditor's Visit

The thaw had come in the night like a thief with soft hands, and by morning the vineyard wept from every row. Meltwater ran the furrows silver, dripped from the bare cordons of the vines in a rhythm so steady it might have been a clock counting down something. Gideon stood at the kitchen window of the farmhouse with his coffee going cold and watched the water take the snow apart, and he thought: *this is what a debt looks like when it starts to move.*

He hadn't slept. He'd sat up most of the night with the ledger open to a page he no longer needed to read—the numbers were carved into him now, grooves worn deep as the tractor ruts in the east field—and he'd found himself instead doing arithmetic of an entirely different kind. Counting hours. Counting the distance between the guesthouse couch and where her hand had rested, six inches of old upholstery that he could still feel against his own skin like a brand. Counting the seconds between her mouth and his, and then the longer count, the one that hurt worse: three days since she'd driven out through the melting slush without looking back, and not one word.

*Weather-induced,* she'd said, smoothing her coat, gathering her bag, her voice pitched in that bright administrative register she used for strangers and suppliers. *Storms make people strange.* And he had let her say it. Had nodded like a man agreeing to terms. Had walked her to her car through a world dissolving into water and said, *Drive safe,* as if safety were the thing either of them wanted.

He drank the cold coffee anyway. It tasted like punishment, which was appropriate.

The appraiser was due at nine.

* * *

Dale Pruitt arrived in a bank-issued sedan so clean it looked embarrassed to be on the gravel drive, and he got out wearing a quilted vest over a button-down and the particular smile of a man who has delivered bad news so many times it has stopped tasting like anything. Gideon had known him ten years—known him back when Dale was a junior loan officer who still flinched when he had to talk about foreclosure, back when the flinching meant something human was underneath.

"Gideon." They shook hands. Dale's grip was warm and dry and brief. "Appreciate you making time."

"You gave me little choice."

"I did give you thirty days' notice." Dale said it gently, which somehow made it worse. "That's more than most get."

They walked the property because that was what appraisers did—they walked, they photographed, they murmured into their phones about square footage and soil samples and comparable sales, translating a living place into a dead man's spreadsheet. Gideon led him past the crush pad, past the barrel room with its smell of oak and damp stone, past the rows of Chardonnay climbing the south slope, their buds still tight and green-furred with promise, and Dale nodded along and took pictures of everything except the things that mattered, because cameras couldn't see those.

It was at the guesthouse that Dale stopped walking.

She stood at the end of the lane the way she always had—small and stubborn under its sagging porch roof, its gray shingles furred with moss, the wisteria Gideon's grandmother had planted still strangling the trellis even though nobody had pruned it in years. The storm had shrugged a last skin of ice off her eaves; the meltwater dripped off the guttering in bright beads. Through the window Gideon could see the great room, the stone hearth, the long couch where—

He made himself look away.

"This is the structure flagged for phase one," Dale said, and there it was: the clipboard coming up, the pen clicking, the voice sliding into its procedural register. "Demolition schedule has it coming down first if the sale closes. It's non-load-bearing history, Gideon. I'm sorry. It's just a building."

"Just a building," Gideon repeated.

"It's got foundation rot. Knob-and-tube wiring. A chimney that's one good earthquake from eating itself." Dale flipped a page. "The remediation costs alone would eat any renovation budget alive. Whoever buys this parcel is going to want it gone inside the month. That's standard."

"Whoever," Gideon said. "You keep saying whoever. Say the name, Dale. You've known the name for two weeks. I can see it sitting behind your teeth."

Dale looked at the guesthouse, then at Gideon, and something old and uncomfortable moved across his face—the junior loan officer's flinch, surfacing one last time before it drowned.

"Vega Family Holdings," he said. "Marisol Vega's firm is the fallback buyer. Contingent offer, already drafted. Full asking on the land parcel, contingent on demolition of all secondary structures within sixty days of close." He hesitated. "Gideon, I assumed you knew. She was here during the storm, wasn't she? I figured she'd have told you herself."

The meltwater dripped. Somewhere down the slope a crow said something ugly and flew off.

"She didn't mention it," Gideon heard himself say, and his voice came out level, courteous, bank-managerial, and he marveled distantly at the machinery of it, how a man could be coming apart inside and sound like a voicemail greeting.

*All secondary structures.* He turned and looked at the guesthouse properly, the way an appraiser would, the way a stranger would—and hated that he could see it. The lean of the porch posts. The shingles curled like old paper. The chimney leaning a degree or two east, toward the morning light, as if craning to watch the road for someone coming back.

Sixty days. She'd have signed before the roads even cleared. While he was sleeping six feet away from her on a couch, memorizing the shape of her face in firelight, she had already—what? Signed? Drafted? Let her partners draft it while she sat with her hands around a candle and told him about her mother's kitchen?

Or maybe she hadn't signed anything yet. Maybe there was still a version of this where—

He shut that door. Locked it. Put the ledger of small debts on top.

"When does the contingency expire?" he asked.

"End of the month." Dale clicked his pen closed, mercifully done pretending to write. "Look, Gideon—I'll say this off the record, and then I'll deny I said it. You've got a counteroffer window until Friday. If you've got anything left to fight with, fight with it now. After that, the file goes upstairs and it stops being mine and it stops being yours."

* * *

He didn't go inside the guesthouse. He told himself it was because Dale was waiting by the sedan, but Dale waited a long time, and Gideon stood in the mud of the lane with the melt running over his boots, and the door stayed shut.

Because here was the thing he could not stop doing, the thing that had colonized every hour since the plows came through: he kept rebuilding that night. Kept re-running it like a film he couldn't return. Her hands around the candle flame. The way she'd said *my mother's kitchen* and her voice had cracked down the middle like river ice. The kiss—or the almost-kiss, or whatever it had been, the thing they'd both agreed afterward not to name, weather-induced, storms make people strange—and beneath all of it, humming like power lines, the sentence. *Their* sentence. The one they'd drafted at twenty-five on this exact patch of ground, drunk on young wine and older hope, laughing, rehearsing, promising to say it someday when it was true.

And now he learned that while she sat in that firelight letting him believe in someday, her firm had a demolition order drafted with her name on it. For this house. For the house where they'd rehearsed it.

*Betrayal,* said the cold, orderly part of him, the part that kept the ledger. *File it under betrayal.*

But the word wouldn't sit straight in the column. It kept sliding sideways. Because he knew Marisol Vega—he had known her at nineteen and twenty-five and, God help him, he had started knowing her again three nights ago—and the woman who cried over her mother's recipe box did not raze houses for margin. The woman who'd stood on the frozen bench-stone and dared him to remember did not do it for margin.

So which was it? Was the fallback buyer ambition wearing her name, partners pushing paper past her while she fought for her bakery? Or was it heartbreak wearing ambition—a woman deciding that if she couldn't have the future, she'd at least control the rubble?

He couldn't tell. That was the unbearable part. Ten years ago he'd have read her face like a vineyard sky; now he stood in the mud and realized he could no longer distinguish her ambition from her grief, or his own love from his own pride, or the truth from the story he needed to survive the week. Every feeling he had was a number in a ledger he'd cooked himself. He had spent a decade training his heart to stay contained, pruned, productive—and now, when he needed it most, it gave him nothing he could trust. A muscle trained too well. A vine pruned back so hard it forgot how to fruit.

Dale honked, once, apologetic. Gideon raised a hand without turning around.

* * *

In the farmhouse office, he pulled the file the bank had couriered over—thick, tabbed, smelling of toner—and found the fallback offer on page forty-one. Vega Family Holdings letterhead. Her signature block was empty. That was the first thing he checked, pathetically, hungrily: the line where her name would go, blank as fresh snow.

Below it, the terms. Below the terms, Exhibit C: *Schedule of Structures for Demolition.* Secondary residence (guesthouse), listed first. Then the old equipment barn. Then the well house.

He sat with the page a long time. Outside, the melt kept ticking off the eaves, patient as interest accruing.

There was a phone in his hand. There had been a phone in his hand for three days, and every morning he composed a message and every evening he deleted it, because what was there to say that wasn't either surrender or accusation, and he was too proud for the first and too much of a coward for the second. But now the accusation had a document attached. Now he had something he could hold up like evidence, and the temptation to use it—to call her, to say *I know what your firm drafted, I know what you'd tear down*—rose in him hot and ugly, and he recognized it for what it was: not righteousness. Fear. Fear dressed in a suit, carrying a clipboard.

Because if he called her angry, he'd never learn which version was true. Anger would make the choice for both of them. Anger would demolish the last secondary structure standing—the one neither bank nor appraiser had thought to list.

He set the phone face-down on the desk.

Then he opened the ledger—not the bank's, his own, the one with the sins in it—and turned past the columns of red to the blank pages at the back, and he wrote, in the careful hand he used for anything he couldn't afford to get wrong:

*Friday. Counteroffer window closes.*

Underneath, after a moment:

*Find out what the heritage block is worth before they find out you know.*

His foreman Tomás had said something, weeks ago, that he'd filed and forgotten—the old Pinot Noir on the north slope, the pre-phylloxera rootstock his grandfather had smuggled cuttings for, the block everyone wrote off as dead. *Dead-looking isn't dead,* Tomás had said, spitting in the mud. *You'd know that if you ever went up there anymore.* Gideon had laughed it off then. Tonight it sounded less like viticulture and more like prophecy.

If there was anything living on this land worth fighting for—vine or otherwise—Friday was the deadline for saying so out loud.

He looked at the guesthouse through the window, gray against the wet gold of late afternoon, dripping, leaning, waiting. *Our* guesthouse, some traitorous voice insisted, and he let it, just once, just briefly, the way a man lets himself look at the sun.

Then he picked up the phone after all—but not to call her. He called Tomás, and asked him to meet at the north slope at first light, and hung up before he could hear how hopeful his own voice sounded.

Somewhere in town, a signature line waited, blank as snow. Two days to find out whether she intended to fill it—or whether he'd have to stand in front of her and ask.
