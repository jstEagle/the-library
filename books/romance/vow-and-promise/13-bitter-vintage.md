## 13. Bitter Vintage

### Gideon

The tasting room had never looked like this before. Gideon had spent the morning making sure of it — the oak wiped down, the crystal polished, the fire in the great hearth built high enough to throw light across the long table like something out of a painting. He told himself it was strategy. You set a beautiful stage when you intended to win the scene. You poured the good wine when you wanted the other side off balance.

He did not tell himself the truth, which was that he had changed his shirt three times and settled on the gray one because Marisol had once said gray made him look like a man who could be trusted, and he wanted her to remember, in some small animal part of her, that she had said that. That she had laughed when she said it, standing in this same room with her hair down and her guard down and the summer light coming gold through these same windows.

The papers were squared on the table. Purchase agreement, appraisal summary, the demolition schedule for the guesthouse printed on the third page in the bank's sterile font. He had read it so many times the words had worn smooth as river stones. *Structure deemed unsalvageable. Recommend full removal to prepare site for commercial development.*

Unsalvageable. He turned his glass by its stem. The last of the '09, the vintage they'd made the summer everything was still possible, the summer he'd carried her over the threshold of that unsalvageable guesthouse with her shoes in one hand and a bottle in the other and a sentence in his mouth that he'd swallowed and swallowed until it dissolved into nothing.

The door opened at four o'clock exactly, because of course it did. Marisol Vega had never once been late, not to a shift at her mother's bakery, not to a harvest, not to a fight.

She came in shaking snow off her coat, and the room rearranged itself around her the way it always had, the way a vineyard rearranges itself around weather. She'd dressed like armor — charcoal suit, hair pinned back, a leather folio under her arm that probably contained every argument she'd rehearsed on the drive up. Her eyes found his and held, level and cool, and Gideon thought: *there she is.* The woman who had walked out of his life ten years ago with her chin up and her hands empty.

"Mr. Thornbury." She set the folio on the table. "Shall we?"

"Ms. Vega. Wine?"

"I'm working."

"So am I." He poured two glasses anyway, because the ritual was the point, and pushed one across the oak. "The '09. Last case. Figured we ought to drink it before someone bulldozes the cellar it came from."

Her jaw tightened, a small movement, quickly controlled. "That's not the plan. The plan is the guesthouse."

"The plan is a parking structure and a gift shop," he said. "I've seen your partners' renderings. There's a fountain shaped like a grape cluster."

"There's a *revenue stream* shaped like a grape cluster," she said, and sat, and opened her folio, and for the next forty minutes they were excellent.

That was the thing nobody warned you about a war between two people who knew each other's language. They were fluent. She cited soil assays; he countered with water rights. She laid out the winery's capital commitment; he laid out the bank's patience, which had none. They traded numbers like fencers, precise, elegant, and underneath it all the fire cracked and popped and the snow came down past the windows in fat silent flakes, and Gideon was aware — with a clarity that felt like a knife between his ribs — that he had never in his life been this alive in a business meeting.

It was going well. That was the trap. They were going to land somewhere in the middle, a price she could defend to her partners, terms he could limp away with, and the guesthouse would come down in April and the bench would come with it and the sentence would die unspoken forever, and they would both call it a fair deal.

Then she said, "The appraisal on the residence is generous. You should take it."

And something in him — the thing he'd spent ten years pruning, the thing that grew back wilder every season — snapped its trellis.

"Generous," he repeated.

"Gideon—"

"Ms. Vega." He stood. The chair scraped. "Say the number. Say the number you've all agreed my family's land is worth, the land my grandfather cleared with his hands, and then say the word *generous* in the same breath. Go on. I'd love to hear how that sounds in your mouth."

"It sounds like arithmetic," she said, standing too, and her coolness cracked down the middle, and there she was underneath, the girl from the storm, the girl by the fire, furious and bright. "Arithmetic is what keeps people fed. Arithmetic kept my mother's ovens lit for thirty years. You want to talk about what things are *worth*? You inherited a working estate and ran it into the ground—"

"I inherited its *debts*—"

"—and now you're going to stand there and lecture *me* about sentiment? You, who won't even say why you—"

She stopped. He watched her stop. Ten years of rehearsal, and they were both fluent in the same unfinished sentence.

"Why I what?" he said quietly. It was cruel and he knew it. "Finish it. You've had a decade."

Her hands flattened on the table. "Don't."

"You walked into my house with a demolition schedule for the room where I—" He heard himself going too far and went there anyway, because the wine was in him and the fire was in him and the wound was in him and all of it wanted air. "Where *we*—"

"Don't you dare." Her voice shook. "Don't you dare stand in front of that fireplace and invoke that summer like you have any right to it. You weren't there when it mattered. You were never there when it mattered. You let me drive away, Gideon. You stood in your driveway and you let me drive away, and I waited three days for a phone call that never came, and then I waited three years, and then I stopped counting."

"I had reasons."

"You had *excuses* dressed up as reasons. Your father's debts, your father's pride, your father's name — I heard it all secondhand, from everyone but you. You know what I think?" She picked up her glass and didn't drink, just held it like a weapon she'd decided against. "I think you loved the vineyard because it couldn't leave you. I think you've been hiding behind this land your whole life. Every bill, every lien, every bad season — you hold them up like a shield and say, *I can't, I have obligations,* and everyone nods, because debt is respectable. Debt is tragic. Debt means you never have to choose anything."

The fire popped. Snow ticked against the glass.

"And what were you doing," Gideon said, "for the last month?"

She went still.

"Because from where I stand," he said, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "you drove up that road in the first snowstorm in twenty years, you sat in my guesthouse, you drank my wine, you slept under my roof — and the whole time, the entire time, you were the acquisition. Your partners' signature on my ruin. Tell me, Marisol — was the kiss in the plan? Was that in the term sheet? Or did you improvise?"

The silence that followed had a texture. He felt it settle on his skin like frost.

Her eyes were wet and she would have died before letting a single drop fall, and he watched her win that fight, watched her swallow it whole, and he understood in one sickening instant what he had just done — that he had taken the truest night of his last ten years and priced it, itemized it, entered it into the ledger as a liability on her side of the column.

"You know what the worst part is?" she said. Her voice was almost steady. Almost. "I came here today ready to save something for you. There's a clause in my folio — a preservation rider. The guesthouse could stay. I fought my partners for it. I fought them for *you*, you impossible, cowardly man, and I wasn't even going to tell you, I was going to let you think you'd won it yourself, because God knows your pride needs the fiction." She closed the folio. "But you'd rather have the wound than the cure. You'd rather be betrayed than loved, because betrayal you understand. Betrayal fits the story."

"Marisol—"

"Keep the land." She pulled on her coat with sharp, economical movements. "Keep the debts. Keep every shield you own. You're right about one thing — I was using this place. I was using it to find out if there was anything left of the man I loved, and now I have my answer, and it appraised low."

The door slammed behind her with a sound like a frost-crack running through stone.

### Marisol

The road down from the vineyard was white and getting whiter, and Marisol drove it too fast because driving too fast was the only honest thing she'd done all day.

Her hands were shaking on the wheel. She noticed it the way you notice a stain on a blouse you can't change — with irritation, with the useless wish that her body would simply cooperate with the story she was telling about herself. The story was: *I am fine. I am a professional. I lost nothing today except a negotiation, and negotiations are weather, and weather passes.*

The story was a lie with good posture.

He had called her an acquisition. The words were still in her, lodged like a splinter, and every heartbeat worked them deeper. *Was the kiss in the plan?* She could have thrown the folio at him. She could have said, *the kiss was the only unpriced thing in that room, the only thing I didn't calculate, the one moment in ten years when I stopped being a Vega and a Vega-Vintners partner and a debtor and a daughter and just was a woman kissing a man in a snowstorm* — but saying that would have been handing him the knife handle-first, and she had already handed him enough.

The preservation rider. God. She'd fought for it for two weeks. She'd sat in a glass conference room in San Francisco across from men who called the guesthouse "the heritage liability" and she'd said, with a straight face and a white-knuckled pen, *we keep it, we restore it, we make it the tasting room's heart,* and she had not said the rest, the true rest, which was: *because a man I love once carried me over its threshold, and I need to know it's still standing somewhere in the world even if I'm not.*

And he'd stood there in his gray shirt and accused her of running a con.

She took the curve at the overlook too fast and the rear wheels slid and her stomach dropped and then the tires caught, and she pulled over, heart hammering, engine idling, snow coming down through the headlights like static. Below her, the valley lights of town were coming on one by one, small and gold, and somewhere down there was the bakery with its lease expiring in eleven days and its ovens cold since Tuesday because she couldn't afford the gas bill and the pride of lighting them for no one.

Her phone buzzed in the passenger seat. She looked at it the way you look at a bill you already know the amount of.

**DANIEL (Vega-Vintners):** Rider's off the table after today's call. Board wants signature by Friday or the deal reverts and your stake goes with it. Sorry, Sol. Business.

*Your stake goes with it.* Eleven years of work. Her mother's name on the letterhead. Everything she'd built after she lost everything else, built specifically so she would never again stand in a doorway waiting for a man to choose her.

And the price of keeping it was Gideon Thornbury's signature on the demolition of the only place she'd ever been happy.

She laughed — one short, ugly sound, swallowed by the car. Of course. Of course it came to this. The universe had always had a taste for symmetry; she should have known it would write her a contract where every line item was a wound. Sign, and lose him forever, with paperwork to prove it. Refuse, and lose herself, the self she'd built out of the wreckage he'd left, the competent, armored, arithmetic woman who kept people fed.

The snow thickened. The wipers couldn't keep up. She thought about the storm, the guesthouse, the firelight on his face when he'd finally, finally cracked open — *my father signed everything away rather than ask for help, and I learned the lesson too well* — and she thought about how she'd fallen asleep believing, for one stupid warm hour, that the worst was behind them, that the thaw had started.

Thaw. The roads would freeze again tonight. Everything would freeze again tonight.

Her phone screen glowed in the dark car, Daniel's message still open, the cursor blinking after it like a small patient heart.

Friday. She had until Friday.

Marisol put the car in gear and drove down toward the lights, and behind her the vineyard vanished into the snowfall, and she did not look in the rearview mirror, because she knew — with the certainty of a woman who has just priced her own heart and found the appraisal low — that if she looked, she would turn around.

And she had a signature to refuse.
