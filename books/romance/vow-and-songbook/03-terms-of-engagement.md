## 3. Terms of Engagement

The voicemail sat in my phone for three days before I played it, which was two days longer than I could afford.

I knew it was him before he spoke. August Dravenmoor left messages the way he wrote them — precise, unhurried, each sentence built like furniture that would outlast the person who bought it. *Wren. It's August. I have a proposition that will sound insane and isn't. Call me when you've decided to be reasonable.* A pause, and then, quieter, almost swallowed: *Please.*

That *please* was what kept me from deleting it. Ghostwriters don't say please. They say *per my last email* and *the client has notes* and *we'll need to revise the mother's death scene; it reads as too hopeful.* I'd spent enough evenings in his studio-adjacent orbit — delivering session tracks for the audiobook productions his publishing house farmed out, watching him through glass while he turned other people's grief into clean copy — to know that August Dravenmoor treated vulnerability like a typo. You caught it before anyone else saw it.

So I let the message sit. I watered my plants. I practiced a Bach cello transcription until my fingers ached in the good way, the way that meant I wasn't thinking about money, which meant I was thinking about money, because the only time I didn't think about money was when I was playing hard enough to drown it out.

The numbers were these: four months behind on the studio where I rented my rehearsal room, a cello with a hairline crack in the table that luthiers quoted me figures to even *look* at, and an agent named Dev who called every Tuesday to say some version of *it's not the writing, Wren, it's the market,* which is what agents say when they mean *no one wants your truth, they want someone else's truth wearing your truth's clothes.*

The manuscript lived in a folder on my laptop called SONGBOOK, which was a lie of a name, or maybe the truest one. Two hundred and six pages about the year my family ate its own tail to survive. About my father's business partner, and the signature on a document my father swore he never read, and the way my mother set two extra places at the table for a year after we lost everything, as if hunger were a guest you could shame by feeding it politely. I wrote it under no name at all. Anonymous the way a wound is anonymous — it doesn't need credit. It needs air.

Dev had read it twice. The second time she'd called me at eleven at night and said, gently, the way you speak to someone standing on a ledge, "It's brilliant and it's dead. No house will touch a memoir where the author won't stand behind it. Anonymity reads as cowardice in this market, fair or not." And then, after a silence: "Unless something makes it news."

I hadn't told her that I'd already tried to make it news once, three years ago, and had gotten as far as a lawyer's office before my mother's voice on the phone — *Wren, please, your father's heart* — sent me back into the folder, back into the dark, back into playing other people's music beautifully and saying nothing.

Which is why, on the fourth day, I put on my least desperate coat and went to hear August's insane-not-insane proposition in person. Desperation should always arrive dressed as indifference. It's the first rule of negotiation, and I learned it from musicians, who will play a funeral for free but charge double for weddings, because grief is honest and joy has to be manufactured.

* * *

He chose a café near the river, the kind with exposed brick and a piano nobody played, pushed against the wall like a retired workhorse. Of course he did. August collected rooms with instruments in them the way other people collected grievances.

He stood when I came in, which surprised me. He was taller than I remembered, or maybe I'd spent three years remembering him smaller, safer. Gray wool coat, dark hair pushed back like he'd argued with it once and conceded. He had the particular pallor of a man who worked indoors with other people's lives and forgot to have one.

"Solano." He said my name like a chord he was testing.

"Dravenmoor." I sat. "You look like a man about to ask for something."

"I am." He slid a cup across the table — Earl Grey, milk on the side, exactly how I took it at sessions, a detail I didn't know he'd noticed and hated that he had. "First, the debt. I covered your studio arrears two years ago. Eleven hundred dollars. I'm not going to pretend I don't remember the number, because pretending is exhausting and you'd see through it anyway."

"You're right. I would."

"I'm not calling it in." He said it fast, like ripping off tape. "That's not what this is. What this is—" He stopped. Restarted. I watched a man who made sentences for a living fail to build one. "My family requires that I bring a partner to a reunion. A reunion that includes a vote regarding the disposition of Dravenmoor House. My brother Julian has engineered the terms such that stability — demonstrated, visible, domesticated stability — weighs in the outcome. I need someone at my side for one weekend who can convince five suspicious relatives that I am a man capable of being loved."

The piano sat silent behind him. Somewhere near the espresso machine, steam screamed briefly and was silenced.

"And you thought of me," I said, "because?"

"Because you're the best liar I know," he said, and then winced at himself. "No. Because you're the best *performer* I know. There's a difference, and I suspect you know exactly what it is."

There it was. The thing about August — and I'd catalogued this across a dozen professional encounters, filed it away like a dangerous document — was that he saw people accurately and then pretended he hadn't. It was his whole method. Ghostwriting is just empathy with the serial numbers filed off. He watched you the way I watched conductors' hands, and then he used what he saw, and then he acted like the seeing had been nothing, a trick of the light.

"Two years ago," I said, "you paid my studio debt without telling me. I found out from the landlord, who found out from a receipt. Now you want me to spend a weekend being looked at by your family. Looked at *closely.* Families don't glance, August. They audit."

"Yes."

"That's not a question. That's just agreement."

"I'm agreeing with you." He leaned forward, and the light caught the tiredness under his eyes, the kind sleep doesn't fix. "Wren, I'll pay you. Properly. Whatever a weekend of session work costs, tripled, plus—"

"No."

He blinked. It was the first genuinely unguarded expression I'd seen on him, and I understood suddenly why he guarded everything else. Unguarded, August Dravenmoor looked young. Unguarded, he looked like someone's brother. Someone's son. Someone who had once been a boy in a big cold house learning that feelings were liabilities.

"No?" he repeated.

"Not tripled. Not *plus.*" I wrapped my hands around the tea he'd ordered correctly and hated myself for how much that small correctness undid me. "Here are my terms. One: flat fee, the number I name, non-negotiable, paid half up front. Two: I am not your employee for the weekend. I'm your partner. Partners don't get stage-managed. If I decide to tell your mother I play cello because I couldn't afford law school, that's the story, and you back me."

"Your mother would believe that."

"My mother believes whatever hurts least. That's term three, actually. Term three: whatever happens between us this weekend stays in past tense afterward. We perform, we collect, we return to our respective debts. No follow-up calls. No *that meant something.* I've watched you delete yourself out of other people's books, August. I know how cleanly you cut. I want that same courtesy."

Something moved behind his face — too quick to name, gone before it finished arriving. He looked at the piano like it might offer him a line.

"Past tense," he said finally. "Agreed."

"Term four." I hadn't planned term four. It arrived in my mouth fully formed, the way melodies do when you've been circling them for years. "You don't ask what I do with the money."

His eyes came up sharp. For a moment I thought he'd push — he was a man who negotiated for a living, who knew that unexplained clauses hide bodies — but instead he studied me with that terrible accurate attention, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly.

"All right," he said. "Counter-term. One counter-term."

"Go."

"When we're there — when we're performing — you don't perform *at* me. Everyone else in that house gets the show. I get the true version, or at minimum, the version you'd give someone you didn't owe anything to." He paused. "You owed me a favor. This clears it. But the favor was never the point. The point is I trust you more than I trust anyone I share blood with, and I find that professionally humiliating, so if we could agree never to discuss it again—"

"Denied," I said. "We're discussing it constantly. Internally. Forever."

And there — God help me — there was the almost-smile. Not the polished one he wore in meetings, the one calibrated to reassure clients. A real one, crooked, startled out of him like a bird flushed from cover. It lasted maybe a second and a half, and I felt it land somewhere beneath my sternum with the specific weight of a note held past its written value.

*Guarded*, I told myself. *Stay guarded. This is a transaction. He's buying a performance and you're selling one, and the manuscript in your bag has nothing to do with any of it.*

Because here was the thing I did not say, the thing I pressed down flat while we shook hands over the table like conspirators, his hand warm and dry and holding on a half-second past professional: the folder on my laptop was open behind my eyes the entire time. Two hundred and six pages about families that survive by betrayal. And I had just agreed to walk into a house where a family was preparing to do exactly that to each other, armed with nothing but a false story and a true one I couldn't tell.

Dev's words came back to me over the rim of the teacup: *unless something makes it news.*

A Dravenmoor reunion. A vote. A house full of people who'd spent generations perfecting the art of the beautiful lie. If I survived that weekend with my eyes open, I wouldn't just be earning a fee. I'd be doing research no journalist could buy.

I told myself that was all it was. Research. Opportunity. The universe's crude sense of humor.

August walked me out. At the door, he hesitated, hands in his coat pockets, and said, "Friday. I'll send the address. Pack for weather — the house holds cold the way some people hold grudges."

"Poetic," I said. "You should write."

"I ghost," he said, and the joke landed wrong in both our mouths, and neither of us laughed, and the silence stretched one beat too long to be professional.

Then he touched the brim of an imaginary hat — an absurd, boyish gesture I'd never have predicted from him — and walked toward the river, coat moving around him like something unfinished.

I stood in the doorway a moment, watching him go, my phone already buzzing in my pocket. Dev. Tuesday was three days away, but Dev called early when she smelled blood or money, and I had a feeling she'd bring news of one or the other.

I answered on the fourth ring, still watching August's gray figure shrink along the waterfront.

"Wren," she said, and her voice had the careful brightness she used before bad news. "You need to sit down. I just got a call from Halloway & Finch."

Halloway & Finch. August's publisher. His client's house. The name landed in my chest like a dropped tuning fork, humming a frequency I couldn't yet name.

"What about?" I asked, though some cold, clear part of me — the part that hears the key change before the modulation arrives — was already beginning to understand.
