## 5. Duet for One Weekend

The dining room at Dravenmoor House was built to make people feel small, and it succeeded without even trying.

Wren counted the ways between courses: the ceiling painted with clouds that had never existed over this particular valley, the table long enough to seat twenty though only seven chairs were filled, the candles — real beeswax, not electric — flickering in silver sticks that must have been older than the republic. Mrs. Dravenmoor sat at the head like a portrait that had learned to breathe. Julian occupied her right hand and spent the meal conducting a quiet campaign of questions disguised as charm.

"And music, Wren. Is that a vocation or a hobby?" He said *hobby* the way other men said *mistress*.

"Vocation," she said. "Hobbies are things you can quit."

August, seated beside her, went very still in the way she was beginning to recognize as his version of laughter.

She'd been at the house for six hours and had already been measured twice — once by the housekeeper's eyes as they followed her through the hall, once by August's mother, who had taken Wren's hand at the door and held it a half-second too long, reading her pulse like a jeweler assessing a stone for flaws. *So you're the one August brought.* Not *girlfriend*. Not *partner*. *The one he brought*, as if Wren were luggage with opinions.

The soup was cold cucumber, the fish was turbot, the wine kept arriving from bottles whose labels meant nothing to her and everything to everyone else. She catalogued it all the way she catalogued everything: for later, for material, for the manuscript she told herself she wasn't writing anymore because no one wanted it. A family performing itself for an audience of itself. The mother's laugh arriving exactly on time, like a metronome. Julian leaning back when he scored a point. The empty chair at the far end that nobody looked at but everybody avoided looking away from.

*Whose chair?* she'd asked August on the drive up. He'd watched the road and said, "My sister's," and then said nothing else for eleven miles.

Now dessert came — some pale confection involving poached pears — and Mrs. Dravenmoor set down her spoon with the small decisive click of a woman beginning a maneuver she'd planned since morning.

"Wren. August tells us you play."

"August tells you correctly."

"The piano is in the music room. It would be lovely to hear something. We so rarely have anyone who plays anymore." A pause, delicate as lace. "Since Iris."

The name dropped into the room like a stone into still water. Julian studied his pear. An aunt whose name Wren hadn't caught examined her napkin. And August — August put his hand briefly under the table, found Wren's knee, and pressed once: *you don't have to.*

Which was, of course, the exact thing that made her want to.

"I'd love to," Wren said.

* * *

The music room sat at the back of the house, past the conservatory where the last light was failing through wet green glass. Someone had opened the windows earlier; the air smelled of rain coming and old roses giving up. The piano stood against the interior wall, and Wren knew before she touched it what it would be — you could tell from across a room, the way certain instruments carried themselves, the way certain people did.

It was an upright, ancient, its walnut case gone soft-edged with a century of hands. Sheet music stood open on the rack, yellowed, in a child's careful pencil-annotated hand. *Iris*, someone had written inside the front cover of one book, along with a date fourteen years old and a small drawing of a bird that couldn't decide whether it was flying or falling.

Wren sat down. The bench creaked. Behind her, the family arranged itself in the doorway and along the wall — Mrs. Dravenmoor in the best chair, Julian standing with his arms crossed like a man auditing a transaction, August half in shadow near the window with a glass of something he wasn't drinking.

They expected a performance. That was the trap of rooms like this: they handed you the stage and then watched to see whether you'd trip on it.

So Wren didn't give them a performance. She gave them the truth instead, or a piece of it — the piece that belonged to no one here. She played the first thing her hands found, which was her own: a slow figure in D minor she'd written the winter her father's business died, four bars of melody that circled and circled like a dog looking for a door that used to be there. She'd played it in hotel lobbies and subway stations and once, memorably, at a wedding where the bride cried for reasons the groom never understood. It was a small piece. It was also, she knew, a loaded gun handled casually — because it was true, and true things in rooms full of liars make a specific kind of silence.

The piano helped her. That was the surprise. She'd expected the dead keys and sour unisons of an instrument nobody loved anymore, but someone had kept it tuned — recently, lovingly, against all reason — and the middle register rang out round and warm as a struck bell. The bass had a growl in it. The top notes thinned to almost nothing, like breath at the end of a long sentence.

She closed her eyes somewhere in the second pass of the melody. This was the part audiences never got, the part she'd never explain to Julian or his mother or any of them: playing wasn't performing. Playing was the only hour of the day when she stopped translating herself into something acceptable. Her hands knew things her mouth wouldn't say. Her hands said: *we lost the house too. We lost it differently, but we lost it.* Her hands said: *my father signed papers he didn't read because reading them would have meant admitting what his partner had done, and I watched him do it, and I have never decided whether that was weakness or mercy.* Her hands said all of it in D minor while her face stayed composed and the family of Dravenmoor listened to a stranger's grief and mistook it for entertainment.

When she finished, the silence had a texture. Not applause-silence. The other kind — the kind where people are suddenly thinking about their own houses, their own fathers, their own unsigned papers.

Then August crossed the room.

She heard him before she saw him — the deliberate footsteps, the glass being set down on the mantel with a click. And then the bench shifted beside her, taking half her seat, his shoulder warm against hers, and his hands came down onto the keys above where hers rested, and Wren's entire nervous system said: *abort.*

Because she knew those hands. Not intimately — not yet, not really — but professionally, the way you know a voice from a voicemail. August Dravenmoor had ghostwritten three memoirs before she ever met him, and one of them had contained a passage about a childhood piano teacher that Wren had read eleven times trying to figure out how a ghost could write something that tender. Now here were the hands that had typed it, settling into position above the keys like they'd never left.

He didn't ask what she'd played. He didn't ask permission. He simply picked up her melody — her four circling bars, her private winter — and answered it.

That was the only word for it. *Answered.* Where her phrase fell, his rose. Where hers circled, his opened a door she hadn't known was in the wall. He played in the upper register where the piano went thin and breathless, and he played quietly, the way you speak in a sickroom, and the harmony he laid under her melody turned it inside out — took her grief and showed her the shape of it from the outside, which was somehow worse and better at once.

Her hands, traitors to the last, adjusted to fit him. She dropped into the lower voice, gave him the melody, took the bass, felt the whole piece reorganize itself around the fact of two people instead of one. It shouldn't have worked. They'd never played together. They'd barely spoken honestly. But music is just listening fast, and August Dravenmoor listened like a man dying of thirst, and every place she left space he filled it exactly, and every place he hesitated she caught him, and by the third pass through the melody they weren't taking turns anymore — they were arguing, gently, musically, her minor insisting, his major refusing, until somewhere in the middle the two keys stopped fighting and became a single thing that was neither sad nor happy but *true*, which is rarer than either.

The room went quiet.

Not polite-quiet. Not wealthy-family-watching-quiet. Wren felt it happen behind her the way you feel weather change — the aunt setting down her coffee untasted, Julian's arms uncrossing slowly, Mrs. Dravenmoor going motionless in her chair with an expression Wren couldn't see but could feel, the way you feel a stare between your shoulder blades. Even the house seemed to lean in. Old houses do that. They hold their breath around the sounds they were built for.

And August — August had closed his eyes.

She saw it in the dark window's reflection, his face lit gold by the lamp: eyes shut, jaw slack, all the careful architecture of him temporarily down. Ten years of professional composure, of the flat pleasant voice and the handshake smile, and it had all come off in four bars. He played like a man confessing. He played like someone who had been holding a note for a decade and finally, accidentally, let it sound.

*Iris taught him,* Wren thought, with sudden certainty. *This piano. This room. She taught him and then she died and he never touched it again, and now he's touching it because I started something he couldn't bear to leave unanswered.*

The piece ended the way tides go out — not stopping, just receding, the last chord decaying into the smell of rain and roses until none of them could say exactly when the music had finished and the silence begun.

Nobody clapped. Thank God, nobody clapped. Clapping would have killed it, would have converted the moment back into currency, performance and payment, the economy they'd all been trading in all evening. Instead Mrs. Dravenmoor said, very quietly, "That was Iris's key," and stood, and left the room, and no one commented on the fact that she'd left, and the candle flames straightened in the draft of the closing door.

Julian recovered first, because men like Julian always recover first. "Well," he said, with a smile recalibrated to charming. "You two have been practicing."

"We've never played together before tonight," Wren said.

Something flickered across his face — the auditor catching a number that doesn't add up. "Never?"

"Never." She turned on the bench, finally, and looked at him directly, because she'd learned years ago that the only defense against men like Julian was to be more comfortable with the truth than they were. "Your brother hired me to attend this weekend. You probably already know that. But nobody can hire what just happened, Julian. Ask any musician."

The silence stretched. Julian looked at August. August looked at his hands on the keys, and Wren watched him decide — visibly, expensively — whether to lie.

"We haven't," August said. His voice came out rougher than she'd ever heard it. He cleared his throat. "We haven't."

Julian's gaze moved between them, doing arithmetic. Wren could practically see the ledger: *staged relationship, yes; staged duet, impossible.* Whatever conclusion he reached, he kept it behind his teeth. "Excuse me," he said, and went to find the whiskey, and the aunt drifted after him like a satellite losing orbit.

And then they were alone in the music room, side by side on a bench built for one and a half, the sheet music with the little falling-flying bird open in front of them.

"You changed my harmony," Wren said.

"It was asking for it."

"It was in minor for a reason."

"I know why it was in minor." He said it lightly, but his hands were still on the keys, and one finger pressed a single low note — D, the root of everything she'd played — and held it, listening to it decay. "Iris wrote in that key. Toward the end. Everything she touched went minor." A pause. The note faded. "I haven't played this piano since her funeral."

"Why tonight?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Outside, the rain finally arrived, first in scattered taps against the conservatory glass, then in earnest, a soft gray roar enclosing the house.

"Because you played something true," he said, "in a house where nobody does. I couldn't just sit there and let it stand alone."

Wren looked at his profile — the jaw, the tired eyes, the mouth that had said *I couldn't just let it stand alone* as if it cost him nothing, as if it weren't the most honest sentence anyone had spoken under this roof in fourteen years. Her chest did something complicated and unauthorized. This was not in the contract. Nothing in the contract covered the way his shoulder felt against hers, still warm, or the way the last chord was still physically present in the room, living in the wood of the piano, in the floorboards, in her sternum.

Fake dating had rules. Fake dating had a weekend, a fee, a performance schedule, an exit clause. Fake dating did not include duets that undressed you in front of your fake boyfriend's family.

"Well," she said, standing, because standing was safe, because distance was a clause she could invoke. "For the record, that's not happening again."

"No," August agreed, not moving, his hands still resting on the silent keys. "Of course not."

But he was smiling — barely, crookedly, the first real smile she'd seen on him — and through the window the rain came down harder on the conservatory roof, and somewhere down the hall a door closed, and neither of them noticed that Julian had paused in the corridor outside the music room, phone in hand, typing a message to their mother that began: *Interesting. The pianist is better than we thought. Which means she's either genuine, or she's the best investment August has ever made. Either way —*

The rest of the message disappeared beneath the sound of the rain.
