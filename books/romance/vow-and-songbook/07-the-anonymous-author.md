## 7. The Anonymous Author

The city let her back in without ceremony. That was the thing about returning from Dravenmoor House — no one on the train platform knew she'd spent a weekend being someone's alibi, someone's proof of stability, someone's beautiful lie in a borrowed dress. Wren Solano stepped off the 6:40 with her guitar case and a week of August Dravenmoor lodged under her ribs like a splinter too deep to dig out with fingers.

Her apartment smelled the way it always did after a few days away: dust and old rosin and the ghost of takeout noodles. The radiator ticked its familiar arrhythmia. She set the guitar down inside the door, hung the dress — *his* mother's approval still hanging off it like a price tag — and stood in the middle of the small room with her coat still on, listening to the silence reassemble itself around her.

It was a good silence. A practiced one. She had built it herself, note by note, over years: the silence of a woman who owed nothing to anyone except a studio she was slowly buying back, measure by measure, gig by gig.

Except that wasn't true anymore, was it. She owed August. And worse — she'd stopped counting what she owed him somewhere between the second glass of wine at dinner and the moment his hands found the piano keys beside hers and the whole drawing room went quiet as held breath.

She took off her coat. She did not think about the way he'd looked at her when the last chord faded. She made tea instead. She was very good at not thinking about things; she'd had practice.

* * *

The email came on Tuesday, which was the day she usually pretended to work on the memoir and mostly just reread it.

She kept the manuscript in a folder called *Setlists*, buried three folders deep, password-protected, the file itself named something anodyne: *Gig Notes 2019.docx*. Old habits. Her agent, Priya, was the only living soul who knew what was actually in there, and even Priya knew it as "the family book," never by its real title. Anonymity wasn't paranoia. It was load-bearing architecture. Take it out and the whole building of her life came down.

The subject line read: **Interesting development — call me?**

Priya's exclamation points were rationed like wartime sugar. One meant genuine news. Two meant catastrophe or a bidding war. One, then, was survivable. Probably.

Wren called her back before she could talk herself out of it, phone wedged between ear and shoulder while she stirred honey into tea that didn't need it.

"You're back from the country house weekend," Priya said. Not a question. Priya tracked Wren's life with the gentle, unnerving attention of a woman who managed three other clients and remembered all their mothers' names. "How was the fake girlfriend business?"

"Lucrative." She hadn't told Priya the details. She'd told her only that an old favor had come due, and Priya, who understood favors the way sailors understand weather, hadn't pressed. "Why do you have an interesting development?"

"Because I got a call this morning I'm still trying to make sense of." Papers rustled on Priya's end; Wren pictured her office, the wall of spines, the mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST EDITOR. "You know Marlowe & Vane?"

Everyone knew Marlowe & Vane. They were the publisher that ate midlist authors for breakfast and called it curation. "I know of them."

"They've approached me. Not about your book — they don't know your book exists, don't panic — but about a project they're assembling. A counter-memoir, essentially. Someone's planning to publish a tell-all about a family that survived bankruptcy by betrayal, and Marlowe & Vane wants to get ahead of it. Buy the competing narrative. Control the story."

Wren's spoon stopped moving. The honey pooled at the bottom of the cup, unincorporated, a small golden sediment of dread.

"A counter-memoir," she repeated.

"That's the phrase they used. There's a manuscript floating around — anonymous, unfinished, apparently quite good — about a family business that went under, and how the surviving partners cut a deal that saved themselves and destroyed the other man. The family that came out ahead wants to bury the version where they're the villains. So they're commissioning their own book. Sanitized. Authorized. The betrayal reframed as hard necessity."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, a neighbor's television laughed at something. Wren stood very still in her kitchen, holding a telephone, feeling the floor tilt by degrees so slight she might have imagined them.

"Priya," she said carefully, "why are you telling me this?"

"Because they want *me* to broker it. My contacts, my discretion. And because—" papers again, or maybe Priya covering the receiver to say something to someone, "—because the description of the original manuscript sounded so much like yours that I nearly dropped my coffee. Family business. Bankruptcy. A father's partner. A daughter who watched it happen and wrote it all down."

The kitchen light hummed. Wren became aware of her own heartbeat the way you become aware of a ringing string — not because it changed, but because everything else had gone quiet enough to hear it.

"There can't be two books like that," she said.

"No," Priya agreed quietly. "There really can't be. Which means either someone shopped a pirated copy — impossible, I've seen the only draft and it lives on your machine — or the family in question has finally started talking, and their version is coming whether you publish yours or not."

"And Marlowe & Vane wants to buy—"

"Their own exoneration. Yes. And here's the part I keep circling back to." Priya's voice dropped into the register she used when she was about to say something she'd already decided was true. "They mentioned they'd already retained a writer for the project. A ghost. Very good, very discreet, apparently known for making other people's truths sound inevitable. They wouldn't give me a name. But whoever it is, they've been given the source material — interviews, documents, the family's own account. Which means somewhere in this city there's a person sitting down every morning to write your life story wrong."

Wren set the spoon down. It clinked against the saucer with absurd loudness.

"Does the family have a name?" she asked. "The one doing the buying?"

"They were cagey. Legal will be involved eventually, there'll be paper trails, but at this stage it's all handshakes and discretion." A pause. "Why? Do you know something?"

*Do I know something.* She thought of a drawing room in a house that remembered everything, of a brother named Julian whose smile had the specific warmth of a locked door, of a mother who looked at people the way appraisers look at silver — checking for flaws, weighing for market. She thought of the name stitched into the estate's history the way names get stitched into old houses: Dravenmoor. A family with holdings, with history, with the kind of money that survives bankruptcies that kill other families.

She thought of August, who ghosted other people's truths for a living, who deleted himself from every page he touched, who had told her over dessert — *no, that was the staged date, that was different, that was real* — that his rule was love stays in past tense.

A man known for making other people's truths sound inevitable.

"I don't know anything," Wren said. "Coincidences happen."

"Mm." Priya did not believe in coincidences; she believed in patterns, and she billed accordingly. "Well. I haven't agreed to anything. I wanted to talk to you first, because if this counter-memoir gets written, it changes the market for yours. Either it preempts you completely, or—" and here Priya's voice did something complicated, something almost hungry, "—or it makes yours the correction the record desperately needs. Anonymous memoirs die quiet deaths, Wren. But a rebuttal? A rebuttal gets read."

"So you want me to publish."

"I want you to *decide*. That's my whole job, deciding things before other people decide them for you." A breath. "Think about it. Call me Thursday."

She hung up. The apartment resumed its ticking, its humming, its small honest noises, and Wren stood among them feeling like a woman who had just learned the tide was coming in while she'd been building her house on what she'd believed was high ground.

* * *

That night she took out the manuscript.

She didn't open the file right away. First she sat with the guitar unplugged in her lap, playing nothing, letting her left hand find shapes without committing to sound — the way you rehearse a confession in the dark of a car outside the house where you have to give it. The chords moved under her fingers in the progression her father used to hum while balancing the shop's ledger, some half-remembered standard from his youth, and she let it loop until the grief of it went smooth-edged, an old stone in a river.

Then she opened the laptop and read the opening pages of her own life.

*My father trusted one man above all others,* it began. *That was the first mistake, though nobody called it that for eleven years.*

She had written it in the winter after the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, in the months when the studio debt sat on her chest like a sleeping animal and August Dravenmoor had appeared in her life the way rich men appear in the lives of musicians — suddenly, generously, with paperwork. She had written it under a pseudonym she'd invented from her mother's maiden name and a street sign. She had written it because the truth had nowhere else to live.

*The partner's name was Halvorsen. He had hands like a carpenter and a laugh you could hear through walls, and when the bank called in the loan he was the one my father wept to on the phone, and he was the one who had already signed the papers that made the weeping pointless.*

Reading it now, she heard it differently. That was the strange, sick gift of the weekend at Dravenmoor House: she'd spent three days watching a family perform itself — the mother's curated warmth, the brother's arithmetic, August's careful, wounded silence — and now she couldn't unread the mechanics of it. Families were instruments. You could learn anyone's tuning by listening long enough. And somewhere out there, the family from her pages — *her* family, the survivors, the ones who'd eaten the betrayal and called it bread — was tuning up its own instrument. Hiring its own player. Commissioning a version of events where necessity wore a white shirt and everyone did what they had to do.

And the ghost they'd hired would write it beautifully. That was the horror of it. Not a hack job — those were easy to dismiss. A *good* one. Seamless, sympathetic, inevitable. The kind of book that made readers feel smart for believing it.

She closed the laptop.

The apartment was dark except for the streetlight leaking orange through the blinds, striping the floor like a fretboard. Wren sat in the stripes and tried to assemble the facts into something she could hold.

Fact: an anonymous memoir about her exact story existed in the world's awareness, described accurately enough to make her agent spill her coffee.

Fact: the family it implicated was spending serious money to control the narrative before it escaped.

Fact: the ghostwriter they'd retained was, by reputation, exactly the kind of man August Dravenmoor was. Exactly the kind of man who had once covered her studio debt without blinking, who played piano like an apology, who kissed — no. Who had *not* kissed her, who had stood close enough in a rain-streaked conservatory that she could have counted his breaths, and who had looked at her afterward like a man reading a contract he wasn't sure he'd signed.

She was jumping at shadows. She knew she was. New York contained thousands of ghosts-for-hire; grief and talent produced them the way damp produced mold. The description fit half the freelancers in publishing.

But.

But the timing. The reunion. The way Julian had watched her across the dinner table like a man auditing a ledger, asking pleasant questions with unpleasant eyes — where she grew up, what her father did, *oh, a family business, how wonderful, and what happened to it?* — questions she'd answered smoothly, expertly, lying with the fluency of a woman who'd been lying about this since she was nineteen. And Julian had smiled at each answer the way you smile at a card you already know.

No. That was paranoia. Julian was suspicious of the *relationship*, not of her. Everyone was suspicious of the relationship, because the relationship was suspicious, because it was fake, because she was being paid to hold a man's hand in a house full of vultures.

Except it had stopped feeling entirely fake somewhere around the duet, and that was its own problem, filed away with the others.

She got up and paced. Four steps to the window, four steps back. The city murmured below — traffic like surf, a siren unwinding somewhere in Midtown, the eternal argument of a couple on the fire escape. All those lives running parallel, none of them knowing hers was quietly converging with a stranger's along two separate lines: the man whose family might be buying her erasure, and the book that might burn both houses down if it ever saw daylight.

Because that was the arithmetic she couldn't escape, wasn't it. If the counter-memoir published first, hers died — preempted, discredited, reduced to a bitter footnote by a better-funded lie. If hers published first, she detonated her own anonymity, invited litigation she couldn't afford, and handed every enemy she'd never met her name on a plate.

Unless.

Priya's voice, hungry and reasonable: *a rebuttal gets read.*

Wren stopped pacing. In the window's orange stripes she caught her own reflection — a woman with a guitar-shaped shadow, hair coming loose, looking less like a session musician for hire and more like a defendant awaiting a verdict she hadn't been told the charges for.

Her phone buzzed on the table. She flinched, then laughed at herself, then picked it up.

**August:** *Home safe?*

Two words. She stared at them for a long time. This was the arrangement working as designed — the follow-up text of a professional maintaining continuity, keeping the fiction warm for any relatives who might be watching, which none were, because they were ninety miles away, which meant the text was just... kindness. Or habit. Or a man practicing a role he'd forgotten how to stop playing.

She typed: *Yes. You?*

The reply came fast, as if he'd been holding the phone: *Yes. The house is quieter without you in it. That's not a complaint. Just data.*

Wren read it three times. Then she put the phone face-down on the table, because her hands had gone unsteady, and because the last thing she needed tonight was to add *whatever this was* to the column of things converging on her.

Data. He'd said *data*. She'd spent one weekend learning the vocabulary of him — past tense, terms, clauses, data — a whole grammar of feeling safely quarantined behind professional language, and she'd caught herself starting to speak it fluently, and that frightened her more than Marlowe & Vane did.

Because publishers she could fight. Contracts she could read. But a man who confessed in footnotes, who hid his heart in appendices, who might — *might*, she reminded herself, *paranoia, remember* — be sitting in some office right now writing her father's betrayal in someone else's voice, making it sound inevitable —

She picked the phone back up. Typed: *Get some sleep, Dravenmoor.* Deleted it. Typed: *Quieter here too.* Deleted that too, thumb hovering, heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional.

Finally she sent nothing at all, set the phone down, and went to the window.

Below, the city went on being the city. Somewhere in it, a lawyer was drafting a term sheet. Somewhere in it, a ghost was sharpening a pencil. Somewhere in it, her father's partner's grandchildren were sleeping soundly in houses bought with money that had a body count, if you followed the ledger far enough back.

And somewhere in it — ninety miles away, in a house that remembered everything — August Dravenmoor was probably awake too, staring at a ceiling, composing sentences he'd never send.

*We survive by what we refuse to say,* she had written once, in the manuscript, in a margin she'd later regret. *Until the day refusing costs more than saying.*

She'd thought it was philosophy when she wrote it. Standing at the window with her phone silent and her book endangered and her heart compromised, Wren understood it was a forecast.

Thursday, she'd call Priya. Before that, she had one more thing to do — one small, private, possibly insane thing. She opened the laptop again, pulled up the folder called *Setlists*, and began to read her own book straight through, cover to cover, for the first time in two years. Not as its author. As its witness.

And on page forty-one, in the chapter about the night the bank called, she found the detail she'd forgotten she'd included — the detail she'd lifted from a newspaper clipping, a minor local story she'd folded into her family's saga like a thread from another garment:

*a benefit concert held at a neighboring estate to raise funds for the ruined partner's family, hosted by a family whose name the clipping gave as Dravenmoor.*

Wren sat very still.

The orange stripes crawled across the floor. The radiator ticked. And the two halves of her life — the musician who owed August Dravenmoor a debt she'd repaid with a weekend of pretending, and the author whose anonymous truth was about to be purchased and buried by his employers — slid together with a click she felt in her teeth, like a lock turning, like a key fitting a cut it had been made for all along.

She reached for her phone. Stopped. Reached again.

Whatever she was going to say to him — *do you know them, did you know, is it you* — she typed it and deleted it, typed it and deleted it, until the screen dimmed and the room went dark and the question stayed where all her truest sentences lived:

Unsent.
