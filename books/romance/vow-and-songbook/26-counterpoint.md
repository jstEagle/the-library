## 26. Counterpoint

The new publisher's office smelled of cold coffee and old paper, and Wren loved it immediately for both reasons.

It was nothing like Halcyon House — no marble lobby, no assistant offering sparkling water in a cut-crystal glass, no sense that the building itself was judging your worth by the label on your coat. This was a third-floor walkup on a street where the gutters still held last night's rain, above a print shop that had been setting type since before either of them was born. The editor who met her was named Odile Ferrant, and she had ink on her fingers — actual ink, not the metaphorical kind — and she did not offer Wren water at all.

She offered her a chair, a contract, and forty minutes of silence to read it.

"You understand what you're doing," Odile said, when Wren finally looked up.

"I'm putting my name on it."

"You're putting your name on a book that names names. The Solano name. Your father's partner. A family that—" Odile paused, choosing her words the way a surgeon chooses an incision, "—that has resources. And you're doing it three weeks before some kind of family reckoning, if my sources are right, which they usually are."

"Your sources are right." Wren set the contract down and squared its edges against the table, a habit she'd inherited from her mother, who squared everything — napkins, receipts, the edges of her life — as though order could hold back ruin. "The Dravenmoor vote is in nineteen days."

"And August Dravenmoor."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. Wren kept her face still. She'd had practice. Ten years of session work taught you to keep time through anything — a bandleader's tantrum, a bride's weeping mother, a string section falling apart two bars before the bridge. You felt the wobble and you played through it, because the song didn't care about your feelings. The song only cared whether you showed up.

"What about him?" she said.

Odile leaned back. Her office was small enough that leaning back put her shoulder blades against a shelf of manuscripts, spines out, titles handwritten. "He ghosted three books for Halcyon before he started working for Corvane. His name appears in exactly none of them. I know because I tried to find it. There are people in this industry who can erase themselves so thoroughly that even their erasure has been erased." She tapped the contract. "And there are people who can erase *others*. If Corvane bought your memoir to bury it, someone wrote the shovel. My money says the shovel has good hands and sad eyes."

Wren looked out the window. Below, the print shop's windows glowed amber, and she could see a man inside moving type around with the tenderness people usually reserved for sleeping children. Letters into lines. Lines into pages. Pages into permanence.

She thought about August's hands. She had watched them on the piano keys in his mother's drawing room, watched them hover over hers during the duet without ever landing, watched them tear open a sugar packet at a diner table while he told her about his sister in a voice like a man reading his own autopsy. Good hands. Sad eyes. Yes.

"He knows," she said quietly. "Or he figured it out. He never said."

"And you never asked."

"I was afraid of the answer."

Odile nodded slowly, without judgment, which was somehow worse than judgment would have been. "Then let me ask it for you. If you sign this — under your own name, with the original text, the betrayal chapters intact — the book comes out in spring. Advance publicity starts in six weeks. Interviews. Excerpts. Your face next to your father's shame in every trade publication in the country. And if any of that reaches the Dravenmoor estate before the vote — and it will, because Julian Dravenmoor reads everything twice — then whatever chance August had of inheriting cleanly dies on the vine. You may cost that man his house, Ms. Solano. Are you prepared for that?"

Wren had rehearsed this answer in the shower, on the F train, at three in the morning staring at her ceiling where the water stain had grown into a shape like a hand reaching down. She had rehearsed it the way August must once have rehearsed his sentence — over and over, perfecting it, never saying it.

But sitting here, with ink-fingered Odile watching her with the patience of a woman who had waited out tougher writers than this, Wren found the rehearsal useless. What came out instead was the truth underneath the truth.

"No," she said. "I'm not prepared. I don't think you get to be prepared for something like this. I think you just do it with your hands shaking." She pulled the contract back toward her. "My father spent the last nine years of his life pretending he hadn't done what he did. He got very good at it. He smiled at dinner. He paid the mortgage. He taught me scales. And every single day, the lie sat between us at the table like a fourth chair nobody acknowledged, and when he died, I realized I couldn't remember a single conversation we'd had that wasn't conducted around it. Not one." Her voice held. Barely. "I am not going to build a life with a fourth chair. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then she slid a pen across the desk — a cheap ballpoint, chewed at the cap, gloriously unglamorous.

"Sign," she said.

* * *

Wren signed her name the way she played a difficult passage: too fast, before fear could catch up.

*Wren Solano.* Two words, eleven letters, and suddenly the anonymity she'd hidden behind for four years — the careful pseudonym, the manuscript sent in brown paper, the agent instructed to say only "a musician, mid-thirties, New York" — dissolved like breath off a window. She stared at the wet ink and felt exposed in a way that had nothing to do with nudity and everything to do with being *seen*. Anyone could look her up now. Julian Dravenmoor could look her up now.

That was rather the point.

She walked home through the early dark — the city had tipped into November while she wasn't watching, and the light now gave up by half past four, surrendering to sodium lamps and shop windows. Her breath made small ghosts in front of her. She passed the club where she'd first played for money at nineteen, passed the corner where her father had taught her to hear the bass line under any song — *"the melody lies, mija, the bass tells you what the song really means"* — and she thought: the memoir is the bass line. Everything else, the fake engagement, the staged dates, the whole architecture of the arrangement she and August had built and then watched collapse into something neither of them had contracted for — all of it was melody. Pretty, deceptive, lying beautifully.

The bass line was this: her family had survived by destroying another family, and she had written it down, and now she was going to sing it in daylight.

Her apartment was cold. She turned on the space heater, the kettle, and the single lamp by the window, and she sat down at the kitchen table with the folder she'd carried everywhere since the sale fell apart — the original manuscript, printed out, dog-eared, annotated in three colors of ink across four years of revision. Chapter One. The chapter she intended to read aloud, someday, somewhere, to whoever needed to hear it.

She read the opening paragraph again, and it undid her a little, the way it always did:

*My father taught me that every betrayal begins as a rescue. Nobody wakes up planning to destroy the person who trusted them. They wake up drowning, and the person who trusted them is floating nearby, holding something buoyant, and the drowning person does the arithmetic — quick, desperate, human — and takes it.*

She had written that before she knew anything about the Dravenmoors. Before August. Before the letter arrived demanding a plus-one, before the house on the hill with its remembering walls, before a man with sad eyes had hired her to be a lie and then failed, so visibly, so tenderly, to keep being one.

But the arithmetic was the same. It was always the same. Julian Dravenmoor was drowning in gambling debts; her father had drowned in the bankruptcy; her father's partner had simply been nearest, holding something buoyant. And now she was about to do her own arithmetic — quick, desperate, human — except this time the thing she was taking was the truth, and the person it might drown was a man who had never lied to her except with his silence.

The kettle screamed. She got up, poured tea she wouldn't drink, and stood at the window watching the street below, where a woman was walking a dog that refused to go in a straight line, and a delivery guy double-parked with the indifference of a man who owned the entire borough, and the world went on being ordinary, indifferent to the fact that Wren Solano had just signed away her safety.

Her phone buzzed against the table.

**AUGUST:** *I need to tell you something. In person. Please.*

She looked at the message for a long time. The cursor blinked after it, patient, expectant, like a held breath.

There was a version of her — the old version, the version from a year ago — who would have typed back *sure, tomorrow, the usual diner,* and gone, and listened, and absorbed whatever confession he brought, and folded it into herself along with everything else, keeping time, playing through. That version believed love meant receiving other people's truths and guarding them like valuables you'd been entrusted with but never allowed to spend.

The new version — the one with ink still drying on a signature — typed:

**WREN:** *Thursday. My place. 8pm. I have something to tell you too.*

She hesitated, then added:

**WREN:** *Bring nothing you've memorized. I want the rough draft.*

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

**AUGUST:** *I'm not sure I remember how to write rough drafts.*

**WREN:** *I'll teach you. It's what I do.*

She put the phone face-down and pressed both palms flat against the table, feeling the grain of the wood, the faint vibration of the radiator, the solid present-tense fact of surfaces. Thursday. Three days. In three days she would sit across from August Dravenmoor and tell him that in six weeks her name would be on a book that might burn his inheritance to the ground, and she would watch his face while she said it, and she would not flinch, and she would not soften it, and she would not — this was the vow, the real one, the one she was making alone in a cold kitchen with bad tea — she would not let him talk her out of it. Because she loved him. She could admit that now, privately, in the safety of her own skull, where it cost nothing. She loved him the way you love a song you can't stop humming even after it breaks your heart.

And loving him was precisely why the book had to come out. Because a man who had spent ten years ghostwriting other people's truths deserved, at minimum, to inherit one told plainly. Even if it ruined him. *Especially* if it ruined him. Some gifts arrive looking like arson.

* * *

The next two days, she prepared the way she prepared for sessions: methodically, obsessively, with lists.

She made copies of everything. The contract with Odile, signed and countersigned. The correspondence trail — her agent Marisol's emails, increasingly strange over the past months, evasive in ways Wren had been too heartsore to parse until now. The letter from Corvane's legal department that had arrived at Marisol's office addressed, impossibly, to the anonymous author. She photographed pages, backed up files to two drives and an email draft folder, and put a physical copy of the manuscript in a padded mailer addressed to Odile, to be sent Thursday morning — insurance, so that even if something happened to Wren or her laptop or her nerve, the book existed outside her hands.

Because she had learned something in the past week, sitting with the ashes of the arrangement, turning over what she knew: someone had sold her out. Someone had known her identity months ago and fed it to Corvane, and the list of people who could have was short enough to count on one hand missing several fingers. Marisol. August. Julian — though how Julian would have gotten it, she couldn't yet see. The thought of August's name on that list made her chest hurt in a way she refused to examine until Thursday, until she heard it from his own mouth, rough-draft version.

Wednesday night she took out the songbook.

Not the Dravenmoor songbook — she'd returned that, or rather, she'd never taken it; it lived in the conservatory at the house, in its drawer, waiting. This was her own notebook, the one she'd carried since she was sixteen, part journal, part sketchpad, part graveyard of unfinished songs. She flipped to the last page she'd written on and found the fragment she'd started the night after the second reunion weekend, after his mother's envelope of cash and Wren's refusal, after she'd stopped being a hireling and become something else, something unnamed:

*You can't owe a debt to a house.*
*You can't inherit a lie.*
*Sing it anyway. Sing it anyway.*

No melody yet. Just the words, sitting on the staff like birds on a wire, waiting for weather.

She hummed a phrase against them — minor key, rising at the end, unresolved — and wrote it down in her terrible shorthand notation that only she and God could read. Then she closed the book and sat with her hands in her lap and let herself feel, fully, for the first time since signing, the size of what she'd done.

Six weeks. In six weeks, *The Arithmetic of Survival* — Odile had fought for that title and won — would exist as galleys, then ARCs, then a publication date circled in red on calendars belonging to people whose business was knowing things. Julian Dravenmoor would know. His mother would know. Every relative assembling for the vote would know that the woman August had paraded as his fiancée was the anonymous memoirist whose book Corvane had bought to bury — and that the burying had failed, and that the failure had a byline, and the byline was hers.

August's standing would not survive it. Even if he was innocent — even if he'd accepted Corvane's takedown assignment to protect her, which was the theory she clung to on her better nights — the optics were fatal. The ghostwriter engaged to the ghostwritten. The fixer married to the problem. Families like his didn't forgive complications; they amputated them.

He might lose the house. He might lose the fee, the reputation, the decade of careful professional invisibility. He might lose everything he'd built out of silence.

And she was going to hand him the match anyway, and ask him to strike it himself, because a truth delivered gently by someone else wasn't a truth at all — it was just another kind of ghostwriting.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the empty apartment, to the space heater's orange glow, to her father wherever he was, presumably nowhere, presumably beyond caring, though she liked to imagine him in some backstage greenroom of the universe, tuning a bass, listening. "Okay, Papá. Sing it anyway."

* * *

Thursday came in on a front of sleet that ticked against the windows like impatient fingers.

Wren cleaned the apartment, then uncleaned it slightly, because a spotless apartment would look like effort and she wanted tonight to look like what it was: two people with nothing left to hide behind. She bought bread and cheese and a bottle of wine that cost more than her dignity could comfortably afford, then hid the receipt. At seven she changed clothes three times and settled on the sweater he'd once said looked like "a thundercloud having a good day," which was the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to her, which said less about the sweater than about the poverty of her recent romantic history.

At 7:52, footsteps on the stairs. Heavy, hesitant, pausing twice — she tracked him floor by floor the way she tracked a tempo change, and when the knock came it was soft, almost apologetic, three raps like a question mark.

She opened the door.

August stood in the hallway with sleet melting in his hair and a paper bag clutched to his chest like a shield, and he looked — there was no better word — *unburdened and burdened at once*, lighter and heavier than she'd ever seen him, a man who had clearly spent the walk over settling accounts with himself and had arrived with the receipts.

"Rough draft," he said. He lifted the paper bag slightly. "I brought bread. Bread isn't memorized. Bread is improvised."

Despite everything — despite the contract in her drawer and the mailer by the door ready for morning pickup and the six-week fuse already burning — Wren laughed. It escaped her before she could vet it, startled and real, and she saw it land on him like sunlight, saw him drink it in like a man who'd been rationing.

"Get in here," she said, "before you melt."

He stepped inside. She closed the door against the sleet, against the street, against the whole listening world, and for one suspended moment they stood in the narrow entryway, close enough that she could smell rain and wool and the particular clean-warm scent of him that her memory had been distorting for weeks, and the air between them was so charged with unsaid things that it practically hummed, a low B, the note before the downbeat.

"I have to tell you something," they said, simultaneously.

They stared at each other. Outside, the sleet thickened. Somewhere below, the print shop's lights went dark, the type set, the pages locked in the chase, tomorrow's truth already composed and waiting for morning.

August set the bread down on the hall table with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a weapon before a parley. "You first," he said. "I've had ten years of practice going last. I'd like to see how the other half lives."

Wren drew a breath that went all the way down to the bass line.

"In my bedroom drawer," she said, "there's a contract with my name on it. My real name. And in six weeks, everyone you've ever known is going to know what's in the book attached to it — including your brother, including your mother, including every Dravenmoor walking into that vote." She held his gaze and did not flinch. "August. I'm the memoirist. And I'm about to burn your inheritance down."

Something moved through his face — she watched for devastation, for betrayal, for the shutters coming down — but what she saw instead, arriving slow and unmistakable, was relief. Profound, exhausted, *grateful* relief, like a man who has carried a secret so long that hearing someone else speak theirs feels like being handed a chair.

"Thank God," he said.

And then, before she could decide what to do with that: "Because I've been carrying yours for a month, Wren. I know exactly what's in that book. I've read every word of it." His voice cracked, quiet and terrible. "Corvane hired me to write its destruction. And I need to show you what I did instead."
