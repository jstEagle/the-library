## 10. Smoke and Second Guesses

**Beatrix**

The vineyard house smelled of pencil shavings and cold coffee and, underneath it all, of him — that faint mineral scent like rain on hot stone that I had spent eleven years trying to forget and had failed at with the dedication of a woman who practices her failures daily.

We had been working for three hours. The manuscript lay between us on the kitchen table in loose stacks, chapters nine through fourteen, the ones I had drafted fastest because they were the ones I remembered best. Marcus read with two fingers pressed flat against the page, as if holding the words down so they couldn't escape. He read slowly. He had always read slowly — I'd learned that the first summer I knew him, watching him work through a single paperback across an entire week of evenings on his porch, mouthing sentences to himself like a man tasting each one before he swallowed.

"You changed this," he said, tapping chapter eleven. "The section about my father's funeral."

My pen stopped moving. "In what way?"

"It's not what I told you." He looked up. His eyes were the color of the light that comes just before rain — gray with something waiting inside it. "I told you it rained. Everyone remembers it rained. But you wrote—" he turned the page toward me, and there it was, my own sentence staring back at me like a confession: *It didn't rain that day, though everyone swears it did; grief is a weather we invent afterward.*

"I took a liberty," I said.

"A liberty." He said it the way other men said *heresy*.

"Memoir isn't transcription, Marcus. It's translation. You gave me the facts. My job is to give them back to you as truth."

He held my gaze for a long moment, long enough that I could count the new lines at the corners of his eyes, the ones harvest had put there, or grief, or time — the three of them working the same field. Then he nodded once and looked back down, and I exhaled so quietly that not even the coffee steam noticed.

This was the danger of him. Not the old danger — the young, dramatic one, the one that announced itself with thunder and bad decisions. This was worse. This was the quiet version, the version where we sat at a table being professionals, and every professional thing — the passing of pages, the reaching for pens, the leaning in over a paragraph — was a small trespass neither of us acknowledged. Longing doesn't always shout. Sometimes it stands very close to you and holds its breath.

"The last chapters," he said. "Fifteen onward. That's where it gets hard."

"I know."

"Do you?" He set down the page. "Because you've written everything else like a woman who wasn't there. And those chapters — you were there for those. Weren't you?"

The question landed somewhere beneath my ribs and stayed. Outside, the late-afternoon light had gone amber, the color of white wine in a cheap glass, and somewhere down the rows a tractor coughed itself awake.

"I was in the valley," I said carefully. "Eleven years ago. For a season."

"For a season." He repeated it flatly, testing the weight of it. "Doing what?"

"Writing," I said, which was true, and also the most elaborate lie I had ever told, because I had been writing him — writing to him, one letter, one night, and then folding my whole heart into an envelope and hiding it from myself for a decade.

He watched me the way he watched the sky in August, reading pressure systems. Then, mercifully, he let it go. "More coffee?"

"Please."

He rose, and as he passed behind my chair his hand came to rest — briefly, accidentally, or in the way that nothing between us had ever been accidental — on the back of it. Five seconds. Maybe six. I felt the warmth of his palm through the wood like a word spoken too low to hear but too close to miss. Then he moved to the counter, and the kettle went on, and we both pretended that nothing had happened, which is its own kind of event.

* * *

**Marcus**

She bit the end of her pen when she was thinking. She had done it eleven years ago and she did it now, and the continuity of it undid me more than any grand gesture could have. People change their hair, their cities, their names. But Beatrix Hartwell still destroyed pens one cap at a time, and some traitor part of me wanted to build a life around that fact alone.

I carried the coffee back to the table and set her cup down at her elbow without asking which side she preferred, because I already knew — left, always left, she'd said once that right-handed people never think about it, and I had thought about it every day since, apparently, whether I meant to or not.

"Chapter fifteen," she said, squaring the pages. "The summer everything went wrong. You'll have to walk me through it again. Slowly."

I sat down across from her. Between us: the manuscript, two cups, and eleven years of unspoken things arranged so densely they might as well have been a wall.

"You know how it goes," I said. "Bad year. Bad decisions. The bank breathing down my neck. And then—" I stopped.

"And then," she prompted softly.

"And then the person I thought would stay didn't stay." I kept my eyes on the page. It was easier to say these things to paper than to her face, which was probably why I'd hired a ghostwriter in the first place, though I'd have died before admitting that the ghost I'd hired had a face I saw every time I closed my eyes. "I've told the story a hundred times. To bankers, to journalists, to myself at four in the morning. It gets smoother every time. That's the problem with telling a story too often — you sand off the parts that hurt, and the parts that hurt are the only ones worth keeping."

She was quiet. When I finally looked up, her eyes were wet, and she turned her head toward the window fast, as if the light had offended her.

"Beatrix."

"Dust," she said. "The valley in autumn. It gets in everything."

There was no dust. The floors had been swept that morning by Rosa, who ran the house like a small efficient navy. But I let her have the lie, because I recognized it — I had used that exact lie, in that exact tone, a hundred times, about a hundred things. *Dust. Allergies. Tiredness.* The small false reasons we offer when the true reason is standing in the room with us.

"Take your time," I said instead, and reached across the table to turn the page for her, and my hand crossed paths with hers.

That was all. Fingers brushing knuckles over a stack of paper, half a second, the kind of contact that wouldn't register in any court in the world. But the current that went up my arm was not half anything. It was the full voltage of a decade, and I saw it hit her too — saw her breath catch, saw her hand go still under mine, saw her lift her eyes to me with an expression I had seen exactly once before, on a night in September eleven years ago, standing under the string lights outside the crush pad with wine on her lips and goodbye in her posture.

The room got very small. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere far away a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Her chin tilted up — a degree, maybe two. An invitation written in a font so small you could only read it if you were already looking. And God help me, I was already looking. I leaned in. She leaned in. The manuscript lay between us like a border crossing, and I was going to cross it, consequences be damned, eleven years of silence be damned—

A truck ground up the gravel drive.

We broke apart like teenagers caught by headlights, both of us suddenly fascinated by our coffee cups, our posture, the fascinating ceiling. By the time the knock came at the door, we were two professionals sitting at a professional distance, and if my heart was going like a threshing drum, well, that was between me and my cardiologist.

Rosa showed them in — a courier, holding a padded envelope. "Sign here," he said, bored, and I signed, and he left, and the envelope sat on the table between Beatrix and me like a third guest who knew something we didn't.

* * *

**Beatrix**

I should have left. That was the thought running on a loop as Marcus slit the envelope open with a pocketknife, as he shook out its contents onto the table. I should have stood up, gathered my pages, thanked him for the coffee, and driven back to my rented cottage where no one's knuckles brushed mine and no one's chin tilted a degree or two toward ruin.

But I didn't leave. I never left. Leaving was the one skill I'd never mastered, even when I executed it perfectly — I had left him eleven years ago and somehow never actually gone anywhere; I had simply relocated the leaving to a place inside me where it could continue indefinitely.

"What is it?" I asked.

"Advance copies." He fanned them out — the galleys, the publisher's early printings of the memoir, my memoir-his-memoir-our-memoir, with a cover photograph of the vineyard at dusk and his name in tall silver letters. *Marcus Vale: Groundwork.* No author credit beyond his own. No trace of me anywhere, which was, technically, the entire point.

"They're beautiful," I said, and meant it, and hated how much I meant it.

He slid one across the table to me. Our fingers did not touch this time. We had both become careful, the way people are careful after nearly stepping off a ledge — exaggeratedly casual, hands in pockets, whistling.

"There's a note," he said, unfolding a card that had come with the package. His brow creased as he read. "From Vivienne Ashford. The publisher's daughter. She runs publicity now." He read a little further, and something in his face shifted — nothing dramatic, just a door closing softly somewhere behind his eyes. "She wants to come out before harvest. Meet the 'collaborative team.' Do a feature on the making of the book."

"That's normal," I said. "Publicity people love process."

"Mm." He set the card down. "She also writes: *'I'm so curious about your ghost. Everyone has theories. I have one of my own.'*" He looked up at me. "Exclamation point," he added grimly, as if the punctuation itself were a threat.

My stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "Everyone has theories."

"Everyone does." He was watching me again, that weather-reading look. "You'd think after eleven years of anonymity, a person would get comfortable in the dark."

"I'm very comfortable in the dark," I said. "It's the lit rooms that worry me."

For a moment — one suspended, dangerous moment — I thought he might say it. Might say *I know*, or *it's you*, or *show me your handwriting*, or any of the sentences that had been circling this table for weeks like birds of prey. His mouth opened. Closed. He picked up his coffee and drank it like a man drowning something.

"I should go," I said, standing, gathering pages with hands that were almost steady. "Chapter sixteen won't write itself."

"No," he agreed quietly. "It usually takes a woman with a pen and a grudge against happiness."

At the door, Rosa's roses nodding in the evening wind behind me, I turned. He stood in the hallway with the galley copy in his hand, backlit by the kitchen, and the distance between us was four feet and eleven years and possibly nothing at all.

"Marcus. About earlier—"

"Don't," he said. Not harshly. Almost gently. "If we talk about it, we'll have to decide something. And I don't think either of us is ready to decide."

The terrible thing was that he was right, and the more terrible thing was that some cowardly archive-deep part of me was relieved. I drove home with the windows down and the manuscript on the passenger seat like a sleeping animal, and I did not think about the degree or two of my own chin, and I absolutely did not think about the envelope in my desk drawer with my own handwriting on it, water-stained, postmarked from a past I'd sealed shut.

I thought about none of it, the whole way home, with tremendous discipline.

And then my phone buzzed. A message from my editor, four words long, and every one of them a stone dropped into still water:

*Vivienne Ashford called me.*
