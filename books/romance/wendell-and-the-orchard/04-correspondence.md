## 4. Correspondence

The guild's pen-pal program arrived in my inbox the way most bad ideas do: dressed as an opportunity. *Reconnect with the lost art of correspondence,* the email said. *Anonymous partners. Monthly prompts. No names, no faces, no professional consequences.*

No professional consequences. As a ghostwriter, I had made a career out of having none of my own.

I told myself I signed up for research. That was the word I used — *research* — as though studying how strangers disarmed each other on paper might teach me something about the memoir I was strangling into shape by day. The truth, which I admitted to no one, not even my sister, especially my sister, was that I wanted to be someone else for an hour a week. I wanted to write without a client's voice in my mouth like a bit.

The rules were simple. First names only, and even those were optional. No professions. No addresses. The guild matched you and let you loose.

My correspondent's first letter began: *I assume you were assigned to me as punishment.*

I read it twice, standing at my kitchen counter with flour still under my fingernails from the morning's baking, and I laughed out loud alone in my apartment, which is either a sign of good writing or a sign I need to get out more.

*Dear Punishment,* I wrote back. *If this is a penalty, I'd hate to see what you consider a reward. I'm told we're supposed to exchange thoughts on memory this month. Fine. Here's mine: memory is a liar with excellent posture. It stands up straight and tells you a story until you believe it, and the story is always about how you were right.*

*Yours in mutual sentence,* — I. V.

I sealed it before I could soften it. Somewhere across the city — or the country; the guild wouldn't say — a stranger was about to open an envelope full of my worst impulses, and I found I didn't mind. There is a particular freedom in being disliked honestly. In my professional life I was liked constantly, professionally, emptily. My clients loved me the way you love a mirror that flatters.

The reply came nine days later.

---

Wendell Marsh did not want a pen pal. He wanted his editor to stop looking at him with that expression she'd perfected, the one that said *the review was brutal but your sales are fine so smile.* He joined the program on a dare he would later deny was a dare, and when the assignment sheet came back with nothing but two initials — *I.V.* — he decided the whole enterprise was exactly as stupid as he'd predicted.

Then the first letter arrived, and it called him *Punishment*, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the review came out loosened by one degree. Not enough to notice. Enough to keep.

He wrote back on a Tuesday, at his desk, with the ghostwritten draft of his own life sitting reproachfully in the tray beside him. Whoever this person was, they wrote like someone who'd been hurt by memory specifically, personally, the way you're hurt by a relative.

*Dear I.V.,*

*You say memory is a liar with excellent posture. I say memory is a witness who took a bribe. It doesn't lie, exactly. It just tells the version that lets everyone sleep.*

*You sound like someone who has already decided what people are. I've met a lot of people like that. They're usually describing themselves.*

*This month's prompt is forgiveness, which I suspect you'll enjoy even less than memory. Try this one: forgiveness is a debt you cancel and then have to live without the interest.*

*— W.*

He read it over three times, decided it was too honest, and mailed it anyway. That was the strange thing about writing to a stranger. Honesty had nowhere to land, so it just fell through you onto the page.

---

I read W.'s letter at the bakery, between customers, standing behind the counter where my mother used to stand, and I hated how well it was written. That's the thing nobody tells you about hating someone's prose: it means they got under your skin, and skin is where I keep everything.

*A witness who took a bribe,* I typed into my journal that night, furious. *Who talks like that? Who thinks like that?*

Someone who'd been bribed, probably. Someone who knew exactly what it cost to buy a memory's silence.

I wrote back:

*Dear W.,*

*You think people can't change, or you hope they can't? There's a difference. One is philosophy. The other is fear wearing philosophy's coat.*

*On forgiveness: I don't cancel debts. I keep meticulous accounts. Every IOU filed, every interest rate calculated, every payment refused and recorded. You know why? Because the moment you forgive someone, you have to admit the account existed. And some of us built ourselves out of that ledger. Take away the debt and there's surprisingly little left standing.*

*Tell me, W. — and answer honestly, since we'll never meet — who owes you?*

*I.V.*

I sent it and felt lighter and worse, both. That was becoming the signature flavor of these letters: lighter and worse, like dessert made with something you shouldn't have eaten.

His answer took eleven days. When it came, I read it four times and then put it face-down on the table like it had seen too much.

---

Wendell almost didn't send the third letter. He'd written it drunk on cheap rye at one in the morning, and drunk-Wendell had a habit of telling the truth, which sober-Wendell then had to manage like a spill.

But he read it again in daylight and found that drunk-Wendell had, annoyingly, been right.

*Dear I.V.,*

*You asked who owes me. Everyone, is the coward's answer. Nobody, is the true one, and here's the ugly arithmetic: whoever I blame, I chose to blame, because the alternative was looking at what I did myself. A fire happened once. Everyone needed someone to carry it. I volunteered, or was volunteered; after twenty years I genuinely can't tell the difference, and maybe that's the same thing.*

*So no, I don't think people can't change. I think change is possible and rare and mostly looks like failure from the outside. I think most people who "changed" just got better at hiding the original model.*

*You keep accounts. Fine. But ask yourself what you're charging yourself. Because in my experience the person with the longest ledger is always on both ends of it.*

*— W.*

He sat with that last line after mailing it, feeling exposed in a way he hadn't since he was a boy standing in a burned orchard while adults pointed at him. He told himself the discomfort was indigestion.

---

*Both ends of it.*

I set the letter down in my mother's old kitchen, in the apartment above the bakery where I'd moved back six months ago to save rent and told everyone it was to save the bakery, and I looked around at the room I'd grown up in and felt the letter land somewhere I hadn't invited it.

Because here was the thing I hadn't written to anyone: I had left. At nineteen, with a scholarship and a suitcase and my mother's voice following me down the driveway saying *you'll come back for harvest season, won't you*, and I had said yes the way you say yes to weather. And I hadn't come back. Not for harvest, not for Christmas, not for the winter her knees went, not until the phone call that starts with a nurse's careful voice. Eleven years. I kept immaculate accounts of what everyone owed me, and the largest entry in the ledger was in my own handwriting, against my own name, and I had never once audited it.

I wrote back carefully, the way you walk on ice:

*Dear W.,*

*You got one thing wrong. I don't charge myself. I exempted myself years ago. That's the trick of a good ledger — you audit everyone but the accountant.*

*You mentioned a fire. You didn't ask if I knew about fires. Smart. People who mention their wounds first are usually fishing for yours, and I don't fish.*

*But I'll give you this much, since you gave me more than you meant to: I think you're wrong about hiding. I think the original model doesn't hide. It waits. It stands in doorways. It shows up in your handwriting when you're tired.*

*Next month's prompt is whether people deserve second chances. I already know my answer. I'm curious whether you'll recognize it.*

*I.V.*

I mailed it and stood a long time at the mailbox, watching the truck take it away, feeling like I'd mailed a piece of myself to a stranger and couldn't remember which piece.

---

By the fifth exchange, Wendell had developed a habit he refused to examine: he checked the mail first, before coffee, before the newspaper, before the draft. His editor noticed. She said, *You're smiling at envelopes now. Should I be worried?* and he said, *It's research,* and heard, with horror, how much he sounded like her.

Her letters were knives wrapped in silk, and he was starting to look forward to being cut. There was something in the way she argued — precise, contemptuous, wounded underneath like a house with good siding and a cracked foundation — that reminded him of someone. He couldn't place who. The resemblance itched.

*Dear I.V.,*

*Second chances. Here's my answer: nobody deserves them. Deserving has nothing to do with it. A second chance isn't a wage, it's a gift, and gifts by definition aren't earned, which is precisely why they're unbearable. You can refuse a wage. You can stand on principle with a wage. A gift leaves you standing there holding it, owing something that can't be entered in any ledger, and I think that's what you actually can't forgive people for — not what they did, but that someday they might hand you something you can't pay back.*

*You said you exempted yourself from your own accounts. Liar. Nobody exempts themselves. They just move the debt to a shelf where they don't have to look at it, and the shelf is usually shaped like a person they're angry at instead.*

*Am I describing you? You tell me. Am I describing me? Don't answer that.*

*— W.*

He read it after sending and thought: *I should not have sent that.* Then he thought: *She'll come back harder.* Then, appalled at himself, he thought: *I hope so.*

---

He was describing me. That was the intolerable part. I sat on the floor of my mother's room surrounded by boxes I'd been avoiding since the funeral — the estate lawyer had given me ninety days to sort the house, and I'd given myself the same deadline for sorting myself, and both were going badly — and I read his letter and felt seen by a man whose name I didn't know and whose face I'd have sworn I hated if I'd ever seen it.

*The shelf is usually shaped like a person they're angry at instead.*

I looked up at the ceiling. Downstairs, the bakery ovens ticked as they cooled. On the wall hung the photograph of my mother at thirty, flour-dusted, laughing, standing in front of trees I hadn't stood under in fifteen years.

"Stop it," I told the letter. "You don't know me."

It didn't argue. It just lay there being right.

I wrote back anyway. I was beginning to understand that I would always write back. That was the crack in the dam, though I didn't have the word for it yet.

*Dear W.,*

*Fine. The shelf. You win this round, anonymous stranger who writes like a man losing an argument with himself in public.*

*Here's what I'll trade you: I left home at nineteen and never came back until it was too late to matter, and I have spent eleven years constructing an elaborate case against everyone except the person who bought the bus ticket. There. Audited. Are you happy?*

*Don't answer. Instead tell me this, since we're apparently doing surgery without anesthesia: what did you do? The fire. You said everyone needed someone to carry it. Did you carry it because you started it? Or because carrying it was easier than watching someone else get blamed?*

*There's a difference. I've watched both happen. Only one of them is forgivable, and it isn't the one people choose.*

*I.V.*

I sealed it with hands that weren't quite steady, and I told myself the question was abstract. Research. Curiosity about a stranger's conscience.

But downstairs, the bakery smelled like cinnamon and scorched sugar, the way it had smelled the autumn everything burned, and I caught myself standing very still in the middle of the kitchen, listening to a memory lie to me with excellent posture.

---

Wendell read her question at dusk, in the window seat of his apartment, with the city going gold below him, and for a long moment he could not breathe.

*Did you carry it because you started it? Or because carrying it was easier than watching someone else get blamed?*

Twenty years. Twenty years of interviews and profiles and one scathing review that had called his memoir-in-progress "a monument to a man who mistakes proximity for innocence," and not one person — not his editor, not his agent, not the ghostwriter churning out his sanitized chapters — had ever asked the actual question. They'd all asked variations of *how did it feel*. She'd asked *what did you do*.

He got out paper. His hand shook slightly, which annoyed him.

*Dear I.V.,*

*Neither. Both. Ask me again sometime when I'm braver.*

*But here's what I'll say tonight, since you bled first: I was fourteen. There was a fire, and afterward everyone agreed on a story, and the story had a boy in it, and I let them have the boy because the alternative was a man I loved, and I was fourteen and loved him, and love at fourteen will sign anything.*

*Some nights I think the boy was innocent. Some nights I think innocence is just guilt that hasn't been introduced to itself yet. I flip between them like a coin that keeps landing on its edge.*

*You asked what I did. What I did was survive being believed guilty, and then build a whole life on top of the belief like a house on a sinkhole, and now the house is famous and the sinkhole is patient.*

*Your turn, accountant. What's on your shelf? Not the confession you traded me — the real one. The one shaped like a place.*

*— W.*

He mailed it before dawn, standing at the blue mailbox on his corner while the streetlights buzzed off, and walked home feeling hollowed and clean, like a tree after pruning.

---

*A place,* he'd written. *The one shaped like a place.*

I stood in the orchard.

I hadn't planned to be there. I'd driven out to look at the property line — the adjoining parcel, the one with the FOR SALE sign going soft with rain, the one my landlord's lawyer had mentioned in a tone that meant *someone else wants it* — and instead of turning around I'd climbed the fence like I was nineteen again and walked in among the trees.

They were older. Neglected, half of them, unpruned, gone wild at the crown. But the rows were still rows. My mother's rows. And at the center, ringed in nettles, the old stone well with its rotted cover, where I'd dropped pennies as a girl and wished things I no longer remembered wanting.

I stood there with his letter in my coat pocket and the rain starting, and I understood, with the terrible clarity that only arrives when it's useless, that I had been writing to this place my whole life. Every letter I'd ever sent. Every argument about memory and debt and who owed whom. All of it addressed here, to forty acres of apple trees and a well full of wishes, returned to sender, unopened, for fifteen years.

And I understood something else, colder: whoever W. was, whatever fire he'd carried, he was real, and close, and I was beginning — dangerously, unforgivably — to care.

I turned to go, and that was when I saw, far down the row, past the wild Gravensteins, a figure standing at the fence line with his hands in his pockets, watching me like he'd been watching for a while.

Tall. Gray at the temples. Familiar in a way that made my stomach drop before my brain caught up and supplied the name from every literary section in the city:

Marsh. Wendell Marsh.

Standing in my mother's orchard, in the rain, ten feet inside the property line he was trying to buy out from under me.
