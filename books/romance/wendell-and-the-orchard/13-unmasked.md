## 13. Unmasked

I should have known something was wrong when the flowers arrived.

Not because publishers don't send flowers — they do, extravagantly, the way other people send apologies — but because these were lilies. Nobody sends lilies to a person they like. Lilies are funeral flowers wearing a nicer coat. I stood in my kitchen holding them at arm's length while my sister, Priya, looked up from her coffee with the expression of a woman who has already read tomorrow's newspaper.

"Those are ominous," she said.

"They're celebratory." I found the card. *To the voice behind the voice. See you tonight. — R.* Rhea Calloway, my publisher's acquisitions director, a woman who once described a manuscript as "edible" and meant it as a compliment. *The voice behind the voice.* It was meant to be flattering. It landed somewhere between my shoulder blades instead.

Tonight was the launch event for Wendell Marsh's memoir — the one I had written. Or co-written. Or, if you asked the contract, "developed in collaboration with," which is publishing's way of saying *a woman did the work and will receive neither credit nor a byline nor, apparently, a coherent sense of self.*

I had spent eleven months inside that book. Eleven months of transcribing Wendell's halting interviews, of shaping his silences into sentences, of learning the particular rhythm of his grief so well that some mornings I woke up homesick for a childhood that wasn't mine. The memoir was called *The Long Row*, and it was good. I don't say that out of vanity, or not only vanity. It was good the way a set bone is good — straightened, knitted, functional. And now it was going out into a ballroom full of reviewers and booksellers and people who held wine glasses by the stem to signal their seriousness, and I was going to stand at the edge of it like a ghost at my own funeral, which, given the lilies, felt thematically appropriate.

"Wear the green dress," Priya said. "If you're going to be invisible, be invisibly well-dressed."

"I'm not invisible. I'm discreet. There's a difference."

"The difference is about four hundred dollars an hour, yes."

I threw a balled-up dish towel at her. She caught it without looking, which is the kind of thing sisters can do and no one else on earth.

Here is what I had not told Priya — what I had barely told myself: for five months, I had been writing letters to a stranger. The guild's pen-pal program, anonymous by design, two names sealed away until the program's end. My correspondent was prickly and precise and wounded in ways he tried to disguise as opinions. We fought about memory and forgiveness and whether a person could change, and every fight felt like being known. Somewhere in the middle of it, without deciding to, I had started telling him things I hadn't told anyone. That I left home at nineteen and let the distance calcify. That when my mother got sick, I sent money instead of coming back, because money doesn't require you to sit in a kitchen and watch someone disappear.

And here is the thing I really hadn't told myself: sometimes, when Wendell Marsh said something across his desk — some deflection, some dry, armored little joke — I heard my pen pal's cadence underneath it. I told myself it was coincidence. Two damaged men, same damage dialect. I told myself a lot of things. Ghostwriters are professional tellers-of-things; we just usually tell other people's.

The event was at the Whitmore Hotel, in a ballroom with ceilings high enough to have weather. I arrived early, because arriving early lets you watch a room assemble itself, and assembled rooms always tell you who holds the power: whoever everyone else keeps glancing toward. Tonight that was Rhea Calloway, in silver, standing near the podium with her phone in her hand and her smile calibrated to the wattage of a runway.

Wendell was already there too, at the far side of the room, looking like a man attending the trial of someone he used to know. He'd worn the gray suit. He hated the gray suit; he'd said so during our third interview, and I'd written the complaint into a chapter about his father's funerals, where it fit like a key in a lock. When our eyes met across the ballroom, he gave me a nod — brief, correct, the nod you give a colleague. The nod you give the tool that worked.

Something in my chest did a small, traitorous thing anyway. Five months of letters will do that. You build a person out of ink and then any man with the right jawline gets mistaken for him.

"You must be Ivo." A young publicist materialized at my elbow with a clipboard and the cheerfulness of the recently hired. "Rhea wants you front row. She said — hold on, she said to tell you — 'front row, center, where the light is.'"

"Where the light is," I repeated.

"For the reading," the publicist said, and moved on to more important clipboards.

I want it noted, for the record, by whatever record such things are kept: I still didn't understand. I thought she wanted me there because I'd earned proximity. Eleven months of work earns you proximity, or so I believed back then, back in the innocent age of forty minutes earlier.

The room filled. Wine was poured. Someone played jazz from a speaker too small for its ambitions. I sat front row center, where the light was, holding a glass of champagne I had no intention of drinking, and watched Wendell take the stage.

He was good at this, which surprised people who only knew his books. He had a low, unhurried way of speaking, like a man laying stones. He thanked the room. He said a few words about his father, about the orchard country they'd come from, about how a memoir is "just a letter you finally get brave enough to sign." The audience made the soft approving noise audiences make when a writer accidentally says something quotable.

Then Rhea took the microphone.

"Ladies and gentlemen, before Wendell reads — a moment about how this book came to be." Her smile went up another hundred lumens. "Because at Meridian House, we believe in transparency. In craft. In the whole story."

My spine registered the temperature drop before my brain did.

"Memoir is a strange genre," Rhea continued, strolling the stage like she owned it, which financially she did. "It asks a person to make meaning of their life. But some people — many people — need help making that meaning. They need a collaborator. A craftsman." She paused, savoring. "So when we began this project, we did something unusual. We commissioned not just a ghostwriter, but a study. An experiment in correspondence. Because we wanted to know: could two strangers, writing honestly to each other, become the emotional engine of a book?"

The room murmured. Somewhere a glass clinked down onto a table with the carefulness of someone who suddenly needed both hands free.

"So tonight," Rhea said, "we're delighted to share a sample of that research. Letters exchanged over five months between our ghostwriter and — well. You'll see." She lifted a folder. Cream-colored paper. My handwriting on the top page, unmistakable, the way your own face is unmistakable in a photograph you never posed for.

"We're also delighted to introduce her properly. Front row, center — Ivo Vasquez, the voice behind *The Long Row*."

A spotlight found me. Actually found me, physically, a warm white circle like a target drawn on the floor. The room turned. Two hundred faces turned, and among them, mid-turn, Wendell's — and I watched the recognition move through him in stages, the way weather moves across a valley. Confusion. Arithmetic. Then the terrible, completed sum.

His mouth formed my name without sound. Not *Ivo*. The other name. The guild handle, the pen-pal pseudonym we'd signed for five months — *Juniper* — except he wasn't saying juniper, he was saying nothing, because there are moments when even a man built of armor forgets how to put it on.

"No," I said, out loud, to nobody, to everybody.

"Oh, don't be shy," Rhea said warmly, misreading the entire room, misreading possibly the entire century. "The letters are beautiful. Truly. Listen."

And she read.

She read my letter — my letter, the one from March, the one where I wrote: *You ask if I've ever failed someone. Yes. I failed the person who loved me most, by leaving, and then I failed her again by coming back too late, and I have been doing penance ever since in other people's kitchens, writing other people's homecomings because I couldn't bear to write my own.*

Two hundred strangers heard it. Two hundred strangers turned to look at the woman in the green dress under the light, the woman who had confessed her worst self to a stranger and been promised anonymity, and the stranger turned out to be the man whose book she'd secretly written, and the promise turned out to be marketing.

Then Rhea read his reply.

*You say you've been doing penance. I know something about penance. When I was fourteen, I burned down the bakery district — that's the story everyone tells, anyway, the story I stopped correcting because the truth required accusing my father, and a boy doesn't know how to do that. So I carried it. Thirty years. And I'll tell you what carrying it does: it makes you cruel in small ways, safe ways, ways you can pretend are wit—*

He crossed the stage and took the microphone out of her hand.

I will give Wendell Marsh this: he did it gently. He did it the way you take scissors from a child. "Thank you, Rhea," he said, and his voice was level, and only I, front row center where the light was, could see what it cost him — the muscle jumping in his jaw, the hand not quite steady around the mic. "That's enough research for one evening."

"But the audience—"

"Has heard quite enough of my private correspondence." He turned to the room. His eyes found mine and stayed there, and I could not read them, and I have never in my life wanted so badly to read something. "I'm sorry," he said — to the room, or to me, or to the boy in the letter, thirty years downstream of a fire. "There won't be a reading tonight."

He walked off the stage past me, close enough that I could have touched his sleeve, and did not stop, and the last thing I saw before the spotlight cut and the room erupted into the special murmur of scandal was the cream-colored folder lying open on the podium, my handwriting facing the ceiling like a confession already taken.

* * *

The ballroom doors had that heavy hotel hush, the kind that swallows footsteps, and I walked through them into the marble lobby with the specific gait of a woman determined not to run. Running confirms guilt. Walking confirms dignity. Neither confirmed anything true, but dignity travels better.

Behind me, the doors breathed open twice. Once for a waiter. Once for Wendell.

"Ivo."

"Don't." I kept walking. "Whatever sentence starts with my name right now, put it back in the box."

"You knew?" He fell into step beside me, and up close I could see the wreckage the level voice had been concealing. "Did you know it was me? The whole time? Was that — was I entertainment? Research?"

"You think I *knew*?" I stopped. Marble, chandelier, my own voice bouncing off all of it. "Wendell. If I had known I was writing to you — to *you* — do you think I'd have told you about my mother? About the bakery? About—" My voice did something humiliating in the middle of the sentence, a crack right through the load-bearing wall. "They read my letters, Wendell. They read *both* of us. To sell books."

"And you didn't know they'd do that."

"I'm a ghostwriter. I know exactly what this industry does. I just believed—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I believed the one anonymous thing would stay anonymous. That's the joke, isn't it? The heart can be trained like any muscle. Mine trained itself straight into a press release."

He flinched. At the muscle theory — his words, from Letter Three, though Rhea hadn't gotten that far, thank God, though God had clearly left the building around the lilies.

"The fire," I said. "The letter she read. Is it true?"

For a long moment he said nothing. A bellhop passed us pushing a luggage cart, giving us the wide berth locals give any scene involving two people standing very still.

"It's true that I stopped correcting the story," Wendell said finally. "Whether that makes it true is — Ivo, I have spent thirty years letting people believe I burned it down because the alternative was my father, and my father was—" He closed his mouth. Opened it. "Not here. Not in a lobby."

"Where, then?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He looked at me, and there it was again, the dissonance that had been growing since the orchard walk, since the packing shed, since every letter I'd reread at two in the morning trying to find the seam between the enemy and the stranger: two men standing in the same body, and I had loved one of them in ink and blamed the other in print, and they were the same man, and I had helped bury him twice.

"The publisher owns the letters," he said. "Legally. We signed releases with the guild — I checked, after. Rhea's within her rights." His laugh had no humor in it anywhere; it was purely structural. "She's going to serialize them. 'The Pen Pal Papers.' Chapter thirteen of the marketing plan. Your bakery, my fire, our — whatever we were. It's the hook of the season."

"Our what," I said, "were?"

"I don't know." And then, quieter, with the awful honesty of a man with nothing left to protect: "I know what I hoped. Which is worse."

Somewhere beyond the ballroom doors, Rhea's voice was rising above the murmur, turning catastrophe into content in real time — *an unprecedented look at the making of a modern memoir* — and I understood, standing there in the green dress under the chandelier, that the book I had spent eleven months setting straight had just been broken again, publicly, by the people who owned it. Both of them. Both of us.

"I have to go," I said.

"Ivo." He said it like the last stone in a wall. "What are you going to do?"

What was I going to do. Go home. Sit with my sister. Reread every letter I'd written and see, line by line, exactly what I'd handed them — and exactly who I'd handed it to. A boy who'd carried a fire that wasn't his. A man I'd spent a career describing as cold, calculating, the rival who'd buried my mother's bakery — a description I'd written into his own memoir, in his own voice, and charged by the word.

"I'm going to read my own mail," I said. "All of it. Starting with the parts that make me look worst."

"And after that?"

"After that," I said, "I think I owe a fourteen-year-old boy an apology thirty years late. And I think I'm going to have to start with the one in the mirror."

I pushed through the brass doors into the night, and behind me the ballroom was already telling the story without us — and for the first time in my life, I intended to take the pen back.
