## 1. The Muscle Theory

The orchid wall had cost eleven thousand dollars, and Noor Sandoval would have burned every bloom of it for a fraction of that price if it meant not hearing the string quartet's third attempt at the same Vivaldi movement while the bride's mother wept into her fourth glass of Krug.

"Third time they've played it," murmured Priya at her elbow, tablet glowing against the dark silk of her blazer. "Should I say something?"

"No." Noor didn't look away from the ballroom floor. "They'll get it right on the fourth. They always do."

She was right, of course. She was always right, which was less a gift than a discipline, the way a pianist's calluses are less a gift than a record of everything she'd practiced through. The quartet found the downbeat, the movement unfurled properly this time, and somewhere beneath the twelve-foot arrangements of white ranunculus — *not* peonies, never peonies in October, no matter what the bride's Pinterest board said — the wedding of Charlotte Vandermeer-Whitfield and Daniel Okonkwo proceeded exactly as Noor had designed it to proceed, down to the minute.

That was the trick nobody understood about her profession. People thought wedding planning was about flowers. It was about gravity. It was about knowing, three hours before it happened, that the groom's college roommate was going to drink too much and that the solution was not to cut him off but to give him a job — a tray to carry, a cousin to escort, a purpose small enough to hold him together. It was about understanding that every wedding was a controlled detonation, and her work was the wiring: precise, invisible, and load-bearing.

At 6:47 p.m., the doors of the Plaza's Terrace Room opened onto a scene so seamless that two guests asked, independently, whether a film crew had styled it. Four hundred candles. A suspended installation of glass orbs over the dance floor, each one holding a single stem of baby's breath, catching light like a slow-motion snowfall. Charlotte's train, bustled by Noor herself with two hidden pins after the photographer's assistant nearly stepped on it, fell in a perfect cascade.

"Mrs. Whitfield," Noor said, materializing beside the mother of the bride with the timing of a woman who had spent fifteen years learning where grief pooled in a room. "Your daughter looks like you did in eighty-nine. I found the photo in your rehearsal dinner slideshow. You were right to trust me with it."

Margaret Whitfield gripped her hand hard enough to hurt. "How do you *know* these things?"

"I don't know them," Noor said gently. "I plan for them."

Which was true, and also a lie, and also the entire architecture of her life.

* * *

Here is what Noor believed, though she'd never put it on a business card: the heart is a muscle. Not metaphorically — functionally. And like any muscle, it responded to training. You could teach it to lift heavier disappointments. You could teach it to endure longer silences. You could, with sufficient repetition, teach it not to reach for things at all.

She had learned this at twenty-six, standing in a kitchen in Brooklyn with a phone that wouldn't ring, teaching herself the first exercise: *do not check the door.* Then the second: *do not reread the messages.* By thirty she could attend a wedding — as a guest, once, catastrophically — and feel nothing but professional admiration for the lighting design. By thirty-five, the muscle was so well-trained that when her mother called to ask when Noor was going to let someone love her, Noor had laughed, actually laughed, and said, "Mom, I plan weddings. That's my relationship to love. I'm management."

Her mother hadn't laughed.

But that was the creed, and creeds exist because they work. Hearts are trainable. Grief is containable. Wanting is a habit, and habits can be broken the way any athlete breaks a bad one — through repetition, substitution, and the ruthless scheduling of one's attention elsewhere.

Noor's attention, these days, was scheduled to the quarter hour.

"Timeline check," Priya said, falling into step as Noor crossed behind the band. "Cocktail hour ends in nine minutes. The ice sculpture arrived with a chip on the left swan's wing — I handled it, rotated it toward the wall. Charlotte wants to do the surprise first-dance-reversal thing even though her dress can't—"

"It can," Noor said. "I had the seamstress reinforce the bodice in August. Tell her yes."

Priya stopped walking. "You reinforced the bodice in *August?*"

"She's been talking about that dance since the engagement party. She was going to do it regardless of what the dress allowed. My job was to make sure the dress agreed." Noor kept moving. "Also, tell the kitchen the vegan entrée count goes up by four. The Bergmans brought their son and his boyfriend; they're both plant-based, and Mrs. Bergman will make it a whole thing if he has to eat bread and a side salad."

"You clocked the Bergman boy's diet from across the room?"

"I clocked his mother watching him look at the menu like it was a verdict." Noor adjusted an orchid on the escort card table — one degree off axis, invisible to anyone but her — and kept her voice level. "It's not detective work, Priya. It's just paying attention."

"That's what's terrifying about you," Priya said cheerfully. "You think this is normal."

* * *

The cracks, though. Noor cataloged the cracks the way other people checked the weather — automatically, constantly, without deciding to.

There was the groom's father, standing too close to the bar at 7:15, laughing a half-beat too long at jokes that weren't funny. There was the bride's maid of honor, who had cried during the vows with an intensity that had nothing to do with joy and everything to do with a history Noor had clocked in the first ten minutes of their planning meeting — the way Charlotte had said *she's like a sister to me* while looking anywhere but at her. There was the couple themselves, sweet and genuine and already practicing the small divorces of long marriage: he checked his phone during her toast to her parents; she corrected his pronunciation of her grandmother's name without touching him.

None of it meant anything. That was the point. Every marriage contained these fractures the way every old building contained settling — hairline, structural, survivable. Love wasn't the absence of cracks. Love was a maintenance contract.

Or so Noor had come to believe, in the years since the alternative belief — that love was a thing that could simply be *kept*, whole, unbroken, chosen daily by two people who told each other the truth — had been taken from her and replaced, quietly, without her consent, with this more durable model.

She stood at the edge of the dance floor during the first dance and watched Charlotte spin out of Daniel's arms and back in, the reinforced bodice holding, the crowd gasping at the reversal, and Noor felt the thing she always felt at first dances: a tightness under the sternum that she had long ago classified as indigestion or fatigue or the espresso she shouldn't have had at four p.m.

The muscle held. The muscle always held. That was what training was for.

* * *

"You know what I've never seen?" Priya said later, at 11:40 p.m., as the cake was cut and the band moved into the set that would carry the night past midnight. "You at a reception. Actually *at* one. Eating the food. Dancing. Sitting down."

"I sit down."

"You sit down *here*—" Priya pointed at the service corridor, the catering office, the bank of chairs behind the production table where vendors ate standing up, "—which is not a reception. It's mission control. Ten years I've worked with you. One hundred and forty-one weddings. I have never seen you on a dance floor. I've barely seen you eat a canapé that wasn't quality control."

"Somebody has to run the show."

"The show runs itself for twenty minutes at a time and you know it. You built it to." Priya popped a stolen macaron into her mouth and studied Noor with the frank, affectionate insolence that was the reason Noor kept her. "You know what I think? I think you're afraid that if you stop working for one song, the whole machine stops. I think you think the music only stays good if you're conducting."

"Your macaron is stolen from the dessert display and Mrs. Whitfield will notice the gap."

"Deflection noted and logged." Priya grinned. "One hundred and forty-two, by the way. This makes one hundred and forty-two."

"What does?"

"Weddings. You've planned one hundred and forty-two weddings and attended zero receptions. That's a statistic, Noor. That's a *paper*. Somebody should study you."

Noor almost smiled. It was a real almost — one of the ones Priya earned maybe twice a year. "Somebody did study me, once. He said I treated love like a logistics problem."

"What happened to him?"

The question landed in the corridor between them, harmless on its face, and Noor felt the muscle do what it had been trained to do: nothing. A clean, total nothing, the way a hand doesn't flinch from a flame it learned about decades ago.

"He got married," she said. "Not to me. I sent a gift. It was very well reviewed."

Priya laughed, and Noor let her, and the moment passed through the corridor and out through the loading dock like all such moments did — cataloged, contained, filed under *handled.*

* * *

By 1:20 a.m., the last of the guests had been funneled into cars, the grandparents dispatched with warm blankets and pre-tipped drivers, the leftover cake routed to the overnight kitchen staff with instructions that would make it look spontaneous. Margaret Whitfield hugged Noor at the door and held on a beat too long, the way mothers of brides always did, as if Noor were the last person still holding some piece of the day together and letting go might unravel it.

"You gave us a perfect evening," Margaret whispered.

Noor performed the smile she kept for this exact sentence. "You gave yourselves the marriage. I just did the lighting."

In the car, Priya fell asleep against the window within six blocks, and Noor sat in the dark of the city sliding past and did the accounting she did after every wedding — not the financial one, which her firm's software handled flawlessly, but the other one. The crack ledger.

*Groom's father: watch the drinking. Maid of honor: unresolved history, will surface by anniversary two. Bride: perfectionist anxiety, will need reassurance texts for the first month; schedule them. Couple: fundamentally sound. Fundamentally sound,* she wrote in her mind, and the words should have been neutral, clinical, the highest grade her ledger issued, and yet they sat in her chest with a strange weight, like a verdict delivered about someone else's case.

One hundred and forty-two weddings. She knew the divorce rate of her client roster better than most divorce attorneys did — not because she tracked it vindictively, but because former clients called her, sometimes, years later, in the wreckage, and she took those calls, and she was kind on them, and she never once said *I saw it coming,* even though she always had.

That was the part Priya didn't understand, would probably never understand, because Noor had built her armor too smoothly for X-rays. It wasn't that Noor didn't believe in love. It was that she believed in it the way a structural engineer believes in bridges: with complete technical respect, and a private, unspeakable knowledge of exactly how they fail.

And hers had failed. Not the way marriages in her ledger failed — slowly, in installments, with warning signs she could now read at a glance. Hers had failed in one night, without explanation, with a silence so complete it had taught her everything she knew about containment. Someone had walked out of her life and left no crack at all, no fault line she could point to, and a woman cannot maintain what she cannot see. So she had done the only rational thing. She had stopped believing in buildings. She had gone into the business of building them for other people instead, and inspecting them constantly, and it was fine. It was better than fine. It was a career, a reputation, a life with clean lines and no leaks.

The car turned onto her street. Rain had started somewhere around midnight, fine and cold, silvering the windshield.

Her phone buzzed. An email, arriving at 1:47 a.m., which meant either a crisis or an opportunity, and Noor had learned long ago that the two wore the same clothes.

She glanced down expecting a venue fire, a vendor collapse, a bride in tears.

Instead: a commission inquiry. Bellamy Vineyard, somewhere upstate in wine country she'd never worked. A destination wedding, full production, a fee with enough zeros to make her breath catch — and a note from the family that read less like a client brief than a plea. *We are putting everything we have into this season. We need someone who can save us.*

Noor stared at it in the dark car, rain ticking on the roof, Priya breathing softly against the window.

She should delete it. She was fully booked through spring; she'd earned the right to be. But the word *save* snagged on something in her, and the zeros snagged on something else, and underneath both — faint, deniable, the kind of thing she'd have denied under oath — was the oldest pull she knew: the wish to stand in a place where things grew whether you wanted them to or not, and see what happened if she stayed long enough to watch.

She forwarded the email to her work address, thumb moving before the trained muscle could object.

Then she sat back, closed her eyes, and let the city carry her home through the rain.
