## 10. The Songbook

The wings of the bandstand smelled of cut grass and old varnish, and Ada Kavanagh stood in them with her heart going like a metronome set to a tempo no orchestra could follow.

She had heard the letter.

Not read — heard. Wendell Osei had walked onto the stage during intermission with a folded page in his hand and his voice shaking in a way she had never once heard it shake in all the years he had been the steadiest person she knew, and he had read eleven years of silence aloud to two thousand people. *I wrote this the night you left,* he had said. *It was never sent. That failure is mine, and I am done hiding behind other people's words.* And then the letter itself — every line of it, the clumsy beautiful boyish lines about her father's kitchen and the rain on the windows and how he would wait, how he would always wait, how if silence was her answer then silence was what he would learn to live inside, though he did not think he would live well.

Two thousand people had held their breath with her. Somewhere in the dark, Marcus Hale sat very still in the front row, his memoir stacked on the table beside him like a defendant's alibi.

And now the stage manager was looking at her, and the microphone was waiting, and the songbook — her songbook, the one she had carried across an ocean and through three apartments and one marriage that never quite happened — was open in her hands.

"Whenever you're ready," the stage manager said gently.

Ada thought: I have been ready for eleven years. I have been ready since the night I packed my father's ultimatum into a suitcase along with my sheet music and left this town believing that quiet meant no.

She walked out into the light.

* * *

The bandstand at centennial festivals is a generous instrument. She had learned that as a girl — learned that the wooden floor carried sound up through your shoes, that the curved shell behind the players returned your own music to you half a second late, so that singing there felt like singing with a ghost of yourself. Tonight the ghost was welcome. Tonight she intended to introduce herself to it properly.

"I'm Ada Kavanagh," she said, and her voice found the microphone and steadied. "Most of you know me as the girl who left. Some of you know me as the music teacher who came back. I'd like to tell you what I actually am, which is someone who has been writing letters for eleven years and sending them to no one."

A ripple went through the crowd — not laughter, not quite. Recognition.

"The first one I wrote the night I got on the train." She lifted the guitar from its stand, settled the strap across her shoulder the way she had settled it ten thousand times in empty rooms. "My father told me that night that I could have the job in Vienna or I could have this town, but holding both was greed, and greed was how mothers like mine lost everything. He was wrong about nearly everything, my father, but he was loud, and I was twenty-three, and loud wins when you're twenty-three. So I chose Vienna. And I wrote a song on the train because I couldn't write anything else."

She played the opening chords — four notes, descending, like stairs going down into a cellar where something had been stored a long time.

The song had no title. In her notebook it was dated and nothing else, the way grief gets filed. It was a young woman's song, and she sang it as a young woman now, letting her voice climb back down the years to find the pitch it had that night: furious and frightened and certain she was doing the bravest thing of her life by leaving without telling him. There was a verse about a letter she was waiting for. *If he writes, I'll turn around at the border. If he writes, I'll come home.* She had sung that verse alone in Viennese practice rooms, in a flat above a bakery in Salzburg, in a hospital corridor the winter her father died — sung it to a silence that never once answered, because the answer had been sitting in a box in Marcus Hale's archive the whole time, postmarked nowhere, addressed to her, held hostage by a man who called it protection.

When the last chord faded, the crowd made the sound crowds make when they understand they've been let inside something private. Ada let it move through her and out.

"That was the question," she said. "Eleven years ago I asked it every day. Here's the answer I finally got tonight, from a man who read his own failure out loud in front of everyone he knows." Her throat tightened; she let it. "So this next one is new. I finished it about four hours ago, in the green room, on the back of a set list. It's the only honest thing I've ever written quickly."

* * *

The second song began where the first one ended — same four notes, but rising now, inverted, the cellar stairs climbed back up. She had written the melody before the words, which was how she always worked, and the melody had arrived whole the moment Wendell's voice broke on the line *I would have followed you to Vienna if I'd known you wanted following.*

The words came slower, between sets of tuning pegs and sips of cold coffee:

*I kept your silence like a room I furnished badly.*
*I put a chair in it for every year.*
*But a letter isn't silence, love, it's just a door nobody opened —*
*and doors don't mean the house wasn't here.*

She sang it looking straight out past the lights, not searching for his face because she didn't need to. She knew exactly where he stood: stage left, just past the curtain, in the spot where he had stood at seventeen watching her rehearse and pretending to read a book. Some things were muscle memory. Some things were truer than memory.

By the second verse people were crying openly, and by the bridge they were singing the refrain back to her — the festival committee, the flower sellers, old Mrs. Adeyemi who had taught them both recorder in primary school — hundreds of voices carrying her own words back to her through the bandstand's curved shell, so that for the first time in eleven years Ada Kavanagh heard her music return to her and it sounded like company instead of echo.

The last line she sang alone, almost spoken:

*Come stand where I can see you. I'm done with songs for ghosts.*

Then she put down the guitar, and stepped away from the microphone, and looked directly at the place where she knew he was standing.

He came out of the wings like a man walking into deep water — slowly, visibly deciding, at each step, to take the next one. The crowd had begun, somewhere, to applaud, but Ada barely heard it, because Wendell reached her at center stage and stopped, and up close she could see that reading the letter had cost him something visible: his eyes red-rimmed, his hands still trembling faintly at his sides, the borrowed composure finally, completely gone.

"You heard it," he said. "All of it."

"Every word." She took his hand. His fingers were cold; hers weren't. "You know what's funny? I spent twelve years thinking you were the brave one. The one who stayed, who built things, who faced people. And you spent twelve years thinking I was. We were both hiding, Wendell. You behind other people's stories. Me behind other countries."

"Terrible hiders," he said. "We hid in plain sight of each other the entire time."

"We did." She squeezed his hand. "I'm not asking for the rest of your life. I'm asking for tomorrow morning. Coffee. The bad café on Mill Lane, the one that's survived three renovations by being too stubborn to close. And the morning after that, if the coffee goes well. And we keep going like that until neither of us remembers why small steps ever seemed safe."

Wendell laughed — wet-eyed, unguarded, entirely his own laugh, no author behind it. "That's the most romantic proposal I've ever heard."

"It's a first draft," Ada said. "I'll revise."

* * *

They closed the festival together, the two of them, with the whole town singing the old centennial anthem off-key and delighted about it. Marcus shook Wendell's hand afterward in the shadow of the bandstand, said something brief about the true chapter being "better than mine, damn him," and meant it, mostly. The memoir would go out with both versions printed side by side — the polished lie and the plain truth — and readers could choose, which was more than most books offered and more than Marcus deserved and exactly what the story required.

Later, when the chairs were stacked and the string lights were the only light left, Ada sat at the bandstand's old upright piano with the fallboard open and the damp night air settling into the strings, and played without playing anything in particular — just wandering, the way she did when a new piece was assembling itself somewhere below her hands.

Wendell sat beside her on the bench. Not touching. Just there, the way he used to be, the way he apparently had always intended to be.

"What are you working on?" he asked.

"I don't know yet. Something with two voices, maybe." She glanced at him. "A duet. I've written eleven years of solos. I think I'm finished with those."

"I could write words for it," he said carefully. "If you wanted. My own name on them this time."

"Wendell Osei, published under Wendell Osei." She played a chord, considered it, changed one note. "Whatever will the neighbors say."

"They'll say it took you long enough," he said. "Everyone in that audience has known since we were nineteen."

"Everyone except us."

"Especially us. We were the last to know anything, including what happened the night you left."

Ada's hands stilled on the keys. Outside the bandstand, the town was emptying toward home — car doors, laughter, someone's dog — and the night felt less like an ending than like a page turned, the way a good book turns: reluctantly, promisingly.

"Play it again," Wendell said quietly. "The second song. The one from the green room. I want to hear it when there's no one listening but me."

"There will be a hundred more after it," Ada warned him. "I have eleven years of material and I intend to spend all of it."

"Good," he said. "I have twelve. We'll trade."

And she began to play — the four rising notes, then the rest, her shoulder warm against his, the unfinished song filling the emptied bandstand like a promise that had finally, at long last, found its addressee.
