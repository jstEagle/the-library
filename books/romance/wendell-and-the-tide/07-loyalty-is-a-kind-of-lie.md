## 7. Loyalty Is a Kind of Lie

The folder sat on my kitchen table for four days before I could touch it again.

I'd carried it everywhere—out to the rows, up to the cider barn, down to the creek where the water ran low and clear over stones the color of old coins. I carried it the way you carry a wound you haven't decided how to treat. Under my arm at first, then against my chest when the wind picked up, then finally pressed flat between two boards in the barn loft because I couldn't stand the weight of it near me and couldn't stand to let it out of my sight.

Four days. The peaches didn't care. They ripened on schedule, heavy and golden, splitting their skins with sweetness while I walked the orchard like a man pacing a sickroom. The grapes came in dark and dusty-bloomed, and the pickers arrived with their ladders and their laughter, and the whole valley smelled of sugar and fermentation and the particular green rot of fallen fruit left too long in the grass. Life went on being abundant around me while I turned one question over like a stone in my pocket:

Tell her, or don't.

Here is what the folder held: photocopies from the county records office, three towns over, where a clerk with kind eyes and a slow afternoon had let me sit with the deed books until closing. The original Marsh deed, signed by my father in his careful draftsman's hand. And beneath it, dated two years after his death, an amendment transferring half ownership into a trust—the Crosswood Trust, registered to a law office in the city, a law office whose letterhead I now knew by heart because Gideon's name appeared on enough of Vivian Cross's old correspondence that the connection was undeniable.

Vivian Cross had bought her way into my family's land. Had engineered a legal quirk so precise it could only be deliberate—so that if Gideon fled, as everyone apparently expected her to flee, her half would forfeit back to the trust. A trap baited with my mother's orchard, sprung on a woman who hadn't set foot in this county in fifteen years.

And there was more. There was always more. A second document, older, creased soft as cloth from handling: a letter on that same letterhead, unsigned but initialed V.C., referencing "the Marsh amendment" and "our mutual friend's cooperation." Dated eleven years ago. My father was still alive eleven years ago. My father, who burned Gideon's letters, who kept his ledgers locked, who died with his affairs so tidy the lawyer had nothing to do but read them aloud.

I knew what *cooperation* meant. I didn't have proof yet. But I knew.

* * *

I found her at the far end of the east row, past the Gravensteins, where the trees grew old and crooked and dropped their fruit early. She was on a ladder with a picking bag slung across her hip, her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hair escaping its tie in copper threads that caught the late light. She'd gotten good at this. Three weeks of shared labor will do that—teach your hands a new language. She picked with a twist of the wrist she'd learned from watching me, gentle, so the stem stayed on and the bruise stayed off.

She looked like she belonged here. That was the cruelest part. That was what made my hands shake on the folder.

"Wendell." She didn't climb down. She looked at me over her shoulder, and something in my face must have told her, because she came down slow, rung by rung, setting the ladder aside with the care of someone buying time. "What is it?"

I held out the folder. It seemed important, suddenly, that I not open it for her. That she see the pages herself, in her own order, at her own speed.

She wiped her hands on her thighs—a habit, I'd learned, that meant she was steadying herself—and took it.

I watched her read. I watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out: first confusion, then recognition, then the particular gray that comes when a person understands they've been known all along by someone who wished them harm. Her thumb stopped on the letter. On the date. On the initials.

"She bought the deed," Gideon said quietly. "Two years ago. Before I ever got those papers."

"Before the train schedule. Before any of it."

"She wanted—" Gideon laughed, one short, ugly note with no humor in it. "She wanted me to run. That's all this ever was. Sign and run, or stay and forfeit. Either way the orchard goes to her, and I go back to being the woman who ruined herself for nothing."

"That's what I needed to know," I said. "Whether you'd run."

Her head came up fast. "Wendell—"

"I've been carrying this for four days." My voice came out lower than I intended, rough as bark. "Four days of deciding whether to hand it to you or burn it. Because here's what I know about you, Gideon Vane. You build walls faster than anyone I've ever met. You apologize for existing. You sleep with your shoes by the door like you're expecting to be evicted from your own life. And I thought—if I show her this, she'll understand that none of it was her fault, and she'll finally stop flinching. Or she'll pack a bag tonight and I'll never get her past the property line again."

The orchard was very quiet around us. Somewhere behind the barns the pickers had knocked off; I could hear the far-off clatter of ladders being stacked, voices thinning toward town.

"So which is it?" she asked. Her chin was up but her eyes were wet, and she hated that they were wet—I could see her hating it. "Which version did you decide I was?"

"I decided I'd rather lose you to the truth than keep you with a lie."

Something moved through her face then—not softening exactly. More like a door being unbarred from the inside. She looked down at the folder again, and when she spoke, her voice had gone flat and precise, the voice I imagined she used on vendors and venues, the voice that had once run the most sought-after wedding planning business on the coast.

"Then we take it to the bar association. To the press. We file everything. She forged client accounts, Wendell—she let me carry the felony while she retired to a corner office and a reputation. If she's committing fraud against your family too, then she's committed it twice, and twice is a pattern, and patterns are provable."

"Yes."

"We do it together. We do it right. Statements, timelines, certified copies—"

"Gideon." I said her name and she stopped. "There's one more thing."

I almost didn't say it. God help me, I stood there in the long gold light with the folder between us and I weighed it one last time—my father's good name against her freedom—and I heard myself say, in my grandfather's cadence, in the voice of every Marsh man who ever chose the land over the truth: *there's one more thing.*

"The letter. The one about cooperation. It's dated eleven years ago. My father was alive."

I watched her arrive at it. Watched her do the arithmetic I'd done four days ago in the records office with dust on my sleeves and my heart going like a hammer.

"No," she said.

"It's not proof. It's an initial. A reference. It could mean anything."

"You don't believe that."

"No," I admitted. "I don't."

She closed the folder. Held it against her sternum the way I'd carried it, and I understood that we were holding the same wound now, hers and mine, and that knowing it didn't make it lighter.

"My father helped her," I said. "That's what you're thinking. That's what the letter says, if letters can be said to say things."

"Your father burned my letters, Wendell. Fifteen years of them. I thought he hated me. I built a whole person out of that hate—built you out of it too, the boy who didn't write back. And now you're telling me he might have been sitting at Vivian Cross's table the whole time?" Her breath shuddered. "Then why? Why burn the letters? Why keep me away?"

"Maybe because keeping you away was the point. Maybe the amendment needed you gone and angry and never coming back, and my father—" My throat closed. I pushed through it. "My father loved this land more than he loved anything, including the truth. Including me, some days. I can't tell you what he did or didn't do. All I can tell you is what the paper says."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she handed the folder back to me, gently, the way you return something borrowed.

"I'm going to the city," she said. "Tomorrow. Alone."

"Alone?"

"Vivian and I have fifteen years of unfinished business, and none of it involves you. If I walk into that office with a Marsh beside me, she'll know we're united, and united people negotiate. Divided people—" she smiled, and it was the old sharp smile, the one from before, from the girl who left, "—divided people underestimate each other. Let her underestimate me. She's been doing it for years. It's how she got caught."

"And if she offers you something? If she offers you your name back, clean, in exchange for walking away from all of it?"

"Then I'll consider it," she said, "and I'll come home and tell you, and you'll hate me for considering it, and we'll fight about it, and eventually you'll forgive me, because that's what people do when they love each other. Isn't it?"

It wasn't a question. It was a test, and we both knew it, and I failed it, because what I said was:

"Don't go alone."

"Wendell—"

"Don't. Go. Alone." I stepped closer, close enough to smell the orchard on her—bruised peach and green apple and sweat and sun. "You want to talk about walls? Fine. Here's mine. Every person I've ever loved has walked out that gate and not come back. My mother died in the house I was standing in. My father died at a desk with his lies arranged neatly around him. And you—you left when we were seventeen and you left again every morning since you got here, a little, in your head, with your shoes by the door. So no. I'm not letting you walk into Vivian Cross's office by yourself, because I have spent my whole life learning what happens to people who go alone, and it is this. It is folders on kitchen tables. It is letters in the fire. It is standing in an orchard at dusk holding proof that everyone you trusted was lying, and having no one to hand it to."

The words came out of me like blood from a cut—too much, too fast, and I couldn't stop them, and I saw them land on her one by one like stones.

Gideon went very still.

"You think that's love," she said softly. "Keeping me. Fencing me. Deciding what I can carry and who I can face." She shook her head slowly. "Wendell. I spent three years being managed. Being handled. Being told by lawyers and publicists and one very kind, very useless therapist that I shouldn't be alone with my own life. Vivian Cross built a cage out of exactly that instinct—out of people deciding what was best for me without asking. And you are standing in front of me doing it with better intentions and worse grammar."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. None of this is fair. Your father may have sold my exile to the woman who ruined me. My mentor bought your mother's orchard like a chess move. And you—" her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it, "—you sat on this for four days. Four days, Wendell. Deciding whether I got to know my own life."

"I was trying to protect you."

"I know." And horribly, terribly, she did look protected, in that moment—wrapped in his certainty like a coat two sizes too small, suffocating politely. "That's the problem. That's always been the problem. Everyone protects me right into the ground."

She turned and walked toward the farmhouse, folder under her arm, spine straight, and the light went with her, draining out of the row until the trees stood gray and anonymous.

I called her name. Once. Twice. The second time it broke in the middle.

At the gate she stopped. Didn't turn around. Just stood there with her hand on the latch, silhouetted against the last of the sky, and said, so quietly I almost lost it to the wind:

"I'll be back before the festival. Whatever happens in that office—whatever she offers, whatever she threatens—I'm coming back. Not because you asked. Because I'm done running. But you should know that tonight, when you lie awake deciding whether you were brave or a coward, the answer is both. It's usually both."

The latch clicked. The gate swung. She was gone, and the orchard stood empty around me, full of ripening fruit nobody was picking, and somewhere far off the tide was coming in over the flats, patient and enormous, taking back what it was owed.

I stood at the property line until dark. Then I went inside, and I took the folder out of the drawer where I'd hidden it, and I sat with it under the kitchen lamp, and I made a decision of my own.

If she wouldn't let me come with her—

Then I'd follow anyway.
