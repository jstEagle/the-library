## 7. Arrivals

The Okafor compound did not have a gate so much as a threshold of judgment. The wrought-iron gates had been replaced years ago by a pair of sliding metal panels painted the green of old naira notes, and beyond them the driveway curved past a bougainvillea hedge that my aunt Chiamaka trimmed into submission twice a year, as if even the flowers in this family were expected to behave.

I hadn't been back in four years. The compound remembered me anyway. It remembered me in the way of all family homes: not with warmth exactly, but with an inventory. Every crack in the driveway tiles was a year I'd been away. The new security post was the year Mama died. The second story added to the east wing was the year Daniel and I called off the wedding and I moved to London and stopped answering calls from numbers that began with 0803.

"Your palms are sweating," Theo said beside me.

"That's not sweat. That's anticipation."

"It's sweat."

The taxi pulled up under the porte-cochère and a small army materialized — cousins, half-cousins, a cousin's husband whose name I'd never learned, two aunties whose faces I knew but whose titles shifted depending on who had offended whom most recently. Doors opened before we could touch them. Luggage vanished from the boot as if by sleight of hand. And then we were inside the current, being pulled toward the veranda where the elders sat in a semicircle of plastic-and-wicker chairs arranged with the precision of a state function.

I felt Theo go still beside me. Not frozen — he wasn't a man who froze — but stilling, the way he must have stilled a thousand times over proofing dough, waiting to see what the heat would do.

"Okay?" I murmured.

"Define okay."

"We're about to be introduced to eleven people who will remember everything about you forever and forgive nothing."

"So a normal Saturday."

Despite everything — the dread sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone — I almost laughed.

* * *

Aunt Chiamaka rose first. She was wearing gold. Of course she was wearing gold; Chiamaka considered funerals, weddings, and reunions to be the same category of event, differentiated only by degree of embroidery. She embraced me with both arms, held me at arm's length, and looked me over the way she'd once looked over caterers' samples: thoroughly, and with visible reservations.

"Anselm." Only Chiamaka used my full name, and only when she wanted me to feel it like a ruler across knuckles. "Four years."

"Auntie."

"You look thin. London is not feeding you?"

"London feeds me very well."

"And this is him." Her gaze slid to Theo with the smooth inevitability of a searchlight. "The one on the phone. The one you said—" She paused, recalibrating, because whatever I'd said on the phone had clearly been less than the full truth, and Chiamaka could smell an omission the way sharks smell blood in water at concentrations no scientist can explain. "The baker."

"Theo Marsh," Theo said, and put out his hand.

Chiamaka looked at the hand. Then she ignored it and pulled him into an embrace instead, which was its own kind of test — you could tell everything about a man by how he received an ambush of affection. Theo took it well. He didn't stiffen, didn't pat her back like he was putting out a fire. He just held on for the appropriate three seconds and stepped back with his hand still half-extended, now offered to nobody, and turned the gesture smoothly into a gesture toward his bag.

"It's good to be here," he said. "Thank you for having me."

"He has manners," Chiamaka announced to the veranda at large, as though reporting a scientific finding. "Anselm, where did you find a man with manners? Certainly not in London."

"Lagos, actually," Theo said mildly. "Heathrow. Long story."

Chiamaka's eyebrows performed a small, interested dance. *Long story* was catnip to her. I watched her file it away for later interrogation and steered us onward before she could begin the extraction.

The introductions came in a blur. Cousin Nnamdi, who ran a logistics company and greeted Theo with a handshake so firm I worried for his metacarpals. Cousin Adaeze, who was twenty-four and filmed everything, and who was already filming us. Uncle Emeka, who asked Theo what he thought of the current government and then, before Theo could answer, answered himself for four minutes. Aunty Ngozi, who pinched Theo's cheek and declared him "fresh from abroad" in a tone that made it unclear whether this was praise or diagnosis.

And through all of it, Theo kept his hand at the small of my back. Not gripping. Just there. A warm point of contact, steady as a metronome, and every time the crowd pressed too close or a question landed wrong, my spine found it and straightened.

We'd rehearsed none of this. In the bakery, over burnt sourdough and a whiteboard of rules, we'd covered origin stories and hand-holding and the correct answer to *how did you meet*. We had not covered how it would feel to have his palm settle against my back like it belonged there, unbidden, while twelve relatives watched.

It felt like shelter. That was the problem. Shelter was not in the contract.

* * *

My father received us last, which was deliberate. Papa believed in entrances the way other men believed in scripture.

He stood at the top of the veranda steps in a kaftan the color of deep water, hands clasped behind his back, and let the crowd part around us as we approached. He was smaller than I remembered — age had been quietly sanding him down for years — but his presence hadn't shrunk with him. Papa had once filled rooms without raising his voice. He still did.

"Papa." I kissed his cheeks. He permitted it the way he permitted rain.

"Anselm." My name in his mouth sounded like a document awaiting signature. His eyes went to Theo and performed a slow, complete assessment — face, posture, shoes, the calluses on his hands, which Papa noticed because Papa noticed hands; he'd been a civil engineer for forty years and trusted hands more than words. "So. You are the baker."

"Yes, sir. Theo Marsh."

"Baking is honest work." A pause, calibrated. "My daughter plans weddings."

"I know, sir. She's very good at it."

"She is very good at many things." Another pause, longer, and I felt the sentence underneath the sentence, the one that had been hanging between us since the wedding that wasn't: *she is very good at many things, and yet.* "You will eat well this weekend. The kitchen starts at five."

With that he turned and went inside, and the audience dispersed, and I exhaled for what felt like the first time since the tarmac.

"Well," Theo said.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were going to say something encouraging and reasonable and I don't have the reserves for it right now."

He considered this with the seriousness he gave to oven temperatures. "Then I'll say nothing," he decided, "and get you a drink."

* * *

The drink was a Star lager, sweating on a coaster, delivered to my childhood bedroom where I'd fled under the pretense of unpacking. Theo leaned in the doorway, holding his own bottle, watching me fold and refold the same shirt.

"This room hasn't changed," he said.

"It's a museum. Mama curated it. Everything stays exactly as I left it at nineteen, including the posters, including the terrible poetry notebook, which is hidden under the mattress if you value our relationship do not look for it."

"I would never." A beat. "Where under the mattress?"

"Theo."

"Sorry. Force of habit. I'm a curious person. It's why the bread works."

I sat down on the bed, which was still made with hospital corners though no one had slept in it in years, and looked at the ceiling fan, which clicked on its third rotation the way it always had, a sound I'd forgotten I knew. Four years away and my body slotted back into this room like a key into a lock it had cut itself.

"Do you want the layout of the battlefield?" I said.

"Absolutely."

"Dinner tonight is the welcome dinner. Formal-ish. That's where Aunt Chiamaka conducts her interrogations — she'll ask you about your intentions, your family, your finances, and your opinion of me, probably in that order, possibly all in one question. Tomorrow there's the family photo, which lasts ninety minutes and has ended friendships. Tomorrow night is talent night, God help us both. Sunday there's church, then the send-off lunch, then freedom."

"And your father?"

"My father doesn't interrogate. He states conclusions and waits for you to disprove them. It's worse."

"Noted." He came and sat beside me on the bed, at a careful distance, though there was no one to perform for here — and that was the strange thing, that he kept the distance anyway, respectful of some line we'd drawn on a whiteboard in a bakery basement. "And the ex?"

I went still. "What about him?"

"Adaeze mentioned him. Filming, obviously. She said, and I quote, 'wait until you meet Daniel, this is going to be content.'" He said it lightly, but his eyes were on me, and they weren't light at all. "Is he coming?"

"He lives in Abuja. He wouldn't—"

The sound reached us before the name did. A car horn, two short blasts, cheerful and entitled, and beneath the window a silver SUV gleamed in the drive, and a man got out of it in a linen shirt worth more than my first car, and beside him slid a woman in yellow, tall and laughing, and she took his arm as they came up the path.

Daniel. Daniel was here. Daniel, who had been told — I had been assured by three separate cousins — that he was not attending. Daniel, who had ended our engagement eleven days before the wedding with a speech about timing and growth that I had memorized against my will and could still recite like a psalm.

Theo followed my gaze to the window. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

"He wasn't supposed to be here," I said. My voice came out flat, which was how I knew how bad it was. Flat was my armor voice. "Chiamaka told me—"

"Maybe she forgot."

"Chiamaka forgets nothing. She remembers what everyone ate at Easter in 2011. She didn't forget. She *invited* him." I laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Of course she did. What's a reunion without a little theatre? What's my life without an audience?"

Down in the drive, Daniel hugged Chiamaka, who beamed at him with open delight, and I understood the whole architecture of the weekend in a single image: I had been invited home not to be welcomed but to be exhibited. The daughter who left. The engagement that failed. And now the before-and-after, side by side, for the family's edification. Daniel with his beautiful new wife, and me with my rented boyfriend, and everyone free to draw their own conclusions about which of us had aged better.

Something hot moved up through my chest, and I recognized it with surprise — not hurt, actually. Hurt I knew how to carry. This was anger, and anger was newer, and stranger, and harder to sit still under.

"Hey." Theo's voice, low. "Look at me."

I looked at him.

"We can leave," he said. "Right now. We'll invent a catering emergency. I'm a baker; nobody questions a baking emergency. We'll be on a train by dark and they can compare notes about us without us."

"We can't leave. If we leave, it confirms everything. It confirms I run. It confirms I couldn't even do one weekend with—" I stopped.

*With someone real,* I almost said, and swallowed it.

Theo watched me not finish the sentence, and something passed over his face — I couldn't name it, then or later, something between understanding and a door closing gently — and then he stood up and drained his beer and set the bottle down with a soft click.

"Okay," he said. "Then we stay. And Ansel — when we walk down those stairs, and they're all standing there with their phones out, you don't have to perform anything. You just hold on to me and walk. Can you do that?"

"Hold on and walk."

"That's the whole job. I've seen you seat two hundred guests with a seating chart and a glare. You can manage a staircase."

* * *

We managed the staircase.

Daniel saw us before we reached the bottom — of course he did; he'd always had an instinct for entrances, it was one of the things I'd once mistaken for charisma — and his face did the thing faces do: a flicker of surprise, quickly lacquered into warmth. "Anselm Okafor," he called, arms opening. "There she is. Four years and you look exactly the same."

"Liar," I said pleasantly, and let him hug me, and counted the seconds — one, two — before stepping back. Up close he smelled the same, that cedar cologne, and it was obscene how memory works, how a smell can put you back in a kitchen at midnight discussing table linens with a man who was already, somewhere inside himself, halfway gone.

"Amara, this is Anselm. Anselm, Amara." His wife's smile was genuine, I think, or as genuine as anything can be at a family reunion where you've been introduced primarily as *the ex*. She had a firm handshake and kind eyes and I hated her a little and then hated myself for hating her, because she'd done nothing except marry a man I no longer wanted, which was, when I thought about it clearly, a public service.

"And this is Theo," I said. "My boyfriend."

The word came out steady. I was almost proud of it.

Daniel shook Theo's hand. I watched them take each other's measure — Daniel with his easy, practiced charm, Theo with his quiet, unhurried attention — and I braced for Theo to shrink, the way people sometimes did in front of Daniel's polish, in front of the linen shirt and the SUV and the wife.

He didn't shrink. He held the handshake a half-second past comfortable and said, "Congratulations on your marriage," in a voice so level and so sincere that it made the words impossible to read, and Daniel's smile tightened by a single millimeter, and I thought: *oh.*

Oh, he's good.

Dinner was announced — the long tables under the canopy, the steam rising off jollof rice in trays the size of coffins — and the crowd began to move, and in the shuffle of it, in the noise and the jostling and Chiamaka's voice conducting everyone toward their seats, I lost Theo for maybe ten seconds. Ten seconds, surrounded by cousins, Daniel at my elbow saying something about London traffic, Amara laughing, Adaeze's phone drifting toward us like a drone.

And then Theo's hand found mine.

Not grabbed. Found. Slid into it through the crowd with the unerring certainty of a man reaching for a tool he'd used ten thousand times, fingers settling between mine, thumb resting along my knuckles, and he didn't look at me when he did it, didn't make it a moment, just kept talking to Nnamdi about supply chains as if his hand hadn't just done the bravest thing anyone had done in this compound in four years.

I looked down at our hands. His were flour-rough and warm. Mine had gone cold without my noticing, and were going warm again now, and I thought: *this isn't in the rules. Rule six, no real feelings. Nobody said anything about real hands.*

"Anselm!" Chiamaka's voice rang out across the courtyard. "You're seated at table one. Beside me. And Theo beside you — I have *questions*, young man. So many questions."

Theo squeezed my hand once, brief and grounding, a pilot's tap before turbulence.

"Ready?" he murmured.

"No," I said honestly.

"Good," he said. "Honesty. It's going to confuse the hell out of them."

And he walked me toward table one, toward the gold embroidery and the long interrogation, holding on.
