## 9. What Mama Left

My father's study smelled the way it had always smelled: old paper, furniture polish, and the faint ghost of cigar smoke from a man who quit smoking twenty years ago but kept the scent as a kind of signature. Chief Emeka Okafor did not decorate rooms; he preserved them, like a man keeping a museum of his own authority.

He'd sent Chinedu to fetch me—"Baba says come now, he's waiting"—which was how my father summoned everyone, as though his time were a train that would not wait and we were all perpetually at risk of missing it. I left Theo in the courtyard, mid-sentence with my cousin Adaeze's twins, both of whom had decided within four minutes of meeting him that he was theirs now. He was showing them how to fold dough over a filling using two biscuits and a napkin, because apparently the man could not be in a room with children without teaching them lamination. I watched him for one second too long through the window before Chinedu cleared his throat.

The study door closed behind me with the soft, expensive click of a room sealing itself.

"Baba."

"Anselm." He used the full name, the one Mama had chosen and he had tolerated, the way he tolerated most things she did—openly disapproving and privately, I suspected, delighted. "Sit."

I sat. There was a chair positioned across from his desk at exactly conversational distance, and I had spent thirty-four years being invited into it for conversations that always began with *sit* and ended with something I was expected to carry out of the room like luggage.

"You look well," he said, which from my father meant *you look tired*.

"Thank you, Baba. The weekend has been—"

"The young man." He didn't ask a question. My father never asked questions when a statement would do more damage. "Theo Marsh. Baker."

"That's right."

"He makes bread."

"He does, yes. Very good bread, actually. You had the agege this morning—"

"I know what I ate, Anselm." He folded his hands on the desk. His hands were beautiful hands, long-fingered, a pianist's hands that had gone into oil instead of music, and I had inherited them, along with his jaw and his talent for making silence do heavy lifting. "I want to understand something. Help me understand. A woman who plans weddings for a living—a woman who has made a considerable name doing it, I grant you, I read the magazine article your aunt framed—chooses to bring home a baker she has known for how long?"

Here it was. The annual interrogation had simply arrived early, wearing my father's reading glasses instead of Aunt Chiamaka's headwrap.

"A few months," I said, which was technically true if you counted the months I'd been smelling his bread through my office floor as knowing him.

"A few months." He repeated it the way a judge repeats testimony he intends to dismantle. "And Daniel? Five years. Five years, Anselm. A doctor. A good family. Your mother knew his mother before either of you were born. And you ended it over a phone call, in a car park, like cancelling an appointment."

"It wasn't a phone call, it was—"

"I know what it was. Everyone knows what it was. That is rather the point." He took off his glasses and pinched the bridge of his nose, and for a moment he looked less like a chief and more like a tired man in his sixties whose daughter frightened him. "You know what people say. You know what they said then and what they're saying now, quietly, in the corners of my own compound. *The Okafor girl. Too big for her wrappers. Plans other people's weddings because no one will plan hers.*"

"They can say what they like."

"They will say it regardless. But you make it easy for them." He stood, walked to the window, looked out at the compound where, presumably, Theo was still being adopted by eight-year-olds. "Your mother worried about this. At the end."

The sentence landed in the room before I understood it. Then I understood it, and the floor tilted a degree or two.

"What do you mean, at the end?"

He turned around slowly. My father was not a man who moved quickly; every gesture was budgeted. "You were in Lagos those last weeks. Flying back and forth. Planning the Adeyemi wedding, the big one, the palace one—you remember."

"I remember." I remembered everything about those weeks except, apparently, the parts that mattered. I remembered the seating chart I redid eleven times. I remembered the florist crying over orchids. I remembered flying home with centerpieces swatches in my carry-on while Mama got smaller in a bed that had been moved to the ground floor so she wouldn't have to climb.

"She made me promise something," my father said. "In her last month, when it was just the two of us at night. She made me promise that if you went too far down this road—the weddings, the celebrations, the—" he gestured vaguely at the air, at my entire life, "—the performing of happiness for strangers—that I would call you back. Remind you of what she wanted for you."

"She never said anything to me." My voice came out smaller than I intended. "She told me to go. She told me the Adeyemi wedding was important and I should—"

"She told you to go because telling you to stay would have broken you, and she was dying, Anselm, not cruel." He came back to the desk but didn't sit. "What she asked me was this: keep the name steady. Keep you steady. Marry sensibly. Stop chasing other people's celebrations and build one of your own. Those were nearly her words. I've had three years to get them exact."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the twins shrieked with laughter, and Theo's laugh answered them, warm and unguarded, and it felt like it belonged to someone else's life.

"Marry sensibly," I repeated. "Like Daniel."

"Daniel was sensible."

"Daniel was a spreadsheet, Baba. Daniel proposed with a PowerPoint."

"And you rejected a man who prepared for marriage the way you prepare for everything else, and you wonder why I'm confused about what you actually want." He sat down finally, heavily, and the chair creaked under him, and I hated that sound, hated the evidence that time was happening to him too. "Your mother was not a foolish woman. She loved you ferociously. But she grew up watching her own mother bury two engagements and a reputation, and she decided early that the Okafor name would stand straight if it killed her. It very nearly did. All those years holding us together, smiling at functions, swallowing every insult with that smile—" his voice caught, briefly, and he mastered it, "—she wanted better for you. Or she thought she did. She wanted simpler. Simple is not always better, but it is safer, and she had earned the right to want you safe."

I stared at my hands. My beautiful inherited hands.

"So this weekend," I said. "Theo. You think I'm embarrassing the name."

"I think," my father said carefully, "that I don't yet know what he is. A few months. A baker with debts—I hear things, Anselm, the landlord has been calling the number on his shop sign since Tuesday, half the street knows. A man whose visa is a question mark. And you arrive with him like a solution to a problem, and neither of you quite looks at the other the way people look at each other when they've had a few months." He paused. "Or perhaps you look at each other exactly that way and I'm wrong. I've been wrong about you before. Your mother enjoyed reminding me."

"When?" I asked. "When did she enjoy reminding you?"

He almost smiled. "Every day. For forty-one years."

* * *

I found the corridor outside the study too bright after the museum-smell of his room. I walked past the living room where the aunties had colonized the sofas, past the dining room where the caterers' staff were setting up for dinner, and stopped at the photograph wall.

Every Okafor house has one. Ours was a corridor of framed decades: my grandfather in his surveyor's khakis, my parents' wedding—Mama twenty-three and furious with joy, my father looking like a man who'd won a war he hadn't expected to survive—and then the progression of us. Me at seven in a lace dress I was destroying. Me at university, mortarboard crooked. Me at thirty, alone, at the opening of my firm's London office, cut from the ribbon with scissors borrowed from a client.

Mama was in most of them at the edges. Standing slightly behind. Smiling the smile my father had just described—the one that swallowed things.

I had built my whole career on the belief that I was nothing like her. That was the story I told myself in taxis and hotel bars: Mama held the family together by disappearing into it, and I had escaped into a profession where love was a product and I was the artisan. I sold forever to strangers. I never had to buy any myself. Clean transaction. No exposure.

But standing there, I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding for fifteen years.

Who was the first person I called when I booked the Adeyemi palace wedding? Mama. Who taught me that a table is a statement and a seating chart is diplomacy? Mama, arranging Christmas dinners like treaty negotiations. Who noticed, before anyone, that I could make forty strangers feel like a family for one evening—and who also noticed, before anyone, that I did it so I'd never have to belong to one?

She saw it. Of course she saw it. She'd invented the technique.

And her deathbed request—*keep the name steady, marry sensibly, stop chasing other people's celebrations*—wasn't a cage built by my father. It was a cage built by a woman who loved me, welded shut with her own exhaustion, handed to the man least equipped to open it gently.

My career wasn't an escape from her. It was a rebellion against her. Every flawless stranger-wedding was a letter to Mama that said *watch me, I'll never need what you needed, I'll never swallow anything, I'll never disappear into a family, I'll run the family from the doorway.* Fifteen years of letters. And she'd died asking me to stop writing them.

"Ansel?"

I turned. Theo stood at the end of the corridor with flour still dusted on his forearm—he'd been teaching the twins something, God help the upholstery—and he had the particular expression of a man who has spotted a bird with a broken wing and isn't sure whether approaching will help.

"My dad wanted to talk," I said.

"I figured. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The one you wear when you're fine." He came closer, stopped at a respectful distance, close enough that I could smell sugar and woodsmoke. "It's a very convincing face. I've seen it across a bakery counter for months."

For a moment I let myself lean toward him—not physically, but the other way, the dangerous way. Here was the thing about Theo: he didn't ask questions about the past because we'd contractually agreed he wouldn't, but he read the present like proofing dough, knowing exactly when it had risen enough and when touching it would collapse it.

"My mother," I started, and stopped. Started again. "She left my father instructions. About me. About my life. And he's just—delivered them. Three years late."

Theo nodded slowly. "Do you want to tell me here, or—"

"No." I glanced toward the kitchen, where the caterers were working, and then remembered the old kitchen—the real one, the original, the one Mama had refused to let my father modernize, with its wood fire and its blackened pots hanging like instruments. Nobody used it during reunions except for show. "Not here. Not anywhere anyone can find us."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "That sounds ominous."

"It sounds like I need to cook something or I'm going to lose my mind, and I'd rather lose it somewhere with a fire and a knife."

Something shifted in his face—not amusement exactly, more like recognition, the same look he'd given me at three in the morning over proofing dough, two insomniacs acknowledging the shift change. "Well," he said. "I do know my way around a kitchen."

"I know," I said. "It's the only reason I keep you around."

We both heard it—the lie in the joke, sitting there between us like a third guest—and neither of us reached for it. Not yet. The old kitchen was at the back of the compound, past the generator house, and I led him toward it through the gold evening light while behind us the reunion hummed on without us, all of it—the interrogations, the expectations, the weight of a dead woman's wishes—temporarily held at bay by the simple, subversive act of two people going to make bread in a room where nobody was watching.

I pushed open the kitchen door. It groaned on hinges that hadn't been oiled since Mama died, and inside, everything was exactly as she'd left it.

So was I, I realized. Exactly as she'd left me.

Time to change that—or at least, time to knead at it until something gave.
