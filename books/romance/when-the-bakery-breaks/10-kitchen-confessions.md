## 10. Kitchen Confessions

There are three ways to escape an Okafor party, I learned by hour two. The first is death, which the aunties treat as a scheduling inconvenience rather than a tragedy. The second is marriage, which buys you maybe twenty minutes of peace before they start asking when you'll produce children. The third is the kitchen.

I found it by accident, following the smell of scotch bonnet and rendered onions away from the compound's main hall, where a DJ was playing highlife remixes at a volume that suggested he had a grudge against the concept of conversation. The kitchen sat in a separate building behind the house—old-fashioned, wide-hipped, built for cooking as labor rather than performance. Someone's grandmother had designed it for feeding forty without breaking a sweat, and someone's granddaughter was currently standing in it with her shoes off, sleeves rolled, staring down a pot of stew like it owed her money.

"You're hiding," I said.

Ansel didn't turn around. "I'm supervising."

"From inside the building. With the door shut."

"The acoustics are better in here."

I shut the door behind me anyway, and the music dropped to a muffled heartbeat. She glanced over her shoulder then, and something in her face unclenched—not all the way, but enough that I could see the shape of what it had been holding back.

"They've got you making stew?" I asked.

"I volunteered." She said it like a confession. "Aunt Chiamaka looked at me like I'd offered to wash the car with my tongue. Nobody cooks at these things anymore. There's a catering company from Enugu. But I needed—" She stopped, recalibrated. "It's quiet in here."

"It's not quiet. You're about to have a pot of stew and a witness."

"That's different," she said, and handed me a knife and a bowl of peppers, which is how I understood I'd been drafted.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't live in the big rooms. It lives in kitchens. It lives in the muscle memory of chopping onions the way Marianne used to insist on—halved through the root so the layers hold together until the last cut—and it ambushes you there, between tasks, when your hands are busy enough to lower your guard.

Ansel Okafor chopped onions like a woman who'd been taught properly and resented every lesson. Her knife work was flawless and furious. I watched her reduce three of them to confetti in under a minute and thought: *there she is.* The woman who'd planned four hundred weddings without a single visible seam. The woman who'd held my hand through two days of family interrogation with the steadiness of a surgeon. All of it was in her hands, and none of it was in her face.

"My mum taught me this recipe," she said, to the pot more than to me. "Well. Not this one exactly. This is my version. She'd be horrified by the amount of ginger."

"She sounds like she had opinions."

"She had *laws*." Ansel smiled despite herself, stirring. "Ginger is measured with your whole palm, never your fingers. Rice is never, ever cooked with tomatoes—you make the stew separately or you've insulted the grain. And you never—" she pointed the wooden spoon at me like a scepter, "—you never let anyone else season the pot. Not even family. Especially not family."

"Harsh household."

"Loving household. Harshly loving." She added ginger with her whole palm, and I saw her hand shake slightly on the way back down, and pretended I hadn't. "She died six years ago. Pancreatic. Fast, which the doctors called a mercy and we called a robbery."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry yet," she said. "Wait until you hear the rest, because apparently I'm doing this now. Apparently the kitchen has broken me." She laughed, short and sharp. "Two days of playing happy couple and it takes one pot of stew to crack me open. There's a lesson in there about my mother's cooking that I refuse to examine."

I set down my knife. Some conversations you don't want to chop through.

* * *

The stew went into the pot in stages—oil first, onions until they surrendered, then the pepper blend that made my eyes water from across the room. Ansel worked with a rhythm that was clearly older than her grief, something her body remembered even when her mouth went careful. Outside, the music thumped on. In here there was only the spit of oil and the two of us and whatever she was working up the nerve to say.

"You asked me once," she said finally, "why I do weddings. At rehearsal. You asked and I gave you the brochure answer—the joy, the meaning, all of it."

"You were very convincing."

"I'm always convincing. That's the problem." She turned the flame down. "The truth is I don't believe in them anymore. Weddings. Any of it. I plan them the way an architect designs prisons—beautifully, functionally, with total professional detachment from whether anyone inside deserves to be there."

"That's bleak."

"I told you not to be sorry yet." She wiped her hands on her apron—a proper apron, hers, packed from London, which meant she'd planned to end up in this kitchen before she ever admitted it to herself. "My engagement ended the same week my mother died. Did you know that?"

"No."

"Nobody knows that. That's the point. Daniel proposed in March. Mama went into hospital in April. He lasted until June." She said it flatly, factually, a report filed long ago. "And everyone—the whole family, his family, my father—they all agreed it was understandable. Sad, but understandable. A man can't be expected to plan a wedding while his fiancée is watching her mother die. And I stood there at the funeral, twenty-six years old, burying the person who loved me most in the world, and I watched everyone look at me with such gentle pity, and do you know what I felt?"

"What?"

"Relief." The word came out like something she'd been keeping in a locked drawer for six years. "Pure, shameful relief. Because I didn't have to marry him. I didn't have to become somebody's wife at the exact moment I'd just stopped being somebody's daughter-in-progress. And I hated myself for it, and I buried the hate along with her, and I went back to London and became the best wedding planner in the city because if I couldn't have a wedding, I would build four hundred of them for other people and never once have to stand inside one as anything but staff."

The oil popped. Neither of us moved.

"So when you watch those ceremonies," I said slowly, "and you feel nothing—"

"It's not nothing. It's everything, all at once, and I've gotten very good at calling it nothing." She stirred the stew. "That bride yesterday, at the welcome thing—Chiamaka's goddaughter, the one who kept asking me about centerpieces? She's going to be fine. They're all going to be fine. And some small vicious part of me watches them say their vows and thinks, *you don't know what you're promising, nobody does, it's a beautiful lie everyone agrees to tell.* And then I think about my mother, who married my father against her own mother's advice and spent thirty-four years being right about him, and I think—maybe the lie isn't the problem. Maybe the problem is me."

"Ansel."

"Don't 'Ansel' me in my mother's kitchen. She can hear you."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and after a second, so did she—that real laugh, the one from the bakery, the one that seemed to surprise her every time it escaped.

* * *

We ate standing up, straight from the pot with spoons, because she said that was also a law: the cook eats first, standing, before anyone can judge the seasoning. The stew was extraordinary. I told her so with my mouth full, which she said was the highest form of review.

Then it was quiet again, the comfortable kind, and I should have left well enough alone. But she'd gone first. She'd cracked herself open over a pot of stew and handed me the pieces, and there are debts you can't leave sitting.

"I said yes because I wanted it to be real," I told her.

She looked up from the pot. "What?"

"The weekend. The arrangement. You asked me to pretend, and I said yes—for the money, obviously, the bakery needs it—but that's not why I said yes. I said yes because I wanted two days of it being true. Holding your hand. Being somebody's plus-one instead of somebody's widower. Eating jollof at a table full of people who aren't looking at me with that face."

"What face?"

"The face." I set my spoon down. "Everyone in my life since Marianne died has the face. Pity, mostly. Or worse—relief, sometimes, from people who found our grief inconvenient. My sister calls every Sunday and asks how I am in a voice like she's defusing a bomb. My landlord looks at me like I'm a sad story he's renting property to. And you—" I shrugged. "You looked at me like I was a man who knew things. Bread, mostly. But still. Things."

Ansel had gone very still. It occurred to me, distantly, that I'd never seen her still like this—not composed-still, not professional-still, but caught-still. Like a deer that had heard the shot but hadn't decided which way to run.

"Theo—"

"And tonight, at that party, when your cousin asked how we met and you told that story about the rain and the delivery—I know we invented it on the train. I know it's fake. But I was standing there listening to you tell it, and I thought: I wish it had happened. I wished it so hard that for a second I couldn't remember that it hadn't. And that's not pretending anymore, Ansel. That's something else, and I don't have a name for it, and I'm telling you in a kitchen in Enugu state because apparently that's where I do my confessing now."

The silence stretched. Somewhere outside, a generator hummed. Ansel looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her do the thing she did—reach for logistics, reach for the exit, reach for the version of this that could be managed.

"The stew needs to simmer another twenty minutes," she said.

"Yes."

"And the caterers will want the kitchen back by ten."

"Probably."

"We should—" She stopped. Her hand was on the counter, half an inch from mine. "This was a mistake. Telling me that. Because I can't—Theo, I have rules for a reason. I built my whole life out of rules, and they work, they keep everything *uncomplicated*, and if you stand there saying things like that I'm going to start believing the weekend is real, and then Monday comes, and the bakery, and the visa, and my father, and—"

"Ansel."

"*What?*"

"Your stew's burning."

She whirled to the pot with a yelp, rescuing it with a flurry of stirring that was frankly heroic, and by the time she'd saved it, the moment had been folded away somewhere, pressed flat, put in a drawer. But not discarded. I knew the difference. I'd spent two years learning the difference between a thing thrown away and a thing hidden.

* * *

We carried the pot out together at quarter past ten, and the party swallowed us back—Chiamaka immediately, declaring that Ansel had inherited her mother's hand and demanding to know why the stew wasn't on the main table, and Ansel saying something smooth and deflecting, and me standing beside her with my hand at the small of her back, no longer sure which of us was performing.

Later, much later, when the compound had gone dark and we lay in separate rooms on opposite sides of a thin wall, I heard her floorboards creak at two in the morning. Heard her pause outside my door. Heard her breathe there for eleven seconds—I counted, because sleep and I weren't speaking—before walking on.

In the morning there would be the talent night, and her father's eyes, and whatever came next. But at two in the morning, in a house full of sleeping Okafors, I lay awake and understood two things with perfect clarity.

One: I was falling in love with her, and the contract said I wasn't allowed, and I was going to break that clause like I broke the lease terms I never read.

Two: she'd stood outside my door. And whatever she'd come to say, she'd carried it away again—but she'd carried it *somewhere*. Not thrown it out. Hidden it.

Hidden things, in my experience, have a way of surfacing at the worst possible moment.

Which is exactly, I would learn by dessert tomorrow night, what they do.
