## 12. Almost

The veranda at the back of the Okafor compound was built for lingering. Someone—some great-grandfather with a sense of occasion, or maybe just a bad back—had set two rocking chairs out there facing a garden gone soft and overgrown in the dark, and strung a single bulb above them that buzzed like it was thinking about giving up. The party was still going inside. I could hear Aunt Chiamaka's laugh through the wall, that laugh that could strip paint, and beneath it the bass line of some highlife song that had been old when my mother was young.

Ansel sat in one of the rocking chairs with her shoes off, her feet tucked under her, looking younger than she had all weekend. Younger than she had all month, if I'm honest. The performance was over for the night—the cousins fed, the father survived, Daniel and his new wife successfully not looked at—and what was left sitting in that chair was just a woman who'd been holding her breath for three days.

"You're staring," she said, without turning around.

"I'm admiring the architecture."

"The architecture is a rotting veranda and a lightbulb with a death wish."

"Then I'm admiring the woman insulting the architecture."

She turned then, and the bulb caught her face at an angle that made half of it gold and half of it shadow, and I thought: *this is the part where you say something you can't take back.* I'd been thinking it since dinner, actually, since she'd put her hand on my knee under the table when Chiamaka asked about my late wife and I told the truth instead of a deflection. She hadn't done it for the audience. There wasn't an angle where anyone could see. She'd done it because she'd known, somehow, that I needed anchoring.

That's the thing about fake relationships. Nobody warns you that the acting stops being the hard part.

"Come here," she said. "There's room."

There was not, objectively, room. It was a rocking chair built for one Victorian-sized person. But I crossed the veranda anyway because I have never once in my life made the sensible choice when Ansel Okafor used that tone of voice, and I wedged myself into the chair beside her, our shoulders stacked, her head finding the hollow below my collarbone like it had been measured for it.

"This is a terrible idea," she murmured.

"Which part?"

"All of it. The chair. The proximity. The fact that I can smell flour on you even after you showered."

"That's commitment to the role."

"That's a health code violation." But she said it into my shirt, so it came out soft, and her hand found mine in the dark and didn't let go.

We rocked. Or rather, the chair attempted to rock and we mostly creaked, two adults in a piece of furniture having a structural crisis, and neither of us moved to fix it because moving would mean separating and neither of us was willing to be the one who suggested that.

Inside, someone started singing. Badly. Enthusiastically. A cousin, probably—one of the ones who'd spent the weekend cataloguing us like customs officers.

"They like you," Ansel said.

"They interrogated me about my sourdough hydration for forty minutes."

"That *is* how they like people. You passed. Ada wants your recipe. That's basically a marriage proposal in this family."

"And what does this family think of you bringing home a widowed baker with a failing business and a visa held together with optimism?"

She was quiet for a moment. The bulb buzzed. Somewhere in the garden, something small moved through the grass.

"My family thinks I brought home the first man who's ever made me laugh at a reunion," she said finally. "My father thinks—" She stopped. Started again. "My father thinks what he always thinks. That I collect other people's happy endings because I don't believe I get one."

"Do you?"

"Believe it?" Her thumb moved across my knuckles, once, absent, like she was checking I was still there. "I used to think believing was the same as wanting. Like if I wanted it badly enough, that counted as faith." She shifted against me, and I felt her breath change. "Theo. Can I tell you something that isn't in the contract?"

My heart did something unprofessional. "The contract specifically prohibits questions about the past. It says nothing about unsolicited confessions."

"Then here's one, free of charge." She tilted her face up toward me, and in the bad light her eyes were doing something complicated. "I haven't stopped thinking about the kitchen. Last night. When we were cooking and you told me why you really said yes."

"Because I wanted the weekend to be real," I said. "I remember. I was there."

"Yes, well." Her voice went dry, but her hand tightened on mine. "I've been trying to figure out how to say the next part. I've drafted it eleven times. In my head. I draft things, it's a problem, I draft emails and speeches and feelings and then I file them somewhere they can't hurt anyone."

"What's the next part?"

"The next part is—" She took a breath, and I watched her decide, actually watched the decision move through her like weather. "The next part is that I don't want to file this one."

I should have kissed her then. Any reasonable person would have kissed her then. The bulb was buzzing its dying hymn, the party was a warm blur behind the wall, her face was tipped up toward mine with all that armor finally, finally set down, and I leaned in—or she leaned in, or the chair betrayed us both and gravity made the decision—and we were close enough that I could feel her breath, close enough that the distance between us had become a technicality—

Her phone lit up on the armrest like a police raid.

We both froze. The screen glowed against the dark, buzzing itself in a slow circle, and the name on it was *Marcus — Landlord* in the aggressive font Theo Marsh uses for people he cannot afford to ignore.

"Don't," Ansel said quietly.

"It might be nothing."

"It's nearly midnight. Nothing doesn't call at midnight."

I reached for it anyway, because that's the thing about joy—it never gets to finish a sentence. And I knew, before I answered, from the way the phone kept buzzing, from the hour, from the simple physics of my life, exactly what kind of call it was.

"Marsh." My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Twenty years of early mornings will teach you to sound awake at any hour; apparently grief and dread use the same muscle.

"Sorry to ring so late, mate." Marcus's voice had that particular landlord timbre, apologetic the way a sledgehammer is apologetic. "I've had the solicitors on. Council's confirmed it. The compulsory purchase went through tonight. End of the month."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own little weight.

"End of the month," I repeated.

"Twenty-ninth. They want vacant possession by then. I know—I know I said autumn, I said we'd have time, but the developer's pushed the schedule forward and there's nothing—" He sighed, and for one strange second he sounded genuinely sorry. "You've been a good tenant, Theo. Best I've had. I fought it. I want you to know I fought it."

I looked down at Ansel. She was watching my face in the dark, reading it the way she reads seating charts, and whatever she saw there made her sit up straight.

"Right," I said. "Thanks for letting me know."

"There's the relocation payment. Not much. And listen—if you need a reference for the visa, I'll write whatever you—"

"Yeah. Thanks, Marcus."

I hung up. The bulb buzzed. Inside, the cousin was still singing, gloriously off-key, about love lasting forever, and I sat there in a rocking chair built for one, holding the hand of a woman I'd been about to kiss, doing arithmetic I already knew the answer to.

End of the month. The ovens. The proofing baskets. The photograph of Elena I keep taped to the till because she liked watching customers' faces when they bit into something warm. Twenty-two years of mornings, cleared out by the twenty-ninth.

And the visa. God, the visa. The renewal application sat in a folder on my desk, half-finished, resting on the business remaining viable. A viable business. A bakery with no premises. I could hear the caseworker's voice already, polite and final: *unable to demonstrate continued operation.*

"Theo." Ansel's voice, careful now. The armor coming back on, but differently—not for herself. For me. "What happened?"

"The demolition's confirmed." I heard my own voice go flat, professional, the voice I use for bad harvests and broken mixers. "End of the month. They moved it up."

She was silent. Then: "Your visa."

"Hinges on the business surviving. Which it now has approximately twenty-six days to do." I laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow as an empty oven. "You know what the funny thing is? I almost didn't answer. First time in three days I've been happy—actually happy, not performing happy—and I thought, whoever this is, they can wait until morning."

"I'm glad you answered."

"Are you?"

"Yes." She turned to face me fully, knees against my thigh, and the dying bulb chose that moment to flicker back to full strength, illuminating her completely, no shadows left to hide in. "Because I'm about to make you an offer that violates several clauses of our contract, and I wanted to see your face when I did."

"Ansel—"

"Before you refuse. You're going to refuse, I can see you loading the refusal, so let me get through it." She held up a hand, counting. "One: I have savings. More than I've ever admitted to anyone, because admitting it means explaining why I never spend them, which means explaining what I was saving *for*, which is a longer conversation. Two: I know planning law people. Actual ones, who owe me favors from weddings I've pulled off miracles for. Compulsory purchase orders can be challenged. Timelines can be contested. It's not hopeless, it's paperwork, and paperwork is my native language. Three—"

"Three is that you'd be buying your fake boyfriend's bakery with money you saved for a life you decided you weren't allowed to have," I said. "No."

"Three," she continued, relentless, "is that I watched you argue with your landlord through my office floor for eight months, and I have been drafting ways to help you since March, and I am very tired of filing drafts."

"No." Softer this time. Because her hand was still in mine, and because the word tasted like a lie even as I said it. "Ansel. Listen to me. If you put money into that building, you're not saving a bakery. You're marrying a sinking ship. You're tying yourself to a man whose entire legal existence expires in five months, in a country that has already made up its mind about him, over a building the council has already sold. That's not an investment. That's charity with extra steps, and you deserve—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the single syllable. "Don't you dare tell me what I deserve. I have spent fifteen years arranging what other people deserve. Centrepieces and first dances and vows I wrote for strangers because they couldn't find the words. I know exactly what I deserve, Theo. What I deserve is to stop filing things."

The bulb buzzed. Inside, the singing had stopped; someone was making a toast. We sat in the wreckage of the almost-kiss, two people who'd spent a weekend pretending, discovering that the pretense had expired and neither of us had a script for what came after.

"Twenty-six days," I said finally.

"Twenty-six days." She squeezed my hand. "Then we'd better start tomorrow."

"*We*?"

"You heard me." And there it was—that look, the one from the kitchen, the one that had undone me over a pot of stew. "I'm coming back to London with you. I'm calling in every favor I have. And on day twenty-five, when we walk into that hearing, you're going to have the best-prepared case that council has ever seen, because Ansel Okafor does not lose events, Theo. I have never once lost an event."

"This isn't an event."

"No." She smiled, and it was the real one, the crooked one, the one that had no audience. "It's much more important than that."

I wanted to kiss her. Properly, this time, no phones, no landlords, no deadlines. I leaned in again, and this time she met me halfway, and we were inches apart, closer than before, the whole night holding its breath—

And the veranda door banged open, flooding us with light and music and the unmistakable presence of Aunt Chiamaka, who took one look at our position—her niece practically in my lap, my hand in hers, both of us flushed and guilty as teenagers—and beamed like the sun rising over Lagos.

"AH!" she announced, to the entire compound, possibly to the entire state. "I KNEW IT. Nne, come and see! They cannot even wait until the wedding!"

"We weren't—" Ansel started.

"Not one word!" Chiamaka wagged a finger, delighted beyond measure. "In my day we had shame. Now there is no shame, everybody is kissing on verandas. Come inside both of you, there is cake, and your father wants to speak with you, Ansel." Her eyes glittered with a satisfaction that had nothing to do with cake. "*Alone.*"

And there it was. The ambush behind the ambush. Because I knew that tone. Every baker knows that tone—it's the tone of someone who has saved the difficult conversation for after the celebration, when the customer is happy and cornered.

Ansel's hand tightened around mine, once, hard, and then let go.

"Go," she said quietly, standing, putting her armor back on piece by visible piece. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we start."

"Ansel. Whatever he says—"

"I know what he'll say." She glanced back at me, and the look in her eyes was equal parts fear and fury and something that felt dangerously like hope. "But he doesn't know yet that I've already decided. Neither do you, really." A pause. "Neither do I. But ask me again on day twenty-five."

She followed her aunt inside, into the light and the noise and whatever waited for her in her father's study, and I stayed on the veranda alone with the dying bulb and the crickets and the arithmetic.

Twenty-six days. Five months of visa. One woman who'd just promised me a war she couldn't yet promise herself.

I sat in the dark a while longer, rocking in a chair built for one, and realized my hands were shaking—not from dread, for once.

From the terrifying, unfamiliar weight of being worth fighting for.
