## 15. Flour on His Hands

The council office on Mare Street smelled of wet carpet and institutional despair, which I suppose is what happens when a building spends forty years deciding nothing important. I'd been there three times that week. The woman behind the counter—her badge said P. OYELARAN, and I'd decided we were friends, whether she knew it or not—had begun to recognize me the way you recognize weather.

"Theo Marsh," she said, before I'd even reached the desk. "Form 22-B?"

"Two copies," I said. "One for the file, one for my conscience."

She almost smiled. Almost. "You know the demolition order isn't something forms fix, Mr. Marsh. It's a planning decision. There's a hearing."

"There's always a hearing," I said. "That's why there's always me."

I took the bus back to the bakery because the walk gave me time to rehearse arguments and the bus gave me time to feel sorry for myself, and I'd been rationing self-pity like wartime sugar. One spoonful a day, no more. Though some days the ration broke.

The shop looked worse in daylight than it did at midnight, when the ovens made it glow like something alive. By afternoon the paint peeled honestly, the awning sagged like a tired shoulder, and the new notice taped to the window—DEVELOPMENT CONSULTATION, with a rendering of a glass tower where my life currently stood—curled at one corner like it was embarrassed to be there.

I took down the notice, smoothed it flat, and put it back up. Evidence, I told myself. You want them to see what they're erasing.

Then I did what I'd been doing all week, which was everything at once and none of it well.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about fighting alone: it's not dramatic. It's not a man standing in front of a bulldozer. It's a man at two in the morning, proofing dough with one hand and drafting a petition with the other, getting flour in the keyboard, discovering that grief and bureaucracy use the same part of your brain—the part that keeps saying *again, again, again*.

I'd started the petition as a joke to myself. *Save Marsh's Bakery: 12 signatures.* Then Mrs. Adeyemi from the dry cleaner's signed, and her whole congregation signed, and the man who ran the phone repair kiosk signed and brought his brother, who turned out to be a journalist for the local paper, which was how I ended up standing in my own doorway at seven a.m., flour on my hands because I'd forgotten to wash them, explaining sourdough hydration ratios to a woman named Priya who kept looking past me at the ovens like they were the real story.

"They are, sort of," I admitted. "Forty-one years. My dad built those benches. My wife—" I stopped. Restarted. "My wife used to say the building had better bones than either of us."

"And she passed?" Priya asked gently.

"Three years ago." The words came out easier than usual, which should have warned me. "The bread got better after. That's the terrible part. Grief makes you pay attention."

She wrote that down. I watched her write it down and felt something crack open in my chest—not pain exactly, more like a window unlatching in a room you'd sealed shut.

The article ran Thursday. *THE BAKER WHO WON'T RISE AND SHINE FOR DEVELOPERS.* Priya had cleaned up my metaphors but kept the line about grief making you pay attention, and by Friday morning there were forty signatures on the clipboard by the till and a queue out the door that had nothing to do with my croissants and everything to do with people wanting to stand inside a thing before it disappeared.

I sold out by ten. I hadn't sold out since Nia was alive and we were young enough to underprice everything.

That night I baked until three, not because I needed the stock but because the ovens were the only place the fear couldn't follow me. Heat is honest. Dough doesn't negotiate. You give it time and warmth and it becomes what it was always going to be, which is more than I could say for councils or landlords or me.

* * *

Ansel texted twice that week.

The first: *How's the fight going?*

I typed back four different answers and deleted all of them. *Losing,* was true but needy. *Fine,* was a lie. *Wish you were here* was all three of those things at once and also the one thing I refused to say first, because I'd meant what I said in that kitchen—that I'd wanted the weekend to be real—and meaning it didn't obligate her to anything. She'd hired me to play a part. The part had gotten away from both of us. That wasn't a debt she owed; it was just weather we'd walked into.

The second text came Saturday night: *Sorry I've been buried. Big wedding this month.*

Professional. Warm-ish. A door closed politely from the inside.

I sent back a thumbs-up, which is the emotional equivalent of a receipt, and then I turned off my phone and burned a batch of rye because I was angry, and the anger needed somewhere to go, and the oven has always taken what I can't say.

Because here's the thing about Ansel Okafor: she plans other people's forevers for a living. She knows every word for love except the ones that cost anything. And I'd watched her, over one weekend, learn to laugh with her whole face again, and I'd thought—I'd *believed*—that something had shifted. But belief, I've learned, is mostly just hope wearing a suit, and hope without action is just a longer way of being disappointed.

So I fought alone. Which was, if I'm honest, what I'd done since Nia died anyway. At least fighting alone was familiar. Familiar things don't surprise you at three a.m.

* * *

Monday, the landlord's solicitor sent a letter so aggressive it practically had teeth marks. The council hearing was confirmed: end of the month. My visa lawyer—God bless Mrs. Okonjo, who charged me half rate and prayed for me in Yoruba whether I asked or not—called to remind me that if the business dissolved, my entrepreneur visa dissolved with it, and then I'd have ninety days to leave the country where my wife was buried.

"So fight," Mrs. Okonjo said.

"I am fighting."

"You are filling forms," she said. "Fighting is when you make other people fight with you."

I hung up and stood in the empty shop, listening to the ovens tick as they cooled, and tried to work out how to make strangers fight for a building they'd known for five minutes against developers with lawyers whose hourly rate probably exceeded my weekly takings.

That was when the bell above the door went—the old brass one, off-key, the one Nia loved because it sounded like a question.

Ansel stood in the doorway in a raincoat, holding a folder, rain still beaded on her shoulders, looking like someone who had run the last hundred metres of a decision.

"I emailed you," she said. "Logistics. About the—"

"I read it." I wiped my hands on my apron, then remembered the flour situation and wiped them again. "It was very thorough."

"It was cowardice with bullet points," she said. "I know. I wrote it."

I didn't say anything. The ovens ticked. Somewhere behind her, London carried on being loud about nothing.

"I ignored your texts," she continued, stepping inside properly now, letting the door swing shut. "Not because I didn't care. Because caring was inconvenient, and I have spent eleven years being professionally excellent at putting inconvenient feelings in a spreadsheet and closing the tab." She held up the folder. "This is me reopening the tab."

"That's not really the metaphor I'd have chosen."

"You try talking to you when you're doing the silent-baker thing. It's like negotiating with a very handsome wall."

Despite everything—despite the letter with teeth marks and the ticking clock and the three a.m.s—I felt my mouth twitch. "Handsome?"

"Focus, Theo."

She opened the folder. Inside: printed emails, annotated in that precise architect-hand of hers. A name I recognized—Barrister Adaeze Cole, housing law, the woman who'd once helped the market traders on Chatsworth Road beat a compulsory purchase order. Ansel had written three pages of notes on precedent. Three pages. While I'd been rationing self-pity, she'd apparently been building a case.

"I called her Sunday night," Ansel said. "At home. I told her it was urgent. She asked me why I cared about a bakery in Hackney and I said—" she paused, and I watched her decide to tell the truth, watch it cost her something visible, "—I said because I left something unfinished there and I intend to finish it."

The bell above the door gave its off-key question into the silence.

"Adaeze will take the case," Ansel went on, brisker now, retreating slightly into logistics the way she did when the feeling got too big for the room. "Pro bono initially, paid later when we win. She wants the petition numbers, the article, photographs of the original fixtures—heritage angle, the benches especially, your father's benches could qualify under local listing criteria. I've drafted a timeline. Hearing's in nineteen days, which is tight but not impossible, and tight-but-not-impossible is literally my profession."

I looked at the folder. Then at her. Rain-damp, determined, terrified underneath it the way she always was underneath everything, and holding three pages of evidence that she'd spent her week thinking about my life instead of hiding from it.

"You didn't have to do this," I said.

"No," she agreed. "I had to do the other thing, and I did that instead, and it was wrong. So this is—" she exhaled. "Theo, I don't know how to apologize in words. Words are what I sell. They come too easily, which means they're worth nothing. So I'm apologizing in *action*. In spreadsheets. In a barrister's home number scrawled on a napkin. This is my love language, apparently. Bullet points."

The word sat between us. *Love.* Neither of us picked it up. Neither of us swept it away either.

"Your hands are shaking," I said.

"My hands are always shaking. It's the caffeine." She looked down at them, betrayed. "All right. Fine. They're shaking. Nineteen days, Theo. Nineteen days and a hearing and a visa clock and a demolition date, and I keep thinking about you here at three in the morning baking bread for a building that might not exist next month, and I can't—I can't plan someone else's forever while yours gets knocked down. I won't."

I came around the counter. Up close she smelled of rain and expensive perfume losing an argument to bakery air, and her eyes had that cracked-open look I'd seen once before, in a family kitchen in Enugu, right before she retreated.

"Don't retreat," I said quietly.

"I'm trying not to. It's a full-time job."

"What happened with the big wedding? The one keeping you buried."

Something crossed her face—guilt, or its cousin. "It's falling apart. Has been for weeks. My fault, mostly. I keep leaving mid-site-visit to call barristers." She laughed, short and unamused. "Eleven years of reputation, and I'm spending it on you. On a bakery. On *us*, if we're using honest grammar."

"We're using honest grammar," I said. "For once."

For once, she didn't flinch. Instead she rolled up her sleeves—actually rolled up her sleeves, Ansel Okafor, whose hands hadn't touched dough in her life—and said, "Show me the heritage photos you need. And then teach me to score a loaf, because if we're saving this place I refuse to be useless in it."

And that—more than the folder, more than the barrister, more than three pages of her precise handwriting—was the apology. Not words. Flour.

I handed her an apron. It was Nia's spare, and I paused a half-second before giving it, and Ansel saw the pause and understood it, and took it gently, like accepting something on loan from the dead.

"Flour first," I said. "Always flour first, or it clumps."

She measured badly. I corrected her. She measured badly again, with more confidence, which is half of baking and most of life. The ovens ticked warmer. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the glass tower's future foundations, and inside, the woman who planned forever for strangers learned how much water a dough can hold before it breaks.

Her phone buzzed on the counter halfway through. She glanced at it, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"Who is it?" I asked.

She turned the screen toward me. My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the earth.

It was her father.
