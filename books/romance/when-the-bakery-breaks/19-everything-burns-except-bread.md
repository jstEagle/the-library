## 19. Everything Burns Except Bread

**Ansel**

The council chamber smelled of old carpet and older decisions, the particular staleness of a room where people's futures were decided between lunch and a tea break. I'd planned four hundred weddings and I had never been this nervous. Not once. Not even at the Adeyemi wedding, when the ice sculpture arrived as a puddle and I had to pass it off as "an intentional water feature."

Theo sat beside me with his hands flat on his knees like a man in a job interview, which, in a way, he was. The petition had gathered sixty-three signatures from the street — Mrs. Osei from the dry cleaner's, the boys from the barbershop who'd eaten free bread through three winters, a woman named Priya who apparently owed Theo her entire sourdough education. Sixty-three names on paper. What we needed was five votes.

"Ms. Okafor," said Councillor Whitfield, peering over her glasses with the expression of someone who has heard every speech ever given and believed none of them. "You have three minutes."

Three minutes. I had once choreographed a first dance to a medley of four songs in under two weeks. Three minutes was nothing.

I stood. My knees did not shake, because I had trained them not to, twenty years ago, in my mother's sitting room, where standing still was a survival skill.

"My name is Ansel Okafor," I began, "and for eleven years I have planned weddings. Large ones. Expensive ones. Weddings that cost more than this building." I let that land. Somewhere behind me, a councillor shifted. "I am very good at what I do. I say that without modesty because modesty would be a lie, and today I have decided to stop lying."

Theo made a small sound beside me — half warning, half prayer.

"I grew up in a family where the highest virtue is being uncomplicated. Where you marry sensibly, work quietly, and never, ever make a scene." I looked down at my hands. They were steady. "For years I thought that was wisdom. Then I met a man who gets up at three in the morning to feed a neighbourhood that mostly doesn't know his name, and who is about to lose everything he built because a spreadsheet says his building would be worth more as flats nobody can afford."

"Ms. Okafor," Whitfield started, "if you could get to the relevant—"

"The relevant point," I said, and my voice came out louder than I intended, "is that I am staking my name on him. My savings. Forty-two thousand pounds, which I will put into the bakery's restoration the moment this committee grants the preservation order. I have the transfer documents here." I held them up like evidence at trial, which was essentially what this was. "You are not deciding whether a bakery survives. You are deciding whether this city still has room for the people who hold it together at four in the morning."

The chamber went quiet in that specific way rooms do when someone has said something true by accident.

And then, because apparently I had lost my mind entirely, I kept going.

"My mother died asking me to keep our name uncomplicated. I obeyed her for fifteen years. I planned other people's happiness because I was afraid of my own. And I nearly let the best thing that ever happened to me walk out of my life because I was scared of a train platform." I turned and looked at Theo, who was staring at me like I'd grown a second head, or possibly like he'd never seen me before. "So no, Councillor, I don't have a tidy economic argument. I have a true one. Those are rarer."

I sat down. My heart was doing something structurally unsound.

Whitfield opened her mouth — and then Theo stood up.

* * *

**Theo**

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it teaches you exactly one skill, which is how to speak at funerals. How to stand up in front of strangers with your voice level and say true things while your whole body screams. I'd done it once, at thirty-four, in front of two hundred people, and I had sworn never again.

Ansel had just broken every rule she'd spent a lifetime building, in public, for me. She'd mentioned the money — the money I told her I didn't want, the money she'd insisted on anyway, waving the papers at me last night in the bakery at midnight with flour on her sleeve and terror in her eyes she thought I couldn't see. She'd mentioned her mother. She'd never once told me the actual words, but there they were, laid out on the council carpet like an offering.

She'd said *the best thing that ever happened to me* and then sat down before anyone could ask her to elaborate.

Cowardice, they call it, when you hide behind dough at three a.m. Courage, apparently, is what happens after.

I stood up. Ansel's hand shot out and grabbed my wrist. "Theo," she whispered, "you don't have to—"

"Mr. Marsh," said Whitfield, with the weary tone of a woman whose agenda was already running forty minutes late. "You have the floor."

"I'm a baker," I said. "I'm going to be brief, because if I talk too long I'll start describing crumb structure and lose everyone." A ripple of something that might have been laughter moved through the public benches. Mrs. Osei was in the second row, arms crossed, nodding like a metronome. "Six months ago my wife died. Eleven months ago I got the demolition notice. I want to tell you which one kept me up at night more, but honestly, some nights it was a tie."

Beside me, Ansel went very still.

"I kept baking anyway. Because that's what she told me to do, the last real conversation we had. She said, *the oven doesn't care how you feel, so give your feelings somewhere else to go.* So I gave them to the neighbourhood. Free loaves during the strikes. Bread for the food bank on Mare Street. Teaching Priya's kids to shape baguettes on Saturdays because their mum needed two hours to herself." I looked down the table at the councillors, one by one, the way Ansel had taught me — eye contact, she'd said, is just generosity performed well. "You have a report in front of you that says this building is worth 1.4 million as residential units. That number is correct. It is also incomplete. Buildings are worth what happens inside them. I know that sounds like sentiment. But I watched a woman plan weddings for a decade without believing in a single one of them, and I watched her believe in mine."

I turned to Ansel. This was the part I hadn't rehearsed. This was the part I'd sworn off along with funerals.

"Ansel. You stood up just now and burned down fifteen years of your family's expectations in under three minutes, and you did it for a man whose business plan is written on the back of a flour sack." Someone laughed; I didn't stop. "I came here today to save a bakery. Standing here, I've realised I don't actually care about the bricks. I care about the woman who walked down my stairs at midnight looking for coffee and stayed for the proofing. I love you. I have loved you since the rehearsal, probably, when you laughed at my terrible joke about croissants and then immediately pretended you hadn't. I'm saying it here, in front of five councillors and Mrs. Osei, because you taught me that the true things should be said out loud, in rooms, where they count."

Silence. The kind with weight to it.

Ansel was looking at me with an expression I'd only seen once before — through the window of the bakery, the night she thought I couldn't see her, watching the dough prove like it was a miracle. Except now it wasn't through glass. Now it was aimed directly at me, and I was standing in the middle of it with flour under my fingernails and nothing left to hide behind.

* * *

**Ansel**

He loved me.

He said it in a council chamber, in front of Councillor Whitfield's disapproving glasses and sixty-three signatures' worth of neighbours, and the world did not end. The ceiling did not fall. My father, three hundred miles away, did not materialise in the doorway with disappointment radiating off him like heat.

I had built my entire life on the theory that love, spoken plainly, was a structural failure waiting to happen. That if you never declared anything, nothing could collapse. Twenty years of engineering, and Theo Marsh had just taken a sledgehammer to the load-bearing wall in front of witnesses, and I was still standing. We both were.

"You absolute idiot," I whispered.

"I know," he whispered back, grinning. "It felt great."

Whitfield cleared her throat with the force of a woman reclaiming territory. "The committee will deliberate. Mr. Marsh, Ms. Okafor, thank you for your... candour." She said *candour* the way my aunt Chiamaka said *drama*, which is to say: with deep personal grievance. "We'll reconvene in twenty minutes."

Twenty minutes. In wedding terms, that was the gap between the ceremony and the reception, the stretch where everything could go wrong — the caterer late, the rain arriving, the best man missing. Twenty minutes was not deliberation time. Twenty minutes was a formality. I had read enough committees to know that decisions were made in the pause before the recess, in the moment a room decides what kind of story it wants to tell about itself.

We filed out into the corridor. Mrs. Osei found us immediately, gripping Theo's arm. "You told them about the Saturday children," she said. "Good boy. That's what they needed. Numbers they can't argue with are children." She patted his cheek and swept off toward the vending machine like a general reviewing troops.

Theo turned to me. Up close, in the fluorescent light, he looked wrecked and lit-from-within simultaneously, a man who had jumped and hadn't yet checked whether there was a net.

"So," he said. "That happened."

"That happened."

"I meant it. All of it. Especially the croissant joke part, which, for the record, was a good joke."

"It was a terrible joke."

"You laughed."

"I panicked," I said. "There's a difference."

He stepped closer. Down the corridor, Mrs. Osei was aggressively not watching us, which in Mrs. Osei's language meant watching us with her full attention via the reflective surface of the vending machine glass.

"Ansel." His voice dropped into the register he used for serious things, the one that always made me want to check the oven. "You don't have to say anything back. Not here. Not like this. I didn't say it to—"

"I know why you said it." I took a breath, and it shook, which annoyed me enormously. Fifteen years of composure, undone by a man with flour in his hairline. "You said it because it was true, and because you're the kind of person who thinks true things belong in rooms. And you're right. That's the worst part. You're right about everything and it's infuriating and I—" My voice cracked. I pressed on, because apparently today was the day I stopped retreating behind logistics. "I love you too. I have since the train. Possibly since the rehearsal. Definitely since you argued with your landlord through the floorboards and I thought, *there, that one, that's the sound of someone who refuses to be small.*"

His face did something complicated and wonderful.

"And I'm putting my savings into the bakery," I added, "so if you ruin this relationship, I will follow you into whatever you do next out of pure financial necessity. Consider yourself warned."

"Romantic," he said, and kissed me — right there, corridor, fluorescents, vending machine reflection and all — and it was nothing like the almost-kiss on the veranda, which had been all hesitation and interrupted air. This one was certain. This one had a signature scored into it.

* * *

**Theo**

They called us back in nineteen minutes, which even I understood was fast.

Whitfield read out the decision in the flat voice of a woman conceding a point she'd fought against losing: the preservation order was granted, the demolition notice rescinded pending the restoration plan, conditional on proof of funds within fourteen days. Conditional. There was always a condition. Life was a lease with clauses.

But Ansel slid the transfer documents across the table before Whitfield finished the sentence, and I watched five councillors look at forty-two thousand pounds and a woman who had clearly been ready for this since before she walked in, and something in the room's posture changed. We weren't petitioners anymore. We were a going concern.

Outside, the light had gone gold and low, the way London does sometimes in autumn as an apology for the rest of the year. Mrs. Osei was on the steps telling anyone who'd listen that she'd known all along, which was a lie, because she'd told me in March that the Okafor woman was "obviously using you, dear, men like you are easy to use." I decided to let her have it. Victories need narrators.

Ansel came down the steps and stood next to me on the pavement, and for a moment neither of us said anything. Across the road, a bus went past with an advert for a wedding fair. I almost laughed.

"Fourteen days," she said finally. "Restoration plan, proof of funds, structural survey. Do you understand what you've gotten yourself into? You've hired a wedding planner with a deadline and a grudge against compromise."

"I'm terrified," I said honestly.

"Good." She smiled, and it was the real one, the unguarded one, the one I'd only caught twice before through windows and half-lit kitchens. "Because I've already started a list. It's colour-coded. There's a phase two."

"There's a *phase two?*"

"Ansel Okonkwo-Okafor does not do things without a phase two." She paused, hearing herself, and winced. "That came out wrong. Ansel Okafor. Just — the name situation is complicated, we'll discuss it later, don't make that face."

"What face?"

"The face that says you're going to propose with a loaf of bread someday."

"I would never," I said, already planning it.

She laughed — full, unarmoured, the laugh from the talent night, the one that had fooled absolutely no one — and took my hand, right there on the pavement, in front of the bus stop and Mrs. Osei and God and everybody.

Then her phone buzzed. She glanced at it, and I watched the joy on her face rearrange itself around something new: her father's name on the screen, and beneath it, a message she read twice before showing me.

*"Your aunt tells me there was news from the council. Come home for Sunday lunch. Both of you. Bring the baker. We will talk."*

Ansel looked up at me. "Well," she said. "Phase two just scheduled itself."
