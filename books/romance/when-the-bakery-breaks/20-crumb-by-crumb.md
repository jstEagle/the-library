## 20. Crumb by Crumb

The first thing we rebuilt was the door.

Not the most romantic choice, I'll grant you. Most people, given a building saved from demolition and a love story fresh off a council hearing, would start with something photogenic—the sign, maybe, or the window display, or one of those slow-motion moments where two people paint a wall and end up painted instead. But the door had swollen shut sometime around the hearing, and Ansel Okafor did not believe in romance that required forcing your way in. She believed in hinges. She believed in things that opened when you asked them to.

So on the first Monday of our new life—our being a word I still handled like hot bakeware, careful not to grip too tight or drop it entirely—we stood in front of Marsh & Co. at seven in the morning, and she held the new hinges while I fitted them, and neither of us said anything profound, and it was, without competition, the best morning of my adult life.

"You're smiling at a hinge," she said.

"I'm smiling at what the hinge represents."

"The hinge represents nine pounds forty from the builders' merchant."

"It represents access," I said. "Possibility. A threshold between the world and everything I've got."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she said, "That's going in my vows," and then went very still, the way people do when they've said something out loud before deciding whether they meant it, and I pretended to be very interested in the screwdriver, and we let it lie there between us like dough left to prove. Some things you don't punch down. You just cover them with a cloth and wait.

* * *

The repairs took six weeks, which is to say they took four weeks of work and two weeks of Ansel reorganizing the work.

I learned quickly that there are three kinds of people in this world: people who see a problem, people who see a solution, and people who see a problem and immediately produce a spreadsheet with colour-coded dependencies. Ansel is the third kind. Within a week of the hearing she had a project plan for the bakery's restoration that ran to eleven tabs, and a rota for the volunteers, and a laminated copy of the council's conditions of approval that she'd annotated in red pen until it looked like a war map.

"Ansel," I said, watching her argue with an electrician about the placement of a socket, "it's a socket."

"It's *workflow*, Theo. If the socket goes there, the mixer goes there, and if the mixer goes there you'll be walking into the counter every morning for the rest of your life, and I refuse to be complicit in that."

The electrician caught my eye over her shoulder with the expression of a man who has been managed by professionals before. I shrugged. *She's right, mate. She's always right. That's the whole situation.*

She was always right. That was the whole situation.

What surprised me—what kept surprising me, morning after morning, like a proof that keeps rising past where you thought it would stop—was how ordinary it all became. Not ordinary in the disappointing sense. Ordinary in the miraculous sense, which is a sentence I never expected to write, because I'd spent three years believing that the miraculous and the ordinary were opposites, that wonder was something that happened to other people in other lives, and that mine had ended on a Tuesday in March with a hospital corridor and a cup of tea gone cold in my hand.

But here is what nobody tells you about grief, or maybe somebody tells you and you don't listen because grief makes you deaf: it doesn't end. It just gets company.

I still baked for Claire. Every Thursday, the same as ever—a honey loaf, her favourite, the recipe in her handwriting taped inside the cupboard door with its grease-spotted corners and its instruction to "beat until it looks right, Theo, you'll know." For three years that loaf had been a wound I tended on purpose, keeping it open so I could prove the loss was real. Now, on the first Thursday after the hearing, Ansel came down at midnight—she still didn't sleep properly, some part of me found this deeply comforting—and found me weighing flour, and instead of saying anything she tied on the spare apron and started zesting oranges, because the honey loaf wanted orange zest and Claire had always said so, and Ansel had read the card twice and remembered.

We baked it together. We ate the heel ends standing up at two in the morning, leaning against the counter, flour on both of us, and Ansel said, "She sounds like someone I'd have liked," and I said, "She'd have liked you back, God help us both," and that was the whole conversation, and it cost nothing, and it healed something I hadn't known was still bleeding.

Love as daily practice. That's what I'd have called it if I'd been the sort of man who wrote things down. Mostly I just called it Thursday.

* * *

The sign went up in week five.

I'd budgeted for a modest one—MARSH & CO., BAKERS, in sensible black lettering, the same as the old sign, because sentiment is expensive and I am, per Ansel, "a man who mourns in Helvetica." But Ansel had opinions, and Ansel had a cousin of a cousin who did signage, and somehow between the two of those facts I came home from a supply run to find a crate on the pavement outside the shop and my girlfriend—I was using the word by then, testing it in private like a new oven, checking whether it scorched—standing over it with the expression of a woman who has already won the argument we haven't had yet.

"Open it," she said.

Inside, wrapped in foam, was a hand-painted sign. MARSH & OKAFOR, it said, in letters that were warm gold against deep green, with a wheat sheaf curling through the ampersand like it had grown there.

"I can't put your name on my shop," I said. "It's not—it's my debt, my lease, my—"

"Your name alone says *widower holding on*," she said, not unkindly. "Both names say *something new*. Besides—" and here she did the thing she does, the thing that undoes me, where she looks away first, "—I'm moving in above it. It seems only fair the building knows who's up there."

Because that was the other thing. The flat above the bakery, the one I'd kept as storage since Claire died because I couldn't face the question of what to do with a home meant for two—Ansel had stood in it for exactly ninety seconds during the first week of repairs, looked at the boxes, looked at me, and said, "This is where my studio goes," and that was that. No discussion. There was never any discussion. There was only Ansel, arriving at a decision the way weather arrives at a coastline.

Her resignation letter went in the following Monday. I know because she read it aloud to me while I shaped baguettes, and it was four sentences long, and the fourth sentence was "*Effective immediately,*" and she hit send before I could point out that she hadn't actually told her firm she was leaving, she'd just told her phone, and apparently those were the same thing now.

"They'll be furious," I said.

"They'll be fine. Marcus can run the high-profile accounts; he's been angling for them for years. And honestly?" She set the phone face-down on the counter with a finality that made me slightly afraid for the phone. "I spent eight years planning other people's perfect days and going home to nothing. I'd rather build one imperfect thing that's mine."

"*Ours*," I said, before my caution could catch it.

She smiled at the dough, not at me, which was somehow more devastating. "Ours," she agreed. "Obviously ours. I'm not doing the books alone, Theo, I've seen your filing system, it's a shoebox."

"It's an *organised* shoebox."

"It's a shoebox with aspirations."

* * *

The studio above the bakery opened—unofficially, one desk, one kettle, one wall of corkboard—in week seven, and I will tell you what it did to me, having Ansel Okafor planning weddings ten feet above my head.

Every morning I'd come up with coffee and find her mid-call, pacing the length of the room, telling some bride that no, the marquee wouldn't collapse, and yes, the string quartet could learn the song, and that under no circumstances should her mother-in-law be seated within throwing distance of the open bar. She'd take the coffee without breaking stride, drink half of it scalding, and I'd go back down to my ovens thinking: *there it is.* The thing she'd lost. Not the belief in weddings—that was still missing, she flinched every time a client used the word *forever*—but the belief in herself as someone whose work mattered. Watching her, I understood she'd never stopped believing in love. She'd only stopped believing she was allowed any.

Some days she'd come down covered in ribbon samples and steal a croissant off the cooling rack, and I'd swat at her with a bench scraper, and she'd kiss my cheek and leave flour handprints on my shirt, and I'd wear them all day like a man wearing a medal.

"You've got—" customers would say, gesturing.

"I know," I'd say. "I know exactly what I've got."

* * *

There were hard mornings, of course. I want to be honest about that, because the version of this story where everything is golden light and banter is a lie, and lies make bad bread.

There was the morning in week eight when the bank statement came and the numbers were worse than we'd hoped, even with the settlement money, even with Ansel quietly transferring funds she claimed were "an investment, Theo, stop making it a whole thing." I sat on the flour sacks behind the shop and did the arithmetic of failure, the old familiar arithmetic, and Ansel found me there and didn't fix it, didn't spreadsheet it, just sat down next to me in the dust and put her head on my shoulder and let the silence do the work. "We're not going anywhere," she said eventually. "You and me or the bakery. Neither one. Get used to it."

There was the evening her father rang, and I heard only her side of it—short answers, longer silences, the word *Daddy* once, very quietly, like something dropped—and afterward she came downstairs and asked if she could stand in the kitchen while I worked, and I said she could stand in the kitchen whenever she liked for the rest of her life, and she laughed wetly and chopped onions for stock until she wasn't crying anymore, or until the onions could plausibly take the blame. He hadn't blessed anything. He hadn't cursed anything either. He'd said he would "come and see for himself," which from Chief Okonkwo, I was learning, was less a promise than a weather warning.

And there was the night I woke at three from a dream of Claire—not a sad dream, just ordinary, her laughing at something on the telly—and lay in the dark of the flat that was finally a home again, with Ansel asleep beside me, and felt the old guilt arrive on schedule like a delivery van. *Is this allowed? Am I allowed?*

Ansel, who slept like a cat and woke like one, rolled over and said, without opening her eyes: "Whatever it is, yes."

"How did you—"

"You breathe differently. Go back to sleep. Yes. Both things. Sleep."

So I did. Love as daily practice. Sometimes the practice is bread. Sometimes it's sitting in flour dust not fixing anything. Sometimes it's a woman answering a question you haven't asked, at three in the morning, in four words.

* * *

By week ten, the bakery was unrecognisable—or rather, it was recognisable again, which amounted to the same thing. New wiring, new floor, the old tiles scrubbed back to their original green. The counter Claire and I had installed young and broke and optimistic survived, scarred but sound, and I decided scars were permitted. The neighbours who'd signed the petition started coming in—Mrs. Adeyemi from the salon with her Saturday order, the boys from the bike shop who'd discovered sourdough like it was a new football team. On Sundays we did a community table, whatever was left over, pay-what-you-can, and Ansel ran it with the ruthless warmth of a woman who had once seated four hundred wedding guests without a single feud.

One evening near the end of the month, she came down after close carrying an envelope, and I knew before she spoke, because envelopes after hours are never good news unless they contain cheques, and this one was thin.

"What is it?"

She handed it over. Heavy cream paper. My heart performed a small, professional-grade collapse before I read it.

*Chief & Mrs. Okonkwo request the pleasure... The Annual Okafor Family Reunion...*

"One year ago," Ansel said, "you walked into that compound as a lie I'd hired. Well. As a lie I'd hired and then, you know. Kept." She was doing the thing where she talked fast when she was nervous, the thing I'd catalogued along with everything else about her, the way you catalogue a country you intend to live in. "He's inviting you back. Both of us. By name. On proper stationery, Theo, my father does not own proper stationery, I think Aunt Chiamaka bought it for him."

"And how do you feel about that?"

"I feel like last year I introduced my fake boyfriend to my family and this year I get to introduce my—" she stopped, recalibrated, tried again. "My boyfriend. Who bakes. Who stayed. Whose name is on the sign." Her voice did something small and true on the last word. "I feel like I want to walk in holding your hand and dare anyone to say a word."

I looked at the envelope. I looked at her. Outside, the new sign caught the streetlight—both names, gold on green, growing through each other like the wheat in the ampersand.

"When do we leave?" I said.

She grinned, and it was the grin from the train north, from the reunion, from the veranda where we almost—no, not almost, not anymore; we'd long since finished that sentence. "Saturday," she said. "Pack the good shirt. And Theo?"

"Mm?"

"Last year they interrogated you for two hours about your intentions." She kissed me, tasting faintly of the day's sugar and entirely of trouble. "This year, try to look like you've already decided."
