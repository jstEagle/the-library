## 4. What the Fire Took

The letter sat on my desk for three days before I answered it.

Not because I didn't want to. Because I did, and wanting things had never gone well for me.

*What made you the way you are?* he'd asked, in that careful handwriting that slanted like a man leaning into wind. *You write like someone who's been warm once and decided against it.*

Which was rude, frankly. And accurate. And the kind of question nobody in Saltmere asked me anymore because everyone in Saltmere already knew the answer, or thought they did, and their version of it was wrong.

I told myself I'd answer honestly. I'd signed up for this program precisely because anonymity was supposed to be a kind of honesty — a stranger with no face to read, no history to weigh, no reason to flinch when I said something true. That was the whole point. The librarian had sold it to me that way, sliding the flyer across her desk like contraband: *Say things you can't say in town.*

So I sat down at the desk in the lighthouse's old watch room, where my grandfather used to chart weather systems nobody needed charted anymore, and I tried to tell the truth.

*Dear Wren* — his chosen name, a bird that sings at dusk — *you want to know what made me. All right. Here is a thing I have never written down:*

*I grew up in a bakery.*

And then I stopped, because the words opened a door and the door opened onto fire.

* * *

Five years ago, on the fourth of November, I woke at 2:17 in the morning to a sky the color of an open wound.

I know the time because I checked it afterward, obsessively, the way you check a wound to see if it's still bleeding. My phone said 2:17 when my mother's call came through, and her voice said only my name, twice, and then nothing, and I drove the nine minutes from my apartment above the chandlery with my hands shaking so hard I nearly put the car in the harbor.

By the time I arrived, half of Saltmere was already there, standing in the cold in pajamas and coats thrown over nightclothes, watching the Ashgrove Bakery burn.

It burned beautifully. That's the thing nobody tells you about fires — the ones big enough to take everything also give you something to look at. The windows glowed orange from inside, like the ovens had all been lit at once, like every loaf my mother had ever baked had caught at the same moment. Embers went up in spirals. The heat reached us across the street, a pressure against the face, and the smell — God, the smell. Burning bread smells almost like baking bread. That's the cruelty of it. My childhood rose out of those windows as smoke, sweet and terrible, and half the town probably stood there thinking of toast.

My mother stood on the cobbles in her apron. She'd run out mid-shift, apparently — she'd been doing the overnight proofing alone since they cut hours — and she still had flour on her hands, white dusting the black soot, and she was watching it burn the way you watch a thing you can't believe.

"It started in the back," she kept saying. To anyone. To no one. "It started in the back wall, but we just had that wiring looked at—"

She was right. We had. An electrician named Tom Brice had come through in September, given the wiring a clean bill, written it up, charged us forty pounds and told my mother the building would outlive us all.

That detail mattered later. It mattered enormously. But at 2:30 in the morning, standing in the street while the roof groaned and folded inward like something exhaling, all it meant was that my mother was confused, and confusion in a woman who runs a business gets translated, quickly, into fault.

* * *

The investigator came three days later.

Callum Vane. I remember everything about that first sight of him because I hated him so completely and so immediately that hatred burned the memory in like a brand. He was younger than I expected — early thirties, tall, with the kind of quiet competence that reads as arrogance if you're grieving. He wore a gray jacket that smelled faintly of smoke even though he hadn't been near our fire yet. He carried a clipboard he barely looked at. He walked the ruin in soft-soled boots, crouching at intervals, touching things gently, and I stood behind the tape with my arms crossed and waited for him to find what I already knew: that this wasn't our fault.

He took eleven days.

Eleven days during which my mother slept at my apartment because she couldn't bear the smell of the flat above the bakery, which smelled of smoke now too, everything in Saltmere smelling of smoke for weeks. Eleven days during which the town's sympathy curdled by degrees into something more useful to it — curiosity. Speculation. The bakery had been struggling; everyone knew the bakery had been struggling. Insurance had been discussed. Hadn't it? Someone's cousin worked in insurance. Someone else had seen my mother counting money behind the counter with a look on her face.

Eleven days, and then Callum Vane held a folder under his arm and delivered his findings in the back room of the library, of all places, with my mother sitting straight-backed in a plastic chair and me beside her with my hand clamped over hers.

"Origin point consistent with the rear wall cavity," he said. "Electrical fault in aged wiring." A pause. "However."

However. That word has lived in my chest ever since. However.

"The ignition pattern suggests the fault was aggravated by accumulated grease residue in the wall voids — residue consistent with long-term deferred maintenance." His eyes came up, and they were not unkind, and that was somehow worse. "Ms. Ashgrove, your mother's own records show the deep cleaning was scheduled and postponed four times in two years."

"We couldn't afford—" my mother started.

"The ruling," he said, not unkindly, "is electrical fault, aggravated by operator negligence."

Operator negligence.

Four words. You could fit them on a coin. They cost my mother the insurance payout — reduced, contested, dragged through eight months of appeals — and they cost her the town, which had loved her bread and now loved the story better. *Careless Maren Ashgrove.* It followed her through the market, through the church queue, through five years of small kindnesses that were really pity. She stopped baking entirely. She sold the recipe books. When people ask her now how she's keeping busy, she says *oh, a bit of this and that,* and her hands stay very still in her lap, and I am the only one who knows they're still shaped around a rolling pin.

And Callum Vane signed the report. I've seen the signature. I've had five years to memorize it: a sharp, economical scrawl, the letters leaning forward like a man in a hurry to be somewhere the report couldn't follow.

Here is what I did with my grief, in the years after: I sharpened it. I honed it the way you hone a blade, on a stone of nightly repetition — *he ruled against us, she wasn't careless, the wiring was fine, he signed it anyway.* Grief without a shape is unbearable. So I gave mine a job. I made it my editor, my discipline, my reason for writing love stories in which nobody gets to be careless with anybody. Every novel I publish under Juniper Vale contains at least one character who refuses to forgive too easily, and readers write to me saying they love her fire, and I think: *if you knew what fed it.*

That's what the fire took, ultimately. Not the building. Buildings go up again. It took my mother's name, and gave me a cause, and causes make excellent company and terrible homes.

* * *

In the present, the lighthouse ticked around me in its old way — settling timbers, the gulls arguing on the gallery rail — and my pen hovered over the page.

*Dear Wren,* I wrote again, and this time I lied.

Not wholly. I've learned that the best lies are load-bearing truths with one beam removed. So I gave him the shape of my life without the center of it:

*You asked what made me. Honestly? A fire. Not mine — my family's. A business that burned, and a ruling afterward that decided whose fault it was, and the ruling was wrong. I won't bore you with details. Suffice to say I learned young that the truth doesn't matter half as much as whoever's holding the pen.*

*Since then I've kept my world small. Lighthouse, market stall, pages. Small worlds don't burn. That's not wisdom, exactly. More like scar tissue deciding the architecture.*

*There. Now you know something real. Don't be kind about it. Kindness about grief always sounds like an autopsy report.*

I hesitated, pen above the paper, and then — because his letter had asked a second question, the one underneath the first, the one that had actually kept me up at night — I added:

*You asked if I've ever been warm. Yes. Once, thoroughly, for about nineteen years. Then I learned warmth is just the temperature things reach right before they catch.*

*Tell me something true back. Something you've never written down. Fair trade.*

I sealed it, addressed it to the library's post box, and set it by the door for morning.

Then I did what I always do after opening a vein on paper: I made tea and reread his letter, hunting for flaws, for tells, for reasons to take it all back.

*What made you the way you are?* Slanted, careful, leaning-into-wind handwriting. A stranger asking questions with a surgeon's timing. Nobody in Saltmere had ever asked me that. Everyone here had already decided what made me — *the lighthouse girl, the odd one, the daughter of Careless Maren* — and had filed me accordingly years ago.

But this stranger wanted the actual file. Wanted the primary document. And some starving part of me — the part that had spent five years being strong and correct and unforgiving — leaned toward that attention like a plant toward a window.

I caught myself smiling at his phrasing. *Decided against it.* As if warmth were a subscription I'd cancelled. I laughed, alone in the watch room, an ugly startled sound, and pressed my hand over my mouth like I'd been caught at something.

This was dangerous. I knew it was dangerous. Borrowed warmth is still warmth; the body doesn't audit the source. I was pouring into a stranger's PO box the tenderness I rationed everywhere else — my mother got duty, the town got politeness, my readers got fiction — and this nameless man with his careful questions was getting the unrationed version, the draft that hadn't been edited for safety.

I should have stopped there. Any sensible person would have.

Instead I took out a fresh sheet and wrote a P.S.:

*P.S. — You write like someone who's seen a lot of endings. What do you do? Professionally, I mean. And don't say "nothing interesting." Nothing-interesting people don't ask questions like yours.*

I sealed that too, before I could reconsider, and stacked it on top of the first letter.

Outside, the sea did its endless arithmetic against the rocks, and the decommissioned lamp turned slowly overhead on its maintenance motor, sweeping its useless beam across water no ship needed guided. My grandfather used to say the light wasn't for the ships, really — it was for the keeper, a way of proving every night that you were still paying attention.

I fell asleep at the desk with his letter under my cheek, and dreamed, as I always do, of the bakery burning — except in the dream, this time, someone was reading the flames like a language, taking notes, and when I woke at dawn with the ink of my own letters smudged on my jaw, I found I couldn't remember whether the note-taker in the dream had been a stranger at all.

His face, in the dream, had been familiar.

I told myself it was nothing. I mailed the letters.
