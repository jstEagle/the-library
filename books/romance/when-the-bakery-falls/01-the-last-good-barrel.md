## 1. The Last Good Barrel

The bottling machine had a name, though Oscar Underhill was the only one who used it. *Gertrude.* His grandfather had christened her in 1974, back when she ran clean and true, and Oscar kept the name the way you keep a dead man's hat — out of habit, out of something you'd be embarrassed to call love.

Tonight Gertrude was wheezing.

He stood in the cellar at eleven p.m., sleeves rolled, forearms slick with wine and the sweat of a September that wouldn't break, feeding empty bottles into her jaws one at a time because the automatic feed had died in June and he couldn't afford the part. The overhead bulb swung on its cord when the compressor kicked, throwing his shadow long across the concrete floor, across the rows of barrels stacked three high like sleeping cattle.

Four hundred and twelve bottles. That's what tonight would yield if Gertrude held together. Four hundred and twelve bottles of the 2019 — or what he'd been calling the 2019, though technically it was whatever the label printer said it was, because labels were another thing he printed himself now, on a machine older than he was, in runs of fifty so the ink never ran dry mid-word.

*Underhill Vineyards. Estate Bottled.*

*Estate* was generous. The estate was forty acres of vines, half of them past saving, a farmhouse with a roof that sang in high wind, and a cellar that smelled of damp stone and honest work. It was also mortgaged to its roots, but nobody put that on a label.

The filler hissed. A bottle filled. He capped it, set it in the crate, reached for the next one. Fill, cap, crate. Fill, cap, crate. There was a meditation in it, if you let there be. His mother used to say the vineyard taught patience the way drowning teaches swimming.

His mother had been gone four years now. Some nights it still caught him sideways — reaching for the phone to ask her about sulfur levels, about whether to pick early before the rain, and remembering, mid-reach, that the only person who ever knew the answer was in the ground behind the chapel on the hill, under a stone he'd paid for in installments.

Fill, cap, crate.

Outside, the night pressed warm against the cellar door. Tomorrow the crew would come — six men this year instead of fourteen; the ones who stayed did it for less than they were worth, out of loyalty or pity, Oscar had stopped asking which — and they'd walk the east slope rows in the dark with headlamps and picking knives, and by noon the bins would come down heavy with fruit that might, if the weather held and the bank held and Oscar held, become something worth drinking.

Might. The whole operation had narrowed down to that one word.

He wiped his hands on his thighs and climbed the stairs to the kitchen, where the table waited under its single lamp like an altar. On it: two stacks of paper. The stack on the left was new, thick, rubber-banded. Final notices, mostly. One from the bank with a font chosen specifically to feel like a hand on your shoulder. One from a supplier who'd been patient for two years and wasn't anymore. One he hadn't opened yet because the return address was a law firm and law firms only wrote when something was over.

The stack on the right was a shoebox.

Cardboard, lid soft at the corners, once white and now the color of weak tea. *WINTER BOOTS,* said the faded print on the side, in a store that no longer existed. Inside: letters. A hundred and six of them, give or take — he'd counted once, drunk, on a New Year's Eve he preferred not to remember — each one postmarked from towns up and down the coast, each one signed with a single word.

*Wren.*

Ten years of Wren. Ten years of a stranger writing to a man she'd never met, telling him things like:

*Dear Oscar — I read somewhere that old vines make better wine because they've suffered. I don't know if that's true of grapes or just something people say to feel better about hard years. But I hope it's true. I hope everything hard in you is making something better. — W*

He'd written back, at first. Eager, clumsy letters full of harvest numbers and complaints about weather, the way you talk to someone you're afraid will stop listening. Then the replies got shorter, his life got smaller, and somewhere around year seven he'd started keeping her letters without answering them — reading them the way you look at a fire you can't afford to feed.

The last one had come in March. Eleven months ago.

He didn't open the shoebox often anymore. Opening it felt like pressing on a bruise to check it still hurt, and it always did. But tonight the cellar was done, the crates were stacked, tomorrow was the beginning of the end or the end of the end, and he wanted — God, he wanted — one voice in the house that wasn't a creditor's.

He untied the twine. Her handwriting slanted right, quick and sure, the loops of her *g*s open like birds taking off. He'd know it anywhere. He'd know it in the dark.

*Dear Oscar,*

*I'm sorry it's been so long. Life has been doing that thing where it picks you up and shakes you, and I find I have nothing worth saying until the shaking stops.*

*But here is what I know tonight: somewhere on your hill, the grapes are almost ready. Somewhere you are counting barrels the way other men count money, and thinking the same desperate arithmetic. So let me tell you the thing I tell myself: a harvest doesn't have to save you. It only has to happen. You show up, you cut the fruit, you do the work in front of you. Saving is a longer story.*

*Pick well, Oscar. I'll be thinking of you on the morning the bins come in heavy.*

*— Wren*

Dated March 3rd. She'd known. Somehow, ten years in, she knew the shape of his seasons better than he did.

Oscar sat down at the table with the letter in both hands, careful as a man holding a bird. The lamp buzzed. The refrigerator cycled off and left the house in that deep quiet that country houses fall into after midnight, the quiet that makes small sounds enormous.

He should write back.

The thought arrived the way it always did, sudden and unreasonable, and he pushed it away the way he always did. What would he even say? *Dear Wren, the bank gave me thirty days. Dear Wren, I sold the truck. Dear Wren, I am thirty-eight years old and the sum total of my attachments is a dying bottling machine and a shoebox of letters from a woman who may not exist.*

Because that was the thing he'd never admitted sober: some nights he wasn't sure she did. A hundred and six letters, ten years, and not one photograph, not one phone number, not one slip of a last name. Just *Wren*, moving up and down the coast like a bird that wintered nowhere. Once, drunker than the New Year's Eve, he'd written a letter addressed to nothing — no town, no street — and walked it to the mailbox at the end of the drive anyway, and stood there holding it over the slot like an offering to a god he suspected was imaginary.

He'd burned it in the woodstove. It seemed more honest.

But tonight — tonight the letter sat light in his hands, and the pen was right there in the drawer where it always was, and the blank paper sat in front of him whiter than anything else in the room.

*Dear Wren,* he wrote. Then stopped.

Then: *Tomorrow we pick.*

Then nothing for a long time. The pen hovered. Outside, a wind came up the valley and moved through the vines, and the whole hillside made that low rushing sound, like the land itself breathing, and Oscar thought about how his mother used to stand on the porch in weather like this and say, *Listen. That's the sound of a place deciding whether to keep you.*

He looked at the two words on the page. *Tomorrow we pick.* As if she were here. As if there were a *we*. As if a man could write three words to a ghost and call it correspondence.

He folded the paper in half, then in half again, and put it in his shirt pocket instead of the mailbox. Close enough. Honest enough.

The lamp buzzed. He sat a while longer, letting himself want things — the wanting was brief, controlled, like opening a window in winter — and then he stood, tied the shoebox closed, and carried it to the shelf above the sink where it lived between the flour and the salt, the most valuable thing in the house and the least explicable.

In the morning the crew would come. In the morning the bins would fill. And in thirty days, if the festival week went badly, the bank would take all of it — the vines, the cellar, Gertrude, the porch where his mother had listened to the wind.

He turned off the lamp and stood in the dark kitchen, and that was when he heard it: tires on gravel, slow, coming up the county road next door. Headlights swept the ceiling through the window, swung wide, and stopped.

Next door. At the Hollis cottage — derelict eight years, windows boarded, ivy eating the north wall. Nobody bought the Hollis cottage. Nobody had even looked at it since the bank took it back from the second failed owner.

Oscar stood at the dark window and watched a rented box truck idle in the cottage drive, its hazards blinking orange against the black, and a figure step down from the cab and shine a phone flashlight up at the boarded porch like a woman checking whether she'd made a terrible mistake.

At eleven forty on the night before harvest, of all the nights, of all the houses.

He told himself it was none of his business. He told himself he had four hundred and twelve bottles and a five a.m. start and no spare attention for strangers.

He watched until the light went out.

* * *

He was up at four-thirty, coffee standing in for breakfast, and out the door before the sky had committed to anything. The crew was already gathering at the top of the east slope, headlamps bobbing in the gray like fireflies, and Oscar fell into the rhythm of it — knife, bunch, bin; knife, bunch, bin — and let the work do what work does, which is hold a man together while he isn't looking.

By noon the bins came down heavy. By two the crush pad smelled of crushed fruit and possibility. By four, filthy to the elbows, he was hosing down the press when he heard the truck again — and looked up to see the stranger from last night standing at his fence line, hands in her pockets, surveying his mud and his chaos with an expression he couldn't read at fifty feet and didn't try to.

She called something over the noise. He killed the engine.

"I said," she repeated, "I hope I'm not interrupting a religious experience."

"You are," Oscar said. "That's the press."

"Marguerite Bellamy." She didn't offer a hand, hers being occupied holding a covered dish. "I bought the Hollis place. Closed yesterday. I appear to own a roof that leaks in four locations."

"It leaks in five. You haven't had rain yet."

Something crossed her face then — quick, there and gone, like a fish under ice. Recognition, maybe, or its cousin. And when she spoke again, her voice did a strange small thing in the middle of the sentence, a cadence bending toward a note he'd heard somewhere before and couldn't place, a song half-remembered from a room he'd never been in.

"Well," she said. "I suppose I'll find out."

She handed him the dish — bread, still warm, which was its own kind of suspicious — wished him luck with his religious experience, and walked back across the stubbled field toward the cottage that had been dead for eight years and had, as of yesterday, a light on inside it.

Oscar stood in the wet, holding warm bread, feeling the strangest sensation: that his life had just changed shape, and that whatever came through the fence line next was going to reach all the way down to the shoebox on the shelf.

He went back to the press. He tried not to think about her voice.

It didn't work.
