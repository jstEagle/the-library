## 7. Arrival at Ravensworth House

The ferry had barely finished bumping against the dock before I heard my aunt's voice.

That was the thing about Vivienne Ravensworth-Hale — she didn't wait for rooms or shorelines to announce her presence. She simply spoke at a volume calibrated for cathedrals and expected the world to arrange itself accordingly. From forty yards away, across a parking lot gravelled with crushed oyster shell, I could hear her instructing Charles on where to park the rental car, and then, when he presumably failed to do it correctly, instructing him again.

Theo set down our bags beside me on the dock. "Is that—"

"My aunt. Yes."

"She sounds like a foghorn with opinions."

"That's the most accurate description of Vivienne I've ever heard, and I've had thirty-one years to work on it." I smoothed my coat, checked that my smile was where I'd left it, and picked up my bag. "Remember: three months, lighthouse first date, you fix boats for a living but not *only* boats, and if anyone asks about your mother you light up like a chapel."

"Got it." He fell into step beside me, close enough that our shoulders nearly touched, which was either good acting or something else I wasn't ready to name. "And your grandmother?"

I hesitated. "Iris is different."

"Different how?"

"Different in the way that weather is different from climate. Vivienne is a storm — loud, dramatic, gone by morning. Iris is the climate itself. She's been here longer than all of us, she'll be here after, and she notices everything."

Theo was quiet for three steps. "So she's the one to worry about."

"Iris doesn't require worrying about. She requires surviving."

* * *

Ravensworth House had been built by a man who'd made his fortune in shipping and wanted everyone to know it. Three stories of grey stone, gables like raised eyebrows, a front door tall enough to admit a grand piano standing upright. My great-grandfather had commissioned it the same year he'd planted the first vines, on the theory — I suppose — that a family should have two legacies and no sense of proportion.

In childhood, the house had seemed enormous and generous, all hidden staircases and window seats deep enough to sleep in. Now, approaching it as an adult who knew exactly how much the roof repair quote had come to, it looked less like a legacy and more like a very large animal I was responsible for feeding.

Vivienne stood on the front steps in cashmere the color of heavy cream, arms crossed, surveying our approach the way an empress surveys a province that has recently begun asking questions.

"Marguerite." She said my name the way she said everything — as though it were slightly disappointing but she'd made her peace with it decades ago. "You look tired."

"Wonderful to see you too, Aunt Vivienne."

"And this must be him." Her gaze moved to Theo and performed a slow, thorough inventory — shoes, hands, jawline, the faint grease stain that had survived two washings on the collar of his good shirt. I watched her file each finding somewhere behind her eyes. "The boyfriend."

"Theo Marchetti," Theo said, and offered his hand.

Here's what I hadn't anticipated: he shook it like a man. Not the crushing, compensating handshake men sometimes deploy around powerful women, and not the deferential half-grip either. Just a firm, level, unhurried shake, accompanied by eye contact that suggested he had all the time in the world and would spend some of it on her, gladly, if she needed it.

Vivienne blinked. It was a small blink, but I'd grown up cataloguing her blinks the way other children collected stamps, and I recognized it. She'd expected to unsettle him. She had not.

"Marchetti," she repeated. "That's a working name."

"It is," Theo agreed pleasantly. "It works."

Charles appeared from behind the rental car, phone in hand, wearing the expression of a man perpetually mid-negotiation even when the only negotiation available was with a parking space. Charles was thirty-four, handsome in the way of stock photographs, and had sold his first startup at twenty-nine, a fact he managed to mention within four minutes of any conversation, including, once, a conversation conducted entirely in French with a waiter who did not speak it.

"Margo!" He hugged me with one arm, phone still live in the other hand. "This is great, this is so great. You brought someone. Finally. Dad was starting to say things."

"What kind of things?"

"You know. Dad things." He turned to Theo with a grin that was, I'll grant him, genuinely warm. "Charles Hale. Welcome to the family circus. Fair warning — Aunt Vivienne will ask what you do within ten minutes, Grandmother Iris will ask what you *believe* within twenty, and Uncle Peter will ask if you sail sometime after dinner whether or not there's a boat involved."

"There's always a boat involved," Theo said. "Eventually."

Charles laughed. Vivienne did not. And somewhere above us, in a second-floor window framed by ivy going rust-red for autumn, a curtain shifted.

"She's watching," I murmured.

"Who?"

"My grandmother. Taking attendance." I took a breath. "Showtime."

* * *

The foyer of Ravensworth House smelled the way it had smelled my entire life: beeswax, old roses from the arrangement on the hall table, and underneath it all, faintly, the mineral breath of the cellar below, where the barrels slept. The floor was black-and-white marble laid in a pattern my great-grandfather had allegedly copied from a Venetian church, on the theory that blasphemy and ambition traveled well together.

Uncle Peter materialized first, as predicted, already discussing wind patterns. Then Cousin Amelia with her husband and their twin girls, who immediately began racing up the staircase until Vivienne's voice reached them from a distance of fifty feet and stopped them mid-stride like a lasso. Then more cousins, more spouses, a small crowd assembling itself in the hall with drinks before anyone had officially arrived anywhere.

Theo stayed at my shoulder through all of it. That was the strange part. I'd braced myself for him to drift, to get cornered by Peter near the window, to be absorbed into the family machinery while I handled my own end of the room. Instead he moved when I moved, laughed at the right intervals, and when Amelia's twins demanded to know if he was a pirate — because of the earring, which I had never noticed in two years of knowing him — he crouched down to their level and told them, gravely, that he was a pirate *retired*, and that ferries were just ships that had settled down.

The twins were devastated with delight. Amelia looked at him the way one looks at a stray dog that turns out to be trained.

"He's lovely," she whispered to me, passing with a tray. "Where did you find him?"

"On the island," I said. "We grow them there."

But the whole time, the whole time, I was aware of the staircase. Of the slow descent happening on it. Iris Ravensworth did not enter rooms; she allowed them to continue existing in her vicinity. She came down one step at a time, one hand on the rail, silver hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had decided in 1962 that fussing with hair was time stolen from better pursuits, and the conversation in the hall dimmed around her like lamps being turned down for a portrait sitting.

She reached the bottom. She looked at the room. She found me.

"Marguerite." She crossed the marble without hurry. Up close she smelled of lavender and, faintly, of the cellar too — she was the only person alive who still went down there regularly, who still drew samples with a thief glass and pronounced judgment. "You've let the east rows go."

"Hello, Grandmother. I've missed you too."

"Don't be clever, it wastes both our time." Her eyes — pale grey, sharp as pruning shears — moved past my shoulder. "And this is the young man."

"This is Theo."

"So I'm told." She looked at him. And I want to be precise about what happened next, because it lasted perhaps four seconds and rearranged the entire weekend in my chest.

She didn't inspect him the way Vivienne had. There was no inventory, no filing. What she did was worse. She looked at him the way she looked at a barrel sample right before delivering a verdict — with total attention, total calm, and the patience of someone who already knew what she was going to taste.

"Marchetti," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Do you know," she said, "you're the first man Marguerite has ever brought through that door who looked at the house before he looked at the family."

Theo glanced back at the door, genuinely puzzled. "It's a good door."

Something happened at the corner of Iris's mouth. On another face it might have been called a smile. On hers it was more like a treaty being quietly signed. "It is. My father stole the design from a church in Venice and never once felt bad about it." She turned back to me. "Dinner is at seven. Your aunt has opinions about the seating. Survive them." And then, to Theo, in a voice pitched just low enough to feel like a secret: "Come find me before dessert, young man. I want to hear how you met her. I suspect the story has been polished."

She swept toward the drawing room, leaving me standing in the foyer with my heart doing something unprofessional.

"*Polished,*" I repeated weakly.

"She's perceptive," Theo said.

"She's a bloodhound in pearls." I pulled him toward the stairs. "Come on. I'll show you your room, and then we're going to spend forty minutes reviewing every detail we invented, because if Iris smells varnish on that story, she will strip it down to bare wood in front of everyone."

* * *

His room was next to mine, which had required diplomacy with Vivienne over the phone and a flat lie about separate rooms having "felt formal." The hallway between them smelled of dust and lemon polish. Through the window at the corridor's end, the vineyard fell away in long dark stripes toward the water, and beyond it, the ferry dock sat empty in the late light, the last crossing of the day already gone.

Theo stood at that window for a moment, looking out at it.

"The last ferry," he said.

"Not until October. We have all weekend." I handed him the index cards. "Now. First date. Go."

He recited it without looking: "The lighthouse, the third Saturday in June. You brought a thermos of coffee that tasted like burnt rope and apologized for it eleven times. I told you it was the best coffee I'd ever had, which was a lie, and you knew it was a lie, and we agreed it was the beginning of honesty between us."

I stared at him. "You memorized it."

"You told me to memorize it."

"I told you to remember the *outline*. You memorized the burnt rope."

"It's a good detail." He shrugged. "Details are what make lies hold weight. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone who?" I asked sharply. "Theo, what kind of lies do you normally tell that you've developed a *theory*—"

He grinned, and the question dissolved, the way most of my suspicions around him tended to dissolve, into warmth and mild self-reproach. But I noticed I'd asked it. I filed it away with the earring and the handshake and the way he'd looked at the empty dock like a man checking an exit.

Downstairs, the gong sounded — an actual gong, my great-grandfather's final flourish — and the house began to move toward dinner.

"One more thing," I said, catching his sleeve at the top of the stairs. "At the table, Charles will be charming, Vivienne will be surgical, and someone — probably Uncle Peter — will ask when we're getting married. It's a test. They ask everyone. If you panic, we fail."

"And if I don't panic?"

"Then they'll assume you're hiding something and dig harder."

Theo considered this. "So the winning move is to be honest about a fake relationship in front of people whose approval doesn't matter to me, using details we invented over wine."

"When you put it that way it sounds insane."

"It sounds like the best evening I've had in months," he said, and offered me his arm, and the terrible thing — the thing I would examine later, alone, in the dark of my childhood bedroom — was that I believed him.

We descended together into the noise and candlelight, performing the beginning of a love story, neither of us yet understanding that we'd walked onto a stage with no wings and no script and only one way out: through.

At the foot of the stairs, Iris was waiting. Not for me.

For him.
