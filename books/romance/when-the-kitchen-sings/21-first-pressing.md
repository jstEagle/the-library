## 21. First Pressing

### Marguerite

The first thing I learned about spring at Bellhaven was that it does not ask permission. One morning in late March I walked the rows with my coffee going cold in my hand, braced for another season of arithmetic — what we owed against what we could pick — and instead the vines had simply decided. Buds everywhere, fat and reckless, as if the land had read the bank's letters and found them beneath its dignity.

"They're showing off," Theo said beside me, because he had developed the habit of appearing exactly when my thoughts got too loud.

"They're gloating."

"Vines gloat?"

"Mine do. They know something I don't." I touched a bud with one finger, gentle, the way you touch anything you've been told is dying. "Six months ago these rows were a eulogy waiting to be delivered."

"And now?"

Now. The word had changed shape over the winter. Now meant restructured debt, Theo's savings folded into the loan like yeast into flour, my name on sixty-one percent of a vineyard that had nearly eaten me alive. Now meant his father's signature on new terms, signed in an office so silent that Theo said afterward he could hear the pen scratching like a small animal trying to get out. Now meant that when I drove past the gate each morning, no envelope waited there like a threat.

"Now," I said, "we press on."

He laughed at that. He laughs easily now, which still startles me. When he first came up my drive with grease on his knuckles and a toolbox older than both of us, I thought of him as a man who kept everything behind his teeth. It took me most of a year to learn that the quiet wasn't withholding. It was attention. He listened the way other people talk — completely, without waiting for his turn.

The first joint harvest began on a Tuesday, which felt right. Nothing at Bellhaven has ever happened on an auspicious day; we are not that kind of family. We are the kind that starts things on Tuesdays and apologizes for them later.

His father came.

I want that recorded somewhere, in case anyone ever tells me that men don't change: Salvatore Marchetti stood at the head of row twelve at seven in the morning wearing work gloves that were clearly new, clearly bought for the occasion, and picked grapes beside my aunt Vivienne without either of them speaking for two full hours. When they finally did speak, it was to argue about trellising methods, and by lunch they were arguing like old friends, which is to say like people who have decided the argument is the relationship.

I watched them from the crush pad and felt something in my chest unclench that I hadn't known was clenched. Two families learning to share a table is not a moment. It's a process, slow as fermentation, and mostly it smells strange and looks like nothing is happening until suddenly everything has turned.

### Theo

Here is what nobody tells you about peace: it's loud.

I grew up thinking peace would be quiet — the absence of the raised voices in our kitchen, the absence of my father's silences, which were louder than the voices. But the first morning of harvest at Bellhaven sounded like an argument between a tractor and a mockingbird, smelled like crushed fruit and diesel, and contained Marguerite shouting my name across four rows because I'd put the wrong bins on the trailer.

And I have never in my life been happier.

"You're smiling," she accused, arriving at a march, sun hat crooked, juice to the elbow. "We are losing a battle with a hydraulic hose and you're *smiling*."

"I fixed the hose."

"You fixed the hose and then smiled about it, which is insufferable."

"Noted." I handed her the water bottle she'd forgotten she was carrying around all morning. She took it, drank half, and looked at me over the rim with an expression I've stopped trying to name because every time I do, I come up with a word too big to say out loud before noon.

Her grandmother found us there. Iris Ravensworth moves through a vineyard the way weather moves through a valley — slowly, inevitably, and with everyone pretending not to watch. She's ninety-one and she has never once let me forget that she knows precisely what I am. Not Marchetti-the-debt. That account closed in January, signed and witnessed. What she knows is the other thing: that I came to this family as a lie and stayed as a truth, and that her granddaughter married me anyway, three weeks after the last ferry of the season, on a dock, in the wind, with the horn still echoing off the water.

She approves of me now. I want to be clear that this is terrifying.

"Theo," she said, in the tone she uses when she wants the whole row to hear. "My granddaughter tells me you can bake."

"I can bake."

"She says your bread rises."

"It rises."

"My bread hasn't risen since 1987." She looked out over the rows, at her granddaughter's flushed face, at the bins filling with fruit that everyone — herself included, though she'd deny it under oath — had written off. "I intend to find out why yours does."

Then she walked on, and Marguerite leaned into my shoulder and whispered, "You realize you've been adopted."

"I realize I'm being *studied*."

"Same thing, in this family."

* * *

### Marguerite

By the third week of harvest, the estate kitchen had become neutral territory, which is a diplomatic achievement no treaty has ever managed in this family's history.

It happened organically, the way real things do. My mother's side ate early; Theo's father ate late; someone was always coming in or going out, and eventually Vivienne — my aunt, my imperious, terrifying, secretly soft-hearted aunt — simply started setting both tables at once and daring anyone to object. Nobody objected. Salvatore brought wine from his own cellar, a Barolo he'd been hoarding for some occasion, and when Vivienne asked what occasion, he said, "Tuesday," and she laughed, actually laughed, and I understood that the war between our families had been over for longer than any of us had admitted. Somebody just had to stop firing.

The radio played while we cooked. It always plays — an old habit of mine, then Theo's, now everyone's. Some nights it's opera, because Salvatore reaches over and turns the dial himself with the confidence of a man who has never once wondered whether he was welcome. Some nights it's that station from the mainland that plays nothing but songs about leaving, which Iris calls "morbid" and requests anyway.

On Thursday nights, Theo bakes.

I stand at the counter beside him and hand him things, which is my official role, though really I stand there because watching his hands work dough is the single most reliable cure for whatever the day has done to me. He doesn't measure anymore. He learned the flour by feel, the water by sound, and when I asked him once how he knew when the dough was ready, he said, "It stops fighting you," and I thought: yes. That's it exactly. That's the whole secret of everything.

Tonight the kitchen was full. Vivienne arguing with Charles about whether the east block should be replanted or nursed along one more year — Charles, my golden-boy cousin, who showed up in February with a check and an apology and has been showing up ever since, which taught me that people can surprise you if you give them a decade and a crisis. Salvatore and Iris at the table playing cards, cheating, both of them, flagrantly, each convinced the other doesn't notice. The radio singing about somebody's hometown. Flour in the air like weather.

And the bread rising.

I checked it the way I always do, lifting the cloth with the reverence other people reserve for infants, and it had doubled, domed, gone golden-pale and patient under the linen. Perfect. I don't say that word lightly. I spent years perfecting the art of the almost — almost viable, almost enough, almost loved — and here was a thing that was simply, entirely, what it was supposed to be.

"It rose," I said, and my voice did something embarrassing.

Theo looked over his shoulder, hands still in the dough. "Told you it would."

"You tell me that every week."

"And every week I'm right."

"That's insufferable," I said, and kissed the flour off his jaw, and across the room Iris said, without looking up from her cards, "In my kitchen, children, there is a time and a place," and Salvatore said, "Iris, it's their kitchen," and she considered this and said, "Hm. So it is," and went back to cheating.

* * *

### Theo

Later, when the kitchen had emptied and the loaves had come out golden and cracking, Marguerite and I sat on the back steps with the last of the Barolo and the whole dark valley breathing below us.

"Do you remember," she said, "when I hired you to fix the tractor?"

"I remember you tried to pay me in pie."

"The pie was excellent."

"The pie was a bribe. You wanted me to stay for dinner and pretend it was incidental."

"It *was* incidental."

"Marguerite." I said it the way I say it now, which is not a name so much as a door I walk through. "You paid me in pie, then in dinners through winter, then in a lie, then in the truth. Best wages I ever earned."

She leaned her head on my shoulder. Below us the vines stood in their long dark rows, heavy now with almost-ripe fruit, and somewhere down the slope the irrigation clicked on, and I thought about how a year ago I'd stood in my father's office holding a foreclosure letter I couldn't deliver, lying to two households, certain I was going to lose everything no matter which way I turned. I'd been right, in a way. I lost everything. The lie, the debt, the feud, the version of myself that thought loyalty meant silence. All of it gone. And what grew back in the cleared ground —

Well. You're looking at it. So is she.

"Next year," she said sleepily, "we host the reunion."

I went still. "Here."

"Here. Both families. Vivienne's already offered to cater, which means she's already planned the menu, which means resistance is theoretical." She tilted her face up to me. "They'll ask how we met. Everyone always asks."

"What will we tell them?"

She smiled, that smile that started as armor and ended as the truest thing I know. "The truth," she said. "That I invented you out of panic and desperation, and you turned out better than the invention."

"That's not how I'd tell it."

"How would you tell it?"

I thought about it. The ferry at dawn. Her humming the radio song into the wind. Dessert. The barn. The dock, the horn, her running the length of the pier toward me with her whole heart held out in both hands, debts included.

"I'd tell it simple," I said. "A woman needed a favor. A man said yes. Everything after that was the harvest."

Inside, the radio played on. On the counter, under its cloth, tomorrow's dough waited, patient, already beginning to rise.
