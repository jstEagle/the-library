## 14. The Editor's Gambit

The email arrived at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, which told Wren everything she needed to know before she opened it. Editors did not send emails before seven unless they were either terrified or triumphant, and Marguerite Hale had never been terrified in her life.

*Wren — Call me today. Big news. The good kind. — M*

Wren sat with her coffee going cold above the shuttered diner, the same ritual she'd performed for a decade: one egg scrambled soft, toast cut diagonal because her mother had always done it that way, the window seat where she could watch the street without being part of it. Invisibility as a discipline. She'd built her whole career on the principle that the best-kept rooms were the ones nobody knew you were standing in.

She read the email three times. *The good kind.* In Wren's experience, other people's good news was almost always her bad news wearing a corsage.

She called at nine.

"Wren!" Marguerite's voice came through bright and polished as a new penny. "Tell me you've seen the numbers."

"I've seen your email."

"The pre-orders, honey. The advance buzz from the excerpt we leaked to *Bon Appétit*. August Reyes memoirs are tracking like a freight train. We're moving the pub date up to March." A pause, the kind Marguerite deployed when she wanted you to lean in. "And there's more."

There was always more. More was how publishers got you.

"We've booked you both for the *Morning Edition* live interview. New York, mid-January. Prime slot, eight-fifteen segment, national audience." Marguerite said it the way people announce lottery winnings—already counting yours as theirs. "You and August, side by side, talking about the book. It'll be electric. The chemistry between you two on the page is—"

"On the page," Wren said carefully, "is where I put it."

"Which is exactly my point." Papers rustled; Marguerite was pacing, Wren could hear it, that metronomic click of heels on hardwood that meant strategy in motion. "The feud is back on, by the way. Did you see *Eater* this morning? 'Reyes and His Ghost: Ten Years of Bad Blood.' Someone leaked the culinary school story. We didn't do it"—a beat too quick—"but honestly? It's doing half our marketing for free."

"Marguerite." Wren set down her coffee. Her hands were very steady, which was how she knew something inside her wasn't. "Why am I really on that stage?"

Silence. Then the click of heels stopped.

"Because the book needs a face," Marguerite said, "and yours photographs beautifully."

"My face has never been on any cover I've written."

"And isn't that a shame?" Marguerite's voice went warm, confiding, the register she used for closing deals. "Wren. Sweetheart. You're a ghost. You've made a fortune being a ghost. But ghosts don't sell March books in a February market. So here's what we're thinking—and think of it as an opportunity, not a mandate—we're considering revealing the pen-pal program."

The kitchen went very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere below, a delivery truck idled outside the dead diner, and Wren thought absurdly of cod browning in butter, of a boy's hands steadying hers over a hot pan ten years ago, of every letter she'd written to a stranger who was never a stranger at all.

"You can't," she said.

"It's in the contract." Marguerite sounded almost gentle now, which was worse than smug. "Section eleven, subsection four. All materials generated in service of the work remain the property of the publisher. Including correspondence." A pause. "We framed it as trust-building, Wren. And it worked—you two are warmer in every session. The letters were brilliant. They were also, and I say this with love, content."

*Content.*

Wren looked out the window at the gray street, at the diner's papered-over windows, at her own faint reflection ghosted over all of it—there and not there, visible only because the light behind her happened to be on. She thought about what she'd written last week, alone at her mother's bedside, pen moving faster than judgment: *I once loved someone so much I had to leave him to survive it, and I have spent ten years pretending the leaving was his fault.* She thought about August reading those words—or whoever he believed was writing them—and answering, *I would trade the whole empire for one honest hour with someone who doesn't want anything from me.*

They'd wanted nothing from each other. That had been the whole point. That had been the first clean thing either of them had touched in a decade.

And it had been a marketing device. A clause in a contract. Two lonely people in a lab, administered intimacy by a publisher measuring the yield.

"When," Wren said, "were you planning to tell us?"

"On stage," Marguerite admitted, without a flicker of shame. "Live reactions, Wren. Unscripted. The network loves it. And frankly, so do the pre-order projections."

Wren closed her eyes. She saw it with terrible clarity: the bright studio, the moderator leaning forward with the reveal, August's face—the real one, the one under the brand—cracking on camera as he understood that his loneliest confessions had been farmed for publicity. And hers beside him, equally exposed, equally owned. Two people discovering their one honest thing had been somebody's Q4 strategy.

"They'll hate it," she said.

"They'll be riveted," Marguerite corrected. "Hate reads as chemistry, sweetheart. You should know that better than anyone—you wrote three bestselling love stories out of other people's disasters."

Wren hung up without saying goodbye. It was, she reflected, the most honest communication she'd had with the woman in six years.

* * *

She took the ferry that afternoon because she needed to move and the island was the only direction that felt like motion. Forty minutes of gray water, gulls hanging motionless over the wake, the mainland shrinking behind her into something abstract. Her mother's care home sat on its hill above the harbor like a house that had given up pretending to be anything but what it was—a place where time went to be stored.

Her mother was having a good day. Not lucid, exactly, but near the surface, eyes tracking Wren across the room with recognition that flickered like a bulb on a bad wire.

"There you are," her mother said. "You look terrible."

"Thanks, Mom."

"You never could take a compliment." Her mother patted the blanket beside her. "Sit. Tell me about the chef."

Wren stiffened. "What chef?"

"The one you write about. You get a line right here"—her mother touched the space between her brows—"when you're working on him. Like you're trying to erase something and write it at the same time."

Wren sat. Outside the window, the harbor was already thinking about winter; two boats had been hauled, a third was going this week. Six weeks until the last ferry. She'd been counting since October, marking the schedule on her calendar like a countdown to an exit she kept telling herself was practical rather than cowardly.

"He stole from us," Wren said. It came out flat, automatic, a sentence worn smooth from handling. "Dad sold the recipe. He bought it. He built everything on it."

"Mm." Her mother gazed at the harbor. "Your father signed things. Your father always signed things. That was his whole problem—he signed and he never read, or he read and never signed, I forget which." She turned her head, and for one disorienting second her eyes were sharp, fully present, aimed at her daughter like a lamp. "But August Reyes never stole anything in his life except maybe extra shifts. That boy used to stand in our kitchen so polite, asking if he could watch me season the fish. Watching him watch you, I used to think: there's a boy memorizing something he thinks he's going to lose."

Wren's throat closed. "Mom—"

"Did you know he still writes?" her mother said, drifting again, back toward the shallows. "Every Monday. Comes to the kitchen down the hall and makes the cod. Says it's for the staff, but he sends some up special. Brown butter. He gets the butter brown just past where you'd dare, and then he pulls it back." Her hand found Wren's and gripped, surprisingly strong. "He pulls it back, Wrennie. That's the whole recipe. Knowing when to pull it back."

The floor tilted. Wren held very still, the way you do when the ground starts telling you it was never solid.

"August comes here?"

"Every Monday," her mother said, pleased, as if reporting weather. "For years. Since before you started visiting regular. I thought you sent him."

The room went white at the edges. Every Monday. Ten years of Mondays. While she'd been building her case file, cataloging evidence, sharpening the theft into a weapon she could carry instead of a grief she couldn't—while she'd been rehearsing her resentment like lines in a play—August Reyes had been standing in a care-home kitchen on an island, cooking her dying mother's dish so it wouldn't die with her. Sending portions upstairs. Getting the butter brown just past where you'd dare, and pulling it back.

She thought of the bank statement in her bag. *Whitlock, R.—recipe rights, purchased.* She thought of Draft Zero, which she'd read twice and then a third time with her forehead pressed to the table: *the girl who left without goodbye, who I loved the way you love a lit window in a house you're locked out of.*

She thought of Marguerite's voice, warm as a closing door: *they'll be riveted.*

Everything Wren had left unsaid, unclaimed, unsigned—every true thing she'd hidden behind a pseudonym, a ghost's name, a stranger's byline—was inventory now. Filed, sectioned, subsectioned. Property of the publisher. Even the letters. Especially the letters. The one honest channel either of them had ever found, and it had been dug by the same company that intended to dynamite it on live television for ratings.

Betrayal, Wren thought, was not a single wound. It was sedimentary. Layer on layer: her father's signature. August's silence. Her own decade of convenient misreading. And now this—Marguerite, smiling, holding the pen-pal experiment up to the light like a gem, when it was a vein they'd been mining all along.

"Well," her mother said softly, watching her face. "Whatever you just decided, you decided it hard."

Wren laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle.

* * *

She wrote to her pen-pal that night from the ferry terminal waiting room, because the island guesthouse Wi-Fi was hopeless and because she wanted, for once, to write within sight of the water that separated everything.

Dear Stranger,

I found out today that the person I've been writing to might be a product. That the kindest thing in my life may have been a focus group. There's a version of this story where I'm furious at the people who arranged it, and I am, don't mistake me. But underneath the fury is something worse: I'm furious at myself, for needing it so badly that I never asked why it was being offered.

I have spent ten years being invisible on purpose. Invisible is safe. Invisible means no one can leave you, because technically you were never there. And then some anonymous stranger started writing me these letters that saw straight through the invisibility, and I let myself be known, and it turns out being known is the most dangerous thing I've ever done—because now there's a corporation holding the receipts.

Here's what I've decided, though. They can own the letters. They can't own what the letters made. Whatever happens on that stage in January, whatever they reveal, whatever they spin—I know what I know now. About the recipe. About the money. About Mondays. I know the man I built my whole resentment around may never have existed, and the man underneath him has been quietly feeding my mother brown butter cod while I sharpened knives.

I'm done being managed. I'm done being a ghost in someone else's machine. If they want a show, I'll give them one—but it will be mine. I'm going to walk onto that stage and say every true thing I have, in my own name, before they can auction off a single word of it.

Burn it down, my mother would say. She'd say it with relish. She'd say: Wrennie, if the house is rotten, you don't renovate. You torch it and plant something in the ash.

Consider this my match.

—W

She hesitated over the signature. Then, deliberately, she typed the initial she'd never used, the one crack in the anonymity she'd allowed herself months ago and never explained.

Send.

The reply came in forty minutes, faster than he'd ever answered:

Dear Stranger,

Something happened today on my end too, and I think we're standing on the same cliff. I was told about a television appearance. I was told the audience is in for a surprise. I've spent the evening trying to figure out how to warn you about something I can't explain without ending the thing I'm trying to protect.

So let me just say this: whatever they've planned, whatever gets revealed, please believe that the person writing to you never lied about a single thing in these letters. The rest of my life is a performance. These letters are the only place I've told the truth in ten years, and if that truth gets taken from me and sold, I don't know what I'll do. Probably something stupid and public.

You said consider this your match. Here's mine: if they burn us, I'm grabbing your hand in the fire. Whoever you are. That's a promise I can actually keep.

—A

Wren read it twice, then a third time, her breath fogging the terminal glass. *Grabbing your hand in the fire.* He didn't know. He still didn't know that his stranger and his ghostwriter were the same woman, that in three weeks they'd be seated side by side under studio lights while a moderator detonated everything.

Or maybe—she turned the thought over, examining it like the recipe card, like the bank statement—maybe some part of him already knew, the way her mother knew things, the way the butter knows. Maybe that was why he'd written *Draft Zero* and sent it to her, the ghostwriter, instead of the stranger: a confession split between his two safest addresses, hoping they'd meet somewhere in the middle.

It didn't matter. What mattered was the decision, which had been forming all day beneath her ribs like a second heartbeat, and which now stood up and announced itself:

No more drafts. No more ghosts. No more letting other people hold her sentences hostage.

January's interview would be a trap. Fine. She'd walked into traps before; she'd lived in one for a decade. But this time she'd bring her own fire, and she'd light it herself, on her terms, in her own name—before Marguerite Hale could sell the matchbook.

Wren pocketed her phone and looked out at the black harbor, at the ferry lights crossing the dark water toward the mainland, toward New York, toward the stage where everything would detonate.

Three weeks, she thought. Three weeks to learn every true thing, and then to say them all out loud.

Behind her, the terminal clock ticked toward midnight. On the schedule board, someone had already posted the winter notice: LAST FERRY OF THE SEASON — JANUARY 14TH.

The day after the interview.

Of course it was. Everything in her life, it seemed, was converging on a single departure—one boat, one tide, one narrow window in which to burn the old story down and decide whether anything of hers was worth carrying onto open water.

She picked up her phone and began, for the first time in ten years, to draft something under her own name.
