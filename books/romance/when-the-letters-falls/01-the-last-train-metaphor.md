## 1. The Last Train Metaphor

The candles were the problem.

Emrys Dravenmoor had specified two hundred and forty of them—beeswax, unscented, ivory-dipped—arranged in descending heights along the banquet tables of the Whitmore-Callahan reception so that the light would fall like late afternoon even though it was nearly eleven at night. She had spent forty minutes that afternoon adjusting them herself, moving three inches here, a half-inch there, because candlelight was the one lie she still believed in. It made everyone beautiful. It made everyone forgiving. It made a room full of strangers look, for a few hours, like a family.

And now, from her post near the service corridor with a glass of champagne she had no intention of drinking, Emrys watched Gerald Whitmore—the father of the bride, the man who had paid for all of this, who had wept during the rehearsal dinner toast about his little girl learning to ride a bike—slip out through the side door of the ballroom with his coat already on.

He didn't look back. He didn't say goodbye to his daughter, who was mid-first-dance under a chandelier Emrys had imported from a decommissioned hotel in Prague. He didn't kiss his wife's cheek or squeeze his son's shoulder. He simply left, the way people leave trains—not dramatically, not with a scene, but with the quiet finality of someone who has decided the destination is no longer worth the ride.

Emrys set down her glass.

She could have stopped him. That was the thing no one understood about her job: she could have stopped him. One hand on his sleeve, one murmured *Gerald, your daughter's watching for you*, and he would have folded himself back into the performance, smiled for the photographer, stayed until the sparklers. She had done it before. She had done it dozens of times, holding marriages together at the seams for six hours at a stretch, long enough for the pictures to come out right.

Instead she watched the door swing shut behind him and felt something in her chest settle into place with an almost audible click. Like a bone going back into joint. Like a verdict being read.

*There it is,* she thought. *There's the truth under the tulle.*

"Ms. Delacroix?" Priya appeared at her elbow, tablet clutched to her chest, headset around her neck like a stethoscope. Twenty-four years old, ferociously competent, still capable of crying at weddings—a flaw Emrys had been trying to train out of her for eight months without success. "The cake cutting is in twelve minutes. The band wants to know if they should hold the set break until after."

"After," Emrys said. "Never interrupt a first dance with dessert. It makes the whole evening feel like a commercial break."

Priya nodded, typed something, hesitated. Emrys knew that hesitation. It was the hesitation of an assistant about to deliver news that did not belong in the wedding-day category.

"What is it?"

"There's—" Priya glanced toward the ballroom doors, lowered her voice as though the music might overhear. "There's a call. From the mayor's office. They've called twice tonight. I told them you were working, but the second time the woman said it was time-sensitive, something about a civic initiative, and she asked me to give you this." She held out a card, heavy cream stock, embossed seal. "She said to tell you: *the city needs its most celebrated voice.*"

Emrys took the card without looking at it. Her eyes were still on the side door.

Through the gap where it hadn't quite closed, she could see the ballroom: the Whitmore girl laughing with her head thrown back, her new husband's hands at her waist, the two hundred and forty candles doing their work, gilding everything, forgiving everything. The bride had not yet noticed her father was gone. Or perhaps she had noticed, and was performing not-noticing, which was its own kind of grief, the kind Emrys knew best—the grief you choreograph.

Three years ago, Emrys Dravenmoor had been engaged. There had been a ring—a thin gold band with a stone the color of weak tea—and a man named Daniel who had taught sailing lessons and laughed with his whole body, and a wedding planned down to the napkin folds. She had been, by every professional metric, excellent at love. She could build it out of light and linen and string quartets. She could make two hundred strangers believe in it for an evening.

Then Daniel had stood up in a restaurant on a Tuesday night, three weeks before their own wedding, and told her he'd been seeing someone else since spring, and that he'd realized—he'd actually said this, she could still hear it, she suspected she would always be able to hear it—that he'd realized he couldn't marry someone who treated love like an event to be produced.

The scandal had been small by the standards of scandal, but she lived in an industry built on romance, and in that industry, being left at the altar of your own design is not a private wound. It is a product defect. Clients had drifted. A partner had left. She had rebuilt, colder and better, into the woman whose waiting list ran fourteen months and whose name appeared in magazines next to words like *flawless* and *unforgettable* and, once, memorably, *the last romantic in a city that has stopped believing.*

The last romantic. She had let them print it. She had even liked it, for a while, the way you can like a scar if the lighting is right.

Now she watched the door where Gerald Whitmore had vanished, and she thought: *the last train.*

That was how she'd begun to think of it, privately, in the vocabulary she never said aloud. Every wedding was a station. Everyone arrived believing they'd caught the express—love, permanence, the whole shining itinerary. But there was always a last train, and it always left, and the only question was whether you were on the platform when it went or whether you'd already slipped out the side door with your coat on, sparing yourself the goodbye. Gerald Whitmore had simply boarded early. He'd seen the schedule. He'd done the math on his daughter's marriage the way Emrys did the math on every marriage she touched—instinctively, involuntarily, counting the load-bearing walls—and he had decided not to wait for the delay announcement.

Love was a performance. That was the doctrine now, and doctrines are just wounds that have learned to speak. Love was a performance, and Emrys Dravenmoor was the best director in the city, and the difference between her and everyone else in that ballroom was that she no longer confused the play with the thing itself.

"Ms. Delacroix?"

"Hm." She looked down. She was still holding the card. She turned it over. **Office of the Mayor — Department of Civic Connection** was printed beneath the seal, and below that, in smaller type: *Letters Between Neighbors: A City-Wide Initiative.*

"They want you to judge it," Priya said. "The letter program. It launches next month—anonymous correspondence between strangers across the city, matched by some algorithm, meant to—I don't know." She made a face that suggested she found the concept either sweet or suspect; with Priya it was often both. "Reconnect people. The press release says loneliness is a public health crisis. They want famous names attached. They asked for you specifically. The woman said—" Priya checked her phone, reading from a note— "*Ms. Delacroix understands better than anyone what connection costs, and what it's worth.*"

Emrys almost laughed. Almost. It came out instead as a slow exhale through her nose, the ghost of a laugh, which was the most honest sound she'd made all evening.

*What connection costs.* Yes. She could invoice them for that.

"I'm a wedding planner, Priya, not a postal worker."

"You're also the most famous romantic in the city, according to *Vogue*."

"*Vogue* called me the last romantic. There's a difference. One implies belief. The other implies survival."

But she didn't hand the card back. That was the strange part, the part she noticed later, turning the evening over in her mind the way she turned over seating charts. Her thumb moved across the embossed seal once, twice, an unconscious gesture, the way you touch a door you're not sure you want to open.

Inside the ballroom, the first dance ended. The room applauded. The bride turned, glowing, searching the crowd for her father, and Emrys watched the glow flicker—watched the exact moment the girl registered the empty chair, the folded coat gone from its hook, the absence shaped exactly like a man. Watched her decide, visibly, in real time, to keep smiling. Watched her turn back to her husband and perform joy so convincingly that only someone trained in the craft would know it was a performance at all.

*Good girl,* Emrys thought, and hated herself for thinking it, and thought it anyway. *You'll go far.*

The band swung into something brassy. The cake was wheeled out, four tiers, sugar peonies, a knife with a ribbon tied to the handle. Everything continued. That was the secret of events: they continued. Grief, doubt, fathers slipping out side doors—none of it could stop a well-run timeline. The show held. The show always held. That was the entire religion of her profession, and she was its most devout nonbeliever.

"Tell them I'll consider it," Emrys said.

Priya blinked. "You will? You just said—"

"I said I'd consider it. Consideration is free. Commitment costs extra." She handed back the champagne she hadn't drunk. "Send the mayor's office my rate card. If they want a celebrity judge, they can pay celebrity rates. And find out who else is judging. I don't share a stage with amateurs."

Priya typed, then paused again, and this time the hesitation had a different texture—softer, more dangerous. "Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

"The answer is no."

"Why do you do it? The weddings. If you don't believe—" Priya gestured vaguely at the ballroom, the candles, the performing bride, all of it. "Why build the thing if you think it's fake?"

Emrys looked at her assistant for a long moment. Somewhere behind them, the father of the bride was probably hailing a cab, and somewhere ahead of him was a divorce lawyer or a mistress or just a quiet apartment where nobody needed him to be anyone's anything, and somewhere in between was the truth, which no one at this wedding had paid to see.

"Because the lie is beautiful," Emrys said finally, "and I am very, very good at it."

She walked back into the ballroom then, spine straight, smile calibrated, and spent the next hour doing what she did best: directing a play about love for an audience of two hundred, while the mother of the bride cried quietly at table nine and pretended it was happiness.

It wasn't until the car home, until the city slid past the window in ribbons of sodium light, until she pulled the cream-colored card from her clutch and read it again under the streetlamp strobing through the glass—*Letters Between Neighbors*—that she allowed herself the smallest, most dangerous thought of the night:

Strangers, writing honestly, with nothing to gain and no faces to protect.

No performances. No timelines. No candles to hide behind.

*Absurd,* she thought, and slipped the card into her coat pocket instead of the trash, and told herself the decision could wait until morning.

It could not, of course. Nothing about what waited inside that program could wait at all—but Emrys Dravenmoor did not yet know that the algorithm had already run, that somewhere across the city a match had already been made, and that the name paired with hers belonged to the one man alive who knew exactly what her engagement had cost, because he had helped bury the evidence.

The last train, it turned out, had not left.

It had merely changed destinations.
