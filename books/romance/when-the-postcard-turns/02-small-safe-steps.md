## 2. Small Safe Steps

Bram Amberdale's life was organized the way a good paragraph is organized: nothing wasted, nothing surprising.

He woke at six-fifteen in the flat above Halloran's Bakery on Wexford Lane, where the smell of the first batch of rolls rose through the floorboards at five and had become, over four years, less a scent than a heartbeat. He made coffee in a dented moka pot that his mother had owned and that he had never once descaled because it seemed disloyal to improve upon her neglect. He wrote from seven until noon — other people's words, other people's memories, other people's triumphs arranged into sentences that sounded effortless because he had spent three hours making them look like they'd cost nothing.

Then he went downstairs, bought two rolls from Mrs. Halloran, who always gave him a third "for the walk," and took the walk, which followed the canal and required him to speak to no one except the swans, who were terrible listeners but excellent at keeping secrets.

This was the architecture. This was the whole cathedral of it: small, load-bearing, safe.

The probate notice arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day the world remembered you existed without bothering to be kind about it. It came in a buff envelope with a window in the front, and it sat on his desk between Julian Halloway's manuscript — chapter eleven, the part where Julian discovered himself in Lisbon and was insufferable about it for forty pages — and a cold cup of coffee shaped vaguely like regret.

Bram read it twice.

*Re: Estate of Edmund Halloway (deceased). Notice of co-tenancy. The undersigned parties are hereby named joint heirs of record to the property known as Thornfield Orchard...*

His name was there. *Bramwell J. Amberdale.* Printed in a font that suggested the law had thought about him recently and not fondly. And beside it, in the same indifferent type:

*Wren M. Halloway.*

He put the letter down. He picked it up again, the way you touch a bruise to check whether it still hurts. It did.

"Well," he said to the empty room, in the tone of a man greeting an old dog that has turned up on the doorstep after ten years, thinner and meaner and somehow still expecting dinner. "Hello."

* * *

Here is what Bram knew about safety, having studied it the way other men study sailing or wine: safety was not the absence of danger. Safety was the management of distance. You could survive almost anything if it happened far enough away — if the heartbreak arrived by post instead of in person, if the apology traveled through a solicitor's office rather than across a kitchen table, if the woman you loved learned that you'd gone from a note you didn't even write yourself but dictated to your own brother at two in the morning while your hands shook too badly to hold a pen.

He had been twenty-six. She had been twenty-four. And the night he left Thornfield Orchard — left *her*, though the distinction had never once survived contact with his conscience — he had told himself it was temporary. A season. He would go to the city, find work, send for her when he had something to offer besides a rented room and a head full of sentences nobody wanted.

What he had found instead was that words paid better when they weren't yours.

It started small. A wedding speech for a man who cried at his own rehearsal dinner. A retirement tribute for a bank manager whose colleagues described him, with love, as "fine." Then a local businessman's memoir, then a footballer's, then — the door that opened onto all the others — a novelist's "autobiography" that won a prize Bram watched someone else accept on television while he ate noodles standing up in a kitchen in Camberwell.

He got good at it. That was the trap nobody warns you about: you can get genuinely, professionally excellent at being no one. There are whole careers built on it. There are men in London who have written the lives of admirals and actresses and never once been asked their opinion on anything, and they sleep fine, most of them, because they have learned what Bram learned — that anonymity is a kind of anesthesia. You feel the work but not the wound.

Except at four in the morning sometimes, when the anesthesia wore off, and he lay in whatever flat he was renting and composed letters to Wren Halloway in his head. He had composed hundreds. He could have published a collected volume: *Unsent, 2014–Present.* Some were short — *I'm sorry, I was a coward, I loved you, I still* — and some ran to twelve pages and included, in draft nine, a metaphor involving orchards that even he recognized as unforgivable.

He had never sent one. Not because he couldn't find the address. Because every version ended the same way: *and here is why,* and there was no why. There was only fear, wearing the costume of practicality. Fear was very good at tailoring.

So he had done the next best thing, which was nothing, beautifully and consistently, for ten years.

* * *

"You've gone strange," Mrs. Halloran said on Wednesday morning, handing him his third roll unasked. "Stranger."

"I haven't gone anything. I'm exactly the same."

"That's what I mean." She was seventy-one and had run the bakery since before he was born and had opinions about him the way the sea has opinions about the shore — constant, erosive, ultimately correct. "You're doing the face. The thinking-you're-hiding-it face. My Harold had that face. Forty years I lived with it. It means either money trouble or a woman, and you've never had enough money for trouble."

"It's legal," Bram said. "A legal thing."

"Legal things," she said darkly, "are worse. Legal things come back." She pushed the roll into his hand. "Eat. Whatever it is, it'll keep till you've eaten."

But it didn't keep. It sat on his desk all day while he tried to write Julian's chapter twelve, which was about Lisbon, and produced instead three paragraphs in which Lisbon appeared to be located somewhere near an orchard in Kent, and a woman with red hair kept walking through the Alfama district carrying a basket of greengages, and Julian — who had never met Wren, who had never seen her stand in a doorway with flour on her hands laughing at something Bram had said — kept looking at this stranger like he recognized her.

Bram deleted the paragraphs. All ghostwriters know the rule: the book is not about you. The book is never about you. That is the entire job. You are a hand, not a heart; you lend your rhythm and withhold your pulse.

He opened the probate notice again instead, because apparently today was a day for self-harm conducted through paperwork.

The details were these: Edmund Halloway — Wren's father, the man who had taught Bram to graft apples the summer he was nineteen and had looked at him, once, with an expression Bram had spent a decade pretending he hadn't understood, the expression of a father taking a measure — had died fourteen months ago. The estate had gone through probate with the leisurely pace of a barge going through a lock. And now, at the end of it, a solicitor's firm called Pell & Associates had determined that the deed to Thornfield Orchard named two heirs of record: Wren Halloway, who had presumably been living there all along, tending it, keeping it alive —

— and Bramwell Amberdale, who had last stood under those trees at twenty-six with a bag packed and a lie in his mouth.

Neither party could sell without the other's consent. Neither party could mortgage. Neither party could so much as fell a diseased tree without the signature of the other. The law, Bram reflected, had finally invented something crueler than marriage: marriage with paperwork and no honeymoon.

There was a clause, too — he read it three times, because the first two times his eyes slid off it like rain off wax. Something about restoration. About the property being held in trust pending its return to "productive condition," with a deadline attached, and the name Pell appearing again like a spider at the edge of a web.

He set the letter down and did what he always did when the past knocked politely: he considered the exits.

Option one: sign everything over to Wren. Clean, generous, final. Except the notice said he couldn't — not unilaterally, not cleanly, the deed bound them like two fingers caught in the same machine. Option two: ignore it. Let the deadline pass, let the trust reclaim the land, let it all dissolve into somebody else's problem. He held this option up to the light and found it rotten through; whatever else he was, he would not be the man who cost Wren Halloway her father's orchard twice.

Which left option three. Which left her.

He imagined it with the thoroughness of a professional. He imagined the train down, the lane, the gate — she'd have fixed the gate latch by now, or she'd have left it broken out of principle, both were equally Wren. He imagined her opening the door, seeing him, and the particular way her face would go still — not angry, worse than angry, *informed*, like a weather report confirming the storm. He imagined saying *hello* and hearing how small it came out. He imagined ten years arriving all at once, unsent letters tearing open in his chest like a crate dropped on a dock.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a watermark, the true reason: he was afraid that if he stood in front of her, he would say everything, immediately, in the wrong order, and it would be about him — his guilt, his decade, his rehearsed apology — when what she deserved was someone who could listen first. He did not trust himself to listen first. Ten years of writing other people's voices had left him unsure he still owned one worth hearing.

So he did the thing his whole life had trained him to do. He chose the small safe step.

He called a lawyer.

* * *

"Not family law," Bram said into the phone on Thursday, "and not criminal. Probate. Co-tenancy. There's a property in Kent I appear to half-own with—" a pause he covered badly, "—someone I haven't spoken to in some time."

"How long is 'some time'?" asked Ms. Okafor, whose voice had the dry warmth of a woman who had heard every possible answer to that question.

"A decade."

Another pause, this one professionally appreciative. "Mr. Amberdale, may I offer some free advice before we begin billing?"

"Please."

"In matters of co-owned property, the cheapest thing you will ever do is talk to the other owner directly. Everything else — correspondence, negotiation, mediation — costs money and adds friction. A phone call costs nothing."

"I'm aware," Bram said. "I'd prefer to pay."

She laughed — one short note, not unkind. "They usually do," she said, and he wondered, briefly and uncomfortably, how many other people had sat in their kitchens composing the same cowardice and called it prudence.

He drafted the instruction letter that evening with more care than he had given any manuscript in a year. *Ms. Okafor will represent me in all matters pertaining to Thornfield Orchard. Please direct any communication regarding the property to her offices.* He read it over. It was polite. It was clear. It was a wall with good manners.

At the bottom, his hand hovered. He almost added a line — *tell her I* — and then deleted it, character by character, the way he had deleted a thousand such lines, each keystroke a small safe step away from the person he might have been.

Mrs. Halloran brought up a roll he hadn't ordered at nine o'clock, which meant she'd heard him pacing. "Still legal?" she asked.

"Handled," he said. "Legally."

"Mm." She set the roll down and looked at him the way she looked at dough that hadn't proved. "You know what my Harold used to say? Man spends his whole life choosing the small safe steps, one day he looks around and finds he's walked in a circle." She patted his shoulder on her way out, leaving flour prints on his shirt like a blessing, or an accusation.

Bram sat down at the desk. Julian's manuscript waited, open to chapter twelve. The probate notice waited beside it, patient as winter. Somewhere south of the city, behind a gate he could picture better than his own front door, a woman was learning from a stranger's letterhead that he existed.

He picked up his pen to work, and found — for the first time in ten years of working — that his hands were shaking.

* * *

That night he dreamed of the orchard in blossom, which it never had been in any season he'd actually witnessed there; in his dreams it bloomed extravagantly, absurdly, trees white to the horizon, and Wren walked ahead of him down the rows and would not turn around, and every tree he passed he could hear — faintly, under the wind — the sound of pages turning.
