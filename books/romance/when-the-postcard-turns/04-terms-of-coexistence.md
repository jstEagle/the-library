## 4. Terms of Coexistence

**Bram**

The letter from Halloway & Pell arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the days Bram's life delivered its least welcome news — royalty statements, rejection letters, once a cease-and-desist regarding a romance novel he'd written under the name "Fern Delacroix" that had somehow offended an actual fern enthusiast society.

He read it three times at the kitchen table of the bakery flat, the radiator ticking like a metronome counting down something.

*The co-heirs are requested to attend a meeting of record at the property known as Thornfield Orchard, Saturday next, ten o'clock, to discuss terms of partition or joint administration.*

Co-heirs. The word sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. Ten years, and the law had decided he and Wren Halloway should be stapled together by a piece of paper neither of them could tear alone.

He drafted four replies. In the first, he declined and offered to sign over his interest for a nominal sum — one pound, say, or a decent bottle of cider, since apparently that was what the orchard produced now: memories and regret. In the second, he was more dignified. In the third, he apologized. He burned the third in the sink and stood there watching the ash swirl down the drain, thinking that was probably symbolic of something and hating himself for noticing.

In the end he sent nothing at all, because Wren's reply came first — forwarded to him by the solicitors with the ruthless efficiency of people who billed by the hour:

*Tell Mr. Amberdale I will meet him at the gate, not before it. And tell him if he uses the word "unfortunately" even once, I will walk into the orchard and let the lawyers sort out the rest.*

Bram read it twice. Then, despite everything — despite the dread sitting in his chest like weather — he laughed. One short, startled bark of a laugh, alone in his kitchen.

She was still in there somewhere. The girl who'd once dared him to eat a crab apple whole and then laughed so hard she cried when he did it. The fury had her voice now, but underneath it, unmistakably, was Wren.

He wrote back a single line: *Saturday. At the gate. No adverbs.*

Then he spent the rest of the week doing what he always did when terrified: he made lists. What to wear (nothing that said *I've done well for myself*, nothing that said *I've been miserable either*). What to bring (the solicitor's draft agreement, a pen, and — after long debate — nothing else; flowers would be absurd, apples would be worse). What to say (this list remained blank until Friday night, when he finally wrote at the top of the page, in his careful ghostwriter's hand: *Hello, Wren.*)

Even that took him forty minutes.

* * *

**Wren**

Wren got to the gate early, because she refused to be the person waiting when he arrived. If she was there first, leaning against the rusted iron like she owned it — which, as far as she'd believed until three weeks ago, she did — then he would be the one walking up the lane toward her, and she would get to watch him come. There was power in that. Small power. But Wren had learned to live on small things: the first blossom in a bad spring, a tree that survived when it shouldn't have, the satisfaction of pruning something back to honesty.

He came up the lane at 9:52, eight minutes early, wearing a coat too nice for an orchard and carrying a leather folder like a shield.

Good, she thought. Be uncomfortable.

"Bram." She said it flatly, a door closing rather than opening.

"Wren." He stopped a few feet away. Up close he looked older, of course — they both did — but he also looked *careful*, the way furniture looks when it's been arranged by someone afraid of knocking anything over. "You look—"

"Don't," she said. "Whatever the next word is, put it back."

He put it back. She almost admired that.

They stood there in the cold April light while somewhere behind her a woodpecker went at the old packinghouse like it was being paid by the hour. Wren had spent the week preparing for this meeting the way she prepared for frost: thoroughly, and expecting damage.

"I'm not here to fight you," Bram said finally.

"You're here because the deed won't let you do anything else."

"That's also true."

"At least we're starting honest." She pushed off the gate and led him up the main row without asking if he wanted to follow. He followed. Of course he followed; he'd always been good at following her through orchards. That was rather the whole problem, wasn't it. "You should see what you're signing for. Since you clearly haven't been here."

"I haven't," he admitted. "I looked it up. Aerial photographs. There's a satellite image from 2019 where you can see—"

"You looked at my orchard from *space*?"

"It seemed less intrusive than driving here."

Wren stopped between rows six and seven, where the Cox's Orange Pippins stood in their ragged line, half of them dead, the rest alive out of what she'd long suspected was sheer spite. "This is what's left. Forty-one living trees out of two hundred. I've kept them going on weekends and stubbornness for nine years. I thought it was mine. My father told me it was mine."

"He told me—" Bram started, and stopped.

"He told you what?" Her voice came out sharper than she intended, and she watched him flinch, and some small vicious part of her was glad, and a larger tired part of her wasn't.

"He told me it would be yours someday. That was part of why I—" He gestured vaguely at the past, at himself, at the entire catastrophe. "It didn't seem like my place to stay and complicate it."

"So you complicated it by leaving instead. Efficient."

"I'd like to propose terms," he said, with the desperate formality of a man grabbing for a handrail.

"Yes," Wren said. "Let's do terms. Terms I can manage. It's the other thing I can't."

* * *

**Bram**

They negotiated at the packinghouse, on two upturned apple crates, with the draft agreement spread across a third. Bram had brought the solicitor's version — eleven pages of clause and sub-clause — and Wren read it the way she presumably pruned: fast, decisively, cutting anything that didn't serve the tree.

"This is nonsense," she said, ten minutes in. "'Party A shall retain primary custodial discretion.' Which one am I?"

"Party B."

"Of course I am." She crossed it out with a pencil stub she produced from her coat pocket, sharpened, apparently, by spite. "Here's how it actually works. One growing season. We share the labor — all of it, equally, and I decide what counts as labor, because you don't know yet. At harvest, if the trees bear, we each take our share of the crop and then one of us buys the other out at valuation. Agreed price, no lawyers dragging it past Christmas."

"And if it doesn't bear?"

"Then the land goes to whoever wants it more." She looked at him over the crates. "Which will be me."

Bram found, to his surprise, that he didn't want to argue. Arguing would mean prolonging this, and some traitorous part of him wanted to prolong it — wanted a season of Saturdays in these rows, a season of her voice correcting his grip on the loppers, a season with a built-in ending he could survive. "Agreed," he said. "One condition of mine."

She narrowed her eyes. "You don't get conditions. You lost condition privileges a decade ago."

"It's small. I work the rows, I follow your rules, I ask nothing. But when the season ends — whatever happens — you let me say one thing to you. All of it. Uninterrupted. And then you never have to hear from me again if that's what you choose."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. A branch scraped the packinghouse roof in the wind, like the orchard itself clearing its throat.

"One sentence?" she said.

"As many as it takes. But only once."

"And if I don't want to hear it?"

"Then you'll have earned the right to refuse. At the end. After a season of my work." He held her gaze, which cost him considerably more than the signature would. "I'm not asking for forgiveness in advance. I'm asking for an appointment."

Something moved behind her eyes — he couldn't name it, and he'd made a career of naming things. Grief wearing armor, maybe. Or armor that had grown into the grief so long ago they'd fused, like graft stock to rootstock.

"Fine," she said. "One appointment. Non-transferable. Miss it and it's gone forever." She pulled the agreement back, struck out three more clauses, wrote new ones in the margins in handwriting that slanted hard to the right, as if even her pen was leaning away from him. "Sign there. And initial every page, because I know how you writers are."

"You know how I—"

"Everyone knows how writers are. You fall in love with your own sentences." She turned the pages toward him. "Go on."

* * *

**Wren**

She watched his hands.

She hadn't meant to. She'd promised herself she wouldn't look at anything but the paper, wouldn't catalog the changes ten years had written on him — the faint scar on his left knuckle she didn't remember, the way his thumb pressed the pen like he was afraid the words might escape. But hands were hands, and his had once been the most familiar landscape in her world, and betrayal apparently didn't overwrite muscle memory any more than it overwrote the way a tree grows toward light.

He signed carefully. Not flourishingly — she'd expected flourish, some writer's vanity — but slowly, letter by letter, like a boy signing his first library card. *Bram Amberdale.* His initials on each page, small and neat.

When he finished, he set the pen down and flexed his fingers, and Wren realized she'd been holding her breath.

"There," he said. "It's done."

"It's done," she agreed, and took the pages before he could touch them again, folding them into her coat. "You start Saturday. Six sharp. Wear boots you don't love."

"What happens at six sharp?"

"Pruning." She allowed herself one glance at his face — the polite bafflement there, the dawning sense of having signed something larger than he understood — and found, to her considerable irritation, that it pleased her. "You'll learn. Most of what we do this season is deciding what dies so the rest can live. You may find it familiar."

"That's cruel."

"That's horticulture." She stood, and he stood, and for a moment they were face to face across the apple crates with the whole ruined orchard breathing around them, and the wind carried the smell of wet bark and old blossoms and something green just beginning under it all.

"Thank you," Bram said quietly. "For the appointment."

"Don't thank me. I gave it to you the way you give a dog a long lead — so I can watch exactly how far you run." She walked to the packinghouse door, then stopped, because there was one more thing and she hated that there was one more thing. "Row twelve. The Russets. My father planted those the year I was born. They're yours to prune this season — all of them. If you kill one, the deal's off."

"Why row twelve?"

"Because it's the hardest row," she said, "and because I want to see what you do with something that matters."

She heard him behind her, not moving yet, and she kept walking, down the row, past the trees she'd saved alone for nine years, telling herself the tightness in her chest was just the cold.

It wasn't the cold. It was the sound of his footsteps starting after her — not following, exactly. Just walking the same rows again, eight minutes behind, the way he always had.

And beneath her fury, in the place she kept locked and inspected daily, something treacherous whispered: *a season is a long time.*

Wren walked faster.

* * *

**Bram**

That night Bram sat in the bakery flat with the copy of the agreement and read his own initials on every page like tea leaves, trying to divine what he'd just done.

Six months. One season. Row twelve.

He got out the notebook he used for Julian's memoir — then, catching himself, put it away and took out a cheap one instead, the kind schoolchildren use, with a marbled cover. On the first page he wrote:

*Terms of coexistence, personal addendum:*

*1. Learn the names of all forty-one trees.*
*2. Do not write about her. Not for him, not for anyone.*
*3. Say the thing by harvest.*

He looked at item three for a long time. Then he added:

*4. Buy better boots.*

Outside, the city went on with its noise, and somewhere east of it an orchard stood in the dark with its dead trees and its living ones, holding its breath for spring. Bram clicked off the lamp and lay awake listening to the radiator tick, and thought about row twelve, and wondered — with the first honest fear he'd felt in years — whether he was capable of keeping anything alive.

His phone buzzed. Julian.

*How's the country air, ghost? Chapter Twelve needs that girl. Make her vivid. Villains always are.*

Bram stared at the screen in the dark, the words glowing like frost warning, and did not reply.
