## 6. The Ghostwriter's Chapter Twelve

The trouble with writing other people's lives, Bram had learned, was that they kept insisting on having them.

Julian Halloway's life arrived in installments: Tuesday afternoons at the bakery flat, a cappuccino ordered with the confidence of a man who had never once wondered whether he was wanted anywhere, and three hours of monologue that Bram transcribed into something resembling literature. Julian talked the way weather happened — everywhere at once, impossible to argue with, occasionally destructive.

"So chapter twelve," Julian said, settling into the good chair — the one Bram had quietly stopped sitting in months ago, because Julian always took it and it was easier to surrender furniture than friendship. "That's where she goes."

Bram's pen hovered. "Where who goes?"

"You know." Julian waved a hand, as if the girl were a region on a map. "The one from before. The orchard summer. She has to be in there or the whole middle of the book sags. My editor said so. 'We need the heartbreak,' she said. 'Readers forgive a man anything if you break his heart early enough.'"

"That's a grim theory of forgiveness."

"It's a *commercial* theory of forgiveness." Julian grinned. He had a good grin; Bram had written it down twice already. "And it happens to be true. So. Chapter twelve. The girl who left."

The words went through Bram like a splinter finding old wood.

Because that wasn't how it had happened. That was not even adjacent to how it had happened. There had been no girl who left. There had been a boy who ran — a twenty-three-year-old boy with a duffel bag and a talent for vanishing, who had stood in Wren Halloway's kitchen at eleven o'clock at night while her father slept upstairs, and had said nothing, and had left nothing, and had spent ten years calling that silence *prudence*.

But Julian hadn't been there. Julian had been in London, being brilliant at someone else's expense. All Julian knew was the shape of the story he needed: a woman who abandoned him, because a man like Julian could not be abandoned by anyone except a woman, and only then romantically.

"She didn't leave," Bram said. He kept his voice level, the way you keep a ladder level when someone's standing on it.

Julian blinked at him, genuinely puzzled, which was somehow worse than if he'd been cruel. "Sorry?"

"I mean — narratively. If she just leaves, she's flat. Readers hate a flat villain. Give her a reason. Give her a wound. Make the leaving cost *her* something too, and suddenly the reader can't quit her." Bram heard himself doing it — building the lie a better foundation so it would stand up straighter. It was the oldest trick he had. You couldn't stop people from lying to themselves, but you could make their lies load-bearing.

Julian considered this with the seriousness of a man considering wine. "Yes. Yes, exactly. She left because she was frightened of me, basically. Of what we were becoming. That's sympathetic, isn't it? Tragic, even."

"Tragic," Bram agreed, and wrote *tragic* in the margin, and hated himself with a thoroughness that would have impressed a tax auditor.

* * *

He wrote it anyway. Of course he wrote it anyway.

That was the arrangement, and the arrangement was the whole architecture of his life: five years of ghostwriting, four memoirs under other names, a byline collection that existed nowhere but in his own head. He was very good at disappearing into other people's first person. He could find a stranger's voice in an afternoon, could make their childhoods luminous and their divorces redemptive, could hand them back their own lives improved, polished, better than true. People cried reading what he'd written for them. They called him a gift.

They never asked what it cost to be a gift. Nobody unwraps a present to ask how it feels about the ribbon.

So on Thursday night, alone in the bakery flat with the smell of yesterday's bread still ghosting up through the floorboards — Mrs. Fenwick below started her ovens at four a.m., and Bram had long ago stopped minding, had come to think of it as the building breathing — he opened the manuscript and typed the heading:

**Chapter Twelve: The Girl Who Left**

And the lie came out of him smooth as water off glass.

*She was the kind of person who decided things in advance and told you afterward,* he wrote, giving Julian's memory a personality it had never earned. *I think she'd already left before she ever stayed. Some people love you the way a tenant loves a house — carefully, temporarily, with one eye on the exit.*

It was good. That was the obscene part. It was *good*. The sentences had rhythm; the metaphors landed; there was a line about her hands — *she had gardener's hands, hands that were always holding something back* — that made Bram sit back and think, with genuine professional admiration, *whoever wrote this should be ashamed.*

He had met Wren's hands. He had held one of them, once, across a table in the packinghouse while her father argued cheerfully about apple varieties in the next room. Her hands had been steady and flour-dusted and absolutely not holding anything back except, perhaps, the urge to throw a paring knife at her brother's head, which Bram had privately considered reasonable.

But the lie flowed anyway. That was the thing nobody warned you about ghostwriting: it got *easier*. The first time you bent a truth, your hands shook. By the fourth book you could bend them into shapes origami artists would envy, and feel nothing but the pleasant click of a well-made sentence.

He wrote until two in the morning. He gave Julian a moonlit walk that had never happened. He gave Julian a farewell scene at a train station — Julian loved train stations, had requested one in every book so far — where the girl who left pressed something into his hand and said, "Don't wait for me," which was dialogue Bram invented out of whole cloth and which his fingers typed without hesitation, the way a tongue finds a cavity.

Then he saved the file, closed the laptop, and sat in the dark listening to Mrs. Fenwick's ovens breathe, feeling like a man who had sold something he'd promised to protect.

There was a stack of unsent letters in the bottom drawer of his desk. He'd moved them twice now, from flat to flat, the way you move a plant you're slowly killing. Ten years of drafts: *Dear Wren, I'm sorry* in forty different fonts of cowardice. The formal one. The funny one. The one that blamed his mother's illness. The one that blamed no one, which was the honest one, and therefore the one he'd never finished.

He did not open the drawer tonight. Tonight the drawer could keep its secrets; he had enough of his own going stale at room temperature.

* * *

Friday morning he took the manuscript pages to the orchard.

This had become a habit he refused to examine. Under the co-tenancy agreement — signed in April, witnessed by a solicitor who smelled of peppermints and doom — Bram owed the land twelve hours of labor a week, and he paid it with the punctuality of a man avoiding a debt of a different kind. But lately he'd taken to bringing work with him, spreading pages on an overturned crate between rows of trees that were older than both of them put together.

Wren worked the row ahead of him. She pruned the way other people prayed — silently, continuously, with visible results.

"You're scowling at that paper like it insulted your mother," she said, without turning around.

"It's work."

"Everything's work with you. You prune sentences the way I prune these." She snapped a dead branch and held it up as evidence. "Cut anything that doesn't bear fruit. Problem is, some things need a season before you know."

"Some things never bear fruit. You cut those."

She turned then, secateurs in hand, and looked at him with an expression he'd spent a decade learning to survive from a distance and was now relearning up close. "Spoken like a man who's never watched something come back from dead wood." She nodded at the tree beside her — gnarled, half-hollow, but pushing out blossom buds along a limb he'd have sworn was finished. "That one was written off in my father's last year. Everyone said take it out. Dad said give it one more season." A pause, precise as a blade. "People are like that too. Worth more than the experts say."

Bram looked down at his pages — Julian's pages, the girl who left, the train station, the invented dialogue — and felt every word of them weigh approximately one ton.

"Wren," he said. "If someone wrote a version of you that wasn't true — a version that made you the villain of somebody's sad story — what would you want done about it?"

She considered this longer than he expected. A blackbird worked the far row, industrious and indifferent.

"I'd want to read it," she said finally. "Every word. And then I'd want to write the true version and nail it to the town noticeboard." She smiled, quick and crooked. "Why? Has somebody slandered me? Because honestly, the list of candidates is short and you're not on it."

"No reason," Bram said. "Hypothetically."

"Hypothetically," Wren repeated, in the tone of a woman filing something away in a drawer of her own, and went back to her pruning.

He spread the pages on the crate and did not look at them for a full hour. When he finally did, the sun had moved, and the line about her hands — *hands that were always holding something back* — caught the light, and he thought about the letter in his desk drawer, the one he'd never finished, the one whose existence he had mentioned to no one, and he thought: *the true version exists. It's been sitting in a drawer for ten years, waiting for someone brave enough to finish it.*

He crossed out the train station scene. Then, because the contract required a chapter and cowardice required a living, he uncrossed it.

* * *

Tuesday, Julian returned, radiant with success.

"Chapter twelve," he announced, dropping into the good chair. "My editor *wept*. Actual tears. She says it's the emotional spine of the book. She wants more of the girl — flashbacks, maybe. A thread running all the way through." He beamed at Bram. "You've done it again, Amberdale. Honestly, I don't know what I'd do without you."

*Neither do I,* Bram thought. *That's rather the problem.*

"There's just one thing," Julian went on, pulling out his phone with the air of a man delivering wonderful news. "For authenticity. My editor wants a detail — something real, something specific, that proves I knew her. A place, an object, a ritual. Readers can smell invention." He scrolled. "So tell me about the orchard. You've been out there — you'd know. What did she love? Give me one true thing and I'll build the rest around it."

The request sat between them like a live wire.

One true thing. Bram had one. Several, actually — the way she talked to trees like disgruntled employees; the way her father's ledger lived on the kitchen table open to whatever page it pleased; the way she'd once spent an entire August teaching him to graft, her hands over his on the knife, saying *you're not cutting it, you're introducing it to itself.* Any one of them would make Julian's fiction bulletproof. Any one of them would take something of Wren's — something real, something hers — and weld it onto a lie she'd never consented to.

"Apples," Bram said. "She loved the apples. The old varieties. Ribston Pippin, mostly."

It was true. It was also a betrayal dressed in work clothes, and he knew it, and he handed it over anyway, and Julian wrote it down with a flourish, delighted, oblivious, already composing the sentence in his head — Bram could see the sentence forming behind his eyes like weather coming in.

"Perfect," Julian said. "God, this book is going to *matter*."

After he left, Bram washed the coffee cups — Julian never washed anything, another detail destined for no memoir — and stood at the window watching the street below, where evening was doing its usual competent job.

Five years of careful living. Five years of other people's words, other people's names on the covers, other people's sins laundered into redemption arcs. He had told himself it was safety. He had told himself it was craft. Standing there, he admitted what it actually was: it was the same night in Wren's kitchen, extended indefinitely. Silence, professionalized. Leaving, industrialized.

His phone buzzed. Julian, already: *Editor also wants to know — does the girl have family still around? Local color. Maybe I visit the brother??*

Bram stared at the message. *Maybe I visit the brother.* Julian, coming to the orchard. Julian, charming Wren with that grin Bram had written down twice. Julian, asking questions with a recorder in his pocket.

He typed: *Bad idea. She's private. Territorial about the land.*

Julian replied instantly, with a thumbs-up emoji that managed to look smug.

Bram set the phone face-down on the counter. Through the floorboards, faintly, Mrs. Fenwick's ovens breathed. Somewhere across town, in a house full of her father's papers, Wren was presumably doing something honest — pruning, preserving, keeping accounts that balanced.

He went back to the desk. He did not open the drawer with the letters in it. But he stood over it for a long moment, hand on the handle, like a man standing at a gate he used to own.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow he'd fix chapter twelve. Tomorrow he'd tell Julian the girl was off-limits. Tomorrow he'd start the true version, the one nailed to the noticeboard of his own conscience.

He had been telling himself *tomorrow* for ten years, and tomorrow had never once shown up. But he went to bed believing it anyway, because belief was the last thing he still wrote under his own name.

Three streets away and one week ahead, in a cardboard box marked *DAD — KEEP*, a ledger waited with its spine cracked and its secret intact, patient as any tree worth saving.
