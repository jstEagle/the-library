## 2. Small Safe Steps

The alarm went off at 6:15, and Cyrus Dunmore was awake before it finished its first ring, the way he was every morning, because a man who had lost everything learned to measure his days in small safe steps and never, ever deviate.

He lay still for ten seconds—his allowed allotment of self-pity—and then got up. Coffee, one cup, black. Toast, no butter. The shower ran exactly four minutes; he'd once timed it with the same stopwatch he used to time wedding processions, which told Marisol everything she needed to know about him and which he refused to discuss.

His apartment was three rooms above a dry cleaner on Delancey Street. It smelled faintly of steam and solvent, and after five years he'd stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a limp. The walls were bare except for one framed thing: not a photograph, not a diploma, but a single pressed sprig of white lilac from a garden he had never been able to visit again. He didn't look at it. He didn't need to. He knew it was there the way he knew the scar on his palm was there.

At 7:40 he walked eleven minutes to the office of Pemberton & Vane Events—or rather, to the office of *Vane & Co.*, since Marisol had won the coin toss over whose name went first and Cyrus had let her, because names were currency and he was trying to live below his means in every sense.

Marisol was already there, of course. She was always already there. She sat at her desk with her reading glasses pushed up into her curls like a crown she kept forgetting she wore, eating a guava pastry and marking up a floor plan in red pen with the serene violence of a woman who had never once doubted her own taste.

"You look terrible," she said, without looking up.

"Good morning to you too."

"You slept badly." She said it as fact, not question. Five years of partnership meant she could read him the way he read seating charts: instantly, ruthlessly, with an eye for where the trouble would sit. "Was it the date?"

Cyrus hung his coat on the hook behind the door. The hook was brass. He'd installed it himself, level, at exactly sixty-six inches, because when your whole life has burned down around you, you learn that the small things are the only things that hold.

"It's just a day," he said.

"It's *the* day."

"It's a Tuesday, Marisol."

She lowered the red pen and looked at him over the top of her glasses, and her face did the thing it always did—the softening, the brief grief that crossed it before she packed it away, because she had loved Theo too, in her way, had catered his and Cyrus's engagement party back when the world still said *Dunmore* with a certain reverence, back when *Dunmore* meant chandeliers and certainty instead of fraud investigations and a fiancé's name spoken carefully, like a word in a language everyone else had stopped using.

"Theo would have hated that you're calling it a Tuesday," she said quietly.

"Theo would have hated a lot of things about my life now." Cyrus sat down at his desk and opened his laptop with the crisp efficiency of a man closing a door. "Fortunately, Theo doesn't get a vote."

The silence between them lasted four seconds. He counted. That was another thing about grief nobody warned you: it made you a bookkeeper. You counted everything—the seconds, the steps, the years—because if you could count it, you could contain it, and if you could contain it, maybe it couldn't reach out in the middle of the night and take anything else.

* * *

The anniversary dinner was booked for 7 p.m., table for one, at Bellamy's, a restaurant so modest it didn't have a website, just a chalkboard menu and a owner named Rosa who had stopped asking why the same handsome sad man came in alone every October 14th and simply started keeping his corner table open.

He planned it the way he planned everything: obsessively, precisely, and with a level of care that would have embarrassed him if anyone knew. He called Rosa at nine to confirm. He chose the wine—a Barolo, because Theo had liked Barolo and Cyrus had never acquired the taste, which meant he could drink it as a form of penance rather than pleasure. He ordered the tasting menu for one, no substitutions, and asked Rosa to bring whatever she thought was best, because deciding what to eat on October 14th was a decision he had never once managed to make himself in five years.

"You could invite someone," Rosa said on the phone, in the tone of a woman who had earned the right to say such things by virtue of having fed him through two of the worst years of his life. "You could invite that partner of yours. The loud one."

"Marisol is loud the way a fire alarm is loud. You don't invite a fire alarm to dinner."

"You could invite someone who isn't a fire alarm."

"I'll be fine, Rosa."

"You're always fine," Rosa said, and hung up, and Cyrus sat with the receiver in his hand for a moment, hearing the echo of it. *You're always fine.* It was the most damning thing anyone had ever said to him, and she'd said it with love, which somehow made it worse.

* * *

His therapist appointment was at 1 p.m. Dr. Okafor—no relation to anyone, though the name would come to mean something different to him soon enough, though he couldn't know that yet—had a small office above a yoga studio on Hester Street. There were plants in the window. There was always a box of tissues within arm's reach of the chair Cyrus had occupied, on and off, for three years.

Dr. Okafor asked how he was sleeping. Cyrus said fine. Dr. Okafor asked whether he'd written to his pen pal this month. Cyrus said yes. Both statements were true in the technical sense and false in every other.

The letter program had started as a condition of... something. Of continuing, maybe. His lawyer—back when he'd still needed lawyers for things other than contract review—had suggested it during the wreckage year, some grief-recovery initiative that paired strangers who wrote anonymous letters through an intermediary service. Write to someone who doesn't know who you are, the brochure said. Say the things you can't say to anyone else.

It was supposed to be temporary. Six months, maybe a year. But the letters had become a strange kind of lifeline, a place where he wasn't Cyrus Dunmore, disgraced planner, but just *C*—a man who could admit that he still set two cups out sometimes in the morning before he remembered, or that he'd kept the engagement ring in a sock drawer for four years before moving it to a safety deposit box, or that he didn't know how to attend a wedding anymore without standing in the back calculating sightlines and thinking about how much better he would have done.

His pen pal signed only *J.A.*

For five years, J.A. had written back. Patient letters. Funny ones, sometimes. Letters that asked questions Cyrus had never been able to answer anywhere else—*what do you miss that surprises you?* (the weight of a hand in his), *when did you last do something badly on purpose?* (never, and isn't that its own confession), *do you think people can start over, or do they just get very good at hiding?*

That last one had arrived in June. Cyrus hadn't answered it yet. Some questions you answer by writing; others you answer by burning.

"So," Dr. Okafor said, folding her hands. "October. How are we doing?"

"We're fine."

"You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Talking about yourself in the plural. As if you're a firm." She smiled—not unkindly. "How's the letter this month going?"

"I haven't started it."

"Cyrus."

"It's due Friday. I'll write it Friday."

"You've said 'I'll write it Friday' for three weeks."

"And I will. On Friday." He shifted in the chair. The plants in the window threw green light across the carpet. Somewhere below them, someone was failing at downward dog. "Can we talk about something else?"

"We can talk about anything you want. That's rather the point of the hour."

"Then let's talk about work. We have a pitch coming up. Big one."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. In three years, Cyrus had never once brought up work voluntarily. Work was the walled garden; therapy was for the wilderness outside it. "Tell me."

"Hollowbrook Estate," he said. "Upstate. Historic property, new ownership, they're soliciting proposals for their relaunch event. A wedding. Marisol thinks it could change our trajectory."

"And what do you think?"

"I think Marisol is right, which is annoying, because she knows it and will be insufferable."

"What I'm asking," Dr. Okafor said gently, "is whether you want it."

And there it was—the question under all the questions, the one he'd spent five years building walls against. Did he *want* things? Wanting was dangerous. Wanting was how you ended up engaged to a man named Theo Ashford with a venue booked and a guest list of two hundred and a life so perfectly planned it looked, from the inside, like proof that the universe rewarded careful men. And then wanting was how you ended up watching that life dismantled in a federal courtroom, piece by piece, exhibit by exhibit, while the man you loved took the stand and—no. Not today. Today was a Tuesday.

"I think," Cyrus said, "that Hollowbrook is a job. Jobs are safe. You do the work, you send the invoice, you go home."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Cyrus agreed. "It isn't." He stood, gathered his coat, and paused at the door. "Same time next week?"

"Same time next week," Dr. Okafor said. "Write the letter, Cyrus."

He didn't answer. Which was, in its way, an answer.

* * *

Marisol ambushed him at 4 p.m. with two folders and the expression she wore when she'd already decided something and was merely permitting him the fiction of consultation.

"Hollowbrook," she said, dropping both folders on his desk. "I pulled everything. Property records, press coverage, the works. The estate sold eight months ago to a private buyer—closed transaction, no public listing of the principal. New management company registered in Delaware, because of course it is. They're staging a relaunch next season and they want a flagship wedding to announce themselves. Budget's open-ended. *Open-ended*, Cy. Do you know how long it's been since someone said 'open-ended' to us?"

"Since before," Cyrus said, which meant: since the scandal, since the clients fled, since the industry that had once competed for him had decided he was radioactive.

"Since before," Marisol confirmed. "We're a good firm now. Solid. We do forty events a year and we do them beautifully and we pay our taxes and nobody writes think-pieces about us. But we're a *small* firm, and we're one bad season away from being a cautionary tale again. This contract changes that. This contract makes us a destination."

Cyrus opened the first folder. Photographs of the estate fanned across his desk: a Georgian manor in weathered limestone, terraced gardens gone slightly wild, a ballroom with windows tall enough to make a person feel briefly forgiven. Something in his chest tightened—an old muscle flexing, the one that used to ache when he walked past venues he'd never work in again.

"Why now?" he asked. "Why would new money buy a falling-down estate and immediately go shopping for planners?"

"Because rich people are bored, and beautiful buildings are their toys." Marisol perched on the edge of his desk. "But here's the part I don't love. The pitch is in person. Upstate. Thursday. And the client rep—the person holding the keys, literally—is flying in from the city to meet us."

"So? We meet reps all the time."

"The rep's name is Julian Ashe."

The room went very quiet. The radiator ticked. Downstairs, the dry cleaner's press hissed against fabric, over and over, like a slow heartbeat.

Cyrus set down the photograph with great care, the way you set down something fragile when what you actually want to do is throw it through a window.

"Ashe," he repeated.

"Ashe. Julian Ashe. Ashe Events, before—" She stopped herself. Before. Everything had a *before* now; his whole biography was divided by it. "Cy, I checked twice because I thought I was seeing things. Same name, same age, same firm. It's him. The rival planner. The one whose testimony—" She didn't finish. She never finished that sentence. Nobody ever finished that sentence; it existed only in fragments, passed around the industry like contraband.

Five years ago, when the investigation broke, when the Dunmore empire came apart in headlines, there had been a deposition. And in that deposition, a competitor—a peer, a man whose champagne Cyrus had drunk at industry galas, whose weddings Cyrus had privately studied and admired and resented in equal measure—had testified. Had answered questions. Had helped, in whatever small or large degree, to build the case that ended everything.

Cyrus had never learned exactly what Julian Ashe said in that room. He knew only the shape of it: that Ashe had been questioned about documents, about payments, about a pattern of irregularities at Dunmore & Associates, and that Ashe's answers had not been friendly. Whether he'd lied, or exaggerated, or simply declined to lie *for* them—that distinction had dissolved years ago. What remained was simpler and heavier: when Cyrus Dunmore was drowning, Julian Ashe had stood on the shore and answered questions.

And now Julian Ashe was holding the keys to the biggest opportunity of Cyrus's second life.

"No," Cyrus said.

"Cy—"

"*No.* Find out who else is pitching. We withdraw. We send a polite decline and we take the Kessler anniversary party and we stay exactly the size we are."

"The Kessler party is forty people in a restaurant back room," Marisol said. "Hollowbrook is a career. It's *the* career. You built empires, Cy—I watched you do it from the second row. This is the door opening again, and you're telling me we close it because the man on the other side of it once hurt you?"

"He didn't *once* hurt me. He testified. There are transcripts. There are—" Cyrus stopped. Pressed his thumb hard against the scar on his palm, the old anchor trick, pain to interrupt the spiral. "You weren't there, Mari. You didn't watch everything I built get taken apart while people I thought were friends discovered urgent business elsewhere. You didn't lose—" His voice caught. He steadied it. "Theo left. Two weeks after the testimony became public. Left a note and took nothing, because there was nothing left worth taking. So no. I'm not walking into a room and shaking that man's hand and pitching him a wedding like none of it happened."

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its boardroom edge.

"I know what you lost. I was at the engagement party, remember? I made the cake. Three tiers, lemon and elderflower, because Theo mentioned once—he mentioned *once*, Cy—that he liked lemon cake, and you built an entire dessert course around it." She picked up the Hollowbrook folder and held it out. "So I'm not going to pretend this is just business. But I am going to tell you that you taught me everything I know about this profession, and the first thing you ever taught me was: the work is bigger than the wound. Remember? You said a planner's job is to walk into rooms full of broken families and old grudges and make something beautiful anyway. Every wedding is two people with exes in the guest list. You said that. You said we don't get to choose the guests. We only choose what we build."

"That was before."

"Everything was before." She set the folder down on his desk, square with the edge, the way he liked it, because she knew that too—knew all his small mechanisms, all his safe steps. "Pitch is Thursday, ten a.m. I already sent the confirmation. Both of us. If you don't show, I'll do it alone, and I'll win it alone, and then I'll spend the next year managing the biggest event of our lives without my partner, and I will *resent* you, Cyrus, politely and forever, the way only a business partner can."

She gathered her coat and bag and paused at the door, the same doorway he'd paused at in Dr. Okafor's office, everyone pausing at doorways today, delivering verdicts.

"For what it's worth," she said, "whoever bought that estate has deep pockets and a closed mouth. And whoever's representing them flew in special for these meetings. People don't fly in special for a wedding they expect to be ordinary." She tapped the folder once. "Read the file. Sleep on it. Be angry tonight—it's the fourteenth, you've earned it. But be angry *and* curious. Curiosity's the only thing that ever got you anywhere."

The door closed behind her. Cyrus sat alone in the office with the photographs of Hollowbrook spread out before him, the limestone manor glowing in late-afternoon light, the ballroom windows full of sky.

At seven, he went to Bellamy's. Rosa had the corner table ready, the Barolo breathing, the tasting menu already underway. He ate each course and tasted almost none of it. He drank the wine slowly, penance by the glass, and at some point between the pasta and the meat course he took out his phone and reread the last letter from J.A.—the one with the unanswered question in it.

*Do you think people can start over, or do they just get very good at hiding?*

He looked at the words for a long time. Then he typed a reply into the drafting app, three lines, deleted it, typed it again, deleted it again, and put the phone face-down on the white cloth beside his plate, next to the second place setting Rosa always laid anyway, without being asked, without comment, because some kindnesses are too large to acknowledge and too necessary to refuse.

He raised his glass to the empty chair.

"To small safe steps," he murmured. "May they take me somewhere."

The wine was bitter. He drank it anyway.

* * *

He got home at ten and did the thing he had promised himself he wouldn't do: he opened the Hollowbrook file.

He read until midnight. Property history, restoration budgets, the management company's glossy prospectus. And on the last page, the meeting confirmation Marisol had sent: *Thursday, 10:00 a.m., Hollowbrook Estate. Attending for Vane & Co.: M. Reyes, C. Dunmore. Attending for the client: J. Ashe.*

J. Ashe.

Two initials. The same two initials that had signed five years of letters—the patient, funny, piercing letters from a stranger who asked questions no one else dared ask. *J.A.*

Cyrus stared at the page until the letters blurred. It was nothing. It had to be nothing. Half the alphabet's worth of people shared those initials; he'd never known his pen pal's name, had never wanted to, had built the anonymity into a kind of religion. The program guaranteed it. Strangers, forever, safely.

But his hands had gone cold, and the scar on his palm ached, and somewhere in the back of his skull a voice that sounded suspiciously like Marisol said: *people don't fly in special for a wedding they expect to be ordinary.*

He got up. He took the month's letter—the one he'd finally drafted that night, three paragraphs, guarded, honest in the way he only ever managed on paper—and held it over the kitchen sink. One match. That was all it would take. Burn the correspondence, end the program, close the last unguarded room in his life before it could betray him too.

The match flared. He watched it burn down toward his fingers.

Then he blew it out, dropped it in the sink, and put the letter in the drawer instead—unsent, unburned, waiting.

Thursday was coming either way.
