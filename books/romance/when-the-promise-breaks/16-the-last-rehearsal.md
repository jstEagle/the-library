## 16. The Last Rehearsal

### CYRUS

The call came at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, and Cyrus knew before he answered that everything had changed.

Marisol's name on the screen. Marisol never called before seven unless the building was on fire or a client was dying.

"Tell me," he said.

"Mrs. Okafor's daughter called Hollowbrook at five. Adaeze." A breath. "Cyrus, they've moved the wedding. Not back—forward. Ten days from now."

He sat down on the edge of his bed in the gray light. "Ten days. We don't have—"

"I know what we don't have. The florist can't get the ranunculus until June. The string quartet is booked. The cake—" Marisol stopped herself, which she only did when the news was worse than the logistics. "There's more. She's asking for you specifically. Both of you. Adaeze said her mother won't hear of postponing again. She says if she waits for perfect, she'll miss it entirely."

Cyrus closed his eyes. He could see Mrs. Okafor clearly: the way she'd gripped his hand at their last meeting, the papery strength of it, the look she'd given him over the tasting menu like she was reading something written on the inside of his skull. *True, not just pretty.* Her words. His wound.

"Tell them yes," he said.

"You haven't even—"

"Tell them yes."

He hung up and sat there for a long moment, watching the light come up over the rooftops across the street. Ten days. Which meant going back to Hollowbrook. Which meant Julian.

They hadn't spoken since the ballroom. Since Cyrus had stood in that vast empty room with the forged documents shaking in his hand and said things he meant and some things he hadn't, and Julian had stood there taking all of it like a man who'd been waiting five years for exactly that weather. Cyrus had walked out. Had quit the contract by email, formal and final, and Marisol had told him the firm would fold without it, and he'd said *then let it fold*, and then he'd spent nine days discovering that he didn't mean that either—that grief without work was just grief with better lighting.

He went to the office. He pulled the Okafor file. He started making lists, because lists were the language he prayed in.

At noon, Marisol appeared in his doorway with two coffees and an expression he distrusted immediately.

"What."

"She asked me to tell you something else." Marisol set the coffee down. "Adaeze. She said—her exact words—'Tell Mr. Dunmore my mother says he should come back for her, not for anyone else, and that everyone will know the difference.'"

"That's not cryptic at all," Cyrus said.

"She's ninety-one years old and sharp as a box cutter, Cy. She knows exactly what she's doing." Marisol leaned against the doorframe. "Are you going back for her?"

"Yes," Cyrus said. And then, because lying to Marisol was like lying to a mirror: "Only for her."

* * *

### JULIAN

Julian found out from the caterer, who found out from Adaeze, and by the time he got Mrs. Okafor on the phone his hands were not steady.

"Ten days," he said. "Grandmother Okafor, the orchids alone—"

"The orchids will manage," she said. "I may not."

The line went quiet in the particular way hospital rooms are quiet, machines breathing where people used to. Julian sat down on the steps of the Hollowbrook terrace, phone pressed to his ear, looking out at the garden they'd built together—the three of them, back when the three of them were still possible.

"You're moving the wedding because you're afraid," he said gently.

"I'm moving the wedding because I'm certain," she corrected. "There's a difference. Fear makes you hurry past things. Certainty makes you hurry toward them." A pause, and then, in the tone she used when she was about to aim at something: "Is he coming?"

Julian looked at the terrace doors, at the ballroom beyond them where he had confessed everything and been left standing in the wreckage of his own honesty. "I don't know."

"He's coming." She said it the way other people said *the sun will rise*. "And Julian—I want a full rehearsal. Vows and all. None of this standing-around-shuffling-papers business. If we're doing it in ten days, we do it properly."

"We'll rehearse the processional, the readings, the exchange—"

"With stand-ins," she said. "You and Cyrus. I want to see how the vows sound in a real voice before I commit mine to them. Adaeze will read the groom's part opposite you, but the vows themselves—" he could practically hear her smiling, "—I want you two. You know how to say those words. You've both said them before, to other people, and watched them break. That's the voice I want practicing mine."

"Grandmother—"

"Don't 'Grandmother' me. I'm old, not foolish. There's a distinction." And she rang off before he could build any defense at all.

Julian sat on the terrace steps for a long time after the call ended. Nine days since the ballroom. Nine days since he'd laid out the whole rotten architecture of his family's sin—Desmond's forgeries, the threats against his father, the testimony he'd given with a gun made of paper held to his head—and watched Cyrus walk out without a word, because what word was there? *Thank you for telling me* was obscene. *I forgive you* was a lie neither of them could afford.

But he'd kept working. That was the strange mercy of it. Every day he'd come to Hollowbrook and done the work—the floor plans, the vendor calls, the seating charts—and every day he'd half-expected Cyrus to appear in a doorway, and every day he hadn't, and Julian had learned that hope and dread could occupy the same body without speaking to each other.

Now Cyrus was coming back. For Mrs. Okafor. Not for him. He understood that completely, and he intended to honor it, and he also understood—with the clarity that had finally arrived somewhere around day four of silence—that honoring it didn't make it hurt less.

He went inside and opened the vow drafts he'd been avoiding for a week and began, once again, to read everything twice.

* * *

### CYRUS

Hollowbrook looked different in the last week of April—the wisteria gone mad along the south colonnade, the lawns that impossible green that only exists for about eleven days a year and photographs like a lie. Cyrus stood in the gravel drive with his overnight bag and felt the place recognize him, which was ridiculous, places didn't do that, except this one did, because he'd poured six weeks of himself into its bones.

Julian was waiting in the entrance hall.

Not lurking—waiting, openly, deliberately, the way a man stands when he's decided that flinching is no longer on the menu. Dark suit, no tie, sleeves rolled. He looked like he'd lost weight. Cyrus catalogued that and hated himself for cataloguing it.

"Dunmore." Just the name. No warmth offered, none requested.

"Ashe." Cyrus set down his bag. "Where do we start?"

Something moved through Julian's face—not relief exactly, but the shadow relief casts. "The timeline. Adaeze printed everything. Kitchen's ours from Thursday."

"Then let's not waste daylight."

And that was the treaty, unspoken but ironclad: they would work. They would speak in the language of place cards and load-in schedules and candle counts, and they would not touch anything beneath it. Cyrus had rehearsed a hundred versions of this return on the drive up, and in all of them Julian tried to apologize again, or explain, or reach, and in all of them Cyrus had answers loaded like rounds. But Julian gave him nothing to fire at. He simply worked—brilliantly, tirelessly, with the same precision Cyrus remembered from the letters, from the tastings, from that night on the rain-lashed terrace when anger had felt so much like desire that he'd stopped being able to tell his own pulse apart from the storm.

That was the cruelest part. Not the forgery, not the silence. The competence. Five years of letters, and the man on the other end of them had been real the entire time. Every insight about grief, every careful sentence about starting over—all of it true, all of it Julian, all of it written by someone who knew exactly what he'd taken from Cyrus and was writing anyway, maybe *because* of it, penance disguised as friendship.

Cyrus worked beside him for three days and did not forgive him and did not stop noticing him, and both of these facts felt like failures.

On the third evening, Mrs. Okafor came to see the progress. They brought her through in a wheelchair—her insistence, so she could "inspect at ankle height"—with Adaeze pushing and an oxygen tank riding behind like a small loyal dog. She was thinner. The illness had taken flesh off her face and left the architecture showing, and somehow the architecture was magnificent.

She surveyed the ballroom, stripped and scaffolded, the chandeliers bagged in muslin, the floor half-polished.

"It's coming back," she said. "This room remembers weddings. You can't fake that. Some rooms only ever held conferences." She looked from Cyrus to Julian and back, and Cyrus saw her take the temperature between them—the careful distance, the politeness with edges—and something in her expression shifted from satisfaction to strategy.

"Tomorrow," she announced, "we rehearse."

"Grandmother, the florals aren't in until—"

"Not flowers. People." She fixed Cyrus with the look. "Full rehearsal. Processional, vows, all of it. And you two will stand in. Don't argue with a dying woman; it's poor sport, we cheat."

* * *

### JULIAN

The rehearsal was set for four o'clock, and by three Julian had reorganized the linen closet twice and alphabetized a wine list that was already alphabetical.

Adaeze found him there, kneeling among napkins like a man hiding in plain sight.

"My mother sent me to fetch you," she said. "She also told me to tell you that hiding in the linen closet is beneath you, and that she raised you better, which is factually wrong, but effective."

Julian stood. "Adaeze—"

"She knows what she's doing." Adaeze's voice softened. She had her mother's eyes and her mother's terrifying perceptiveness. "Whatever is between you and Mr. Dunmore—she's not meddling for sport. She told me once that the great tragedy of her life wasn't losing Baba. It was the year she wasted being angry before she let herself love him out loud. She's ninety-one, Julian. She doesn't intend to watch two more people waste a year."

"I destroyed that man's life."

"And he came back." She shrugged. "People do strange arithmetic at the end. Come on. She's waiting."

The ballroom had been transformed by rehearsal necessity into something almost honest: chairs in rows, a temporary arch of bare birch branches, afternoon light falling through the tall windows in long gold panels. Mrs. Okafor presided from a chair at the front like a general reviewing troops, oxygen tubing discreet behind her ear, a folder of ceremony notes on her lap.

Cyrus stood near the arch, clipboard in hand, and when Julian entered he looked up and away again in one motion, like touching something hot.

"Positions," Mrs. Okafor commanded.

What followed was forty minutes of genuine chaos and genuine beauty. Adaeze processed to a recording of a quartet arrangement, and halfway down the aisle her composure cracked and she cried, and Mrs. Okafor said, "Good, save some for Saturday," and everyone laughed, and the laughing loosened something in the room. The officiant—a friend of Adaeze's with a wonderful voice—ran the readings. The ring bearer, Adaeze's son, age six, practiced his march with the gravity of a state funeral and dropped the pillow twice.

And then Mrs. Okafor said, "Vows."

"We have your drafts here," the officiant began—

"No. Stand-ins first." She pointed one imperious finger. "Julian. Cyrus. Face each other. I want to hear how the words sit in real mouths before I trust them to mine."

"Grandmother Okafor," Cyrus said carefully, "that's really not necessary—"

"It is entirely necessary. I have planned this wedding for eight months and I have learned one thing about the two of you: separately, you are the best in the business, and together you are something the business doesn't have a word for yet. I want my vows tested by that. Now stand there, both of you, before I stand up myself, and we'll all regret it."

The room went quiet. Adaeze had stopped pretending to organize her bouquet. The officiant was studying his shoes with tremendous interest.

Julian crossed the floor. There was no other option; refusing would have been its own confession. He stopped an arm's length from Cyrus—close enough to see the tiredness under his eyes, the discipline holding his jaw together—and turned to face him, and Cyrus, jaw tight, turned too.

They stood there in the gold light, facing each other across thirty inches and five years, while a dying woman watched them with the patience of someone who had already won.

"Read the groom's vows," Mrs. Okafor instructed. "Both parts. Alternate lines. Go."

The officiant handed Cyrus a card. Cyrus looked at it like it might detonate.

*"I promise to love you,"* he read, and his voice came out steady, which cost him, *"not despite what we survived, but because of what surviving taught us about each other."*

Julian took the second card. His mouth was dry. *"I promise to stop protecting you from the truth, even when the truth is mine to be ashamed of."*

A flicker crossed Cyrus's face. He read on. *"I promise that when you show me your worst, I will not use it as a weapon. I have been wounded by weapons. I know the difference between a wound and a war."*

Julian's throat worked. The next line: *"I promise to earn back, daily, what cannot be returned in a single act. I know some debts compound. I am not asking for a discount."*

Someone in the chairs made a small sound. Adaeze had given up all pretense.

*"I promise,"* Cyrus read, and now his voice was not quite steady, and he let it not be steady, which was perhaps the bravest thing Julian had ever watched him do, *"to believe that a broken promise honestly mourned is worth more than a perfect promise never tested."*

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Mrs. Okafor looked between them for a long moment, and then she smiled—not the shrewd smile, something older and sadder and kinder.

"There," she said softly. "That's the voice. That's exactly the voice." She tapped her folder. "Those are my vows now. Whatever you two think you were doing just now—you were doing them for me. Remember that."

But her eyes, moving from Cyrus to Julian, said something else entirely, and everyone in the room heard it.

* * *

### CYRUS

Afterward, the ballroom emptied by degrees—officiant, family, wheelchairs, echo—until Cyrus was alone stacking rehearsal cards he no longer needed, arranging them because his hands required occupation or they'd shake.

Julian hadn't left.

He stood by the windows with the last of the light on him, and when Cyrus finally couldn't bear the pretense of tidying, Julian spoke without turning around.

"You read those like you meant them."

"They're Mrs. Okafor's vows."

"They're not." Julian turned. "I wrote those drafts. Every line. And you read them like—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "For three days you've barely looked at me. Then you stood there and promised me, in front of God and Adaeze and a six-year-old ring bearer, never to use my worst as a weapon. So which is it, Cyrus? Are those just words? Because I need to know which promises in this room count."

Cyrus set down the cards very carefully.

"Here's what I know," he said. "In ten days, a woman who deserves the most beautiful wedding of the year is going to walk down that aisle, and I am going to give it to her if it kills me. Everything else—" his voice caught on something and he pushed through it, "—everything else is a question I'm not answering today."

"That's not a no."

"It's not anything. It's ten days of work." He picked up his clipboard. "Go home, Ashe. Load-in is at seven."

Julian nodded slowly. But at the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said, without turning: "For what it's worth—I've read every letter you ever sent me more times than I can count. Tonight I finally understand why you always signed them the way you did."

Cyrus went cold. "What way?"

Julian glanced back, and in the dusk his face was unreadable and anything but. "'Until the truth finds us both,'" he said. "You wrote that in every single one. I thought it was a greeting." A pause. "It's a prophecy. Goodnight, Cyrus."

And he was gone, leaving Cyrus alone in the darkening ballroom, holding a stack of borrowed vows and the sudden, vertiginous knowledge that Julian Ashe had just quoted his own secret handwriting back to him—and that somewhere between now and Saturday, he was going to have to decide what a man does with a prophecy that's already come true.
