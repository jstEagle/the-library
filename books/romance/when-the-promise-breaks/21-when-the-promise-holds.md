## 21. When the Promise Holds

The trouble with rebuilding a life from the wreckage of two ruined ones is that nobody tells you where to put the furniture.

I'd been thinking about furniture a lot lately—actual furniture, literal tables and chairs—because Hollowbrook reopened in April and we'd spent three weeks arguing about whether the great hall wanted round tables or long ones. Cyrus wanted long. "Communal," he said. "People should have to pass the salt." I wanted round because round meant no head of the table, no seat of honor, nobody at the top looking down. He called it avoidance dressed up as democracy. I called it the whole point.

We compromised with ovals, which Cyrus declared was what happened when neither person would surrender, and which I privately thought was what marriage was going to be like, if I ever worked up the nerve to ask for one.

That was the thing. The nerve. Five years ago I had testified against him in a deposition room that smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, and I had watched his face do something I still saw when I closed my eyes—the specific way a man's expression collapses when he understands that the person across the table has chosen the other side. And now I wanted to stand in front of him and ask him to legally bind himself to me forever. There were days the audacity of it made me dizzy.

So instead I did what I always did. I drafted.

My desk drawer at Hollowbrook held eleven drafts of a proposal speech. Not letters—I was done hiding behind letters; we'd burned through five years of those in a single night in November, reading them aloud to each other until dawn, until the lies were all on the table and the truths underneath them too, negotiated like a contract with better terms than any I'd ever drawn. No, these were speeches. Spoken aloud, face to face, nowhere to hide. Draft one was formal and terrible. Draft four quoted Mrs. Okafor, who had died in June with her hand in both of ours and her last word a satisfied *there*. Draft seven was funny, or thought it was. Draft nine mentioned Desmond's restitution fund and the scholarship in Theo's name and tried to make our love story sound like justice served, which Cyrus would rightly call sentimental accounting.

Draft eleven was just the words *Marry me* on an index card, because by March I'd stopped trusting myself to say anything longer without ruining it.

I never got to use the index card either. Life, it turns out, does not wait for you to finish drafting.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday in May, during the Fontaine-Reyes wedding, which was the first event Hollowbrook hosted under the new joint letterhead—*Dunmore & Ashe*, a name Cyrus had fought harder against than anything Desmond ever threw at us. "It sounds like a law firm that sues widows," he'd said. Marisol had pointed out that clients loved it, and Cyrus had looked at the booking sheet, and then at me, and surrendered the way he only surrendered now: completely, and with his eyes open.

The crisis began, as crises do at weddings, with flowers.

Specifically: four hundred stems of white ranunculus, ordered months ago from a grower in the valley, failed to arrive. What arrived instead was a truck containing four hundred stems of red dahlias and a driver with a delivery note for a different estate entirely. The ceremony was in ninety minutes. The florist was unreachable—her phone went straight to voicemail, and later we learned she'd been in a ditch outside Petaluma, unhurt, phone shattered, swearing creatively at her insurance company.

Cyrus took the news the way he took all bad news now, which was nothing like the man I'd first met at Hollowbrook's gates, all armor and cold arithmetic. He went very still, then said, "Okay," and then said it again, quieter, the way he did when he was turning panic into a checklist. "Okay. Options."

"Option one," I said, "we call every florist within fifty miles and pray."

"It's a Tuesday in May. Everyone's booked."

"Option two, we pivot the palette. Dahlias are beautiful. We tell the couple red was always the plan."

"They chose white because it was her grandmother's color." Cyrus was already moving down the service corridor toward the prep kitchen, coat off, sleeves rolled, and I followed because following Cyrus Dunmore into a crisis had become, over these months, the most natural motion of my body. "Option three is we make white exist out of nothing. Candles, linens, the birch branches from the winter storage, spray paint if we have to—"

"We are not spray-painting flowers."

"I know we're not spray-painting flowers." He yanked open the door to the supply closet off the kitchen—the narrow one, the one that held the linens and the spare chair legs and the box of Mrs. Okafor's things we still hadn't decided what to do with—and stepped inside, and I stepped in after him, and that was my first mistake, because the closet fit one person comfortably and two people like a confession booth.

"What are we doing in here?" I asked.

"Looking for the emergency candle stock. Forty votives minimum, we can line the aisle—" He reached up to the top shelf, on his toes, arm stretched, and the shelf, which had been asking for attention since February, chose this moment to give it. A crate of glass hurricane lamps came down. Cyrus caught one against his chest. Two more hit the floor and burst. And a third clipped me on the shoulder on its way past, which is how I ended up pressed flat against the back wall of a linen closet with a florist's disaster unfolding thirty feet away and Cyrus Dunmore holding a lamp like a shield, laughing—not the dry half-laugh he permitted himself at my expense, but something helpless and full, his head tipped back, his eyes shut, the sound coming out of him like water from a cracked dam.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only apology I have left to offer anyone: I had never heard him laugh like that. Not in the letters, not at the tasting events, not on the night we read everything aloud. In five years of knowing him—first as a rival, then as a ghost, then as a stranger with familiar handwriting, then as whatever this was—I had catalogued his smiles the way other men catalogue weather. The professional smile. The wry one. The one that meant he was hurting and covering. But laughter, real laughter, unguarded and undignified, echoing off shelves of folded tablecloths—that I had never once been given.

And it did what it did. I'm not going to pretend I deliberated.

"Marry me," I said.

The laughter stopped. Not died—stopped, cut clean, the way a needle lifts off a record. Cyrus stood there in the dim closet light with a hurricane lamp in his arms and looked at me, and I watched three separate expressions cross his face in about a second and a half: disbelief, suspicion, and then something so raw and startled that it made my chest hurt to witness it.

"That's—" He set the lamp down on a shelf with exaggerated care, the way people handle objects when their hands aren't steady. "Julian. There are four hundred wrong flowers outside and glass on the floor and you're proposing in a supply closet."

"I had eleven drafts," I said miserably. "There was an index card. This wasn't—I didn't plan for it to happen here."

"You planned it?"

"I planned everything except the part where I actually did it. That's rather the pattern of my life."

He stared at me. Somewhere beyond the door, Marisol's voice was rising in fluent Spanish at the delivery driver, and a votive candle rolled across the kitchen tile and came to rest against the threshold like a small orange witness.

"Say it again," Cyrus said quietly.

"Marry me."

"Not like an apology."

I took a breath. The closet smelled like lavender sachets and broken glass and, faintly, Mrs. Okafor's cedar chest, and I thought: she would have loved this. She engineered half the important moments of our lives from beyond reasonable doubt; it seemed fitting she'd get credit for this one too.

"Marry me," I said, "because I love you. Because I testified against you and you let me back into your life anyway, which is more forgiveness than I will ever understand how to deserve, so I've stopped trying to deserve it and started trying to earn it daily instead. Because you make oval tables and I make round ones and somehow the room always ends up beautiful. Because when the file went out and Desmond folded like cheap chairs, you could have taken the victory and left, and instead you stayed and rebuilt this place with me, joint ownership, joint losses, joint everything. Because I read your letters twice even before I knew they were yours, and I've read everything else of yours since, and I want the rest of it. All of it. The unedited drafts. Marry me."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Then Cyrus said, "You rehearsed that in the shower, didn't you."

"Draft nine," I admitted. "Mostly."

And he laughed again—that same cracked-dam laugh, doubling over, pressing the back of his hand to his mouth, and I realized with a jolt that this was the first time in the entire history of us that I had made him laugh like this, that maybe I was the first person in years who had, and that I would spend the rest of my life committing crimes against floral logistics if this was the reward.

"Yes," he said, when he could breathe.

"Yes?"

"Yes. Obviously yes. God, Julian, you proposed in a closet surrounded by Mrs. Okafor's belongings—she planned this, you know. She absolutely planned this. She's probably watching from somewhere taking notes." He picked up the intact hurricane lamp and handed it to me like a scepter. "Now help me find forty candles. We have a wedding to save, and then—" the smallest pause, the ghost of the old guardedness surfacing and being deliberately set down, "—then apparently we have one of our own to plan."

* * *

We saved the Fontaine-Reyes wedding. Of course we did. Cyrus lined the aisle with forty-one votives—he found one extra in the chapel annex and refused, on principle, to leave a candle unemployed—stripped the dahlias down to a few dramatic crimson accents at the altar, and draped the birch branches with white silk from the winter stock until the hall glowed like the inside of a lantern. When the bride saw it she cried, and told Cyrus it was better than what she'd imagined, and Cyrus accepted the compliment with a grace that would have been unthinkable eighteen months ago, and then found me in the kitchen doorway and kissed me in front of the entire catering staff, which Marisol captured on her phone and which I have not asked her to delete.

Later—much later, the hall dark, the couple gone, the two of us sitting on the floor of the empty ballroom with our backs against the stage because neither of us wanted to go home yet—we talked about rings, and dates, and then Cyrus went quiet in the particular way that meant a plan was forming behind his eyes.

"What?" I asked.

"Nothing."

"Cyrus."

"I'm just thinking," he said, "that I've planned roughly four hundred weddings in my career, and I have opinions. Strong ones. About all of it." He turned his head, and in the dark his profile was all I could see, and there was something in it I hadn't seen since before the scandal—something young, almost mischievous, the man Theo must have known first, before everything. "And I think I'd like to write some vows. Actual vows. On paper."

"You're going to draft them, aren't you. Multiple versions."

"Eleven, minimum," Cyrus said. "It's tradition now."

He got up then, and pulled me up after him, and we walked out through the sleeping house together—but I noticed, as we passed his office, that he paused in the doorway, looking at the blank legal pad on his desk, and that his hand flexed once at his side like a man picking up a pen in his mind.

I fell asleep that night happier than I had any right to be, and if I had known what he was already beginning to compose on that legal pad—if I had known he intended to plan our wedding the way he planned everything, flawlessly, secretly, down to the last candle—I might have slept even better.

Or I might have been terrified. With Cyrus, it was always both.

The next morning I found the first page of his notes pinned inside the supply closet door, right where the shelf had fallen: a single line, underlined twice.

*Small. Imperfect. Ours.*
