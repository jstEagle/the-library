## 22. One Last Wedding

The guest list fit on an index card.

Cyrus had planned weddings for four hundred people in ballrooms gilded like the inside of a jewelry box. He had once managed a seating chart so complex it required color-coded software and two interns. He had negotiated with florists in three languages and once talked a bride out of releasing doves by explaining, gently, that doves were actually pigeons with good PR.

And now he stood at his kitchen counter with an index card, a pen, and eleven names.

Marisol. Mrs. Okafor's daughter Adaeze and her husband. The therapist he'd finally stopped deflecting—Dr. Nwosu, who had earned her invitation by refusing, for three years, to let him leave a session early. The coordinator from the grief program, who cried when she opened the envelope. Two of Julian's friends from the city who had chosen him over his family without hesitation. And at the bottom, in smaller letters, as if the card itself were shy: *Julian.*

That was it. That was everyone.

He looked at the card for a long time. Then he laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that still surprised him when it arrived—and wrote at the top, where a client's name would normally go:

*Dunmore–Ashe. Keep it simple. Keep it true.*

* * *

They'd bought Hollowbrook together in the spring, which sounded romantic until you saw the paperwork. Joint ownership meant joint everything: budgets, vendor contracts, arguments about chair covers that lasted forty minutes because neither of them knew how to lose gracefully. The estate had reopened in June with a wedding for a couple who'd met in a hospital waiting room, and Cyrus had stood in the back during the vows and discovered that his eyes did something embarrassing now whenever anyone said *in sickness and in health.*

Mrs. Okafor would have teased him mercilessly. He thought about her every day. Some days it felt like grief; more days now it felt like inheritance. Her wedding had been the hinge of his whole life—the one honest wedding, the one that cracked him open and let everything old drain out and everything new pour in. Adaeze had asked him once, near the end of the reception, what her mother would have wanted them to do with the place.

"Fill it," Cyrus had said. "She hated empty rooms."

So they filled it. And Desmond's restitution money—the first installment of it, wired with a lawyer's cold precision and not one word of apology—funded the scholarship in Theo's name, sending two young planners a year through hospitality school. Cyrus had written the selection criteria himself. The last line read: *Preference given to candidates who have failed publicly and kept working.* He liked to imagine Theo reading it and groaning at the sentimentality.

Now it was October, and Cyrus Dunmore was planning the only wedding he had ever planned for someone he loved, and he was doing it badly on purpose.

That was the hard part. Not the budget—he could plan a wedding on a shoestring in his sleep. Not the logistics. The hard part was the discipline of imperfection. Every instinct in him screamed to make it flawless, to build one last monument, to prove something to an industry that had already stopped watching. But he'd learned what monuments cost. He'd built one at thirty-one and lived in its rubble until he was thirty-six.

So: no ballroom. The terrace, where rain had once stranded two enemies overnight with a bottle of wine and five years of unsaid things. No imported flowers—Marisol was cutting dahlias from the garden that morning, arguing cheerfully with Julian about stem length. No seating chart at all, because with eleven guests you could just let people sit where their hearts told them. A cake baked by Adaeze, who warned them it might collapse. Cyrus had inspected the recipe and pronounced the structural risk acceptable.

"Acceptable," Julian had repeated, delighted. "Five years ago you'd have flown in a pastry architect from Vienna."

"Five years ago I believed a wedding was a performance review."

"And now?"

Cyrus had considered the question longer than Julian expected. "Now I think it's a promise you make in front of witnesses so you can't quietly take it back. Everything else is decoration."

Julian had kissed him then, flour on his hands, and gotten it on Cyrus's collar, and Cyrus hadn't cared, which was its own kind of miracle.

* * *

The night before the wedding, Cyrus sat alone at the kitchen table in the cottage on the estate grounds and wrote a letter.

He almost didn't. It felt ceremonial in a way that embarrassed him—except that was exactly why it mattered. Five years ago, a stranger's handwriting had saved his life without either of them knowing it. The grief program had matched him with "J.A." and told him to write honestly to someone who couldn't see his face, and he had, week after week, pouring out the things he couldn't say aloud: that he missed Theo with a pain that had no etiquette, that he was afraid he'd never believe in anything again, that some mornings getting dressed was the entire victory. And someone on the other end had written back—not with advice, never with advice, just with presence. *I read this twice,* the letters always began. *I'll read it again.*

He hadn't known it was Julian. Julian hadn't known it was him. They had been telling each other the truth for years under false names, and then they'd met in a doorway at Hollowbrook holding keys to the same venue, and the truth had gotten complicated, and then it had gotten catastrophic, and then—slowly, over one long night of reading letters aloud, over months of negotiation instead of absolution—it had gotten simple again.

One letter remained unwritten. The last one. The program had closed their file in the summer—*participants have reconnected; correspondence concluded*—but Cyrus had asked for an exception. One final letter, hand-delivered. The coordinator had agreed, on the condition that he actually deliver it, because she knew him well enough by now to know he'd otherwise draft it forty times and burn it.

He'd burned things before. He'd fished them out of sinks before, too. He was, if nothing else, consistent.

The pen hovered. Then he wrote the way he'd learned to write in that program—no polish, no performance, no empire-building in prose.

---

*Dear J.A.,*

*This is the last letter I'll write you as your pen pal, because tomorrow morning you'll be standing across from me on the terrace, and after that I can just tell you things, which is terrifying and wonderful and mostly terrifying.*

*I've been thinking about what these letters were. For a long time I thought they were a lifeline, and they were. But I think they were also practice. You practiced listening to a man in pieces. I practiced saying true things to another human being. We were rehearsing each other for years without knowing it, which is either a coincidence or proof that Mrs. Okafor was right about forgiveness being the point of all this. She was usually right. I miss her. So do you. We'll light the candle tomorrow like we promised.*

*I want to be honest about one more thing, because honesty is the only foundation we've ever had that held. I don't forgive you the way people mean it in movies—one grand gesture and the ledger clears. I forgave you the way you renovate a house: room by room, finding damage you didn't expect, deciding again and again that the house is worth it. Some rooms I'm still working on. I'll tell you when I finish them. That's my vow, really—all of them, the ones I'll say out loud tomorrow and the ones I won't. I will keep telling you the truth even when it costs me, and I will stay even when the truth is ugly, and I will never again measure my life in safe steps.*

*Because here's what I know now, at the end of all this correspondence: the largest step I ever took wasn't rebuilding the company, or buying back my name, or standing in front of Desmond Ashe with a file full of his sins. It was answering the first letter. It was writing back to a stranger when every part of me wanted to stay sealed. Everything good in my life came from that one unsafe thing.*

*Tomorrow there will be eleven guests, collapsing cake, dahlias with the stems cut wrong, and rain in the forecast, which we are not moving the date for, because the rain is ours. It rained the night everything started. Let it rain the day everything continues.*

*I believe in weddings again. One, anyway.*

*It's yours.*

*C.*

---

He read it twice. Then, because some rituals deserve to be inherited, he read it a third time, and folded it into an envelope, and wrote *J.A.* on the front in the same handwriting Julian had recognized across a conference table five years ago and been too much of a coward—or too much of a man in love, the distinction had taken them a long time to settle—to mention.

He put it in his jacket pocket, where it sat over his heart like a small, patient fire.

* * *

It rained, of course. It rained exactly the way it was supposed to—soft and steady, silvering the terrace, drumming gently on the canopy Marisol had rigged up over his objections ("You said no tents." "I said no *tents.* This is an *architectural canopy,* Cyrus, learn the difference"). The dahlias were slightly wrong-colored and perfect. The cake leaned. Nobody minded.

Eleven chairs. Eleven people. Dr. Nwosu cried before the processional even started, which Cyrus pretended not to notice and appreciated more than he could say. The candle for Mrs. Okafor burned at the front beside the candle for Theo, two small flames that refused, in the breeze off the wet garden, to gutter.

Adaeze played no music; instead she read the note her mother had left with Cyrus a week before she died, the one he'd kept in his desk drawer through everything—the confrontation, the walkout, the file, the reckoning. *If those two fools haven't figured it out by my wedding, lock them in the wine cellar until they do.* Everyone laughed. Cyrus had to take a moment.

Then it was time, and Julian stood across from him under the canopy with rain-washed light on his face, and Cyrus reached into his jacket.

"Not yet," Julian whispered, smiling. "Vows first."

"This is a vow."

He handed over the envelope. J.A., in his own hand. Julian looked down at it and understood instantly—Cyrus watched it land, watched five years of letters rearrange themselves behind his eyes—and his breath went ragged in a way that two hundred witnesses at any of Cyrus's old empire weddings had never once pulled out of him.

"You're supposed to save it," Julian managed.

"I spent five years saving things. I'm done." Cyrus took both his hands. His own voice held, barely. "Read it tonight. I already know what it says. It says yes."

Julian opened it right there, because he was Julian, and read the last line with his lips moving, and then he laughed and cried at the same time, which Cyrus had once believed was structurally impossible in a person and now knew was simply what wholeness looked like on short notice.

"I believe in weddings again," Julian recited back to him, wet-eyed, grinning. "One, anyway."

"One," Cyrus agreed. "Mine."

The officiant cleared her throat. Somewhere behind them, Marisol was sobbing into a dahlia. The rain kept its soft time on the canopy like a metronome set to the pace of a heartbeat that had finally, after five long years and twenty-two letters, stopped counting steps—

and Cyrus Dunmore, who had measured his whole life in safe ones, took the largest step of it, forward, toward the man holding his last letter, and did not look back at the man he'd been before.

The rain fell. The candles held. And the promise, this time, held too.
