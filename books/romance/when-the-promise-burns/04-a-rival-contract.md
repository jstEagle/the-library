## 4. A Rival Contract

The letter lived in the second drawer of Declan Roth's desk, beneath a stack of vendor contracts he never read past the first page because his lawyer read them for him now. It was folded into thirds, soft at the creases from ten years of being taken out and put back, and it was addressed to no one, which was the whole problem.

He hadn't opened it in four months. That was a record. He'd gone three months once, in 2019, and then his sister had gotten married and he'd sat through the vows with his jaw tight as a piano wire and come home and read it twice by the light over the stove.

Tonight he didn't open it either. Tonight he opened the Whitmore file instead, because Camille Whitmore — third generation estate money, first generation taste — had finally returned his calls, and her assistant had sent over the preliminary details at 6:47 p.m. on a Tuesday like it wasn't the biggest contract either of their companies would fight for this year.

Three hundred guests. The estate itself, all eleven acres of it, tented on the south lawn. A budget that made Declan's accountants sit up straighter than they had since the Halvorsen account dried up. And one line in the RFP that had made him laugh out loud alone in his office, a short ugly sound:

*Please note: Vane & Co. Events has been invited to submit a competing proposal.*

Of course they had. Camille Whitmore ran her daughter's wedding search the way old-money families ran everything — like an auction where the bidders didn't know each other's numbers but knew each other's names.

Declan knew the name. He'd spent ten years knowing the name.

He leaned back in his chair. The office around him was good — he'd made sure of that, made it good on purpose, glass and walnut and a view of the harbor that said *successful* to every client who walked in. His company had survived the split, thrived even, thirty employees and a waitlist and a wall of press clippings his publicist kept framed. *Roth Events Plans the Impossible.* He'd built exactly what he'd set out to build.

It just turned out that what he'd set out to build was a very expensive way of being alone.

His phone buzzed. His sister, Nora, because sisters could smell avoidance through cell towers.

**Nora:** *Did you eat*

**Declan:** *Yes.*

**Nora:** *Liar. Did you look at the Whitmore thing*

**Declan:** *Working on it.*

**Nora:** *Is she bidding*

He looked at the message for a long time. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, patient as anything.

**Declan:** *Yes.*

**Nora:** *God help us all. Eat something. You get weird when you don't eat and you're already weird*

He put the phone face-down. She wasn't wrong, which was the irritating thing about Nora. She'd been telling him he was weird for a decade, ever since the night he'd shown up at her apartment at two in the morning, gray-faced, and said only, "She's gone," and Nora, who had never met Ulyana Vane except across a ballroom, had understood immediately that something had ended that was never going to be explained to her satisfaction.

"Was it a fight?" she'd asked once, years later, wine-loosened.

"No."

"Then what?"

"I rehearsed something," he'd said, "and she heard the rehearsal."

Nora had stared at him like he was speaking a language she almost knew. "That's not an answer, Dec."

"It's the only one I have."

* * *

The Whitmore estate sat on a ridge above the river, white columns and black shutters and a lawn so green it looked lit from underneath. Declan arrived forty minutes early because arriving early was free armor, and walked the grounds with the site map in one hand and a measuring wheel he didn't need in the other, because clients liked to see a man with a measuring wheel. It said *thorough*. It said *this is a man who counts things*.

He counted things. That was most of what he did anymore.

The ceremony would go there, under the copper beech — Camille's mother had been married under it, Camille herself too, and the daughter, Lila, wanted the same tree, which meant the tree was non-negotiable, which meant the seating geometry had to bend around its roots. Declan stood under the branches doing the math in his head, sight lines and aisle width and where the string quartet would fit without drowning in the tree's acoustic shadow, and he was deep enough into it that he didn't hear the car until the gravel crunched.

He knew the car before he saw it. Of course he did. Charcoal Audi, spotless, parked at an angle that managed to be both efficient and slightly territorial.

Ulyana Vane got out wearing a cream suit that probably cost more than his first year of rent, sunglasses pushed up into dark hair, a leather portfolio under one arm, and an expression that suggested she'd known he'd be early and had come early-er specifically to ruin it.

"Roth." She said his name the way you'd identify a stain.

"Vane." He let the measuring wheel click to a stop. "They gave you the same memo, huh."

"They gave everyone the same memo. I assume you're here to measure things." Her eyes flicked to the wheel. "Very masculine. Very reassuring."

"You'd be amazed how much clients trust a man with a wheel."

"I'd be amazed how much clients trust a man who needs one." She was already walking, heels sinking slightly into the turf, heading for the beech tree with the unerring instinct of someone who had done this four hundred times. "Ceremony goes under the tree. Don't tell me you just figured that out."

"I figured it out twenty minutes ago."

"Mm." She stopped under the branches, tilted her head back, and for one unguarded second her face did something complicated — the look of a person seeing a room she'd already furnished. Then it closed again, smooth as a door. "Sight lines are a nightmare here. The canopy eats sound below eight feet. You'd know that if you'd ever produced anything with live strings."

"I produced the Marchetti wedding. Live orchestra. Forty pieces."

"And how many guests complained they couldn't hear the vows?"

Declan opened his mouth. Closed it. Three guests. There had been three complaints and he'd fixed it with directional mics and nobody outside the production team ever knew, but she knew, because she always knew, because they had built the same company out of the same brain once and then torn it down the middle like a wishbone.

"You've been reading my press," he said.

"I read everyone's press. It's called due diligence." She flipped open her portfolio and made a note, actual handwriting, fountain pen, because Ulyana Vane still used a fountain pen in the year of our lord, and watching her do it did something to the back of his throat that he chose to ignore. "Lila wants lanterns on the water. Floating installation, the pond past the hedge line. Your proposal will say drones or projections, because you have no soul."

"My proposal will say whatever wins."

"There it is." She snapped the portfolio shut. "Ten years and that's still your whole philosophy."

"No," Declan said, and heard his own voice come out lower than he intended. "My philosophy used to be that we win together. You're the one who left."

The silence after that lasted maybe two seconds and cost him about a decade.

Ulyana's face went carefully, professionally blank — he watched her do it, watched the shutters come down floor by floor like a building closing for the night — and when she spoke again her voice was pleasant and dead as a dial tone. "Enjoy the measuring, Roth. I hear the grounds are lovely this time of year."

She walked back to her car without looking at him, and he stood under the copper beech with his measuring wheel in his hand feeling like a man who had brought a knife to a gunfight he'd started himself.

* * *

Back at the office, the team was waiting, because Declan paid people to be waiting.

Theo had the conference room set up — Theo always had the conference room set up, Theo was the kind of junior partner who color-coded other people's calendars unprompted, sharp-suited and hungry and twenty-six years old and reminded Declan, uncomfortably, of himself at twenty-six, minus the conscience. The wall screen showed the Whitmore RFP broken into workstreams. Priya-equivalents and Marcus-equivalents, his own versions, lined the table with coffee and competitive hunger.

"Talk to me," Declan said.

"The budget's real," Theo said. "Not vanity real. Real real. If we land this we clear last year's entire Q3 in one contract." He tapped the screen. "One problem. Vane & Co. is bidding, and Vane & Co. has the better reputation with legacy-family clients. They've done three multi-generational estates in two years. We've done—"

"We've done the Marchetti," someone said.

"We've done the Marchetti," Theo agreed, "which was spectacular and also, per the trade press, 'cold.' Their word. 'Technically flawless, emotionally cold.'"

Declan felt that one in the sternum. *Emotionally cold.* Ten years of building and the industry's verdict on him fit in two words, and both of them were her fault, or his fault, or nobody's fault, depending on which 2 a.m. you asked him.

"So we don't bid cold," Declan said. "We bid warm. Lila Whitmore is twenty-eight, she grew up on this property, she cried when they told her the boathouse was coming down. I read the interview she gave the regional magazine. Find me the emotional architecture. What does this wedding have to protect?"

The room shifted, energized, pens moving. Theo smiled his fast smile. "On it. Also—" he slid a printed sheet across the table, "—the charity thing. The hospital program. HR signed us up, remember? Pen-pal fundraiser, anonymous matches, donors underwrite letters between staff and, uh, whoever. You got assigned a match. First letter came in."

Declan looked at the envelope. Plain, forwarded through the program office, his initials on the front in someone else's handwriting — the program matched by initial only, some half-clever scheme about anonymity and connection. *D*, written in careful block letters.

"I don't have time for this."

"It's a charity event, boss. You have to show up at the gala anyway. You have to write at least three letters or the PR photos look weird." Theo's grin widened. "Your pen pal's first letter's actually funny. Read it."

Declan read it standing up, half-listening to the room argue about lanterns versus projections.

*Dear D,* it began. *I'm told I have to write to a stranger for charity, which is either noble or extortion, depending on how you feel about hospitals. I feel fine about hospitals. I feel less fine about strangers, so consider this letter a hostile act of generosity.*

*I'm told we can't share names. Good. Names ruin everything. Ask anyone I've ever worked with.*

*A question, since we're apparently doing this: what's the biggest thing you've ever ruined? Professionally speaking. I ask because I ruined a wedding once — not mine, I don't have one, that's a different tragedy — and I've built a career on making sure it never happens again. I find most people are running from something. I like to know what.*

Declan read it twice. Something about the voice snagged in him like a fishhook — the dryness, the precision, the way the humor was load-bearing, holding up something heavier behind it. He'd known a voice like that. He'd sat across a desk from a voice like that for six years, trading barbs over seating charts, and the memory of it landed in his chest with a weight completely disproportionate to a paragraph on a page.

"You're smiling," Theo said, alarmed. "You never smile at correspondence."

"I'm not smiling."

"You're absolutely smiling. Who is she?"

"It's anonymous, Theo. That's the point of the program."

"Uh-huh." Theo collected his papers, insufferable. "Well, anonymous funny-person is waiting on a reply. And separately — the Whitmore site visit. Vane's office requested the same walkthrough slot as us. Facilities says we can't split it. So it's you and her, same morning, same estate."

Declan looked at the window, at the harbor lights, at nothing.

"Fine," he said.

* * *

The house was quiet when he got home, in the way it was always quiet — not peaceful quiet, just unaired quiet, the quiet of rooms that waited for him rather than lived around him. Four bedrooms, two occupants, one of them a man who ate standing at the counter because the dining table seated twelve and twelve felt like an accusation.

He fed the cat. The cat had shown up two winters ago and appointed himself, which was the longest relationship Declan had maintained since—

Since.

He poured two fingers of whiskey he didn't want and carried it to the study, and before he could stop himself — because he never could stop himself, that was the whole disease of him — he opened the second drawer and took out the letter.

Folded in thirds. Soft at the creases. Unsent.

He didn't read it. He'd memorized it years ago; reading it was just a ritual, like visiting a grave. He held it instead, feeling the paper's age, and thought about the walkthrough. About her face under the copper beech, that one unguarded second where she'd looked at the tree like she was already dressing it in flowers, and how fast the shutters had come down when he'd said *you left*.

You left. As if that were the whole story. As if she'd walked out of that office for no reason, on no evidence, out of nowhere — when really she'd walked out because of what she'd heard him say, or half-say, or fail to say, standing alone in a dark office rehearsing a sentence to the empty chairs, flat and cold and terrible, practicing love like a pitch deck while she stood in the doorway listening.

He'd never known she'd heard. He'd learned it only later, in fragments, in the way she flinched from the word *together*, and by then she'd rebuilt her life on the assumption that Declan Roth rehearsed his feelings for an audience of investors.

And he had let her believe it. Ten years of letting her believe it, because the truth required a conversation he didn't know how to start, and pride was easier, and ambition filled the hours.

Regret disguised as ambition. Nora's phrase, thrown at him across a Thanksgiving table two years ago, and he'd thrown a dinner roll back at her, and neither of them had mentioned it since, because it was true.

He set the letter down on the blotter, next to the Whitmore file, next to the pen-pal letter with its hostile generosity and its question — *what's the biggest thing you've ever ruined?*

He got out a pen. Fountain pen, because she'd ruined him for ballpoints too, one more thing on the long invoice.

*Dear stranger,* he wrote. *Biggest thing I ever ruined: I had a partnership once. Business. Mostly business. I had a speech prepared and I practiced it until it died, and the person it was for heard the autopsy instead of the birth. So now I plan other people's weddings, which is either penance or cowardice, and I haven't decided which.*

*You asked what people run from. In my experience: not the thing they lost. The thing they almost said.*

He read it back. It was too honest. It was the most honest thing he'd written in a decade, addressed to a nameless stranger through a children's hospital charity, and his hand hovered over the signature line where the program rules said *initials only*.

He wrote *R*.

Then he folded it, sealed it, and set it on top of the outgoing mail — and only afterward noticed that his hands were shaking, and only afterward wondered why a letter to a stranger had done what ten years of silence couldn't.

Outside, the harbor lights burned on, patient as anything, and in the second drawer of his desk the old letter lay folded in thirds, waiting — though for what, after ten years, Declan Roth no longer pretended to know.
