## 6. Letters Without Names

The second letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Ulyana would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day she least trusted anything.

She found it in the charity program's pigeonhole — a literal wooden pigeonhole, because the firm's founder had insisted on antique furniture and the program's coordinator had insisted on anonymity, and the result was a slot labeled only with a number: *Match 14*. Inside sat a cream envelope, hand-addressed in a slanted, impatient script that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper. No name. Just a single letter in the upper corner where a return address should have been:

**R.**

Ulyana stood in the hallway outside the mail room for a full thirty seconds before she picked it up. She told herself it was because she was busy. She had a timeline review at eleven, a tasting menu dispute between a groom and his mother-in-law-to-be that required diplomatic intervention by noon, and a florist who kept texting her photos of ranunculus with the caption *THESE OR I QUIT.* She did not have time for pen pals. She had signed up for this program because Marcus had guilt-tripped her in front of the entire staff, and because the children's hospital got twenty dollars per completed exchange, and because refusing would have made her look like exactly what she was.

Which was not, she reminded herself, a person who wanted to know what R had to say.

She carried the envelope to her office anyway. Unopened. She set it beside her keyboard, where it sat like a small accusation, and she answered forty-one emails, approved two contracts, talked Priya down from the ranunculus ledge, and drank an entire cup of coffee while it went cold.

Then she opened it.

*Dear V,*

*I've been thinking about what you wrote — that you don't believe in grand gestures, that the best weddings are the ones where nobody cries during the vows. You're wrong, by the way. But you're wrong in an interesting way, so I'll allow it.*

*You asked why a man in my line of work signs up to write letters to strangers. Fair question. Here's the honest answer, or as honest as I get on paper: my therapist says I'm better in writing. Apparently there's a version of me that only exists when nobody can interrupt.*

*So let me tell you about the worst day of my professional life. Ten years ago, give or take, I destroyed something I'd spent years building. Not through scandal or bankruptcy — nothing dramatic enough to make a good story at dinner parties. I destroyed it slowly, the way you ruin a foundation: one compromise at a time, each one reasonable, each one small, until one morning you look around and realize the whole structure is leaning and you can't remember when it started.*

*There was a person involved. There always is. Someone who built the thing with me, who saw every compromise I made and stayed longer than I deserved. And on the last night — the night it all came apart — I had something I needed to say to them. Something I'd been rehearsing. I'd rehearsed it so many times it had gone smooth as river stone, worn down from carrying it.*

*I never said it. They left before I could. And here's the part I've never written down: I don't blame them. If I'd heard someone say those words in the flat, careful voice I used — practice voice, rehearsal voice, the voice of a man trying to make something enormous sound manageable — I'd have run too.*

*Anyway. That's why I write letters. The page doesn't have a door anyone can walk out of.*

*Your turn, V. Tell me something you've never said out loud.*

*R.*

Ulyana read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you re-read a contract looking for the clause that will hurt you later.

The office noise — phones, laughter, Priya arguing with a delivery driver about peony availability — receded until she was sitting inside a small, cold silence of her own manufacture.

*A sentence he never said. Rehearsed. A person who left before he could say it.*

Ten years ago. Give or take.

Her hands were steady. She noticed that with a kind of clinical detachment, the way she noticed everything — the way she'd learned to notice everything, because noticing was safer than feeling. Her hands were steady and her coffee was cold and somewhere outside her window a delivery truck was beeping its way backward down the street, and all of it continued as if the universe had not just played a small, precise joke at her expense.

Because it was a joke. It had to be. The world was full of men who'd ruined partnerships and lost people and rehearsed sentences they never delivered. Statistically, it was practically common. Somewhere in this city right now there were probably dozens of middle-aged men drafting apologies to ghosts, and the odds that her anonymous pen pal — some stranger matched to her by an algorithm designed by an intern — happened to be *that* man, from *that* night, were so vanishingly small that entertaining the thought for more than four seconds would constitute a failure of the exact rationality she prided herself on.

Except.

Except that the letter was funny in a way that snagged something familiar. Except that the handwriting pressed into the paper like it was trying to get through. Except that *the page doesn't have a door anyone can walk out of* was the kind of line a certain kind of man wrote — a man who once, in a conference room in a hotel in Napa, had said *I think better on paper, you know that about me,* and she had said *I know everything about you,* and she had been wrong about at least one thing, possibly more.

Ulyana closed her eyes.

The memory arrived the way it always did — uninvited, punctual, merciless.

A Thursday night, ten years ago. The Roth & Vane office after hours, the good lamp still on over Declan's desk, his voice carrying through the glass partition because he'd never learned that glass partitions carry voices, or maybe he'd never imagined anyone was there to hear. She'd come back for her laptop charger. She'd stopped in the hallway when she heard him talking to no one.

*"I've loved you since—"*

A pause. A frustrated exhale. Then again, flatter, more careful, the words sanded down: *"I've loved you since the first wedding we ruined together."*

And then a third time, quieter, testing it, and she hadn't stayed for the third time. She'd walked out with her charger and her heart hammering and her mind already doing what her mind always did — protecting her, translating, explaining. *He's rehearsing it for someone else. He's practicing on the empty office because saying it to her will be hard. It isn't for me. It can't be for me. People like Declan Roth don't—*

She'd quit the next morning. Effective immediately. She'd given a speech about diverging visions and creative differences and she'd shaken his hand and she'd watched his face do something complicated and then go carefully blank, and she had told herself for ten years that she'd read the situation correctly. That walking away was the rational move. That a woman who planned two hundred weddings a year knew better than anyone how the story went: the confession, the awkward pause, the slow fade, the anniversary nobody remembers.

He'd been rehearsing. Of course he'd been rehearsing. People rehearsed things that mattered to them. And she had heard the rehearsal — the flat, careful, practice-voice version, the draft, not the performance — and she had fled from a rough cut of a sentence and never stayed for the premiere.

*If I'd heard someone say those words in the flat, careful voice I used — practice voice, rehearsal voice — I'd have run too.*

The pen on her desk suddenly looked very far away.

"Boss?"

She surfaced. Junie was standing in her doorway holding a binder like a shield, twenty-two years old and radiating the kind of optimism Ulyana had spent a decade professionally dismantling. "Sorry — you had your thinking face on. The Whitmore confirmation came through. Site visit Friday, both firms, their lawyer is going to be there, and also Priya wants to know if you died because you didn't answer the ranunculus text."

"I didn't die."

"You have dead eyes, though."

"I always have dead eyes. It's called composure."

Junie grinned and retreated, and Ulyana looked back down at the letter, at the single bold **R** in the corner, and felt the past knock politely against the inside of her ribs. Not breaking in. Just knocking. Just asking to be acknowledged.

She should stop writing to him. That was the clean move, the smart move. She could write one final note — *program requirements fulfilled, best of luck* — and close Match 14 and never think about any of it again. That was what a guarded woman did. That was what the armor was *for.*

Instead she pulled a sheet of the firm's heavy correspondence stock — ivory, the good stuff, reserved for apology letters to clients whose weddings had caught fire, metaphorically speaking — and uncapped her pen.

*Dear R,*

*You don't get to ask me for something I've never said out loud in letter two. There are rules to this. I'm inventing them now, but they exist.*

*Also, you're wrong about crying during vows. Nobody cries at the good weddings. The good weddings are the ones where everyone is too busy being happy to perform it. Crying is for weddings where the bride knows something the guests don't.*

*Here's what I'll give you instead, since you gave me your worst day: I once walked out of something big. Partnership, business, the whole architecture of a life — gone in a morning. Everyone said I was rash. Maybe I was. But I heard something I wasn't supposed to hear, and I made a decision in about nine seconds, and I have spent ten years telling myself the decision was correct. I've gotten very good at telling myself that. I could teach a seminar.*

*What I've never said out loud is this: I don't actually know if I was right. I know I was fast. Fast and right are not the same thing, and I built a career on confusing them.*

*You asked why I signed up for this program. The real answer is that a colleague ambushed me in front of witnesses and I have a public image to maintain. The truer answer is that I like letters. Letters wait. Letters don't interrupt. A letter can't look at you with an expression you have to interpret in real time while your pulse betrays you. Everything important I've ever failed at, I failed at face to face.*

*Tell me about the person. The one who left. What were you going to say?*

*Or don't. Some sentences are better as ruins.*

*V.*

She read it over once. Her pen hovered over the last question — *what were you going to say?* — and she almost struck it. It was too naked. It was the kind of question a woman asked when she already suspected the answer and wanted to be wrong.

She sent it anyway. Dropped it in the pigeonhole marked *Match 14* on her way out, past Junie's desk, past Priya's wall of color swatches, past Marcus's whiteboard covered in the Whitmore countdown, and she told herself the strange lightness in her chest was just hunger, just low blood sugar, just Tuesday doing what Tuesdays do.

At home that night she ate standing at the counter and did not check the mail slot twice. She checked it three times. Nothing. Of course nothing — the program collected and redistributed weekly; the coordinator had explained this in the orientation email Ulyana had skimmed. Letters took days. That was the point of letters. That was the whole unbearable point of them.

She washed her one plate, dried it, put it away, and stood in her quiet, immaculate apartment — a space so controlled it looked like a showroom, a space no guest had entered in eight months — and admitted the thing she'd been not-saying since noon.

She wanted the reply.

Not curiosity. Not professional interest in a charity initiative. She wanted to sit on her floor with an envelope and find out whether a stranger named R was going to tell her what she'd spent ten years refusing to learn. She wanted to know if the sentence had been for her. She wanted, with an intensity that embarrassed her, for the answer to be yes — and she was terrified, with the same intensity, that it would be, because a yes meant ten years of certainty had been armor built over a mistake, and Ulyana Vane did not make mistakes, Ulyana Vane made *decisions*, and the entire load-bearing wall of her life was the decision she'd made in nine seconds in a hallway on a Thursday night.

Her phone buzzed. Priya: *ranunculus secured. also marcus says whitmore site visit friday 8am sharp and declan roth's firm confirmed attendance so wear the blazer that scares him.*

Ulyana stared at the message.

Declan Roth. Eight a.m. Friday. The Whitmore estate — the biggest contract of the year, the one that would fund her team's salaries for a season, the one she intended to win with the ruthless precision that had made her name.

She set the phone face-down on the counter.

Somewhere across the city, presumably, Declan Roth was also standing in a quiet apartment, also not sleeping, also carrying a folded piece of paper she didn't know existed. Or maybe he wasn't. Maybe he was fine. Maybe he'd moved on, married, forgotten, become a man who rehearsed nothing because he felt nothing worth rehearsing. She had no way of knowing. That was the price of walking out without turning around — you never got to see what the leaving did to the room behind you.

Friday, then. She would win the Whitmore bid, crush Roth & Associates — *Roth & Vane*, the ghost-name whispered through the industry like a cautionary tale — and she would file the letter under *coincidence* where it belonged, and she would not think about practice voices or river stones or doors.

She thought about all three until two in the morning.

On Wednesday, the third letter was already waiting in the pigeonhole — early, off-schedule, as if R hadn't been able to wait either. Her name wasn't on it. Just **R**, and beneath it, in that embossing script, a single line before she even opened the envelope, visible through the paper's fold:

*You asked what I was going to say.*

Ulyana's hand shook, just slightly, just once.

She took the letter back to her office, closed the door, and did not open it. Not yet. Because once she read the answer, there would be no filing it under coincidence, no unhearing it, no ten more years of telling herself the decision was correct — and some part of her, the part that had run down a hallway with a laptop charger and a hammering heart, was already reaching for the door.

She opened it anyway. She always opened things eventually. It was the closing she'd never learned.

*My dear V,*

*It was for her. It was always for her. And the worst part — the part I've never written down — is that I think she heard me rehearsing it. I think she heard the draft and mistook it for the truth, and I have spent ten years wondering how you explain to someone that the flat, careful version wasn't the real one. That the real one was bigger. That I just hadn't earned the delivery yet.*

*How do you tell someone that?*

*R.*

Ulyana sat very still in her office while, three desks away, Junie laughed at something on her phone, and Priya's music played low, and Marcus argued cheerfully with a caterer, and the whole warm machinery of her chosen family hummed on without her — and for the first time in ten years, Ulyana Vane had absolutely no idea what to write back.
