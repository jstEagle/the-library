## 9. Sabotage, Suspected

The email arrived at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, which was the first sign something was wrong. Nothing good had ever arrived at Ulyana Vane's inbox before seven.

She was still in bed, coffee untouched on the nightstand, reading — for the third time — the latest letter from R. He'd written about failure again, that decade-old one he kept circling like a tongue probing a broken tooth. *I had the words ready,* he'd written. *Ten years of ready. And I've started to wonder if the problem wasn't the words but the room I never said them in.* She'd read it twice last night and once more at dawn, and she'd caught herself doing the thing she despised in brides: composing a reply in her head while smiling at nothing.

Then the phone buzzed with Marcus's name, and the smile died of natural causes.

"Tell me you're seeing this," Marcus said, no good-morning, no preamble.

"Seeing what?"

"The Whitmore deck. Our deck. All forty-one pages of it." His voice had the flat calm he only deployed when he was furious, the way some people went quiet instead of loud. "It's posted on an industry forum. Anonymous account. Timestamped four hours ago."

Ulyana sat up so fast the coffee tipped and spread a brown stain across her copy of R's letter, ink bleeding, sentences drowning. She watched it happen with a strange detachment, as if the universe were offering her a metaphor and she was declining to pick it up.

"Our internal deck," she said. "The one with our pricing structure. Our vendor margins. The floor plan we built around Priya's arch concept."

"The one we haven't sent to anyone outside this office. Ever."

She was already out of bed, laptop open on the kitchen counter before Marcus finished talking. He was right. There it was — page after page of her work, her team's work, laid out for the entire industry to see, watermarked with nothing because they'd trusted each other enough not to watermark. The Whitmore estate wedding. The biggest contract of the year. Their bid, gutted and displayed like a specimen pinned to a board.

And beneath it, forty comments and climbing. Half were gloating speculation about which firm would undercut whom. The other half had already done the math on Roth Events' public pricing and found it suddenly, conveniently competitive.

"They're going to say we leaked it ourselves," Marcus said. "You know that, right? That's the play. Make it look desperate."

"I know the play," Ulyana said. "I invented half the plays in this industry."

* * *

By eight, the whole team was crammed into the conference room that doubled as a storage space for chair covers nobody had ever used. Priya sat with her arms crossed tight, a stem of eucalyptus still tucked behind her ear from the cooler — she'd come straight in without dropping anything, which told Ulyana everything about how bad this was. Junie had her laptop open and her jaw set in a way that made her look twelve years older and much more dangerous. Marcus stood at the whiteboard like a general who'd already lost two battles and intended to win the third.

"Timeline," Marcus said, marker squeaking. "The deck lived in three places. Our shared drive, my encrypted backup, and the PDF I sent to exactly one external address — the Whitmores' assistant, per their request, under NDA, ten days ago." He wrote a single word on the board: **ROTH**. "Their assistant's husband works in Roth Events' production department. I checked at six fifty-five this morning."

The room went still.

"That's circumstantial," Ulyana said.

"It's a thread," Marcus said. "Pull it."

"You want me to pull it publicly? Accuse Declan Roth of industrial espionage based on a marriage certificate?" She kept her voice level, professional, the voice she used when a bride's mother demanded the impossible. Inside, something colder was taking notes. "Because if we're wrong, we don't just lose the Whitmore contract. We lose every client who ever wondered if we'd do the same thing to them."

"So we pull it privately," Priya said quietly. She hadn't uncrossed her arms. "Uly. Whoever did this didn't just steal a document. They stole Junie's floor plan. They stole my arch design — the one I sketched at your kitchen table at midnight because you said, quote, 'this could be the one that makes your name.' They put all of it on a forum for free." Her voice cracked on *free*, and Ulyana felt it land somewhere below the ribs. "This wasn't business. This was a violation. And everyone in this room knows exactly whose company benefits."

Junie looked up from her laptop. "There's more," she said. "The forum account that posted it? Created three weeks ago. But the email used to register it—" she turned the screen around— "is a burner. Except whoever made it reused a recovery phone number. I ran it through two reverse-lookup services and a very questionable website Junie-the-intern should not have access to." She swallowed. "It pings back to a number registered to T. Okafor."

Ulyana knew the name before she knew why she knew it. Then she placed it: Theo Okafor. Declan Roth's junior partner. The young man she'd met twice at industry events — sharp suit, sharper ambition, the kind of hungry that reminded her uncomfortably of herself at twenty-eight.

"Junior partners don't act alone," Marcus said. "Not on something this big. Not against us. Either he's freelancing, or someone above him signed off, or someone above him is going to claim they did the moment it's convenient." He capped the marker with a decisive click. "Your call, boss. But the team wants war. All of us. Unanimous."

Three faces turned toward her. Priya's fierce and wounded. Marcus's grim and ready. Junie's — Junie's was the worst. Junie looked like someone who'd just learned Santa wasn't real, except the Santa in question was the entire premise that people who planned weddings for a living might be decent.

Ulyana thought about saying *let's be careful*. She thought about due process, about calling Declan first, about the professional courtesy between firms that had kept this industry civil since before either of them held a license.

Then she looked at the stained letter on her desk through the glass wall of her office — R's handwriting drowned in coffee, his careful sentences blurred beyond saving — and she thought about how she'd woken up this morning actually happy, actually soft, actually entertaining the idiotic idea that a stranger with initials could know her better than anyone alive.

The world had apparently decided to correct that error immediately.

"Here's what we're not doing," she said. "We're not posting rebuttals. We're not feeding the forum. We're not giving the trade press a quote, because quotes become stories and stories become identities." She stood. "What we are doing is rebuilding the Whitmore presentation from scratch — new concepts, new numbers, better than what leaked, so even if they stole our homework, they copied the wrong draft. Priya, I want a new centerpiece concept by Friday. Something the old deck doesn't contain. Marcus, get me a forensic IT person, someone discreet. Junie—"

"Yes?"

"Print everything. Screenshots, timestamps, the registration trail. If this goes where I think it goes, we'll need it."

"And Roth?" Marcus asked. "Do we confront them?"

Ulyana paused at the door. Ten years ago she'd have said yes without hesitating. Five years ago she'd have said no, out of caution. This morning, with R's ruined letter sitting on her desk and Declan Roth's face unaccountably present behind her eyes — the way he'd looked at the site visit, tired and human in a way that had annoyed her precisely because it worked — she heard herself give the answer her team needed, not the one her curiosity wanted.

"Not yet," she said. "When we go at Roth, we go with proof, not suspicion. But understand me." She looked at each of them in turn. "If it's true — if he did this, if he took your work and put it on a board for the world — then there is no truce, no history, no nothing. We end him. Professionally. Thoroughly. Permanently."

Junie nodded slowly. Priya's arms finally came uncrossed. Marcus almost smiled.

"War it is," he said. "Pending evidence."

"Pending evidence," Ulyana agreed, and walked into her office, and closed the door, and stood with her forehead against the cool glass until her reflection stopped looking like someone else.

* * *

The reply to R sat unsent in her drafts folder all day, which was unlike her. She answered his letters within hours, always had — spitefully fast at first, then quickly for reasons she'd declined to examine. Now she stared at the cursor blinking after the sentence she'd written at dawn, before the leak, back when she'd been a woman with a normal morning:

*I keep thinking about the room you never said them in. Was the door locked, or were you?*

She'd meant it lightly. Now it read like an accusation aimed at someone else entirely — at a man whose junior partner had possibly gutted her team's work overnight, at a man she'd sworn off a decade ago and was apparently still capable of swearing off again on schedule.

She deleted the line. Typed it again. Deleted it.

Finally, near midnight, she wrote:

*Something happened today that reminded me why I stopped believing in partnerships. Someone I once trusted the shape of — not the person, just the shape, the outline of a man I thought I knew — may have done something unforgivable. I don't have proof yet. What I have is a team of people who stayed late tonight rebuilding something a stranger tried to destroy, eating cold pizza and arguing about flower arches like the fate of the world depended on it. Maybe it does. Here's what I've decided: loyalty isn't believing the best about people. It's standing in front of your people when the worst arrives, before you know anything at all. I used to think that made me weak. Tonight it made me certain.*

*Tell me about your failure. The real version. Did you betray someone, or did you just fail to save them? There's a difference, and I've spent ten years pretending there isn't.*

She hit send before she could soften it, then sat in the dark of her office listening to the building hum, telling herself the tightness in her chest was anger.

It mostly was.

* * *

The proof began arriving Thursday, and it was worse than suspicion.

Marcus's forensic consultant traced the forum post's upload location to a co-working space four blocks from Roth Events' office. Junie, operating with a terrifying aptitude for digital sleuthing that Ulyana resolved to both cultivate and monitor, found that Theo Okafor had attended — publicly, on LinkedIn, timestamped — a Whitmore family foundation event three weeks ago, working the room in a way the event photos made unmistakable. And the final piece landed Friday afternoon: a former Roth Events coordinator, now freelance, called Marcus out of the blue because she'd heard rumors and "wanted no part of whatever's coming."

"He asked me about the Whitmore bid in September," she said, voice low over speakerphone while the whole team leaned in. "Theo Okafor. Wanted to know who handled their security, who their assistant trusted, whether the family was loyal to Vane or shopping. I told him I didn't know and hung up. I should have called you then. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," Ulyana said. "Be willing to say it again. To the Whitmores. In writing."

After she hung up, the conference room was silent for a long moment.

"That's it," Marcus said. "That's the chain. Access, opportunity, motive, inquiry. It's him, Uly. And him means Roth."

"We don't know it reaches Roth," Ulyana said, but her voice came out thinner than she wanted. Because here was the thing she couldn't say out loud: some part of her had been waiting all week for the evidence to exonerate him. Some part of her — the part that read letters by coffee-light at dawn, the part that had laughed alone in her car at R's joke about cake tastings being "speed-dating with frosting" — needed Declan Roth to be innocent, and she hated that part with a clean, clarifying fury.

"Why are you defending him?" Priya asked. Not cruelly. Worse — gently. "You hated him before any of us knew his name. You've got ten years of reasons. Why is now the week you get fair?"

Because he writes me letters, Ulyana did not say. Because some anonymous man with an R for a signature has been dismantling my armor one envelope at a time, and I'm apparently incapable of distinguishing him from the enemy. Because I am, at forty-one, having feelings in two directions at once and it's making me stupid.

"Because fairness wins cases," she said instead. "And because when we take Roth down, it will be because he earned it. Not because we were angry."

But that night, alone in her office, she pulled the file together herself — screenshots, timelines, the coordinator's statement — and laid it out across her desk like a hand of losing cards. And she understood, with the clarity that only comes at the edge of a decision, that the question was no longer *did Declan Roth do this.*

The question was what she would do to him if he had. And the answer frightened her less than it should have, because the armor was back on, buckled tight, and underneath it the woman who wrote honest letters at midnight had gone very quiet.

Her inbox chimed. R. A reply, already — he was fast lately, faster than her, as if he too had something burning a hole in his pocket.

She opened it with hands she pretended were steady.

*V. You asked if I betrayed someone or failed to save them. God. The honest answer is I don't know, and not knowing has been its own punishment. Here's what I'll tell you: the worst thing I ever did wasn't the failure itself. It was staying silent afterward, when one sentence — one — might have changed everything. I told myself silence was protection. Hers, mine, everyone's. It was cowardice wearing a nice coat. Don't let anyone tell you silence is loyalty. Loyalty says the true thing out loud, at cost, especially when it costs.*

Ulyana read it three times. Then she looked at the evidence folder on her desk, at the word **ROTH** still ghosted onto the whiteboard through the glass, and felt the two halves of her week grind against each other like tectonic plates.

One of these men was lying to her. She just couldn't determine which one.

She reached for a fresh sheet of paper — real paper, because some answers deserved weight — and began the letter that would decide everything, addressed to the one man in her life who had never once made her feel like a fool.

Whoever he was.
