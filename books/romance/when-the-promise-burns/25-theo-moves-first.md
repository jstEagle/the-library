## 25. Theo Moves First

Declan Roth learned about the story the way he learned about most disasters in his life — from someone else's phone.

He was standing in the break room of his own office at 7:40 in the morning, waiting for the kettle, when his junior event coordinator, a sweet-faced kid named Dana who still said "Mr. Roth" like it was an honor instead of a burden, came around the corner with her eyes wide and her phone held out like evidence at a trial.

"Have you seen this?"

The headline on the trade site's morning blast was clean, professional, and vicious:

**VANE & CO. UNDER FIRE: WHISTLEBLOWER ALLEGES STOLEN CLIENT DATA AT TOP WEDDING FIRM**

Below it, a photograph of Ulyana taken at some gala two years ago, caught mid-laugh in a way that made her look, in this context, like a woman laughing at other people's misfortune. The article was three paragraphs long and every one of them was a knife wrapped in journalism. *An anonymous source close to the Whitmore estate bidding process alleges that Vane & Co. obtained proprietary vendor pricing and client preference documents belonging to a competing firm.* *The Whitmores have declined to comment.* *Vane & Co. did not respond to requests for comment before publication.*

Declan read it twice. The kettle screamed behind him and he didn't move.

He knew the shape of the lie because he had watched Theo build it. He had sat across from Theo eleven days ago in a glass-walled conference room while Theo slid a folder across the table and laid out exactly what would happen if Declan didn't sign the withdrawal papers — the fabricated paper trail, the "anonymous source," the trade press contacts Theo had been cultivating for two years like a man tending a garden he planned to burn.

"You leak this," Declan had said, "and you don't just hurt her firm. You hurt forty people who had nothing to do with any of it."

"That's the point," Theo had said, with the mild patience of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "You can protect your reputation or you can protect hers. You can't do both. Sign."

Declan hadn't signed. He'd told himself there was time. That he'd find another way — go to the press himself first, torch Theo's credibility, eat whatever fallout came. He'd spent the last week and a half building that plan in the margins of his day, between site visits and vendor calls, telling himself *tomorrow, tomorrow, tomorrow.*

Theo had not waited for tomorrow.

The kettle was still screaming. Dana reached past him and turned off the burner, and the sudden silence in the break room was so complete Declan could hear the HVAC ticking and his own pulse in his ears.

"Mr. Roth? Is it true?"

"No." His voice came out flat and certain, and he was grateful for that much. "It is not true. And by end of day, everyone is going to know it isn't."

Dana looked at him for a long moment — she was twenty-four and sharper than her resume suggested — and then nodded slowly. "Okay. What do you need us to do?"

That was the question, wasn't it. What did he need them to do. What could forty people do against a story that was already in inboxes, already screenshot, already metastasizing through the industry's group chats where reputations were decided before lunch.

"I need you to work," he said. "And I need you to let me handle the rest."

* * *

His office at nine a.m. looked like a war room if the general had already lost.

Declan had his laptop open to the article, his phone on speaker with his attorney, and the folder Theo had given him spread across the desk like an autopsy. His sister Nora had let herself in without knocking — she did that, always had, since they were kids sharing a bathroom — and stood in the doorway with her coffee and her arms crossed, reading the room before she read the screen.

"How bad?" she asked.

"Theo went public."

Nora set her coffee down. In thirty seconds she'd read the article, and Declan watched her face do what their mother's face used to do when the news was worse than expected — nothing, absolutely nothing, which meant everything.

"He threatened you with this," she said. Not a question. She'd known about the ultimatum; he'd told her the night it happened, pacing his kitchen at midnight while she ate his ice cream straight from the carton. "You didn't sign, and he did it anyway."

"He didn't need my signature. He needed me to believe he'd use it."

"And now?"

"Now I stop protecting a company that doesn't deserve it." Declan pulled up the email thread with the trade outlet's editor — a woman named Camille Okafor whom he'd known for fifteen years, back to the days when they were both nobodies covering hotel ballroom openings. He'd emailed her at 7:55, four words: *Call me. It's false.* She'd called back at 8:10 and listened to him for six minutes without interrupting, and at the end she'd said, "Declan, I have to tell you, my source gave us documents. Real ones. Forged, maybe, but real paper. If you're telling me this is fabrication, I need more than your word."

"You'll have it by noon."

"Then I'd hurry. There's a second piece queued for tomorrow. Someone's feeding us a follow-up."

Someone. As if Theo were a stranger. As if Declan hadn't hired him eight years ago out of a second-tier firm because the kid was hungry and brilliant and reminded Declan, God help him, of himself.

Nora pulled a chair around backwards and sat on it the way she'd sat through every crisis of their shared childhood, facing him, forearms on the backrest. "Walk me through it. What does Theo actually have?"

"A forged procurement trail. Fake emails between a Vane coordinator and one of our vendors — timestamped during the bid window. A 'whistleblower' statement that reads like it was written by someone who's never worked at Vane & Co. but has read about them." Declan rubbed his eyes. "It's good enough to run. It's not good enough to survive scrutiny. But scrutiny takes weeks, and the Whitmores are deciding in days."

"The Whitmores." Nora's voice went careful. "What are they saying?"

As if summoned, his phone lit up on the desk. **Eleanor Whitmore**.

Declan looked at it the way a man looks at a diagnosis. Then he answered on speaker, because Nora needed to hear it and because he was tired of carrying things alone.

"Declan." Eleanor Whitmore's voice was the voice of a woman who signed seven-figure contracts and un-signed them just as easily. "I assume you've seen the morning's news."

"I have, Mrs. Whitmore. And I can tell you—"

"What you can tell me," she cut in, smooth as poured cream, "is whether my daughter's wedding is about to become a tabloid story. Because that is the only question I have. Charles and I chose your firm and we considered Ms. Vane's, and now one of you is accused of stealing from the other, and I will not have my family's name dragged through either of your messes. Do you understand me?"

"Perfectly."

"If this isn't resolved — publicly, cleanly, within seventy-two hours — we're canceling with both firms and going to Charleston. My sister knows a planner there." A pause, delicate as a blade. "I'd hoped for better from you, Declan. You struck me as a man who kept his house in order."

The line went dead before he could answer.

Nora exhaled slowly. "Seventy-two hours."

"Seventy-two hours." Declan stared at the folder. At the forged emails with his own company's letterhead on them, deployed against a woman whose name he'd been writing into letters signed only *R*. Whose handwriting he knew better now than he'd known it ten years ago, from a hundred pages of her anonymous soul. Who had stood in his office five days ago and apologized to him — *apologized*, Ulyana Vane, apologizing, the eighth wonder of the world — and walked out of his office more hurt than she'd come in, because he'd been too busy protecting her from Theo's threat to tell her the truth.

She thought he believed she was a thief. She thought the apology changed nothing. And now the whole industry thought it too, and her team — Priya with her flower farm loan, Marcus with his fresh sobriety chip and his rebuilt career, Junie, who was twenty-two and believed in everything — all of them had mortgages and payrolls and futures tied to a firm that was bleeding out in the press before ten a.m.

Declan picked up the folder and held it for a moment, feeling its weight, which was nothing, which was everything.

"Nora," he said. "Get me everything we have on Theo. Every email, every expense report, every access log. And call Camille Okafor back. Tell her the source is my junior partner, and I'll prove it on the record."

His sister studied him. "You know what that means. Even if it clears Vane, our name is in every headline. 'Roth Events executive leaks fabricated story.' We're done. Ten years, Declan. Everything you built."

He thought about an empty house in Georgetown with four rooms he never used. About a folded letter in a desk drawer that had survived three moves. About a sentence he'd rehearsed for a decade and still hadn't managed to say out loud to the only person it belonged to.

"Yeah," he said. "I know what it means."

* * *

By noon, the story had grown teeth.

A second outlet picked it up — smaller, hungrier, less careful — and added details Declan recognized from Theo's folder with a sickening clarity: the specific vendor, the specific dates, the specific claim that a "senior Vane employee" had photographed confidential pricing pages. Whoever wrote it had been handed a script. The comments sections were doing what comments sections do, which is execute people without trial. *This tracks, honestly. Vane's always been ruthless.* *Where there's smoke.* *Poor Roth, getting robbed by his ex-partner, that's cold.*

His ex-partner. Ten years later and the industry still introduced them that way, a matched set, a cautionary tale. Declan read the phrase four times before he realized his jaw ached from clenching.

At 12:20, Marcus Cole called him directly. That surprised him — Marcus was Ulyana's lead coordinator, a big quiet man who'd once told Declan at a conference, over terrible buffet chicken, that he'd left corporate event planning because he "got tired of lying for a living." They weren't friends. They were barely friendly. But Marcus's voice on the line was stripped down to wire and bone.

"I'm not calling to yell at you," Marcus said. "Though I want to. Priya wants to. Honestly, Junie wants to and she doesn't even fully understand what's happening."

"Marcus—"

"Let me finish. I'm calling because Ulyana won't ask anyone for help, which means nobody's asking the obvious question, so I will. Did you do this?"

"No."

Silence on the line. Declan could hear Marcus breathing, could hear the particular quality of a man deciding whether to believe something he desperately wanted to be false.

"Here's the thing," Marcus said finally. "I've watched you two circle each other for a decade. I've watched her sharpen herself on you like you're a whetstone. And I know what a guilty man sounds like on the phone, because I used to be one. You don't sound guilty. You sound—" he paused, searching, "—wrecked. Which is different."

"Marcus, I swear to you, I found out this morning like everyone else. And I'm going to fix it. Today. Publicly."

"Don't fix it for her." Marcus's voice hardened. "Fix it because it's right. Because if you fix it for her, she'll owe you, and Ulyana Vane would rather burn the whole building down than owe anybody anything. You know that better than anyone."

The line went dead, and Declan sat with the truth of it settling over him like snowfall. Marcus was right. Every rescue Declan staged from here on would read as strategy, as leverage, as the final move in a decade-long game. The only currency he had left that couldn't be discounted was the truth itself, spent recklessly, at his own expense.

He opened his laptop and began to write.

* * *

The statement took him two hours and four drafts, because the truth, it turned out, was harder to write than a lie. A lie has structure. A lie knows where it's going. The truth had to be built by hand, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall, and every wall was a confession.

*To the editors, colleagues, and clients of the wedding and events industry:*

*This morning, a story circulated alleging that Vane & Co. obtained confidential materials from Roth Events during the Whitmore estate bidding process. I am the founder of Roth Events, and I am telling you plainly: the allegation is false. The documents cited are fabrications. The anonymous source does not exist.*

*I know this because I have identified the person who created them. He is a partner at my firm. He acted without my knowledge or consent, and he did so, in part, to pressure me in a separate business dispute. I will be providing full documentation to the press and to Vane & Co.'s legal counsel today.*

*I am not issuing this statement to defend a competitor. I am issuing it because a lie was told about forty people who had nothing to do with it, and silence made me complicit in it. Whatever damage this does to my own firm, I accept it. Some things cost more to keep than to lose.*

*Declan Roth*

Nora read it over his shoulder and was quiet for a long moment. "Some things cost more to keep than to lose," she repeated. "That's the line that's going to get quoted."

"Good."

"You understand that when this runs, Theo's lawyers are going to come after you. He'll claim defamation. He'll claim you're scapegoating him to cover your own involvement. He might even produce those forged emails with your systems' metadata attached, because he had admin access, Declan. He built the trail inside our own walls."

"I know."

"And you understand that Ulyana is going to read this and know that you knew. That you knew who did it, and when, and why — and that you sat on it for eleven days while she apologized to *you*." Nora's voice cracked slightly on the last word, and he realized his sister was angry at him, properly angry, the way only family can be. "She is going to be so hurt, Declan. Not by Theo. By you."

He closed his eyes. On the desk, half-hidden under the folder of evidence, lay a sheet of stationery — cream, heavy, the kind the pen-pal program provided — covered in a draft he'd written three nights ago and never sent. *Dear V,* it began. *There's something I should have said ten years ago, and something I should say now, and I'm afraid they're the same thing.*

"Yes," he said. "I understand that."

"Then why wait? Send the statement and call her. Tell her everything — Theo, the ultimatum, all of it. Let her hear it from you before she reads it."

"Because if I call her now, before it publishes, she'll think I'm warning her as a courtesy between rivals. She'll think it's positioning." He opened his eyes. "But if she reads it cold — if the whole industry reads it cold, my name on it, my firm burning with it — then there's no version where it's strategy. It can only be the truth. It's the only gift I can give her that she can't refuse."

Nora looked at him for a long time, and something in her face softened into an old grief. "You rehearsed that answer," she said quietly. "Didn't you. You've been rehearsing it for days."

"I rehearse everything," Declan said. "It's the only thing I've ever been sorry for."

He hit send at 3:47 p.m.

* * *

The statement ran at 4:15. By 4:30, the industry's group chats had detonated. By 5:00, Camille Okafor had published a follow-up headlined **ROTH BLOWS WHISTLE ON OWN FIRM: "SOME THINGS COST MORE TO KEEP THAN TO LOSE"**, and the narrative flipped with the speed of weather — *Roth sacrifices his company to clear his rival*, *the decade-long feud ends in sacrifice*, *what really happened between Roth and Vane?* — and Declan watched his inbox fill with messages from vendors, clients, and old friends, some supportive, some bewildered, a few already angling for his client list like gulls following a sinking ship.

He felt nothing. That was the strange part. He'd expected relief, or terror, or grief, and instead there was just a vast quiet, like the air after a storm front passes. He'd burned his own ship. The water was cold and he was swimming, and the only direction left was toward her.

At 5:22, his phone rang. **Ulyana Vane**.

He stared at the screen for three full rings, heart hammering like a kid's, and answered on the fourth because answering on the first would have told her everything.

"Declan." Her voice was controlled, which he knew meant she was holding herself together with both hands. "I just read your statement."

"Ulyana—"

"Why?" One word, and it carried eleven days of hurt behind it. "You knew. You knew what he did, you knew it was coming, and you let me sit in that conference room Monday and apologize to you for something I didn't do. You let me walk out of your office believing you thought I was a thief. Why?"

Because I was trying to protect you. Because Theo promised to destroy you either way. Because I thought I could fix it quietly. Because every protective instinct I have curdles into control and I've never learned the difference.

"Because I was a coward," he said. "In the only way that mattered."

The silence stretched so long he checked whether the call had dropped.

"There's more," she said finally, and her voice had shifted — lower, rougher, the voice from the letters, the voice that wrote about running. "Eleanor Whitmore called me twenty minutes ago. She saw your statement. She wants both firms in her office tomorrow morning, together, to hear how we propose to save her daughter's wedding. Joint presentation. Both companies. One contract."

Declan closed his eyes. Of course. Eleanor Whitmore missed nothing and wasted nothing, and a scandal between two rivals was, to a woman like her, simply a dramatic third act awaiting resolution.

"She's testing us," he said.

"She's entertaining herself," Ulyana corrected, and despite everything — despite the wreckage, the headlines, the eleven days — he heard it, faint and unmistakable: the ghost of a laugh. "Seven a.m., Declan. Don't be late. And Declan—"

"Yes?"

A pause. He could hear her deciding something, weighing it, the way she weighed everything.

"Thank you," she said, very fast, like a door closing. "For the statement. We'll talk about the rest later."

The line went dead, and Declan sat alone in his office as the light went gold and then gray outside the window, surrounded by the ruins of the company he'd built instead of a life, holding a phone that still felt warm, thinking: *she said thank you. She said we'll talk about the rest.*

On the corner of his desk, the unsent letter waited under the lamp's small circle of light. *Dear V. There's something I should have said ten years ago.*

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow, after the Whitmores. Tomorrow, he'd tell her everything — Theo, the drawer, the sentence, all of it.

He had already waited ten years. What was one more day?

Outside, the first drops of rain began to hit the glass.
