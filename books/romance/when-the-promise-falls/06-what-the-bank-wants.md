## 6. What the Bank Wants

The letter came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Bad news should at least have the decency to arrive on a Friday, when you could drink through the weekend before facing it.

Ivo Montrose stood in his father's study with the envelope still sealed in his hand, watching dust turn in the light from the window. He'd known what it was before he opened it. The bank's stationery had a weight to it, a cream-colored confidence that said *we have already decided and are merely informing you*. He'd been getting versions of this letter for three years—polite reminders, firmer reminders, a phone call from a man named Whitfield whose voice was engineered to sound sympathetic while saying unsympathetic things. This one was different. He could tell by the thickness of it. Thin envelopes were routine. Thick ones were decisions.

He sat down in his father's chair, which he still did sometimes even though the seat had molded itself to a dead man's posture, and slit the envelope with the bone-handled letter opener that had been in the family since before anyone could remember why bone was better than steel.

*Dear Mr. Montrose.*

Nobody at the bank called him Ivo. That was telling too. You call someone by their first name when you're on their side.

*Following our review of the outstanding balance on the Montrose Vineyard operating line and mortgage facility, we are writing to inform you of the following:*

There followed four paragraphs of language so carefully constructed it might as well have been written in Latin. Restructuring options exhausted. Payment history inconsistent. Collateral valuation under review. And then, buried in the third paragraph like a stone in soft bread, the actual sentence:

*In light of the foregoing, we require demonstration of operational viability prior to the annual Harvest Festival, failing which the institution will be compelled to initiate formal proceedings.*

Ivo read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting each word land where it wanted to land.

They'd given him until the festival. Six weeks. The festival—the same festival his father had helped found, the one where Montrose wine had taken the top prize eleven times, back when there was wine worth judging. The bank wasn't just calling his loan. They were making him perform for it. Prove the vineyard could live, in public, on a stage, or they would take it apart row by row.

He set the letter down on the desk, next to the shoebox of letters he still hadn't put away, and laughed once—a short, ugly sound with nothing in it.

"Operational viability," he said to the empty room. "Sure."

Outside the window, the vineyard lay in the late-morning light like something convalescent. The east block was dead—he'd stopped pretending otherwise in June—and the middle rows were hanging on out of spite more than health. Only the south slope, the old Chardonnay his grandfather had planted, looked like anything a person might call a vineyard. Forty years of roots in good limestone. If the Montrose name meant anything anymore, it meant those rows.

Six weeks to make forty acres look like a future.

* * *

Whitfield met him at the branch office in town on Thursday, in a conference room with a view of the feed store and a table polished to the point of dishonesty. Ivo had worn his good jacket, the one without vineyard dust ground into the elbows, and he felt like a kid in a borrowed coat. Whitfield wore a suit the color of oatmeal and a tie knotted with geometric precision, and he smiled the way men smile when they've rehearsed compassion in a mirror.

"Ivo. Thanks for coming in." A handshake, firm but brief, the handshake of a man who doesn't want to be touched longer than necessary. "Can I get you coffee?"

"I'm fine."

"Water? We've got—"

"I'm fine, Mr. Whitfield. Let's not do the part where we pretend this is a social call."

Something flickered behind Whitfield's eyes—not offense, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who appreciated efficiency even when it was aimed at him. He sat, opened a leather folder, squared its contents against the table edge twice, and folded his hands.

"All right." He said it gently, which was worse than saying it coldly. "You've read the letter."

"I read it."

"Then you know where we stand. But I want to say plainly, because I think you deserve plainness." He leaned forward an inch. "The bank isn't your enemy here, Ivo. Your father was a fixture in this community. My mother bought Montrose Chardonnay every Christmas for twenty years. Nobody in this building wants to see that sign come down."

"But."

"But." Whitfield spread his hands, a gesture of magnificent helplessness. "The numbers are the numbers. You're carrying debt service on an operation that hasn't turned a profit in four seasons. The east block is written off—we drove past it, you can see it from the road, and believe me, appraisers see it too. Your equipment is aging past the point where it's collateral. And the operating line has been extended twice on goodwill alone."

"Goodwill." Ivo tasted the word. "Is that what we're calling it."

"It's what I'm calling it, yes." No flinch. Give him that. "Because the alternative word is *sentiment*, and sentiment is not something I can take upstairs. What I can take upstairs is a plan. Viable, documented, with numbers attached. That's what the letter means. It's not a threat, Ivo. It's a deadline."

"A deadline for a miracle."

"A deadline for a harvest." Whitfield pulled a single sheet from the folder and turned it around. "The festival matters because the festival matters to the cooperative lenders, the buyers, the press. If you walk into that tent with a bottle that makes people sit up—if you can show standing orders, show the south slope producing, show a path—there are people in this room's orbit who will fight for you. I'm one of them, if you make it possible for me to be."

Ivo looked at the sheet. It was a checklist, essentially. Production figures. Sales commitments. A projected budget that assumed money he didn't have doing things he couldn't afford. It was a map of a country that didn't exist.

"And if I don't."

Whitfield closed the folder. For the first time, he looked tired. "Then after the festival, the file goes to proceedings, and you know what happens after that. Appraisal. Auction. I won't dress it up. There's already interest in the property."

That landed somewhere under Ivo's ribs and stayed there. "Interest. From who?"

"I'm not at liberty—"

"Mr. Whitfield. I've got nothing left to leverage except my ability to sit here all afternoon."

Whitfield held his gaze for a moment, then glanced toward the door, an old habit of discretion. "A development group. They've been acquiring parcels along the valley road for two years. Quiet about it. They approached us last month about a package acquisition—your parcel and the adjacent one."

"The Okonkwo place."

"We're not discussing the adjacent property." Which was, of course, a confirmation. "My advice, and it's freely given: whatever happens between now and the festival, don't let them be the only ones with a plan for your land. Because right now, Ivo, they're the only ones bidding."

* * *

He drove home with the windows down even though it was cold, because the truck cab smelled like the letter—paper and cream and other people's decisions—and he needed wind in his face to blow it out.

The valley road unspooled ahead of him, gold fields running down to the creek, and he made himself look at it the way the developer would look at it. Not as home. As acreage. Flat buildable ground near water, mature trees for landscaping, a winery building with charm that could be photographed for brochures. He could see it the way they saw it, and that was the worst part. He could see exactly why they wanted it, and he could see how little of it they'd keep.

He passed the Okonkwo farmhouse and slowed without meaning to. There was a truck in the drive now. Had been for two weeks. Boxes going in, a woman carrying them, her hair tied back, moving with the particular stubborn rhythm of someone unloading a life by hand. He'd watched her from behind the glass of the front door more times than he'd admit to himself, working up to a hello he hadn't managed yet. A Montrose walking over to the Okonkwo fence line—that was a sentence with sixty years of history coiled inside it, and he hadn't found the courage to say any version of it.

Now the bank was telling him the developer wanted both properties in one package. Her farm and his, bundled. Sold together. Erased together.

He pulled into his own drive and sat in the idling truck, staring at the vineyard.

Six weeks. He ran the math the way he'd been running it for months, the way that always ended in the same red number. The south slope would yield—maybe. A small vintage, but if the fruit was clean and he didn't botch the fermentation, he could bring maybe three hundred cases to the festival. Three hundred cases wasn't a business. Three hundred cases was a hobby with delusions. To show viability he'd need orders, commitments, a buyer willing to sign before tasting—and buyers didn't sign before tasting unless the name carried weight, and the name carried weight only if—

If there was a miracle. That was the plan. His entire operational strategy, six weeks out from losing everything his grandfather had planted and his father had bled for, was to hope for a miracle.

His father would have had a plan. Edmund Montrose had plans the way other men had heartbeats—involuntary, constant, occasionally painful. Edmund would have been on the phone tonight, working angles, calling in favors, arm-twisting some distributor over dinner. Edmund had also mortgaged the place twice and died owing everyone including, in some spiritual sense, the weather. Ivo loved him and was furious at him in roughly equal measure, and neither feeling brought back a dead man's Rolodex.

What Ivo had instead was a south slope, a cellar full of potential, and ten years of unanswered letters in a shoebox.

He went inside, washed his hands, and stood in the kitchen eating cold rice out of the pot because cooking felt like a lie you told yourself about the future. Through the window he could see the fence line, and beyond it the farmhouse lights coming on, warm against the blue dusk. The woman moved past a window carrying something. A lamp, maybe. Making a house livable in a house that had been dead as long as his vines.

The developer wants her place too, he thought. She probably doesn't know. She's over there unpacking dishes and she has no idea there are men in oatmeal suits who've already drawn lines around her ground.

It wasn't his problem. That was the honest accounting. He had enough ruin of his own to manage; charity across a fence line was a luxury for people whose own houses weren't on fire. His father would have said exactly that—had said versions of it, in harder words, for years. *We look after our own, Ivo. We don't owe that family anything.* And the thing about inheriting a farm was that you inherited the debts and the grudges in the same crate, and nobody gave you a receipt for either.

But he kept looking at the lights next door.

* * *

That night he took the shoebox down from the study shelf and sat with it on his knees, not opening it, just holding it. Ten Octobers of unsigned letters, addressed simply to *the house*, arriving like clockwork, asking nothing, signed with no name. And beneath them, in his own handwriting, the replies he'd never sent—written at nineteen, twenty, twenty-two, at the kitchen table after midnight, answering a stranger's loneliness with his own because it was easier than answering his father's expectations. He'd never mailed a single one. He'd never even understood why he wrote them, except that whoever wrote those October letters knew the valley, knew the orchard, knew the dog that used to sleep under the porch, and writing back had felt less like correspondence and more like remembering.

He hadn't opened the box since the day he'd found it. Tonight he lifted the lid anyway, because the bank letter had scraped something raw in him, and raw was apparently the condition under which he made his worst and truest decisions.

The top letter was hers—or theirs, the stranger's—dated last October. *Dear House,* it began. *Another year. Another autumn I don't come back. I heard the east block finally died. I'm sorry. Whoever you are now, I hope you're sleeping.*

Whoever you are now. She didn't know Edmund was dead. She didn't know the son who'd read her letters in secret was the only one left. She was writing to a ghost, and he was the ghost, and the ghost had never once answered.

He thought about Whitfield's checklist. Production, orders, viability, a plan with numbers attached. He thought about the developer buying parcels along the valley road, patient as water. He thought about the woman next door, whose name he still didn't know, carrying boxes into a house the bank's client had already circled on a map.

And a thought arrived—not fully formed, more like weather changing—that the answer to his problem might be standing thirty yards away behind a broken fence, and that the history between their families, the history he'd spent his whole life treating as a wall, might be the only asset he had left that the bank hadn't figured out how to value.

He put the lid back on the box.

Six weeks, he told the dark kitchen. Fine. He'd grown up on these rows; he knew what stubborn looked like. He'd make the south slope sing if it killed him, and if the miracle didn't come to him, he'd go find it—even if it lived next door, behind a fence his father built high on purpose.

Outside, the lights in the farmhouse went out, one window at a time, like a slow blink. Somewhere over the hills, the first real cold of the season was gathering itself, though he didn't know that yet.

He slept badly, and dreamed of auction paddles.
