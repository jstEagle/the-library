## 8. Borrowed Ladders

The knock came at seven in the morning, which was the first suspicious thing about it. Nobody knocked on Ivo Montrose's door at seven in the morning unless they wanted something, and nobody wanted anything from him except money he didn't have.

He opened the door with a coffee mug still in his hand and found Sena Okonkwo standing on his porch steps with her arms crossed against the October chill, wearing work gloves and an expression that suggested she'd rehearsed this and hated every draft.

"I need to borrow your ladders," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"It's not a good morning. It's a morning." She nodded past him into the house, as if she could see through walls to the equipment shed. "Your father had three orchard ladders. The tall ones, the wooden ones with the flat tops. I saw them through your shed window when I was walking the fence line."

"You've been looking in my shed?"

"I've been walking my property line, which happens to run past your shed. There's a difference, though I understand how a Montrose might struggle with it."

Ivo took a slow sip of coffee, mostly to buy time, partly to be irritating. "What do you need ladders for? Your yard's been fallow for ten years. There's nothing up high worth reaching."

"There's nothing *down low* worth keeping either. That's the problem." She pulled one glove off and flexed her fingers. "The blackberries have taken the whole south hedgerow. There's a mulberry tree back there that hasn't been pruned since my grandmother died, and if I don't cut it now, before it wakes up properly next spring, it'll split its crown in the first ice storm. And there are—" she hesitated, and something flickered across her face that looked almost like embarrassment— "there are four old vines behind the barn. My grandfather planted them. They're buried under everything, but they're alive. I checked."

"Four vines."

"They're Muscat. Old stock. You can't buy that anymore around here."

Ivo leaned against the doorframe. The morning light came off the hills behind her and caught the gray in her hair—she couldn't have been more than thirty-five, but the last decade had put silver at her temples like frost that never quite melted. He noticed things like that now. He'd been noticing too much about her since the fence-line argument two weeks ago, cataloging details the way you catalog details about weather systems moving toward your house.

"So borrow the ladders," he said. "Take them. Bring them back whenever."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again, visibly annoyed with herself. "I can't carry them alone. They're twelve feet. And I don't own a truck anymore, and the rental place in town wants forty dollars for a half day, which is robbery, and I'm not asking you to help. I'm asking you to lend me the ladders and let me figure out the rest."

"But you'd rather I helped."

"I'd rather I didn't need anything from you at all," she said flatly. "But here we are."

*Here we are.* He heard the echo of his own thought from the bank meeting, from the debt notices, from every morning of the last year. Here we are, standing in the wreckage of other people's decisions, being asked to make our own.

"The shed's unlocked," he said. "Ladders are on the left wall."

She turned to go, then paused on the bottom step. "There's a second part."

Of course there was. "Let's hear it."

"If you help me clear the hedgerow today, I'll show you something about your Pinot rows. Something wrong with them."

"Nothing's wrong with my Pinot rows."

"No," she agreed. "Nothing's wrong with them, which is exactly what's wrong with them. A vine that isn't struggling isn't working. Your grandfather knew that. Your father forgot it, and so did you." She pulled the glove back on. "Seven o'clock tomorrow. Or don't. It's your vineyard dying, not mine."

She walked down his driveway without waiting for an answer, which was, he was learning, her signature move. She made offers shaped like insults and left before you could decide whether to be offended or intrigued. By the time he'd settled on both, she was already through the gap in the fence line and gone.

He stood on the porch holding his cooling coffee and understood, with the particular dread of a man who has just lost an argument he hadn't known was happening, that he would be helping her clear the hedgerow.

Not because of the Pinot. Because of the four vines behind her barn. Because someone had kept them alive for ten years in a yard full of blackberry thorns, and he wanted to know what kind of person did that.

* * *

The Okonkwo farmhouse looked worse up close than it did from his kitchen window, and it had looked bad from the kitchen window. The paint had gone the color of driftwood. Half the porch railing was missing, and the other half was more suggestion than structure. But the yard itself—the yard was a war zone. Blackberry canes had overrun the south hedgerow in a green tidal wave, some of them eight feet tall, arched and rooted where their tips touched ground, building new plants out of themselves in an endless colonial expansion. Beneath them Ivo could see the skeletons of an old vegetable garden, a collapsed cold frame, and what might once have been a rosebush, now a thorned monument to futility.

Sena was already at work when he arrived with the ladders over his shoulder and a pair of loppers he'd sharpened the night before, telling himself it was just good practice to keep tools sharp, nothing to do with her.

"You brought the big loppers," she said, eyeing them.

"I brought appropriate tools."

"You brought tools for a man who's never fought blackberries." She held out her hand. "Give them here. You take the sickle and start on the low growth. Cut at the base, don't hack at the middle—all you do hacking the middle is make the plant angry."

"The plant gets angry."

"All plants get angry. Blackberries hold grudges." She took the loppers and moved into the thicket like she was entering a room she knew well, finding paths Ivo couldn't see, cutting with short economical strokes. Within minutes she'd opened a corridor six feet deep. Within twenty, she'd reached the mulberry tree, and he heard her go quiet.

He worked the sickle along the edges, dragging cut canes into piles that grew faster than seemed possible. Blackberry work was honest work—it punished hesitation and rewarded commitment—but it was also endless, and by the second hour his forearms were burning and his hands were striped with scratches despite the gloves. Sena climbed the ladder against the mulberry and pruned with a focus that shut him out entirely, and he found himself stopping to watch.

She didn't cut the way he'd been taught. His father had been a butcher-pruner—open the center, remove anything crossing, impose order on the tree. Sena worked differently. She'd study a branch for a long moment, turn it in her gloved hand, follow it back to the trunk, and then either leave it entirely or take it in one clean cut at the collar. She removed less than half of what his father would have. When she came down to reposition the ladder, she caught him watching.

"What?"

"Nothing. You prune like my grandfather's photographs. The old Italian method. Leave the tree its own shape."

"My grandmother's method. She learned it from her mother, who learned it in Enugu from a man who learned it from an Italian missionary who probably stole it from somebody's nonna." She wiped her forehead with her wrist. "The idea is the tree already knows what it's doing. You're not the architect. You're the editor. You cut what's dead, what's diseased, what's in the way, and what's greedy—and then you stop. Most people can't stop. Stopping is the whole skill."

"And my father?"

"Your father imposed." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "His trees were beautiful. Symmetrical. Productive for fifteen years and then exhausted. A tree that's been told what to be its whole life doesn't know how to age." She climbed back up the ladder. "Your Pinot rows are the same. Too tidy. You're editing them to death."

He wanted to argue. He stood there among the blackberry corpses wanting very much to argue, and instead he went back to the sickle, because the argument would require admitting she'd seen his vineyard closely enough to judge it, and he wasn't ready to ask when that had happened.

* * *

They broke for water at noon, sitting on the porch steps because the porch furniture had rotted. Sena handed him a thermos cup without ceremony. The tea was strong and slightly too sweet, and he drank all of it.

"Behind the barn," she said. "You wanted to see them."

"I didn't say I wanted—"

"You've been circling toward the barn all morning. Every time you 'rest,' you rest facing that direction." She stood, brushed off her jeans. "Come on, then."

The path behind the barn was barely a path—more a memory of one, hemmed in by bramble. She led him single file, holding branches aside with a hooked stick, until the space opened into a small clearing where the barn's shadow fell long across the ground.

And there they were.

Four vines, ancient, their trunks thick as his forearm, gnarled into shapes that looked carved rather than grown. Someone had trained them onto a wire trellis that had mostly rusted away, and the canes sprawled wild across the ground, but the plants themselves were unmistakably alive—bark supple at the scratch test, buds set tight for spring, roots running deep under ten years of neglect.

"Muscat Blanc," Sena said quietly. "My grandfather brought the cuttings himself. From a grower outside Fresno, 1974. Everyone told him table grapes were a waste in this soil and he planted them anyway, right here, behind the barn, where the drainage is best." She crouched and pulled a weed from the base of the nearest trunk with a tenderness that undid something in Ivo's chest. "When the bank took the farm, these weren't on the assessment. Nobody counts four vines behind a barn. So they stayed ours. My mother used to drive out twice a year, before she got sick. Just to check on them. Just to touch them."

Ivo crouched beside her. Up close he could see the work she'd already done—one vine carefully pruned, its canes tied loosely to a replacement wire she'd strung herself, the cuts healed and clean. She'd been coming out here for weeks. Since before the fence-line fight. Maybe since before she'd moved in.

"You've been tending them since you got back."

"Since before I got back, technically. I came out in August, before the house closed. Told myself I was just looking." She ran her thumb along the bark. "Then I signed the papers and here I am, spending money I don't have on wire and pruners for four grapevines, and I can't explain to you why, except that they're the only thing on this property that was never taken. Everything else—the house, the land, the name on the mailbox—they took it and sold it and resold it. These stayed mine because nobody thought they mattered." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry but fierce. "So. That's what I needed the ladders for. That's the whole embarrassing truth. Are you going to say something cruel now? Because I'd rather get it over with."

Ivo looked at the vines. At the clean pruning cuts. At the replacement wire, tensioned correctly, anchored to posts she must have reset herself, alone, in the dark probably, after days of whatever work paid her bills.

"You tie a knot I've only seen in one place," he said.

Her head came up sharply. "What?"

"On the replacement wires in my east block. My father used a specific anchor hitch—double loop, tail through the eye, twice. Nobody around here uses it. I learned it from watching him, and I've never seen anyone else do it." He pointed at the post. "That's his knot."

Sena stared at the wire. Then at him. Something moved through her face—confusion, then a strange, guarded stillness, like a door closing softly somewhere inside her.

"That's not possible," she said. "I taught myself. Off memory. From watching—from years ago." Her voice had gone careful. "From watching your father's crew, when I was young. Before."

"Before the foreclosure."

"Before." She stood abruptly, brushing off her knees, and the openness of a moment ago was gone, packed away. "Well. Two people can learn the same trick. Doesn't mean anything."

"It means your hands remember this valley better than your head does," Ivo said, before he could stop himself.

She turned and looked at him fully then, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The afternoon wind moved through the clearing, rattling the old canes. Somewhere behind them, a crow complained about the destroyed blackberries.

"Your Pinot rows," she said finally, businesslike again, retreating. "Tomorrow. Bring your pruners and an open mind, and try not to argue with everything I say before you've tried it."

"I reserve the right to argue."

"Of course you do. You're a Montrose." But she said it almost lightly, almost like a joke between them, and then ruined it by adding, quieter: "Thank you for the ladders. And the loppers. And—" a small gesture at the cleared hedgerow, visible even from here, acres of cut cane stacked in ragged windrows— "the rest."

"Don't mention it."

"I won't," she said, and he laughed despite himself, one short exhale, and she almost smiled, and then they were both looking anywhere else, two people standing in a dead woman's garden surrounded by living vines, pretending the afternoon hadn't happened.

He gathered his tools and walked back through the bramble path. At the edge of the clearing he stopped, because there was one thing he hadn't asked, and it had been sitting in his chest since the knot.

"Sena."

She looked up from coiling wire.

"The letters that used to come to our house. Every October. Unsigned." He kept his voice casual, a man asking about weather. "Did your family ever write to the farmhouse? To the Montroses, I mean. Back when."

The silence lasted three seconds. He counted them.

"No," she said. "Why would we?"

"No reason. Curiosity."

"We didn't write to your family, Mr. Montrose. We wrote to ourselves." She turned back to the vine, dismissing him with the gentleness of total indifference. "See you tomorrow. Seven o'clock. Don't be late—I hate pruning with an audience that shows up at seven-fifteen."

He walked home along the fence line with the loppers over his shoulder, turning her answer over like a stone with something glinting underneath it. *We wrote to ourselves.* Strange phrasing. Strange, specific phrasing, the kind of sentence a person constructs when the true answer is complicated.

Behind him, in the clearing, four Muscat vines stood newly freed in the late light, and he found himself thinking—not for the first time, but for the first time without dread—that whatever she was growing over there, he wanted to taste it.

He was halfway across his own field when he remembered the shoebox in his father's study. Ten Octobers of unsigned letters. Ten replies he'd written and hidden and never sent.

*We wrote to ourselves.*

He stopped walking. The wind came down the hill and moved through the bare rows, and for a moment he stood perfectly still in the middle of his failing vineyard, feeling the shape of a suspicion assemble itself in his chest, patient and unwelcome as a bud set for spring.

No, he told himself. Coincidence. Two families, one valley, the same knots, the same autumns.

He started walking again, faster than before, and did not look back at the farmhouse.

But that night, he sharpened his pruners a second time, and set them by the door for tomorrow, and lay awake listening to the wind come off the Okonkwo hill, wondering what else her hands remembered that she wasn't saying.
