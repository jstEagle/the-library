## 12. Reading Between the Lines

The rain started at four in the afternoon and didn't stop, which meant no tractor work and no excuses. Ivo told himself he was going into the study to sort the last of his father's files—the bank had asked for three years of soil reports he couldn't find—and instead he found himself standing at the desk with the shoebox in both hands, the way you pick up something that might be sleeping.

He'd been avoiding it for two weeks. Ever since Sena Okonkwo had come over the fence line with her grandmother's pruning shears and a mouth like a whetstone, ever since she'd stood in his rows and told him his canopy management was "sentimental," he'd been aware of the box sitting on the shelf behind the ledgers like a held breath.

He set it on the desk. Sat down. The rain ticked against the window in the rhythm of someone tapping a pen.

*Just file it away properly,* he told himself. *Put it in the archive cabinet with the rest of the house's ghosts.*

Instead he lifted the lid.

Forty-one letters. He'd counted them once before, drunk, at two in the morning, and then put the count out of his mind because forty-one letters meant ten Octobers and ten Octobers meant someone had been writing to this house since before his father died, since before the vines started dying, since before everything went soft and gray. Someone had been writing to a house with no one in it but a man too proud to answer and a boy who answered and hid it.

Ivo took out the oldest letter. The paper had gone the color of weak tea. He knew what it said; he'd read it so many times the sentences lived in him like scripture learned by rote:

*Dear House —*

*I know houses can't read. My brother says I'm ridiculous. But you're the only thing on this road that's been here longer than us, and my mother says old things keep secrets well. Today we picked the first quinces off the tree by your fence line. They were sour as sin. I ate one anyway, to prove a point to nobody.*

*— S.*

*S.* He'd never thought much about the initial when he was sixteen. A stranger was a stranger. Now the single letter sat in his chest like a stone dropped into still water, and the ripples went out and out.

Because there was an orchard by the fence line. He could see it from his kitchen window—a ragged half-acre of quince and apple gone feral since the Okonkwo farm emptied out, since the bank took it and let it sit like a pulled tooth. And two weeks ago, standing in the wet grass between their properties while she lectured him about trellis wire, Sena had glanced at the orchard and said, almost to herself: *"That quince tree used to beat me every October. Sour as sin. I ate one every year anyway."*

She'd laughed when she said it. He hadn't laughed. He'd felt the ground tilt a degree, the way ground tilts just before you realize the landslide already started.

He set the first letter down and pulled the second from the box. Year two of the letters. The handwriting had changed—looser, faster, the loops of the capital letters unspooling like thread off a spool. She'd been older. Seventeen, maybe eighteen.

*Dear House —*

*We got a dog. Nobody asked for him. He's brown and stupid and he keeps chasing the tractors up your lane until your foreman yells at him in French, which I did not know was a language they spoke here. I've named him Biscuit because he steals them off the drying rack. If you find biscuit crumbs anywhere near your porch, that was him, and I apologize in advance for the next ten years.*

*— S.*

Ivo's father had complained about that dog. He remembered it now with terrible clarity—his father at dinner, wine-flushed, going on about *"those Okonkwo people and their mongrel, tearing up my lane like they own it."* His father had said *my lane*. As if the road itself belonged to the Montroses. As if the dog didn't have as much right to it as any of them.

Biscuit. A brown, stupid dog named after theft.

Last month—Ivo counted back, and it was exactly twenty-six days ago—Sena had come over to borrow a post-hole digger, and while he'd dug around in the equipment shed she'd stood in the doorway watching the rain start, and a fox had crossed the far field, and she'd said, "We had a dog like that once. Brown and stupid. Named him Biscuit because he stole them off the rack. Best animal I ever knew."

She'd said it lightly. The way you say things you've said a hundred times to yourself, worn smooth as river stone. And Ivo had grunted some non-answer, because that was what he did, because the box was already whispering to him from the shelf even then and he hadn't wanted to hear it.

He laid the letters out across the desk in order, all forty-one, like evidence at a trial where he was both prosecutor and defendant. The rain kept its metronome. Somewhere below, in the cellar, the tanks murmured their fermentation murmur, indifferent.

Year three: a letter about frost. *Dear House — the radio says frost tonight and my father is up on the hill with the smudge pots and I am not allowed to help because I'm "too young to matter" which is a lie adults tell. If you have any vines left alive tomorrow, House, it's because men like my father stayed up all night burning trash in tin cans for people who won't even say thank you.*

Ivo remembered that night. He'd been eleven. His father had stood on the porch with binoculars watching the Okonkwo fires dot the hillside like fallen stars, and Ivo had asked if they should help, and his father had said, *"They chose their own way, boy. Let them burn their own trash."* And the frost had come anyway, and taken rows on both sides of the fence, and the difference was that the Montrose insurance covered theirs and the Okonkwos had none.

Year five: the letters got shorter. *Dear House — we're fine. Don't worry about us. — S.* That was all. He remembered reading that one at sixteen and feeling, without understanding why, like he'd been punched. He'd written three pages in reply that night, pages that ended up folded under his mattress, asking *are you okay, who is okay, tell me what's wrong*, and then torn them up because his father had come up the stairs and Ivo had heard his own heartbeat like a knock at a door he wasn't allowed to open.

Year seven: the handwriting changed again. Smaller. Controlled. The loops reined in. *Dear House — I'm writing from my aunt's place in the city. We lost it. You probably know that. Everyone knows that. I'm not writing to blame you. I don't know why I'm writing. Habit, maybe. The house across the road from my aunt's window has a light that comes on at nine every night and I pretend it's you.*

And the last letter, year ten, the autumn before last, arrived six weeks after his father's funeral, when the house was Ivo's and empty and the debt notices were arriving faster than the mail could carry them:

*Dear House —*

*I heard about Mr. Montrose. I'm sorry. I mean that, whatever else is true. Grief doesn't check whose side you're on.*

*I'm not coming back. I want you to know that so you can stop being a habit. I've got a life here now. A job. A lease with my name on it.*

*But I drove past in June. The east rows are dead—all of them—and nobody's cut the suckers off the rootstock, and it made me so angry I had to pull over. Ten years and I still can't stand to see bad pruning. Isn't that the stupidest thing?*

*Goodbye, House. Be well. Somebody ought to love you properly someday.*

*— S.*

*Somebody ought to love you properly someday.*

Ivo read that sentence four times. Then he got up and walked to the window and stood there with his hands flat on the sill, looking out at the rain coming down gray and steady over the vineyard, over the dead east rows, over the fence line and the feral orchard beyond it, and he tried to feel surprised and found that he couldn't. Surprise required the possibility of innocence, and he'd spent that somewhere around the day she'd said *sour as sin* and he'd said nothing.

* * *

It wasn't proof. That was the thing he kept circling, the lawyer in his head making its careful distinctions. An orchard by a fence line—half the farms on this road had one. Dogs named after stolen food—common enough. A woman who hated bad pruning—she'd grown up on a vineyard; of course she had opinions about suckers.

Coincidence stacked is still coincidence. Four coincidences deep, though, and the stack starts to look like architecture.

He needed more. He needed something that couldn't be explained away, and he hated himself a little for needing it, because what he actually wanted—what he'd wanted since the day she climbed his fence with those shears—was for it to be her, and wanting it that badly made every piece of evidence suspect. You couldn't trust a man to identify a face he was praying to see.

So he did what he always did with unbearable things. He worked. He went down to the cellar and checked brix levels he'd checked that morning, and topped up a barrel that didn't need topping, and stood in the cool dark listening to the wine breathe and telling himself: *Monday. Her loan paperwork comes through the co-op office Monday. There'll be a signature on it. Handwriting is handwriting. That'll settle it.*

Except he didn't make it to Monday.

Sunday morning the rain had cleared and the light came in low and gold, the kind of light that makes even dying vineyards look like paintings, and Sena came over the fence line at eight a.m.—eight, on a Sunday, because she had no respect for the sabbath or for him—with a folder under her arm and frost-burnt determination on her face.

"The co-op sent my forms back," she said. "There's an error on page two. I need to correct it and resubmit today or I lose my processing slot." She said it fast, clipped, the way she said everything that cost her pride. Asking him for anything cost her pride; he'd learned to see the tax she paid on every request.

"What kind of error?"

"They transposed digits in my parcel number. I have to initial the correction." She flipped the folder open on the tailgate of his truck, produced a pen from her jacket, and bent over the form.

And Ivo stood three feet away and looked at her handwriting.

Not the signature—signatures are performances, everyone's signature is a costume. The ordinary writing. The printed characters of the parcel number she copied out, slow and careful, her tongue-tip of concentration showing at the corner of her mouth. The way she made her lowercase *e* with a flat crossbar instead of a loop. The way her *7*s carried a slash through the middle like they were trying to escape the line.

He had forty-one letters in a shoebox upstairs written in that hand. Forty-one flat-crossed *e*s. Forty-one slashed *7*s marching through ten years of Octobers.

Sena straightened up, initialed the margin, and caught him looking. "What?" she said. "Do I have something on my face?"

"No," Ivo said. "You're fine."

She narrowed her eyes at him—the look she gave the vines when they were hiding something, mildew maybe, or a broken drip line—and for one long second he thought she was going to ask. He braced for it. He ran the sentence through his head at panic speed: *I think you wrote to this house. I think I wrote back. I think we've been talking for ten years without either of us knowing it.* It came out garbled even in imagination, a wreck of a sentence, and he watched it crash and burn behind his eyes and said none of it.

"You're being weird," she decided, snapping the folder shut. "Weirder than baseline. Which for you is saying something."

"I'm always weird before coffee."

"You've had coffee. I can smell it on you." She hopped the fence line—not around, through the gap, over, because she never used the gate, gates were apparently a concession she refused to make—and paused on the top rail, silhouetted against the gold light, and looked back at him. "The east rows," she said. "Have you given any more thought to what I said about grafting over the dead stock instead of replanting? Your father's rootstock is better than anything you could buy new. It'd be a shame to waste it."

"It's on my list."

"Your list is a shoebox of good intentions, Ivo Montrose. I've seen how you organize things." She dropped down onto her own side of the fence and was gone, boots crunching through the wet grass toward the feral orchard, and he stood at the tailgate holding a corrected loan form and feeling like a man who has just been handed the answer to a question he's terrified to ask aloud.

* * *

That evening he climbed to the study and took down the shoebox and did the thing he'd been refusing to do for two days: he compared them directly. Letter beside loan form, on the green blotter under the lamp, like a man comparing faces in two photographs and admitting, finally, that they were the same face.

Flat-crossed *e*. Slashed *7*. The particular forward lean of her capitals, urgent, like the words were trying to get somewhere ahead of her. And underneath the mechanics, the thing that settled it beyond all the lawyerly hedging: the voice. *Sour as sin. Too young to matter. Somebody ought to love you properly someday.* The same person who stood in his rows and called his canopy management sentimental. The same person who'd said, three nights ago, standing over the crush pad in the dark: *"Ten years and I still can't stand to see bad pruning."*

Ten years. She'd said it herself. Twice now, twice, like a key turning in a lock she didn't know she was standing next to.

Ivo sat back in his father's chair—it was still his father's chair; he kept meaning to replace it and kept not replacing it—and looked at the forty-one letters spread across the desk, and understood the shape of the situation with total clarity, which was somehow worse than confusion.

She had written to this house for ten years. Through the foreclosure, through the exile, through the years of pretending she had a life in the city. She had written *goodbye* and then kept writing anyway, because habits don't honor their own farewells. And he—he had read every word, answered every word, and buried the answers in a barn wall like a coward burying evidence, and now she was twenty feet away across a fence line, real and breathing and furious and funny, pruning his vines and borrowing his tools and paying pride-taxes on every favor, and she had no idea. Not a suspicion. Not a flicker. To her, the house had been silent for a decade. To her, she had written forty-one letters into a void.

And the worst part—the part that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone—was that she'd told him things in those letters she'd never told anyone. He knew that the way you know a thing you have no right to know. Year four, the letter about her mother crying in the packing shed. Year six, the one that said *sometimes I hate this valley and I'm afraid that means I'm the kind of person who leaves*. Year nine, the one that was just one line: *do you think a person can miss a place that hurt her?*

He knew her answers to questions she'd never asked him. He was carrying a decade of her interior life around like contraband, and she didn't know he had it, and every day he didn't tell her the contraband got heavier.

*Tell her,* said the voice in his head that sounded suspiciously like his mother, dead eleven years now, the only person who'd ever caught him hiding things and made him feel ashamed rather than clever. *Tell her tonight. Walk over there. Say: I found the letters, and I answered them, and I was a coward, and I'm sorry, and also I think the quince tree misses you.*

And the other voice—his father's voice, low and certain, the voice that had said *let them burn their own trash*—said: *And then what? She learns the Montrose boy read her grief like a serial novel for ten years and never sent a word? She learns you knew her mother cried and you said nothing? You'll lose the fence line, boy. You'll lose the partnership. You'll lose the only ally this vineyard has left, and the bank will take everything, and you'll deserve it.*

Both voices were right. That was the trap of it. Telling her was honest and might destroy them. Not telling her was cowardice and kept them safe, and every day of safety was another day of theft.

Ivo gathered the letters up, squared their edges, and put them back in the shoebox—but he didn't put the box back on the shelf. He set it on the corner of the desk where he'd see it every morning, where it would sit like a debt notice he couldn't pay and couldn't throw away.

Outside, the last light was going off the hills, and he could see, across the fence line, the lamp come on in the Okonkwo farmhouse window. Nine o'clock, more or less. *A house across the road with a light that comes on at nine every night, and I pretend it's you.*

She was in there right now. Drinking tea, probably, or cursing at co-op paperwork, or standing at her window looking out at his dark house and pretending it was something it wasn't—something that listened, something that answered.

Soon, he told himself. After the festival. After the bank thing was settled, after the competition entry was filed, after there was something solid under them besides a shared desperation. When the truth couldn't cost them the harvest. That was reasonable. That was strategic. That was, said his mother's voice in his head, exactly what a coward would call it.

Down the hill, the farmhouse light went out.

And in the silence after it, Ivo realized something that turned his blood cold and hot at once, something he should have seen days ago: she'd said she wasn't coming back. The last letter said it plain as print—*I'm not coming back, I want you to know that so you can stop being a habit.* And yet here she was. Leased farm, co-op forms, pruning shears, fence line. Here she was, home, ten minutes' walk from the shoebox, with no idea what waited in it.

Which meant something had brought her back that she hadn't put in any letter.

And he was suddenly, desperately certain he needed to know what it was—before he said a word about the letters, before he opened his mouth at all—because if she'd come back for reasons that had nothing to do with the house, then the whole decade of her writing had been to a ghost, and he was about to confess to a ghost that he'd been keeping its heart in a shoebox.

The phone buzzed on the desk. A text from Sena, sent ninety seconds ago, from ninety seconds' walk away, because she communicated by text even across an open field:

**Co-op says the festival committee needs our joint entry intent signed by Friday. Also I found something weird in my mother's papers today. Ask me later. Maybe.**

Ivo stared at the screen. *Something weird in my mother's papers.* His thumb hovered over the keyboard, and the shoebox sat on the desk corner like a witness, and outside the window the valley went fully dark, row by row, as if someone were switching off the past one field at a time.

He typed: **Friday works. What kind of weird?**

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

Then, after a long minute: **The kind that makes me wonder if everything I believed about this valley is wrong.**

Ivo set the phone down and looked at the shoebox, and for the first time in ten years, he was afraid of what was inside it—for a completely different reason than before.
