## 19. Accusations at Dusk

Ivo found the card on his kitchen table at four in the afternoon, wedged under the sugar bowl where he never put anything, which meant someone had come inside while he was in the north field. He stood there with dirt on his hands and read it twice, then a third time, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something less obscene.

*Halloran & Voss Land Development. We look forward to finalizing arrangements with the Montrose estate.*

Finalizing arrangements. He turned the card over. Nothing on the back but a phone number and a small embossed vine, tasteful, expensive, the kind of detail a company adds when it wants you to forget it's about to flatten everything you love into a parking apron.

He thought of the man he'd seen twice now—Voss, silver-haired, patient, with the unhurried smile of someone who has never been told no by anything except a court. He'd appeared at the festival committee meeting, then at the bank, then, apparently, at Ivo's own kitchen table while Ivo was on his knees in the dirt trying to coax a season out of dead ground.

And Sena. Voss had mentioned her, hadn't he? That second time at the bank, standing too close, lowering his voice like a man sharing a confidence: *I've made approaches to the neighboring parcel as well. Ms. Okonkwo is a pragmatic woman. She'll see the arithmetic.* Ivo had bristled at the presumption and then, in the days since, let the sentence rot in him. *She'll see the arithmetic.* As if Sena were a column of numbers waiting to be summed.

He told himself he wasn't suspicious. He told himself he was only going to ask a direct question and expect a direct answer, the way neighbors did, the way partners in a joint competition entry—co-signed, notarized, filed with the committee two weeks ago—ought to be able to.

He told himself a lot of things on the walk across the fence line.

* * *

Sena was in the old Okonkwo orchard when he arrived, up on a borrowed ladder with a pruning saw, working the apple trees back from a decade of neglect. She'd been at it for hours; he could see it in the pile of cut branches, in the sweat-darkened collar of her shirt, in the way she moved with the tired economy of someone who'd learned young that work doesn't wait for your feelings.

She saw him coming and didn't come down. That was the first wrong note. Sena came down off ladders when people arrived. She waved, she called out, she had a whole repertoire of easy greetings she'd rebuilt from nothing, from memory, from the child she'd been in this orchard. Instead she finished her cut, set the saw down on the ladder's shelf, and looked at him with a stillness he'd learned to read as caution.

"Ivo."

"Sena." He held up the card. "This was on my table."

She looked at it for a long moment from her height, and he watched her decide something, and he watched her decide wrong—or what he would spend the whole evening believing was wrong.

"He came here too," she said. "Last week. I told him no."

"You told him no."

"Those are the words. I said them out loud, in that yard, standing right about where you're standing." She came down the ladder now, slowly, and the descent had a controlled quality to it, like a woman arranging herself before a storm she could see coming. "He didn't take it well. Men like that never do. But I said no."

"And then he came to my house. Let himself in. Left his card under my sugar bowl like he owns the place already." Ivo heard his own voice climbing and didn't like the sound of it and kept going anyway. "He told me at the bank you were pragmatic. He said you'd see the arithmetic."

Something moved through her face—fast, quickly buried, but not fast enough. He saw it. Recognition, maybe. Or guilt. He couldn't tell the difference at that distance and in that light, and the not-knowing did what suspicion does: it grew to fill the available space.

"And what did you say to him?" she asked.

"I said I wasn't selling."

"Good."

"That's it? *Good?*"

"What do you want me to say, Ivo?" She spread her hands, and he noticed they were nicked and sap-stained, hands that had been working his competition entry's orchard section all week without being asked, and he hated that he noticed, and he noticed anyway. "You came over here holding that card like a warrant. So say what you came to say."

"What I came to say." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "Fine. Here's what I came to say. A man is going door to door trying to buy this valley out from under us, and he told me, with a straight face, that you were already half-convinced. And you don't look surprised. You don't look angry. You look like a woman managing a situation."

"I look like a woman who told the man no last week and is now being cross-examined about it by her—"

"By her what?" The word was there, right at the front of his mouth. *Partner.* He swallowed it, which was its own kind of answer. "By her neighbor. Is that what we are? Because the paper we filed with the committee says something else. It says we're pooling land. Pooling labor. Pooling our names. And if either one of us has been sitting across a desk from Halloran and Voss—"

"I *told* you I sat across a desk from him. That's what telling the truth sounds like, Ivo. It sounds like *I told him no, last week, before you even knew he existed.*" Her voice had gone quiet, which was worse than loud. He'd learned that about her in the frost fields, in the barn with the rain hammering the roof. Sena got quiet when she was most herself. "You know what I was doing when he showed up? I was reading my mother's deed papers. Going through everything she kept, because I thought—I actually thought—we were past this. Past the part where a Montrose assumes the worst of an Okonkwo first and asks questions never."

"Don't." The word came out of him like something fired. "Don't make this about my father."

"I didn't say your father."

"You didn't have to."

The orchard went very still around them. Somewhere behind the house a crow said one thing and thought better of it. Sena looked at him for a long time, and he watched the warmth go out of her face by degrees, the way a stove goes cold, not all at once but in a sequence, and by the end what looked back at him was a stranger wearing her features.

"You want to know what I found in those papers?" she said. "You want to know why I've been quiet this week? Because it wasn't guilt, Ivo. It was grief. My father spent his last good year trying to save this farm and I found out—*I found out*—that yours knew. Yours sat at our kitchen table and drank my mother's coffee and told my father he'd find a way, and then the bank moved, and the auction happened, and your family signed the winning bid on our land, and your father never came to that table again. Never came to the funeral. Never sent so much as a card." Her voice didn't rise. It got flatter, which was worse. "So yes. When a developer comes sniffing around both our properties in the same month, some old arithmetic of my own wakes up. Forgive me for having it."

"That's not—" Ivo stopped. Because the terrible thing, the thing he would carry afterward like a stone in his boot, was that a part of him had always suspected something like this. His father's silences around the Okonkwo name had a shape. A weight. He'd told himself it was contempt. It might have been shame. He didn't know. He had never once asked, because his father was dead and the asking was now impossible, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like a coal.

"That's not what?" Sena pressed. "Say it. That's not what happened? You know what happened? You were a child. I was a child. We both inherited other people's war, and I have spent four months—*four months*—deciding to put it down. Deciding that the woman who came back here was going to be a different woman than the one who left. And you—" she pointed at the card in his hand, at him, at the whole gray distance between them, "—you took one visit from a man in a good suit and decided I was for sale. You didn't ask. You *decided*. That's the inheritance you kept, Ivo. Not the vines. The assuming."

"You're one to talk about assuming," he said, and heard it come out wrong, heard it come out cruel, kept going. "You came back here with a plan and a timeline and a word for this place—*temporary*—you said it, you've said it a dozen times, this is all temporary, you're just here until—until what, Sena? Until something better comes along? Until a buyer makes you an offer good enough to make leaving easy? Maybe Voss did make that offer. Maybe it *was* good enough."

The silence after that was total.

He watched it land. He watched the exact moment the sentence finished its work in her, and some distant, drowning part of him screamed at himself, because he knew—he *knew*—what that word did to her, what *temporary* cost her, what it meant to have your belonging questioned in the one place you'd bled for it. He'd watched the town do it to her at the general store. He'd been angry at them for it. He'd stood in that aisle and hated every one of them.

And now he'd said it himself. In her own orchard. Under her own trees.

"Get off my land," Sena said.

"Sena—"

"Get off my land." Her voice didn't shake. That was the worst part. It was perfectly, terribly steady, the voice of a woman who had been removed from property before and knew the procedure. "You want to talk about who's for sale? My family lost everything in an auction with a number on it. I was eleven years old watching strangers carry our furniture into a truck, and a Montrose family signed the paper that made it legal. And you stand here, in my orchard, and ask me if I'd sell. Ask me if leaving would be *easy*." She took one step toward him and stopped, and her eyes were bright and dry, which somehow was worse than tears. "There is nothing easy about any of this. There has not been one easy day since I came back. And I did it anyway. I did it for the trees. For the house. For some idea I had that home was a thing you could return to if you were brave enough. So no, Ivo. I would not sell. But I will tell you what I would do."

"What," he said, barely a sound.

"I would burn this place down myself before I let a Halloran or a Voss or a *Montrose* decide what it's worth."

* * *

He walked back across the fence line with the card still in his fist, and halfway there he stopped and read it again, as if it might explain him to himself. *We look forward to finalizing arrangements.* He hadn't made any arrangements. He hadn't said a single word to Voss except no. And she knew that. Somewhere under all the ruin of the last twenty minutes, she knew that he hadn't sold, the same way he knew, in the same buried place, that she hadn't either.

That was the trap of it. They had both said no to the same man and somehow ended up accusing each other of yes.

He crumpled the card and stood in the dusk holding it, and the vineyard stretched away from him in its long wounded rows, and the light went out of the valley the way it did every evening, indifferent, complete. He thought about walking back. He thought about the words that would fix it—*I'm sorry, I didn't mean it, I know you wouldn't sell, I know*—and found that his mouth wouldn't make them. Pride, his father's oldest heirloom, sitting in his jaw like a lock.

Behind him, faint across the fields, he heard the ladder being folded. Heard the saw being set down hard on stone. Heard, very distinctly, the back door of the Okonkwo farmhouse close with the particular finality of a door that intends to stay closed.

In his pocket, his phone buzzed. A message from the festival committee, forwarded from an unknown address, already circulating:

*Questions have been raised regarding the financial standing of one co-entrant. The committee will review all joint applications before final approval.*

He read it twice. Somewhere out there, Voss was smiling.

And the vines—both vineyards, both halves of the only thing they had left—would go another day without anyone's hands in them.
