## 25. The Festival Committee

The meeting was scheduled for two o'clock in the back room of the general store, which meant it would start at twenty past and run until someone's wife called them home for supper. I had been to exactly one committee meeting before—when I was nineteen, arguing on my father's behalf that the Montrose entry should be judged separately from the co-op wines—and I remembered the smell of it: burnt coffee, wool coats, the particular staleness of a room where men rehearse old arguments like liturgy.

I arrived at ten past two with a folder of soil tests under my arm and found Sena already there, sitting at the far end of the folding table with her hands folded, looking like a woman waiting for a verdict she'd already decided not to appeal.

"You're early," I said.

"I'm always early." She didn't look at me. "It's the one thing they can't take away from you."

There it was again—that edge she'd carried since the confrontation at the fence line, since the developer's name had entered our conversations like smoke under a door. We'd signed the joint entry three days ago. Her signature sat above mine on the form, both of us choosing the vineyard over the grudge, and we'd managed almost forty-eight hours of civil, functional, entirely bloodless cooperation. This morning she'd pruned her half of the shared block without speaking to me except to say *your secateurs are dull*.

She wasn't wrong about the secateurs.

The committee filed in by degrees. Marta Voss first, who ran the store and therefore ran the town's opinions along with its inventory. Then old Henrik Dahl, who had judged the festival competition for thirty years and considered his palate a public trust. Then the Okafor brothers, who grew apples up the valley and cared about the festival mainly as an excuse for the dance afterward. And last, ten minutes late, came Gerald Pruitt.

Pruitt didn't sit on the committee. He didn't need to. He owned the feed store, half the commercial strip, and—as I'd learned the week before—an option on the Hollis parcel that put him within bidding distance of everything else. He wore a linen jacket in October because he could afford to be cold. He took a chair against the wall, uninvited, and arranged himself like a man attending a funeral he'd personally arranged.

"Mr. Pruitt," Marta said carefully, "this is a committee session."

"Public building," Pruitt said pleasantly. "I'll just listen."

Marta looked at me. I looked at the table. There was no rule to invoke and we all knew it, and Pruitt knew we knew it, and that was the whole architecture of the man.

* * *

The agenda item was simple enough. Our joint entry—the Montrose rows and the Okonkwo rows, blended into one submission for the harvest competition—required committee ratification, because the rules had never contemplated two properties entering as one. Henrik read the rulebook passage aloud twice, running his finger under the words like a schoolteacher hoping the text would confess something.

"'Entries shall represent a single vineyard operation,'" he said. "That's the language. Single operation."

"It is a single operation," Sena said. "Shared labor, shared equipment, shared block. The wine comes from grapes we picked together."

"The land isn't shared," Henrik said. "Two deeds, two owners."

"One fence," I said. "Which we're taking down."

Henrik looked at me over his glasses with the expression of a man who has heard every kind of promise a vineyard owner can make and buried most of them. "Ivo. No offense meant to you or your father's memory. But your father entered this competition eleven times and won four, and even he never asked the committee to bend a rule for him."

"He also never needed to," I said, and heard how it sounded the moment it left my mouth.

Sena's jaw tightened. Marta's pen stopped moving.

"What Mr. Montrose means," she said, level and clear, "is that his father had a bank that believed in him. We have a bank that's counting the days. The rule exists to keep people from gaming the competition. We're not gaming anything. We're trying to save two farms with one harvest."

"It's a reasonable request," Marta said, in the tone of a woman opening a negotiation rather than closing one. "But there are concerns."

"Name them," Sena said.

And that was when Pruitt cleared his throat from the wall.

"I can save everyone some time," he said. "Since nobody in this room wants to say it to her face."

The room went very still. I felt Sena go still beside me—not relaxed stillness, but the stillness of a drawn wire.

"Gerald," Marta said, warning in it.

"Somebody has to." Pruitt pushed off the wall and stood, hands in his pockets, radiating regret the way other men radiated cologne. "Ms. Okonkwo. Your property carries a mortgage held by Meridian Capital. That's public record. What's also becoming public record—I hear things, it's a small town—is that Meridian has been shopping that paper around. That there are parties interested in acquiring it. That if those parties acquire it, this valley gets a resort where your farmhouse stands and a parking lot where your vines are." He spread his hands. "Now. I don't know if any of that is true. But the committee has to ask: if the Okonkwo farm goes to auction in the spring, what happens to the wine? To the block? Does the entry collapse? Are we certifying a vintage from land that might not have an owner by planting season?"

Every eye in the room turned to Sena.

I want to record, here, what she did, because I've thought about it every day since. She did not flinch. She did not look at me. She turned in her chair to face Pruitt fully, the way you face weather, and she said:

"That's a fair question, Mr. Pruitt. Here's a fair answer. My mortgage is current. My taxes are paid. My vines produced more tons per acre this year than any block in this valley, including yours—" a small ripple went through the Okafors at that; Pruitt didn't grow grapes but he leased land to people who did—"and if you know something about my loan that I don't, you should say it plainly instead of dressing a rumor up as a question and calling it concern for the committee."

Pruitt smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile of a man who had gotten exactly what he came for, which was not an answer but the sight of the question landing in front of thirty witnesses.

"I only repeat what I hear," he said. "Small town."

He left. The door swung shut behind him and the room exhaled, and I understood, sitting there with my folder of soil tests, that the meeting was already lost. Not the vote—the vote we might still win. But Pruitt hadn't come to kill the entry today. He'd come to plant something. A doubt doesn't need to win an argument. It just needs to be spoken aloud in a warm room by a man with money, and then it grows on its own, like bindweed.

* * *

They voted anyway. Four to one, with Henrik dissenting, the committee ratified our entry—provisionally. Provisional pending verification of the operational stability of both properties. Which meant paperwork, which meant disclosure, which meant Sena's finances laid out on a table for Henrik Dahl's reading glasses while Pruitt's rumor did its quiet work in the town's bloodstream.

We walked out together into the cold bright afternoon. Sena carried the ratified form rolled in her fist like a baton she wasn't sure she wanted to finish holding.

"Well," she said. "That went better than expected."

"You expected worse?"

"I expected him to say my name more times." She stopped at the edge of the store porch and looked down the street—at the feed store with its fresh paint, at the noticeboard with its festival posters, at the whole small machinery of a town deciding what it believed. "You know what he's doing, don't you? He's not trying to beat us at the competition. He's trying to make the competition irrelevant. If the town decides my farm is already dead, then whoever buys the paper at auction is doing us a kindness. He's making himself the mercy."

I had thought roughly the same thing at roughly two-thirty that afternoon, standing in the back of a room watching a man in a linen jacket perform reluctance. Hearing her say it made it worse, somehow. She saw the shape of the trap from inside it.

"There's one thing he said that matters," I said. "Meridian shopping the paper. Is that true?"

Her face did something complicated. "I don't know. My loan officer stopped returning calls two weeks ago. That's either nothing or it's everything." She laughed, short and without humor. "My mother used to say silence from a bank is like silence from a doctor. You can pay for the good version or wait for the free one."

"Sena." I hesitated. Everything I wanted to say next was either too large or too late. *Let me help. Let me put my land up. Let me—* Ten years of unsent sentences stacked behind my teeth. "If the paper gets bought. If it comes to auction. You'd have options."

"Would I." She looked at me then, straight on, and I couldn't read whether what was in her face was gratitude or contempt or the thing underneath both that neither of us had named yet. "Ivo, I watched your father stand in that same doorway"—she nodded back at the store—"in 2014, when the foreclosure notice went up on our gate, and he told my mother he'd have options for us. Options. Do you know what happened to the options?"

I knew. I'd learned it in chapter after chapter of my father's ledgers, later, too late—he had tried, quietly, clumsily, and failed, and had never once told anyone he'd tried, least of all me. But she didn't know that. All she knew was the sentence her mother had carried home.

"No," I said. "Tell me."

"They evaporated." She rolled the form tighter. "So forgive me if 'options' isn't the word I want to hear from a Montrose. I'll handle my own paper. You handle yours. We have a wine to finish and a committee to survive. That's the deal."

She walked to her truck. She got in. She sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel, not starting it, looking through the windshield at nothing—or at something, maybe, some version of this valley where none of it had ever gone wrong—and then the engine caught and she pulled out onto the road and was gone.

* * *

I stayed on the porch longer than I meant to. Marta Voss came out with two cups of coffee, handed me one, and stood beside me looking down the empty street.

"She's got spine," Marta said.

"She's got more than that."

"Mm." Marta sipped. "You want to know what's being said? Since you'll hear it eventually anyway, better from me."

"Marta—"

"Pruitt's telling people the Okonkwo girl came back broke, that the vineyard partnership is a rescue mission dressed up as romance, that you're carrying her the way your father carried her family and we all remember how that ended." She said it flatly, without cruelty, the way you report weather damage. "He's also telling people you're leveraged to the eyebrows yourself, so the joint entry is two drowning people holding each other under. Some of it's even true, Ivo. That's what makes it work."

I stared into the coffee. "What do you believe?"

"I believe I've known you since you were nine years old and you cried at your own birthday party because the pony looked tired." She set down her cup. "I believe that girl worked her rows alone through the night when you collapsed, and if you don't know that, you should, because half the town does and the other half will by Sunday. And I believe Pruitt doesn't spend money on rumors unless there's something he's afraid of." She picked the cup back up. "Fix your secateurs. Fix whatever else needs fixing. The vote was provisional, and provisional means the story isn't written yet."

She went inside. I stood there until the coffee went cold, thinking about Sena working my rows alone in the dark while I slept off exhaustion on the barn floor, thinking about how the town knew and I hadn't thanked her properly, hadn't found words that weren't ten years stale.

Then my phone buzzed. Unknown number. A text, short and businesslike:

*Mr. Montrose — Gerald Pruitt. I think you and I should talk before the festival. There are things you don't know about Ms. Okonkwo's situation. Things she hasn't told you. I'd hate for you to sign your name to something that breaks. Tomorrow, my office, 9 a.m.?*

I read it twice. Three times. The sun was dropping behind the ridge and the frost fans on the far hills were starting their slow evening turn, and somewhere down that road Sena was driving home to a farmhouse whose mortgage was being shopped around like a horse at market, and she hadn't told me. Or hadn't known. And I couldn't tell which possibility frightened me more.

I typed *no*, deleted it. Typed *not interested*, deleted it.

Then I typed: *9 a.m.*

Because there are only two ways to fight a man who trades in what you don't know. You can refuse to listen. Or you can walk into his office, look him in the eye, and find out exactly what weapon he's holding—before he gets the chance to use it in public, at the festival, in front of everyone, with her standing beside you.

I told myself it was strategy.

I told myself that all the way home, past the dead rows and the living ones, past the fence line where her headlights had swept an hour earlier, into the dark of the house where a shoebox of letters sat in my father's study like a conscience I kept meaning to answer.

Tomorrow, 9 a.m. Whatever Pruitt knew, I'd know it first.

And God help me, some small treacherous part of me—the part that had spent ten years reading her handwriting and saying nothing—already dreaded what I'd find out.
