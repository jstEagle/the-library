## 28. The Tasting

The pavilion smelled of canvas and cut grass and the particular nervousness of forty people pretending they weren't competing.

Ivo stood at the edge of the tasting hall with his hands in his pockets because if he took them out, he'd wring them, and he'd promised Sena he wouldn't wring them. *You look like a man awaiting sentencing,* she'd said that morning, straightening his collar with two efficient tugs that had left him unable to speak for a full minute. *Stand like the wine is already won.* He was trying. His shoulders had other ideas.

The joint entry sat on table fourteen, third row from the windows, in a bottle with no label yet—just a strip of masking tape on which Sena had written, in her quick slanted hand: **OKONKWO-MONTROSE, RED, 2023**. Her name first. She'd insisted. He'd argued for exactly four seconds before realizing he didn't care about order as long as both names were on it, and then he'd cared so much about both names being on it that he'd nearly said something stupid right there in the barn, and had to go check the press instead.

Forty entries this year. A record, someone said, though Ivo suspected the committee said that every year. Long tables draped in white cloth ran the length of the pavilion, each bottle flanked by numbered glasses and a small card bearing only the entry number—blind tasting, always blind, though everyone knew whose wine was whose by the second pour anyway. Vineyards had accents. You could hear them.

The judges moved down the rows in slow procession: three of them, as always. Marta Voss, who ran the co-op and had judged every festival since before Ivo could drive. A sommelier up from the city named Dele Adeyemi, young, serious, with the kind of palate people whispered about. And the third chair—the one that made Ivo's stomach tighten—was filled this year by a woman named Catherine Lindqvist, a consultant who wrote for two national wine publications and who had, three weeks ago, stood in the Montrose tasting room with a developer's business card sticking out of her coat pocket and asked very polite questions about the property's debt load.

She hadn't known he'd seen the card. He hadn't mentioned it. But he watched her now the way you watch weather coming over a ridge.

*Stop,* he told himself. *You've done the work. The wine is what it is.*

The wine was what it was, and what it was terrified him.

They'd blended it eleven days ago, in the barn, at two in the morning, after the night harvest had emptied them both past speech. Two barrels' worth of trials lined up on sawhorses under the work lamp: his fruit, pressed by his father's methods, fermented cold and patient; her fruit, handled the way her grandmother had insisted, picked earlier, fermented warmer, aged rougher and briefer. Separately, each was decent. Honest. The kind of wine that placed fourth and kept a farm alive.

Together—

He still remembered the moment the pipette crossed from one glass to the other. Sixty-forty, her blend to his. Sena had swirled, sniffed, tasted, and gone completely still, the way she did when something mattered, her whole face closing like a door so no one could see through it. Then she'd handed him the glass without a word, and he'd tasted it, and understood the silence.

It wasn't a compromise. That was the thing he couldn't get over. He'd expected a middle—a wine that split the difference between two houses, polite and forgettable. Instead the two methods had done something to each other. His father's patience had given her fruit structure, a spine of tannin running through it like fence posts. Her grandmother's early picking had lifted his, pulled brightness up through all that weight, made the dark fruit taste awake. It tasted, absurdly, like the valley itself—the cool mornings and hot afternoons, the limestone under everything, the two farms that shared a fence line and half an aquifer and twenty years of silence.

It tasted like an argument that had finally become a conversation.

*Terrifyingly good,* Sena had said, and laughed, and the laugh had cracked open her closed face, and Ivo had stood there in the lamplight holding a glass of wine that might save everything and thought: *if we lose tomorrow, at least this existed.*

Now the judges were at table nine.

He found Sena across the pavilion, standing near the cider stall with her arms folded, wearing the blue dress she saved for occasions that required armor. She caught him looking and gave him a single nod: *steady*. He nodded back: *trying*. Around them the crowd murmured—farmers in their good jackets, wives in festival colors, kids weaving between legs with paper cups of sweet cider, old men who remembered when the Okonkwos and Montroses had entered separately, year after year, and never once together.

That history hung over table fourteen like heat haze. Everyone knew. Everyone had an opinion. Half the town had spent three weeks deciding whether the joint entry was reconciliation or desperation or some scheme, and the other half had spent those weeks watching the rival bidder's lawyers move through the bank records like weasels through a henhouse. Rumor had it the developer, Halloran, had told anyone who'd listen that the Okonkwo-Montrose wine would be a novelty act—two failing farms stapled together, sentimental, undrinkable.

Ivo had stopped repeating the rumor to himself around dawn. Mostly.

Table ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Marta Voss paused longest at twelve—an old friend of his father's, a woman who had once told sixteen-year-old Ivo that Montrose wine used to be worth the drive. She tasted, set down her glass, and looked out across the room until she found Ivo, and gave him nothing. No nod, no frown. Marta's face at a judging was a locked barn.

Thirteen. Fourteen.

Time went strange. Ivo heard the pour—the thin bright sound of wine hitting glass—and his own pulse answering it. Dele Adeyemi lifted the glass first, tilted it against the window light, and Ivo watched the color catch: deep garnet, almost black at the center, a rim the color of brick. Good color. Young but not green. His father would have approved of the color, and the thought arrived with its usual freight, grief and pride fused into a single dull ache he'd learned to carry like a full bucket.

Adeyemi swirled. Sniffed. And then—he leaned back. Actually leaned back from the glass, eyebrows up, and looked at the number card as if checking he'd read it right.

Catherine Lindqvist stepped in beside him. She raised the glass, and Ivo saw the exact moment her professional neutrality slipped—not into warmth, not yet, but into attention. Real attention. The look of a person revising a judgment mid-sip. She tasted. Held it. Tasted again.

Marta Voss took her turn last. She held the glass up to the light for a long time, longer than she'd held any other, and then she drank, and closed her eyes, and stayed that way for three full seconds while the entire pavilion pretended not to watch.

When she opened them, she looked directly across the room—at Sena, not Ivo—and something passed over her face that might have been the beginning of forgiveness, or memory, or both. Twenty years ago, Marta Voss and Amara Okonkwo had judged the youth preserve competition together. Ivo knew this because his father had complained about it every October of his life.

The judges withdrew to the far end of the pavilion behind a folding screen to confer. The crowd exhaled as one animal.

Sena appeared at his elbow. He hadn't seen her cross the room; she moved like that when she was nervous, quiet and fast, a girl sneaking back into church.

"Well," she said.

"Well," he said.

"They liked it."

"You can't know that."

"Dele Adeyemi physically recoiled from the glass, Ivo. People don't recoil backward from wine they hate."

"He could have been recoiling from the—" He gestured vaguely. "The ambient canvas."

"The ambient canvas." She turned to face him fully, and up close he could see she was doing the same thing he was—holding hope at arm's length because hope, in their experience, was the expensive part. "Look at us. We survived a frost, a foreclosure threat, a hostile developer, a public screaming match at the fence line, and your entire personality, and the wine is still good. Whatever they say in there, the wine is good. I tasted it. You tasted it."

"I know."

"So stand like it."

"I'm standing."

"You're standing like a man at a funeral."

"It's a family trait."

Something flickered across her face at that—his father, her mother, all the dead and the estranged stacked up behind them like casks in a cellar—and for a second he thought she'd take it back, soften it, do the kind thing. She didn't. That was one of the ten thousand things he loved about her: she refused to let the dead off the hook just because they were dead. "Then break the trait," she said. "Start today."

Behind the screen, low voices rose and fell. Once, sharply, Marta's voice cut through—too quiet to make out words, too forceful to be neutral—and Ivo felt Sena go rigid beside him, felt his own stomach drop through the floorboards of the moment.

Then the voices stopped, and the head judge—Marta, always Marta—stepped out, and the pavilion went quiet enough to hear the flags snapping outside.

"Before the placements," Marta said, in the voice she probably used on co-op members who were late on dues, "the panel wishes to note one entry." She looked down at her card. "Entry fourteen."

The murmur that went through the crowd was physical. Ivo felt it in his sternum. Beside him, Sena's hand found his sleeve—not his hand, her discipline wouldn't allow his hand, but his sleeve, gripping the fabric hard enough to pull it crooked.

"In thirty-one years of judging this festival," Marta continued, "this panel has rarely encountered a wine that could not have been made by either contributing vineyard alone." She looked up, and found the two of them across the room, and did not smile, but her voice did something it almost never did—it warmed. "One method supplies the bones. One supplies the blood. Neither is recognizable without the other. Whoever you are"—and here several dozen heads turned, following her gaze, and Ivo felt the full weight of the town's attention land on them like a hand on the shoulder—"you should understand that you have made something neither of your families ever managed alone. Whether it places first or fifth today, that will remain true."

She stepped back behind the screen.

The pavilion erupted—not applause, not yet, but the roar of forty conversations igniting at once, and somewhere in it Ivo heard his own name and hers braided together, *Okonkwo-Montrose*, spoken by strangers like it was a word that had always existed.

Sena's grip on his sleeve had gone slack. She was staring at Marta's empty spot behind the screen with an expression he'd only seen once before—through the barn window, at dawn, after the night harvest, when she'd looked out at the picked-clean rows and realized they'd actually done it.

"Bones and blood," she said quietly.

"That's us?"

"That's us." She turned to him. "God help this valley."

He laughed—actually laughed, out loud, in public, a sound that startled a nearby child and, frankly, startled him more—and for about ninety seconds the world was simple: the wine was good, the town had seen it, and whatever came next, they'd made something true.

Which was, of course, when he saw Catherine Lindqvist slip out the side of the pavilion, phone to her ear, and stop beneath the big oak where a man in a gray suit was waiting beside a car Ivo recognized from the bank parking lot.

Halloran's car.

Lindqvist spoke for less than a minute. The man in the suit nodded twice, checked his watch, and got back in the car.

And across the green, pinned to the announcement board beside the cider stall—where it hadn't been twenty minutes ago—hung a fresh sheet of paper with the county seal at the top and, in bold type, a date and a time.

Tomorrow. Noon. The words **PUBLIC AUCTION** visible even from fifty feet away.

Sena followed his gaze. He watched her find the notice, watch her read it from across the green, watch the color leave her face in the exact order it had arrived.

"Ivo," she said. "What is that?"
