## 34. Judgment Day

**IVO**

The morning of judgment, I woke before the alarm and lay in the dark listening to the vineyard breathe.

That's a thing you learn, growing up in a place like this. The vines don't make noise exactly, but they have a sound—the creak of wire trellises cooling from the day's heat, the rustle of leaves when the valley air moves through. My father used to say you could hear whether the vines were happy. I'd thought it was nonsense for most of my life. Lately I wasn't so sure.

I dressed in the good shirt. The one Sena had ironed without being asked three days ago, hanging it on my barn door with a look that dared me to comment. I didn't comment. I was learning.

By seven I was at her kitchen table, because that had become the arrangement—coffee at her place, since she owned an actual coffee maker and not the camping contraption I'd been using since March. She slid a mug across the wood and sat down across from me, and we looked at each other over the steam like two people about to walk into an exam neither had studied for.

"Second place," she said.

"You said that last night."

"I'm saying it again so it doesn't feel like a hope. Hopes get people hurt." She wrapped both hands around her mug. "If we go in expecting second, then second is fine. And if it's first—"

"It won't be first."

"—then it's a miracle, and miracles make me nervous."

I almost laughed. Almost. My chest was too tight for laughing. "My father placed second here once," I heard myself say. "1998. He talked about it for a decade like it was a coronation."

Sena looked up sharply. "You never told me that."

"There's a lot I never told you." I meant the letters. I always meant the letters now; the sentence lived under my tongue like a stone I kept swallowing. "He framed the ribbon. It hung in the study until—" I stopped. Until the bank started sending letters. Until he took it down himself one night, and I found it in the trash the next morning, and neither of us ever spoke of it.

Sena reached across the table and put her hand over mine. Her fingers were cold from the morning, calloused from three weeks of shared work. I held still and let her.

"Whatever happens today," she said, "the wine was good. You know that, right? Whatever they say in that tent, the wine was *good*."

"I know."

"Say it back."

"The wine was good." My voice came out rougher than intended.

She nodded once, satisfied, and stood to rinse her mug, and that was how we went to judgment: two people who had learned to steady each other in small ways, walking side by side down a road that had divided our families for twenty years.

* * *

**SENA**

The festival grounds smelled like fried dough and cut grass and, underneath it all, the green-metallic tang of crushed grapes—a smell that hit me in the sternum and took me straight back to being nine years old, riding my father's shoulders above the crowd while he pointed out every farmer he'd ever traded seed with.

The judging tent was bigger than I remembered. White canvas, rows of folding chairs, a long table where the judges sat with their numbered glasses and their clipboards, faces arranged into expressions of professional neutrality. Mrs. Adeyemi from the festival committee stood at the front with a microphone she clearly didn't trust.

Ivo and I took seats near the back. Half the town was there—Mr. Calloway from the general store, the Henderson twins, old Pastor Webb, half the volunteer harvest crew from Tuesday night, dusty and sunburned and squinting at us like we were something they'd helped build. In a way, they had.

And near the front, alone, arms crossed: Whitfield. The developer. He'd come to watch us lose, and he wasn't even pretending otherwise. When he caught me looking, he smiled with his teeth and raised two fingers off his crossed arms—not quite a wave, not quite a salute. A promise.

I looked away. Found Ivo's hand instead. He laced his fingers through mine without looking, without hesitating, and I understood that this was also new—that a month ago he would have checked who was watching first.

Mrs. Adeyemi tapped the microphone. Feedback squealed. Two hundred people flinched.

"Good morning," she began, and then did the thing every committee chair does, which is thank approximately forty people by name while the crowd shifted and sweated. I squeezed Ivo's hand. He squeezed back. We endured.

Then: "Before we announce this year's placements, I want to say a word about the entries themselves."

Oh no. A word. Committees loved words.

"This year we received eleven entries, which is the most in fifteen years. But I want to speak particularly about entry number nine." She peered at her card. "A joint submission. Montrose Vineyard and the Okonkwo parcel, entered as a single cooperative blend."

The tent went quiet in a specific way. Not hostile quiet. *Attentive* quiet. I felt the whole room turn slightly toward the back row, toward us, and I felt Ivo go rigid beside me.

"That blend," Mrs. Adeyemi continued, "was made from fruit grown on two farms that haven't spoken in twenty years, using methods from two traditions that some folks in this valley thought were lost. The committee wants it noted that whatever the judges decided, this entry represents something this festival exists to celebrate."

She looked directly at us. "Well done, you two."

Applause broke out—uneven at first, then fuller, Mr. Calloway clapping with his big butcher hands, Pastor Webb nodding along, the harvest crew whistling. Ivo's grip on my hand tightened to something just short of painful. I leaned into his shoulder.

"Breathe," I murmured.

"I am breathing."

"You're performing breathing."

* * *

**IVO**

They announced tenth place. Ninth. Eighth. Each name a small mercy of omission—we were still standing.

Seventh. Sixth. Whitfield's smile hadn't moved. Fifth.

Fourth place went to the Delacroix brothers, who'd taken the ribbon four years running. Sena exhaled beside me, slow and controlled, and I realized she'd been holding her breath since third-to-last call.

Third place: the Hollis orchard cider, which wasn't even wine, which meant—

"Second place," Mrs. Adeyemi read, "goes to entry number nine. The Montrose–Okonkwo cooperative blend."

The tent erupted. Not politely—fully. The harvest crew was on its feet. Somebody whooped. I sat there stunned while Sena grabbed my arm with both hands and shook me, laughing, saying something I couldn't hear over the noise, and I thought absurdly of my father's ribbon in the trash, and of the shoebox of unsent letters, and of every October for ten years when I'd written to a stranger because the house was too quiet.

Second place. *Again.* The Montrose curse, except this time it wasn't a curse. This time it was a door.

Because here's what nobody outside the valley knew, what Sena and I had learned hunched over her kitchen table with a calculator two nights ago: the prize money for second place—$4,000—wasn't enough. Not for the equipment loan payments, not for the winter, not for either farm. First place would have covered the worst of it. Second place bought us time and nothing else.

But the prestige—Mrs. Adeyemi's word, though she'd never know how precisely she'd chosen it—the prestige was currency of a different kind. Valley Bank & Trust had told me, in the person of Mr. Okafor, that a festival placement counted as "demonstrated viability" for their agricultural cooperative program. A cooperative loan. Lower interest, longer terms, structured for farms doing it together rather than alone. The program required a placement. Any placement.

We had a placement.

Mr. Okafor himself found us after the ceremony, working through the crowd with his hand extended. "Congratulations. Both of you." He shook my hand, then Sena's, and held hers a beat longer. "Your father," he said to me, "banked with us thirty years. And your mother opened her first account with us at seventeen." To Sena. "I remember when this valley had both your families in it. Good to see the ledger correcting itself."

"We'll need the paperwork Monday," Sena said, all business, though her eyes were shining.

"You'll have it Friday. Come in together." He nodded at our joined hands as if confirming a line item, and moved off into the crowd.

Sena turned to me. "Did you hear that? Friday. Together. That's—"

"That's survival," I said. "Barely."

"Barely counts." She was grinning now, wide and unguarded, and I thought: I could live forty years on that grin. "Ivo. Barely *counts*."

* * *

**SENA**

We should have gone straight to celebrating. Everyone expected us to—the crew wanted to buy us drinks, Mrs. Adeyemi wanted photographs, Pastor Webb wanted to say a blessing over the vines that had produced "this miracle of reconciliation."

Instead I saw Whitfield slip out the back of the tent, and something in the set of his shoulders snagged on me like a briar.

He hadn't clapped. Through the entire announcement, through the applause, through Mr. Okafor's congratulations—he had stood with his arms crossed and his face arranged into patience. The patience of a man holding a card he hasn't played yet.

"Ivo." I tugged his sleeve. "Whitfield just left."

"He's had a bad day. Let him have it."

"No." I watched the tent flap settle behind him. "Men like him don't have bad days. They have *adjustments*. He came here expecting to watch us fail publicly, and instead we handed the bank a reason to say no to him. He's going to adjust."

Ivo followed my gaze to the empty flap. I watched him do the same math I was doing—the mortgage papers, the auction threat, the way Whitfield had smiled at the bank manager during the tasting, the conversation that had stopped when we approached.

"You think he's got something left," Ivo said quietly.

"I think men like him always have something left. I think the auction notice for my farm is dated for next Saturday, and I think a second-place ribbon doesn't cancel a foreclosure." I turned to face him fully. "We have five days, Ivo. The cooperative loan takes weeks to process. Even if Okafor fast-tracks it—and he might—it won't clear before that auction date. Whitfield knows that. That's why he's patient."

Ivo was silent for a long moment. Around us the festival roared on—music starting up on the bandstand, children shrieking through the games, the sweet smoke of the grill—but inside our small circle the air had gone still.

"Then we find another way," he said finally. "There's always another way."

"Is there?" I asked, and hated how small my voice came out. "Because I've spent ten years learning that sometimes there isn't. Sometimes the house gets sold anyway. Sometimes the family leaves anyway. Sometimes you write and write and nothing answers."

His face changed. Something flickered there—guilt, or grief, or recognition—and for a moment I thought he was going to say something, something important, something with weight behind it. His mouth opened.

"Ivo! Sena!" Mrs. Adeyemi bore down on us with a camera and two paper ribbons. "Photograph. Now. Before the light goes."

The moment folded itself away. We posed under the white tent with our second-place ribbons pinned crookedly to our shirts, smiling for a camera while somewhere beyond the canvas walls a patient man walked back to his car carrying an ace we couldn't see.

Later, much later, when the lanterns were lit and the music softened and Ivo walked me to the fence line where our two properties met, he stopped and faced me in the warm dark.

"Sena. There's something I need to tell you."

My heart did something complicated. "Okay."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. And then, from the direction of the road, headlights swept across the vineyard rows—a car pulling slowly past the festival grounds, slowing, stopping. Even at this distance I recognized the shape of it. Whitfield's car. Parked. Engine idling. Watching.

Ivo exhaled, and whatever the sentence was, he swallowed it back down.

"Tomorrow," he said. "It can wait until tomorrow."

But as we stood there in the dark, watching those headlights burn against the rows we'd nearly died to save, I had the distinct feeling that tomorrow was already on its way—and it wasn't coming to congratulate anyone.
