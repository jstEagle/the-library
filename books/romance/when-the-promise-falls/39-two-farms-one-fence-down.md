## 39. Two Farms, One Fence Down

**IVO**

The fence came down the way old things do—reluctantly at first, then all at once.

Ivo had walked that fence line his whole life. As a boy he'd ridden it on his father's shoulders, told it was the edge of the world and everything past it was someone else's problem. As a young man he'd leaned on it during the bad years, watching the Okonkwo farmhouse go soft at the edges like fruit left too long in the sun, and he'd said nothing to anyone about how much he hated looking at it.

Now he stood at the middle post with a pry bar in his hands and Sena beside him with a hammer, and the morning was cold enough to see their breath braiding together over the wire.

"You want to say something first?" she asked. "A eulogy? It's been in your family longer than either of us."

"It's been in *both* our families," Ivo said. "That's rather the point."

He set the bar under the bottom rail of the middle section—the section they'd argued over in October, when she'd accused him of letting it rot out of spite and he'd accused her of climbing it as a teenager, which she had, which he'd known because he'd watched her do it from his bedroom window more than once and never once told her so. Some confessions you save for the right season. This one, maybe, he'd never need.

The rail groaned. Gave. Came free with a sound like an old man clearing his throat after forty years of silence.

Sena laughed—a short, startled laugh, the kind that escapes before permission is granted—and swung her hammer at the next post cap. Dust jumped. A spider the size of a coin evacuated with what Ivo considered reasonable dignity.

"Careful," she said, breathless, hair escaping its knot. "You'll make me sentimental."

"Too late. You've been wiping your eyes since the first post."

"That was sawdust."

"It was not."

"It was *mostly* sawdust."

They worked down the line post by post, and Ivo found the rhythm of it was its own kind of conversation. Pull, pry, pass. She knew where every nail wanted to be coaxed; he knew which posts had concrete footings his father had poured the summer before everything went wrong, and those they dug out together, standing hip-deep in the same hole, passing the same shovel, close enough that her shoulder knocked his when she turned.

Neither of them moved away.

By noon the fence lay in sections along the grass like a sentence someone had finally finished writing. The two properties looked at each other across open ground for the first time in thirty years, and Ivo felt something in his chest unclench that he hadn't known was clenched—some posture his body had been holding since he was nine years old and learned that the word *neighbor* could be a wound.

Sena stood at the boundary, boots in the grass, and looked from her land to his and back.

"It looks bigger," she said quietly.

"It always was."

* * *

**SENA**

Her grandmother had planted the fence, or paid men to plant it, back when the Okonkwo farm was still called by its whole name and not by the bank's shorthand. Sena had grown up believing the fence was protection. Then she'd grown up further and understood it was a scar, and then further still and understood it was neither—it was just wood and wire, and the thing it divided had never actually been divided at all. The water table didn't care. The wind didn't care. The frost, that first terrible night in November, hadn't cared whose side of the line it landed on.

She'd spent twenty years away learning that lesson everywhere but here.

Now she knelt in the trench they'd dug along the old boundary, her hands around the roots of a vine that was half hers and half his in a way no document would ever capture—Ivo's cuttings, grafted using her grandmother's method onto rootstock from his father's block—and lowered it into the earth between them.

"Hold it straight," she said.

"I am holding it straight."

"You're holding it *politely*. Straighten your arms. Vines can tell when you're being careful instead of honest."

"That is not a real horticultural principle."

"It's my grandmother's principle, and your wine placed second at the festival using her principles, so." She let him have the grin. He took it the way he took most gifts now—with a small suspicious pause, as if checking it for fine print, and then all at once, completely.

They planted the row together through the afternoon. Twelve vines, six rooted on each side of a line that no longer existed except in the county records, spaced exactly as both their families had always spaced them, which turned out to be the same distance, because of course it was. The Montroses and the Okonkwos had learned from the same soil. They'd only ever disagreed about who owned the learning.

Halfway down the row, Ivo stopped, trowel in hand, and looked at her with an expression she'd come to know as the one that preceded either a joke or a truth.

"My father would have hated this," he said.

"I know."

"He'd have called it—" Ivo paused, searching, and she watched him decide not to use whatever bitter word he'd found. "He'd have had opinions."

"And yours?"

Ivo looked down the row—at the twelve new vines standing in their cold brown beds, at the open ground running unbroken from her porch to his, at the space where the fence had been and the light that came through now without asking anyone's permission.

"I think," he said slowly, "that he spent thirty years defending a border nobody was attacking, and it cost him the only ally he had. And I think I inherited the defense before I ever inherited the land." He drove the trowel into the soil, firm, final. "So. Yours?"

Sena thought about her mother's deed papers, folded soft as cloth in the drawer upstairs. Thought about the covenant clause, invoked in a bank office while two dead men's friend watched them with wet eyes. Thought about a shoebox of letters she'd read through one long night, laughing and crying, ten years of answers addressed to a girl who'd written to a house because she couldn't bear to write to a person.

"I think," she said, "my family kept the fence up too. We just did it from the other side."

They finished the row in silence—not the old silence, the armed kind, but the other one, the kind that has nothing left to prove.

When the last vine was in, Ivo stood, wiped his hands on his thighs, and offered her his arm with a formality so absurd it could only be sincere. Sena took it. They walked the length of their shared row like inspecting generals, or like a couple walking out a church door, and she decided there was no meaningful difference.

"One year," Ivo said. "If these take, we'll have grapes off this row next autumn."

"And if they don't?"

"Then we'll have twelve holes and a very good story."

"We already have the story."

"Yes," he said. "But I'm greedy now. Ten years of writing letters taught me to want sequels."

* * *

**IVO**

Dusk came early this time of year, blue and quick. They sat on the porch step of the farmhouse—the Okonkwo porch, though Ivo no longer thought of it as belonging to a category—with a bottle of the festival blend between them, unlabeled, saved from the second-place case. Second place. He'd spent his whole life thinking first place was the only grade that meant survival, and then a panel of judges had handed him silver and a cooperative loan officer had handed him a future, and he'd learned that barely was sometimes another word for enough.

Sena's shoulder rested against his. Below them, the new row caught the last light, twelve small sentinels standing watch over ground that belonged, finally, to no argument.

"Do you remember," she said, "what you wrote in the third letter? Year three?"

Ivo did. He remembered all of them, the way you remember the shape of your own handwriting, the way you remember the exact temperature of the room where you wrote things you couldn't say aloud. Year three: *the orchard on your side still drops apples into our ditch every fall and I've decided not to return them. Consider it interest on a debt I'm glad to owe.*

"You stole my apples," she said.

"For a decade."

"Ten years of apple theft, conducted entirely by correspondence, with the stolen goods left conspicuously in plain sight." She shook her head. "My mother used to collect them off the ditch bank and say, *someone over there has a conscience.*"

"She wasn't wrong."

"No," Sena said softly. "She wasn't. She just didn't know it had a name."

Ivo turned the bottle in his hands. There was a thing he'd been carrying since the lantern ceremony, since he'd said *stay* and she'd said *yes* before he finished the word, and it wasn't doubt exactly. Doubt would have been simpler. It was more like vertigo—the sudden understanding that the wall he'd been bracing against for ten years was gone, and his body hadn't yet learned what to do with all that open air.

"Sena."

"Mm."

"The fence is down. The loan is signed. Your mortgage is safe, my vineyard is solvent, the town has decided we exist." He counted them off against his knee like a man taking inventory. "Every problem we have left is the ordinary kind. Weather. Prices. Whether you're going to keep reorganizing my barn by a system only you understand."

"It's a *good* system."

"It's alphabetizing *soil*."

"Soil types, Ivo. It's not difficult."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into something quieter, and he set the bottle down in the grass and took her hand instead, because some sentences you rehearse for ten years and others you simply have to build as you go.

"I keep waiting for it to be taken back," he admitted. "All of it. The way it was taken from you. From both of us, really, in different currencies. I keep waking up at four in the morning expecting the bank, or the developer, or my father's ghost, to stand at the end of the row and explain the terms." His thumb moved over her knuckles. "And every morning it's just... morning. And the vines are just vines. And you're just there, making coffee in my kitchen like you've always—"

He stopped. Not because the sentence failed, but because it arrived complete, and completeness frightened him more than failure ever had.

Sena waited. She was good at waiting; she'd had a decade of practice writing into silence, and she'd sworn she'd never let him write into it again.

"Like I've always what?" she prompted, gently.

"Like you've always been the answer," Ivo said. "To a question I didn't know I was allowed to ask."

The dusk deepened around them. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and gave up, and the first star came out over the ridge with the punctuality of a thing that has never once doubted its place.

Sena lifted his hand and pressed it briefly against her cheek, and he felt her smile there before he saw it.

"Well," she said. "Ask it anyway. We've got time now. That's the whole point of what we built today—we've got *time*, and ground, and a row that belongs to both of us." She stood, pulling him up with her, and nodded toward the darkening fields, toward the open line where the fence used to stand. "Come on. Walk me home the long way. Past the orchard."

"The orchard's on your side."

"Not anymore," Sena said, and stepped off the porch into the open ground, into the space where the border used to be, and held out her hand for him to cross it.
