## 1. Counting Turtles

The trick to a small life, Isandro Vasquez had learned, was to make each step so small that no single one of them could be called a decision.

Wake at five. Coffee, black, in the dented blue mug he'd bought the week he arrived in Punta Clara and had never replaced because replacing it would mean admitting three years had passed. Drive the washboard road past the bait shop and the shuttered arcade to the sanctuary gate. Count the nests. Log the temperatures. Check the sump pumps. Feed the recovery patients—this morning, two green turtles named after weather systems and one obstinate Kemp's ridley who answered to nothing at all.

By seven-fifteen he had counted forty-one hatchlings from Nest Twelve, marked them in the ledger with his own handwriting, steady and unremarkable, and felt the particular satisfaction of a man whose day contained no surprises larger than a tide chart.

Forty-one. He wrote it down twice because the first entry smudged.

That was the whole architecture of it: numbers that added up, tasks that ended, creatures that asked nothing of him except consistency. Three years ago he had been Dr. Isandro Vasquez, rising star of the Delgado Marine Lab, publishing on nesting-site fidelity with a mentor's arm around his shoulders and a future so bright it had seemed almost rude. Now he was the guy at Punta Clara who knew every turtle by its tag number and no person by anything more intimate than their coffee order.

He'd stopped calling it exile around year two. He called it *a posting*. The way you'd call a scar a mark.

"Vasquez." Rosa, the sanctuary's other full-timer, appeared at the door of the counting shed holding her phone like it was ticking. "Your sister's called the landline. Twice. Then she called my cell, which means she's escalated."

Isandro did not look up from the ledger. "Tell her I'm elbow-deep in a turtle."

"She asked me what kind."

"Then tell her I'm elbow-deep in an *unidentified* turtle."

Rosa snorted and left. He lasted ninety seconds before he picked up his own phone, because Marisol did not do unanswered calls; she did sieges.

* * *

"Hermanito." Her voice came through warm and dangerous, the way it always did, like sunlight through a window with no screen. "You got the invitation."

"I got the invitation."

"And?"

"And it's very nice. Heavy cardstock. You spent money on cardstock."

"I spent *Dante's mother's* money on cardstock, which is even better. Isandro. Are you coming alone?"

He looked out the window of the counting shed at the flat gray-green water, at the safety of it. "I haven't RSVP'd yet."

"It's been eleven days. The RSVP date is Friday. Mamá has already started a spreadsheet."

"Mamá doesn't know how to use spreadsheets."

"Papá is doing it for her. There's a column for dietary restrictions and a column for *guests*, Isandro, and your row is empty and it's making them insane. Tía Carmen thinks you're depressed. Abuela thinks you're secretly married. Papá thinks you're gay, which, fine, but he's told everyone, so now there are *expectations*."

"I'm not—" He pinched the bridge of his nose. "Why does everyone need a theory? Can't I just be busy?"

There was a pause on the line, and in the pause he heard everything Marisol wasn't saying, because Marisol's silences were louder than most people's arguments. She knew. Not the details—he'd made sure of that, three years of careful, boring phone calls, *work's fine, the turtles are fine, I'm fine*—but she knew the shape of what he'd done, the way you know a house down the street burned even if you never saw the flames.

"You can be busy," she said finally, gentler now, which was worse. "But you're going to stand at my wedding alone, and Mamá will spend the entire reception introducing you to the daughter of someone's dentist, and you'll do that thing where you smile like a customer service representative, and I will have to watch it happen at *my wedding*, Isandro, while wearing a dress I cannot run in."

"That dress has pockets, Mari. I saw the photos."

"The dress is irrelevant. Focus." A breath. "Are you seeing anyone? Real answer. Not the answer you give Mamá."

The honest answer was *no*. The honest answer was *not since Elena, not since before, not since I learned what it costs to trust someone with the things that matter*. But honesty, Isandro had found, was a luxury item, and he'd been living lean for three years.

So instead he said—and later he would replay this moment many times, usually at two in the morning, wincing—

"Yeah. Actually. There's someone."

The silence that followed had texture. It had *grain*.

"There's *someone*," Marisol repeated.

"We've been—it's new-ish. A few months. It's low-key."

"Low-key," she said. "You. Low-key. Isandro, you color-coded your study flashcards in high school. You once broke up with a girl because she double-booked a Tuesday. Who is low-key with *you*?"

"Someone who appreciates structure," he said, hearing himself dig, hearing the hole get deeper, and unable to stop, because the alternative was the conversation underneath this conversation, the one about why a thirty-four-year-old marine biologist lived alone above a bait shop and flinched when people said the name Delgado. "Look, I'll bring her. To the wedding. If—if that's—"

"*If that's—*" Marisol made a sound like a kettle achieving enlightenment. "You'll BRING HER. Oh my God. Okay. Okay okay okay. What's her name?"

And here was the flaw in the plan, the load-bearing wall he hadn't checked: he had built the lie out of pure reflex, no blueprint, no materials. His mind went white. It swept the town of Punta Clara for a name, any name, and landed—because his mind had apparently been waiting months for an excuse—on the only person in this town who was interesting.

"Wren," he said. "Wren Okafor."

Another silence. Longer. He could hear Marisol recalibrating, running the name through the family database.

"The vet? The one who fixed Señora Ibarra's dog and then told Señora Ibarra she was overfeeding it?"

"That's—that's the one."

"The one who told Father Benítez his 'miracle' hermit crab just needed a bigger shell?"

"She's very direct," Isandro said, with a pride he made no effort to disguise and then immediately regretted, because Marisol pounced on it like a gull on an unattended fry.

"Ohhhhh," she said. "*Ohhhh.* You LIKE her. This isn't a plus-one, this is a CRUSH. Isandro Vasquez, you absolute disaster, you've been sitting on a crush for MONTHS—"

"It's not a crush. We're—we're seeing each other. Low-key."

"You said that already and it convinced no one. Fine. FINE. Bring Wren Okafor, the hermit crab truther, to my wedding. Rehearsal dinner's Friday night, ceremony Saturday at four, and Mamá will want to meet her before that, so don't even think about arriving late. And Isandro?" Her voice went soft again, the dangerous kind of soft. "She better be real."

"She's real," he said, and hung up before his sister—who had known him for thirty-four years and could smell a lie through drywall—could ask a single follow-up question.

* * *

He stood in the doorway of the counting shed for a long time afterward, ledger forgotten under his arm, watching the tide come in over the flats the way it had come in every day of his carefully small life.

Here was the thing about lies: he was good at them now. That was the part nobody warned you about. Lying was a skill like any other, and he'd had three years of practice—the cheerful *everything's great* to his family, the vague *left for personal reasons* to anyone who asked about the lab, the nightly performance of being a man at peace. He'd gotten so good that the lies came out smooth as sea glass, worn into something you could hold without cutting yourself.

*She's real.* Smooth as glass.

Except.

Except that Wren Okafor *was* real, that was the problem, that was the trap he'd walked into with both eyes open. Wren Okafor was real the way a riptide was real. She'd shown up eight months ago to treat a boat-strike loggerhead, took one look at his intake paperwork, and said, "Whoever filled this out alphabetizes their sock drawer, huh?" and he had—God help him—*laughed*, out loud, in front of a colleague, like a person. Since then she'd been at the sanctuary maybe twice a month, stitching flippers and draining abscesses and insulting his coffee, and every visit left him feeling like he'd been gently mugged. She noticed things. She said true things. She was, in short, the single worst possible person to construct a fiction around, because Wren Okafor audited fictions for a living.

He could call Marisol back. Say he'd exaggerated. Say there was no Wren-and-him, never had been. Twenty minutes of humiliation, one spreadsheet cell deleted, crisis contained.

Or.

Or he could walk down to the clinic cottage this afternoon, where Wren was due to re-check the loggerhead's sutures, and ask the sharpest-tongued woman on the coast to spend a weekend lying to his entire extended family, including an abuela with a reputation for extracting confessions from hardened criminals, or at least from Isandro, age nine, re: the missing flan.

It was a terrible idea. It was the kind of idea that ended careers, or at least ended the quiet, defensible, numbingly safe arrangement he'd spent three years building out of routine and distance. Every instinct he'd honed since Delgado—the instinct that said *want less, risk less, be less, and nothing can be taken from you*—screamed against it.

But he thought about Marisol's voice cracking on *while wearing a dress I cannot run in*. He thought about his mother's spreadsheet, and his father's hopeful theories, and the empty row with his name on it. He thought about standing at the edge of a dance floor alone for the fourth wedding in a row, smiling his customer-service smile, dying by degrees in a room full of people who loved him.

And beneath all of it, quieter, in the place he didn't look at directly: he thought about Wren's hands on the loggerhead's shell that first day, sure and unhurried, and how for one stupid shining second he'd wanted to be the kind of man those hands might reach for.

The tide slid in over the flats, patient, inevitable, indifferent to plans.

"Okay," Isandro said to no one, to the water, to whatever was left of his common sense. "Small steps."

He grabbed his keys.

* * *

The clinic cottage sat at the far end of the sanctuary grounds, a converted boathouse with a rusted weathervane shaped like a marlin that pointed in whatever direction gossip flowed. Through the open door he could hear her voice before he saw her—low, dry, addressed to the loggerhead on the table.

"No offense, big guy, but you have got to stop dating boats."

Isandro stopped on the threshold, ledger clutched to his chest like a shield, heart doing something unsanctioned, and realized with perfect clarity that he was about to make the largest step of his last three years.

He knocked on the doorframe anyway.
