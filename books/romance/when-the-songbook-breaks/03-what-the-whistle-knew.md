## 3. What the Whistle Knew

The storm came in off the water like something with a grudge, and Wren watched it swallow the harbor lights one by one from the tall windows of the festival hall. Inside, the air smelled of dust and old varnish and the ghost of a thousand performances — the particular hush of a room built for listening, now holding only the two of them and the last unstacked chairs.

"We should go before the roads flood," Silas said, though he made no move toward the door. He stood at the edge of the stage with his hands in his pockets, looking up at the rigging where the banners would hang in three weeks' time. The revival's banners. Her revival. His planning.

"The ferry's already stopped running." She said it lightly, as if it were nothing, as if her heart weren't climbing her ribs like ivy. "We're marooned."

He turned to look at her then, and in the blue-gray light of the storm-darkened windows his face was unreadable — that careful, weathered stillness he wore like a coat. Ten years of letters had taught her every variation of his handwriting but none of his expressions. She had imagined this face a hundred times: older, guarded, lit by some small lamp in a rented room while he read what she'd written and did not know who wrote it.

She had never imagined she'd be standing in the hall itself when he learned the truth.

"Marooned," he repeated. The word sat strangely in his mouth.

Thunder rolled down the coast, long and low, and the first hard rain began against the roof — a sound Wren knew intimately, because she had been listening to recordings of this hall for a decade. Rain on this roof was on the archival tapes. Rain on this roof had been falling over stolen rehearsals and secret auditions and a man named Aldous Vane teaching children to sing with their whole bodies and then taking payment in ways no child should ever have to understand.

She had been fourteen when she met him. So had Silas.

"Wren." His voice was quiet under the rain. "There's something you're not saying. You've been not saying it since the day you hired me."

* * *

She had rehearsed this confession for ten years, which meant she had never once said it aloud, which meant all her rehearsals were worthless. Words she had polished in the dark of a hundred rented rooms — *I wrote them, I wrote every one, I'm the songbook* — dissolved on her tongue like sugar in rain.

Instead she walked to the piano at the side of the stage, the old upright that had survived everything, and lifted the lid. Inside, wedged against the frame where she'd left it that afternoon, was the folder. Tenth anniversary. She had told herself she'd know the moment. She had told herself a lot of things.

"What's that?" Silas asked.

"You already know," she said. "Some part of you has known since the cottage. Since you saw me through the window with the boxes."

He didn't deny it. He came down the stage steps slowly, each footfall a small percussion in the empty hall, and took the folder from her hands. Their fingers touched. Neither of them pulled away.

He opened it.

Inside were the letters — not copies. The originals. Every draft of every letter she had ever sent him, ten years of them, in her own hand, crossed out and rewritten, the ink of grief changing color as the years passed, blue to black to a gray pencil she'd used the year she couldn't afford anything else. He looked at the first page for a long time. Then the second. Then he closed the folder very gently, the way you close something that might be wounded.

"Read the top one," she whispered. "The first draft. Please."

He did. And she watched him read the words she had written at nineteen, shaking, in a motel outside the courthouse during the trial: *I don't know how to tell you this without losing you, so I'll tell you this way instead, every year, until I'm brave enough or you're gone.*

The rain thickened. Somewhere in the walls the old building groaned and settled, like a sleeper turning over.

"I testified," Wren said. The words came out of her at last, flat and terrible, like stones dropping into deep water. "Against Aldous. I was the one who went to the prosecutors. My testimony is what ended him — what ended the festival, what ended everything. And my testimony is also what kept them from charging you, because I told them what he did to you too, Silas. I told them everything. I saved you." Her voice cracked. "I saved you without asking. Without your consent. I decided your survival for you, and then I hid behind unsigned paper for ten years because I couldn't bear to watch you hate me for it."

Silas hadn't moved. The folder hung in his hands. Outside, lightning turned the windows white, and in that flash she saw his face completely — not anger, not yet, something worse, something being dismantled.

"The songbook," he said. His voice was almost steady. Almost. "Ten years. Ten years of — I built my whole life around those letters. Around someone who *saw*. Someone who understood what it cost. And it was you. It was you standing thirty feet away from me in a planning meeting, watching me arrange chairs, letting me—"

"Letting you what?" she asked, though she knew. God, she knew.

"Letting me fall in love with you," he said, "while you knew exactly why I stopped singing."

* * *

The hall felt different after that — smaller, colder, the way a room feels when a window has been opened in winter. Wren wrapped her arms around herself and kept talking, because if she stopped she would never start again, and if she never started again she would carry this into the ground.

"He groomed us both. You know that. You've always known that, even if you never say his name. He picked us out at fourteen because we were hungry and poor and talented enough to be worth the trouble. He called it mentorship. He called it love. He called it *investing*." She heard her own voice shake and let it. "I didn't testify to be brave. I want you to understand that. I testified because I found out you weren't the only one — there was a boy from the 2009 cohort, and another girl, and I couldn't—"

"Don't." One word, and it stopped her like a hand on the chest.

Silas set the folder down on the piano lid. He did it carefully, precisely, the way he did everything, and that precision was the most frightening thing she had ever seen — the control of a man holding himself together with both hands.

"You could have told me," he said. "At any point. Before you hired me. Before the meetings. Before—" He stopped. Ran a hand over his mouth. When he spoke again his voice was lower, rougher. "Do you know what those letters were? They were the only thing that made what happened survivable. Once a year, someone out there remembered. Someone out there thought I mattered more than the scandal. I used to sit on my kitchen floor and read them and think, *someone knows, someone chose me.* And it was you. It was guilt. I was your penance."

"No." She stepped toward him. "No, Silas, listen—"

"I sang for him!" The words tore out of him louder than the thunder, and the hall took them and gave them back, multiplied, filling every corner — the first time in ten years she had heard his voice raised, and it was magnificent and ruined and breaking. "I gave him my whole voice, my whole *life*, and when it all burned down everyone looked at me like I was the ash. And the one person who knew — the one person who was *in* it with me — spent a decade writing me anonymous mercy instead of knocking on my door!"

"Because I was afraid!"

"Because you wanted to be forgiven without having to ask!"

The silence afterward was enormous. The rain filled it, drumming on the roof, on the windows, streaming down the glass in silver ropes. Wren realized she was crying, had probably been crying for some time, and that Silas was looking at her tears the way a man looks at evidence.

"I loved you," she said. "Before the trial, during it, after it. I loved you in every letter. That part was never penance. That part was the truest thing I've ever done."

Something moved across his face — grief, maybe, or its close cousin — and then he stepped back, once, twice, putting distance between them like a man retreating from a shoreline before the wave comes.

"I can't do this here," he said quietly. "I can't breathe in this building."

"Silas—"

He walked past her, down the center aisle between the stacked chairs, and pushed open the great front doors into the storm. Rain swept in across the threshold, spattering the floorboards, and then he was in it — coatless, head bowed, wading through the downpour toward the dark line of the road, getting smaller and smaller until the night took him.

Wren stood in the doorway with the wind tearing at her hair and did not call after him. Calling after him would have been another decision made on his behalf, and she had finally, finally learned the cost of those.

Behind her, on the piano, the folder lay open to the first draft, the ink blurred where the storm-damp had reached it, her nineteen-year-old handwriting bleeding softly at the edges:

*I don't know how to tell you this without losing you.*

She had known, even then. She had always known.

Three weeks remained until closing night. And somewhere out there in the rain, walking away from her, was the only audience whose verdict had ever mattered.
