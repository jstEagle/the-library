## 4. Closing Night

The hall filled the way water fills a basin — slowly at first, a trickle of early arrivals testing the seats, and then all at once, a rising tide of coats and laughter and programs rustling open. Silas stood in the wings with his hands in his pockets and watched it happen through the gap in the curtain, and he thought: *Aldous would have hated this.* Not the crowd — Aldous had lived for crowds — but the honesty of it. No velvet myth, no gilded legend of the Eastwick Songbook Festival. Just folding chairs, a rented soundboard, a banner Wren had sewn herself because the budget wouldn't stretch to printing, its letters slightly crooked: *THE SONGBOOK RETURNS.*

He had spent seven years building a life out of silence the way other men built houses out of stone. Careful walls. Load-bearing absences. And now here was a full house, four hundred people who had come back to a thing that had broken them all in different ways, and Silas could feel every wall he'd ever built groaning under the weight of what he intended to do tonight.

Wren was not in the hall.

He had looked for her twice — once from the wings, once from the soundboard where Theo fussed over levels — and both times come up empty. Her seat, third row center, the one she'd argued for and then refused to sit in during rehearsals ("I want to see it the way they'll see it"), sat vacant. Her coat was gone from the hook by the stage door.

The program told him why before his heart did. He'd found the folded page an hour ago, tucked into his clipboard where she must have slipped it sometime that afternoon while he was arguing with the caterers about the placement of the coffee urns. Her handwriting — the handwriting he had memorized across ten letters, ten anniversaries, a decade of ink on cheap stationery — slanted across the page in lines that shook only at the end:

*Silas —*

*You don't owe me a stage. You never owed me anything; that's rather the whole point of everything I did wrong. The festival is yours now, truly yours, and I won't stand in the audience of your life pretending I have a right to be there. I've packed the car. By the time you read this I'll be gone, and you can sing or not sing, speak or not speak, and either way it will be the truest thing in the room.*

*I loved you in letters because I couldn't say it to your face. That cowardice is mine to keep. Don't forgive me out loud. Forgive me quietly, if at all, and live loudly, and that will be enough.*

*— the songbook*

He had read it four times. On the fourth reading he noticed she hadn't said goodbye, because Wren Calloway understood, better than anyone alive, that some words are doors you can't close behind you.

* * *

The first half passed like weather. A youth choir sang arrangements Aldous had written when he still believed music could save people without costing them. An old cellist, one of the original festival faculty, played a piece so quiet that the whole hall leaned forward to hold it up. Between sets, Theo found Silas in the wings and pressed a bottle of water into his hand and said nothing, which was its own kind of speech between them after these weeks of planning meetings and storm-trapped confessions and the long silence since.

"She's really gone?" Theo asked finally.

"Her car's not in the drive."

"You could let her go."

"I know." Silas turned the water bottle over in his hands. "That's what makes it a choice."

Intermission ended. The hall resettled. And then there was nothing left between Silas and the microphone except the walk across the stage, which was perhaps forty feet long and had taken him seven years to cross.

He had rehearsed the sentence. God help him, he had rehearsed it — in the shower, on the cliff path, into the dark of his bedroom ceiling at three in the morning — the way you rehearse testimony, the way you rehearse a confession you're not sure you'll survive giving. He had built it out of ten years of unsent replies, every letter he'd started and abandoned, every word he'd swallowed on the anniversary mornings when he read her handwriting and felt his throat close around a voice he no longer trusted.

He stepped into the light.

The hall went still the way rooms do when they recognize something — not applause, not yet, just the collective intake of four hundred people seeing a man they had only ever known as a name attached to a scandal, standing upright, walking toward a microphone as though his legs remembered how.

"Good evening," Silas said.

His voice came out rough. Ten years of disuse will do that; so will fear. But it came out. It carried. Somewhere in the third row a woman put her hand over her mouth.

"For those who don't know me — most of you, I hope — my name is Silas Eastwick. I was supposed to be a singer. Aldous Merrick said I had the kind of voice that made people believe things, and for a long time I believed that was a gift." He paused. His hands were shaking. He let them shake. "It wasn't a gift. It was a leash. And Aldous held it."

No one moved. The crooked banner hung above him like a small brave flag.

"I'm not going to tell you the whole story tonight. The story is long, and it belongs to more people than me, and some of them aren't ready. What I'm going to tell you is this: the man who founded this festival hurt people. He hurt me. He hurt someone else whose name I will not say without her permission, but whose courage saved my life without asking me first — and I spent ten years being angry about that, about having my survival decided for me, until I understood that anger was the last room Aldous ever built for me, and I was living in it rent-free."

He looked out at the faces. Somewhere near the back, the old cellist nodded, slow and grave.

"There is a sentence I have been rehearsing for months," Silas said. "I wrote it down and threw it away and wrote it again. I said it to my bathroom mirror like a madman. Because saying it here means it's real, and real things can't be taken back, and I have spent a decade mistaking untakeable-back for unbearable."

He closed his eyes. Opened them.

"Wren Calloway testified against the man who was destroying us both. She did it badly, and secretly, and out of love, and it cost her everything she had, including me. She wrote me letters for ten years signed 'the songbook' because she thought the songbook was the only part of me left that would listen. She was wrong about that. She was wrong about almost everything except the one thing that mattered." His voice cracked, and he let it. "She was right that I was worth saving."

The hall held its breath.

"So here is the sentence. Here it is, unrehearsed now, ruined forever, mine." He gripped the edges of the podium. "Wren — wherever your car has gotten to on whatever road you took out of town — I forgive you. Not quietly. Out loud, in front of everyone, on the record, in the place where our worst story happened. I forgive you, and I love you, and I am done letting the past decide who gets to stand next to me. Come home. Or don't. But know that the door isn't locked anymore. It hasn't been locked since the day you moved in next door and I watched you unpack a decade of my own history through your kitchen window and couldn't look away."

He stepped back from the microphone. The silence lasted three seconds, four — long enough for doubt to get a foothold — and then the hall broke open. Applause first, and then something louder than applause, people on their feet, the old cellist weeping openly into her bow hand, and Theo at the soundboard with his head down and his shoulders shaking.

And beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, the sound of the stage door at the back of the hall opening — and closing — and footsteps in the aisle, unhurried, certain, coming toward the light.
