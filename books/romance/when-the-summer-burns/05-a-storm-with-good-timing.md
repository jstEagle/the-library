## 5. A Storm with Good Timing

The sky had been lying to me all morning.

It wore that flat, pearl-gray expression of a Central Coast November—harmless on its face, the kind of day that made tourists say *isn't it lovely* while locals squinted at the ridgeline and said nothing. I'd grown up reading this particular gray. My father used to call it *la barba del cielo*, the sky's beard, and he claimed it meant nothing worse than rain. But there was a weight to it today, a low sag in the clouds over the Santa Lucias, and when I stepped out of the farmhouse at seven a.m. and smelled the air—cold iron and something almost sweet, like snow before snow—I felt the first prickle of dread walk up my spine.

Jonah Reyes was due at ten.

We had negotiated nothing in three weeks of phone calls. His press was newer, his crusher-destemmer had the capacity I lacked, and my cellar had temperature control his didn't, and between those two facts lay the only viable path through this winter for either of us—and between us lay eleven years of silence, one catastrophic summer, and a bidding war at the growers' auction that had ended with him paying four hundred dollars too much for a barrel of yeast just because I wanted it.

He'd called it *market forces*. I'd called it pettiness. We had agreed, through gritted teeth, to meet in person and try to be adults about equipment.

I spent the hours before he arrived doing what I always did when I couldn't stand still: walking the rows. The vines were bare now, pruned back to their winter skeletons, black arms against the pale grass, patient as monks. Tomás was already out there with his shears, moving down row fourteen with the unhurried precision of a man who had been pruning these same vines since before I was born.

"Storm coming," he said, without looking up.

"You think?"

"I *know*. Smell it?" He snipped a cane, held it up to the light like a jeweler assessing a stone. "Your father would have already moved the tractors under cover."

"My father also thought the '87 Merlot would win gold," I said, and immediately regretted it, because grief still lived in my mouth sometimes and fell out sideways as cruelty.

Tomás didn't take offense. He never did. "The '87 Merlot *should* have won gold," he said mildly. "The judges were idiots." He went back to his cutting. "Move the tractors, Rosalind."

I moved the tractors.

* * *

Jonah's truck came up the drive at 9:52, eight minutes early, which annoyed me more than lateness would have. It was a big gray diesel, mud-splattered to the windows, and it looked exactly like him—too much machine for the job, running anyway. He climbed out in a canvas jacket I recognized and hated myself for recognizing. He'd bought it the summer we were twenty-four, from a thrift store in Paso Robles, and he'd worn it every day until it fit him like a second skin, and apparently he had never thrown it away, and apparently neither had I thrown away the memory of taking it off him.

"Quintana," he said.

"Reyes."

"Weather's turning."

"So I've heard."

We stood in the courtyard between the farmhouse and the crush pad, two professionals conducting a professional meeting, and if you'd photographed us you'd have seen nothing but two winemakers with clipboards. If you'd listened closely you'd have heard both our hearts going like threshing machines.

"The cellar," I said, and led the way.

Here is what nobody tells you about old love: it doesn't die. It goes into barrels. It sits in the dark, in oak, doing slow chemistry, and every year it changes—harshness mellowing, sugar fading out, tannins softening or hardening depending on conditions—and then some idiot opens it up and pours a glass and finds out that what's in there now is not what went in, but it is unmistakably, chemically, the same wine.

We walked the cellar together. I showed him the tank room, the press, the glycol system my father installed in the nineties that still ran like a Swiss watch. Jonah put his hands on the press plate and looked at it the way a carpenter looks at good joinery, and for a moment—for one unguarded moment—he looked like the man who had once stood in this same cellar at midnight, barefoot, our first co-ferment bubbling away, and said, *Rosalind, listen, it sounds like rain.* Then he straightened up and became Jonah Reyes, of Reyes Family Vineyards, again.

"Your glycol compressor is original," he said. "What happens when it dies mid-ferment?"

"It won't."

"Everything dies mid-ferment eventually."

"Then I'll fix it."

"With what money?"

And there it was. The bank's name hanging in the air between us, unsaid but loud. He knew about the loan—he knew everything; small towns are telegraphs with legs—and he had come here knowing it, and he was going to use it, and I was going to let him, because he was right.

"Name your terms," I said.

We sat across from each other at the tasting bar in the empty tasting room, a legal pad between us, and for about forty minutes we were almost civilized. He'd share the crusher; I'd share cellar space and cold storage. We argued about scheduling, about sanitation protocols, about whose yeast protocol was less insane, and it was almost—almost—like working with a colleague. There were even moments where we laughed, short startled laughs, like people surprised by a familiar smell.

Then he wrote a number on the legal pad and turned it toward me, and the number was for equipment access fees, and the number was insulting.

"This is a charity rate," he said. "Given your situation."

My pen stopped moving. "My situation."

"Your debt load. Your deadline. I'm pricing accordingly."

"You're pricing because you can."

"I'm pricing because last month someone bid against me on a barrel of yeast they didn't need, driving the price up four hundred dollars, purely out of spite."

"That was *you*—"

"And yet somehow," he said, leaning back, arms crossing, that infuriating half-smile arriving like weather, "we both ended up paying for it."

I stood up so fast the stool went over backward. Somewhere in the walls the old house ticked and settled, and outside the light through the tasting room windows had gone strange—darker than noon should be, bruised, the color of a storm cloud's underside.

"Get out," I said.

"Gladly." He gathered his jacket. "This was productive."

"This was *nothing*."

"This," he said, pausing at the door, looking back at me with something that wasn't quite anger and wasn't quite anything else, "was the most honest conversation we've had in eleven years."

* * *

He didn't make it off the property.

I was in the kitchen twenty minutes later, drinking coffee I didn't taste and reheating arguments in my head like leftovers, when Marisol came in from the barn at a jog, which Marisol never did, because Marisol considered jogging undignified.

"Rosa. You need to see the sky."

The sky had gone the color of wet slate, and it was full of birds—hundreds of them, starlings and cowbirds wheeling south in ragged rivers—and the wind had swung around to the north and gone cold enough to bite. And falling out of that slate ceiling, fat and slow and utterly wrong for California in November, was snow.

Not flurries. Snow. Real snow, the kind I'd only ever seen on television and once, memorably, on a family trip to Tahoe when I was nine. It came down in sheets, in curtains, thickening by the minute, erasing the ridge, erasing the far vineyard blocks, erasing the road.

Dev appeared from the equipment shed, face split in an enormous grin. "It's *snowing!* Do you understand? It's SNOWING! In CALIFORNIA!"

"Get the pipes wrapped," I said. "Insulate the pump house. Tomás—the frost fans, check the fuel. Marisol, get everyone inside in an hour whether we're done or not."

They scattered, laughing, treating it like a festival, and I suppose part of me did too—a wild, superstitious part that remembered being nine years old and catching snowflakes on my tongue. But another part of me stood on the porch watching the road disappear and thought about a gray diesel truck, and about how the pass over the hill was the only way out, and about how the county hadn't plowed that road before noon in the best of years.

By two o'clock there were six inches on the crush pad. By three, the power flickered for the first time—a brownout dip that made the cellar lights stutter like a held breath—and Tomás called the county road department and got a recording. The pass was closed. Everything was closed. A storm system the radio called *historic* and *unprecedented for early season* had parked itself over the coastal range and intended to stay awhile.

Marisol found me staring out the window at the white world.

"He left at ten-thirty," she said. Not a question. She'd seen the truck go up toward the pass.

"He tried to leave."

"Mm." She handed me a mug of something with cinnamon in it. "So he's in the bunkhouse."

I turned. "What?"

"Dev saw the truck stuck at the bend by the oaks. Wheels spinning. He walked back up to the bunkhouse before he froze. Dev gave him blankets." She sipped her own drink, eyes bright with a mischief I distrusted completely. "He's been there an hour. He hasn't asked to use the phone even once. Though the phones are dead, so."

"Marisol."

"Yes, Rosa."

"Do not."

"I haven't said anything."

"You're saying it with your entire face."

She smiled, patted my arm, and left me alone with the snow and the cinnamon and the sinking, swooping, furious knowledge that Jonah Reyes was forty yards away, snowbound, in my bunkhouse, indefinitely.

* * *

Dinner was an event, because dinner was always an event in my house, because my crew refused to let me eat alone, which was one of the thousand ways they kept me alive without ever saying so. We crowded into the farmhouse kitchen—Tomás, Marisol, Dev, and, at the far end of the table, sitting with the wary posture of a man in enemy territory, Jonah.

Dev interrogated him about soil microbiology for an hour. Tomás told stories about the '87 Merlot and the idiot judges, and Jonah—traitor—laughed in the right places and asked real questions and clearly knew who Tomás was, or knew *of* him, because every old-timer in the valley knew Tomás. Marisol refilled plates like a general supplying a front. And I sat at the head of my own table feeling like a hostage at a dinner party, watching the man I'd ordered off my property charm my family, and hating how easily they liked him, and hating more how easily I did.

At nine, the power died for good.

The well pump went silent. The refrigerator hummed itself into stillness. The house dropped into a darkness so complete it rang, and then Marisol was lighting candles with the efficiency of long practice, and Dev was digging out the oil lamps, and the kitchen came back to life in amber pools, softer and older, a kitchen from a hundred years ago.

"The generator will kick in," I said.

"The generator," Tomás said gently, "has been asking to retire since 2015."

I closed my eyes. Of course. Of course the generator was dead. Of course the roads were closed. Of course the storm of the century had arrived with the timing of a farce, sealing me into a dark house with Jonah Reyes and no electricity and no exit and no possible version of this that ended quietly.

"I'll sleep in the bunkhouse," Jonah said, standing, gathering his jacket. "Thank you for dinner. All of you."

"There's a candle in your room," Marisol said. "And extra blankets. And Rosa, show him where the woodpile is, the fireplace in the bunkhouse draws funny if you don't know it."

"I do not need to—"

"It's thirty degrees and dropping," I said. "You'll freeze. Come on."

* * *

The bunkhouse was one room with a potbelly stove, a cot, a shelf of Tomás's western novels, and a window full of snow. I showed him the stove, the damper, the trick of the draw—open the flue all the way for the first ten minutes or the whole room filled with smoke—and he crouched next to me at the stove door, close enough that I could feel the heat coming off him as well as the fire, and neither of us moved away, and the silence stretched out like a wire.

"Your crew," he said finally, "is remarkable."

"They're staying through winter without pay they may never see. Remarkable isn't the word I'd choose. Reckless, maybe."

"Loyal."

"Loyal to a losing cause."

He looked at me then, firelight on his face, and said, quietly, "I don't think you believe that."

"Excuse me?"

"The vineyard. You called it a losing cause. You don't believe it. You walked those rows today like—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Never mind."

"No. Say it."

"Like a woman guarding something alive." He fed a split of oak into the stove. "Not a balance sheet."

Outside, the wind rose and the snow came harder, hissing against the window, and the stove ticked and breathed, and I stood there in a bunkhouse that smelled of woodsmoke and old novels and, faintly, horribly, of that summer, because memory is a scent and it lives in things.

"Ten-thirty," I said. "You were really going to drive over the pass in that."

"I was really going to get as far away from you as the gas tank allowed."

"Honest."

"You asked for honest." He sat back on his heels. "You always did hate it, though."

"I don't hate honesty. I hate *weaponized* honesty. There's a difference."

"Is there." He stood, brushed off his knees, and looked at me across the small hot room, and the resentment in his face was curdling into something else, something wearier and more dangerous. "Eleven years, Rosalind. You want to talk about weapons? You left without—"

"Don't."

"—one word. One note. I woke up and you were gone, and your father told me you'd taken a job in Oregon, and I believed him for two years, and then I found out there was no job, there was nothing, there was just you deciding I was—"

"*Don't.*"

The word came out cracked. He heard it. I heard it. The stove popped, throwing sparks, and somewhere above us the storm leaned its whole weight on the roof, and we stood three feet apart in the firelight with eleven years between us and nowhere either of us could go.

Jonah exhaled slowly. Ran a hand through his hair. When he spoke again his voice had dropped its armor, mostly, and what was underneath was tired.

"Truce," he said. "For the duration of the storm. Professional courtesy. We'll figure out the equipment thing tomorrow, on paper, like adults."

"A truce."

"One glass of whatever terrible wine you keep in the farmhouse for emergencies, and a ceasefire, and in the morning we pretend none of this happened."

"I have excellent wine."

"You have '87 Merlot that lost to idiots, and whatever's open in the fridge, which has been above forty degrees for two hours. So. Terrible wine."

Despite myself—despite everything—the laugh escaped before I could catch it. He caught it instead. Something in his face shifted when he heard it, some door opening a crack after years locked, and I turned quickly toward the door before he could see what his hearing my laugh did to mine.

"One glass," I said. "Then I'm going to bed, and tomorrow we negotiate like adults."

"Like adults," he agreed.

I paused at the door, snow-light blue behind me, firelight orange ahead, and heard myself ask, before I could stop it:

"Why did you bid against me at the auction? Four hundred dollars. For yeast. Why?"

Jonah looked at me for a long moment, and the firelight moved in his eyes, and he smiled—not the armored smile, the other one, the one from the summer, the one I had spent a decade trying to forget the shape of.

"Because you wanted it," he said. "Some things don't age out, Rosalind. They just go into the barrel."

I left before he could see my face. Behind me the stove hummed, the storm pressed down, and the whole vineyard lay buried and silent under a snowfall no one could remember, holding its breath—like me—waiting to find out what the morning would thaw.
