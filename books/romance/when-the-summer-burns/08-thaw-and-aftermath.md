## 8. Thaw and Aftermath

Jonah woke before the light did, which was how he knew it was time to go.

The fire in the great room had burned down to a red memory, and the house around him was silent except for the small sounds a cold building makes when it's thinking—timber contracting, wind testing the eaves, snow sliding off the roof in soft collapses. He lay on the couch with his boots still on, because he'd never intended to sleep, and stared at the ceiling beams while his body catalogued everything that had happened in the last twelve hours.

Her mouth. Her hands fisted in his shirt. The way she'd said *I'm sorry* like it cost her a kidney. The way she'd pulled back afterward and looked at him with an expression he recognized from ten years ago—the look of a woman doing math against her own heart and finding the sum unacceptable.

He should have felt triumphant. He didn't. He felt like a man who'd been handed a lit match in a room full of gunpowder and told not to think about it.

Jonah sat up slowly. Across the room, the hallway to the bedrooms was dark. Rosalind had gone up hours ago, after they'd sat in a silence so complete he could hear the fire eating wood, and said, "We should sleep," in the voice of someone closing a ledger.

He found paper in the drawer of the sideboard—a legal pad, half-used, covered in her handwriting. Numbers mostly. Column after column of numbers, because of course she dreamed in arithmetic. He tore off three clean sheets and stood at the counter with a pen borrowed from the same drawer, and for a long time he wrote nothing at all.

Outside, the storm had spent itself. Through the window over the sink, the vineyard lay under six inches of blue-white silence, the rows running down the hillside like sentences waiting to be finished. Somewhere under that snow were her vines—stressed, underfed, carrying fruit that would decide whether she kept the only thing left of her father. And somewhere across the valley, under its own snow, was his.

He thought about the way she'd flinched when he'd mentioned money last night. Not at the word—at the *implication*, the idea that anyone might do something for her without invoicing it first. He'd watched her build that wall all evening, brick by brick: the deflection about yeast strains, the joke about his taste in barrels, the sudden fascination with the weather report on her dead phone. Every time the conversation drifted toward them, she'd thrown numbers in its path like sandbags.

Fine. If she could only hear him through arithmetic, he'd speak arithmetic.

He put the pen to the paper and began.

* * *

By the time the sky went from black to iron to the pale bruised gray that passes for dawn in winter, he had filled two pages and crossed out most of one. He read it over standing at the counter, coffee-less because the generator was dead and the machine was decorative anyway, and decided it would have to do. He was not a writer. He was a winemaker, and winemakers know that you don't get to control what the wine becomes—you only control whether you're brave enough to put it in the bottle.

The note said:

*Rosalind—*

*I'm leaving before you wake up because I don't trust either of us at close range before coffee, and I need to be gone before you can turn this into a negotiation.*

*Here is the negotiation anyway.*

*Your fruit this year is better than your equipment deserves. My equipment is better than my fruit has any right to expect. You know it, I know it, and I've known it since we were twenty-four years old making terrible wine in your father's cellar and lying to each other about how good it was.*

*So: co-ferment. Your grapes, my cellar, or split it—half here, half there, doesn't matter. We make one wine from both vineyards. One label. We split the proceeds down the middle, and if it medals—and it will medal, Rosalind, because I've tasted what happens when your fruit and my patience get in the same room—we both survive the bank. Neither of us owes the other anything except grapes.*

*This is not charity. This is not me buying my way back into anything. This is the best business decision available to either of us, and you know it, and the fact that it also means I get to see you every week through crush is a bonus I'm prepared to live with.*

*You'll want to say no. You'll run the numbers and find a reason. So let me save you the trouble: whatever number you come up with, I've already run it, and mine says yes.*

*Think about it. That's all I'm asking. Think about it with the part of you that tasted that barrel in the storm cellar and couldn't lie about it.*

*—J*

He read it twice himself, wincing once at *patience* and deciding to leave it, because flattering her hadn't worked in a decade and honesty was the only currency she'd ever accepted at face value.

Then he folded it, weighed it down on the kitchen table under the salt cellar where she couldn't miss it, and went out into the cold to dig out his truck.

* * *

The world after a storm has a particular kind of hush, and Jonah moved through it like a trespasser. His truck wore a smooth white carapace; he cleared it with the brush from the bed, breath smoking, and considered the driveway—a quarter mile of unplowed white ending at a county road of unknown condition. The truck was heavy, four-wheel drive, and stubborn, which he'd always felt made it a good match for him.

He got it moving on the third try. The tires churned, gripped, and the truck surged out of the yard trailing powder, and he drove slow past the rows with the window cracked so the cold could keep him honest.

At the gate he stopped. Looked back once.

The house was dark. She was asleep in it, one floor above the cellar where they'd kissed by a dying fire, and he had absolutely no idea whether he'd just made the biggest mistake of his life or finally stopped making it. Both things could be true. With Rosalind Quintana, both things were usually true; that was the whole problem with her, and the whole reason he'd never managed to want anyone else.

He drove east toward the pass, where the plows would be working, and let the engine noise fill up the space where an argument used to be.

* * *

Rosalind woke to light that was too clean for a storm day and lay still for a moment, disoriented by the quality of the silence. Storms roar. This was the silence that comes after, the held-breath quiet of a world buried and blinking.

She came downstairs in socks, hair unbrushed, expecting an empty house and braced for the specific awkwardness of finding Jonah in it.

The house was empty. The couch cushions still held the shape of him. Two coffee mugs sat in the drying rack—he'd washed them, which was somehow more intimate than if he'd left them dirty—and the fire had been built up and banked, professional work, the muscle memory of a man who'd spent winters in cellars.

Then she saw the note.

She saw it from across the room, weighted under the salt cellar, and her stomach did something complicated and unwelcome, because she already knew what it was. There was only one reason a man like Jonah Reyes would leave before dawn instead of staying to fight about it in person, and that reason fit on a piece of paper.

She picked it up. Read it standing at the counter with the cold coming off the window at her back.

She read it again.

The second time was slower, and worse, because the second time she couldn't pretend she hadn't understood. Co-ferment. Split everything. His cellar, her fruit, one wine. It was—she hated the word her mind produced, but it produced it anyway—*elegant*. It solved the equipment problem that had brought him here in the first place. It solved the volume problem, the labor problem, the crushing cash-flow problem of getting wine to market fast enough to mean anything to a bank. It even solved the pride problem, or claimed to, by dressing itself as a transaction: grapes for capacity, nothing owed.

And every single line of it was a man saying *I never stopped choosing you* in the only dialect she'd ever let him use.

She set the note down. Picked it up. Set it down again, harder, as though it might behave differently the second time.

"Impossible," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, and the word came out steadier than she felt.

Because it wasn't impossible. That was the trap of it. If it had been impossible she could have filed it away with the rest of the fantasies—the ones where the bank extended terms out of kindness, where her father walked back through the door with an explanation, where the last decade undid itself. But this was possible. It was, in fact, the most possible thing anyone had offered her since the shoebox of receipts had turned her life into a countdown. A real plan, from a real winemaker, with real numbers behind it, requiring nothing from her but the one thing she'd sworn off at twenty-four: trust.

She put the kettle on the wood stove because the electric one was dead, and while the water heated she did what she always did, what she was built to do, what her father had trained into her before he ever taught her to prune: she ran the numbers.

His cellar capacity, which she knew from auction gossip and from a tour he'd given her seven years ago, back when they were still pretending to be colleagues. Her tonnage estimate, conservative. Crush schedule, fermentation timeline, the brutal arithmetic of getting a young wine bottled and into judges' glasses before spring. It worked. That was the humiliating part. On paper, in her own handwriting, in columns she trusted more than people, it worked—not marginally, not hopefully, but with room to spare. The blend they'd tasted by firelight floated up in her memory, dark and strange and better than it had any right to be, and her own traitorous palate whispered *yes* while the rest of her stood guard.

She poured the water over the last of the instant coffee and carried the mug to the window.

The vineyard glittered. Six inches of snow on the rows, and beneath it, her father's vines, holding on the way they'd held on through every bad year he'd ever drunk his way through—stubborn, unsentimental, alive because nobody had given them permission to die. She thought about what Marisol would say if she showed her the note. Marisol would say *say yes, jefa* before finishing reading it. Dev would already be calculating sulfur additions. Even Tomás, who trusted Jonah Reyes approximately as far as he could throw the man's ego, would squint at the proposal and admit, grudgingly, that it was sound.

Everyone would say yes. Everyone except the one person whose permission mattered, who had learned at twenty-four exactly what it cost to hand this man her future and watch it burn.

Except that wasn't fair, and she knew it wasn't fair, and knowing it changed nothing. Because the truth—the truth she'd admitted to him for one hour by a dying fire and then resealed like a barrel headed for long storage—was that she hadn't left because he'd failed her. She'd left because loving him had felt like standing in a burning field, and she was a woman who ran toward fires only when they belonged to other people.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down the hill, the county road showed a stripe of bare asphalt where a plow had been through. The world was reopening. Within the hour, trucks would move, phones would find towers, and the strange snowbound country where she'd told the truth would dissolve back into the ordinary geography of debts and deadlines.

She took the note to the drawer where she kept the important papers—the deed, the loan documents, the shoebox's surviving contents—and then stopped with her hand on the handle.

Filing it there meant keeping it. Keeping it meant deciding later. Deciding later meant hope, and hope was a vintage she'd never learned to cellar without spoilage.

Instead she smoothed the note flat on the counter, folded it in thirds, and slid it into the back pocket of her jeans, which was a compromise her body made without consulting her brain. Then she drank her coffee standing up, watching the road, and began—methodically, line by line, the way she did everything—to build the case against it.

It wasn't hard to find flaws. There was the optics problem: a joint venture with Jonah Reyes, weeks after their public bidding war, would have the valley's tongues going inside a day. There was the control problem—his cellar, his protocols, his name on the facility lease. There was the timing problem, the bank problem, the *him* problem, which was not really a problem at all but a wound wearing a problem's clothing.

There. See? Flaws. Reasons. A list long enough to hide behind.

She was halfway through composing the refusal in her head—something crisp, something grateful-but-no, something that would prove she'd heard him and remained unmoved—when Marisol's truck crunched up the drive, and Dev leaned on the horn twice, their signal for *we're alive, are you*, and the ordinary machinery of the vineyard resumed around her like water closing over a stone.

She tucked the note deeper into her pocket, where it lay against her hip like a second pulse, and went out to meet the day.

*Impossible,* she told herself one more time, out in the cold, stamping her feet on frozen ground.

But she didn't throw it away. And somewhere across the valley, in a cellar she'd refused to picture, a man was betting she wouldn't.
