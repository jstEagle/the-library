## 16. The Fight in the Cold

I drove the forty minutes to Quintana Vineyard with the heater off and the window cracked, because I wanted to be angry all the way there and I knew comfort would dilute it.

Marisol had called me at six a.m., her voice low and urgent, the way people talk when they're betraying someone out of love. *She's going to sign it over, Jonah. Friday. She won't tell you. She packed up your barrels—she moved them out of the barn like you never existed.* And then, before hanging up: *She thinks she's protecting you. Somebody has to protect her from that.*

So here I was, seven-fifteen in the morning, frost on the windshield wipers, fury in my chest like a second heartbeat.

The vineyard looked the way it always did in deep winter—the rows bare and black, pruned back to skeletons, the hills behind them dusted white as if God had floured the whole valley. Beautiful. Stubborn. Hanging on by roots nobody could see. It had always reminded me of her.

Her truck was in the yard. Good. I didn't want to do this by phone. Some things you have to say standing in the cold where a person can't hang up.

She was in the cellar when I found her—or rather, she wasn't in the cellar, which was the point. The barn door stood open, and inside, the corner where our barrels had slept for three months was empty. Clean concrete, a few pale rings where the barrels had stood, a coil of rope left behind like a shed skin. She'd erased us. Neatly. Efficiently. The way she did everything.

She came around the side of the barn carrying an empty crate, saw me, and stopped so abruptly that frost crunched under both boots at once.

"Jonah."

"You moved them." My voice came out quieter than I intended. That happens when the anger is real—it goes cold instead of hot. "You moved our barrels out of my barn at five in the morning like a criminal disposing of evidence."

"They were on my property," she said. "Technically."

"Technically." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Rosalind, we have been fermenting a future together in that barn since December. You tasted that blend with your own mouth. You danced with me in the cellar—you danced, Rosalind, I have witnesses—and now you've moved the barrels and you're what, going to sign the vineyard over on Friday and pretend none of it happened?"

"Who told you?" Her eyes went narrow, and I watched her do the arithmetic of betrayal—Marisol, obviously, who else—and something in her face hardened into the expression I had spent ten years learning to hate. The closed one. The ledger face.

"It doesn't matter who told me."

"It matters," she said, setting down the crate with exaggerated care, "because I asked them not to. Because this is my decision, on my land, about my debt, and I don't need—" 

"—you don't need what?" I crossed the distance between us in four strides. Up close I could see she hadn't slept; the shadows under her eyes were the color of the frost-burned vines. "Say it. Finish the sentence. You don't need me. Go ahead. Say it and watch me believe you."

"Jonah—"

"You didn't even ask me." The words finally broke loose, all of them at once, ten years and three months of them. "That's the part I can't—that's the part that's killing me, Roz. You ran the numbers. I know you did, because you always run the numbers, a hundred ways, on napkins, at two a.m.—and every single version ended with my name on your debt, and you never once picked up the phone. You decided for me. Again."

"I was not going to gamble your vineyard." Her voice cracked on *vineyard*, and I hated how much I loved hearing it crack. "Your collateral. Your family's land. Everything your parents built. For what—for a coin flip? A medal? You'd be betting everything you have on a wine that took silver at best, and silver doesn't save either of us, and I will not—I cannot—be the reason the Reyes name ends up on a bank auction list next to mine."

"So your solution was to lose alone."

"My solution was to lose *alone*!" she shouted, and the sound went out across the frozen rows and came back off the hills, and somewhere in the bare vines a crow lifted and flapped away, offended. "Yes! That was the plan! That was always the plan!"

"And how's that working out?"

Silence. Breath steaming between us. Her hands were shaking, and she shoved them into her coat pockets to hide it, and I almost—almost—reached for her. I didn't. Not yet. There were true things that needed saying first, the unforgivable kind, the kind you can't take back and shouldn't want to.

"Do you know what I thought," I said, "that summer? When you left?"

"Don't."

"You didn't leave a note. You didn't call. I came back from my father's place and your room was empty and your car was gone and your father told me—" I stopped. Swallowed. "He told me you'd said it was a mistake. That I was a mistake. Twenty-four years old, first harvest I ever made that I was proud of, the best summer of my life, and the woman I loved vanished and her father handed me a sentence I carried around for a decade like a stone in my boot. *It was a mistake, mijo. She's ambitious. Let her go.*"

"That's not—" Her face had gone white. "Jonah, that's not what happened."

"No," I said. "It isn't. I know that now. Now that Marisol told me. Now that I'm thirty-five and finally got the story from literally anyone but you. You left to protect him. His reputation, his debts, whatever mess he'd made—you folded yourself up small and disappeared so his lie wouldn't catch fire, and you let me believe I was the mistake because the truth would have burned down your family." I stepped closer. Frost crunched. "And here we are, fifteen years later, and you're doing it again. Word for word. Same move. Protect the man by erasing him. Decide his heart is a liability and cut it out of the equation without asking."

"You don't understand the numbers—"

"I understand the numbers fine! I have the same numbers! I've had them since February!" I was shouting now too, and I didn't care. "What I don't understand is why you think love is something you can unilaterally manage. Why you think protecting me means lying to me. Roz—Rosalind—look at me. Being kept safe without my consent isn't protection. It's just another way of leaving."

That landed. I watched it land—watched her flinch like I'd struck her, watched her jaw set against the flinch, watched her lose the battle between them.

"You want unforgivable true things?" she said, very quietly, which was worse than shouting. "Fine. Here's one. If I put your name on that loan and we lose—if the wine takes nothing, if the bank comes for both properties—your mother loses her house. Do you hear me? Elena Reyes, who taught me how to prune when I was nineteen and didn't know bud break from a weed, loses her home because I fell in love with her son twice and couldn't stop either time. That's the arithmetic, Jonah. That's what you're asking me to risk. Not you. *Her.*"

"There it is," I said softly. "There's the noble exit. Always a good reason. There's always a good reason, Rosalind, and it's always shaped exactly like a door."

"Don't you dare psychoanalyze me in my own barn—"

"Our barn. Our barrels. Our blend." I grabbed her shoulders—not hard, just to make her stand still, because she was already angling toward the house, toward escape, and I would be damned if I let her walk out of this conversation the way she'd walked out of my life at twenty-four. "You don't get to do the math alone anymore. That's the whole point. That's what I've been trying to tell you since the storm, since the note, since the first barrel—we are a *we*, whether you sign papers or not, and every time you decide my fate in secret you prove you still think of me as something that happened *to* you instead of someone who *chose* you."

"I am trying to save you!"

"From what?" My voice dropped. "From loving you? Too late. Ask me how long. Go ahead, ask."

Her breath caught. The frost held its silence around us. Somewhere up the hill, old Tomás's dog barked once and quit, as if even the dog knew to stay out of this.

"Ten years," I said. "It was supposed to be ten years, and then I saw you at the auction and it reset to zero, and I have spent every day since—every single one—choosing this. Choosing you. Knowing the odds. I'm a winemaker, Rosalind. Risk is my entire profession. We bet a year of our lives on weather, on yeast, on whether some fruit fly crosses a county line. Every bottle I've ever sold was a wager I couldn't guarantee. And you stand there telling me you can't let me gamble—as if I haven't been gambling since the moment I kissed you in that snowbound kitchen and knew, *knew*, I'd never get free of you again."

Tears. She was crying, furious about it, blinking them back like they were a fault in the equipment. "You should have let me protect you."

"You can't protect someone from a thing they want."

"Yes, you can," she whispered. "I've done it my whole life."

And there it was. The whole architecture of her, laid bare in nine words: a girl who learned early that love meant sacrifice performed in secret, that devotion was measured in what you gave up quietly, that the people you loved were safest at a distance where your disasters couldn't reach them. I wanted to hold her. I wanted to shake her. Both hands wanted different things and settled for staying where they were, on her shoulders, feeling her tremble under the wool coat.

"Here's my last true thing," I said. "Then you can go back to your crate and your arithmetic. Ten years ago you left to be noble, and it didn't work. Your father lost the vineyard anyway. He lost it anyway, Roz. All that sacrifice, all that silence, all those years you spent paying for a sin that was his—and the land went under regardless. Nobility bought nothing. It never does. It's just pride wearing a halo." I leaned in close enough that my breath fogged her lashes. "And if you sign this place away on Friday to spare me, in five years you'll be standing in some tasting room pouring somebody else's wine, and you'll look at me across a festival crowd the way you looked at me at the auction, and you'll spend the rest of your life doing arithmetic about the night you threw away the only blend that ever mattered. Ours."

Her lips parted. Closed. A tear made it past her defenses and froze bright on her cheekbone, a tiny lens catching the morning light.

"The panel results come Thursday," I said, stepping back, letting the cold rush in where my hands had been. "Before your deadline. Before Elliott's paper, before the bank, before any of it. One day, Rosalind. Give me one day before you decide we're a loss you can write off."

"And if it's nothing?" she asked, wrecked and defiant. "If it medals nothing? Then what, Jonah? What's your grand plan then?"

"Then I'll be standing next to you when you sign," I said. "Because that's the difference between us. You love people by leaving them. I love people by staying. Even for the losing. *Especially* for the losing."

I turned and walked to my truck because if I stayed one more minute I was going to kiss her, and neither of us would survive that right now—some fires you don't put out, you just refuse to feed. At the door I paused, hand on the cold metal, and looked back at her one more time: small and straight-backed in the middle of her dying vineyard, arms wrapped around herself, tears freezing on her face, the most stubborn, maddening, magnificent woman I had ever known.

"Thursday," I said.

She didn't answer. But she didn't look away either, and she didn't run, and after ten years of watching Rosalind Quintana choose doors, I stood there long enough to see her choose, for the first time, to stay standing in the open cold—until my truck bumped down the drive and the frost swallowed the sound of it, and all the way home I prayed to a God I didn't believe in that the wine would speak louder than her fear.

Two days. One barrel. Everything we had.

Thursday was coming either way.
