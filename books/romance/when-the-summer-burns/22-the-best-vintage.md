## 22. The Best Vintage

The first Saturday of October arrived the way the good days always did at Quintana-Reyes: without warning, and smelling of crushed fruit.

Rosalind stood on the crush pad at seven in the morning, watching the light come up over the eastern ridge the color of new copper, and tried to remember when she had last woken with dread sitting on her chest like a cat that refused to be moved. She couldn't find the date. It had been months, maybe. Maybe longer. The dread was still around somewhere—she could feel it lurking at the edges of things, in the shape of unpaid invoices and the weather report and the way her hand still flinched toward her phone when it buzzed—but it had lost its weight. It had become a small animal instead of a large one.

"Boss." Marisol came across the pad carrying two cups of coffee and the particular expression she wore when she'd already decided something and was merely informing Rosalind of it. "The tents go up at nine. Dev's got the band coming at four. Tomás has been cooking since five, and if you try to help him he will hit you with a spoon."

"I wouldn't dream of it."

"You would absolutely dream of it. You dreamed about it last year and I watched you get within six feet of his paella pan before I intervened." Marisol pressed a cup into her hands. "Also, your husband is looking for you."

"He's not my husband."

"He signs everything 'J. Reyes-Quintana' now. He put it on the tank tags. He put it on the *truck*. I have seen the truck registration, Rosalind. Legally speaking, I think the truck is your husband."

"That's not—" Rosalind started, and then gave up, because arguing with Marisol about the nature of her relationship with Jonah was like arguing with the tide about the shore. "Where is he?"

"The barrel room. Where else. He's been down there since dawn doing something secretive with the storm barrels, and he's wearing his *festival face*, which means either he's planning something wonderful or something that will cost us money, and historically it's been both."

Rosalind drank her coffee and looked out over the vineyard. A year ago this week, she had walked these rows with a shoebox under her arm and frost on the vines and no idea whether she'd still be standing here by spring. A year ago the bank's letter had been folded in her back pocket like a knife she carried for someone else to use. A year ago Jonah Reyes had been a name she said only in the past tense.

Now his name was on her trucks.

She finished the coffee, kissed Marisol on the cheek—which Marisol permitted exactly once per season, as a matter of policy—and went to find him.

* * *

The barrel room was cool and dim and smelled the way it always did: oak and yeast and time. Jonah was standing at the far end with his back to her, bent over the row of barrels they all called the storm lot—the wine made from the fruit they'd crushed during those snowbound weeks, when the power was out and the roads were closed and the two of them had worked by lantern light with numb fingers, mostly silent, occasionally not silent at all, the whole crew taking turns hauling water and stoking the fire while the two rival winemakers made the only honest thing either of them had ever made.

He heard her come in. He always heard her come in; he claimed it was the way her boots caught on the third floorboard, but she suspected it was something older than that, something from before floors.

"You're up early," she said.

"Festival day." He turned. He was wearing the shirt she liked, the soft blue one, and his festival face, which meant he was hiding something behind his teeth. "I wanted to check them one more time before we pour them for forty people."

"They're ready, Jonah. They've been ready since August. You checked them Tuesday."

"I know what they were Tuesday." He pulled the bung from the middle barrel with a practiced twist and drew a small sample into the glass pipette, and there was a moment—he held it up to the lantern light, and the wine glowed that deep garnet with the violet edge that meant youth and life and the specific miracle of a cold winter followed by a patient spring—when Rosalind saw his hands weren't quite steady.

"Jonah," she said slowly. "What did you do?"

"Nothing. I did nothing. It's the same wine." He handed her the glass. "Taste it anyway. Humor me. It's festival day."

She tasted it. And it was the same wine—it was their wine, the blend that had taken silver, the wine that had saved them, dark and bright at once, with that long mineral finish that Tomás swore came from the north slope and Dev swore came from luck and Jonah swore came from the fact that they'd argued about every single decision in its making—but tasting it next to him, in the room where it was born, she felt the thing she always felt and had stopped trying to name: that the wine was true, and that she had helped make something true, and that this was allowed.

"Well?" he said.

"It's perfect. You know it's perfect. You've known since August."

"Yeah." He set down the pipette. His festival face cracked, just slightly, and underneath it was the look he wore only in this room, or in the dark, or at four in the morning when neither of them could sleep and they lay in her old bed in the farmhouse talking about brix levels like other people talked about feelings. "There's one more thing. Don't be mad."

"I'm already mad. What did you do?"

He reached into his jacket pocket and brought out a piece of paper, folded in quarters, soft at the creases like it had been carried around for a while. She took it. Unfolded it.

It was a label proof. Their label printer's work, clean and sharp, and printed across the front in type she didn't recognize—something older-looking, almost handwritten—

**WHEN THE SUMMER BURNS**

Below it, smaller: *Quintana-Reyes Vineyards. Storm Lot, First Vintage.*

"I sent it to the printer three weeks ago," Jonah said. "Before you could vote on it. Which I know is a crime against our entire organizational structure, and Marisol has already fined me two cases of the rosé, and I accept the punishment." He rubbed the back of his neck, that gesture she'd learned a decade ago and never unlearned. "But you kept saying you hadn't picked a name yet. Every time I asked, you said you'd know it when you tasted it, and then you'd taste it and go quiet, and I figured—I figured maybe you already knew too, and saying it out loud was the hard part. So I said it for you. If it's wrong, we reprint. But Rosalind. It isn't wrong."

She looked at the label for a long time.

When the summer burns. The summer her father died, and the vineyard nearly burned with him—not in flame but in debt, in silence, in all the things he hadn't told her until Marisol found the letter in the shoebox. The summer she was twenty-four, making bad wine and worse decisions with a boy from the valley who loved her without conditions and whom she left on a road at dawn. The summer the snow came early and trapped her with that same boy, ten years later, in a cellar with a dying generator and nothing left to lose but everything she'd been pretending not to feel.

"It's right," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "It's terrifyingly right."

"Yeah?"

"Yes." She laughed, and it turned wet halfway through, and she let it. That was another thing she'd learned this year: that crying was not a leak in the hull. That she could cry and still be the person holding the wheel. "You absolute menace. You let me spend three weeks telling everyone the name was undecided."

"I did. It was very funny. Dev has a betting pool."

"Of course Dev has a betting pool."

"Marisol bet on this exact outcome. She wins the pool. She also planned the party, so honestly the economics are questionable."

Rosalind folded the label proof along its worn creases and slid it into her back pocket, where a year ago a bank letter had lived. It fit better there.

* * *

By noon the vineyard had transformed. Tents bloomed between the rows nearest the house; long tables appeared under strings of lights that Dev had rigged with the same reckless competence he applied to everything; Tomás's paella pans—there were three of them, an armada—sent up clouds of saffron-scented steam that drifted over the crush pad and made everyone hungry on schedule. People came from everywhere. Neighbors who had once smiled banker smiles and now brought their kids. The judge from the panel who'd given them silver and told Jonah afterward, quietly, that it should have been gold and that the panel knew it. Buyers. Other growers. Elliott.

Elliott came, and Rosalind watched him arrive, and braced herself the way you brace for weather. But he crossed the grass with a bottle of his father's white as a gift and shook Jonah's hand and hugged her—briefly, kindly, the hug of a man who had withdrawn an offer and grieved it and made his peace—and said, "Save me two bottles of the storm wine. My father doesn't believe the reviews. I want him to taste it blind and suffer."

"Elliott," she said, "you're welcome here any time."

"I know." He smiled, and it was a real smile, a little sad at the edges, entirely without bitterness. "That's why I can come." And he went off toward the paella, and Rosalind stood in the sun feeling the strange lightness of a door closed without slamming.

The crews merged seamlessly, the way they had from the beginning. Jonah's people and hers had stopped being two crews sometime in late winter and become one sprawling, argumentative, loyal organism—a found family that had voted itself into existence, twenty-two hands raised in Tomás's kitchen, and then gone back to work the next morning. Dev was teaching Jonah's youngest cellar hand to play guitar badly. Marisol was running logistics with a clipboard and the serene tyranny of a field marshal. Old Tomás presided over the paella like a bishop, and when Rosalind came near he pointed the spoon at her—*the* spoon, the legendary one—and said, "Sit. Eat. You are too thin and too serious and today is not a day for either."

She sat. She ate. The sun came through the tent canvas in warm coins.

* * *

At sunset, Jonah climbed onto the low stage Dev had built, tapped a spoon against a glass until the crowd quieted, and held up a bottle of the storm wine, unlabeled, its dark shoulders catching the last orange light.

"A year ago," he said, "I got snowed in at this vineyard by accident, which remains my official story, and I want the record to show that the wine we're drinking tonight was made during that week by two crews who had every reason to hate each other and one reason not to." He looked down at Rosalind, and the crowd, being a crowd, turned to look too, and she resisted the urge to hide behind Tomás. "We argued about every choice. We fought about temperature and time and whether to punch down twice a day or three times. And somewhere in the middle of all that fighting, we stopped making two wines and started making one. So tonight we're naming it. Some of you guessed already because my fiancée cannot keep a secret from her crew, and Marisol wins the pool, and yes, Dev, pay up."

Laughter rolled through the tents. Rosalind felt her face heat and didn't care.

Jonah poured the first glasses and handed them down the line—to Marisol, to Dev, to Tomás, to Elliott, to the crews, to the neighbors—until everyone held one, and then he stepped down and came to stand beside her, and together they faced the people who had stayed.

"When the Summer Burns," Rosalind said. Her voice carried further than she expected. "For the summer that nearly ended this place. For the ones who stayed through the winter. For—" she paused, and found that the next part was easy after all, easier than arithmetic had ever been— "for second growth. Vines burn back and grow again. So do we. Drink, please, before it warms up."

They drank. The wine went around the tables like a rumor, like good news, like a blessing, and the band struck up, and the string lights came on all at once as the sky went violet, and Rosalind stood in the middle of her own harvest festival and let herself be happy without checking the ledger first.

Later—much later, when the band was down to its last slow songs and Tomás had fallen asleep in a chair with his spoon still in hand and Marisol was herding teenagers away from the barrel room with terrifying gentleness—Rosalind and Jonah walked the rows at the top of the north slope, where they'd once stood in the frost and tallied debts. The vines were heavy with next year's promise, leaves going gold at the edges, and below them the festival glowed like an ember that refused to die.

"Do you remember," Jonah said, "when you told me the heart was a muscle you could train to stay quiet?"

"I remember."

"How's the training going?"

Rosalind considered the question seriously, the way she considered everything, and found the answer waiting for her, simple as a label on a bottle.

"I fired the trainer," she said. "Turns out it was never supposed to be quiet. It was just waiting for something worth saying."

Jonah laced his fingers through hers, ink-stained and grape-stained and steady. Below them, someone laughed; above them, the first stars of autumn came out over the ridge; and in the cellar beneath their feet, a thousand bottles slept under a name that told the truth.

"Next year," Jonah said, "we make it gold."

"No," Rosalind said, smiling, squeezing his hand as the music swelled and the summer finally, gently, let go. "Next year we make it ours. The medals can take care of themselves."

And for the first time in her life, she believed it all the way down.
