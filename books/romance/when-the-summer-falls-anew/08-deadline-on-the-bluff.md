## 8. Deadline on the Bluff

The email arrived at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, while Theo Marsh was standing in his rented kitchen watching fog burn off the harbor like a curtain being drawn.

*RE: Bluff Acquisition — Timeline Adjustment.*

He read it twice. The words didn't change. Thirty days. Secure signatures from sixty percent of bluff-parcel owners or the firm pulled funding, and with it, the partnership track he'd spent eleven years clawing toward. His name would come off the letterhead of a future that had been promised to him in a glass conference room forty stories above a city he no longer missed.

He set down his phone. Picked it up. Read it a third time, as if the deadline might soften under scrutiny.

It didn't.

"Thirty days," he said aloud to the empty kitchen, and the house—a narrow rental with salt-stained windows and a porch that groaned when the wind came off the water—said nothing back.

He should have felt the old engine turn over. That was the feeling he knew best, the one that had carried him through every acquisition since he was twenty-six: the cold bright clarity of a countdown. Deadlines were how Theo Marsh understood himself. Give him a number and a date and he became a machine of clean purpose, all friction burned away.

Instead he stood there holding his coffee, looking out at the water, thinking about the way Cyrus Reyes's hands looked around a pruning shear—sure, unglamorous, patient—and hating himself for it.

* * *

Headquarters called at nine. Diane Voss's voice came through the speaker crisp as cut paper.

"Walk me through the map, Theo."

He spread the parcel survey across his desk. Sixty-one acres along the northern bluff, stitched together from eleven owners: three retirees, a church camp, two absentee heirs, Marisol Vega—who'd told him, cheerfully, that she'd sell her bee farm over her dead body and that the bees could probably arrange it—and Reyes Vineyards. Fourteen acres of neglected vines on the most beautiful stretch of coastline in the county.

"The holdouts are emotional, not financial," he said. "Reyes is the anchor. If he signs, the rest follow. He inherited eight months ago, the property's bleeding money, and he knows it."

"And your read on him?"

Theo was quiet a beat too long.

"Theo?"

"He's stubborn," Theo said. "But stubborn men respond to respect. I'm working him slowly."

"Slowly." He could hear her pen stop moving. "Theo, you have thirty days. I need signatures, not courtship."

Something turned over in his chest at the word *courtship*, an ugly little flare of guilt, because that was exactly what it had started to feel like—not the negotiation, but the other thing. The letters. Wren's letters arriving every few days now, longer each time, the handwriting loosening like a man unclenching his fist.

"I'll get them," he said. "Thirty days."

He hung up and sat for a long moment with his hand flat on the survey map, over the rectangle marked *PARCEL 7 — REYES — 14 ACRES*, as if he could press the signature into existence through sheer weight of wanting.

Wanting what, though. That was the question he kept not asking.

* * *

He called Cyrus that afternoon and pitched it as strategy: walk the rows together, parcel by parcel, so Cyrus could see exactly which acreage the resort footprint touched and which it didn't. A reasonable man's meeting. No lawyers, no pressure.

"That's the first thing you've said that sounds like a person," Cyrus said down the line, dry as August. "Fine. Thursday. But we're starting at six a.m., before the heat, and if you call this land 'inventory' even once, the meeting's over."

"Noted."

"And bring boots. You're not walking my rows in those city shoes."

Theo looked down at his city shoes. "How do you know what shoes I wear?"

There was a pause on the line, and then Cyrus laughed—one short, surprised note, like a cork slipping—and hung up.

Theo sat with the phone against his ear long after the dial tone died, smiling at nothing, feeling the countdown in his chest warp into something with a different shape entirely.

* * *

Thursday, 6:04 a.m. The vineyard was silvered with fog, the rows running up the slope like dark ribs. Cyrus was already out there, of course, in a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled, handing Theo a pair of rubber boots two sizes too big.

"They were my grandfather's," Cyrus said. "Lace them tight."

They walked. Cyrus talked the way he never did in meetings—about the vines, about the soil, about the particular grudge the north slope held against wind. His voice changed out here. In town, at the general store, across a zoning table, it was armored, clipped. Out here it moved like the rows themselves, turning, following the contour of something.

"This block's Pinot Noir," he said, stopping at row twelve. "Planted '89. My grandfather said it was too cold here for Pinot, that everyone told him so, and he planted it anyway out of spite." He touched a leaf, almost tenderly. "First decent vintage came fourteen years later. He used to say spite makes excellent terroir."

"He sounds like he was a piece of work."

"He was the best man I ever knew." Cyrus said it simply, without performance, and then seemed to catch himself at the openness of it and turned away down the row. "Keep up, Marsh. This parcel borders yours—the resort's, I mean."

*Yours.* The resort's. Theo filed the slip away and hated that he noticed it at all.

They walked the boundary where Parcel 7 met the church camp land, and Cyrus stopped at a break in the hedge where the bluff dropped away and the whole coast opened below them—gray water, white lighthouse, the far smudge of the shipping lane.

"This is what your renderings don't show," Cyrus said quietly. "I've seen them. Infinity pool, cabanas, the works. Very pretty. But you can't render this." He gestured at the whole breathing morning. "The fact that it's empty. That nobody built anything here because they understood you don't improve on this. You just ruin it more slowly."

"You think I don't understand that?"

"I think you understand it and do it anyway. Which is worse."

The words landed harder than they should have, because they were true, and because standing next to Cyrus in his grandfather's boots, Theo could feel the truth of it in his body—in the ache behind his sternum that had been getting louder for weeks and that he kept misdiagnosing as stress.

"I have thirty days," he heard himself say.

Cyrus turned. "What?"

Theo hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, raw, unplanned. "My firm. If I don't get enough signatures in thirty days, the project dies. And—" He stopped. There was a version of honesty available to him right there, standing in the fog: *and I lose everything I've worked for, and maybe I want it to die, and I don't know who I am without the wanting.*

"—and I lose my partnership," he finished. "Eleven years. So when I push, it's not cruelty. It's survival. I thought you deserved to know the clock exists."

Cyrus studied him for a long moment. The fog was lifting; light came through it sideways, gilding one side of his face.

"Thank you," Cyrus said finally, and it sounded like it cost him something. "For telling me the truth. It's disorienting. I'll need a minute to adjust."

Despite everything, Theo laughed.

* * *

They ended up walking longer than any negotiation required. Two hours. Three. Cyrus showed him the cellar, cool and dim and smelling of oak and stone, and talked about his grandfather's journals—how he'd found them only recently, how every harvest was recorded in a hand like iron wire. He did not show Theo the journals themselves. He held that door closed, and Theo, who made his living finding the doors people left open, found himself not pushing. Not wanting to push.

At some point Cyrus said, "You're lingering."

"I'm assessing sightlines."

"You've been assessing the same sightline for ten minutes. It's a hillside, Marsh. There are hillsides."

"It's a very good hillside."

Cyrus gave him a look—suspicious, amused, something underneath both that Theo refused to name—and turned back toward the house. And Theo stood there another moment, lying by omission to a man whose letters he knew by heart, and understood with terrible clarity that he wasn't lingering for the firm. He was lingering because these were the last weeks of something, and he wanted to memorize them before he destroyed them.

* * *

That night he sat at his desk with the survey map and the signature folder and wrote his weekly report to headquarters. It was due Friday. He typed:

*Progress steady. Reyes engaged. Expect movement within window.*

All true. All hollow. He deleted it and retyped it and deleted it again, and finally saved a draft titled *VOSS REPORT* containing only the sentence *I don't know how to want this anymore,* which he would obviously delete before sending, and closed the laptop.

Then he took out paper. Real paper, because Wren's correspondent—that was the arrangement, the beautiful anonymous accident of the town program—wrote on real paper, and some things couldn't be typed.

*Dear Wren,* he wrote. *Today I stood on a hillside with a man who hates me, and he was right about everything, and I stayed too long because leaving felt like a kind of theft. I keep telling myself the deadline is the enemy. But deadlines don't make you lie. They just give the lie a schedule...*

He filled four pages. He said things he had never said to Diane Voss, to his father, to anyone: that he had gotten into development because he loved building things and had woken up at thirty-seven realizing he mostly demolished them; that the letters were the only place he recognized himself; that he was afraid the person on the other end of this correspondence, whoever they were, was the last honest room in his life, and he was terrified of what happened when the door opened.

He sealed it. Stamped it. Set it on the hall table for morning pickup.

Across town—he wouldn't learn this until much later, would learn it in the worst possible way, in a lit library circle with everyone watching—Cyrus Reyes was doing the same thing, writing to a stranger about a developer who was starting to seem less like a vulture and more like a man, and hating himself for the softening, and unable to stop it.

Two envelopes. One mailbox between them, eventually. Thirty days on the clock.

Theo turned off the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the foghorn, and the last thing he thought, drifting down, was not about the partnership, or the bluff, or the numbers.

It was: *I hope Wren never finds out who I am.*

And beneath it, quieter, the thought he'd been refusing for weeks, surfacing now like something drowned finally letting itself rise:

*I hope Cyrus does.*
