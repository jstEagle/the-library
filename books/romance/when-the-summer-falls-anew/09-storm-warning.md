## 9. Storm Warning

The sky turned the color of a bruise at four in the afternoon, and by five Marisol was on Cyrus's porch with a jar of honey in one hand and a look of grim prophecy on her face.

"Southeast gale coming up the coast," she said. "Junie's radio said it'll make landfall past midnight. You've got young vines on the exposed rows."

"I know." He'd been watching the clouds stack themselves over the water all day, gray plates sliding one atop another like the sky was setting a table for something enormous.

"Do you?" Marisol set the honey down on the railing with a click that sounded like punctuation. "Because I watched you watch that sky for an hour this morning and then go back inside to your typewriter, Cyrus Reyes. The sea doesn't care about your book."

"The trellises are anchored."

"They're anchored for weather. This isn't weather. This is the ocean deciding it wants its bluff back." She squinted past him at the rows, her silver hair whipping loose from its braid in the strengthening wind. "You'll need hands. More than yours."

"I have hands."

"You have two, and one of them still doesn't grip right since you crushed it under that crate in July." She turned to go, then paused. "That developer man came by my place asking about access roads. I told him if he set one boot on my bees' flight path I'd sue him into the next county." A pause, weighted. "He asked about your vines, too. Whether they'd hold through a blow like this."

Cyrus felt something tighten behind his ribs. "Why would he care?"

"Why does any man ask questions he pretends are about business?" Marisol pulled her collar up against the wind. "Tie everything down before dark. And Cyrus—" she looked back at him, and her voice gentled in a way that was somehow more alarming than the storm report, "—whatever's eating you lately, storms have a way of dragging it out where everyone can see it. Might as well drag it out yourself first."

She crunched away down the gravel drive, and Cyrus stood on his porch holding a jar of honey and feeling, absurdly, like he'd been read aloud.

* * *

Theo Marsh arrived at seven, just as the first fat drops began to fall, driving a truck with out-of-state plates up a road he had no business knowing led to the vineyard.

Cyrus met him at the end of the drive with a rain jacket half-zipped and every intention of being unpleasant. "This is private property."

"It is." Theo climbed out anyway, rain already plastering his hair flat, wearing a slicker that looked new and boots that did not. "And in approximately six hours it's going to be private property underwater. Your south rows—the ones on the slope facing the water—your anchors are shallow. I saw them when I walked the site last month. They'll pull in sustained winds above fifty knots."

"You walked my vineyard."

"With a survey crew, yes, and you called me a vulture in front of the entire zoning board, so let's not pretend we don't know each other's worst moments." Theo shoved his hands in his pockets, and there was something underneath the arrogance that hadn't been there before—something that looked, impossibly, like nerves. "I'm not here to buy anything tonight. I'm here because those vines are two years old and if they go over, you lose the season. Maybe the rootstock. I know what that means for a place like this."

"And what would you know about what it means?"

The question came out crueler than Cyrus intended, and he watched it land—watched Theo flinch and then, remarkably, refuse to fall.

"Nothing," Theo said quietly. "I know nothing about what it means. That's rather the point of me standing here getting soaked, isn't it? I'm trying to learn something I can't put in a report."

Rain ran off the brim of Cyrus's hood. Somewhere down the slope, the wind found the young vines and made them shudder, a whole row moving like a single nervous animal.

"South rows," Cyrus said finally. "You'll do what I tell you and nothing else."

"Yes."

"And if you mention square footage or ROI even once, I will personally feed you to Marisol's bees."

Something flickered across Theo's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first Cyrus had ever seen there. "Understood."

* * *

They worked.

It was not gentle work and it was not quiet work. The storm came off the water in long shoving gusts that made conversation impossible and cooperation mandatory. Cyrus showed Theo how to double-peg the anchor lines, how to run twine from the trellis tops down to stakes driven at an angle against the prevailing wind, how to read a vine's lean and compensate for it. Theo learned fast—that was the infuriating thing about him. Show him a knot once and he had it; explain why the young canes snapped rather than bent and he was repeating it back in better language than you'd used.

"Left stake's loose," Theo shouted over the wind, kneeling in the mud with his sleeves soaked to the elbow. "Give me the mallet."

Cyrus handed it over and crouched beside him, both of them wrestling the stake while the gusts tried to peel them off the hillside. Mud went everywhere. It went specifically, comprehensively, all over Theo Marsh's expensive rain gear, and when the stake finally bit and they sat back gasping, Cyrus looked at the developer—gray-faced, mud-splattered, hair in his eyes, grinning like a boy—and laughed.

He didn't mean to. It came out of him rough and surprised, a sound his body made without consulting him, and Theo laughed too, breathless and disbelieving, and for a moment the two of them were just men sitting in the dirt on a drowning hillside laughing at the sheer audacity of weather.

"What?" Theo shouted, when he could.

"You look terrible!"

"You should see yourself!" Theo gestured at him. "You look like you lost a fight with the Pacific!"

"I did! We both did! It's winning!"

"It always wins!" Theo yelled back, and there was something wild and free in it, something that sounded like a man setting down a weight he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his spine. "That's the whole lesson, isn't it? You just keep tying things down anyway!"

Cyrus wanted to say: *Since when do you tie things down? You pave things.* But the words wouldn't come, because looking at this mud-caked, laughing stranger, he couldn't find the vulture anywhere. What he found instead was someone who had walked into a storm for a hillside he was supposed to want destroyed, and Cyrus did not have a shelf in his mind built strong enough to hold that.

Lightning stitched the horizon white. They got back to work.

* * *

By midnight the worst of the front had passed over and settled into a hard steady rain, the kind that meant business but not catastrophe. The south rows held. Two trellis posts had cracked and would need replacing come morning, and one section of netting had torn free and wrapped itself around Cyrus's legs like a spiteful ghost, but the vines stood. Every last stubborn green line of them stood.

They ended up in the barn because it was closer than the house and neither of them could see straight enough to argue about it. Cyrus lit the old lantern on its nail by the door—it still worked, because his grandfather had believed in maintaining things you might need in the dark—and the yellow light filled the space with a warmth that felt almost embarrassing after the violence outside.

Theo stood dripping in the middle of the barn floor, surveying the crates and coils of rope and the dust-sheeted tractor with the expression of a man reading a room he'd only ever seen as a figure on a map.

"It smells like earth in here," he said. "Real earth. Not landscaped earth."

"That's generally what barns smell like."

"I know. I just—" He stopped. Started again. "I grew up in a city where everything smelled like exhaust and other people's air conditioning. My mother kept one plant on the windowsill. A geranium. She was very serious about it. I used to think that was the whole extent of what growing things could be—one geranium, defended against the world." He looked at his hands, which were shaking faintly with spent adrenaline, caked to the wrist in Cyrus's soil. "Tonight I tied down several thousand living things in a storm because a man who hates me told me to, and it felt like the most useful hour of my life. That's probably pathetic."

"It's not pathetic." Cyrus heard his own voice come out lower than usual, stripped of its armor. "It's the least pathetic thing anyone's done on this hillside in years."

Theo looked up at him. In the lantern light his eyes were tired and open in a way that made Cyrus's chest hurt, and the rain drummed on the tin roof, and the silence between them stretched until it became a thing with weight and texture, a fabric they were both holding an edge of.

"Cyrus." His name, in that voice, careful as a hand extended over a gap. "Can I say something that isn't about the vines?"

The heart is a muscle. His grandfather had said that once, kneeling in these same rows with a grafting knife in his hand. *And like any muscle, it grows toward whatever you make it lift.*

"Don't," Cyrus said. Not unkindly. Almost pleadingly.

Theo held his gaze a moment longer, then nodded slowly, accepting the boundary—or appearing to. "All right." He picked up his ruined gloves from where he'd dropped them. "For what it's worth, your grandfather's vines are well-built. Whatever else happens—with the land, with us, with any of it—they were planted by someone who expected them to survive him."

"He expected them to outlive everyone," Cyrus said. "That was sort of his whole philosophy."

"Then he'd be pleased tonight." Theo pulled his slicker closed. "I'll come by tomorrow to help replace the posts. If that's—"

"If you want."

Both of them heard how that sounded. Neither of them took it back.

* * *

Theo's headlights swept the yard and vanished down the coast road, and Cyrus stood in the barn doorway a long time, listening to the rain ease off the way a fever eases, gradually and then all at once.

He should go inside. He should dry off, check the cellar for seepage, sleep. Instead he found himself walking out into the rows with the lantern, lifting its light to each young vine the way you'd check on a sleeping child. Wet leaves gleamed. Stakes leaned but held. The whole hillside smelled rinsed and raw and alive.

At the top of the south slope he stopped, because there was a coil of twine lying in the mud where Theo had been working, and beside it, caught on a broken cane, a strip of blue fabric from his sleeve. Small evidence. Proof of presence.

Cyrus crouched and picked up the twine, and found he couldn't make himself drop it again.

*I almost told him,* he thought. *In the barn. He almost told me something, and I almost let him, and either of those things happening would have changed the shape of everything.* Because he knew—he had known for weeks now, in the place where he kept the knowledge he refused to look at directly—that the letters he wrote at night to a stranger named nothing, signed Wren, were becoming the truest sentences of his life. And he knew that the man who had stood in his barn smelling of rain and honest work was becoming the truest person in it. And some traitorous arithmetic in the back of his skull kept trying to carry a number between those two facts, and every time it tried, Cyrus slammed the ledger shut.

Not yet. Not yet. There were signatures pending and a town divided and a grief he hadn't finished carrying, and falling for Theodore Marsh—of all the improbable, catastrophic people—was a cliff edge he was not ready to stand at.

But the heart, his grandfather had said, grows toward whatever you make it lift.

And tonight, without permission, it had lifted a great deal.

Cyrus pocketed the twine, doused the lantern, and walked back toward the house through the dripping rows—past the kitchen window, where he stopped dead.

On the table, visible through the glass, lay the day's mail he'd ignored since noon, rain-spotted and forgotten. And standing on a chair beside it, barefoot, in pajamas, sorting through the pile with the intense concentration of a customs inspector, was Junie—who was supposed to be asleep at Marisol's, who had apparently ridden out the storm's edge with her grandmother and now, evidently, had found something in the stack that interested her greatly.

She was holding one of the letters. Wren's handwriting was unmistakable even upside down.

Through the window, Cyrus watched her turn it over, frown at the postmark, and glance toward the stairs—toward wherever she thought he was—with the slow dawning look of a nine-year-old who has just noticed that the world contains patterns.

The rain fell soft on the vines behind him, and Cyrus Reyes, who had faced down a gale without flinching, felt his stomach drop like a stone.
