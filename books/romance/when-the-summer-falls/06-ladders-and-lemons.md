## 6. Ladders and Lemons

The storm had a name before it had a shape — *Béatrice*, the radio said, as though christening it made it polite — and Odile heard the forecast at six in the morning with a lemon still warm from her palm.

She had been standing in the orchard in her nightgown, barefoot in the wet grass, doing the arithmetic her grandmother used to do: four rows of late lemons not yet picked, forty-eight hours of grace, then wind enough to strip every branch bare. The fruit that fell would bruise. The fruit that stayed on would be flung against the fence like green hail. Either way, the harvest her grandmother had guarded through sixty winters would end up rotting in the grass, and Odile would stand at the council hearing two weeks from now holding nothing but a deed and an apology.

She went inside. She dressed. She told herself she would do it alone, because she had always done everything alone, and alone was at least familiar.

Then she looked out the window and saw Elias Vane already in his own garden, studying her trees over the hedge with the expression of a man reading a difficult page.

* * *

Elias had learned the orchard's language years ago, secondhand, from a boy who talked about it the way other boys talked about football. *Grand-mère Nystrom picks the low branches first,* Théo used to say, sprawled across Elias's bed with a stolen cigarette he never lit. *She says the high ones ripen sweeter because they have to reach.* He'd laughed when he said it, that laugh Elias could still hear sometimes in the space between sleeping and waking, and now here was the orchard itself, real and heavy with fruit, and the woman who owned it standing at its edge in work gloves like armor.

He hadn't planned to offer. He had planned, if anything, to stay behind his hedge and let the weather decide things, the way weather decided most things. But she came out the front door with two ladders instead of one, and there was something in the way she carried them — stiff-armed, jaw set, a woman daring the world to comment — that made the words leave him before he could vet them.

"You'll want help," he called over the hedge. "Two days, one pair of hands, that's a losing sum."

She stopped. The ladders trembled slightly, or perhaps that was the first breath of wind off the sea.

"I've managed worse sums," she said.

"I don't doubt it." He came to the gate anyway. "But you shouldn't have to."

For a long moment she looked at him the way she had looked at him on moving day — as if he were a sentence she'd read a hundred times and still couldn't parse. Then she thrust one ladder through the gate at him, handle-first, like a sword being surrendered.

"Low branches are mine," she said. "You take the high ones. And if you drop a single lemon, Vane, you buy the whole crate."

* * *

They worked through the morning in a rhythm neither of them acknowledged aloud. She moved along the lower boughs with a picking bag slung at her hip; he climbed until the cottage roof was below him and the whole valley opened out — grey-green olive terraces, the church tower, the road that wound toward the coast where the developers wanted their marina. From up here he could see why she fought for it. From up here the orchard didn't look like land. It looked like a held breath.

"Left," she called up. "There's a cluster you're missing. Left, your other left."

"My other left is a philosophical impossibility."

"So is a man who moves next door after ten years and starts by borrowing my ladder."

"That was chapter four," he said, before he could stop himself. "The borrowed ladder. Very original opening."

Above him, silence. He kept his eyes on the fruit, on the small suns of it, and wished the wind would hurry.

"You write?" she asked finally, and her voice had gone careful, the way voices go around a wound.

"Badly. Occasionally." It wasn't a lie; it just wasn't the truth either. The truth was a drawer full of notebooks, ten years of letters written to a woman who never answered, prose that only ever made sense addressed to her. "Mostly I fix other people's sentences. Structural engineering for buildings that were already standing."

"Hm," she said, in a tone that suggested she found this either ironic or obscene, and he couldn't tell which, and didn't ask.

* * *

By noon the sky had gone the color of pewter and the air tasted metallic, and they had filled nine crates. Odile called a halt for water and bread and cheese, laying it out on the stone table under the fig tree the way her grandmother had laid out lunch for hired hands every September of Odile's childhood. Elias sat across from her, forearms dusted with leaf-grime, hair pushed back, and she hated how easily the picture assembled itself — him at this table, as if he'd always belonged to it, as if Théo hadn't been the one who brought him into their lives and death hadn't been the thing that took him back out.

"The forecast says it turns tonight," she said. "We should get the last three rows."

"We will." He tore the bread, handed her the larger piece without appearing to notice he'd done it. "Your grandmother grew these?"

"Her mother planted half of them. The rest she planted herself, the year my grandfather died. She said grief needs somewhere to put its hands." Odile bit into the bread and looked down the long allée of trees, their leaves already shivering. "I used to think that was just something old women said. Now I mostly think she was right."

"And you? Where do yours put their hands?"

The question landed soft but true, the way his questions always had, even at fifteen, even at seventeen, leaning against the kitchen doorframe while Théo raided the fridge. She set down the bread.

"In drawers," she said. "Mostly. Locked ones."

He nodded slowly, and she saw that he understood — really understood — and that understanding sat on him like weight rather than comfort. For a moment neither spoke. The first drops wouldn't come until dark, but the light had begun to fail early, going amber and strange between the trunks.

"Elias." His name felt foreign in her mouth, a coin from a country she'd stopped visiting. "That night. The accident."

His hand stilled on the cheese knife.

"I know what happened," she said. "I've known for ten years. Théo told me himself, weeks before — that you'd drive him home, that you always drove, that he'd promised my grandmother he'd never walk the coast road drunk again. I knew you were driving. I said I blamed you, and I did, but—" Her voice caught on something. "I need to hear you say it. Not for the trial version. For the true one."

* * *

Elias set the knife down very carefully, the way you set down something that might otherwise become a weapon.

"There isn't much of a true version," he said. "It's smaller than people expect. That's the terrible part. Ten years of it and it fits in a paragraph." He looked at his hands. "Théo called me at eleven. He was at Marta's birthday, he'd had wine, he was laughing on the phone — you could hear the party behind him. He said, *come get me, Elias, come get me before I do something stupid.* So I went. I was twenty minutes away. I got there, I put him in the car, he was fine, he was singing, some awful song about sailors. And on the coast road a dog came out of the dark. A dog, Odile. All of it, everything after — a dog."

He heard his own voice flatten and hated it, that old habit of making catastrophe sound procedural.

"I swerved. We went off at the bend past the chapel. I walked away with a broken wrist. He didn't walk away at all." The words came out level, and then, for the first time in front of her, they didn't. "And the thing I've never said to anyone — the thing I couldn't put in any letter — is that for about four seconds afterward, I was glad. He was asleep against the window and there was blood and I thought, *at least it was me driving. At least he didn't walk it alone.* Four seconds of relief before I understood what I was relieved about. I've spent ten years trying to un-deserve those four seconds."

The orchard was very quiet. Somewhere above them a branch creaked, testing the wind.

Odile discovered she was crying without having decided to — not the dramatic kind, just a steady leaking, tears she hadn't budgeted for. Because here was the thing she had never said either, the twin secret, the matching wound: *so was I.*

"For months after the funeral," she heard herself say, "I was almost grateful it happened. Isn't that monstrous? Because Théo had been drowning for a year by then — the drinking, the debts, the 3 a.m. phone calls where he cried and swore he'd stop. And when he died, everyone grieved the boy they remembered, and I got to grieve someone who still existed. I got a clean version of my brother. I built him a shrine out of the good years and I locked the rest in a drawer, and then I needed someone to blame so the shrine would hold, and you were—" She pressed the heel of her hand to her eye. "You were right there. You were drivable. You were the last person to see him alive, and you never once defended yourself, which made it so easy."

"I didn't defend myself," Elias said quietly, "because I believed it too."

"I know. That's what made it unforgivable — that you agreed with me."

A laugh escaped him then, cracked and startled, grief's strange cousin. "God. Ten years of hating each other for agreeing."

"Not hating." She shook her head, wiping her face with the back of a gloved hand, leaving a smear of leaf-dust on her cheekbone. "Hating would have been simpler. I ended our letters, Elias. Did you know that? They didn't stop coming because I moved. I wrote to the post office myself and had them returned. Every one. I stood at that counter and killed the only good thing left in my life because I couldn't bear to be forgiven by the person I was punishing."

She watched the sentence land on him — watched it move through his face like weather, disbelief first, then something rawer, then a stillness that frightened her more than anger would have.

"The letters," he said. His voice had dropped to almost nothing. "They came back marked *return to sender*. All of them. For ten years I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought you'd changed your address and never told anyone. I thought you'd simply... gone somewhere I couldn't follow."

"I was exactly where you sent them. I just refused to receive you."

The wind arrived then, properly, the vanguard of Béatrice, moving through the orchard in a long silver rush that set every leaf talking. Lemons swung on their stems like lanterns. Neither of them moved to shelter from it. It felt, absurdly, like the orchard exhaling — sixty years of held breath released over two people sitting at a stone table with the truth finally out between them, ugly and ordinary and shared.

"We should pick," Odile said at last, standing, pulling her gloves tight. "Three rows before dark."

Elias rose too. At the foot of the ladder he paused, one hand on the lowest rung, and looked at her with an expression she hadn't seen on him since he was nineteen — open, undefended, lit from inside by something that had no business surviving a decade of silence.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the letters never stopped. Not once. Whatever comes with that storm tonight — I want you to know that."

He climbed. She watched him rise into the grey-gold light among the branches, and then she turned to her own row, heart hammering like rain against glass, because she had just realized what he'd meant — that somewhere in that cottage across the hedge, right now, there might be letters. Years of them. Words addressed to her that she had never read, waiting for her the way the lemons waited for the storm.

She picked faster.
