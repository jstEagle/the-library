## 7. Return to Sender

The first letter he wrote the week after the funeral, on paper stolen from a hotel desk in Lyon, because his own flat still smelled of smoke and he could not bear to be inside it.

*Dear Odile,* it began, and then nothing else for three days. He sat with the salutation like a man sitting at the edge of a pool he had no intention of entering, and finally he wrote: *I don't expect you to read this. I don't expect anything. I only need you to know that I think about that road every hour, and I will think about it every hour for the rest of my life, and if there were a way to trade places I would have taken it before the ambulance came.*

He posted it from a post office two towns over, because some cowardly instinct told him that if he mailed it from home, he might walk to her door and take it back out of the box.

The reply, when it came — and he had not dared hope for one — was four lines long. Her handwriting, which he knew better than his own by then, from a childhood of passed notes and borrowed homework and one folded map of the orchard she'd drawn him at fourteen, was smaller than he remembered. Tighter. As though she were writing through clenched teeth.

*I don't want your guilt. I want Théo. You can't send either. Stop writing.*

So of course he wrote again.

* * *

People who have never lost anyone imagine grief as a wound — something that bleeds and then scabs and then, given time, closes. Elias learned early that grief was closer to weather. It moved in systems. Some mornings it was a fine grey drizzle he could work under; other days it arrived without warning, a whiteout, and he would find himself standing in the middle of a supermarket aisle holding a jar of the lemon curd Odile's grandmother used to buy, unable to remember what year it was or why he was allowed to be buying things at all.

The letters became his barometer. He wrote when the pressure dropped.

He never sent more than one a month. He had rules — he made them up as he went, the way drowning men make up strokes. One letter a month. Never more than two pages. Never ask her to write back. Never mention the road by name, though every letter circled it the way a tongue circles a broken tooth. And after the first year, a new rule, the hardest one: never tell her he loved her. That truth was too heavy to put into a box that already weighed something. She would open it and feel its full weight land on top of hers, and she had enough to carry. Everyone said so. Her mother said so at the graveside, gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise: *She can't carry you too, Elias.*

So he carried it instead. Ten years of carrying it, and the strange thing about a weight you carry long enough is that it stops feeling like a burden and starts feeling like a body part. He loved her the way he had a left hand. He did not examine it. He simply could not pick anything up without it.

* * *

The letters came back marked *return to sender* in the fourth year.

Not all of them. First just one — the October letter, the one where he'd told her about the dog he'd adopted from the shelter, a brindled lurcher he'd named Verlaine because Théo had once recited poetry badly and beautifully on a beach in Brittany. He'd thought perhaps she'd moved. Then the November letter came back. Then December's. By spring, every envelope returned to him unopened, stamped in red ink like a verdict.

He stood in his kitchen with the stack of them fanned across the table and understood, with the particular clarity of the heartbroken, that she had not moved. She had closed. There was a difference, and the difference was a door with a lock on it, and he had heard the lock turn from forty kilometres away.

His sister Margaux found him there at midnight, still standing, coat still on.

"You could go," she said. "You could knock."

"And say what? *Sorry I killed your brother, here's a dog named after a poet*?"

"You didn't kill—"

"I was driving, Margaux." His voice came out flat, factual, the voice of a man reading a police report about someone else. "It doesn't matter whose idea the trip was. It doesn't matter that Théo grabbed the wheel — no. No, forget I said that."

Margaux went very still. "Grabbed the wheel?"

"It was a joke between us. He was pretending to swerve at the sign for the orchard turn-off, because he always pretended, because he loved winding me up—" Elias stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations. "It doesn't matter. The coroner ruled it an accident. Accidents need someone to blame and I'm the one who was holding the wheel. That's arithmetic. She needs the arithmetic to hold. If I show up and start complicating it, I take away the one thing keeping her upright."

"Or you're taking away the one thing keeping *you* from her."

He didn't answer that. But he kept the letters. Every returned envelope went into a shoebox, and the shoebox went under his bed, and then — when he moved flats, when he moved cities, when the job took him abroad for two years — the shoebox came with him, wrapped in a jumper, packed last so it would be unpacked first.

* * *

He learned things about her anyway. This is the secret shame of the devoted: they become scholars of the person who has shut them out. He read her name in the acknowledgements of books — *with thanks to O.J., without whom* — and wondered which book, which sentence, which midnight had been hers. He saw a photograph once, in a magazine profile of Camille Rousseau, the celebrated novelist whose memoir-in-progress everyone in publishing talked about the way people talk about weather coming: a woman half-turned from the camera in a sunlit kitchen, face angled away, but he would have known the set of those shoulders anywhere, the way she held her head slightly down and slightly left, listening harder than she let on.

The caption didn't name her. *A friend of the author at her Provençal house.* But he knew. God help him, he knew, and he sat with the magazine open on his knees for an entire evening, feeling like a man reading letters addressed to someone else — which, in a way, he had been doing for years.

Because here was the thing he never told anyone, the thing that lived in the same locked room as the wheel: Odile had written to him too. Before. Before the accident, back when they were nineteen and twenty and Théo was alive and everything was summer. Not love letters — or not only, not recognizably, not in any form either of them would have signed their names to. Letters about books. About the orchard. About the future, laid out in hypotheticals so careful they were practically architectural drawings. *If a person were to stay in this town, what would keep them? If a person were to leave, what would they miss first?*

He had answered every one. He had kept every one. They lived in the shoebox too, underneath the returned envelopes, the way the living live above the dead.

And sometimes, in the worst years, he took them out and reread them and tried to find the exact sentence where the future had gone wrong. He never found it. There wasn't one. That was the cruelty of it — no single wrong word, no hinge moment, just a road at dusk and a hand on a wheel and twenty seconds of physics, and then ten years of silence stacked on top like sediment.

* * *

Why he finally came. People asked him that — Margaux asked, the estate agent asked, the woman at the bakery in Saint-Rémy asked with the frank curiosity of small towns everywhere — and he gave them the respectable answers. Remote work. Cheaper rent than the city. Always loved the region.

The true answer was a Tuesday in February, two months before the removal van.

He had gone to see his doctor about the insomnia — the kind where you sleep and sleep and wake exhausted, as though sleep itself were labour — and the doctor, a patient man with reading glasses pushed up into grey hair, had looked at him over the prescription pad and said, "Tell me about the dream."

"There's no dream."

"There's always a dream. You woke at four a.m. four times this month according to your own chart. What do you see at four a.m., Monsieur Vane?"

Elias looked out the window. A plane was crossing the sky, drawing a line that would be gone in minutes.

"A mailbox," he said. "I'm standing in front of a mailbox and I'm holding a letter and I can't remember whether I've posted it. Every night. Same mailbox. And in the dream I open the little door and look inside, and it's empty, and I don't know if that means I posted it or if it means there was never anything to post, and I wake up trying to remember her address."

"Whose address?"

He told him. All of it, or enough of it — the accident, the letters, the returns, the decade. The doctor listened the way good doctors listen, without hurrying, and at the end he took off his glasses and cleaned them and said the thing that undid him:

"You've spent ten years writing to a closed door. Have you considered that the letter isn't for her anymore? Have you considered that you're the one waiting for a reply?"

Elias walked out of that surgery into a cold bright morning and stood on the pavement and felt the ground tilt beneath him, gently, the way it tilts in dreams. Because the doctor was right, and he had known it for years, and knowing it changed nothing, because there are truths we hold at arm's length precisely so we can go on living.

But that night he took the shoebox out. He counted. Forty-one returned envelopes. And beneath them, folded small, a letter he had written eleven months ago and never posted at all — the last one, the one he'd written the night he learned the orchard was threatened, the night he'd read the rezoning notice in a regional paper and seen, in the developer's rendering of the proposed complex, the exact slope of the hill where Théo used to dare him to climb the oldest lemon tree.

He hadn't posted it because it broke all his rules. Two pages? It was nine. Never ask her to write back? The last line asked. Never tell her he loved her?

The last line told her.

He sat on the floor of his flat with the letter in his lap and the shoebox open beside him and he understood that he had been given a deadline. Not the council's — his own. The orchard was going to be bulldozed, the mailbox of his dream was going to be bulldozed, and one day soon there would be no address at all to fail to write to. Whatever he was going to do, the window was closing, and windows like that do not reopen. He was thirty-four years old. Théo would be thirty-four forever.

He rented the cottage the following week. He told himself it was proximity, not presumption — that living next door was not the same as knocking, that she could pretend he wasn't there, that he would wait for a sign and take none. He packed the shoebox in a box marked BOOKS, and then, at the last moment, took the final letter out of it and slid it into the inside pocket of his winter coat, where it lay against his chest like a second heartbeat, through the drive south, through the loading and unloading, through the whole long day of arriving.

* * *

Now, weeks later, he stood at his kitchen window in the blue hour before dawn, watching the light come up over the orchard, and the letter was still in the coat. The coat was still on its hook by the door. Every morning he touched the pocket on his way to make coffee, the way other men touch a photograph or a wedding ring, and every morning he did not take it out.

There had been the ladder. There had been the terrible, necessary conversation on her step — civil as diplomacy, brittle as old porcelain — and she had stood in her doorway with flour on one wrist and looked at him the way you look at weather you've been promised and hoped against. He had told her about the letters stopping. He had watched her absorb it, watched something shift behind her eyes that he couldn't name and didn't dare interpret.

And yesterday, harvesting lemons ahead of the storm, shoulder to shoulder among the trees his hands remembered better than his head did, she had laughed. Once. At something stupid he'd said about Verlaine and fallen fruit. It had lasted two seconds and he had carried it home like contraband.

Today there was a different errand. Today he had woken at four a.m. and not dreamed of the mailbox at all — he had dreamed of the orchard, whole and golden and saved, and Odile standing in it with her face turned toward him instead of away, and he had woken with the strange, terrifying lightness of a man who has stopped bracing for impact.

He took the coat off its hook. He took the letter out of the pocket. Nine pages, soft at the folds now from a winter of being carried. He set it on the kitchen table, weighted it with the lemon bowl she had once — years ago, another life — given Théo for their shared flat, and he looked at it for a long time.

Not yet. Not quite yet. Something was moving in the town — he'd seen Camille Rousseau's car outside the bakery twice this week, seen Odile's face when she mentioned her client, a tightening around the mouth he recognized because he'd spent a decade learning her face from memory. Something was coming. He could feel it the way the trees must feel a storm still hours out over the sea.

When it came, he decided, folding the letter back into his pocket, he would not be a man holding an unposted letter anymore.

He would be a man who handed it over.
