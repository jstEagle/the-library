## 8. Camille's Gambit

The excerpt went live at six in the morning, though Odile wouldn't learn this until nearly eight, when Marguerite from the bakery knocked on her door with a bag of croissants she hadn't ordered and an expression she couldn't mistake for anything but pity.

"You've seen it, then," Marguerite said, holding out the paper bag as if it were a peace offering from the entire town.

"Seen what?"

Marguerite's eyes flicked past her shoulder, toward the kitchen table where Odile's laptop sat closed among the jam jars and the unopened post. "It's on *La Lettre*. The literary site. Camille Arnaud posted an excerpt of her memoir. They're calling it—" Marguerite hesitated, and Odile watched her search for a kinder word and fail to find one. "They're calling it her most vulnerable work yet."

Odile took the bag. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady, the way one notices small mercies in the moments before catastrophe, and she thanked Marguerite in a voice that sounded like it belonged to someone else, someone composed, someone who had not just heard the word *vulnerable* attached to her own stolen life.

She didn't open the laptop right away. That was the strange part, looking back. She made coffee first. She fed the cat that wasn't hers but had adopted the orchard sometime in the spring. She stood at the window watching the light come up over the plum trees, gold spilling through the leaves the way it did every morning, indifferent to whatever was about to happen to her. The ordinary world going on with its ordinary beauty while somewhere out there, in the borderless country of the internet, her summer was being read by strangers over their breakfasts.

Then she sat down, and opened the page, and read the first line.

*That June, I learned that grief and desire are not opposites. They are twins.*

Her own sentence. Not paraphrased. Not inspired-by. Her own words, lifted whole from the letters Elias had written her at nineteen — no. No, that wasn't right either. It was worse than that. It was from the chapter she'd drafted for Camille three weeks ago, the lost-summer chapter, the one Camille had demanded be "more raw, more specific, more *lived-in*, darling, you write like you're hiding something." Odile had given her a version stripped of names, of places, of anything identifiable. She had sanded it down until it was smooth and anonymous and safe.

Camille had simply unsanded it.

*He was my brother's best friend. I want to say I hated him for what happened, but hatred requires a cleanness of feeling I have never possessed. What I felt was closer to worship with the lights turned off.*

Odile read it twice before her body allowed her to feel anything, which is a thing she would later understand: shock as a form of anesthesia, the mind sealing itself off from the wound so the wound could be examined at leisure. The prose was hers. Every cadence, every comma. But threaded through it now were details she had never written for Camille — details only she knew. The smell of cut grass and petrol the night of the accident. The way her brother Julien used to balance a plum on his nose to make her laugh. The exact phrasing of the last letter Elias had sent her, the one she'd never answered, the one whose final line — *I will keep writing even if you never write back, because the writing is how I stay* — appeared in the excerpt, verbatim, attributed to Camille's fictionalized lover.

She put her hand flat on the table to steady herself against a dizziness that had nothing to do with standing.

How. How did Camille have the letter. Odile had kept those letters locked in the drawer of her grandmother's writing desk for ten years, had never shown them to anyone, had transcribed fragments of them into the ghostwritten chapters only in the most oblique ways — or so she had believed. Had she been less careful than she thought? Had some phrase slipped through, some fingerprint of the original, and Camille — who read everything the way a jeweler reads stones, hunting for flaws and value in the same glance — had recognized it? Recognized it and then done what? Asked? No. Camille would never ask. Asking conceded that the material belonged to someone else.

And then the comments section. God help her, the comments section.

*Devastating. I cried into my coffee.*
*Who was he? Does anyone know if this is autobiographical?*
*The detail about the plums — you can't invent that. This really happened to her.*
*Camille Arnaud continues to prove she's the bravest writer of her generation.*

Bravest. Odile read the word and something in her chest went very quiet and very cold, the way a house goes cold when the fire has finally burned down to nothing. Bravest. For telling a story that wasn't hers, wearing a pain she had never earned, harvested from a woman who had spent a decade being paid to disappear.

* * *

By nine o'clock the town had read it. Of course the town had read it; Sainte-Anne-sur-Orge was the kind of place where news traveled faster than electricity, carried along the counter of the bakery and across the churchyard wall and through the open windows of the pharmacy, and a scandal involving one of their own — because despite everything, despite the rezoning fight and the two-seasons clause and her decade of careful distance, Odile Joubert was one of their own — a scandal like that ripened overnight.

She should have stayed inside. She knew that even as she tied her boots and picked up the harvest basket, because the plums wouldn't wait and neither would the season, and there was a stubbornness in her grandmother's blood that expressed itself, in moments of crisis, as an insistence on doing the next necessary thing. Grand-mère had buried a husband and two bad harvests in the same year and had still gone out each morning to check the trees. *The orchard doesn't care about your feelings, Odile. Neither does the work. That's why we love them.*

So she went out. Into the lanes, past the low stone walls, toward the orchard path that ran behind the church — and the looks began almost immediately.

They weren't cruel. That was somehow the unbearable part. Madame Roussel, watering her geraniums, gave her a nod weighted with such tender significance that Odile understood she was being looked at the way one looks at a woman in a novel, a tragic figure, a subject. Two teenagers on bicycles slowed as they passed her, and one whispered — not quietly enough — *"C'est elle."* At the corner by the war memorial, old Henri Delacroix touched his cap and said, "Courage, mademoiselle," in the tone reserved for widows and the bereaved, and Odile realized with a lurch that the entire town believed Camille's story was Camille's story, and that she — pale, silent, harvesting plums on schedule — was merely its living echo. Its ghost.

Which was, she thought, walking faster now, the basket handle biting into her fingers, exactly what she had trained the world to see. A ghostwriter who had ghosted her own life. She had spent ten years making herself illegible, and now the universe had performed the cruelest joke available: it had made her legible everywhere at once, in every screen and every conversation, under another woman's name.

The temptation arrived on schedule. She felt it waiting for her at the edge of the orchard like an old dog that had learned the sound of her footsteps: the temptation to disappear. To pack the laptop and the letters and the shame into the car and drive until Sainte-Anne was a rumor behind her, the way she had once driven away from a hospital corridor and a folded flag and a boy standing in the rain whom she couldn't look at without wanting to die. Disappearing was the one skill she had mastered. She had a talent for exits. She could leave a room, a town, a life, without disturbing the air.

She stood at the gate of the orchard with her hand on the latch and let the temptation speak its whole piece. *Go. You owe these people nothing. You owe Camille nothing. You owe the council nothing — let them have the land, let them pour concrete over your grandmother's grave-rows, none of it matters compared to this.* And beneath it, quieter, the older voice, the true one: *You always run. That's the family inheritance nobody wrote into the will.*

But the will. The will required two full seasons. She was one season and a month in. If she left now, the orchard reverted to the commune, and the developers would win by default, and Grand-mère's trees — the Williams pears, the Reine des Reinettes, the ancient quince at the far corner that had been old when the Republic was young — all of it would go under the bulldozers because Odile Joubert could not bear to be looked at.

Camille had counted on that. Odile saw it suddenly, standing among her own trees with the morning warming around her and the fruit heavy on the boughs. Camille had counted on it the way she counted on everything: precisely, coldly, with an accountant's love of leverage. Publish the excerpt, let the town gawk, let Odile feel the full weight of exposure — and then offer the escape hatch. *Come back to Paris, darling. Finish the book. We'll say the excerpt was a misunderstanding, I'll take the chapter down, everything can go back to normal.* Normal meaning: invisible. Normal meaning: mine.

The basket grew heavier as she worked. She picked without thinking, thumb testing the bloom of each plum, the purple skins coming away warm from the sun, and the rhythm of it steadied her the way it always did — hands busy, mind free to circle the wound. Because here was the thing she kept arriving at, from every direction, like water finding level: the excerpt was good. That was the humiliation under the humiliation. Stripped of her name, her history, her heartbreak, the words still landed. Readers were crying into their coffee over sentences she had written at her kitchen table at two in the morning, alone, unpaid, unnamed. The story survived its theft. Which meant the story was real, and strong, and hers — and no amount of borrowing could change whose pulse it carried.

*Let them think it's Camille's,* said the disappearing voice.

*No,* said the other voice, the one that had been getting louder since a man with a borrowed ladder had knocked on her door and said her name after ten years. *Let them find out it isn't.*

* * *

She found the printed pages pinned to the noticeboard outside the mairie at noon.

Someone — she suspected Madame Roussel, who had appointed herself the town's minister of information decades ago — had printed the excerpt and posted it publicly, the way one posts a lost-cat notice or the schedule of the fête, and a small crowd had gathered to read it in the manner of villagers reading a proclamation. Odile stopped at the edge of the square. She watched them. A woman she'd sold plums to all August pressed her hand to her mouth. The butcher's son read aloud, badly, stumbling over the beautiful parts. Someone said, "Imagine losing your brother and loving him all at once — imagine carrying that for ten years," and Odile thought, with a wild, unhinged flicker of humor: *I don't have to imagine.*

And then, cutting through the crowd's murmur, a voice she knew.

"—it's fiction, obviously." Camille. Standing by the fountain in white linen, sunglasses pushed up into her hair, radiating the particular grace of a woman performing modesty. She had come down from Paris. Of course she had. A gambit this size deserved its author on-site. "People assume everything I write is autobiography, and I've stopped correcting them. A writer's job is to make the invented feel lived and the lived feel inevitable. Don't you agree?" This last directed, with a smile like a paper cut, directly at Odile, who had drifted close enough to be addressed without quite consenting to exist.

"I'm sure I wouldn't know," Odile said.

"No," Camille agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't."

There it was. Barely a second long, hidden inside courtesy, visible only to the two of them and perhaps to anyone in the crowd who happened to be paying attention to the wrong things: the flash of the blade under the silk. *I know what you are. I know what I've taken. And you will smile and swallow it, because you always do.*

The crowd dispersed slowly, reluctantly, the way crowds do when they sense a drama they haven't fully decoded. Camille lingered. She drifted over to Odile with the unhurried confidence of a cat approaching a bird it has already decided is tame, and lowered her voice to the register she used for notes on drafts.

"You've seen the numbers, I assume. Forty thousand reads by ten a.m. My publisher wants to move the release date up. Which means I need the rest of the manuscript sooner." She examined her nails. "Chapter twelve especially. The confrontation scene. You have a gift for those."

"You published my life," Odile said. Quietly. The square was emptying; the fountain covered them. "Under your name."

"I published *a* story," Camille corrected. "One of many in a long career. If it resembles something of yours, well." A shrug, Parisian, weightless. "Writers borrow, chérie. You above all people know that. What else would you call what you do?"

The word *chérie* landed like a slap delivered with a glove. Odile felt her face do something dangerous — felt the composure crack along a fault line she hadn't known was there — and Camille saw it, and smiled wider, and Odile understood that this, too, was data to her. That her flinch had been collected and filed.

"Take the weekend," Camille said, already turning, already leaving, trailing scent and certainty. "Grieve your little episode of stage fright. Then send me chapter twelve. Or don't, and we'll both find out what happens to ghostwriters who develop opinions."

She walked away across the square, and several people greeted her warmly, and she waved like visiting royalty.

Odile stood alone by the fountain for a long time. The water made its small tireless sound. In her chest, betrayal and fury and — she was honest enough, finally, painfully, to name it — relief were tangled together in a knot she couldn't yet untie. Because underneath the humiliation, underneath the theft, one fact gleamed like a coin at the bottom of a well: the secret was out. Not her name, not yet. But the story was loose in the world, alive, *read*, and it could never again be fully buried, fully denied, fully owned by anyone but the woman who had lived it.

All she had to do was claim it. All she had to do was stop running.

She thought of Elias — of course she thought of him; she had been thinking of him in the background of every hour since the removal van — and wondered what he did when he read it. Whether he'd recognized himself in the fictionalized lover with his borrowed sentences. Whether he was angry on her behalf, or hurt, or whether he'd simply closed his laptop and looked out his window at her lit kitchen and understood, before she did, what Camille had done and why.

She was still standing there when her phone buzzed. A message from an unknown number, short, no signature:

*I know that chapter isn't hers. I know whose it is. Come to the cottage — I need to show you something before you decide what to do next.*

Odile stared at the screen. The fountain murmured. Somewhere behind her ribs, the old instinct to delete, to deny, to vanish raised its head — and found, for the first time in ten years, that it no longer commanded unanimous consent.

She started walking toward the cottage.
