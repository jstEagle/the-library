## 1. The Last Ferry Season

The groom ran at 4:17 in the afternoon, and Bram Voss would remember the exact time because he was looking at his watch when it happened.

He was standing at the back of the Rosecliff Conservatory with a headset on and a clipboard he no longer needed — everything had been rehearsed twice, every candle placed to the millimeter, every petal of the two thousand garden roses imported from a farm in Ecuador that charged by the stem and delivered like clockwork. The string quartet had just finished the second movement of something Brahms-adjacent that Delia Chen's mother had insisted upon over Bram's quiet objection that it sounded like a funeral for someone important. Four hundred guests sat in white folding chairs on either side of an aisle strewn with petals so fresh they still smelled green.

And then Marcus Chen — six foot one, orthopedic surgeon, owner of a smile that had photographed beautifully at the engagement party eleven months ago — set down the card with his handwritten vows, looked at Delia's face for a long moment with an expression Bram would later describe to no one as *the look of a man doing arithmetic*, turned, and walked. Not ran, at first. Walked, with terrible calm, up the aisle between four hundred people who thought he was having a moment of emotion, through the conservatory doors, and then — once the glass doors sighed shut behind him — he ran. Across the lawn. Past the valet stand. Into a yellow cab that may or may not have been waiting for him, though Bram would spend the next three weeks wondering about that.

The quartet played on for another eight bars because musicians are professionals and professionals finish the phrase. Then the first violinist looked at Bram, and Bram gave the smallest shake of his head, and the music died mid-note, and four hundred people made the sound that four hundred people make when a beautiful thing breaks in front of them — not a gasp exactly, more like a single collective intake of breath, the sound of a room inhaling and forgetting to exhale.

Bram walked up the aisle. It took forty seconds. He used them to compose his face into something that was not pity, because pity is the one thing a bride cannot survive in the first minute. He knelt beside Delia Chen in her twelve-thousand-dollar gown — he knew the price because he'd negotiated the sample sale himself — and he said, quietly, so that only she could hear:

"Delia. There is a car out front. There is a suite at the Whitmore with your name on it, already booked, already paid. Your sister has your bag. You do not have to make a single decision right now except whether you want me to walk you out or clear the room first."

She looked at him with mascara beginning its slow dark migration down her cheeks, and she said, "How long have you known?"

And there it was. The question under all the other questions, the one that would keep him up that night and several nights after.

"I didn't," Bram said. And it was true, mostly. He hadn't *known*. But he had noticed things — the way Marcus checked his phone during cake tastings, the way the couple sat through their menu consultation like colleagues reviewing a merger, the way Marcus had asked, three weeks ago, in a voice too casual, whether the deposits were refundable. Bram had noticed all of it and filed it under *pre-wedding nerves*, because noticing it under anything else would have meant doing something about it, and doing something about it would have meant admitting what he privately believed: that most weddings are beautiful lies that two people agree to tell each other in front of witnesses, and that his job was not to prevent the lie but to stage it so well that everyone, briefly, believed it.

"I didn't know," he repeated, and helped her stand.

* * *

By seven that evening the conservatory was empty, the roses were being boxed for donation to a hospital lobby that wouldn't know their provenance, and Bram sat alone at the head table eating cold filet off a charger plate because wasting good catering felt like a sin even on the worst day of his career.

His phone buzzed against the linen. Then again. Then a third time, which meant it wasn't a client.

It was Priya, his office manager, his sole remaining employee, his last line of defense against the truth about Voss & Co. Events.

"Don't check the email," she said, before he'd said hello.

"What email."

"The Hollis wedding. October. They're moving to another planner."

"They signed in March."

"They read the news about the Chen thing. Someone posted video, Bram. It's everywhere. 'Groom flees luxury wedding' — there's a drone shot. Somebody's aunt had a drone."

Bram closed his eyes. Somewhere behind him, a member of the waitstaff dropped a tray of champagne flutes, and the sound was very small and very final, like the last coin in a machine that keeps asking for more.

"Priya. How much is in the operating account?"

A pause. He heard her breathing change, the way people breathe when they're deciding whether to lie to you and then decide, generously, not to.

"Enough for payroll through November," she said. "If nothing goes wrong."

"Nothing's gone right since June."

"That's not true. The Ibarra anniversary—"

"Lost money on the Ibarras. I comped the florist overrun myself." He rubbed his eyes with the hand not holding the phone. "Go home, Priya. It's Friday. Tell the team Monday—"

"There is no team, Bram. It's you and me and a freelancer who does our lighting. That's the team."

He laughed, which surprised them both. "Right. Go home anyway."

He stayed another hour. He walked the empty conservatory while the staff struck the set, watching two thousand roses go into boxes, watching the white chairs fold and stack, watching the string quartet pack instruments worth more than his car into cases worn soft at the handles. The cleanup after a failed wedding is the same as the cleanup after a perfect one; that was the lesson of the day, if he needed one. The machinery doesn't care. You build the cathedral, and whether God shows up or not, Tuesday morning it all comes down.

He had started this business nine years ago with a borrowed van and a conviction — sincere, unironic, almost embarrassing in retrospect — that love deserved beautiful rooms. That ceremony mattered. That if you built the space correctly, with the light falling right and the music entering at the correct moment, two people might actually feel what they were pretending to feel, and the pretense might curdle into something real, the way a performance can become a life. He had believed weddings were not lies but *rehearsals for honesty*.

Nine years, four hundred events, and one abandoned altar of his own later, he believed they were theater. Gorgeous, expensive, emotionally load-bearing theater, and some nights the actors meant it and some nights they didn't, and the audience paid either way.

His own wedding had been scheduled for a Saturday in late September, seven years ago now. He did not think about the date. He had gotten very good, over seven years, at not thinking about the date, the way a man gets good at not touching a bruise.

* * *

Monday brought the creditors.

Not literally — literally it brought emails, letters on law-firm letterhead, and a voicemail from the bank's small-business officer whose tone had migrated over the past quarter from *warm professional concern* to *procedural regret*. Bram spent the morning at his desk in the office above the dry cleaner on Calloway Street, a space he'd chosen years ago for the light and now tolerated for the cheap rent, working through the arithmetic of his own collapse with the same spreadsheets he used for clients. Line items. Deposits held in trust. Refundable versus non-refundable. The word *insolvent* kept trying to insert itself into his thinking and he kept deleting it, the way you'd swat a fly at a funeral.

Priya appeared in his doorway at noon holding two coffees and an expression he recognized from funerals.

"You should see this before you see anything else," she said, and handed him her tablet.

"It's a bill?"

"It's an inquiry. A big one." She set the tablet flat on his desk rather than handing it over, which he understood, dimly, was because she wanted to watch his face. "Delphine Marsh."

The name landed somewhere in the part of his brain where he kept celebrity gossip he pretended not to know. Delphine Marsh. Twenty-nine years old. Heiress to whatever the Marsh fortune was built on — shipping, he thought, or logistics, old money wearing new clothes. Famous mostly for being famous, photographed leaving restaurants, engaged last spring to some European financier whose name Bram couldn't retrieve. Her wedding had been written up in three magazines before a venue existed.

"She wants—" Bram scrolled. Read. Scrolled again. "This is real?"

"We verified. Her assistant called twice while you were with the bank guy. They want a full-service production. High profile. And here's the part—" Priya leaned in, and he watched her try to keep her voice level and fail. "They've already chosen the venue. It's not negotiable, apparently. Some island. Saltglass. An orchard there — Marrow Orchard? Never heard of it. But the photos are insane, Bram. Old apple trees, a stone barn, water on three sides. Delphine saw it in some architectural digest and refused to consider anywhere else. The wedding's in six weeks. Six weeks, full fee, half up front."

Bram stared at the photographs. He had planned events in vineyards and museums and a private botanical garden that required permits from two governments, but he had never seen anything like this — rows of gnarled trees marching down toward gray-blue water, a barn with doors tall enough to drive a hay wagon through, a stone wall gone silver with lichen. It looked less like a venue than like a place where something had happened, long ago, and left its shape behind.

"Six weeks is impossible for a production this size," he said automatically.

"Yes," Priya agreed. "Also, we're bankrupt in November. So."

He kept scrolling. The contract draft was attached, along with a venue agreement requiring counter-signature from the property's owners. His eye went to the signature block the way a doctor's eye goes to the malignancy on a scan — professionally, involuntarily.

Two names. One line already executed, a confident slanted script he didn't recognize. The second line blank, pending.

The second name printed beneath it, though. Printed, typed, in plain black letters, the way names are typed on contracts when someone's lawyer has drawn them up without any sentiment whatsoever:

**Wren Halloway.**

The office got very quiet. Outside the window, the dry cleaner's steam press hissed and released, hissed and released, keeping time like something patient.

Seven years was a long time. Long enough for a name to become just a name, a word on paper, five letters that shouldn't mean anything more than *Chen* or *Marsh* or *Ibarra*. He told himself this. He was very reasonable about it. He read the name three times and felt nothing except a slight coldness, the way you feel nothing when you press on a scar until suddenly you do.

"Bram?" Priya said. "You've gone weird."

"It's nothing." He set the tablet down with great care, the way you set down something that might go off. "Saltglass Island. Where is it, exactly?"

"Two ferries north of Harwick Point. Why?"

"No reason." He picked up his coffee and found it had gone cold without his permission, which seemed, today, entirely appropriate. "Book me on tomorrow's ferry."

Priya hesitated in the doorway. She was twenty-six and sharp as a tack and she had worked for him long enough to know when her boss was lying, and also long enough to know which lies were structural — load-bearing lies, the kind you don't pull out of a wall.

"Six weeks," she said. "Half up front. We can survive on this, Bram. Whatever's making your face do that — it can't be worse than November."

He looked at the photograph of Marrow Orchard again. The east gate, in the corner of the frame, half open, leading somewhere the camera couldn't follow.

"No," he said. "It can't."

But his hand, resting on the tablet's edge, was not quite steady, and the name Wren Halloway sat on the signature line like weather coming in from the sea.
