## 4. Eleven Years of Tuesdays

The house smelled the way it always had — lemon polish and lavender and something faintly burnt, as though his mother had never quite forgiven herself for the toast of 1997. Theo Marchetti stood in the doorway of her bedroom with a roll of packing tape in one hand and a box labeled MISC. in his careful surgeon's print, and he could not make himself cross the threshold.

Three days he'd been back in Saltmere. Three days of sleeping in his childhood bed, which was too short for him now, of drinking coffee from a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, which had been a gift from a man named Quentin Okafor, though the mug had long since stopped reminding anyone of that fact. Three days of telling himself he was here to do a job: empty the house, settle the estate, sign what needed signing, drive back to Boston by Sunday.

He had not gone to the bakery.

He told himself this was efficiency, not avoidance. There was a list. The list did not include sourdough.

* * *

He started with her dresser, because it was the least dangerous room, or so he believed. Her clothes went into donation bags — the green cardigan she wore every Christmas, the silk scarf she'd bought in Lisbon and never once worn without apologizing for the price. In the second drawer down, beneath a layer of tissue paper she'd clearly laid there herself, he found the photographs.

Not an album. A shoebox. His mother had kept her real archive in a shoebox, the way ordinary people keep their actual lives in places no archivist would think to look.

The first photo was of him at nine, gap-toothed, holding up a fish nearly as big as he was on the Saltmere pier. He remembered that day. He remembered who had taken the picture. He turned it over: *Theo & Q, August 2003, Q behind camera.* His mother's handwriting, small and slanted.

Q behind camera. Of course. Quentin had hated being photographed; he'd claimed it was because he blinked, but Theo had known even then that it was because Quentin watched people the way other people breathed — constantly, involuntarily, hungrily — and being watched back felt to him like drowning.

The next photograph stopped Theo's hands.

His mother's kitchen table, set for two. Two plates of something golden and braided — challah, he realized, though neither of them was Jewish; Quentin had learned to bake it from a library book one winter and made it every Friday for a month until Theo left for his residency and the tradition, whatever it was, apparently hadn't ended. The date on the back read *October 2019.* Six years after Theo had last stood in that kitchen. His mother was laughing at something out of frame, and across from her sat a man with flour on his forearms and gray coming in early at his temples, and it took Theo three full seconds to understand that the man was Quentin.

Quentin, who was supposed to have followed him to Boston. Who had said, the night before Theo's residency began, *I'll be right behind you, I just need to finish the novel, I just need to save some money, I just need—* and then had never come. No letter. No call. Eleven years of silence so complete that Theo had eventually done what surgeons do with necrotic tissue: cut it away, closed the wound, and refused to look at the scar.

Except the wound had apparently been alive this whole time, sitting at his mother's table every week, eating challah.

*October 2019,* he thought. *A Tuesday.* He didn't know why he was sure it was a Tuesday. He just was.

* * *

By afternoon he had moved from the dresser to the living room, and the shoebox had become an excavation site spread across the floor. Theo sat cross-legged among it like an archaeologist who kept finding himself in the strata.

There were more photos. Quentin at the bakery's opening, 2016, ribbon of flour-dusted bunting across the door, looking terrified and proud in equal measure. Quentin and his grandmother — the lighthouse keeper, the legend — on the seawall, the old woman mid-laugh, Quentin caught looking at her like she was the sun. Quentin's thirtieth birthday, a cake shaped like a stack of books, his mother's arm around his shoulders while Quentin grinned at the camera with the unguarded joy Theo had spent eleven years convincing himself he'd imagined.

And clippings. That was the part that undid him. His mother had saved clippings — actual newspaper clippings, cut with scissors, edges yellowing — from the *Saltmere Sentinel*. A review of the bakery from 2018: *"Okafor's cardamom buns alone justify the ferry fare."* A piece about the lighthouse's centennial, quoting Quentin's grandmother. And then, folded twice, soft as cloth from handling:

*"Saltmere Writer Wins Regional Short Fiction Prize."*

Theo unfolded it slowly. There was a photograph of Quentin at a reading, standing at a podium in what looked like the library, holding a book. The article said his story, "The Keeper's Arithmetic," had been published in a journal Theo had actually heard of — had, in fact, rejected submissions to once upon a time, back when he still pretended he'd write again someday. The story was about a lighthouse keeper who counted ships instead of letting herself be counted.

His mother had underlined one sentence in pencil: *"'Grief is just love with nowhere to go,' Okafor told the audience. 'I try to give mine somewhere.'"*

Theo put the clipping down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

She'd known. His mother had known where Quentin was — had known him, had loved him, had eaten his bread every Tuesday for years — and she had never told him. He tried to summon anger and found something worse underneath it: understanding. Because what would she have said? *Theo, Quentin's here, Quentin stayed, Quentin asks about you* — and then what? He'd have had to decide. And deciding meant risking the answer to the question he'd carried since 2014, the question he'd never let himself ask out loud because asking it would mean admitting it mattered:

Why wasn't I worth staying for?

* * *

He worked until the light went blue and then gold and then gone, and when he finally surfaced it was dark and he was hungry in the hollow, graceless way of a man who has been eating gas station food for three days. There was nothing in the house but tea and the ghost of toast.

The bakery's lights were on. Of course they were — it was barely eight, and the whole town seemed to orbit that warm square of window the way his mother's bridge club orbited gossip. Theo stood across the street in the cold longer than any reasonable person would stand anywhere, watching a silhouette move behind the glass. Broad shoulders. Flour-white apron. The particular way Quentin pushed his hair off his forehead with the back of his wrist, wrist-first, so he wouldn't get dough in it — a gesture Theo's body remembered before his mind could stop it.

He didn't go in. He walked back to the empty house and ate dry cereal over the sink like a divorced man in a movie, and hated himself a little for the drama of it.

But lying awake in the too-short bed, staring at the ceiling glow-in-the-dark stars he'd stuck up there at eleven and never removed, Theo finally let the thought arrive — the one that had been circling all day like a gull over a fish market.

He had built the whole architecture of his grief on a single assumption: that Quentin had chosen Saltmere over him. That the novel, the money, the *I'll be right behind you* had been a polite fiction, and Quentin had simply — stayed. Stayed the way water stays in a low place. Stayed because leaving was hard and Saltmere was easy and Theo was, in the end, dispensable.

But the photographs told another story. A man who had learned to bake his mother's grief into bread. A man who had won a prize writing about counting ships instead of being counted. A man whose grandmother had raised him inside a lighthouse — a building whose entire purpose was to say *here, here, here, don't crash.*

Invisible is just another word for afraid. Theo didn't know yet that the old keeper had said that; he only knew that Quentin had spent his whole life half-hidden, even from the people who loved him most. Even from Theo. Especially from Theo.

So maybe the silence hadn't been a verdict. Maybe it had been a symptom. Maybe Quentin had stood in front of a mailbox eleven years ago the way Theo was standing in front of a lit bakery window tonight — wanting, unable, going home hungry.

It didn't fix anything. It didn't un-betray the betrayal, if that's even what it was. But it shifted something in Theo's chest, the way a tide shifts a boat on its mooring line: same position, different tension.

Tomorrow, he decided. Tomorrow he would buy a cardamom bun and stand at the counter and see what eleven years had done to Quentin Okafor's face when it had to look at him.

He fell asleep before he could talk himself out of it, which was, he suspected, the only way he was ever going to manage anything brave in this town.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain. Theo shaved, put on a shirt that wasn't wrinkled, and walked the four blocks to the bakery with his hands in his pockets and his pulse doing something medically embarrassing.

The bell above the door chimed. The smell hit him first — yeast and cardamom and caramelized sugar, warm as a held breath — and then Quentin looked up from behind the counter, and the tray in his hands tilted, and for one suspended second Theo watched eleven years happen to another person's face in real time.

"Welcome to Okafor's," Quentin said, voice only slightly wrecked. "What can I get you?"

Behind the register, half-hidden by a stack of pastry boxes, sat a dented tin — the kind flour came in — and Theo noticed it only because Quentin's eyes flicked to it, once, quick as a blink, before coming back to him.

Theo ordered a bun.
