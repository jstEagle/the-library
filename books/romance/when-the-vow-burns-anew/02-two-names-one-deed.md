## 2. Two Names, One Deed

The bus let Hazel off at the end of a gravel road that the map app insisted was Bramble Hollow's Main Street, and she stood there a long moment with her cello case in one hand and her suitcase in the other, listening to the sound of nowhere. Gulls somewhere out over the water. Wind moving through grass that had never been mowed with any urgency. The distant, rhythmic clank of a buoy that had been ringing the same note since she was nine years old.

She had forgotten how loud silence could be.

The orchard began where the road ended — or rather, it had once begun there, marked by a hand-painted sign her grandfather had made before she was born: *Lockridge Orchard*, the letters faded to ghosts of themselves, the O tilted like a man leaning against a fence post to watch the world go by. Beyond it, rows of apple trees ran up the gentle slope toward the farmhouse, and they were drowning. Blackberry canes had swallowed the lower rows whole. Grass stood waist-high between the trunks. Some of the trees were dead — she could tell even from here, their bare limbs raised against the sky like questions no one had stayed to answer.

Fifteen years. It looked like fifteen years.

Hazel set down her suitcase and pressed her palm flat against the sign, the way she used to when she was small and the wood was warm from the sun. Her hands had been steady then. They had been steady for twenty-nine years, through ten thousand hours of practice, through conservatory auditions and competitions and the slow, brutal climb to a chair in one of the great orchestras of the world. They had been steady for every note of the first half of the Whitmore Hall concert, the career-defining performance, the night the critics were already calling hers.

And then, in the second movement, in front of a thousand people, they hadn't been.

She curled her fingers now, reflexively, testing. Steady. Of course they were steady. That was the cruelest joke of it — here on a gravel road in the middle of nowhere, her hands worked perfectly. It was only the music that broke them.

*Distance will help,* her manager had said, in the tone people use for the terminally optimistic. *Get away, rest, come back when you're ready.* So she had gotten away. She had gotten all the way away, to the one place on earth she'd sworn never to return, because her grandmother's lawyer had sent a letter with a ferry schedule tucked inside it and the words *your inheritance* underlined twice, and Hazel had been so hollowed out by shame that anywhere sounded better than the city where everyone had watched her fail.

The path up through the orchard was barely a path. She carried the cello case in front of her like a shield, branches catching at her sleeves, burrs attaching themselves to her hem with the enthusiasm of small green creditors. Halfway up the slope she stopped at a tree she recognized — an old Gravenstein, gnarled and enormous, its trunk scarred where a ladder had leaned against it for decades. She had climbed this tree at seven years old with a boy named Jonah Reyes, both of them sticky with stolen apples, laughing so hard they couldn't breathe.

She did not let herself think about Jonah Reyes. She was very good at not thinking about Jonah Reyes; she'd had fifteen years of practice.

The farmhouse came into view around the last bend of trees, and Hazel stopped walking.

It was worse than she remembered and better than she feared, which is to say: the porch sagged, the paint had gone gray and peeling, ivy had colonized the eastern wall entirely — but someone had replaced the broken kitchen window. Someone had cleared the blackberries back from the foundation. Someone had nailed a new hinge onto the gate, and the gate swung shut behind her with a click that sounded, absurdly, like a verdict.

There was smoke coming from the chimney.

Hazel stood very still. In her experience, abandoned farmhouses did not produce smoke. She was still constructing explanations — a caretaker, a squatter, some elaborate mistake — when the front door opened and a man stepped out onto the porch with a mug of coffee in his hand and stopped as if he'd hit glass.

Jonah Reyes was thirty-three years old, and time had done to him what it does to men who stay: it had written him into the landscape. He was broader than the boy she remembered, darker through the shoulders, his jaw shadowed with a beard he kept short, his hair longer than fashion allowed in cities. He wore a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled and work gloves hanging out of his back pocket, and he looked at her across forty feet of overgrown yard with an expression she could only describe as the weather turning.

"Huh," he said.

"I—" Hazel's voice came out higher than she intended. She lowered the cello case to the ground, mostly so she'd have something to do with her hands. "I got a letter. From Ellery Pratt, the attorney. About my grandmother's estate."

"I know about the letter." He drank his coffee without hurry, watching her over the rim of the mug. "I wrote half of it."

"You—what?"

"Come inside," he said. "You'll want to sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

He almost smiled. It wasn't a kind smile. "You're standing in a burr patch, Hazel."

She looked down. She was standing in a burr patch. She picked her way out of it with as much dignity as the situation permitted, retrieved her cello, and followed him up the porch steps, acutely aware of the boards groaning under both their weights, of the smell of coffee and woodsmoke and, beneath it, apples rotting sweetly somewhere in the grass.

Inside, the farmhouse smelled like her childhood and didn't look like it. The same wide-plank floors, the same view of the orchard through windows that needed washing. But there was a new stove, a table built from raw lumber, tools hung on pegboard with the precision of a man who believed everything had its place. He had lived here. He had been living here, alone, tending this ruin, while she played concert halls on three continents.

He took a folded paper from the drawer of the sideboard — her grandmother's sideboard, the one with the lion's feet — and laid it on the table between them.

"Read it."

It was a deed. She read it twice, because the first time her eyes refused to cooperate. *Lockridge-Reyes Orchard, parcel 14-B,* and then two names, typed in the dispassionate font of legal documents:

**HAZEL MARIE LOCKRIDGE — fifty percent interest.**
**JONATHAN AMOS REYES — fifty percent interest.**

"That's not possible," Hazel said. "This was my grandmother's land. Ours. The Lockridges'."

"It was your grandmother's land," Jonah agreed. "And my mother worked it for thirty years for less than nothing, because your grandmother paid her in trust and promises instead of money. When your grandmother got sick, she sat them both down with Ellery Pratt and they redrew the deed. Fifty-fifty. Your grandmother said—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes, some old grief he shut the door on quickly, "—she said the orchard belonged to both families whether the paper said so or not. She just finally made the paper honest."

"My grandmother died eight months ago. Why am I only hearing about this now?"

"Because she made Ellery wait." He said it flatly. "Her instructions. The letter goes out when it goes out. I got mine the same week you got yours. Difference is, I was already here."

Hazel set the deed down carefully, as if it might go off. "So you've known. For months. You knew I'd be coming eventually, and you didn't—"

"What? Call you?" Now the smile came, and it was sharp enough to cut. "What would I have said, Hazel? *Hi, welcome home, sorry about the hands, I saw the video too?*" 

The words landed exactly where he aimed them. She felt her face go white and hated him for it, and hated herself more for letting him see.

"There were videos," she said quietly. "Of course there were videos."

"Four million views," Jonah said. "The last time I checked, which was more times than I'm proud of." He turned to the counter and poured a second cup of coffee, set it in front of her without asking if she wanted it, an old habit from a life neither of them lived anymore. "Ellery's letter says we have until the harvest festival. Six weeks. Then we decide together what happens to this place. Sell, keep, split — whatever 'together' means when the two people involved haven't spoken since they were eighteen."

"We don't need six weeks." Hazel wrapped her hands around the cup. The heat felt good; her fingers always ran cold now, another gift of that night at Whitmore Hall. "I want to sell. My half, your half, whatever the lawyers call it. List it, take the money, and I'll be gone before the frost."

Something flickered across his face — fast, quickly buried, but she caught it. Something that wasn't relief.

"No."

"No?"

"I'm not selling." He braced his knuckles on the table, and she saw the calluses on them, the split nail on his index finger healing crooked. Hands that worked. "I spent four years getting these trees producing again. Four years clearing, grafting, hauling water during the drought summer because the irrigation failed and nobody was coming to help me. There's a crop on those trees right now that is worth real money for the first time since you left. I am not selling it out from under myself so you can have a clean exit."

"A clean exit." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "You think I *exited*? You think I left because I wanted—"

"I think you left," Jonah said, "and the reasons stopped mattering somewhere around year five."

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, wind moved through the orchard, and somewhere in the tall grass a pheasant startled into flight, wings hammering the air.

"You don't know anything about why I left," Hazel said.

"You're right." He straightened up. "I know you bought a bus ticket with money I loaned you for a cello repair. I know your grandmother drove you to the ferry herself and told the whole town you'd won a scholarship. I know you called Marisol's house exactly once, got me on the phone, and hung up without saying a word. And then I know fifteen years happened." His voice stayed level the entire time, which somehow made it worse. "So no, Hazel. I don't know why. I just know that you did."

She should say something. There were things to say — there had been things to say for fifteen years, stacked up like unsent letters — but her throat closed around all of them, and what came out instead was the coldest thing available, the lawyer's word, the safe word:

"The deed says fifty percent. You can't sell without me, and I can't sell without you. Fine. Then we're at an impasse."

"We are."

"So what do you propose?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and she realized he was deciding something, weighing her the way he'd weigh a tree — whether it was worth the work of saving or better off cut down and burned.

"Ellery's terms," he said finally. "We run the harvest together. Both names on the work, both names on the books. You wanted distance from the city? Congratulations. This is the farthest from it you can get and still have cell service. At the festival, we decide. Until then, you don't touch my trees without asking, and I don't ask why you're really here."

"I told you why I'm here."

"No," Jonah said, picking up his coffee and moving toward the door, "you told me what you're running from. Different thing." He paused on the threshold, silhouetted against the bright overgrown yard, and delivered the last line without turning around: "Bunkhouse is behind the house. It's got a bed, a stove, and a roof that mostly holds. House is mine. Farmhouse was yours once — maybe you remember the way to your own room."

Then he was gone, boots on the porch stairs, and Hazel sat alone in her grandmother's kitchen holding a cup of coffee she hadn't drunk, listening to the sound of him walking away from her for the second time in her life.

On the table, the deed lay open between them like a wound that had finally been given two names.

She should leave. Book the ferry back, call Ellery, let the lawyers fight it out in conference rooms where no one had to look at anyone. It would be easy. She was good at leaving; it was the one thing she'd never fumbled.

Instead, Hazel carried her cello into the bunkhouse, unzipped the case, and lifted the instrument out into the dusty light. Her hands, traitorous, faithful hands, settled into position on the strings without being asked.

She did not play. But she held it a long time, and outside, faintly, she could hear the sound of a man who hated her starting the tractor anyway, because the trees didn't care whose name was on the deed, and the harvest wouldn't wait for either of them.

Six weeks. She could survive anything for six weeks.

She was wrong about that, though it would take her most of those weeks to find out how.

* * *

That evening, unpacking the few things she'd brought, Hazel found the attorney's letter again at the bottom of her suitcase and reread it by lamplight. Most of it was boilerplate. But the last paragraph, the one she'd skimmed past on the ferry, snagged her eye now:

*Your grandmother additionally instructed me to convey the following, verbatim: "Tell my granddaughter the orchard keeps its own accounts. Tell her some debts are paid in apples."*

Hazel read it three times. Somewhere beyond the bunkhouse wall, the tractor fell silent, and in the new quiet she could hear the wind working through the rows, hundreds of trees whispering in a language she had almost forgotten she spoke.

Debts paid in apples. Her grandmother had never said a single thing in eighty-one years that didn't mean exactly what it meant and something else besides.

Hazel folded the letter, put it under her pillow like a child with a secret, and lay awake listening to the orchard breathe, telling herself the tightness in her chest was resentment.

It was, mostly.
