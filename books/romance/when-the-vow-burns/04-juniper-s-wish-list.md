## 4. Juniper's Wish List

The thing about homework was that it got done faster in the shop.

Juniper had tested this theory scientifically, the way her teacher Ms. Alvarez said real scientists did: one week at the kitchen table (average time to finish math sheet: forty minutes, plus crying), one week in the back of Eastwick Books on the big leather chair that smelled like vanilla and dust (average time: twelve minutes, no crying). The variable, she'd decided, was the sound of pages turning. It was like the books were all whispering the answers to each other, and some of the answers drifted over to you if you sat still enough.

So on Tuesday afternoons she did her homework in the shop, feet hooked over the armrest of the leather chair, pencil between her teeth, and pretended not to listen to everything.

Pretending not to listen was a skill. She'd been practicing since she was six, which meant she was basically an expert now. Adults talked differently when they thought you weren't paying attention — their voices went softer and more true, like the difference between singing a song and just humming it while you washed dishes. Dad hummed a lot. Juniper had gotten very good at hearing the songs underneath.

Today the song underneath was coming from the front counter, where Dad stood with the telephone pressed to his ear and his shoulders up around his ears like he was trying to make himself smaller, which was silly, because Dad was already the smallest-shouldered person in any room and also the best one, so shrinking seemed like a waste of effort.

"No," Dad said into the phone. "No, I understand what it says, Ms. Okafor. I'm asking whether there's any — any flexibility in how it reads."

A pause. Juniper wrote *7 x 8 = 54* and then, because Wren had once told her that mathematicians were allowed to change their minds, crossed it out and wrote 56.

"I see." Dad's voice did the thing where it went flat and polite at the same time, like a book with a beautiful cover and nothing inside. "And if an heir simply... doesn't attend. With anyone."

Another pause. Longer.

"Then the claim lapses. Yes. You said." A breath. "Thank you, Ms. Okafor. No, I won't — I'll think about it. Thank you."

Dad hung up the phone very gently, the way you put down something sleeping, and then stood there looking at the shelf behind the register where they kept the local history section, which nobody ever bought except Mr. Hobbes from the hardware store, who bought them all twice.

Juniper bent over her math sheet and became a child who was absolutely, completely doing homework.

* * *

She gave it eleven minutes. Eleven was a good number for pretending; anything shorter felt rude, anything longer felt like lying.

"Dad? What's 'lapses' mean?"

Dad flinched. Actually flinched, hand to chest, like she'd thrown something. "Junie. How long have you been sitting there?"

"Since four. What's lapses?"

"It means..." Dad straightened a stack of receipts that didn't need straightening. Dad did that when a question was too big: organized something small instead. "It means when something runs out. Like library borrowing privileges."

"Whose ran out?"

"Nobody's." And then, because Dad could not lie to her — this was a documented fact of the universe, right up there with gravity and the existence of the Loch Ness Monster (real, Wren said, and Wren had been to Scotland) — Dad added, quieter, "Something boring. Grown-up paperwork. Eat your apple."

The apple was on the counter by the register, cut into slices, because Dad still cut her apples even though she was nine and perfectly capable of biting a whole apple like a normal person. Juniper ate a slice and watched Dad pretend to alphabetize the poetry section, which was already alphabetical, which was how she knew the grown-up paperwork was not boring at all.

Boring things made Dad relaxed. This thing made Dad do the receipt-straightening.

Juniper finished her math sheet in record time and began, in the back page of her notebook, under a heading she drew a box around so it would look official, a list.

**REASONS DAD SHOULD GET A PERSON**

She underlined it twice. Then she thought about it, and added *(a person who stays)*, because there was an important difference between people who came and people who stayed, and she was nine, not stupid.

Item one practically wrote itself:

**1. He talks to the portrait of Grandma Odile when he thinks nobody's home.**

This was true. Juniper had heard him through the storeroom door last month, saying things like *"I know, I know"* and *"you always did like making things difficult"* in the voice he used for family, which was different from every other voice he had. Talking to a painting was fine as far as it went. But paintings never answered, and Juniper felt strongly that Dad deserved answers.

**2. He only makes tea for one.**

This sounded small but wasn't. Juniper had counted. When Wren was in the back room working, Dad made two cups. When it was just them, one cup, and he held it with both hands like it was a small animal he was keeping warm. Two cups changed the whole shape of a kitchen. Juniper had read enough books to know that kitchens were where love lived in most stories, right after forests and slightly ahead of lighthouses.

**3. He laughs at Wren's jokes before the joke is even finished.**

This was maybe the most important item so far. Dad laughed politely at other people's jokes — the kind of laugh that was really just a sound you made so people wouldn't feel bad. But Wren told some long winding story about a train in Lisbon or a wedding in Chicago or a librarian in Portland who repaired books with beeswax and spite, and Dad would start laughing halfway through, at nothing yet, at the promise of the joke, like his body knew something good was coming and couldn't wait. Juniper had never seen Dad laugh early for anybody else. Not once. Not even for Uncle Theo, who was technically funny.

**4. Wren knows about cities.**

Juniper paused here, pencil hovering, because item four needed care. Wren had a rolling case full of tools and glue pots and rolls of gold leaf, and hands stained different colors depending on the week (currently green, from re-backing somebody's botany atlas), and had slept in — this was the part that mattered — *fourteen cities*. Lisbon. Vienna. Montréal. A place in Portugal called Porto that Wren described as "what would happen if a book decided to become a city." Wren told stories about these places while working, the way other people hummed, and the stories had smells and wrong turns and old women in them, and Juniper would sit on the workbench stool and forget her homework entirely until Dad appeared in the doorway with that look that said *homework*, and Wren would wink at her, and the story would fold itself away like a letter being tucked into an envelope, saved for later.

Cities were out there. Dad was in here. Somebody had to connect those facts, and apparently it was going to be a fourth grader.

**5. He hasn't danced since the songs stopped.**

Juniper chewed her pencil on this one. There was a memory, soft-edged, from when she was very small: being lifted onto feet much bigger than hers, in the kitchen, music from a little radio, everyone laughing. She couldn't remember whose feet. She thought maybe she wasn't supposed to remember, because whenever the memory came near Dad's face, Dad's face did the thing where it closed like a shop at closing time, lights off one by one, *we're closed, come back never.*

She wrote it down anyway. Lists were for truth.

**6. Because the will thing is probably about this.**

She'd written it before she'd fully decided to. She looked at it for a long moment. She hadn't heard the whole phone call, but she'd heard *heir* and *attend* and *with anyone*, and she'd heard Dad say *lapses* about the building, and the building was the shop, and the shop was — well. The shop was the shop. The shop was the whole world with a bell on the door.

Grown-ups thought kids couldn't understand paperwork. But paperwork was just stories with the feelings taken out, and Juniper was extremely good at putting feelings back in. It was basically her whole reading level.

Somebody wanted Dad to arrive somewhere *with a person*. And Dad, who could fix anything with tape and patience, had gone flat-voiced and receipt-straightening, which meant Dad thought there was no person to bring.

Which was ridiculous. Because —

**7. WREN.**

She boxed that one too. Then, after consideration, starred it three times.

* * *

The bell over the door rang at 4:40, which meant Wren, because Wren arrived at 4:40 the way trains arrived at trains, and Juniper slammed the notebook shut with the speed of a trained professional.

Wren came in shaking rain off their coat, green-stained hands, a paper bag from the bakery balanced on top of the rolling case. "Your daughter," Wren announced to the shop at large, "is corrupting me. She has informed me that my account of the Vienna book market lacks sufficient dragons."

"It lacked dragons," Juniper confirmed from the leather chair.

"It's a historical account."

"Historical accounts are better with dragons. That's why they invented them."

Wren laughed — the real laugh, the one that came from down low — and Dad appeared from the poetry section wearing the expression he wore around Wren, which Juniper had privately named *the weather in April*: bright and changeable and trying very hard to seem like ordinary weather.

"You're late," Dad said.

"I'm punctual. Your clock is ambitious." Wren set the bag on the counter. "Almond croissants. The last two. I fought a man."

"You did not."

"I gave him a look. In bindery circles, that counts as combat." Wren shrugged off the coat and headed for the back room, and then paused, and turned, and said, casually, in the voice of someone mentioning the weather: "Oh — I heard the tail end of your phone call earlier. Sounded official. Everything all right?"

Juniper went very still inside, the way you do when a deer steps into a clearing.

Dad's face did several things in fast succession, none of which stayed. "Fine. Estate business. Boring grown-up paperwork."

"Mm," said Wren, who had traveled through fourteen cities and could clearly smell a locked door from across a room. "Well. If it's ever boring in a way that involves heavy boxes or strong opinions, I'm cheap labor."

"It's fine," Dad said again, too quickly, and carried his one cup of tea into the back room anyway, and the afternoon folded itself back into its usual shape: pages turning, glue pots warming, the rain starting proper against the windows, croissant flakes shared three ways without anyone deciding to share them.

Juniper opened her notebook a careful inch and looked at item seven.

*WREN.*

Underneath, in smaller letters, she added what she actually knew, which was more than either of the grown-ups probably realized she knew:

**8. Wren fixed the Sam book without being asked.**

This one she almost didn't write. The Sam book lived on the high shelf behind the register — a slim first edition with a cracked spine and water damage along the bottom edge, the one thing in the shop Dad never let anyone touch, including himself, especially himself. Last week she'd seen it on Wren's workbench, open, being eased back together with little tongs and a brush and a patience Juniper had never seen anybody use on anything. She hadn't asked. Wren hadn't explained. Some repairs you did quietly or not at all.

But it meant Wren understood something important: that some broken things weren't broken because nobody cared. They were broken because caring hurt too much to look at directly. And Wren had looked anyway. Had picked it up with both hands.

That was the whole list, really. Items one through eight were just different ways of writing the same sentence, and Juniper was a good enough reader by now to know a thesis statement when she wrote one.

* * *

At closing time, Dad walked Wren out to the van under one umbrella, and Juniper watched through the window as they stood there a beat too long in the rain-smell, saying the goodbye that wasn't the real goodbye, saving the real sentence for tomorrow, the way Wren saved stories. Dad came back in damp and humming. Actually humming. Juniper noted this in the notebook under **9. Evidence**, and then closed it, and felt the plan settle into place in her chest, warm and enormous and slightly terrifying, like a plan should feel.

Because here was the thing she'd figured out, sitting in the leather chair with the books whispering: Dad was going to spend the whole autumn straightening receipts and saying *it's fine* until the whatever-it-was lapsed and the shop — the world with the bell on the door — slipped away. Unless somebody connected the facts. Cities to Marrow's Bend. Item seven to item one. The two cups to the one cup.

And Dad would never ask. Dad would rather alphabetize poetry into oblivion than ask for anything, Juniper had ten years of field data on this.

Which left her.

She was nine. She had a notebook, a plan-shaped feeling, insider knowledge of both parties, and access to a workbench stool. Compared to the obstacles in her reading list — cursed swords, winter lasting a hundred years, entire dark lords — this was honestly a small job.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would ask Wren to tell her the one about the night train to Vienna, the long version, the version with the conductor and the lost manuscript and the snow. And somewhere in the middle of it, casually, the way you slip a bookmark into a book you want someone to find, she would mention the reunion. She'd heard Aunt Verity's name on the phone twice this month, always in Dad's flattest voice. Reunions needed partners. Partners needed reasons. Juniper had a list of nine.

Outside, the rain kept on, and the shop glowed yellow at the edges of its windows like a lantern someone had forgotten to blow out, and upstairs Dad started the kettle for the second cup of tea he wouldn't drink, out of habit, out of hope, out of something he'd say was nothing.

Juniper clicked her pencil twice, drew a tenth line, and waited for morning.
