## 5. An Offer, Badly Made

There is a particular kind of cowardice that looks like hospitality. I know this because I practiced it for three days before I asked Wren Calloway to lie to my entire family.

It began with tea. It always begins with tea in this shop; I have a kettle older than most of my customers and a firm belief that no conversation worth having should be conducted without something warm in hand. But the tea on Thursday evening was not ordinary tea. It was *strategic* tea. I brewed it with the oolong—the good stuff, the tin I keep at the back of the cupboard for occasions I never seem to have—and I set out two cups instead of one, and I arranged a plate of shortbread like an offering to a god whose name I hadn't learned yet.

Wren was in the back room, as they had been every weekday since September, bent over the water-damaged stock from the cellar flood with their sleeves rolled and their hands moving in that way they had—quick, certain, almost rude in their confidence, like the books had personally offended them and were being forgiven one signature at a time. They'd been repairing my broken things for six weeks now. Six weeks of watching them work while pretending to inventory the front shelves, which were inventoried to within an inch of their lives.

"Tea," I said from the doorway, in the tone of a man announcing a fire drill.

Wren looked up. There was glue on their knuckle and a curl of Japanese tissue stuck to their wrist like a small pale feather. "Is it the good tea or the punishment tea?"

"The good tea."

They set down their bone folder with exaggerated care. "Then someone wants something."

I want it noted, for the record, that I did not flinch. I turned and walked back to the front counter with the dignity of a man who has absolutely nothing to hide, and I heard Wren's soft laugh follow me through the doorway like a cat that knows where you sleep.

* * *

Here is what I had rehearsed, three days running, in the shower, on the school run, once into the mirror above the kitchen sink until Juniper banged on the door and demanded to know who I was talking to:

*Wren. I find myself in a situation of some administrative difficulty, and I believe there may be a mutually beneficial arrangement available.*

That was the version I'd drafted at two in the morning, and even half-asleep I could hear how it sounded: like a tax notice wearing a cardigan.

The truth was uglier and simpler. My grandmother Odile Eastwick—portraitist of her own legend, matriarch of a family that treated affection like a line item to be audited—had died in March and left behind a will with teeth. Ms. Okafor had explained it to me in an office that smelled of lemon polish and old money: every Eastwick heir must present themselves at the reunion at Hollowmere Hall with a partner of no less than one year's standing, or their claim to their inheritance lapses entirely. No exceptions. No proxies. No appeals.

And my inheritance was not money. Money I could refuse with a clear conscience. My inheritance was the building. The actual bricks and mortar of Eastwick Books, held in trust since my grandfather's day, deeded to me on the condition of my attendance, accompanied, at Hollowmere, in October.

Without the building, there was no shop. Without the shop, there was no—well. There was no anything, really. There was the flat above it, which came with it. There was Juniper's bedroom with the glow-in-the-dark stars she'd insisted on arranging into a constellation she'd invented called The Reading Dog. There was the counter my grandfather had planed smooth with his own hands, the ladder that sang when you pushed it, the bell over the door that had announced ten years of my life.

I had considered, briefly and shamefully, simply not going. Letting it lapse. Telling myself the shop had been a good decade and everything ends. But then I'd pictured the FOR LEASE sign in the window, and the new tenants—there would be a phone shop, or worse, a *café concept*—and something in my chest had closed like a fist.

Which left one option, and one person, and one cup of very good tea going cold between us.

* * *

"So," Wren said, wrapping both hands around their cup. They sat across the counter from me the way they always did, sideways, one elbow planted, occupying space with an ease I found frankly suspicious. "You've been circling me like a moth for three days. Ask."

"I haven't been—"

"Gideon." They said my name the way they said it sometimes, flat and warm at once, like setting a book down gently but firmly. "I repair things for a living. I know what damage looks like before it's admitted. Ask."

I looked at the tea. The tea offered no support whatsoever.

"I have a family obligation," I said. "At the end of October. A reunion, at my grandmother's estate. Attendance is—" I searched for a word that wasn't *mandatory*, failed, and settled on, "expected."

"Mm."

"And there's a condition attached regarding guests."

"Mm-hm."

"It's a condition I currently fail to meet," I said, with the crisp clarity of a man reading his own autopsy report, "in that I am required to attend with a partner of at least a year's standing, and I have been single for ten years by deliberate policy, and the nearest thing I have to a long-term companion is a kettle."

Silence. Wren took a sip of tea. I watched them do it and understood, distantly, what it must feel like to be a manuscript under review.

"A kettle," they said.

"A very loyal kettle."

"And what exactly are you asking me, Gideon?"

And here—here is where the rehearsal collapsed, the way rehearsals do, all at once and in front of witnesses. Because I had meant to say it gracefully. I had meant to frame it as a favor between colleagues, a lark, a weekend of bad theater in exchange for something generous. Instead what came out of my mouth, in the voice of a man negotiating a lease, was:

"I'd like to hire you. To pose as my partner. For the weekend. And in exchange, I can offer you the bindery."

The words landed on the counter between us like dropped crockery.

Wren didn't move. Their face did something complicated—a flicker of genuine surprise, quickly shuttered, replaced by an expression I can only describe as *appraising*, the look they gave water-damaged first editions before deciding whether they were worth saving.

"The bindery," they repeated.

"The original one. In the east wing of the building. My grandfather built it—proper benches, the presses, a nipping machine from 1911 that still works if you speak to it nicely. It's been locked since he died. Nobody's used it." I made myself keep going, because stopping would be worse. "If you do this, it's yours. Permanently. Deed transfer, whatever the paperwork requires. You could set up properly. Stay through winter. Stay as long as you liked."

"You're offering me real estate," Wren said slowly, "to pretend to date you."

"When you put it like that—"

"How else would you like me to put it?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Took a sip of tea that had gone bitter with cooling and my own mounting horror.

"In fairness," I said, "I also planned to cover travel expenses."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because it matters, though I didn't understand why until much later.

Wren laughed. Not cruelly—that was the strange part. They laughed the way people laugh at a joke they've caught themselves mid-fall into, startled and a little breathless, and they pressed the heel of their hand to their eyes and said, "God. You absolute *disaster* of a man," and kept laughing, and I sat there holding my cup like a shield and felt something in my chest do a slow, traitorous roll over.

Because here's the thing I had not put in any of my drafts: I liked them. I had liked them since the second week of September, when they'd carried a box of my flooded stock up from the cellar two steps at a time and said, "Whoever shelved these upside-down should be tried at The Hague," and I'd had to admit it was me, and they'd looked at me with such delighted contempt that I'd laughed for the first time in—I checked later, against my own records—fourteen months.

Six weeks of them in my back room. Six weeks of their voice drifting through the door while I worked the register, singing under their breath in a language I couldn't place, arguing with dead binders about adhesives. Six weeks of Juniper abandoning her homework the moment they arrived, dragging her chair to the threshold of the back room to listen to stories about Lisbon, about Istanbul, about a night market in Taipei where a bookbinder had taught Wren to fold paper cranes that actually flew. Six weeks of catching myself listening. Of catching myself, worse, *watching*—the flex of their hands, the ink stain on their left thumb, the way they tucked their hair back with their wrist because their fingers were always full.

I had filed all of it under *later*, along with the solicitor's letter. I was, I will admit, developing quite a filing system for things I refused to think about.

"You're insulted," I said, when the laughter subsided.

"I'm—" Wren exhaled. "Yes. A bit. You've just offered me money-adjacent compensation to perform intimacy, Gideon. Do you know how that sounds?"

"Like a transaction."

"Like a *transaction*. Like I'm a service you're procuring. 'Travelling binder seeks weekend engagement, references required, payment in historic architecture.'" They turned the cup in their hands. "Do you ask everyone for things like you're apologizing for the asking?"

"Yes," I said, before I could stop myself.

Something shifted in their face. The sharpness gentled into something more careful. "Why the bindery?" they asked. "Really. You locked that room for years—you told me yourself you couldn't stand to open it. Why give away the one thing you couldn't let go of?"

And that was the question I'd been avoiding for three days, wasn't it. Because the answer was simple and terrible: because it was the only currency I had that was worth anything to either of us. Because I'd watched them work in my cramped back room with borrowed light and inadequate bench space and I'd thought, *they deserve the real room, the good presses, the whole inheritance of craft my grandfather left gathering dust*—and I'd thought it with a warmth that had nothing whatsoever to do with business.

"Because you're the first person in thirty years who'd use it properly," I said. "And because I'd rather it be yours than Marcus's. My cousin. He'd turn it into a home gym."

"That's not the whole answer."

"No," I agreed. "But it's the part I'm prepared to say out loud tonight."

Wren studied me for a long moment. Outside, the streetlights came on down Marrow's Bend high street, one by one, the town folding itself up for the night. Somewhere upstairs Juniper was supposed to be doing spelling and was almost certainly drawing dragons instead. The bell over the door hung silent. It was, I realized, the first time we'd been alone together in six weeks—not employee and employer, not customer and tradesman, just two people and a pot of tea and a lie sitting on the counter between them, waiting to be picked up or put down.

"One year's standing," Wren said at last. "Your family expects a year. We've known each other six weeks."

"They don't know that. They know nothing about me. That's rather the point."

"So we'd be lying."

"We'd be *performing*," I said, and heard how thin the distinction was even as I made it.

"Mm." Wren leaned back. The lamplight caught the edge of their jaw, the ink stain, the small scar on their chin I had absolutely no business having memorized. "Tell me about the family."

"Why?"

"Because if I'm going to consider selling myself into genteel fraud, I want to know the terrain. Who's dangerous?"

"Verity. My aunt. She's been running the family since Odile's health declined, and she regards me as a rounding error that learned to read. Then there's Marcus, who wants the building, and Priya, who wants the art collection, and a scattering of others who want whatever's left. They'll interrogate you. Politely. With wine."

"What will they ask?"

"Where we met. How it started. Why you'd stay in a town like this with a man like me."

"And what's the true answer to that last one?"

I hadn't prepared for that. I stared at them, and they stared back, mild and merciless, and the silence stretched until I heard myself say, honestly, stupidly: "I don't know. I've been trying not to wonder."

Wren went very still. Then they smiled—not the sharp appraising smile, but the other one, the rare one, the one that reached all the way up and made them look younger and slightly dangerous. "Well," they said. "That's the first honest thing you've said all evening."

"I said several honest things."

"You said several *transactional* things. There's a difference." They stood, collected their cup, and carried it around the counter to the sink, passing close enough that I caught wood shavings and bergamot and something like rain. "Here's what's going to happen," they said, rinsing the cups with their back to me, casual as weather. "I'm going to go home and think about it. Properly. Not because of your bindery—though God help me, Gideon, a 1911 nipping machine is a genuinely indecent offer—but because you stood there offering me a deed like a man paying off a debt, and I want to understand what debt you think you're in."

"There's no debt."

"There's always a debt with you. I can see it. You carry it around like a book too heavy to shelve." They dried their hands. "One condition of my own, if I say yes. Non-negotiable."

"Name it."

"No payment. None. The bindery stays yours—we'll talk about access another time. If I do this, I do it because I choose to, not because I'm bought. I've spent ten years travelling, Gideon. I know exactly what I'm worth by the hour, and I refuse to find out what I'm worth by the weekend."

The relief that went through me was so violent and so obviously not about the money that I had to look down at the counter and pretend to check the ledger.

"Thank you," I managed.

"Don't thank me yet." They shrugged into their coat, that long canvas one with the pockets full of awls and thread, and paused at the door with their hand on the frame. "For what it's worth—the answer's probably yes. I like the shop. I like the kid. I'm still deciding about you."

"Deciding what?"

But they were already through the door, the bell chiming their exit, and their laugh came back to me through the glass, low and warm, trailing down the high street like a lit fuse.

* * *

I stood in the empty shop for a long time after, turning off lamps one by one, telling myself the heat in my face was the tea.

Upstairs, Juniper looked up from her homework—dragons, as predicted—and said, "Did you ask them?"

I froze halfway through hanging up my coat. "Asked who what?"

"Wren." She said it with the infinite patience of the young for the slow. "You made the *good tea*. You only make the good tea when you want something." She went back to her dragon. "So did you?"

"Juniper—"

"'Cause if you didn't," she said, selecting a green pencil with great deliberation, "you should. They tell better stories than anyone in this whole town. Even Mrs. Adeyemi. And Mrs. Adeyemi met the Queen."

I stood in the doorway of my daughter's room, nine years old and already a better negotiator than I would ever be, and felt the evening tilt beneath me in a way I couldn't yet name.

"Not yet," I said carefully. "But they're thinking about it."

Juniper grinned at her dragon like it had told her a secret, and somewhere down the hill, in a rented room above the chandlery, Wren Calloway was deciding my future—and, though I didn't know it yet, drafting terms I hadn't imagined, ones that had nothing to do with deeds or machines, and everything to do with teaching me my own town as if I'd never once lived in it.
