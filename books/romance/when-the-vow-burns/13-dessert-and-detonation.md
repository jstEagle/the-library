## 13. Dessert and Detonation

The welcome dinner at Hollowmere Hall was served in a room built to make people feel small. I don't mean that as a figure of speech. The dining room's ceiling had been painted in the 1890s with a fresco of the Eastwick family tree, every branch and twig of us rendered as cherubic faces drifting among gilded clouds, so that no matter where you sat at the table, some ancestor looked down on you with the serene disapproval of the dead. Grandmother Odile used to say it was the only honest portrait in the house, because at least up there nobody was pretending not to keep score.

I had been seated—deliberately, I suspected—at the far end, near the salt cellar and the candleabra and Marcus Eastwick, who had been my cousin for thirty-four years and my rival for approximately twenty-nine of them. Wren sat beside me, luminous in a jacket the color of storm water, radiating the particular calm of a person who has survived stranger rooms than this one. Juniper had been granted the children's table, which she had immediately reorganized into what she called "the good table," and from which she kept shooting me thumbs-up signals like a tiny handler coaching an anxious spy.

The soup was cold cucumber. The conversation was colder.

"So." Marcus set down his spoon with the care of a man laying a card face-up. "Wren Calloway. Bookbinder. Traveling bookbinder, I'm told—which means what, exactly? You drift from town to town fixing spines until the money runs out?"

"Until the books run out," Wren said pleasantly. "The money is incidental."

"And now you've drifted all the way to Marrow's Bend." He smiled at me across the candlelight. "Gideon. Ten years in that shop and you never once brought anyone to a family occasion. Not a birthday, not a christening, not poor Odile's funeral. And now, three weeks before a will reading with real money attached, here's a charming stranger with beautiful hands." He spread his own hands, which were neither. "Forgive me if I don't applaud the timing."

The table went quiet in the way tables do when everyone has been waiting for exactly this and wants to enjoy it. Down at the good table, Juniper had gone very still, her fork suspended, her eyes enormous.

I felt the old machinery engage—the decade of discipline clicking into place, gears oiled by grief and rehearsal. Deflect. Shrink. Make yourself boring enough that they lose interest and move on to someone whose humiliation is more entertaining. I opened my mouth to produce something mild and forgettable—

And Wren laughed.

Not a nervous laugh. A real one, warm and unbothered, the laugh of someone who has just heard a joke they intend to improve upon. It moved through the silence like light through water.

"Marcus, right? The property developer?" Wren said. "You're worried about timing. That's fair. Let me tell you about mine."

"Wren," I said under my breath, a warning with no force behind it, because some traitorous part of me wanted to hear whatever came next more than I wanted air.

"I found your cousin's shop two autumns ago," Wren said, addressing the table now, or possibly the chandelier, or possibly me. "I'd been on the road eleven months. My van's heater had died somewhere outside of Ohio, my left boot had a hole in it, and I was down to repairing paperback romances for gas money. I walked into Eastwick Books because the window display said *Every book deserves a second life* in handwriting that clearly belonged to someone who meant it."

"That's marketing copy," I managed. "I wrote it at two in the morning."

"It's a confession," Wren said. "Anyway. I brought in a damaged volume—a first edition, water-stained, spine cracked clean through—and I watched Gideon hold it like it was a sleeping animal. Didn't check the price guide. Didn't ask what it was worth. Asked what had happened to it. Who had owned it. Whether the person who'd loved it was still around to see it mended." Wren turned to look at me then, and the candlelight did something unfair to their eyes. "I have worked in forty-one shops in six years. Yours was the first one where the owner asked about the reader before the resale value."

Aunt Verity, at the head of the table, made a small sound that might have been interest or indigestion.

"The heater story," prompted a cousin I didn't recognize—Dana, maybe, or Diane—one of the twins, watching Wren with open delight.

"Right, the heater." Wren leaned back. "So Gideon learns I live out of a van with no heat in October, and what does your cousin do? Offers me the back room of the shop for the season. Not rent-free—Gideon insisted on rent, actually, drew up a lease, because apparently generosity without paperwork makes them break out in hives—"

"It's called professionalism," I said weakly.

"—but the rent was a number so low it was basically a fiction we agreed to maintain. And then, every morning, there's tea already made when I come downstairs. Two sugars, no lecture about how sugar is going to kill me, even though I know for a fact Gideon has opinions about sugar, because I've seen the face. And every evening, when I finish a repair, Gideon comes back to look at it. Not to inspect it. To *look*. Like the book matters. Like the mending matters." Wren picked up their wine glass, unhurried. "I want to be clear: I have met a lot of people in the book trade. Most of them love books the way collectors love coins. Your cousin loves them the way nurses love patients. It's slightly alarming, honestly. I kept waiting for the catch."

"And?" This from Verity, silk over steel. "Was there one?"

"There was a child," Wren said, "who decided I was her friend before I'd finished unpacking my tools, and who has since appointed herself my apprentice, my critic, and my conscience. There was a shop cat named Tennyson who sleeps in my glue pot when I'm not looking. There was a Tuesday in November when the power went out and Gideon read *The Wolves of Willoughby Chase* aloud to six kids by lantern light rather than send them home in the rain." Wren shrugged, easy as anything. "No catch. Just the slow, humiliating discovery that I'd stopped wanting to leave."

Silence. The good kind, for once—the kind where people are recalibrating.

Marcus recovered first, because Marcus always recovers first; it's his single talent. "Charming," he said. "But none of that explains why we're only meeting you now."

"No," Wren agreed. "It doesn't. But here's what does." They set down the glass and looked directly at him. "Your cousin spent ten years being the only Eastwick who showed up. Every school book fair. Every hospital fundraiser. Every time the library needed a roof or the food bank needed shelves or old Mrs. Halloran needed someone to read her mail aloud because her eyes went. Gideon did it quietly, without a press release, without telling any of you, because asking for credit would have required believing anyone was paying attention." A pause, surgical. "I know this because I checked. I asked around town, the way you'd expect a suspicious drifter to. Do you know what they said? Every single person said the same thing: *Oh, Gideon will help. Gideon always helps.* Not one of them could remember the last time your cousin asked for anything back."

"That's not—" Marcus began.

"It's the whole point," Wren said, still pleasant, still terrible. "You're wondering about timing. So am I, sometimes. Then I remember that the timing of a thing says nothing about the truth of it. Some people take ten years to walk through a door. Doesn't mean the door wasn't theirs."

Down the table, one of the twins raised her glass in a small, unmistakable salute. Even Verity's expression had shifted into something more complicated than hostility—something that looked, from certain angles, like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like the answer to.

And me. I sat there with my heart doing something it had not done in ten years, which was beating loudly enough to embarrass me.

Because here was the thing, the detonation, the reason I would later blame the wine: every word Wren had said was invented. I knew it was invented. We had rehearsed histories over tea for exactly one evening, and none of this had been in the script. The van, the boot, the lantern reading, the lease—I had never told them half of it. And yet it was all true. Every detail, true. Wren had simply *watched*, the way they watched damaged books—with patience, with attention, with that unnerving binder's habit of seeing the shape of what a thing used to be and could be again—and assembled me from observation alone, and set me down in front of my family whole.

Nobody had ever described me accurately before. Sam had come closest, but Sam had loved me, and love forgives its inaccuracies. Wren had known me for eight weeks and had just performed my biography flawlessly in front of the people paid to find the flaws.

I looked at my dessert plate. Poached pear, almond cream, something candied. I had no memory of eating the course before it.

"Gideon," Verity said, and her voice had changed registers entirely—from interrogation to something almost gentle, which was infinitely more dangerous. "You've gone quiet."

"I'm having an epiphany," I said. "It's indigestion-adjacent. I'll be fine."

Beside me, Wren made a sound they converted, barely, into a cough.

The dinner broke apart into smaller conversations after that, the family digesting both the pears and the spectacle. Marcus retreated to sulk near the port. The twins migrated closer to ask Wren increasingly personal questions, all of which Wren answered with the serenity of a saint or a con artist, and I genuinely could no longer tell the difference. At the good table, Juniper caught my eye and gave me a double thumbs-up, mouthing something that was either *you're welcome* or *we need to talk later*, and knowing my daughter, it was both.

It was during coffee, in the drawing room, that it happened properly.

The room was crowded, the fire too large for the grate, the portraits of dead Eastwicks presiding from every wall. I had stationed myself near the window with the strategic goal of invisibility, and Wren appeared at my elbow holding two cups, handing me one without asking how I took it, because they already knew, because of course they did, because they had been cataloguing me for weeks the way I catalogued first editions, and I hadn't noticed, or worse, I had noticed and filed it under *later* along with everything else I couldn't afford to feel.

"You okay?" Wren asked quietly. "That got away from me a little. The story. I improvised."

"You lied," I said.

"I invented," Wren corrected. "There's a difference. Lies are false. Inventions are just truths that haven't happened yet." They sipped their coffee. "Tell me which parts weren't accurate. I'll revise."

I opened my mouth to list them. The tea with two sugars—accurate. The lease—accurate, down to the clause about glue fumes. Tennyson and the glue pot—accurate, and a source of ongoing household tension. The lantern reading—accurate; I could still feel the scratchy warmth of that November night, six kids cross-legged on the rug, the rain hammering the windows while Alanna and her wolves crossed the frozen sea. Mrs. Halloran and her mail. The food bank shelves. All of it. All of it true, delivered better than I could ever have delivered it myself, because Wren had understood something I had spent ten years refusing to understand: that my life, viewed from the outside, was not small. It was only invisible because I had built it that way, brick by careful brick, and called the wall a virtue.

"None of it," I said finally. "That's the problem."

Wren looked at me. The firelight moved across their face, and for a moment the performance dropped—not the charm, not the composure, but the professional distance underneath it, the traveling craftsman's practiced impermanence—and what was left was just a person standing close to me in a hot room, holding a cup of coffee, looking at me like I was a book they'd already decided to mend regardless of cost.

"Yeah," Wren said softly. "I know."

Across the room, Verity was watching us over the rim of her sherry glass with the expression of a chess player who has just realized the board is not the board she thought it was. Juniper had fallen asleep against one of the twins, who was pretending not to be charmed and failing. Somewhere above us, the painted ancestors drifted in their gilded clouds, keeping score.

I stood there in my great-grandmother's drawing room, holding a cup of coffee, and admitted it to myself in the smallest possible internal voice, the voice I used for things that couldn't be unsaid:

By dessert, it had stopped being pretend. Maybe long before dessert. Maybe from the first morning there was tea waiting, warm, two sugars, no lecture.

The trouble—the detonation, the crater where my careful decade used to be—was that Wren had just told a room full of hostile witnesses the entire truth about me, and I had never felt more seen in my life, and I wanted, with a want that frightened me considerably more than losing the shop, to spend the rest of the weekend earning the right to deserve a single word of it.

From the hallway, I heard Ms. Okafor's voice, crisp and formal, calling the heirs together: "If I could have everyone's attention. Before the evening concludes, there is a matter regarding the estate that must be addressed tonight."

Wren and I looked at each other.

"Trouble," they murmured.

"Family," I said. "Same thing, at Hollowmere."
