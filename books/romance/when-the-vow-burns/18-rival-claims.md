## 18. Rival Claims

Marcus Eastwick wore his money the way other men wore cologne—liberally, and with the assumption that everyone within a ten-foot radius would appreciate it. He'd arrived at Hollowmere on the second morning in a car that cost more than my annual inventory, and he'd spent breakfast telling anyone who would listen about a vineyard he was thinking of buying in Portugal, which I suspected meant he had already bought it and wanted to be congratulated twice.

So when he found me on the terrace after lunch, alone with a cup of coffee going cold and a view of the rose garden that Verity maintained like a grudge, I knew before he opened his mouth that this was not a social call.

"Gideon." He settled into the chair across from me without being invited, which was very Marcus. "Mind if I sit?"

"You're already sitting."

"I like to be thorough." He smiled. He had Odile's smile, actually—the same sharp, amused curve of the mouth—but where hers had always seemed to be laughing *at* the world with affection, Marcus's laugh was strictly at your expense. "How's the weekend treating you? Enjoying the festivities?"

"The festivities are fine."

"And your guest? The bookbinder." He said the word the way you might say *the taxidermist* or *the snake charmer*—fascinated, faintly appalled. "Wren Calloway. Interesting choice."

"They're not a choice, Marcus. They're a person."

"Of course they are." He waved this away with one manicured hand. "I'm sure they're lovely. Very... hands-on profession, bookbinding. All that leather and thread." He let the innuendo hang there like a bad smell, then leaned forward, elbows on knees, and dropped the performance entirely. "Can we talk plainly for five minutes? Cousin to cousin?"

I had known Marcus for thirty-eight years, give or take the years he'd spent pretending our branch of the family didn't exist. Plain talk from him was either a trap or a mercy, and historically it had been a trap wearing mercy's coat.

"Five minutes," I said.

"I'm going to make you an offer." He glanced toward the house, checking sightlines, then back at me. "Not through Okafor. Not through Verity. Just between us, man to—" he paused, recalibrated, "—cousin to cousin. I want the building."

I set down my coffee. It had gone past cold into something geological anyway. "The building isn't mine to sell."

"It's yours if the will goes your way, and it isn't if it doesn't, and either way you don't want it. That's what nobody wants to say out loud at these things, so I'll say it: Gideon, you don't *want* it." He spread his hands, reasonable as a banker. "You've been running that shop for ten years on fumes and stubbornness. The roof leaks—I know it leaks, I drove past last spring and saw the tarp. The heating system is older than Juniper. You're one wet winter away from a structural survey that'll bankrupt you. And now, thanks to Grandmother's little game, you have to stand up in front of this family every year until the estate settles and perform a marriage for their entertainment. Does that sound like a life to you?"

It sounded, annoyingly, like a Tuesday.

"What's the offer?" I asked, because the fastest way through Marcus was to let him think he'd won the framing.

He named a figure.

I won't write it here. Not because it's secret—Marcus would tell anyone who stood still long enough—but because writing it down makes it real, and for about four seconds it was real, and those four seconds were the most honest I'd been with myself all weekend.

It was enough money to pay off the shop's debts twice over. Enough to fix the roof somewhere else, somewhere smaller, somewhere without a family name carved over the door. Enough to send Juniper to any school she wanted, to stop doing the arithmetic every month where new stock competed with new shoes. Enough to be, for the first time since Sam died, *not afraid*—and God, I hadn't realized how much of my life was fear wearing a cardigan until someone offered to buy it off me.

"That's generous," I said carefully.

"It's fair," Marcus corrected. "I'm not doing charity. That building sits on the best commercial corner in Marrow's Bend, and the town's dying but the highway reroute is coming in three years, mark me. I'll put in something tasteful. A café-wine-bar-bookshop hybrid, maybe—keep the books, even, as a theme. People love a theme." He smiled again, that Odile smile gone wrong. "You could stay on, if you wanted. Curate. Work the register. No ownership, no liability, no will, no Verity reading your mail. You'd just be the bookseller again. Isn't that what you actually are?"

And there it was. The hook under the bait.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: after enough years, invisibility starts to feel like safety. Ten years of being the quiet one, the contained one, the one who kept everything running and asked for nothing—that's not just discipline anymore. It becomes a shape you fit. And Marcus was offering to pour me permanently into that shape, with a nice sum attached and everyone's blessing, and some tired cellar-deep part of me whispered: *wouldn't that be easier?*

No more claim. No more clause. No more standing in front of the Eastwicks with Wren Calloway's hand in mine, waiting for someone to ask how we met and having to decide, sentence by sentence, how much truth the fiction could hold. No more of whatever was happening in my chest when Wren laughed at my terrible jokes, because a man who sells the shop sells the reason Wren stays, and a man without a reason gets to go back to being no one in particular, safely, forever.

The coffee cup trembled slightly against its saucer. I put both hands around it like a man warming himself at a very small fire.

"Marcus," I said. "Why do you want it?"

He blinked. I don't think he'd prepared for the question; he'd prepared for yes, and for haggling, and possibly for tears, but not for curiosity.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to me."

He sat back, and for a moment—just a moment—the vineyard-and-cologne version of him flickered out and I saw underneath: a man in his forties who had spent his whole life collecting things because the Eastwicks had taught him, early and thoroughly, that love was a limited resource and you'd better secure your share in assets.

"Verity thinks she's going to run this family someday," he said. "She's probably right. And Verity has decided that the shop is the heart of the estate—Grandmother's sentimental project, the legacy, all of it. Which means whoever holds the shop holds the story." He shrugged. "I don't care about the story. I care that Verity cares. If I own the building, her legacy is a lease agreement. It's not personal, Gideon. It's chess."

"Not personal," I repeated.

"Well." He had the grace to look mildly embarrassed. "It's a little personal."

* * *

I didn't answer him. I said I'd think about it, which is what you say to a cousin on a terrace when your hands are shaking and the answer is forming somewhere below your conscious mind, and he took it gracefully, shook my hand—his grip warm, dry, final, a handshake that already considered itself a signature—and went inside to charm someone else or check his portfolio or whatever Marcus did in the intervals between acquisitions.

I stayed on the terrace. The rose garden went on being beautiful in that severe, disciplined way, every bush pruned into obedience. Odile's garden. Odile's rules, still running the place two years after her death, the way certain voices keep talking in your head long after the person is gone.

*I'll keep everything safe,* I had promised Sam. *I'll keep myself contained.*

A number like the one Marcus had named doesn't just buy a building. It buys an exit from the vow's hardest test. Containment, perfected. A life so small and tidy that nothing in it could ever break my heart again, because nothing in it would ever matter enough to try.

I tried to picture it. That was the exercise, wasn't it—picture the clean exit, weigh it honestly. So I did.

I pictured the shop without us in it.

Not closed—Marcus would keep the books, he'd said, as a theme—but emptied. The shelves rearranged by someone who thought literature was décor. The creaky third step of the ladder replaced with something efficient. The children's corner—Juniper's kingdom, the rug with the rocket ships, the beanbag shaped like a whale—cleared out for a wine display. The back room where Wren worked, the good north light, the smell of paste and leather and old paper: converted to a stockroom, or worse, a tasting bar, strangers drinking prosecco where Wren's hands had mended a hundred broken spines.

I pictured the register. My register. With someone else's elbow-worn groove in the counter where mine had been, and someone else answering Mrs. Hollis when she came in every Thursday wanting "something like that book she liked but newer," and someone else keeping the account behind the counter for the schoolteacher who paid in installments and never once missed one, and someone else knowing—which was the whole job, really, the entire secret architecture of the place—knowing that Mr. Adeyemi's son needed the dragon books in order and that the widow Prewitt bought romances but hid them inside gardening manuals and deserved to have her secret kept.

Ten years. I hadn't built a business. I'd built a nervous system for a town, and Marcus wanted to buy the building and keep the organs as a theme.

And underneath that—underneath the shop, underneath the fear, underneath even the vow—there was a smaller picture, and it was the one that finished me:

Juniper, age six, standing on a step stool behind the register, stamping receipts with tremendous seriousness because I'd told her she was staff now and she had believed me. Juniper asleep in the reading nook with a book open on her chest, rising and falling. Juniper last night at dinner, watching Wren tell a story about a bindery in Lisbon with her chin on her fists, drinking in cities the way other kids drank juice, and saying afterward, walking up the stairs, *"Do you think people can just pick a town and stay in it? Like, on purpose?"*

If I sold, I'd have to explain it to her. I'd say something about opportunity and fresh starts, and she would nod, because she was nine and kind, and she would understand exactly what had happened, because nine-year-olds always understand exactly what has happened: that her parent had been offered a way out and had taken it, that the shop—the only church she'd ever known—had been traded for a number, and that the person who told her stories about staying had left before the story ended.

The wind moved through the rose garden. Somewhere inside the house, someone laughed at something Marcus said.

"No," I said aloud, to the garden, to Odile, to myself. Just to hear it in the air where it couldn't be retracted.

* * *

I found Wren an hour later, and I want it noted that I wasn't looking for them. I was looking for Juniper, who had vanished after lunch with a paperback and the specific silence of a child planning something, and the search simply... terminated in the east library, where Wren stood at the window with a mending needle tucked behind one ear like a carpenter's pencil, watching the storm clouds assemble over the hills.

"Looking for Juniper?" they asked, without turning. They always knew. It was unnerving. It was—other words I wasn't ready to use yet.

"She's hiding somewhere with a book and an agenda."

"Mm. She asked me earlier what 'buying someone out' means." Wren turned then, and their eyes were mild and absolutely lethal. "I told her it means someone with too much money trying to solve a problem money can't solve. She seemed satisfied."

"Wren."

"I wasn't eavesdropping. The terrace carries. Old houses are terrible gossips." They crossed the room, unhurried, and stopped close enough that I could smell the bindery on them—wheat paste and beeswax, the smell that had started living in my clothes too, lately, so that I carried the back room around with me like a confession. "So. What did he offer?"

"How do you know he offered anything?"

"Because you have your negotiating face on. The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic against your will." They tilted their head. "Well? Was it insulting or terrifying?"

"Both," I admitted. "That's rather the problem. It was a good offer."

Something flickered across their face—quick, quickly mastered—and I realized, with the delayed horror of a slow learner, that I had just told the person playing my devoted partner that another man's money had briefly looked good to me. That I had stood on a terrace and weighed the exit against them and taken a full hour to report my findings.

"A good offer," Wren repeated, evenly.

"For the building. For—for walking away from all of it. The will, the reunion, the performance." I heard my own voice doing the thing it did when I was being careful, each word sanded smooth. "I didn't take it."

"I didn't think you would."

"Why not?"

They were quiet for a moment. Outside, the first fat drops of the coming storm hit the window, one, two, then a scatter of them, like applause starting in the cheap seats.

"Because you flinch every time someone says the word 'theme,'" Wren said. "Because when Marcus's type talks about 'curating,' your jaw does a thing. Because you keep a ledger behind the counter for a schoolteacher who pays in installments, and I know about the ledger because you think I don't notice anything, and Gideon—" they reached up and took the mending needle from behind their ear, gesturing with it like a tiny conductor's baton, "—I notice everything. It's the trade. Broken things come to me and they can't hide what's wrong with them, and neither, apparently, can you."

"I haven't decided," I said, because honesty apparently had a hold on me today and wouldn't let go. "About the offer. I said I'd think about it."

"I know. You should think about it." They said it easily, and that easiness frightened me more than anger would have. Anger would have meant I mattered enough to lose. Ease meant they'd already survived worse than me. "Think about it properly. But while you're thinking, consider one thing." They stepped closer, and the storm pressed its face against the glass behind them, and their voice dropped into the register they used for rare books and other fragile truths. "Whatever you decide, decide it as the keeper of the shop. Not as the man trying to survive his family. Those are two different decisions, and only one of them is yours."

Then they walked out, calm as weather, leaving me alone in Odile's library with the rain coming down and the arithmetic finally, mercifully, finished.

I knew what I had to tell Marcus. But it was the other sentence—the one about the keeper, the one Wren had handed me like a key to a door I'd been leaning against for days—that kept me standing there long after the light went out of the sky.

Down the hall, faintly, I heard Juniper's laugh, and beneath it, low and urgent, her whisper: *"—no, listen, I found another one—"*
