## 23. Juniper Asks the Question

There are three things I know for absolute certain in this world. One: Mrs. Pellow in 4B grades math tests while eating yogurt and you can hear the spoon. Two: the best hiding spot in Eastwick Books is behind the biography section because nobody has looked at a biography since before I was born. Three: grown-ups think kids don't notice anything, which is the single dumbest thing grown-ups believe, and they believe a lot of dumb things, like that we can't tell when the milk is going off or when they've been crying.

I noticed everything.

I noticed that Wren had been at our house — well, above our shop, technically, but it was our house the way the shop was our house, which is to say completely — for almost two months now, and that their coat had stopped smelling like train stations and started smelling like our kettle. I noticed that Parent laughed at Wren's jokes with their whole face now instead of just their mouth. I noticed that Wren had fixed the wobbly leg on my desk chair without being asked, and had put a little brass star sticker underneath where nobody would ever see it except me, except I saw it, because like I said, I notice everything.

And I noticed that Parent had been sleeping badly. You can tell from the tea. When Parent sleeps well, they make proper tea in the morning, the good loose stuff from the blue tin, and there's time for eggs. When Parent doesn't sleep, it's teabags, fast, and toast with nothing on it, and they stand at the counter drinking like the tea is a chore they're trying to finish before it notices them. We'd had eleven days of teabag mornings. I was keeping count in the back of my homework notebook, under a flap of paper where I also kept my wish list and the score of every argument I'd ever won against Danny Okafor (forty-one to six, and one of his wins didn't count because he cheated with facts).

The wish list was getting old. I'd written it back when it was all still pretend, back before the reunion, back when Wren was just the person who came and fixed books and told me about cities with names like Lisbon that sounded made up but weren't. Back then the list was funny. *Get Parent a person. Person should be nice and know stories. Person should not be Aunt Verity.* Now the list felt less like a joke and more like a plan that had mostly worked, except for the part where nobody had told me what happens after the plan works.

That was the part I needed to ask about.

* * *

It happened on a Tuesday, which is the slowest day at the shop, when the only customers are Mr. Aldous, who comes in to read the newspapers without buying them, and occasionally a lost tourist looking for the lighthouse, which is nine miles away and closed for the season anyway.

Parent was upstairs doing invoices, which means sitting at the kitchen table with their glasses on and their mouth doing the tight line it does when numbers are being difficult. Wren was in the bindery, the back room that used to be Grandpa's workshop and was now full of smells I loved — glue and leather and that sharp clean smell of fresh-cut board. Wren let me watch them work if I promised not to breathe on anything wet. There was a book open on their bench with its spine peeled back like a banana, and Wren was sewing it with thread so thin it looked like hair.

"Wren," I said, climbing onto the stool that was officially mine, the short one with the cushion shaped like an owl, "can I ask you a question?"

"You just did," Wren said, not looking up. They always said that. It was their joke and I allowed it because their hands were busy and busy hands mean honest answers, in my experience.

"That wasn't the question. That was asking permission for the question."

"Legally distinct." Wren tied off a stitch with their teeth, which I was not allowed to do with thread even though I'd asked four times. "Go on, then."

"Is Parent your real one or your pretend one?"

Wren's hands stopped.

I want to say, for the record, that I hadn't planned to ask it right then. I'd been carrying the question around for days like a stone in my pocket, turning it over, figuring out how to phrase it so nobody could wriggle out of answering. But watching Wren sew a broken book back together, I realized something important: Wren fixed broken things for a living. And if anyone knew the difference between a book that was really repaired and a book that just looked repaired — the kind where someone slaps tape on the spine and calls it done and it falls apart again by spring — it was them.

So it was actually the perfect time to ask. I stand by that.

Wren set down the needle very carefully, the way they did when they were buying themselves time. "What makes you ask that?"

"Because at the reunion everyone thought you were Parent's real one," I said. "But before the reunion, I heard Ms. Okafor say the will wanted a partner of a year's standing, and Parent went all gray, and then Parent asked you to come, and you said yes, and I heard Juniper, this is practice, we talked about it." I paused. "You called it practice."

"I did say that."

"Practice is what you call it when it isn't real yet." I'd learned that from piano. Ms. Ferro made me practice scales, and scales were not music, they were just the shape of music waiting to happen. "So which is it now? Because you stayed after the reunion. Practice usually ends."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Their eyes are brown, regular brown, nothing magic, but they have a way of going very still and steady that makes you feel like whatever you say next is going to be taken seriously, which is rare. Most adults do a thing where they look at you like you're a puppy doing a trick.

"Juniper," Wren said, "do you know what pretend means?"

"It means acting. Like a play. You know the words but they aren't yours."

"That's exactly it." Wren turned the damaged book over in their hands, considering its broken spine like it might answer for them. "Your parent asked me to help with something difficult. To come to a big scary family weekend and play a part. And I said yes because—" they stopped, and I watched them pick a different word, the way you pick your way across a creek, "—because I wanted to help. And then somewhere in the middle of the pretending, some of it stopped being pretend. But I haven't figured out how much. Or whether your parent has figured it out either. Does that answer you?"

It answered about half of me. The other half was still holding its breath.

"One more question," I said.

"You're rationing me."

"Pretend ones leave. Right? That's how pretending works. The play ends and everybody takes off the costumes and goes home." My voice did something weird on *home*, went small, and I hated it, so I pushed through. "Real ones stay. So if it's half and half — which half is winning?"

Wren didn't answer right away. They reached over and straightened the owl cushion on my stool, which wasn't crooked, which meant they needed something to do with their hands more than the cushion needed fixing.

"I don't know," they said finally. "But I'll tell you something true, since you asked true questions. I've been leaving places my whole life. Towns, cities, workshops, whole countries. I got very good at it. Leaving is easy once you practice." They smiled, but it was one of their complicated smiles, the kind that had weather in it. "And then I came here to fix some water-damaged stock, and I met a person who runs a bookshop like it's a lighthouse, and a kid who asks questions like a customs officer, and I keep finding reasons the trains can wait. So maybe the leaving half is losing. Maybe. Ask me again when I'm braver."

I nodded slowly, the way I'd seen Ms. Okafor nod at documents, like I was filing it away for later use. Then I went upstairs to find Parent, because Wren had answered honestly, which meant now it was Parent's turn, and fair was fair.

* * *

I caught Parent at four o'clock, during the dead hour, when the shop was empty and the light through the front windows went gold and made all the dust float around like the air itself was thinking. Parent was shelving returns, which they did when they were upset, because shelving was alphabetizing and alphabetizing was control, and Parent believed very strongly in control. I'd figured that out years ago. It explained the labeled jars. It explained the ten-minute warnings. It explained a lot.

"Parent."

"Mm?" They didn't turn around. They were putting a returned cookbook back in Cooking, upside down first, then correcting it, which was a thing they did.

"Is Wren your real one or your pretend one?"

The cookbook slipped. Parent caught it against the shelf with a thump, and for a second they just stood there with their hand flat on the cover like they were taking its pulse.

Then they turned around, and I watched them do the thing. Every grown-up has a version of it — the quick flicker where they decide whether to give you the real answer or the kid answer. Dad used to do it. Aunt Verity does it so fast you can't even see it happen. But Parent's version had gotten slower over the years, and lately, since Wren, it had gotten slower still, and sometimes — like now — I could see them decide. Really decide. To not lie to me.

"What brought this on?" Parent asked, which was not an answer, which I noted.

"I asked Wren. They gave me half an answer. You owe me the other half, because it's about you." I crossed my arms. "Also I'm nine and a half, which is basically ten, and Ms. Ferro says I read at a seventh-grade level, so don't try any vocabulary tricks."

A sound came out of Parent that was half laugh and half something sadder. They came and sat down on the bottom step of the ladder, which put them almost at my height, which they only did for serious conversations. Serious conversations had rules: no distractions, eye contact, and I was allowed to interrupt if I didn't understand, but not if I just didn't like the answer.

"You're right," Parent said quietly. "It started as pretend. Wren agreed to come to the reunion as my partner because the will required it, and I was afraid of losing the shop. That was the whole arrangement. A performance. With terms." They rubbed the bridge of their nose under their glasses. "And it was supposed to end when the weekend ended."

"But it didn't."

"No. It didn't." Parent looked past me, at the gold dust in the light, at the shelves, at all of it. "And here's the truth, Juniper, the whole truth, because you asked for it and you deserve it: I don't know what it is now. I know what I want it to be. Wanting and knowing are different animals, and one of them is much scarier than the other."

"Why is wanting scarier?"

Parent was quiet for long enough that I thought they might do the vocabulary-trick thing after all. Then they patted the step beside them, and I sat down, and we were both on the ladder like a couple of crows on a fence.

"Because of your dad," Parent said.

My stomach did the drop thing. Not the bad drop. The serious drop, like the first hill of a rollercoaster, where you know you're safe but your body hasn't been told yet.

"When Sam died, I made him a promise. At the very end, when he couldn't talk much anymore, I promised I'd keep everything safe. Him, and you, and the shop, and myself. Keep it all safe and contained, like packing something fragile for shipping. And I kept that promise for ten years, Juniper. Ten years of being careful. And careful worked — mostly. Nobody got hurt. Nothing broke." Parent took off their glasses and cleaned them on their shirt, which they only did when they were stalling. "Except careful has a cost. The cost is that nothing gets *fixed* either. You can't repair anything if you never take it out of the box."

"Like the books Wren fixes."

"Exactly like that." Parent put the glasses back on and looked at me, and their eyes were shiny but not spilling, holding it the way they held everything, expertly, tiredly. "Wren is — Wren is the most real thing that's happened to me in ten years. And that's precisely why it terrifies me. Because I learned that loving people means losing people, and I built my whole life so I'd never have to learn it twice. And now there's this person sewing broken books in my back room, and my daughter asking me questions I can't dodge, and I'm standing at the edge of the box wondering if I remember how to climb out."

I thought about that for a minute. Nine and a half is young but it's not stupid, and I'd sat through enough of Ms. Ferro's lessons about theme to know when a metaphor was happening.

"So Wren is a real one," I said, "who's pretending to be a pretend one, because you're pretending you don't want a real one."

Parent made a noise like a hiccup and a laugh having an argument. "When you say it like that—"

"Somebody has to say it like that. You two keep saying it in fancy grown-up ways where nobody understands anything." I stood up and brushed off my skirt, because I'd delivered my point and standing made points land better. "Here's what I think. I think pretend ones leave and real ones stay, and Wren keeps finding reasons the trains can wait — they told me that, it's a direct quote — and you keep making teabag tea because you're scared, and between the two of you, somebody has to go first. And it can't be me, because I'm the kid, and also I already did my part, I made the wish list, item one was *get Parent a person*, and look around, Parent. Look around the shop."

Parent looked around the shop. At the shelves, at the gold light, at the door to the bindery where a lamp was burning and someone was humming something low and off-key. Parent's face did several things in a row, too fast to name them all, and the last one was the one that mattered — soft and wrecked and awake, like a person remembering they own a heart and it's been beating the whole time.

"The stakes were never the building, were they," Parent said. It wasn't a question. It was the sound of someone finally reading the last page of a book they'd been avoiding.

"Nope," I said, popping the p, because some moments require it. "The building was just the excuse. The will was just the excuse. Everybody in this family hides behind paperwork." I headed for the children's section, because I'd said my piece and also there was a new arrival I hadn't inspected yet. At the corner I stopped and turned back, because there was one more thing, and noticing everything means saying everything.

"Parent? For what it's worth — if you figure out how to climb out of the box, I vote Wren. Item two on the list was *person should know stories*, and Wren knows Lisbon is real. That's basically the whole test."

I left Parent sitting on the ladder in the gold light, doing the face, and I went to shelve the new arrivals wrong on purpose, because someone in this family had to introduce a little chaos, and clearly it was going to be me.

Behind me, very quietly, I heard Parent get up off the ladder and walk toward the bindery door. They stopped just outside it. I could see them through the biography section — the one hiding spot nobody checks — standing in the doorway with their hand raised to knock, not knocking, listening to the humming, deciding.

I opened a picture book and pretended to read it and watched everything, because that's what I do.

The knock, when it finally came, was the softest sound in the world, and Wren's humming stopped, and neither of them said anything for a long, long moment — and then Parent said, in a voice I'd never heard them use, not once in my whole life:

"Wren. Do you have a minute? There's something I need to say. All of it. Out loud."

I turned the page.
