## 27. The Town Weighs In

I learned about Verity's ultimatum the way I learn most things in Marrow's Bend: sideways, through a customer who couldn't keep a secret any better than she could keep a bookmark.

Mrs. Hollis came in on Thursday morning for her weekly romance novel—she liked them with dukes, the more improbable the better—and while I wrapped it in brown paper with the care of a surgeon closing a wound, she leaned across the counter and said, in the voice people use for weather reports and funerals, "You heard what that Eastwick woman's threatening, then?"

"Which Eastwick woman?" I asked, though there was only one who mattered, and only one threat worth reporting.

"Verity. Down at the diner last night, telling anyone who'd stand still long enough that your arrangement with our Gideon is a fraud, and she'll see the claim struck out before the leaves finish falling." Mrs. Hollis accepted her parcel and did not move toward the door. "Fraud. As if Gideon Eastwick could commit fraud against their own family when they can't even bring themselves to shortchange a customer by a penny."

"They'd chase you down the street to return it," I said.

"They would." She finally turned to go, then paused, hand on the doorframe, and looked back at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "You know this town talks, Wren Calloway. But it also remembers. Ten years of remembering is a heavy thing to argue with."

She left. I stood behind the counter holding nothing, feeling like a man who has just been told the bridge he's standing on was built for someone else's weight entirely.

* * *

Here is what I knew by Friday, because Marrow's Bend has no secrets, only delayed announcements:

Verity had come to the shop with Ms. Okafor two days after we returned from Hollowmere. She had laid out her evidence—the timeline, the photographs someone had taken at the reunion, the neat little arithmetic of a relationship that began six weeks before a will demanded one—and she had offered Gideon a choice: withdraw the claim quietly, or be publicly exposed as a fraud and lose everything anyway, plus the town's respect into the bargain.

Gideon had not withdrawn. That much I knew from the inside; I'd been shelving biographies ten feet away when it happened, dusting spines with hands that wouldn't stop shaking, listening to my own name get dissected like a specimen. What I hadn't known—what I still didn't know until the town told me—was how completely Gideon had refused to confirm or deny anything. Not a lie to save the shop. Not a confession either. Just that steady, maddening, heartbreaking silence, the same silence I'd stood outside the bindery door and listened to three nights ago, the half-spoken sentence hanging in the air between us like a wire neither of us would touch.

*I promise to keep everything safe—including my heart from—*

From what? From me? From ending? From burning down the careful house they'd built out of grief?

I'd started packing on Wednesday. Suitcase open on the bed of the room above the Hollises' where I lodged during the season, my good brushes rolled in leather, my presses crated. It seemed kinder, somehow, to leave before being asked. To let Gideon lose the building without losing me too—to subtract myself from the equation so the arithmetic got simpler. Except Juniper had caught me at it, nine years old and ferocious as a small hawk, and informed me in no uncertain terms that leaving was "the dumbest plan you've ever had, and you once tried to fix a book with tape." And then she'd told me about the sleepless nights, the sentence spoken aloud to an empty shop, and something in my chest had rearranged itself from departure to siege.

So I stayed. And I fought badly, and gently, and mostly by making tea and waiting, which is a bookbinder's whole strategy anyway: you cannot rush glue. You can only prepare the surfaces and hold them together and trust the chemistry.

But Verity wasn't waiting. Verity had gone to the diner, and the diner had gone to the church committee, and the church committee had gone everywhere, because that is how information travels in a town of four hundred souls: faster than light, slower than gossip, which is to say instantly.

* * *

Saturday morning, I opened the shop before Gideon arrived. This had become our unspoken division of labor—I was always early, Gideon was always precise, and Juniper was always late in the manner of children who have discovered the strategic value of lateness. I put the kettle on, unlocked the door, flipped the sign, and stepped out onto the sidewalk to sweep the step, because Gideon swept it every morning for ten years and if I was going to be here I was going to do it properly.

That's when I saw them coming.

Mrs. Okafor first—not the solicitor version but the Saturday version, in a red coat, carrying nothing legal-looking, which somehow made it more ominous. Behind her, Mr. Adeyemi from the hardware store, pushing his bicycle. Then the Delgados, both of them, and their twin boys, whom I recognized from the reading nook. Then a cluster of teachers from the primary school, still in their weekend clothes, moving with the determined air of people attending something. Then old Tom Bracken, who bought Westerns exclusively and had never once spoken above a murmur. Then more. A dozen. Two dozen. People I'd sold books to all autumn, faces I knew from the counter, names I'd learned from the way Gideon greeted each one without ever quite touching them.

They filed into the shop the way water finds its level—no announcement, no banner, no speeches. They took up positions. Mrs. Okafor settled into the armchair by the poetry section as though she'd been assigned it. The Delgado twins went straight to the picture books with the discipline of trained operatives. Tom Bracken stationed himself by the Westerns and picked up a Louis L'Amour he had almost certainly already read.

"What's happening?" I asked Mrs. Okafor, keeping my voice low.

"We're shopping," she said serenely. "It's Saturday. One shops on Saturday." She pulled a volume of Mary Oliver off the shelf beside her and opened it to a random page. "Also, we are present. There is a difference, and today the difference matters."

I looked out the front window. More were coming. A woman I recognized from the bakery, carrying a box. The pastor, off-duty, in jeans. Teenagers who had never voluntarily entered a bookshop in their lives, drifting in with the studied boredom of their species and immediately gravitating toward the graphic novels.

By the time Gideon arrived at nine-fifteen sharp, with Juniper trailing and complaining about the injustice of Saturday-morning errands, the shop held forty people and smelled of coffee and rain-damp wool, and nobody had bought anything yet, and everybody was staying.

Gideon stopped dead just inside the door.

I watched it happen from behind the register—the exact moment the understanding landed. I've repaired enough broken bindings to know what it looks like when something long-compressed finally relaxes: the spine eases, the boards settle, the whole structure exhales after years of holding itself together through sheer tension. Gideon stood there with the morning light behind them and their keys in their hand, and their face did something I had never seen it do. Not the armored kindness. Not the careful shopkeeper's smile. Something younger and more undone than either.

"You're—" Gideon started, and stopped, and started again. "What is all this?"

"Browsing," said Mrs. Okafor, not looking up from Mary Oliver.

"It's Saturday," said Mr. Adeyemi.

"We needed books," said one of the teachers, who was holding a book she had clearly brought from home.

Juniper, who understood everything instantly because children are born knowing what adults spend decades forgetting, grabbed her parent's sleeve and whispered—loudly, the way children whisper—"They *know*, Papa. Everybody knows. Mrs. Hollis told my teacher and my teacher told everyone and nobody thinks it's bad except mean Cousin Marcus and he's not here."

The word *Papa* did something to the room. I felt it move through the crowd like a current through wire. Because that was the other thing the town knew, apparently—that Gideon Eastwick had spent ten years showing up for every school fundraiser, every flooded basement, every widow who needed a ladder and every kid who needed a place to sit after school, and had done it all so quietly that even Gideon had forgotten it counted.

* * *

The day passed in a strange, holy quiet.

Nobody made speeches. Nobody confronted anything. They simply stayed, and shopped, and talked, and slowly the shop filled with the ordinary music of a community doing what it does: Mrs. Okafor debating theology with the pastor over the religion shelf, both of them enjoying it immensely; the Delgado twins staging a slow-motion war among the picture books; Tom Bracken finally buying the Louis L'Amour he'd read twice already, because today buying it meant something. The bakery woman's box turned out to contain cinnamon rolls, which appeared on the counter beside the register without explanation or attribution, like manna with icing.

And through all of it, Gideon worked the register, and I watched Gideon work the register, and what I saw was a person being slowly cracked open by kindness.

Because here's the thing about Gideon Eastwick that I'd understood since my first week in that back room: they believed, with the total conviction of the truly wounded, that they were invisible. That the vow they'd made at Sam's deathbed had shrunk them down small enough to slip through life untouched, and that this was the price of safety and a fair price too. I'd watched them deflect thanks, wave off gratitude, redirect every compliment toward the book or the shop or the weather. Ten years of practice at being no one in particular.

But you cannot spend a decade being quietly indispensable and expect the ledger to stay blank. Kindness compounds. It accrues interest. And today the bank had called in the account, and the payment was presence—forty-odd people standing in a bookshop on a rainy Saturday saying, without saying it, *we see you, we have always seen you, and we are not leaving.*

Around noon, a girl from Juniper's class—Mabel, gap-toothed, relentless—tugged Gideon's apron and asked, at full volume, "Is Wren your true love? My mom says you're not allowed to say it's pretend anymore because that's lying and also sad."

The shop went very quiet. Forty people found fascinating things to study on the shelves.

Gideon crouched down to Mabel's level, which is the thing they do, the thing that undoes me, the gravity of them bending toward small people and broken books alike. "That's a big question," Gideon said carefully. "Wren is—" and here their eyes came up and found mine across the crowded shop, and whatever armor had survived the morning failed completely, "—Wren is the person I'm trying to be brave enough for."

It wasn't a confession. It wasn't the sentence. But it was said aloud, in front of witnesses, and I watched Mrs. Okafor's hand drift toward her bag where I happened to know a certain letter lived, and I watched Gideon notice the gesture and go pale and steady at the same time.

Mabel considered this. "Okay," she said. "That's still kind of pretend-ish," and ran off to join the twins, mission accomplished, childhood intact.

* * *

Closing time came the way it does on days like that—all at once and reluctantly. The crowd thinned by degrees, the way tide goes out, each person pausing at the register to buy something, anything—a paperback, a card, a candle from the impulse shelf—as if paying admission. By six the shop held only the three of us and the wreckage of a besieged bookstore: displaced volumes, crumbs, a single small shoe belonging to Mabel, retrieved later by a sheepish mother.

Gideon locked the door, leaned their forehead against the glass for a moment, and then turned around and looked at the shop—at the shelves, the reading nook, the cinnamon-roll tin, me—and I saw the thought complete itself behind their eyes.

"I thought I was invisible," Gideon said. It wasn't to me, exactly, and it wasn't to Juniper, exactly. It was to the room. To ten years. "All this time. I thought if I was quiet enough and careful enough, I'd keep everything safe, and it never occurred to me that—" Their voice snagged. "They came. On a Saturday. In the rain. For me."

"For us," Juniper corrected, mouth full of last cinnamon roll. "I live here too."

"For us," Gideon amended, and crossed the floor to me, and stopped just close enough that I could count the exhaustion and the wonder fighting for space on their face. "You knew," they said softly. "This morning. You saw them coming and you didn't warn me."

"I wanted you to see it fresh," I admitted. "Some things you should meet unarmored."

"That's a terrible thing to do to a person."

"Yes," I agreed. "It's also the only thing I know how to do to you. You keep insisting on armor, and I keep finding the seams."

For a moment—one suspended, humming moment—I thought they might say it. The sentence. The rest of it, the part that ended in something other than a dash. Their hand rose halfway toward mine and hovered there, trembling slightly, at the border of the country they'd sworn off a decade ago.

Then the phone rang. The landline, ancient and brass-belled, the one that only ever meant family or emergency, and usually both.

Gideon closed their eyes. Exhaled. Answered it.

"Eastwick Books." A pause. I watched their shoulders change shape. "Ms. Okafor. Yes. Yes, I—what do you mean, *another* letter?" Another pause, longer. The color left their face in stages, like a tide going out over sand. "Sewn into the binding. Of course it was. Of course she would." Their free hand found the edge of the counter and gripped it. "No—don't tell Verity. Not yet. I'm coming now. Tonight."

They hung up. Turned to me. And in their voice was the sound of a door opening onto a room that had been locked for fifty years.

"Wren," Gideon said, "there's a final letter. Odile's last one. And Okafor says it changes everything—it says who the building was *always* meant for." A breath, ragged at the edges. "We have to read it tonight. Both of us. Before Verity finds out it exists."
