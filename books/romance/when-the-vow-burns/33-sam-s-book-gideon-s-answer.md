## 33. Sam's Book, Gideon's Answer

The shop was empty at seven in the morning, which is the only hour it ever truly belongs to me. By eight it belongs to Juniper's cereal bowls and the delivery van and Mrs. Adeyemi asking if we've got anything where nobody dies. By nine it belongs to everyone. But at seven it's mine—cold tea, warm radiator, and the particular silence of a room full of books that haven't been judged yet.

I'd put Sam's book on the counter last night and then, like a coward with a filing system, I'd turned it face-down.

You should understand what this book is. First edition, *The Lark Ascending Country*, a minor novel by a minor poet that Sam loved with an intensity they reserved for exactly three things in this life: me, Juniper before she existed, and books with marginalia by strangers. Sam used to say a book with someone else's notes in it was a conversation you got to eavesdrop on. Sam bought this copy at a church fete for fifty pence because the previous owner had argued with the protagonist in pencil, and Sam thought that was the best entertainment value in the county.

Then, three years into our marriage, our basement flooded. Not dramatically—no cinematic rescue, no box of photographs held above the waterline. Just a slow, ignoble seep while we were at Sam's sister's for a weekend, and when we came home, half the stored stock from the shop's overflow was ruined, including this. Sam had cried over it. Actually cried, sitting on the basement steps with a swollen, warped book in their lap, and I remember thinking—not unkindly, but with the practical arrogance of a person who has not yet been tested—that it was only a book.

It was only a book the way a heart is only a muscle.

I'd kept it all these years anyway. In a box, in the flat, in the dark, because throwing it out was unthinkable and looking at it was worse. And then Wren arrived in September with their traveling bindery and their terrible, wonderful habit of noticing things, and one afternoon they found the box while hunting for workspace and held the book up to the light like a doctor holding an X-ray.

"This can be saved," Wren said. "Not restored. Saved. There's a difference. Restoration makes it look like nothing happened. Saving lets it keep its history."

I said something deflecting and bookish. I always say something deflecting and bookish. It's practically my catchphrase; I should have it printed on a tote bag.

Wren took the book anyway. Took it without being asked twice, which I later understood was itself a kind of question. And they worked on it across weeks—in the evenings after closing, sleeves rolled, that focused silence they do—and last night, when I came down to lock up, it was finished and waiting on the counter with a note in Wren's slanted hand: *For you. Whenever you're ready.*

*Whenever you're ready.* Which is the cruelest possible thing to write on a note, because it means the person knows perfectly well you are never ready, and they're leaving the door open anyway.

So here I was at seven in the morning, tea going cold, book face-down on the counter, conducting my usual negotiation with myself. The terms were familiar. I have made this deal with grief a thousand times: *not today*. Today I'll open the shop, today I'll do the school run, today I'll alphabetize the poetry section for no reason. Today is survivable precisely because today contains nothing that matters.

But here's what ten years of discipline teaches you, and it teaches it slowly, in the margins, the way water gets into a basement: *not today* is a loan, not a gift. The interest compounds. And eventually the debt comes due on a Tuesday morning in November with your tea going cold and your daughter asleep upstairs and the whole careful architecture of your restraint balanced on one face-down book.

I turned it over.

The cover had come back to life under Wren's hands—the cloth rebacked, the corners rebuilt, the water-staining left visible along the bottom edge like a tide line. Wren had been right about the difference between restoring and saving. The book didn't pretend the flood never happened. It wore the flood. It had survived the flood and was still, unmistakably, itself.

I opened it.

And there, on the flyleaf, in Sam's handwriting—the handwriting I would know anywhere, the handwriting I had spent ten years not looking for in other people's grocery lists and library sign-out sheets—there was an inscription.

I hadn't known it was there. Or rather—I must have known, once, and buried it so deep that finding it now felt like archaeology, like uncovering a room in your own house you'd bricked over and forgotten. Sam had given me this book. Of course Sam had given me this book. Sam gave me books the way other people give flowers, except flowers die and books just wait.

The ink had feathered slightly where the water touched it, but it was legible. It was legible, and it was dated—our third anniversary, two years before the diagnosis, four years before the hospital room—and it read:

*For Gideon, who keeps everything safe. Keep everything safe—except your heart. Let it burn.*

I stood there for a long time. Long enough for the tea to go from cold to abandoned. Long enough that when the radiator clicked on I startled like a burglar had entered.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I need you to understand, the thing I have spent thirty-two chapters and ten years and one entire marriage failing to understand:

Sam wrote that before. Before the deathbed. Before the vow. Before I knelt beside a hospital bed and promised a dying person that I would keep everything safe and keep myself contained, and watched them close their eyes as if I'd said something comforting.

Sam had already told me. Years earlier, in ink, in a book, in the language we both spoke fluently: *keep everything safe—except your heart. Let it burn.*

And I had taken the first half and built a religion out of it and discarded the second half like a misprint.

* * *

Let me tell you about the deathbed, since apparently we're doing this now, since apparently the morning has decided to be thorough.

The hospice room was small and too bright and smelled of lilies and antiseptic, which is a combination no florist or doctor should be permitted to arrange. Sam had maybe a week. Maybe less; the doctors spoke in ranges the way weather forecasters do, hedging against being blamed for hope. Juniper was three. She'd come that morning and fallen asleep across Sam's legs, and Sam had held very still under her for an hour, which cost them, and I knew it cost them, and neither of us said so.

When she'd gone—carried out by Sam's mother, small and boneless and dreaming—Sam had looked at me with that look. You know the look. The one where someone is already halfway gone and using what's left to make sure you'll be all right, which is the most generous and most infuriating thing a dying person can do.

"Promise me," Sam said.

"Anything."

"Keep everything safe." A breath. "The shop. Her. You."

"I promise," I said, and meant it, and have kept it, every single day, with the discipline of a monk and the warmth of a fire extinguisher.

And then Sam tried to say something else. I remember this. I have spent ten years not remembering this, and now I remember it with the awful clarity of a man reading his own handwriting from a flooded book. Sam started another sentence—"and Gideon, don't"—or "Gideon, you have to let"—something. Something beginning with an instruction and ending, I was certain, in permission I didn't want. And I did what any reasonable person does when a dying person tries to revise the terms of a promise mid-sentence.

I stopped them.

I said, "No. Just that. Just keep everything safe. That's enough. That's everything." I pressed their hand. I smiled the smile you smile at funerals, forward-looking and load-bearing. And Sam looked at me for a long moment—there was something in it I filed under *weakness* or *drugs* or *goodbye*, some category that required no action from me—and then closed their eyes, and that was nearly that.

I have replayed that moment perhaps four hundred times in ten years, and in every version I am right. In every version, stopping them was mercy. In every version, the unfinished sentence was going to be *don't wait too long*, or *don't blame yourself*, or some other sentence whose only function was to make my guilt heavier or lighter, and either way I didn't want it, because I wanted the promise clean. One instruction. Containable. A vow you could keep with your hands.

It never once occurred to me—not in four hundred replays—that the unfinished sentence might have been the important one.

*Except your heart. Let it burn.*

Sam had already said it. Two years earlier, in a book I shelved and loved and flooded and boxed and never reread. They'd said it when there was time to say it properly, when there was no hurry, when I could have laughed and kissed them and argued about whether hearts actually burn or merely smolder. They'd said it in the good years, planted it like a bulb, trusting the ground.

And then, when the ground froze, when I was standing in a hospice room refusing to hear the last line, the message was already in the earth. Waiting. Ten years, as it turned out. Waiting for a traveling bookbinder with patient hands and an offensive talent for saving things I'd classified as lost.

* * *

I sat down on the step stool behind the register, which is where I sit when the shop delivers news, and I held the book in both hands like something that might bolt.

Here's what the old story was, the story I'd been living: love is a debt that comes due catastrophically. Love is signing a contract whose termination clause is written in a hospital. I had loved Sam wholly and lost them wholly, and the arithmetic was simple and brutal: the depth of the love determined the depth of the crater. Therefore, contain the love, contain the crater. Keep everything safe. Keep myself safe. Keep myself, if necessary, from myself.

Ten years of that arithmetic. Ten years of declining dinner invitations, of answering *how are you* with inventory figures, of raising a magnificent child with the emotional availability of a well-organized spreadsheet. Ten years of telling myself it was discipline, when the truer word—the word I couldn't afford—was *fear*. I wasn't keeping everything safe. I was keeping everything *small*, on the theory that small things can't break.

And the whole time, the correction had been sitting in a box in my own flat, in Sam's own hand, dated before I ever needed it:

*Keep everything safe—except your heart. Let it burn.*

Do you see? Do you see what Sam was saying, what they'd been saying all along? Not *guard yourself*. Not *be careful*. The opposite. Sam—who knew me better than anyone who has ever lived, who knew my filing systems and my deflections and my habit of mistaking control for care—Sam was saying: *the keeping is for the shop and the child and the ordinary precious things. But not you. Not your heart. Your heart is the one thing you're supposed to risk.*

The vow was never a prohibition. It was permission wearing a disguise, and I'd read it upside down for a decade.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in my shop at seven-forty in the morning, the unhinged bark of a man who has just discovered the answer key was in his pocket during the entire exam. Somewhere upstairs Juniper shifted in her sleep, and I clapped a hand over my mouth like a schoolboy, and then I sat there with wet eyes and a crooked grin, talking to a dead person in a bookshop, which is either grief or prayer or the world's most literary breakdown.

"You absolute menace," I said to Sam, to the flyleaf, to the feathered ink. "You put it in a *book*. You knew I'd find it. You knew I'd take ten years, but you knew I'd find it."

Because that was Sam all over. Sam never argued with me directly—they left things where I'd trip over them. A novel on my pillow instead of a difficult conversation. A pencil note in a margin instead of a confession. And now, posthumously, a sentence sewn into a saved book, delivered by the exact person I was busy pretending not to fall in love with. If that isn't orchestration from beyond the grave, I don't know what is. Sam would have adored Wren. That's the thought that arrived next, uninvited and devastating: Sam would have adored Wren—the stories, the soldering-iron confidence, the way they talk to Juniper like she's a colleague. Sam would have taken one look at the two of us circling each other in that back room and said something unbearable like *oh, thank God, finally,* and then made popcorn.

The grief didn't leave. I want to be honest about that, because the alternative is the lie everyone sells you, the tidy version where revelation dissolves mourning like sugar in tea. It doesn't. Sam is still dead. Juniper still doesn't remember their voice. There is still a chair in my life shaped exactly like a person, and it will always be empty, and I will still sometimes set a cup of tea down near it out of pure animal habit.

But the grief changed shape. That's the only way I can say it. For ten years it had been a wall—load-bearing, structural, the thing I leaned my refusal against. *I can't love again; look what loving cost.* And now, holding a water-stained book in an empty shop, the wall became a doorway. Same bricks. Different function. Because the vow I'd been keeping wasn't Sam's. It was mine—a forgery, a document I'd drafted in a bright terrible room and signed in someone else's name. Sam's actual will, actual testament, actual last-word-on-the-matter, had been sitting in my house for twelve years:

*Let it burn.*

* * *

The bell over the door rang at nine, which meant the world was resuming whether I'd finished my epiphany or not. Mrs. Adeyemi, right on schedule, wanting a mystery where nobody dies.

"We've got a new arrival," I heard myself say, "but fair warning, the detective's marriage is on the rocks."

"Oh, lovely," she said. "Realistic."

She paid and left, and I stood at the register with Sam's book open beside the till, and I thought about Odile. Of course I thought about Odile. The old woman had spent her last years sewing letters into bindings, hiding her heart in the family's books the way Sam had hidden theirs in mine, and I had spent months reading her words as a puzzle, a legal trap, a dead matriarch's meddling. It had never occurred to me that she was doing the same thing Sam did. Leaving instructions where the right person would eventually trip over them. Loving forward, into the future, through paper.

Two people I loved had told me the same thing in the same medium, decades apart, and I had needed a flood, a bindery, a forged relationship, a storm, a suitcase, and a child with excellent instincts to finally read the inscription.

Fine. I'm slow. I'm a slow reader of my own life. But I'm thorough, and I finish books.

I closed Sam's book gently and did not put it in a box. I put it on the shelf behind the register—the one spot in the shop that's mine, where I keep the things that aren't for sale—and I propped it facing outward, inscription visible, like a small stubborn lighthouse.

Then I took out my phone, and my thumb hovered over Wren's name for approximately forty-five seconds, which for me is roughly the decision-making speed of a fighter pilot.

What I typed: *The book. I read it. Thank you isn't—*

Delete. Too much. Not enough. Both.

What I typed second: *Can you come by the shop?*

Send. Before the vow could file an objection. Before the old arithmetic could point out that Wren had said, five days ago, standing in my kitchen with their suitcase half-packed and their voice steady in the way that costs the most: *I'm leaving at week's end. Not from anger. Because I won't stay where I'm only half-chosen.*

Week's end. Which meant I had days. Which meant the platform scene I'd been dreading was no longer hypothetical—it was scheduled, ticketed, approaching at the speed of a normal train on a normal line.

I looked at the book behind the register. *Let it burn,* said Sam, in feathered ink, patient as paper.

All right, I told them. All right. But I'm doing it properly. I'm doing it the way you'd have wanted—out loud, in public, witnessed, embarrassing, complete. No more unfinished sentences. No more stopping people halfway. The whole thing, start to end, even the parts that terrify me.

Especially those.

Outside, the November light came down Marrow's Bend like it had somewhere to be, and upstairs a drawer opened—Juniper, awake, rummaging for something—and I realized I was smiling at a telephone like an idiot, and that for the first time in ten years, the fear and the hope were the same size.

The week's end was coming. So was the train. So was the sentence I'd rehearsed a thousand times and never once finished.

I picked up the phone again, found Wren's name, and typed the truest thing I've typed in a decade:

*Don't leave yet. I have something to say, and this time I'm going to say all of it.*
