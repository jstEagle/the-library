## 37. Renovations and Revels

The hammer was my idea.

Okay, technically it was Wren's idea, because Wren is the one who said, "Juniper, do you want to help build your reading nook?" and I said yes before they finished the sentence, which is how I ended up holding a hammer at seven in the morning on a Saturday while sawdust drifted through the shop like weather. But the *nook* was my idea. It was item number three on my list, and I want that on the record, in writing, possibly framed above the register next to Great-Grandma Odile's portrait.

Let me back up.

After everything — the reunion, the storm, Verity's face when the gardener showed up with the letter, Dad's whole speech at the train platform that made a lady drop her suitcase — the shop got *famous*. Not famous-famous. Nobody put us on television or anything. But people from two towns over started driving to Marrow's Bend just to see "the bookshop where the will thing happened," and Ms. Okafor said we should probably get a sign that says *Yes, this is that bookshop* so she didn't have to keep explaining it at dinner parties.

Dad said no to the sign. Dad says no to most signs. But he did say yes to the renovations, and by *yes* I mean he stood in the middle of the shop one Tuesday night with the blueprints spread out on the counter and his hands doing the thing where they hover like the papers might bite him, and Wren came up behind him and put their chin on his shoulder and said, "It's going to be wonderful," and Dad's hands went still and he said, "Fine. Wonderful. Fine."

That's basically how all decisions happen now. I've studied it. It's very efficient.

* * *

Item one on my list was *get a person for Dad*, and that one's been checked off so thoroughly it's almost embarrassing. Wren has a toothbrush in our bathroom now. A *toothbrush*. And a mug — the chipped blue one with the heron on it — that lives on the second hook of the mug rack, which used to only have Dad's mug and mine and the one shaped like an owl that nobody uses because it's a decoration, obviously. The heron mug gets used every single morning. Sometimes I stand in the kitchen doorway and watch Wren drink tea out of it while Dad reads the weather page aloud even though neither of them cares about the weather, and I think: *item one, complete, and I didn't even have to do anything.* Well. Except the fake dinner. And the letters. And standing up at the reunion in front of forty Eastwicks with my heart going like a woodpecker. Minor contributions.

Item two was *learn bookbinding*, and that's been happening too, sort of. Wren set up a little practice station for me at the end of their bench — smaller tools, duller blade, a stack of ruined paperbacks from the donation bin that I'm allowed to destroy and resurrect as I please. My first attempt at reattaching a spine looked like the book had been in a fight with a stapler and lost. My fourth attempt actually held. Wren pinned it up on the corkboard over the bench with a label that said *APPRENTICE WORK — DO NOT TOUCH*, and I have never been prouder of anything, including my diorama of the water cycle in third grade, which was genuinely excellent.

But item three. Item three was the big one.

*A reading nook,* I wrote, back when I was ten and scheming at the homework table between the poetry section and the atlases. *A real one. With cushions. Where you can hear the rain.*

I wrote it because the shop had exactly zero places to sit that weren't a ladder or a stepstool, and if you wanted to read a book before buying it you had to stand in the aisle like some kind of Victorian orphan. Also, honestly, I wrote it because I wanted a place in the shop that was *mine*. Dad had the register. Wren had the bindery. I had a folding chair and a view of the radiator.

Now I have a window seat.

Well — I will by tonight. Right now I have a window seat-shaped hole in the wall where a radiator used to be, plus lumber, plus sawdust, plus Marcus.

Yes, *that* Marcus. Cousin Marcus, who tried to buy us out, who offered Dad escape money like we were a failing restaurant. Turns out when you lose a family argument as publicly as Marcus did, you have two options: never show your face again, or show up with a truck full of lumber and your sleeves rolled up and pretend the buyout offer never happened. Marcus picked option two, which Wren says is called "making amends" and which I call "being bored at his own house," but either way, he's here, and he's actually good with wood. He built the frame for the nook in about four hours while telling a story about a canoe he built in college that sank, and Dad laughed — a real laugh, the kind with sound — and I decided maybe Marcus could stay.

"You're drilling that crooked," Marcus told me, watching me attack a screw.

"I'm drilling it *enthusiastically*," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is not."

"Dad," I called across the shop, "Marcus is being mean to me."

"He's building your nook," Dad said, without looking up from the shelf he was sanding. "He's allowed one mean comment per board."

"That's generous," Wren murmured, carrying a stack of books past us toward the new shelves. They were wearing the canvas apron with the burn mark on the pocket, the one they'd had since before Marrow's Bend, and there was glue on their knuckles and sawdust in their hair, and they looked — I don't know how to say it right. At the reunion they looked like a guest. Now they look like furniture. Good furniture. Load-bearing furniture. The kind you build a room around.

* * *

Here's what the shop looks like now, in case you're keeping track, which I am:

The back wall — the one that used to be all gray and sad with the water stain shaped like Ohio — is gone. In its place: windows. Big ones, floor-ish ones, that Wren insisted on because "a bindery needs light" and Dad insisted on because "the shop needs light" and really because they both wanted to watch the street, which is what they do now instead of television. Through those windows you can see the new door on the side of the building, the one with the little glass pane and the brass handle that says **CALLOWAY BINDERY** in letters Wren painted themselves, freehand, no stencil, which everyone agrees is showing off.

Through that door is Wren's workshop, except it's not really a workshop anymore, it's more like a classroom, because three times a week the town comes in and learns things. Mrs. Adeyemi from the library comes Tuesdays to learn gold tooling. The twins from the bakery come Thursdays and are terrible at sewing and wonderful at gossip. Old Mr. Pell, who fixed watches for forty years until his hands got shaky, comes whenever he wants and sits in the corner repairing the spines of hymnals from three different churches, and Wren lets him, and doesn't charge him, and pretends not to notice when he leaves money in the teacup anyway.

And the shelves — okay, the shelves. We spent all last week building them. Real ones, oak, floor to ceiling along the east wall, replacing the metal supermarket ones Dad inherited from the previous owner twenty years ago. Every Eastwick who wasn't a coward showed up to help, which turned out to be more of them than I expected. Aunt Prue, who I'd written off as a Verity-adjacent person, turned out to be extremely good at leveling things and bad at following anyone's instructions, including her own. Second-cousin Dov brought his kids, who mostly moved books from one box to another box and back again, which is honestly the correct way for children to help. Even Verity sent something: a check, and a note that said *For the shelving. Oak, I assume. She would have wanted oak.* No signature, but the handwriting was unmistakable, all sharp angles and no apologies. Dad put the check in the drawer and the note in a book about forgiveness that he then couldn't find for a week, which Wren says is poetic and Dad says is annoying.

So Saturday morning, the shop smelled like cut wood and paste and coffee, and everybody was there, and the light through the new windows came down in long yellow bars, and I stood in the middle of it with my crooked screw and my hammer and thought: *this is what the list was for.* Not the paper list, even. The real one. The one underneath.

* * *

At noon, Wren called a break, which meant everyone sat on paint cans and crates eating the sandwiches the bakery twins sent over, and Mr. Pell told the story about the time Odile Eastwick came into his shop to fix a pocket watch and argued with him for an hour about whether the mainspring was original. "She was right," he said, shaking his head. "Course she was right. That was the trouble with Odile. Always right, and always alone with it."

I looked at Dad when he said that. Dad was looking at the floor, doing his quiet face, the one where his mouth does a small thing that isn't quite a smile. Wren was looking at Dad too. They do that a lot — the looking. It used to be sneaky-looking, back in the pretending days. Now it's just looking, out loud, like it's allowed.

"Juniper." Wren snapped their fingers at me. "You're drifting. Board. Screw. Enthusiasm."

I went back to my board. But I kept thinking about Odile alone with her rightness, and about the letter she sewed into a book binding because she couldn't say it out loud, and about how Dad stood on a train platform and said his whole sentence in front of God and everybody because a nine-year-old read a dead woman's mail to a room full of strangers. Things get passed down in families. Sometimes it's cheekbones. Sometimes it's silence. Sometimes it's the opposite of silence, and you have to break a whole generation of it with a hammer, which — look at that — I happened to be holding.

* * *

The nook was finished by four o'clock.

That's not true. The *frame* was finished by four o'clock. The cushions came later — Wren ordered them special, deep green, with a seam of gold thread down the middle that they swore was "just a nice detail" and everyone else correctly identified as showing off again. But by four o'clock the structure existed: a window seat built into the south wall, under the last of the old windows, the ones with the wavy glass that makes the street look like a painting. There's storage under the lid — Marcus's idea — and a shelf above it at exactly my height, which took three tries to get right because I kept standing under it saying "lower" and Marcus kept saying "you're going to grow" and I kept saying "not if I can help it, growing is a scam."

Dad did the last screw himself. He wouldn't let anyone else near it. He got down on his knees on the floorboards with the screwdriver and turned it slow, all the way, until the head sat flush with the wood, and then he stayed down there a second longer than he needed to, his hand flat on the seat like he was checking it for a pulse.

"Well?" Marcus said. "Somebody test it."

Everybody looked at me. Obviously. It was my nook. I climbed up, arranged myself against the corner, pulled my knees up, and looked out through the wavy glass at Marrow's Bend going gold in the late afternoon — the diner, the church steeple, the long empty block where the mill used to be, which even looked less empty from in here, like the window itself was doing something charitable to the view.

It fit. It fit *exactly*. Like someone had measured me years ago and waited.

"Well?" Dad asked, from the floor, still kneeling, looking up at me with the light behind him.

"It's perfect," I said. "Item three: complete."

"What are items one and two?" Wren asked, though I know for a fact they've seen the list, because I found it once tucked inside Wren's copy of *The Hobbit* with a pencil mark next to item one that just said *✓✓✓*, which felt like a lot of checkmarks for one item but also accurate.

"A person for Dad," I said. "Learning bookbinding. And the nook."

"And what else is on it?" Dad asked. He was smiling now, the full version, the one with teeth that he saves for real things.

I had to think. The list was old. Some of it was embarrassing. Item five was *convince Dad cats aren't dirty* and we were nowhere on that one. But there was one more line at the bottom, added later, in different ink, after the reunion, after everything, when I understood that lists change.

"Item six," I said. "*Stay.*"

The shop went quiet in the specific way it goes quiet when something true has just been said and everyone is deciding whether to be normal about it. Wren's hand found Dad's shoulder. Dad cleared his throat and examined the window frame with great professional interest.

"Already done," Wren said. "That one was easy."

* * *

We opened the bindery doors officially the next weekend, and I use the word *opened* loosely, because the doors had technically been open for weeks and people had technically been wandering in and out the whole time, but Wren wanted a ceremony, so we had one. There was a ribbon — green, naturally — and scissors, and Dad gave a speech that lasted eleven seconds ("This is Wren's. Be kind to the books. Be kinder to each other.") and then cut the ribbon and the whole town poured in like we were giving something away, which, in a way, we were.

Mrs. Adeyemi signed up for the winter tooling course on the spot. The bakery twins brought a cake shaped like a book, which is harder to eat than it sounds. Somebody's kid stuck a sticker on the press and Wren left it there, permanently, and calls it "provenance." Mr. Pell sat in his corner and let people bring him broken hymnals like he was a small shrine. And Dad stood behind the register the whole afternoon, ringing up actual customers, actual sales, more in one day than some months last year, wearing the expression of a man who keeps waiting to wake up and being pleasantly disappointed.

At closing, when the last people left and the light went blue outside the new windows, the three of us sat in my nook, all squished together, which it turns out fits two adults and one child exactly if nobody minds elbows. Dad had tea. Wren had tea. I had hot chocolate, because I am a child and I know my rights.

"We should do something," Wren said, after a while. "For the anniversary."

I felt Dad go still beside me. Not bad-still. Thinking-still.

"Ten years," Wren went on, gently, the way they say careful things. "Next month. I thought — maybe we could visit. All three of us. If you want."

Dad was quiet long enough that I finished half my hot chocolate. Then he said, "Sam would have liked the nook," which wasn't an answer, and was completely an answer, and Wren nodded like it was both.

"Can I come?" I asked. "To the cemetery? I never met Sam but I have questions."

Dad laughed — that wet kind of laugh that isn't really laughing — and put his arm around me and squeezed. "Yeah, sprout. You can come. Sam would've had questions for you, too."

"Like what?"

"Like where you learned to drive a screw like a woodpecker with a grudge."

"That was ONE board—"

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I did what I always do, which is sneak downstairs and read in the dark shop with the lamp from the register, which Dad pretends not to know about. I sat in my nook — *my nook*, still thrilling to type-say-think — with the wavy glass turning the streetlight into a soft gold smear, and I took out the list, because I keep it in the nook now, taped under the shelf where nobody can see it, which means everybody can see it if they're me-sized.

Items one through three: complete. Item four (*teach Wren the town*) — well, that one had sort of completed itself, hadn't it? Wren knew every regular by name and order now. Knew which porch railings were historic and which were lies. Knew that the diner's Tuesday soup was a lie and the Friday pie was true. Item five remained a work in progress; there was a flyer in my desk about an adoption event at the shelter and I intended to deploy it strategically.

And item six. *Stay.*

I looked out at the dark street, at the shop around me — the new shelves full, the bindery door with its painted letters, Odile's portrait above the register watching everything with her always-right eyes — and I thought about how a year ago this was just a shop with a folding chair and a radiator and a dad who kept himself shut like a book nobody was allowed to open. And now the whole town had keys, practically. Now there were cushions on order and cake crumbs in the carpet and a heron mug on the second hook.

Lists are funny. You write them thinking you're asking for things. Really you're describing the shape of the hole, so that someday, when somebody fills it, you'll recognize what happened.

Down the hall, the floor creaked — Dad, doing his last round, the same patrol he's done every night for ten years, except now it takes longer because he stops at the bindery door and stands there a minute, and I know why, and I pretend I don't, and that's its own kind of love.

I taped the list back under the shelf, clicked off the lamp, and started planning item seven.

It was going to require a dog.
