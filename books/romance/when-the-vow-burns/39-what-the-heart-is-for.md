## 39. What the Heart Is For

People assume a bindery is quiet. They imagine the hush of a library, the reverent whisper of tissue paper, some monk-like figure bent over a vise in a shaft of golden light. And I let them imagine it, because it makes me seem more mysterious than I am, and because the truth—that a bindery sounds like a small war conducted with bone folders and awls—discourages visitors during working hours.

But it's seven in the morning, the shop doesn't open for another hour, and I am alone in the back room with a copy of *The Collected Poems of Edna St. Vincent Millay* that some flood three owners ago turned into a brick, and I am thinking about hearts. Specifically about the fact that mine, which spent forty-odd years being a reasonably well-behaved organ, has apparently been going to the gym.

That's not a metaphor I'd have used a year ago. A year ago I'd have said the heart was a pump with delusions of grandeur, and that the business of living was best conducted with the head, which at least keeps receipts. I was a person who moved every eighteen months. I could pack my entire bindery in under two hours; I'd timed it. I had a system: tools in the green trunk, paper in the flat box, and nothing—*nothing*—that couldn't be carried by one person with two good arms and a healthy suspicion of permanence.

The system is in ruins. The green trunk lives under the workbench now, holding spare awls. The flat box holds Juniper's crayon drawings, which she keeps adding to at a rate that suggests she's preparing for an exhibition. And I have a drawer. A *drawer*, in a building I don't own, in a town I couldn't have found on a map four years ago, containing a spare sweater, a second toothbrush, and a photograph Gideon took of me at the harbor last spring that I pretended to hate and then stole when they weren't looking.

A drawer. I've become a person with a drawer. My old self would be appalled. My old self, frankly, was exhausting.

Here's the thing nobody tells you about the heart: it's a muscle. Not poetically—anatomically. And muscles do what you train them to do. Mine had been trained, by decades of leaving, for one motion only: the clean break. Lift, sever, walk away before the roots set. I was very good at it. I could leave a town, a job, a person, with the efficiency of a surgeon and the sentiment of one too, mostly. You tell yourself it's freedom. You tell yourself you're the kind of person who belongs everywhere and therefore nowhere, which sounds like a philosophy and is actually just a way of never having to be missed.

Then I came to Marrow's Bend to repair some water-damaged stock for a bookshop keeper who said thank you like it cost them money, and my heart, untrained for anything else, did the only athletic thing it had ever attempted: it stayed.

* * *

Gideon comes in at seven-forty, as they always do, with coffee and that particular walk of theirs—shoulders first, like the rest of them is following under protest. Ten years of running a shop in a dying town will give you a walk like that. Or maybe the walk came first. I've never asked. There's a whole category of things I've never asked because the answer arrived anyway, over months, in the space between shelving and closing time, the way water arrives in a cellar.

"You're here early," they say, setting my cup on the workbench without asking what I want. Oat milk, one sugar. They've known that since roughly the second week, which should have been my first warning.

"You're here early," I return, because romance is built on a foundation of echoing each other like idiots.

"I'm always here early."

"I'm always here early."

"We're both here early." They lean against the doorframe and look at the Millay, which is open on the bench with its spine splayed like a patient on a table. "How's she doing?"

"She'll live. Someone loved this book hard enough to break it, which is the only way to break a book worth breaking." I smooth a warped page flat. "The owner wants it by Friday. Her mother read it to her, apparently. Every night, the same poems, in the same order."

Gideon is quiet a moment. When I look up, they have that expression—not the shopkeeper's face, not the armor, the other one, the one I had to earn. "Sam read aloud," they say. "Badly. All the voices were the same voice."

"I know."

"You know because I told you."

"I know because you told me, and because you still do all the voices the same way when you read to Juniper, and it's terrible, and she loves it."

The corner of their mouth moves. It's a small economy, Gideon's face. Everything is rationed. But I've learned to read the ledger.

Juniper arrives at eight-fifteen, as she always does, at a velocity that suggests the school run was a chase scene. She has a backpack, a library book, a half-eaten piece of toast, and an announcement.

"We're getting a new kid," she says, dropping onto her stool in the reading nook—the nook Gideon built her in October, with the window seat and the cushion she picked out herself, the exact color of a storm cloud, because she said storm clouds were underrated. "In my class. Her name is Priya and she just moved here and she looked scared at lunch so I sat with her."

"Good instinct," Gideon says.

"I told her about the shop. I said her mom should come here because it's the best place in town and also because you have the good chair." This to me. The good chair is the armchair in the corner of my bindery that I dragged in off the street during a clear-out, reupholstered badly, and defended against Gideon's suggestion that we sell it. People sit in it while I work. It has become, without my planning it, a feature of the establishment.

"Does her mother read?" I ask.

"Everyone reads," Juniper says, with the contempt of the nine-year-old for a stupid question. "She just might not know she does yet. That's what you said about Mrs. Hollis."

I did say that. Mrs. Hollis, who came in last month asking for a gift for her sister and left with four novels and a standing order. The heart is a muscle. So is the reading habit. Both respond to training.

Juniper finishes her toast, announces that Priya likes horses and drawing and "probably books, I'm going to find out," and is collected by the school bell's distant summons, hugging us both with the efficient brutality of a child who has places to be. The door bangs. The shop settles.

Gideon goes to open the front. I hear the familiar liturgy—the deadbolt, the blinds, the sign flipped, the small sigh they make when the day becomes official—and I stand in my bindery with glue on my fingers and think: *I know exactly what happens next.* Not the schedule. The shape of it. And the strange thing, the thing I keep having to relearn like a language I'm fluent in but still sometimes dream in the old one, is that knowing the shape doesn't frighten me.

It used to be that the shape of a place was the first thing I memorized so I could recognize the moment to leave. The shape of Marrow's Bend is different. The shape of Marrow's Bend is: Gideon's coffee at seven-forty, Juniper at eight-fifteen, Mrs. Adeyemi and her mystery paperbacks at nine, the lull at eleven when Gideon and I argue about whether the poetry section should face the window (it should; they say no out of principle and then move it when they think I've forgotten the argument). The shape is the bell over the door. The shape is home, and I keep waiting for the old reflex, the itch between the shoulder blades that always meant *time*, and it doesn't come. The muscle has been retrained. It turns out that's all staying is—not a decision you make once, at some altar, with witnesses, but a decision you make daily, in small motions, until the body believes it.

* * *

The bell rings at ten past nine, which is early for customers, and in comes a woman I don't know, which is rare enough now to be an event. Mid-forties, coat still buttoned, the specific posture of a person who has been driving for a while and isn't sure she's in the right place.

"Hi," she says, in the voice of someone rehearsing. "I'm—this is going to sound strange. I found a book."

Gideon looks up from the register with the expression they reserve for exactly this sentence, which is the expression of a priest hearing a confession they've heard a thousand times and still find holy.

"Okay," Gideon says.

"In my late mother's things. She lived here, years ago. Before I was born, mostly. And there's a book, and it's falling apart, and it has—" the woman's rehearsed speech fails her, which is how you know it was never really a speech, "—writing in it. In the margins. Lots of it. And I thought, maybe, someone here would..."

She trails off. She doesn't have to finish. This is the oldest story in the trade: the damaged book, the dead hand in the margins, the stranger at the counter holding a piece of someone they never got to know.

"Bring it in," Gideon says gently. "That's what we're for."

"And the margins," I call from the back, before I can stop myself. I come to the doorway, wiping my hands. "If the marginalia matters to you, tell me before I start. Some things should be preserved exactly as they are, mess and all. Repair is for the object. The writing is already whole."

The woman looks at me like I've said something profound, which I haven't, or like I've said something she needed, which is better. "It's poetry," she says. "Edna St. Vincent Millay."

I laugh. I can't help it. Of all the benches in all the binderies in the world.

"What?" she says.

"Nothing. Bring it Friday. Or today. Today's fine."

She leaves with the particular lightness of a person who has handed a weight to someone else, and Gideon and I look at each other over the empty counter.

"Millay," they say.

"I know."

"The flood copy on your bench is—"

"Millay. Yes. The universe is showing off." I go back to my bench and look at the broken book and think about the other one, the one with a dead woman's handwriting climbing the margins like ivy, and about how a book is never just a book, it's a hand passing through time, and about how I used to think I was in the business of repairing objects.

I'm not. I never was. I'm in the business of keeping things—spines, stories, marginalia, mornings—intact long enough for the next hand to take them up. That's what a bindery is. That's what a town is. That's what, God help me, a family is: a thing you mend and mend and mend, not because it should last forever but because the mending is the point. The mending *is* the love. Everything else is just the object it happens to.

* * *

At noon we eat lunch on the floor of the poetry section because the table is covered with a delivery of stock and neither of us has the energy to clear it. Gideon has made sandwiches, which they do badly and with tremendous seriousness—too much butter, always, like butter is a moral position—and we sit shoulder to shoulder between the shelves while the shop does its midday breathing.

"I had a thought," Gideon says, around a mouthful.

"Dangerous."

"Verity wrote."

I stop chewing. Verity's letters have a whole taxonomy by now: the formal ones, the grudging ones, the one that was almost an apology and got rerouted at the last second into a complaint about our window display. "Which kind?"

"The kind where she says the family foundation might fund the children's wing." They hand me the letter. I read it. It's three sentences long and every one of them has been sanded down to remove any feeling whatsoever, and at the bottom, in handwriting that loosens just slightly, just once: *Juniper's nook sounds lovely. Odile would have approved of the window seat.*

"Odile would have demanded a bigger one," I say.

"She would have demanded the whole wall." Gideon takes the letter back, folds it along its existing creases—precise, always precise—and puts it in their pocket. "I don't know what to do with her. With any of them. Ten years of nothing and now they keep arriving like weather."

"You could let them."

"Let them what?"

"Arrive. Be weather. You don't have to do anything with weather, Gideon. You just stand in it."

They look at me for a long moment. There's a thing that happens in their face when I say something that lands somewhere they weren't guarding—it's like watching a window open in a house you thought was sealed. "When did you get wise," they say.

"About a year ago. It was very sudden. I blame the company."

The bell rings. Mrs. Adeyemi, right on schedule, wanting her mystery and her gossip in equal measure, and Gideon rises off the floor with the groan of the middle-aged and goes to be the shopkeeper, and I stay sitting among the poems for another minute with my too-buttery sandwich and my retrained heart, and I think about the woman who'll come in on Friday with her mother's Millay, and about Priya who likes horses and drawing and probably books, and about Verity's sandpaper letter with one soft line at the bottom, and about how full all of it is—not sweetly, not simply, there's grit in it and old grief and a woman across town who may never say the word sorry—but full the way a well-used room is full. Lived in. Mended and mended.

The heart is a muscle like any other. You can train it to leave. I did, for years, and I was fast, I was clean, I was gone.

But you can also train it for this: the seven-forty coffee, the eight-fifteen velocity, the bell over the door, the stranger at the counter with a dead woman's handwriting, the shoulder against yours on the poetry-section floor. You can train it to stay. It turns out the muscle doesn't care which. It only wants the work.

I get up, brush the crumbs off, and go to the front, where Gideon is wrapping Mrs. Adeyemi's book in brown paper and lying cheerfully about having read the ending, and the bell rings again, and a young man comes in holding a cardboard box with holes punched in the top, and he looks around the shop with the dazed expression of someone who has been sent here by someone who said, *go to Eastwick Books, they'll know what to do.*

"We will," I say, before he's said a word.

Gideon glances at me. "We will," they agree.

He sets the box on the counter, and from inside it, unmistakably, comes a small, indignant mewing.
