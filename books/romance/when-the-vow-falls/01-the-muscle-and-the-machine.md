## 1. The Muscle and the Machine

The cake arrived at 4:12 p.m., which was eleven minutes later than the contract specified, and Ada Sterling did not panic.

She did not panic because panicking was a luxury item, like peonies in December or sentiment at a reception — lovely in theory, ruinous on the invoice. Instead she did what she had trained herself to do for ten years, which was to become very still and very efficient at the same time, the way water becomes ice.

"Marcus," she said, without raising her voice, "the cake is late."

Marcus, her second-in-command and the only man alive who could tie a chair sash in under four seconds, appeared at her elbow as if summoned by the word *cake*. "Traffic on the bridge."

"There's no traffic on the bridge. There's never traffic on the bridge. There's one road and it goes in a straight line."

"He says there was a goose."

"A goose."

"A whole situation with a goose, apparently."

Ada looked at him. Marcus looked back at her with the serene expression of a man who had learned long ago that arguing with Ada Sterling about geese was like arguing with a tide.

"Fine," she said. "The goose happened. The cake is here now. We have forty-eight minutes until the couple's grand entrance, the buttercream needs twenty minutes to settle after transport, and the photographer wants his golden-hour shots on the terrace at 5:30 sharp. So we cut the cake table setup from fifteen minutes to nine, we skip the ribbon, and nobody tells Priya that her three-tiered ivory dream nearly died on a bridge because of poultry." She was already walking as she talked, heels clicking across the ballroom floor like a metronome setting the tempo of the evening. "Also, tell the kitchen the vegan plates go out first this time. Last month they came out last and Mrs. Okafor's sister cried, and I refuse to cry-adjacent anyone before the toasts."

"Cry-adjacent," Marcus repeated, delighted, and went off to do battle.

Ada allowed herself exactly one breath — in through the nose, out through the mouth, four counts each, the way she'd taught herself at nineteen when her whole life caught fire — and then she surveyed the room.

It was, objectively, perfect.

Two hundred candles burning at identical heights. White roses and ranunculus spilling down the archway in a cascade she had spent three hours arranging by hand because the florist's version had been, in her professional opinion, *trying too hard*. The string quartet tuned in the corner. The place cards alphabetized with an attention to family feuds so precise that Uncle Robert and Cousin Daniel were seated at opposite ends of a room the size of a football pitch, and neither would ever know it was deliberate.

This was the thing people didn't understand about weddings, Ada thought. They thought it was about love. It wasn't. Love was chaos — love was a goose on a bridge, love was a father in a courtroom, love was every uncontrolled variable in the world wearing a nice suit. A wedding was the opposite of love. A wedding was the moment humanity looked directly at chaos and said, *no thank you, we'll take a seating chart instead.*

She had built a career on that principle. Sterling Events — just Ada, technically, but the plural made her sound established — was known across the city for one thing above all others: nothing went wrong at a Sterling wedding. Brides recommended her in the hushed, reverent tones usually reserved for surgeons and saints. *She thinks of everything,* they said. *Nothing rattles her.* One mother-of-the-bride had once told a local magazine that watching Ada work was "like watching a Swiss watch fall in love with a spreadsheet," which Ada had framed, because it was accurate and because framing it annoyed her ex, who said she took compliments like performance reviews.

At 5:31 p.m., Priya and Tom made their grand entrance to a string arrangement of the song from their first date, and Priya cried, and Tom cried harder, and two hundred guests pretended not to film it while filming it, and Ada stood at the back of the room with her clipboard against her chest and felt —

Nothing, she told herself. Professional satisfaction. Which is a feeling. It counts.

But there was a moment — she'd noticed it over the years, always the same moment — when the couple turned to face each other during the vows and the entire room seemed to hold its breath, and Ada, standing in the wings with her radio earpiece and her contingency plans, felt something press against the inside of her ribs. Not longing. She'd had that word surgically removed years ago, along with several other soft tissues. More like... recognition. Like watching someone else open a door she had personally welded shut, and finding herself curious about the weather on the other side.

She dealt with the feeling the way she dealt with all feelings: she gave it a task. She checked the sparkler inventory for the send-off. Twice.

* *

By 11:40 p.m., the last guest had wobbled into a taxi, the sparklers had smoked themselves out, and the venue staff were breaking down chairs with the cheerful brutality of people paid hourly. Ada signed off with the coordinator, tipped the kitchen crew extra because the vegan plates had gone out first and Mrs. Okafor's sister had, blessedly, remained dry-eyed, and walked out into the parking lot carrying a box of leftover candles and the particular exhaustion that comes from being flawless for fourteen straight hours.

Her flat was twelve minutes away by car and a hundred years away in every other respect.

It sat above a shuttered bookbinder's shop on a quiet street where the trees grew old and unbothered, and it was small and clean and arranged with the same precision she brought to ballrooms — books shelved by mood rather than author, because Ada believed alphabetical order was a coward's system; one armchair angled toward the window; a kitchen used mostly for tea and the occasional attempt at her mother's lemon cake, which always failed, which she always attempted anyway, which was possibly the most sentimental thing about her and therefore classified information.

She set the candle box down. She took off her heels at the door — a ritual, the armor coming off joint by joint — and padded across the floorboards in stockinged feet to the bedroom, where she changed into an ancient university sweatshirt that said ROWING CLUB even though she had never rowed anything except a deadline.

Then she made tea, and then she did the thing she did every night.

The tin box lived on the top shelf of her closet, behind the winter scarves she never wore. It was an actual tin — shortbread, once, a gift from a client — dented at one corner, and inside it were letters. Ninety-one letters, if you counted the first half-finished one, which Ada did count, because she counted everything.

Ten years' worth. Unsigned. Untraceable. Unstoppable.

They had started the autumn her father went to prison, arriving like weather — the first one slipped under the door of the flat she'd shared with two roommates who'd stopped asking her if she was okay because she kept answering *fine* in a voice that ended conversations. No return address. No name. Just careful, slanted handwriting on cheap paper, and an opening line she had never forgotten:

*I don't know how to say sorry for something this size, so I'm going to say it slowly, for as long as it takes.*

She had assumed it was a mistake. Then a prank. Then, when the second letter came, and the third — each one somehow knowing exactly the shape of the week she'd had, the exact weight of the thing she couldn't say out loud — she had decided it was either a miracle or a madness, and she'd stopped questioning it somewhere around year three.

Tonight she pulled the tin down, sat cross-legged on the bed with her tea steaming on the nightstand, and chose at random, the way you'd draw a card.

March, six years ago. The paper had gone soft at the folds.

*You wrote that you planned a wedding this week for a couple who met at a funeral. I've been thinking about that. About how you spend your days building the happiest rooms in the world while standing in the saddest one yourself. I don't think that's irony. I think it's you refusing to let the world's worst argument win.*

Ada read it twice. Her thumb traced the edge of the page, the way you'd touch a scar to check it was still yours.

Whoever he was — she'd settled on *he* around year five, based on nothing but instinct, the way you settle on a name for a stray — he knew things about her that no living person knew. He knew about her father. He knew about the trial, though he never asked questions about it, never pried, just left space around the wound the way you leave space around furniture in a dark room. He knew she hadn't attended a wedding as a guest since the conviction — only as staff, always as staff, behind the rope line of other people's joy. He knew about her mother's silence, which was louder than shouting. He knew, somehow, that Ada had decided at some point in her twenties that love was less like falling and more like training — that the heart was a muscle, and muscles could be disciplined, conditioned, contained — and in letter forty-something he had written:

*You talk about your heart like it's a horse you're trying to break. I hope someday somebody lets it out to pasture. Not me. I don't get to be anybody. But somebody.*

She had cried at that one, once, alone, briefly, and then washed her face and gone to a site visit, because grief was also schedulable.

That was the creed, anyway. Her creed, self-authored, rigorously enforced: the heart is trainable. Feelings arrive uninvited; you are not obligated to let them stay. Wanting things is a fire hazard in a house built of paper, so build with stone. She had watched what wanting did — to her mother, who wanted justice so badly it ate the rest of her; to her father, who had wanted, allegedly, the wrong things, depending on which lawyer was talking. Ada wanted nothing. Ada needed nothing. Ada planned perfect days for other people and went home to an empty flat and a tin box, and called it peace.

Most nights she almost believed it.

She finished the tea. She read the letter a third time, then returned it to the tin with the others, ninety-one envelopes in loose chronological order, a decade of being deeply known by absolutely no one. She was reaching up to shelve the box when she heard it — a sound from outside, from the direction of the house next door, which had stood empty since spring and whose FOR LET sign she'd mentally catalogued months ago the way she catalogued everything.

A truck. An engine cutting out. And then — unmistakable, at half past midnight — the scrape of something heavy being dragged across pavement, followed by a low male voice swearing at it with real feeling.

Ada stood at her window in her ROWING CLUB sweatshirt, tin box under her arm, and looked down into the dark at the silhouette of a man wrestling a piece of furniture up the front steps of the empty house next door.

"Huh," she said, to no one.

She didn't know yet that his name was Elian Marsh. She didn't know why he'd come, or what he was carrying besides furniture, or that he had chosen this street — this exact street, this exact house, twelve feet of garden wall away from her window — with the precision of a woman planning a wedding.

She just knew that someone had moved in wrong: at midnight, alone, cursing at a sofa under the stars.

Ada watched for another thirty seconds — purely for security purposes, she told herself, neighborhood awareness being simply good practice — then closed the curtain, shelved the tin, and got into bed.

The heart is trainable, she reminded the ceiling.

The ceiling, like the rest of the world, declined to comment.
