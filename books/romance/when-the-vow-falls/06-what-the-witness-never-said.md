## 6. What the Witness Never Said

The courtroom smelled of lemon polish and fear.

Elian Marsh had noticed the polish first — how could he not, at twenty-three, sweating through a suit his mother had bought him for funerals, sitting in a room where every surface gleamed like it was being watched? The fear he noticed second. It came off the gallery in waves, off the Sterling family in the third row, off the man in the dock who kept turning to look at his daughter as if she were the only fixed point in a spinning world.

Ada Sterling had been twenty then. Elian knew this because he'd read it in the trial packet the night before, along with everything else the prosecution thought he needed to know: her name, her age, her father's business, the amount of the missing funds, the shape of the story they wanted him to help them tell.

What the packet hadn't told him was that she would look at him.

Witnesses weren't supposed to be looked at, he'd assumed. They were furniture with mouths. But when the bailiff walked him to the stand, Ada Sterling turned in her seat, and her eyes — gray, level, unblinking — went through him like a drill through soft wood. She didn't glare. Glaring would have been easier. She *assessed* him, the way you'd assess a load-bearing wall before deciding whether to trust the house.

He had been carrying a lie in his chest for eleven months by then. It sat under his sternum like a swallowed stone, and when he raised his right hand and swore to tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, the stone shifted, and he nearly gagged on the words *so help me God.*

"State your name for the record."

"Elian James Marsh."

"And Mr. Marsh, what is your relationship to Halvorsen Freight Solutions?"

"I was an accounts clerk there. Two years."

The prosecutor, a broad, ruddy man named Calloway with a voice built for auction houses, smiled at him the way you smile at a horse you've already bought. "And in your role, did you have occasion to observe the financial practices of the company's founder, Mr. Thomas Sterling?"

Here is what Elian had seen, in those two years, from his desk by the window with the broken blind:

He had seen Thomas Sterling sign off on invoices late into the evening, alone, because he didn't trust anyone else with them and couldn't afford an auditor. He had seen the man eat dinner out of a paper bag at his desk and apologize to the cleaning staff for the mess. He had seen him take a call from a supplier and argue them down from a price increase with the weary patience of a man protecting something small and fragile — payroll, probably. He had seen him give an interest-free loan to a driver whose wife was sick, and mark it in the books, openly, in his own handwriting, as what it was: a loan, repayable, no interest, *because that's what we do here.*

And he had seen — this was the part Calloway loved, this was the part they'd rehearsed four times in a conference room that smelled of burnt coffee — a signature on a transfer authorization that wasn't quite right. A signature that moved money from the pension buffer into an account Elian had never been able to trace, in the spring of the year everything fell apart.

What Elian had also seen, and what nobody had asked him about, and what he had not offered:

The authorization form had come up through the office while Sterling was in hospital. Appendicitis, emergency surgery, nine days gone. In those nine days, the man actually running the company was Gerald Voss, the operations director, the one with the boat and the laugh and the friendship with two of the board members. The signature on the authorization was Sterling's name in someone else's hand — good enough to fool a junior clerk skimming a stack of forty forms on a Friday afternoon, which is exactly what Elian had been.

He'd realized it three weeks later, lying awake, doing what accountants do: replaying numbers. By the time he worked up the nerve to say anything, Voss had resigned — *pursuing other opportunities*, the email said — and the money was somewhere unfindable, and the police had already decided the story was simpler than the truth. Simpler stories convict faster.

He'd told Calloway all of this. That was the thing people never understood later, when they called him a liar. He *had* told him. In the conference room, in the fourth hour, he'd said: the signature's wrong, Sterling was in hospital, Voss had access, I think it was Voss.

And Calloway had leaned back in his chair, laced his fingers over his vest, and given Elian the gentlest, most reasonable smile in the history of the justice system.

"Son," he'd said, "do you know what happens to cases built on speculation? They collapse. And do you know what happens to witnesses whose testimony collapses?" He hadn't answered his own question. He'd just let it sit there, fat and patient, between them.

Then, softer: "You saw a forged signature. You'll testify to the forged signature. The jury will draw the obvious conclusion. Justice gets done, the guilty party pays, and a young man with a bright future doesn't find himself remembered as the witness who let a thief walk." A pause, a pen tap. "Voss has lawyers, Elian. Good ones. Sterling has grief and a public defender. Which of those men do you want to owe anything to?"

It hadn't felt like being threatened. That was the trick of it, the thing Elian would turn over for ten years like a stone looking for its underside. It had felt like being *helped*. Like an adult taking a confused boy by the shoulder and pointing him at the version of events that made sense. Calloway had even seemed kind about it. He'd bought Elian lunch. Turkey sandwich, chips, a coffee that tasted like a tire fire. He'd said, "You're doing the right thing," and for a while — for a terrible, malleable while — Elian had believed that the right thing and the easy thing had simply, for once, aligned.

So on the stand, he testified to the forged signature. He described the form. He confirmed the date, the amount, the account. And when Calloway asked, "Did you have any reason to believe Mr. Sterling was not responsible for this transaction?" — the question hanging there like a door left open on purpose — Elian heard himself say:

"No."

One word. One syllable, really, stretched over the gap where nine days of hospital and a wrong-handed signature should have been. The stone in his chest rolled over. Somewhere behind him, a pen scratched. In the third row, Ada Sterling's father closed his eyes, and his daughter reached over and took his hand, and Elian Marsh, twenty-three years old, understood with total clarity that he had just helped bury an innocent man — and told himself he hadn't, because the question hadn't technically asked about hospitals, and because Calloway had promised, afterward, squeezing his arm outside the courthouse steps, "You did well. You did *right*," in the voice of a man closing a deal.

The verdict took the jury six hours.

* * *

Ten years later, Elian still dreamed about the turkey sandwich.

Not the courtroom — the sandwich. His mind, in its nocturnal cruelty, kept returning to that detail: Calloway unwrapping the wax paper, asking if he wanted the chips too, the ordinary decency of it, the way evil apparently couldn't be bothered to announce itself when a decent lunch was available. In the dream he sometimes said no thank you. Sometimes he threw the sandwich at the wall. Once — his favorite version — he stood up in the conference room and said, "Actually, Detective, let me tell you about the nine days," and the walls peeled back and everyone applauded, which is how he knew it was a dream, because in life the walls stayed put and nobody applauded and the sandwich got eaten either way.

He woke from that version at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, in the flat next to Ada Sterling's, with his heart doing its new irregular stutter — *ba-dum, ba-ba-dum* — like a drummer who kept losing the count.

He lay still and waited for it to settle. This was the routine now. Wait, breathe, catalog. Chest: complaining but cooperative. Hands: steady. The letters: stacked in the bottom drawer of his desk, ten years' worth of carbon copies, every word he'd ever sent her unsigned.

That was the other ritual, the one that came after the breathing. He got up, made tea he wouldn't finish, and opened the drawer.

The first letter was the hardest to reread, so he always read it first. *Dear Miss Sterling* — he hadn't known what else to call her — *I don't expect you to read this, and I won't blame you if you burn it.* He'd sent it eight months after the verdict, after he'd tracked her down through a wedding magazine feature (of all things; of course that thing), after two years of drafting and burning drafts in the sink of a rented room that smelled of damp. He'd signed it with nothing. He'd told himself anonymity was mercy. Later, more honestly: anonymity was cowardice wearing mercy's coat.

She'd written back anyway. Three lines, guarded as barbed wire. So he'd written again. And again. And somewhere in the third year, without either of them naming it, the letters stopped being penance and became the truest conversation of his life. She told him things she told no one — he was almost sure of that, and the sureness was its own kind of theft, intimacy acquired under false papers. He told her almost everything back. Almost. There was a hole in every letter, shaped like a courtroom, and she had never once asked him to fill it, and he had never once found the courage to do it unprompted.

Today, though, today the drawer ritual got interrupted, because today was Thursday, and Thursdays belonged to Dr. Okafor.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Dr. Okafor said.

"Doing what?"

"The math." She tapped his chart against her knee. She was a compact woman with reading glasses pushed up into her hair and the bedside manner of a foreman — which was, Elian had decided years ago, exactly what you wanted in a cardiologist. Pity was useless. Schedules were useful. "You sit there running numbers on how many beats you've got left. I can see it. You get this look like an accountant at audit time."

"That's insulting to accountants. We have better posture."

"Mm." She flipped a page. "Your ejection fraction's down another point. The medication's buying us time, not winning the war. And the fatigue you keep describing as 'nothing' is not nothing." She looked at him over the glasses she'd now pulled down onto her nose, and her voice did the thing it did maybe twice a year — dropped the foreman, let the person underneath show through. "Elian. I need you to hear a number."

"Don't write it down," he said.

"What?"

"If you say a number, I'll want to write it down, and if I write it down, it becomes real, and if it becomes real—" He gestured vaguely at himself, at the examining room, at the whole enterprise. "I've spent ten years living inside a number I wrote down. Six hours, jury deliberation. I don't recommend it."

Okafor took the glasses off entirely, which was unprecedented, and folded her hands. "Then hear it without writing it. Months, not years. Could be many months. Could be fewer. The transplant list is a lottery ticket and you know how I feel about lotteries." She paused. "There are things you could still do. Things that are easier sooner rather than later. If there's anyone you need to talk to—"

"There's someone," he said.

"Then talk to them."

"It's complicated."

"Everything worth saying is." She stood, brisk again, armor back on. "Same time next month. Walk, don't run. And Elian — stop doing the math. Do the thing instead."

He walked home the long way, because walking was permitted and he intended to wring every permitted thing dry. Past the bakery where the woman with flour on her apron saved him the end piece of the seed loaf without being asked. Past the school where kids screamed at a ball with pure uncomplicated joy. Past the flower stall where he stopped, as he'd stopped every week for a month now, and looked at the buckets of ranunculus without buying any, because he hadn't yet figured out what kind of man buys flowers for a woman who doesn't know he ruined her family, and whether that man is worse or better than the kind who doesn't.

Months, not years.

The words kept their own rhythm against his stuttering heart. *Ba-dum. Ba-ba-dum.* He found he wasn't afraid of the number itself. What frightened him was the arithmetic of what remained undone. Ten years of letters, and she still didn't know his name. Ten years of penance, and her father was still inside. Ten years of telling himself *next letter, next letter, I'll tell her in the next letter* — and the next letter had become a country he lived in.

He climbed the stairs to his flat, slowly, honoring the new rules, and passed her door on the landing. Through it, faintly, he could hear her moving around — the particular sound of Ada Sterling at the end of a working day, which he had learned the way you learn a neighbor's floorboards: the clink of the kettle, the sigh of the sofa, once, memorably, a burst of singing so brief and so unguarded it had made him sit down on his own stairs like a man struck.

She was in there. Living. Planning strangers' weddings, drinking whatever coffee he'd started leaving by her door — she'd never mentioned it, but she'd never thrown it away either, and he counted the empty cups in the shared bin like a miser counting coins.

*Talk to them,* Okafor had said. As if talking were a door and not a dam.

That night he sat at his desk and pulled out fresh paper. Not the letters — those had rules, a voice, a decade of careful architecture. This was going to be different. This was going to be the whole thing, start to finish: the conference room, the sandwich, the nine days, the signature, the promise, the verdict, the drawer full of copies, all of it, laid out in order like evidence, because that's what it was. Evidence. For a case he was finally ready to bring before the only judge who mattered.

*Dear Ada,* he wrote.

His hand shook. He blamed the medication, though he suspected it was something older.

*My name is Elian Marsh, and ten years ago I—*

He stared at the sentence. The stone under his sternum rolled. Outside, rain began against the window, soft and insistent, like fingers testing a lock.

Downstairs, or rather sideways, through the wall, he heard her kettle click off, and he thought: soon. Soon I will say it out loud, to her face, and lose her, and deserve to.

But not tonight. Tonight he had a letter to draft, and a timeline he refused to write down, and for the first time in ten years, a deadline that had nothing to do with courts.

He bent his head and began.
