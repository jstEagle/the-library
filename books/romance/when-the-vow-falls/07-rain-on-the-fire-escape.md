## 7. Rain on the Fire Escape

The storm came in off the river at four o'clock, the way summer storms do in this city — without apology and without an appointment, which is more than I can say for most of my clients.

I was on the fire escape with a clipboard when the first drop hit the page, blooming like a bruise across the seating chart for the Halloway rehearsal dinner. Forty-six guests. Two feuding cousins who could not be seated within eleven feet of each other. One grandmother with a walker and strong opinions about the band.

"Come on," I told the sky, as if it had ever once listened to me. "I had a system."

The sky, characteristically, did not care about my system. The rain came down in earnest then, fat and sudden, and I did what any reasonable person does when caught between a fourth-floor window and a downpour: I scrambled for the awning two floors down, the rusted canvas thing bolted over the bakery's back door, and I dropped onto it with all the grace of a woman who plans other people's elegance and owns none herself.

Except the awning was already occupied.

"Careful," said Elian Marsh, not looking up from the notebook in his lap. "You nearly took my eye out with your clipboard."

"You nearly took out my seating chart," I said, clutching it to my chest, water running down the back of my collar. "There are forty-six people on this page. Forty-six. Do you know how long it took to balance the Halloway cousins?"

"I can't say I've given it a great deal of thought."

"Eleven feet," I said. "That's the minimum safe distance between Marcus and Denise Halloway. Eleven feet, or the rehearsal dinner becomes a crime scene."

He looked at me then — that look he does, the one that's less a glance than an audit. Rain drummed on the canvas above us, loud as applause. He was sitting on an upturned crate with his notebook, and there was a pencil behind his ear that he seemed to have forgotten about, and for a man who had spent six weeks being aggressively un-neighboring, he looked almost — I want to say *comfortable*, but that would be a lie I'm not ready to tell.

"You're going to catch your death out here," he said.

"It's July."

"You're going to catch a *cold* out here, then. Devastating for the wedding industry."

I should have gone back up. The window was right there. My flat was right there, with its kettle and its dry socks and its one letter, the Tuesday letter, which I read on Tuesdays the way other people take communion. I had a ritual. I had a system. I had eleven feet of cousin management to finalize.

Instead I sat down on the opposite end of the awning, on an overturned bucket that some bakery employee had clearly abandoned, and told myself it was because the rain was too heavy to move through.

It was not too heavy. But I stayed.

* * *

We watched the rain for a while. That's the thing nobody tells you about storms — they give you permission to be silent with someone. You don't have to fill the space, because the sky is doing it for you, drumming on the canvas like it has opinions of its own.

"You know," Elian said eventually, "you're the only person on this street who goes out the fire escape instead of the front door."

"The front door sticks."

"The front door sticks because you won't let the landlord fix it. I've seen you oil it yourself. Twice."

I turned to look at him. He was watching the rain, not me, which was the only reason I let myself look back. He had that quality — I'd noticed it in the stairwell, in the doorway, over the fence he'd mended without being asked — of a man who pays attention to everything and admits to nothing.

"Why do you watch me?" I asked. It came out blunter than I'd planned, which, with me, is a redundancy.

"I don't watch you."

"You watch me the way I watch weather. You knew I'd be on that fire escape before I did."

He was quiet for a moment. The pencil came down from behind his ear and turned over in his fingers, once, twice.

"You come out at the same times," he said. "Seven-forty with coffee. Six-fifteen with the clipboard. Sundays you sit out there with a letter and read it like it's going to change its mind."

The bottom of my stomach dropped, the way it does in elevators. "You read my mail?"

"I see you *reading* your mail. There's a difference, though I'll admit the difference is thin." He still wasn't looking at me. "I see a lot of things. It's a bad habit. I don't have much else to do."

"Everyone has something to do."

"Not everyone." He said it lightly, but something underneath it wasn't light at all, and for a moment I thought he was going to say more. He didn't. The pencil went back behind his ear, retired.

"Your coffee," I said, because I needed to move the conversation somewhere I could survive it. "The other week. You left a bag on my doorstep. The Ethiopian one, light roast, the kind nobody around here carries. How did you even know?"

"Lucky guess."

"Nobody guesses that. The corner shop owner didn't know that bean existed. I had to special-order it for two years."

"Then you're easy to please and hard to shop for, and I got lucky." He shrugged, and the shrug had a wall built into it. "Take the win, Ada."

I took the win. I want that on the record. I took the win, and I didn't push, because pushing is what I do at work, not in my life, and because there was a look on his face I recognized from funerals — that specific stillness of a man standing very close to something he can't afford to knock over.

* * *

The rain got heavier. It came down the alley in sheets now, bouncing off the pavement, silvering the air between the buildings, and the awning groaned under it like an old man asked to dance.

"Do you have family around here?" I asked. It was an ordinary question. I'd asked it of a hundred strangers at a hundred weddings, standing at bars with a glass of champagne I never drank, making small talk the way other people breathe.

"No."

"That's it? No?"

"My father's dead. My mother and I aren't on speaking terms. There's a brother in Vancouver who thinks I'm someone I stopped being a long time ago." He said it the way you'd read a grocery list. "That's the whole tour."

"I'm sorry. About your father."

"Don't be. He was a complicated man." A pause. "Mostly in the way that a car crash is complicated."

I laughed. It escaped before I could stop it — a short, surprised sound, undignified, nothing like the warm professional laugh I keep holstered for clients and their mothers. Elian turned and looked at me like I'd done something in a language he'd only read about.

"Sorry," I said. "That was — I shouldn't laugh at your dead father."

"He'd have laughed. It's the only nice thing I could say about him." He shifted on the crate. "What about you? You've never once mentioned family, and I've been next door for six weeks. I know your coffee order and your Sunday letter ritual and that you talk to your seating charts, but I don't know if you have a mother."

"I have a mother."

"That's a sentence that's doing a lot of work."

"She's — " I looked at the rain. It was easier than looking at him. "We're close. We're very close. We just don't — there are things we don't do anymore. Weddings, mostly."

"Weddings."

"I don't go to them. As a guest, I mean. I plan them, I run them, I stand in the back with my clipboard and my headset and I make sure the day goes perfectly for other people. But I haven't sat in one of those chairs in — " I did the math and didn't like the answer. "Nine years. Ten in October."

"Why?"

And here's the thing. Nobody asks me that. Clients don't ask because I'm the help. Friends stopped asking years ago because my answer never changed and my face, apparently, does something when it's asked, some small closing, like a shop pulling down its gate. My mother doesn't ask because she knows, and knowing is the heaviest thing we carry between us.

But this man, this gruff, fence-mending, coffee-guessing man who had lived next door for six weeks and knew my bean origin, asked it like it was a simple question with a simple answer, and I heard myself say:

"My father's in prison. He was convicted when I was twenty-two. Fraud — investment fraud, they said, though the whole thing was more complicated than that, it always is. And the trial — " The rain filled the pause for me. "There was a witness. The star witness. His testimony was the thing that did it. And after, my mother stopped going to anything joyful. Weddings, christenings, graduations. She said we didn't get to have nice days while my father was counting days in a cell, and I — " I shrugged, and hated that the shrug was his shrug, inherited in six weeks. "I was twenty-two and I wanted my mother to not be in that much pain, so I stopped too. And then I got good at planning the days other people were allowed to have."

The words hung there in the drumming quiet. I hadn't said any of it out loud in years. It came out smoother than I expected, worn smooth, like a stone I'd been carrying in my pocket so long I'd forgotten it was a stone and not part of my hand.

Elian was very still. I mean *very* still — stiller than a man should be, stiller than the rain, and when he finally spoke his voice had changed, dropped down into something careful.

"What was your father's name?"

"Gerald. Gerald Sterling." I smiled, and it felt strange on my face. "You wouldn't have heard of it. It was in the papers for a while and then it wasn't. The world is very good at moving on from other people's disasters."

"No," he said quietly. "The world is terrible at it. It just pretends."

I looked at him. He was looking at the rain, but he wasn't seeing it — I know the difference, I've watched a thousand grooms wear that face in the fifteen minutes before the ceremony, that look of a man staring at the life he's about to not have. His hands were folded around the notebook like it was something breakable, or like he was.

"Elian," I said. "Are you all right?"

"I'm fine." He stood, too fast, and the crate tipped over behind him. "It's the rain. My chest doesn't love the rain."

"Your chest?"

"Bad heart," he said, with the same grocery-list flatness. "Congenital. It's being managed." The way he said *managed* — I've heard brides say *compromise*. "I should go up. Before the awning gives out and takes us both with it."

He gathered his notebook, and I saw — just for a second, just a flash of the page before he closed it — that it wasn't sketches in there at all, or not only. It was writing. Rows and rows of small, tight handwriting, the kind of handwriting that has a lot to say and no one to say it to.

"You write," I said.

"Badly."

"Everyone says that. It's never true."

"Sometimes it's true," he said, and there was something in it, some weight I didn't have the code for yet. He paused at the edge of the awning, rain sheeting past him, and looked back at me. "Ada. Your father. The trial — was there ever anything that didn't sit right? About the case itself?"

I blinked. "What kind of question is that?"

"A badly worded one. I'm out of practice asking things."

"Out of practice," I repeated. "You talk like a man who used to do a great deal of asking."

"Used to," he said. "Goodnight, Ada."

"It's four-thirty in the afternoon."

"Goodnight anyway." And then he stepped out into the rain and walked through it, unhurried, getting soaked to the skin in the eight steps it took him to reach his door, and I sat there on my bucket under the groaning awning with my ruined seating chart and my heart doing something it had no business doing.

* * *

I didn't go up right away. I sat there while the storm spent itself, and I thought about the strange arithmetic of the afternoon — that I had told a near-stranger about my father, that he had asked about the trial like a man checking a lock, that his chest didn't love the rain and his notebook was full of handwriting and he knew my coffee the way you know a prayer.

I laughed today, I thought. Actually laughed. And it had felt — God, it had felt like a window opening in a house I'd sealed shut years ago, and I hated it, I hated how good it felt, because windows let things out but they let things *in*, and I had a system. I had eleven feet of cousin management. I had a creed.

The heart is trainable. The heart is containable. The heart is a muscle you keep on a schedule.

The rain thinned to a drizzle. Somewhere across the alley, through his window, I could see Elian Marsh at his desk, lamp on, hunched over that notebook, writing — no, *copying*, I realized, copying something from one page to another with the concentration of a man defusing a bomb.

And on the floor beside his chair, half in shadow, I could see the edge of something that made my whole body go still.

A box. A small, old post-office box, the kind with a brass slot.

The kind you keep letters in.
