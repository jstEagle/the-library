## 15. What My Father Actually Said

The bus to Halden Correctional Facility left from a depot that smelled of diesel and old pretzels, and I spent the entire ninety-minute ride rehearsing arguments with a man who wasn't there.

That's what I do. It's what I've always done. When a seating chart goes wrong, I run the disaster in my head three times before it can happen once. When a florist threatens to deliver four hundred white peonies instead of blush ones, I have the confrontation won before my phone even rings. Control is just rehearsal with better lighting.

So on the bus, in the gray light of an October morning, I rehearsed.

*You lied to me.* No — he hadn't lied. He'd simply never told me the whole truth, which is a different crime and one I was in no position to prosecute, given that I'd spent ten years writing my heart out to a stranger whose name I never asked for because asking felt like breaking a spell.

*You let her poison us against him.* Better. Closer to true. But "let" implied my father had ever once been able to stop my mother from doing anything, and anyone who'd met Ruth Sterling knew that was fantasy.

*Why didn't you tell me?*

There it was. The real question, the one underneath all the others, the one that had kept me awake since I'd stood in my mother's kitchen two days ago with a photograph of a young witness in one hand and ten years of unsigned letters burning a hole through my memory.

I had come to hate Elian Marsh. I had done it thoroughly, professionally, with the same precision I brought to everything — color-coded rage, itemized betrayal, a timeline of every moment I'd almost trusted him annotated in red. My mother would have been proud. Her fury was the family heirloom, passed down like the good china: *that man destroyed your father, Ada, and don't you forget it.*

But somewhere around hour six of not sleeping, a small, traitorous voice had asked: *destroyed him how, exactly? You've only ever heard one version.*

And so here I was, on a bus full of Sunday visitors clutching vending-machine flowers, going to ask my father for his.

* * *

Halden is not the kind of prison movies prepare you for. There are no towers, no snarling dogs. It sits behind birch trees like a community college that lost its funding, all pale wood and glass, as if someone had designed a building to apologize. Even the visiting room is gentle — soft chairs, a play corner for children, windows that look out onto actual grass.

My father was already at our table when they brought me back. He always is. Ten years of practice; he knows exactly how long the processing line takes, and he times his arrival so that I never have to walk into a room and search for him. It's such a small kindness. It's the kind of thing I plan into weddings for strangers and never noticed he'd been doing for me.

"Ada." He said my name the way he says everything — quietly, like it costs nothing and means everything. He'd gone grayer since spring. The beard he'd grown after the conviction — my mother called it his protest, I'd always thought of it as armor — was trimmed short now. "You look tired."

"I look like someone who took a bus at seven a.m."

"You used to love buses."

"I used to love a lot of things." I sat down across from him. Between us: a bolted-down table, two paper cups of terrible coffee, and eleven years of things we didn't say. "Hi, Dad."

"Hi, sweetheart."

We did the ritual first. It takes about twenty minutes and neither of us calls it a ritual. He asks about work; I tell him about the wedding that imploded — the bride who eloped with the sous-chef, the venue that canceled, the eighteen-hour days I've been pulling to salvage something beautiful out of wreckage. He laughs in the right places. He always laughs in the right places. Then he tells me about the greenhouse program, the tomatoes he's growing, the novel he's reading, the man in C-block who cheats outrageously at chess. His life, narrated in the tone of a man describing a long layover.

It's how we protect each other. We talk about everything except the thing.

Today, I broke the pattern.

"Dad." I turned my cup in a slow circle. "I need to ask you about the trial."

His hands went still on the table. Not dramatically — my father doesn't do dramatic — but still, the way water goes still before it freezes. "Okay," he said.

"The star witness. Elian Marsh." Saying the name out loud, to him, made my chest hurt in a way I hadn't budgeted for. "Mom says he destroyed you. That his testimony put you away. She's said it so many times it feels like scripture. But—" I made myself look up. "I only ever heard her version. And Mom loves a story where she's the last honest person standing. So I came to hear yours."

For a long moment he just looked at me. And I saw something cross his face that I'd never seen there before — not pain, exactly. Something older and sadder. Recognition, maybe. Like he'd been waiting ten years for this question and grieving the whole time that I'd have to ask it.

"You know," he said, "your mother used to be funny. Did I ever tell you that? Before all this. She had this laugh — she'd snort, right at the end, completely undignified. She'd have hated me telling you that." He smiled faintly. "People aren't their worst year, Ada. Your mother isn't her bitterness. I try to remember that when her letters arrive full of fire and brimstone."

"Dad."

"I'm getting there." He wrapped both hands around his coffee cup like it was something warm, which it wasn't. "What do you want to know?"

"Start with him. Marsh. Was he lying?"

"No." The answer came without hesitation, and it knocked something loose in me. "No, he wasn't lying. That's the part nobody wants to hear. Everything he said on that stand was true. Every word." My father looked out the window at the birches. "He just didn't say everything that was true. There's a difference, and it turns out the difference is ten years."

My heart was doing something unprofessional. "Tell me."

"He worked for me, you remember. Nineteen years old, summer job, sorting inventory. Good kid. Quiet. The kind of quiet that notices things." He turned the cup again. "When the auditors came through, there were discrepancies. Real ones — I'm not going to stand here and tell you the books were clean, because they weren't entirely, and that's on me and on your Uncle Theo, may he rest in chaos. Embezzlement, skimmed accounts, the works. But it wasn't me, Ada. I signed things I shouldn't have signed, I looked away from things I should have burned down, but I didn't take the money."

"And Marsh knew who did."

"He knew enough. He'd seen Theo in the office at hours Theo had no business being there. He'd found paperwork. He was nineteen and scared and the prosecutor's office got hold of him and — you know how that machine works. They promised him immunity, protection, that he wouldn't be dragged in. They explained, very patiently, which parts of what he knew would be helpful to say." My father's voice stayed level, but I could hear the effort holding it level, like a hand pressing down a lid. "And I begged him. Did you know that? I begged him. Not to lie — God, no. I begged him to tell the whole truth. To say the names. I sat with him in a hallway outside the courtroom and I said, *Elian, son, you know it wasn't me. Say the rest.*"

"What did he say?"

"He cried." My father said it simply, without cruelty. "Nineteen years old, shaking like a leaf, and he cried and he said, *Mr. Sterling, if I say the names, they'll charge me too. They told me. I can't. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.* And then he went in and testified, and he told the truth, the portion of the truth he was permitted to survive, and the jury heard a young man with honest eyes describe documents with my signature on them, and that was that." He exhaled slowly. "Forty-one months became eight years became — well. Here we are."

The visiting room hummed around us. Somewhere a toddler was negotiating loudly for another juice box. I sat very still, because the ground under my rage had begun, unmistakably, to shift.

"He was nineteen," I said finally. "Dad, he was a kid. They threatened him."

"They did." My father nodded. "And here's the thing I need you to understand, because I suspect you came here ready for a fight and I'd rather you leave with something else." He leaned forward slightly. "Your mother needs him to be a monster. A monster keeps the story simple. If it's a monster, then it's not bad luck, or a cowardly uncle, or a system that grinds up frightened teenagers and calls it justice. A monster means somebody's to blame, and blame is a place to live. Your mother has lived there a long time. It's warm there. I understand why she stays."

"And you don't live there."

"I tried. First year, maybe two. Rage is exhausting, sweetheart, and prison gives you nothing but time to be exhausted in." He smiled, rueful. "So I got quieter. And somewhere in the quiet, I started thinking about that boy in the hallway. How his hands shook. How he said *I'm sorry* like it was costing him blood." He paused. "Do you want to know something else? Something I've never told either of you, because your mother would have found a way to return the money out of spite, and I couldn't bear that?"

My mouth had gone dry. "Tell me."

"Every December, since my second year inside, money arrives. Cashier's checks, no name, sent through a law firm that won't disclose the client. Enough to cover my commissary, my phone time, Christmas presents for you — real ones, good ones, the years you still believed they were from Mom's bonus." He watched me carefully. "The handwriting on those envelopes, Ada. I've had ten years to study it. Neat. Careful. A draftsman's hand. And every single check has a note in the memo line, the same note, every year: *For the family. With respect.*"

The room tilted, gently, like a boat.

Elian Marsh. Who mended my fence without being asked. Who learned my coffee order by watching through a window. Who wrote me letters for ten years — letters that held me together through the nights my mother's grief became my inheritance — signing them with nothing, explaining nothing, asking for nothing.

*With respect,* he'd written to my father. For ten years. While dying, though I hadn't known that yet, while carrying a guilt he never once tried to unload on any of us, while choosing penance over pardon.

"That's him," I said. My voice came out strange. "Isn't it. The checks."

"I believe so, yes."

"Why didn't you tell me?" And there it was, the real question, the one from the bus, and it cracked open something in my chest on the way out. "Ten years, Dad. Ten years of Mom teaching me to hate him, and you knew — you suspected — and you let me—"

"Let you what?" he asked, gently. "Hate a man who was paying for my Christmas presents and writing you anonymous letters to keep you upright? Ada. What would the truth have changed, back then? You were a child. Then you were a young woman building a life out of other people's happy days because you couldn't trust your own family's story. If I'd handed you his name, you'd have had to choose between your mother and the truth, and no child should make that choice." He reached across the table, and I let him take my hand. "Besides. I wasn't sure. Not until recently. A man doesn't get to accuse on a hunch. Not in this family. We've seen what testimony does."

I laughed — a wet, broken sound that surprised us both.

"There she is," my father said softly. "Your mother's fire and my terrible jokes. You're the best of both of us, you know. Don't let her convince you otherwise."

"Dad." I pressed my free hand flat against my eyes. "He's sick. Elian. His heart. It's bad — I don't know how bad, he won't say, but I found a folder, and there was a letter that started *if you're reading this, I ran out of time*, and Dad, I've been so angry at him that I forgot to be terrified, and now I don't know which one I am."

My father was quiet for a moment. When I lowered my hand, his eyes were bright.

"The testimony," he said. "The part he withheld. Do you think it's still — could it still matter? After all this time?"

"It could reopen the case. Lawyers I've talked to think it might. But it'll take months, Dad. Hearings, filings, and he doesn't have months. Maybe not even weeks. And every hour I spend fighting the courts is an hour I'm not—" I stopped. My throat closed.

"An hour you're not spending with him," my father finished.

I nodded.

He sat back. He looked out at the birches for a long time, and when he spoke, his voice was quieter than I'd ever heard it — quieter than the courtroom, quieter than the verdict, quieter than all the birthdays and Christmases and visiting-room Sundays.

"When your mother and I were married, thirty-one years ago, I stood in front of everyone we loved and I promised her things. Forever things. And I meant them — I want you to know I meant every word. But forever isn't a length of time, Ada. Nobody gets to promise that. Forever is a direction. You promise the direction, and then you keep walking toward it for as long as you're given." He turned back to me. "If this man gave you ten years of letters without asking for a single thing in return — if he carried my name like a stone for a decade and paid for my daughter's Christmas presents in secret — then whatever time he has left, it isn't yours to spend on paperwork. It's his. Spend it with him. The case will wait. The dead don't need hurry. Only the living do."

"Dad, you could come home. If the case reopens—"

"If." He smiled, and it was the saddest, kindest smile I'd ever seen. "Then I'll come home to a daughter who chose love over vengeance, and I will consider these ten years more than paid for. Ada — listen to me. Your mother taught you that justice is a debt someone owes you. It's not. Justice is a thing you build, brick by brick, and forgiveness is the mortar. I forgave that boy in the hallway the day he cried. It took your mother ten years and counting. Take however long you need. But take it *with* him, if he'll let you. Time spent alone is just time. Time spent together—" he squeezed my hand, "—that's the only wealth I've missed in here."

A guard glanced our way; visiting hours were winding down. Around us, families gathered coats and juice boxes and unfinished conversations.

I stood, and then — because I am my father's daughter, and because some rituals deserve to be broken open rather than abandoned — I leaned across the bolted-down table and hugged him, hard, breathing in the institutional soap smell of him, memorizing it.

"Fight for the case," he murmured into my hair. "But live for the man. In that order, sweetheart. Always in that order."

* * *

On the bus home, the sun came out low and gold through the birches, and I didn't rehearse anything. I just sat with my forehead against the cold window and let the ground finish shifting beneath me.

My mother's version had a villain. My father's version had a frightened boy, a crying apology, ten years of cashier's checks marked *with respect*. Two truths, and I had built my whole rage on the smaller one.

By the time the city lights rose out of the dusk, I knew what I had to do. Two things, in his order.

First, the lawyers. The folder. The motion.

And then — tonight, before I lost my nerve, before the fear dressed itself up as anger again — I was going to knock on Elian Marsh's door, folder in hand, and ask him to help me finish the truth.

I just prayed I wasn't already too late.
