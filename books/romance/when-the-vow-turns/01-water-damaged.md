## 1. Water-Damaged

The storm came in off the sound the way storms always did in October — sideways, furious, and without apology. Mirabel Eriksen stood at the front window of Eriksen & Daughters Books and watched the harbor disappear into a gray smear, the ferry dock lights blinking like something drowning.

"Mom." Wren appeared at her elbow, twelve years old and unimpressed by weather, phone glowing in her hand. "School's saying early dismissal tomorrow. Maybe."

"It's Sunday, Wren."

"Maybe Monday, then." She popped a piece of gum. "The basement's making that noise again."

Mirabel's stomach dropped before her mind caught up. That noise. The wet, rhythmic slap of water finding its way where it shouldn't be.

She took the stairs behind the poetry section two at a time, Wren's footsteps thundering after her, and threw open the basement door to the smell of it — cold brine and old paper, the two smells that had defined every bad day of her adult life. Water was sheeting across the concrete floor in a thin, glittering skin, maybe an inch of it, coming in under the door seam from the flooded alley drain. Her mother's stockroom. Forty years of inventory, of first editions wrapped in brown paper, of the signed Ursula K. Le Guin her mother had traded a month of rent for in 1989.

"No," Mirabel said, to no one, to the storm, to God if He was taking requests. "No, no, no."

They worked for three hours. Wren ferried towels and plastic bins while Mirabel waded through the dark with a headlamp strapped over her hair, hauling boxes onto the worktable, peeling back sodden cardboard, laying ruined paperbacks spine-up on every flat surface like casualties. The shop's ancient dehumidifier groaned in the corner, doing what it could. By nine o'clock the water had stopped rising, and by ten it had stopped mattering; they'd saved what could be saved, and the rest would mold or wouldn't.

Wren fell asleep on the couch upstairs at some point, still in her rain boots, one arm hanging off the cushion. Mirabel covered her with the blue quilt and went back down alone, because there was one box left, wedged behind the furnace in the corner her mother had always called *the deep archive* — which meant, in practice, the place where things went to be forgotten.

It was heavier than it should have been. Saturated. When she lifted it, the bottom sagged and gave, and everything inside slid out across the wet floor with a soft, terrible whisper.

Books, mostly. Water-swollen, their covers fanned open like birds shot mid-flight. A tin of her grandmother's buttons, rusted shut. And beneath all of it, flattened against the concrete, an envelope.

She almost didn't pick it up. It looked like trash, like flood debris, like one more thing October had taken. But the corner of it had caught on a nail head, and when she tugged it free and turned it over in the cone of her headlamp's light, she stopped breathing.

*Mirabel Eriksen.*

Her name, in handwriting she would have known blindfolded, underwater, at the bottom of the sea. The tall, impatient capitals. The way he crossed his T's like he was striking something through. Jonah Vale had written her name perhaps ten thousand times in the years they'd been together — on passed notes in AP English, on the birthday card he'd made her at seventeen because neither of them had money, on the lease application they'd filled out together the spring he left, both of them laughing about how they'd furnish a place they'd never even seen.

She hadn't seen that handwriting in eleven years.

The postmark read: **JUN 14** — and below it, faded but legible, the year. Eleven years ago. Three weeks after he'd boarded the ferry to the mainland and then a plane to Singapore, three weeks after the last phone call that had lasted four minutes and ended with him saying *I'll figure this out, Bel, I promise,* and then nothing. No letter. No call that mattered. Just silence stretching out until she'd understood it as an answer, and built her whole life on top of it like a house on a fault line.

He had written. In June. Eleven years ago.

And the envelope had never been opened.

Mirabel sat down right there on the wet floor, not caring, her jeans soaking through, and held the letter in both hands the way you hold a bird with a broken wing. The paper was damp at the edges, tide-marked, but intact. Sealed. His mother's address was on the return label — Edith Vale, Gull Lane — which meant Edith had received it, and Edith had—

Had what? Hidden it? Forgotten it?

*Edith,* who used to press Mirabel's hand across the table at the diner and say, *that boy loves you more than he knows how to carry.* Edith, who had cried at the funeral of Mirabel's mother and let Mirabel cry into her cardigan. Edith, who had died in March, alone, while Mirabel had stood at the graveside feeling grief and something angrier underneath it that she hadn't let herself examine.

Her thumb found the sealed flap. The glue had long since given up; it parted at the slightest pressure, a sigh.

She didn't open it.

That was the thing she would remember later — that she had the letter open in her hands, alone, at midnight, with every excuse in the world, and she did not open it. Because opening it meant deciding what it was. If it said *I'm sorry, I met someone, don't wait* — fine, that was the past, that was settled history, she could put it in the fire pit and burn it and go on being the woman she'd spent a decade becoming. But if it said anything else — if it said *come with me* or *wait for me* or *I love you* — then the last eleven years rearranged themselves around a hole she hadn't known was there. Every hard night, every year she'd told herself *he chose the career, people do that, it wasn't anyone's fault* — all of it would crack.

She was thirty-four years old. She had a daughter asleep upstairs who needed her steady. She had a bookshop with a flooded basement and a roof that leaked and a life assembled so carefully, so quietly, that even she sometimes forgot where the seams were.

Curiosity is just fear wearing a nicer coat, her mother used to say. Mirabel had always thought it was a warning against prying. Tonight, holding the letter, she finally understood it the other way around: she was afraid of what she wanted to know.

She put the letter in her apron pocket instead. Dried her hands. Climbed the stairs.

* * *

She should have slept. Instead she sat at the kitchen table with the letter lying in front of her under the overhead light, and she stared at it the way you stare at a locked door when you've lost the key and know, absolutely know, that what's behind it will change the shape of every room in your house.

At 12:40, bare feet padded on the stairs.

"You're still up." Wren, wrapped in the blue quilt like a burrito, hair a dark riot. She squinted at the table. "Is that mail?"

"It's old mail."

"Old mail is making you cry?"

Mirabel touched her own face and found it wet. She hadn't noticed. "Something like that."

Wren came and stood beside her chair, reading upside down the way kids do, shameless. Mirabel watched her daughter's eyes move across the envelope — the name, the postmark — and saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished.

"That's from before I was born," Wren said slowly. "Who's Jonah?"

The name in her daughter's mouth landed somewhere under Mirabel's ribs and stayed there.

"Someone I knew," Mirabel said. "A long time ago."

"Before Dad?" Wren's father had been a gentle, brief chapter — a carpenter from the mainland who'd lasted eighteen months and sent birthday cards now, mostly on time. "Or—"

"Before everything," Mirabel said. "He went away. This letter—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, hated being legible to a twelve-year-old. "This letter got lost. It just showed up tonight."

Wren studied her for a long moment with her father's gray eyes and Mirabel's stubborn jaw, and then she did the most frightening thing imaginable: she reached out and laid one small hand flat on top of the envelope, keeping it closed.

"Do you want to read it or not?" Wren asked. "Like — actually. Not what you're supposed to want."

Outside, the storm was moving off, dragging its rain east across the water. The house ticked and settled. Somewhere down the street, the harbor bell rang once against the wind, and Mirabel thought of the ferry schedule pinned above the register — *last crossing of the season, October 19th* — and of the estate notice she'd skimmed past in last week's paper without letting herself read it twice.

*Estate of Edith Vale. Heir: J. Vale, currently residing abroad.*

"I don't know yet," Mirabel said honestly.

"Well." Wren picked a fleck of lint off the quilt, considering. "You can't leave it in your pocket forever. It'll get soggy. Everything gets soggy here."

Mirabel laughed — a cracked, surprised sound — and pulled her daughter close against her side, the letter between them like a third heartbeat.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow she'd decide whether to open a door that had been shut for eleven years.

But even as she thought it, her eyes went again to the return address — *Gull Lane* — and to the fact that the house on Gull Lane had a new owner now, and that owner, according to the notice in the paper, was due to arrive on the island within the week.

On the ferry.

The last ferry of the season.
