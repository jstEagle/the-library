## 3. A Stranger at Table Nine

The café on Delancey Street was the kind of place Petra went when she couldn't afford to be seen anywhere she was known. It had mismatched chairs, a chalkboard menu with a smudged apostrophe, and coffee that tasted like it had been argued over rather than brewed. She'd claimed the corner table by the window with her laptop open to a blank page — not her blank page, technically. The blank page belonged to a woman named Marguerite Vance, whose memoir of falling in love in Provence Petra had been hired to make sing. Marguerite's voice was warm and unhurried, full of lavender and second chances. Petra's own voice, if anyone had ever asked for it, would have sounded like an overdue notice.

She ordered the cheapest thing on the menu — a filter coffee, $3.50, which she entered into the ledger in her head under *sustenance, unavoidable* — and told herself she'd work until her laptop battery died, then walk home through the rain that hadn't started yet but kept threatening.

That was the plan. That had always been the plan: work until something ran out, then find more work before the running out became visible.

The stranger was at table nine.

Petra noticed him the way you notice weather — peripherally, at first. A person sitting alone with a notebook, hunched over it like it might bolt. But there was something about the quality of his attention that made her look twice. He wasn't scrolling. He wasn't performing solitude for an audience of strangers. He was *drawing*, long deliberate strokes of pencil, and every so often he'd stop, close his eyes, and move his hands in the air as though folding something invisible.

She watched him do this three times before she caught herself staring.

He was maybe her age, hard to say. Dark hair pushed back off his forehead by his own restless hand, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms dusted — actually dusted, she realized, with flour, a fine white ghosting he seemed unaware of or indifferent to. There was a canvas bag slumped against his chair leg with the tired, trusted look of a bag that had been carried to work every day for years. On the table beside his notebook: a coffee untouched long enough to go cold, and a small plate holding what remained of a croissant.

Not just any croissant. Petra had spent three weeks last year ghostwriting a food critic's memoir, and somewhere in those weeks she'd absorbed enough vocabulary to know what she was looking at. The thing on his plate had shatteringly distinct layers, a honey-dark crust, a structure that suggested patience measured in days, not hours. He'd eaten half of it with the absent reverence of someone grading their own exam.

Then he did something strange. He tore off a corner of the croissant, held it up to the window light, and counted — *counted* — the layers visible along the torn edge. His lips moved slightly. Then he wrote something down with the grim satisfaction of a man recording evidence.

Petra looked at her own screen. *Marguerite felt the first warmth of the Provençal sun and knew, finally, that she was home.*

She looked back at table nine.

The man caught her looking.

She expected him to glance away — that was the choreography of cities, the mutual agreement not to see each other. Instead he held her gaze for a moment with an expression of mild, unbothered curiosity, the way you'd look at a bird that had landed on your table. Then he nodded, almost politely, and returned to his diagram.

Petra's heart was doing something unprofessional.

She told herself it was nothing. She was thirty-four years old, she had $212 in her checking account, a mother who called on Sundays with a voice like a drawn breath, and a family reunion eleven days away where she would be displayed, examined, and found wanting. She did not have bandwidth for whatever this flutter was. She opened the document and typed two sentences about lavender.

At table nine, the man turned his notebook around, studied it upside down, frowned, and crossed out half a page.

It shouldn't have mattered. It didn't matter. But there was something in the gesture — the way he destroyed his own work without drama, without self-pity, just *correction* — that snagged on something in Petra. She thought of all the drafts she'd buried over ten years of ghostwriting. Whole selves, erased without ceremony. At least this stranger let himself frown at what he'd made.

The rain started, soft against the window, and the café filled with the smell of wet pavement and cinnamon from the kitchen. Petra drank her bad coffee and wrote nothing for twenty minutes while pretending to write everything.

It was the phone call that broke her.

Her mother, 4:47 p.m., a Tuesday, which meant it wasn't even Sunday — which meant emergency protocol. Petra watched the screen light up with *Mom* and felt the familiar cold slide down her spine, the one that arrived now whenever any family member's name appeared on her phone.

She didn't answer. She never answered anymore; she called back on her own terms, with her script prepared. But the call went to voicemail, and thirty seconds later the voicemail appeared, and Petra — because she was weak, because she was dreading it anyway — played it with one earbud in.

*"Petra, sweetheart, it's Mom. Just confirming you're coming to the reunion, and — well, I may have mentioned to your Aunt Carol that things are going well for you. And she may have asked about... company. And I said — I hope this is okay — I said you've been seeing someone. Someone serious."* A pause, in which Petra could hear her mother's apology being rehearsed and abandoned. *"You don't have to bring him. But Carol is bringing Daniel, and the Hendersons' son just got engaged, and honestly, sweetheart, it would just be easier. Call me back. Love you."*

Petra sat very still.

The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere behind the counter the espresso machine screamed and fell silent. In her chest, the old machinery engaged: shame first, hot and familiar, then the swift cold calculation that always followed it, the part of her that treated catastrophe as a logistics problem.

Eleven days. One weekend, really — Saturday and Sunday at her aunt's house upstate, forty-eight hours of casseroles and appraisal. Her relatives didn't ask what you did; they asked what you *made*, and the answer was always a number. Salary, square footage, carat weight. Petra's number was $212 and a career spent making other people sound like they'd lived.

Options. She ran them like a card sharp.

One: confess. Tell her mother the truth — that she was broke, alone, and professionally anonymous, that the last man she'd dated had left when he'd realized she remembered his stories better than he did because she'd heard so many. This option ended with her mother's face doing the thing it did, the slow collapse from hope to concern to that terrible gentle pity, and with Aunt Carol's kindness afterward, which was worse than cruelty.

Two: fake an illness. Plausible, but her grandmother was ninety-one and this might be her last reunion, and Petra was not a monster, whatever else she was.

Three.

Three was insane.

Three was sitting at table nine, counting croissant layers in the rain.

Petra stared at him and felt the idea arrive whole, uninvited, fully formed — the way her best sentences sometimes came, the ones she could never keep for herself. *Hire someone.* Not an escort, God, no — a partner. A boyfriend-for-hire. Someone presentable, quiet, good with families. Someone who needed money — everyone needed money — and who owed her nothing and would owe her nothing after.

It was a terrible idea. It was fraud-adjacent. It was the kind of thing that happened in the novels she wrote for other people, not in her life, because her life had no room for plot, only for deadlines.

But her mother's voicemail was still warm in her ear, and Aunt Carol's pity was already rehearsing itself, and $212 sat in her account like a dare.

Petra closed her laptop.

Later, she would try to reconstruct why she did what she did next, and she would fail. The honest answer was that she'd been erasing herself for so long that some starved, reckless part of her had taken the wheel, and it drove straight at the only interesting thing in the room.

She picked up her coffee, crossed the café, and stood at table nine.

The man looked up. Up close he was taller than he'd seemed, with ink on the side of his right hand and a small burn scar on his forearm, the kind bakers collected like stamps in a passport. His eyes were dark and level and entirely without suspicion, which somehow made this harder.

"Hi," Petra said. "I'm sorry to interrupt. Can I buy you another coffee? Yours has been dead for an hour."

He glanced at the cup, then at her, and something dry moved at the corner of his mouth. "It's intentional. Cold brew, sort of. By neglect."

"It's a technique."

"It's poverty and poor planning." He said it easily, without embarrassment, and gestured at the chair across from him. "You can sit. You were going to anyway. You've been working up to it for twenty minutes."

Heat climbed Petra's neck. "I have not."

"You checked my croissant three times. I could feel it. Bakers can feel it when people judge their lamination." He turned his notebook toward her briefly — a precise, obsessive diagram of folded dough, layers numbered in tiny handwriting, arrows annotating butter temperature. "Seventy-two layers. The book says sixty-four is standard. I'm trying to beat my father's record."

Something in his voice when he said *my father* — a door closing gently on a lit room — made Petra want to ask. She didn't. She had a script. She was terrified of her script.

"I'm going to say something strange," she said, sitting down. "And I want you to hear the whole thing before you react, because I promise it sounds worse at the start than it does at the end."

His eyebrows rose a fraction. "Now I'm listening."

"My name is Petra." She didn't offer a last name; hers belonged to other people's books. "I'm a writer. And in eleven days I have to attend a family reunion where I will be measured, weighed, and found insufficient, largely because I'm single and poor, in that order of importance to my relatives. My mother has already told my entire extended family I'm seeing someone serious." She took a breath. "I need to produce that someone. For one weekend. Two days. And I am prepared to pay for the service."

The silence lasted four seconds. She counted.

"You're asking me," he said slowly, "to pretend to date you."

"For one weekend. In front of my family. Nothing physical beyond what's publicly reasonable — hand-holding, maybe an arm around shoulders. You'd have a cover story we build together, so nothing you say will be improvised. I'll brief you thoroughly. And I'll pay you —" her mind raced through the arithmetic of ruin — "four hundred dollars. Plus travel, plus meals, plus lodging; my aunt has a guest room, separate rooms, obviously."

"That's more than I make in a week."

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a *concern*." He leaned back, arms crossing, and studied her with an expression she couldn't parse — not offense, not amusement exactly. Something more careful. "Most people lead with hello. You led with a business proposal and a confession of fraud."

"I led with the coffee offer."

"The coffee offer was reconnaissance."

Petra laughed — a short, startled sound that escaped before she could vet it. She couldn't remember the last time she'd laughed in public without a reason she'd manufactured. "Okay. Yes. Maybe. Are you offended?"

"I'm deciding." He turned his cold coffee slowly on its saucer, watching it like it might tell him something. "Here's my problem. I've done desperate things for money recently. I know what they cost. So I want to understand what this costs *you*. Because a woman who pays a stranger to love her in front of her family isn't short a boyfriend. She's short something else."

The observation landed with uncomfortable precision, and Petra felt it lodge under her ribs like a splinter. She reached for deflection — her native language — and found, annoyingly, that she didn't want to use it on him.

"I'm short credibility," she said. "My family thinks I'm a success story that hasn't happened yet. I write books — real books, bestsellers — but none of them have my name on them. When I tell people that, they get a look. Pity dressed up as respect. I'd rather they meet a fiction that works than a truth that doesn't."

"And after the weekend?"

"After the weekend we never speak again. You take your four hundred dollars, I take my dignity, and everyone goes home believing a harmless lie."

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, blurring the street into watercolor. He looked at his diagram — seventy-two layers, his father's record — and Petra watched him weigh something she couldn't see.

"Here's my counteroffer," he said finally. "I'll do it. But not for four hundred."

Petra's stomach dropped. Of course. He'd smelled desperation; they always did. She braced for a number that would send her into actual debt, actual ruin —

"Five hundred," he said, "and you have dinner with me first."

She blinked. "What?"

"Not a date." He held up a hand, quick, almost flustered — and there, suddenly, was the person under the composed surface, someone younger and less certain than the flour-dusted professional at table nine. "A rehearsal. If we're going to sell this, we can't be strangers wearing a story. We need history. Real details — how we met, what I call you when you're annoying, why you put up with me. Lies need anchors, or they sink in the first five minutes with your Aunt Whoever."

"Carol."

"Lies need anchors or they sink with Aunt Carol." He shrugged, and the corner of his mouth did the dry thing again. "So. Dinner. We build the anchor together. You tell me true things — boring ones, the real texture, your mother's name, what your childhood kitchen smelled like — and I'll do the same. And then, if we both still want the money, we do your reunion."

Petra stared at him. It was, infuriatingly, a better contract than hers. He'd understood in five minutes what took her a decade to learn as a writer: the lie is only as strong as the true things holding it up.

"One condition," she said. "Dinner's on me. Five hundred total, all-inclusive. I'm not paying for the privilege of my own fraud."

"Deal." He extended his hand across the table — flour-dusted, warm, a baker's grip, firm and brief. "Wren."

"Wren," she repeated. "Like the bird."

"Like the bird." Something flickered behind his eyes at that, some private weather, gone before she could read it. "Thursday. Seven o'clock. There's a noodle place two blocks from here where nobody will bother us and the tables are small enough that we'll have to lean in to hear each other. Couples lean in."

"Couples lean in," Petra agreed, and heard her own voice come out softer than intended.

He gathered his notebook, his dead coffee, his flour-ghosted bag. Standing, he paused, and looked down at her with an expression she'd spend the next several chapters failing to categorize.

"For what it's worth," Wren said, "you're a much better liar than you think. You lied beautifully just now."

"I didn't lie."

"No?" He smiled — the first full one, and it rearranged his whole face into something warmer and more dangerous. "You said 'everyone goes home believing a harmless lie.' Nobody who believed that would be sitting here."

He walked out into the rain without an umbrella, unbothered, and Petra sat alone at table nine with her heart hammering out a rhythm she refused to transcribe.

$212 in the bank. Five hundred promised. The math was insane. The plan was insane. She had just hired a stranger whose last name she didn't know, whose hands knew secrets about butter, whose eyes had seen directly through her on the first pass.

She opened her laptop. The cursor blinked on Marguerite's blank page, patient as a witness.

Petra typed: *She should have known better. She did it anyway.*

And for the first time in months, the words felt like hers.
