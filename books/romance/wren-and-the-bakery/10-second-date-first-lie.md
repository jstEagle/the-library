## 10. Second Date, First Lie

The text arrived at 6:47 on a Tuesday evening, while Petra was standing in her kitchen eating cold risotto directly from the container, and it said:

*You owe me a dinner. A real one. No invoices, no rehearsal, no agenda. Friday, seven o'clock, I pick the place. Wear something you didn't buy for the reunion.*

She read it three times. The risotto went tasteless in her mouth.

This was the problem with the weekend having worked too well. This was the problem with the porch, with the hand-holding, with the way Wren had looked at her when the porch light buzzed on and moths began their small suicides against the bulb — that look, unhurried and unguarded, like Petra was a recipe worth writing down.

She typed *I'd like that* before her better judgment could intervene, which was the whole trouble with her better judgment lately: it kept arriving late, like a guest to a party already ruined.

Then she sat down at her kitchen table, opened the folder she'd sworn she wouldn't open again until she absolutely had to, and looked at the name.

*Wren Ashgrove.*

The Marchand file lay there like something dead on a road. She'd gone back to Theo's interview transcripts on Monday — professional habit, she'd told herself, just keeping the material fresh — and the name had been waiting for her the way it was always going to be waiting, patient as a trap. She had read the relevant passage four times, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something else. They hadn't.

*"My sibling,"* Theo had said, in that warm radio voice of his, the one that made millions of strangers feel personally known, *"sued me over the bakery. Our father's bakery. The place I built into what it is. They wanted half of something they abandoned, and when the courts said no, they walked away and never looked back. I don't talk about it. But it's the truth of the book, Petra. It has to be the truth of the book."*

Wren had sued no one. Petra was almost sure of that. Wren, who rehearsed lies so carefully because lies needed anchors, who had refused to invent a fake anniversary without first finding the real date Petra's parents had actually married — that person did not file frivolous lawsuits. Which meant either the lawsuit was real and justified, or it wasn't a lawsuit at all but something Theo had dressed up for the memoir, and either way, Wren had been on the losing end of it. Wren had lost the bakery. Wren had lost the name on the window. And Petra had been hired, with money she badly needed, to write the version of that loss that made Theo the hero.

She closed the folder. She opened it again. She closed it.

Friday was three days away. She had three days to decide what kind of person she was going to be at dinner, and three days was not nearly enough, and she accepted the invitation anyway.

* * *

She broke her own rule about the outfit, of course. She bought something new — a deep green dress she found in a shop she normally walked past, the kind of purchase that used to feel like a luxury and now felt like a small act of fraud against her own bank account. She stood in front of the mirror Thursday night and told herself it wasn't for Wren. It was for her. It was for the version of herself that got asked to dinner by someone who meant it.

The lie was so smooth she almost believed it, which was, she was beginning to understand, her defining talent.

Friday, seven o'clock. Wren was waiting outside a small restaurant in a neighborhood Petra didn't know well — a place with no sign, just a door and a window fogged with steam, and when they stepped inside, the smell hit her like a memory she'd never had: brown butter, rosemary, bread.

"You picked a restaurant that smells like a bakery," she said.

"I picked a restaurant that smells like *my* bakery," Wren said. "Or what I want it to smell like, someday. The chef here trained under someone I admire. I've been coming for a year. They know me."

"They know you," Petra repeated, and felt the strange vertigo of realizing how little she knew, how much of Wren was still unmapped — and how she now had a professional obligation to map it, and a personal terror of what the map would show.

They sat at a corner table. Wren ordered for both of them without asking, confidently, and Petra found she liked it — liked being chosen for, liked surrendering a decision she was tired of making. The first course arrived and it was so good she made an involuntary sound, and Wren laughed, a real laugh, unperformed, and Petra thought: *I am in danger. I have been in danger since the porch.*

"So," Wren said, tearing a piece of bread with the efficiency of someone who'd done it ten thousand times. "Tell me something true. Since we're not being paid to lie tonight, I want to hear what you sound like without a script."

"That's a terrible question."

"It's the only question."

Petra turned her water glass. "I ate cold risotto out of the container when you texted me."

"That's not true, that's a confession."

"Confessions are true things."

"Fine. Your turn. A real one."

She should have said something safe. She should have said something charming and hollow, the kind of thing she fed interview subjects to loosen them up. Instead she heard herself say: "I haven't written anything under my own name in six years. I've written eleven books. Bestselling ones. And my name isn't on any of them."

Wren went still, the way they went still when they were really listening — total, undivided, almost unnerving. "Eleven books."

"Twelve, if you count the one that got shelved."

"What was it about?"

"A woman who bakes bread for a man who's dying, and the bread is the only honest conversation they ever have." She heard it come out of her and winced — *the bread, the dying, the honesty* — everything in her life circling back to flour like a moth to a lit bulb. "It was too quiet. Nobody wanted it."

"I would have wanted it," Wren said simply, and Petra had to look down at her plate, because that sentence landed somewhere no sentence had landed in years.

* * *

Here is what Petra discovered over the next hour: espionage and intimacy use the same muscles.

When Wren talked, she leaned in — and leaning in was both tenderness and technique. When Wren mentioned the past, she asked gentle follow-up questions — and gentle follow-up questions were both care and extraction. *Tell me about where you grew up.* *What was your father like?* *Do you still talk to your family?* Each question was a coin with two faces, and she spent them one after another, and Wren paid her in trust, and every coin spent made her feel richer and more like a thief.

"Your turn," Wren said, somewhere past the second course. "You've been interviewing me all night. Very smoothly. I almost didn't notice."

The word *interviewing* went through her like a needle finding a nerve.

"I'm a ghostwriter," Petra said. "Interviewing is how I breathe."

"I know. But this isn't work." Wren's eyes held hers, warm and steady and — she would remember this later, would replay it at 3 a.m. with the fidelity of a nightmare — *searching*. "Is it?"

"No," she said. "It isn't work."

That much, at least, was true. That was the cruelty of it: the true things and the false things had become so braided she could no longer tell which was which. She wasn't working. She was falling. And she was also, in the same motion, gathering material for a manuscript that would take everything Wren had left and hand it to the person who'd taken it.

"Your family," Wren said. "The reunion people. Tell me about your mother."

And Petra — because Wren had asked, because the candlelight made honesty feel cheap, because she was tired of being a locked room — told her. About a mother who measured love in casseroles and disappointment in silence. About the reunion, and the measuring, and how she had hired a stranger because the truth of her life was a savings account at zero and a byline that belonged to no one. She watched Wren listen, and she thought: *this is the most honest I have been with anyone in a decade, and I am doing it while concealing the single most important thing.*

"You could have just shown up alone," Wren said quietly.

"And been pitied by my Aunt Carol? I'd rather be set on fire."

"But you weren't pitied. You were *believed*. That's different." Wren turned the wine glass slowly. "Being believed by people who are supposed to love you — that's a strange drug. I know something about not being believed."

Petra's heart performed a small, traitorous leap. *Ask. Ask now. What do you mean, not believed? Who didn't believe you?* The interviewer in her reached for the question like a hand reaching for a knife.

She put her hand on the table instead, palm up, an offering or a surrender, and Wren covered it with their own.

"Tell me someday," she said. "When you want to. Not because I asked."

The look Wren gave her then — startled, moved, like she'd handed them something breakable and they hadn't expected her to know how — that look was going to be the thing that undid her. She could feel it. It was already undoing her.

* * *

They walked afterward, because neither of them wanted the night to end, through streets gone soft with lamplight, past shuttered shops and one bakery with its lights still on, a lone baker visible through the glass, shaping dough with the patience of someone who loved the dough more than sleep.

Wren stopped at the window.

"I used to do that," they said. "The night shift. My father believed dough proofed better when someone was nearby. Not touching it. Just *there*." A small smile, aimed at the glass. "He said bread could tell when you'd abandoned it."

"Was he right?"

"Doesn't matter. I still believe it." Wren's breath fogged the window. "That's the thing about what you learn in a bakery. It's not technique. Technique you can get anywhere. It's the *believing*. And you can't unlearn it, even when the bakery's gone."

*The bakery's gone.* Petra stood very still, watching Wren's reflection in the dark glass, and the guilt rose in her like floodwater — not a pang but a tide, cold and total. Somewhere in her bag, metaphorically, was a manuscript outline with a chapter titled *The Lawsuit* and a sibling painted as a grasping stranger. Somewhere in her future was Theo Marchand's warm voice on the phone saying *the whole ugly truth, Petra, that's what I'm paying for.* And here was the ugly truth, standing at a bakery window, fogging the glass with breath, believing in bread.

"You went quiet," Wren said.

"I'm listening."

"You went quiet like a writer. I've seen it. You go somewhere else and take notes."

The accuracy of it was a slap. "I'm not—"

"You are. It's fine." Wren turned to face her, and the streetlight caught the edge of their jaw, the tired kindness of their eyes. "It's what you do. I just want to know — when you're taking notes on me — which book are they for?"

And there it was. The question, arriving without warning, without armor, standing in the middle of the street with its hand out.

Petra opened her mouth. Closed it. The lie she needed was right there — *no book, no notes, just you* — and it would have been easy, she was the best in the business, she could have made Wren believe it the way Aunt Carol had believed in the anniversary, the way the whole Delacroix family had believed in the matching sweaters.

But Wren had taught her something at that flour-dusted counter, something she'd thought was a lesson about lies and had actually been a lesson about herself: *lies need anchors.* And she was so tired of building. So tired of anchoring falsehoods in true details until the true details drowned.

"Yours," she said. "The notes are for a book about you. That I'll never write. Because some things should stay off the record."

It was not the whole truth. It was a truth with the center cut out of it, a truth shaped like a donut hole. But Wren smiled — slow, real, the porch-light smile — and took her hand again, and they walked on, and Petra catalogued, with the precision she hated herself for, that Wren's father had run a bakery, that Wren had worked nights, that Wren had been *abandoned* by a bakery and still believed in bread.

She got home at midnight. She did not open the folder.

She opened it at 12:40, because she was weak, because the deadline was a wire tightening around her throat, because Theo had left a voicemail that afternoon — *just checking in, Petra, excited for this one, the sibling chapter is the heart of the book* — and she sat at her kitchen table in the green dress she'd sworn was for herself and read the transcript again, and this time she underlined things. Dates. Amounts. The name of the lawyer. The phrase Theo had used twice, in two interviews, word for word, which no innocent man did: *"they walked away."*

People who walked away, she thought, did not stand at bakery windows at midnight, missing them.

Her phone buzzed. Wren.

*Had a good time. Really good. Same time next week? There's a place that does a kouign-amann that will make you emotional.*

She stared at it for a long time. Then she typed *yes*, and meant it, and hated that she meant it, and meant it anyway.

Then, because the night demanded one more cruelty, her email chimed. Theo Marchand. Subject line: *Quick thought.*

*Petra — had an idea for the sibling chapter. What if you talked to them directly? A conversation, on the record. It would be so much stronger with their voice in it. I'll make an introduction. — T*

She read it twice, and the risotto she hadn't finished at 6:47 came back up her throat as pure cold dread.

Because she already knew Wren's voice. She'd spent all evening learning it. And Theo Marchand had just, with one generous, self-serving email, scheduled a collision between the two halves of her life — the woman at the bakery window and the man with the contract — and put her name on the invitation.
