## 12. Breadcrumbs

The first thing Wren noticed was the way Petra asked about the ovens.

Not the way people usually asked — the polite curiosity of someone making conversation at a counter, *is it true you have to wake up at four?* — but with a specificity that sat wrong in Wren's chest like a swallowed stone. Petra wanted to know what year the second deck oven had been installed. She wanted to know whether Theo had kept their father's old proofing cabinet or sold it. She asked about the lawsuit the way a person asks about weather in a town they're planning to move to: lightly, but with an itinerary already written.

They were sitting in the corner booth of the Bluebird Diner on a Tuesday night, two weeks after the reunion, three weeks after a tart had disarmed an aunt and a porch had undone them both. Petra had her notebook out — she always had her notebook out, and Wren had found that endearing once, the way she captured everything, even the way steam curled off a diner coffee cup, as if the world were a draft she couldn't stop revising.

"So the original shop," Petra said, pen hovering. "On Delancey. When did you last go inside?"

Wren turned their coffee mug a slow quarter-turn on its saucer. "Why do you keep asking me that?"

"Keep asking you what?"

"The Delancey questions." Wren kept their voice level, the way they'd learned to keep their hands level when piping a border that had to be perfect, because trembling showed. "You've asked about the lease, the equipment, the name on the deed, and now the building itself. That's not curiosity, Petra. That's research."

Petra's pen stopped moving.

For a moment the diner noise filled the space between them — the hiss of the griddle, a waitress calling *two over easy*, the door chime letting in October air. Wren watched Petra's face do something complicated, a series of small doors opening and closing behind her eyes, and hated how beautiful it still was, how much they still wanted to believe whatever came through those doors when they opened.

"I'm a writer," Petra said finally. "I ask questions. It's a disease."

"You write other people's stories," Wren said. "You told me that. You said you erase yourself from every book." They leaned back against the vinyl booth, arms crossed, and heard their own voice come out quieter than intended. "So whose story are these questions for?"

* * *

Wren hadn't always been suspicious by nature. Their father had been the suspicious one — checking the locks twice, reading contracts aloud at the kitchen table with a pencil in his teeth, saying *trust is earned in flour, not words*. Wren had inherited the flour and none of the suspicion. It was Theo who'd gotten the caution, and look where that had landed them both.

But suspicion, once planted, grew fast in Wren's soil. And Petra had been planting things for weeks without knowing it.

There was the night she'd asked, casually, over takeout containers, *what did your father actually promise Theo? Was anything in writing?* There was the morning she'd looked at the lamination diagram taped to Wren's fridge — the one from the café, the day they met — and said, *this is the same method Marchand uses in his cookbook, isn't it? The one he published under just his name.* There was the fact that she never wrote anything down when Wren talked about the bakery. Not one word. A writer, leaving a blank page, and Wren had thought it was tenderness. Restraint. *She doesn't want to make a story out of my grief.*

Now they wondered if she simply didn't need to write it down. Now they wondered if she remembered everything because remembering was her profession.

After the diner, Wren walked home the long way, past the dark windows of boutiques, and let the cold air do what cold air does to a fever. Because that was the other thing — the thing that made this worse than simple doubt. Wren didn't want to be right. Being right about Theo had cost them a family, a home, a name on a window. If they were right about Petra too, then the weekend at the reunion — the matching sweaters, the invented anniversary, the hand-holding they'd both forgotten to stop — all of it collapsed into performance, and Wren would be left standing in the wreckage of a second betrayal by a second person who'd smiled at them across a table while measuring the exits.

*Don't,* they told themselves, climbing the stairs to their apartment. *You're doing the thing where you burn the bread before you've checked if it's done.*

But their hands were already shaking, and shaking hands don't lie.

* * *

It was the coat that undid everything.

Petra had started leaving things at Wren's apartment — gradually, the way water finds a crack. A toothbrush first, presented with elaborate casualness. Then a stack of novels she claimed were "lending, not abandoning." Then, on Thursday nights that had become most nights, herself: asleep on Wren's couch by eleven, up and gone by seven, leaving the apartment smelling faintly of her perfume and something like guilt.

On Friday morning, needing a scarf against the cold, Wren reached into the closet where Petra's wool coat hung, and the pocket gave them a page.

Not a page of fiction. Wren knew fiction; they'd read Petra's ghostwritten romances before learning they were hers, had loved them without knowing why they felt so honest. This was different. This was typewritten, single-spaced, with handwritten corrections in the margins — Petra's handwriting, which Wren knew intimately now, had watched loop across grocery lists and napkin sketches. The header read: **MARCHAND MEMOIR — CH. 4 DRAFT — DO NOT CIRCULATE**.

Wren stood in the hallway with the coat still half off its hanger and read.

*Theo Marchand built the bakery on Delancey Street from nothing — or so the story goes. The truth is more complicated, and more painful. In 2009, following the death of founder Alain Ashgrove, a dispute erupted between Marchand and his step-sibling over ownership of the shop their father left behind. The sibling — whose identity we have agreed to protect — claims Alain promised them the business. Marchand maintains the promise was never made, or was made to a child too young to understand what was being promised.*

*What is not in dispute is this: within six months of Alain's funeral, the locks had been changed.*

The page went soft in Wren's grip. They made themselves sit down on the hallway floor, coat pooled around them like shed skin, and read it again. And again. The words didn't change. The words never change; that was the whole cruelty of documents, of contracts, of the letter their father's lawyer had sent that Theo had answered with a lawyer of his own instead of a phone call.

*Whose identity we have agreed to protect.*

Agreed. We. As in, agreed with Theo. As in, a deal struck in some office Wren would never see, about a life Wren had actually lived, priced and packaged and filed under *protect* — as if Wren were a liability, a scandal to be managed, a wound Theo could finally close by paying someone to narrate it shut.

And Petra had known. Petra had sat across from them at the Bluebird asking about the proofing cabinet, about the locks, about the last time they'd stood inside the shop — asking, Wren realized now, not because she didn't know the answers, but because she needed them confirmed. Verified. Sourced. Every question had been a check against her draft, and Wren had answered each one honestly, generously, handing her the bricks and watching her build the wall around them.

The anniversary they'd invented at the reunion. The sweaters. The tart that had charmed Aunt Renata. Had any of it been real? Or had it all been rehearsal — a ghostwriter practicing intimacy the way she practiced voice, until the client's estranged sibling could be handled correctly?

Wren put the page back exactly where they'd found it, folded along its existing crease, deep in the left pocket. Then they took down their own coat, wrapped the scarf around their throat, and went to work, because dough waits for no one and grief, unlike dough, cannot be punched down and left to rise again.

They burned the first two batches of croissants. Nobody at the café mentioned it. Chefs can always tell.

* * *

That night, Wren didn't confront her. That surprised them most of all — that they had it in them to wait. But confrontation required a decision about what happened after, and Wren wasn't ready to decide. So they did what bakers do with a suspect batch: they set it aside, marked it, and watched.

Friday night, Petra arrived with wine and takeout and a kiss that landed warm on Wren's cheek, and Wren kissed her back and catalogued everything. The way her eyes went to the lamination diagram on the fridge and away again, quickly, like touching a bruise. The way she asked, over noodles, "Did I ever tell you my mother thinks you're the best thing that's happened to me in years?" — deflection dressed as confession. The way, when Wren mentioned in passing that they'd driven past the old shop on Delancey that afternoon, Petra's chopsticks paused for exactly one and a half seconds before resuming. Wren counted. Counting was easy. Counting was what you did instead of screaming.

"It's smaller than I remember," Wren said, watching her face. "The shop. Or maybe I got bigger."

"That happens," Petra said softly. "Places shrink when we grow." And there it was — the writer's instinct, the aphorism, the line that belonged in a manuscript more than a conversation. Wren smiled and passed the dumplings and inside their chest something cracked another degree, quietly, the way ice cracks on a pond before anyone falls through.

Later, in the dark, Petra asleep against their shoulder, Wren lay awake doing arithmetic. The reunion had been in September. The memoir chapter was dated — Wren had seen the date in the margin, small and damning — August. Before the café. Before Table Nine. Before the offer of money for a weekend of pretending.

Which meant Petra had been writing Theo Marchand's story before she'd ever met Wren Ashgrove.

Which meant either the universe was crueler than Wren had budgeted for, or — and this possibility was worse, this possibility kept them staring at the ceiling until three a.m. — Petra had known exactly who Wren was from the start. Had recognized the name, the face, the estranged sibling from Chapter Four, and hired them anyway. Or pursued them anyway. Or let herself be pursued, whichever version let her sleep at night.

*No,* Wren told the ceiling. *No, that's not — she didn't know. She couldn't have.*

But their father's voice, dead eight years and still the sharpest instrument in the room, said: *Read the contract twice, mon petit. People show you who they are in the fine print.*

* * *

Sunday morning, Wren woke alone, which was unusual — Petra usually stayed for breakfast, claimed Sundays were sacred, made eggs badly and coffee well. There was a note on the counter in her looping hand: *Deadline emergency. Back tonight. Eat something. — P*

Underneath the note, where she'd weighted it with the salt dish, was a bakery box from Marchand's flagship store on Fifth. Inside, two perfect croissants, laminated to a standard Wren could neither criticize nor forgive, and a small card: *Thinking of you. Even his croissants are better shared.*

Wren stood in the kitchen holding the box and understood, with terrible clarity, that they were holding the whole problem in miniature. Because the croissants were genuinely excellent. Because the note was genuinely sweet. Because Petra Delacroix was, in every way Wren could touch and taste and measure, genuine — and somewhere in her apartment or her bag or her locked laptop was a file with Wren's family tragedy in it, being polished sentence by sentence into somebody else's redemption arc.

You couldn't return a gift like that. You couldn't un-eat a croissant. You couldn't unknow that the person who'd held your hand on your aunt's porch might have been taking notes.

Wren ate one of the croissants standing at the counter, mechanically, tasting nothing. Then they washed the box clean, dried it, and set it on top of the fridge — next to the tin where their father used to keep the recipe cards, the ones Theo had thrown out with the renovation debris, the ones Wren had spent eight years trying to reconstruct from memory and spite.

And that was when they saw it. Tucked behind the tin, half-forgotten since the day they'd shoved it there: a folded printout Wren had grabbed from the café printer weeks ago by mistake, meaning to toss it. Someone else's pages, spat out after Wren's own. They'd skimmed it, frowned at the unfamiliar subject matter, and forgotten it in the rush of service.

They unfolded it now with fingers that had gone very still.

It was a manuscript page. Chapter Nine, according to the header. And the subject matter was not unfamiliar at all.

*Wren Ashgrove left the city in 2011 and did not speak to Theo Marchand again for eight years. Friends of the family describe the sibling as "the talented one" — the child Alain favored, the one who inherited the gift without inheriting the burden. Whether that inheritance was ever formally promised remains the question at the heart of this book.*

At the bottom of the page, in the footer, the author's initials: **P.D.**

Wren set the paper down on the counter beside the clean bakery box, side by side, evidence arranged the way their father used to arrange mis en place — everything in its place, everything accounted for, nothing hidden.

Then they picked up their phone, found Petra's name — *Petra 🥐*, God help them, they'd chosen that emoji, they'd chosen it laughing — and typed:

*We need to talk. Tonight. Before you come over.*

Three dots appeared immediately. She was awake. She was answering fast, which meant she was either innocent or frightened, and Wren no longer trusted themselves to tell the difference.

*Everything okay?*

Wren looked at the manuscript page. At the box from Marchand's. At the diagram on the fridge from the day a stranger had looked up from sketching croissants and changed their life, or seemed to.

*Just talk,* they typed, and put the phone face-down on the counter, and began, without quite deciding to, to make dough.
