## 17. What Wren Bakes at 3 A.M.

Sleep had become a country Wren could no longer find on the map.

They lay in the dark of their apartment above the closed café where they worked weekends, listening to the radiator tick like a patient clock, and counted the things they'd said to Petra that they couldn't take back. *You were writing his story. You were in my kitchen with your notebook and your soft questions and you were writing his story.* The words kept replaying with different endings, the way dreams do — in one version Petra cried; in another she explained everything; in the best one, which was the cruelest, she simply said *you're right* and handed over the manuscript so Wren could burn it themselves.

The ceiling held no answers. The ceiling never did.

At 2:47 a.m., Wren gave up on sleep the way their father used to give up on weather — not with anger, but with the shrug of a man who knew the sky would do what it wanted. They got up. They put the kettle on because the ritual demanded it, even though they wouldn't drink the tea. And then they did the thing they always did when the night got too loud: they went to bake.

* * *

The café kitchen was small and honest, all stainless steel and scuffed tile, nothing like the cathedral of marble and brass that Marchand et Fils had been at its height. That was part of why Wren loved it. There was nowhere here for ghosts to hide.

Except tonight. Tonight the ghosts came in with them.

It started, as it always started, with the hands. Wren was measuring flour for no particular reason — just wanting the weight of it, the soft resistance against the scoop — when their hands moved without permission. Not toward the croissant dough already proofing in the walk-in. Toward the rye.

Their father's rye.

Pain de Papa, the regulars had called it, half-joking, because Marcel Ashgrove had baked one dark, dense, faintly sweet loaf every morning of his life and refused, absolutely refused, to write the recipe down. "A recipe is a will," he used to say, flour to the elbows, his reading glasses pushed up into his curls — the same curls Theo had, before Theo cut his hair short and started wearing chef's whites with his name embroidered over the heart. "You write it down, you're telling people what they get. You leave it in your hands, you're telling them who you are."

Wren had been twenty-two when the recipe died with him. Twenty-two and standing in this same posture — hands buried to the wrist in flour, throat tight — trying to reconstruct a loaf from memory and muscle and grief. They'd tried eleven times that first year. Twelve. They'd lost count after thirty. Every attempt was close. Every attempt was wrong. Too sour, too sweet, too dense, too airy, crust too thick or too thin, the crumb tight where it should have been open, open where it should have been tight. Their father's rye had a crumb like lace made of night, and no matter what Wren did, theirs came out like brick or like apology.

Theo claimed he'd figured it out. Of course he did. Two years after the funeral, Marchand et Fils — rebranded, renovated, Theo's name suddenly twice the size of anyone else's on the awning — began selling Pain de Marcel, ninety-five percent identical, according to every customer who didn't know better. Theo said he'd reverse-engineered it from memory, the way Wren had. Theo said a lot of things.

Wren had never believed him. But standing here at three in the morning with flour dusting the steel counter like the first snow of a bad winter, Wren realized something that loosened something in their chest:

They'd never believed *themselves*, either. Not really. All these years, all these failed loaves, and somewhere underneath the technique and the stubbornness had run a quieter, meaner thought: *maybe I don't remember right. Maybe Dad's hands live in Theo's memory now, and mine only have the leftovers.*

That was the wound under the lawsuit, wasn't it? Everyone — the lawyers, the mediators, Theo himself, and God, Petra, Petra most of all with her careful questions about *the early days* — everyone assumed the fight was about equity. About the building. About the fourteen percent Theo's lawyers insisted Wren had signed away and Wren's lawyer insisted had been forged or coerced or at minimum extracted from a grieving twenty-three-year-old across a desk from men in expensive suits.

And yes. Fine. It was about those things too. Money is never *not* about money, not entirely, not when you've spent a decade renting rooms above other people's kitchens while your brother's face smiled down from magazine covers beside bread that should have been yours by blood.

But money was the shape of the wound, not the wound.

Wren pulled the starter from the fridge — the rye starter they'd maintained for eleven years now, fed and carried through four apartments like a hostage, like an heirloom, like a heartbeat in a jar. Their father had started it the year Wren was born. Or so the story went. Maybe he'd started it the year the bakery opened and told his children it was as old as they were because that was the kind of man he was: a man who understood that a thing becomes true if you love it long enough.

He'd named the starter Henri. Nobody knew why. He talked to it. He checked it before he checked the weather, before he checked the news, sometimes, Wren suspected, before he checked on his children — no, that wasn't fair, that was the bitterness talking, the 3 a.m. bitterness, which was honest but not accurate. Their father had checked on them. He'd taught them lamination on a marble slab with the windows fogged, their small hands inside his big ones, folding butter into dough, folding patience into a child. He'd let them press their thumbprints into the bottom of every test loaf, hidden signatures, so that "even when you're gone, the bread knows you."

Wren fed Henri. Watched it bubble. Waited.

* * *

By four, the kitchen smelled like beginnings.

Wren worked the way their father had worked, or the way they remembered him working, which at this hour was the same thing. No scale at first — hands first, scale later, measure by feel and correct by science, the old dance. Dark rye flour, bread flour, a whisper of cocoa their father swore was for color and everyone knew was for depth. Molasses, black and slow-pouring, the smell of it hitting the warm water like a memory arriving. Caraway — no. Wren stopped. Put the caraway back.

Waited. Tried again. Yes, caraway, but *toasted*, almost burnt, ground coarse. That was the trick they'd been missing for eleven years. Their father had toasted the caraway until it was one breath from ruined, because that was who he was too — a man who took everything one breath further than safe. The bread, the risks, the loans that eventually sank the original shop. One breath further than safe, every time, and mostly it made him magnificent and once or twice it made him gone.

Wren stood very still in the empty kitchen, holding a spoonful of nearly-burnt caraway, and felt eleven years of failure rearrange itself into instruction.

*You weren't getting it wrong,* they thought. *You were getting it polite.*

* * *

The loaf went into the oven at 4:40. While it baked, Wren sat on an upturned milk crate with tea they finally drank, and let the truth arrive, because it had been circling the whole time and it deserved to land.

The bakery was never about money.

Not the original one, not the lawsuit, not any of it. Oh, Wren had told themselves the money story so many times it had worn smooth as river stone — the inheritance, the shares, the name on the deed, justice, fairness, what Dad would have wanted. And there was truth in it. There was. Theo *had* taken advantage; Wren's own lawyer, a tired honest woman named Okafor, had looked at the signature page and said quietly, "This isn't right, Wren. Whatever else is true, this isn't right." Wren wasn't retracting anything. The betrayal had been real. The erasure had been real — watching *Marchand et Fils* on the awning when the fils had been two, and one of them had been scrubbed out of the story like a smudge on proofing film.

But underneath, in the place where motives keep their oldest furniture, the bakery had never been about equity percentages.

It was about this: their father's thumbprint pressed into the bottom of a loaf. It was about being *remembered*. Not famous — their father hadn't been famous, hadn't wanted to be, had turned down the television feature twice. Remembered the specific way bread remembers: in hands, in habits, in the muscle memory passed wrist to wrist across a floured table. Marcel Ashgrove had built a business, sure. But what he'd actually been building, the way a cathedral builder is building a prayer, was a continuity. *When I'm gone, the bread knows you.* He'd meant: when I'm gone, you'll still be able to find each other. You and Theo. You'll stand at the same counter, making the same thing, and you'll know whose hands you have.

And instead Theo had taken the recipe — or claimed to — taken the name, taken the story, put it in a book with his face on the cover, and left Wren holding a jar of starter and a signature they didn't remember making.

So yes, Wren had sued. And yes, when the ghostwriter had appeared — soft-voiced, sharp-eyed, asking gentle questions about *the early days* with her pen moving like a small bird — Wren had answered, because some starving part of them thought: *finally, someone is writing it down. Someone is putting us in ink.*

And then the someone turned out to be writing *him*.

Wren set down the tea. The oven ticked and breathed. Through the little window, the loaf was rising into its final shape, cracking along the score line the way good rye does, like the bread was opening its mouth to speak.

Here was the clarity, arriving not as lightning but as dawn, gray and gradual and total:

If the fight was about money, Theo could win it or lose it and either way Wren stayed broken. If the fight was about being remembered, then no verdict, no settlement, no fourteen percent of anything could give Wren what they actually needed. What they needed was the loaf. The counter. The hands. The name on the window — not as property, but as *presence*. Marcel Ashgrove's bread, existing in the world, made by the child whose thumbprints he'd pressed into every test batch. That was the inheritance. That had always been the inheritance. And it wasn't in dispute. It had never been in dispute. It was sitting in an oven at five in the morning in a borrowed kitchen, and all Wren had to do was claim it.

The timer rang.

* * *

Wren turned the loaf out onto a rack and stood over it while it cooled, which took forever, which was the point, which was the lesson their father had tried to teach with every fold of every croissant: *good things require you to wait without touching them.* Steam rose off the crust. The kitchen filled with the smell — dark, sweet, faintly bitter at the edges, caraway like a struck match — and Wren's eyes stung, and they let them.

Then they cut it.

The knife went through with the exact resistance their body remembered, and the crumb opened, and Wren made a sound they'd deny later, low and cracked, because there it was. Lace made of night. Eleven years of failure ending in one slice of success at 5:20 on a Tuesday morning in a kitchen that wasn't theirs, in a life that currently resembled a demolished building, with a lawsuit dying slowly in court and a woman's handwriting all over the ruins of their trust.

They ate a piece standing up, tears running freely into the flour on their shirt, and it tasted like their father's kitchen, and it tasted like forgiveness they hadn't earned yet, and it tasted like a plan.

Because here was the other thing the loaf told them, plain as print: they didn't want Theo's money anymore. They wanted Theo. Not the brother who'd erased them — the brother underneath, the one who'd been nine years old standing on a milk crate doing the same dance Wren was doing now, hands inside their father's hands, learning folds. That brother existed. Wren had seen him flicker at the mediation table, in the half-second when Theo had said, unguarded, "Dad would hate this," before the lawyers smoothed him flat again.

Two unreliable narrators, Petra had written in her notebook — no. Wren hadn't read the notebook. But they'd heard the phrase somewhere, maybe from her, maybe from a book, and it fit: two siblings telling two versions of the same wound, both true, both lies, both starving.

The bread didn't take sides. The bread just remembered.

Wren wrapped the rest of the loaf, washed the bowl, scraped the counter clean, and left one slice on a plate in the café's front window facing the street — their father's habit, an offering, *for whoever's hungry first* — and stood looking at it for a long moment.

Then they took out their phone, because clarity is cheap at 5 a.m. and expensive by noon, and typed a message to their lawyer before the feeling could evaporate:

*Okafor. I want to change the terms. I don't want damages anymore. I want the original storefront lease, the name Marcel Ashgrove restored to the registry, and Theo at the same table — voluntarily. Can we ask for that?*

Send.

Then, before the fear could catch up, a second message, to a number Wren had typed and deleted a dozen times since the night they'd walked out of their own kitchen:

*I know what you did. I'm not ready to talk about it. But I need you to know that last weekend was real anyway, and I'm figuring out what to do with that. — W*

They stared at it. Their thumb hovered.

Outside, the sky was doing its gray-to-gold trick over the rooftops, and the first delivery truck of the morning rattled past, and somewhere across town Theo was probably awake too, insomnia apparently genetic, probably standing in his own gleaming kitchen being haunted by the same dead man.

Wren hit send on both messages, put the phone face-down on the counter, and picked up the bread knife.

There was one more person who needed to taste this loaf. And Wren had a sudden, unshakable feeling — the kind their father would have called one breath further than safe — that whatever Petra Delacroix was deciding at this exact moment, alone with her manuscript and her debts, the next few hours were going to decide everything.

The phone buzzed against the steel.

Wren didn't look. Not yet. First, breakfast. Some things you do in order.
