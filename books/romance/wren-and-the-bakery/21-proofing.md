## 21. Proofing

Petra stood outside the old Marchand bakery for eleven minutes before she let herself touch the door.

She had counted them. She was good at counting now — words per chapter, dollars in her account, days since Wren had walked out of that kitchen and taken the light with them. Eleven minutes of standing on a cracked sidewalk in the gray light of a Tuesday morning, watching her breath fog the glass of a shop that hadn't opened its doors in nine years. The sign still said MARCHAND & ASHGROVE, though the paint had faded until Ashgrove was barely a ghost of letters, a shadow of a name under Marchand's bolder gold. Someone — Theo, probably, or the city — had chained the door at some point, but the chain hung loose now, pooled on the ground like something shed.

Inside, past the dusty glass, she could see shapes. A counter. The dark mouths of ovens. A wooden rack tipped against the wall like a man sleeping off a long night.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this the way she noticed everything these days — from a slight distance, as if she were drafting herself into someone else's memoir. *The writer stood outside the bakery, afraid.* Delete. Revise. Try again.

But there was no one to draft it for. That was the whole point. For three weeks she had been living inside her own unedited life for the first time since she was twenty-two years old, and it was unbearable, and it was the only honest thing she'd ever done.

She pushed the door open. A bell above it gave a single flat note, half-choked with dust.

* * *

Wren was there.

Of course they were there. Petra had heard through the neighborhood's small nervous telegraphy — the florist who supplied the café where they'd first met, the woman at the hardware store who sold Wren a wire brush and two cans of enamel — that someone had been working in the old Marchand shop at all hours. She'd told herself she wasn't going to come. She'd told herself that for six days, which was exactly how long it took her to run out of other things to tell herself.

Wren stood at the far counter with their back to the door, sleeves rolled to the elbow, scraping decades of burnt sugar off a sheet pan with a putty knife. Their hair was tied back with what looked like a strip of flour sack. They didn't turn around when the bell rang, but their shoulders changed — a small tightening, a held breath made visible.

"I saw you out there," Wren said. "Through the window. You've been standing there forever."

"Eleven minutes."

"That's very you." They set down the putty knife. Still not turning. "I almost came out and told you the sidewalk's free real estate, stand all day if you want."

"I would have deserved that."

"Yeah," Wren said. "You would have."

The silence that followed wasn't empty. It was full of everything Petra had rehearsed on the walk over — three drafts' worth of apology, each one revised and cut for length, each one collapsing the moment she tried to imagine saying it aloud. Apologies were just another kind of manuscript, she realized. You could polish them forever and they'd still be lies if you wrote them for the wrong reason: to be forgiven rather than to be true.

So she threw out all three drafts.

"I'm not here to ask you to forgive me," she said.

That got Wren to turn around. Their face was thinner than she remembered, or maybe she'd been remembering it wrong — softened by guilt into something gentler than it had ever actually been. There were smudges of rust on their forearms. Their eyes did the thing they always did, the thing that had undone her from the first weekend: looked at her directly, without flinching, like she was a person and not a problem to be solved.

"No?" Wren said.

"No. I don't get to ask for that. I lied to you while I was learning how much I loved you, and then I kept lying after, and then when you caught me I lied again by omission because the truth was going to cost me everything and I thought—" Her voice cracked. She let it. "I thought I could manage it. Manage you. Like a source. Like a chapter."

"You're good at managing," Wren said quietly. "It's the thing you do instead of being alive."

The words landed like a blade finding bone. Petra nodded, because it was true, and because nodding was easier than defending, and because she had sworn on the walk over — an actual oath, muttered to a bus stop — that she would not defend herself today. Not once.

"Theo published," she said. "From his own notes. I know you know. I know you've read it, or parts of it, because Marisol told me you went into the bookshop on Delancey and stood there reading the lawsuit chapter for forty minutes and then bought it and paid cash and left without a word."

Wren's jaw tightened. "You've been asking about me."

"A little. Not to find you. To make sure you were—" She stopped. Started over. "No. That's a lie too, the polite version. Yes. I've been asking about you. I'm a coward in most directions but I'm trying to be honest in the ones I can reach."

Something flickered across Wren's face — not softness, not yet, but the place where softness might eventually be built. They wiped their hands on the flour-sack cloth and leaned back against the counter, arms crossed, and finally looked at her fully, and Petra understood that she was about to be interviewed. That Wren had learned this from her, or maybe had always known it: how to wait a person out, how to make silence do the work.

"Say the rest," Wren said. "Whatever you rehearsed. Say it badly. I'd rather hear it broken than polished."

* * *

So Petra said it broken.

She told Wren about the manuscript — the real one, hers, the one she'd finished at four in the morning in a rented room that smelled like pencil shavings and despair, the final chapter rewritten to omit the lawsuit entirely, omitting Wren from the story of their own family because Wren had asked her to and because it was right. She told them how she'd sent it to Theo with her resignation attached, how he'd called her seven times and then stopped calling, how the advance had evaporated along with the career she'd spent fifteen years building one borrowed voice at a time.

"I burned nothing, technically," she said. "I submitted the clean version. But it amounts to the same thing. He'll never publish my draft now, and no one else will touch me once word gets around that I burned a celebrity client. In this business, loyalty to the subject is a rumor; disloyalty is a headline."

"What did you write instead? In the last chapter. If not the lawsuit."

Petra looked down at her hands. "I wrote about the bread."

"The bread."

"Your father's bread. What Theo remembers of it. I wrote that some things can't be divided in court — that the recipe belonged to both of them the way a childhood does, and that the tragedy of the Marchand-Ashgrove split wasn't the money, it was that two people who loved the same man ended up unable to say his name in the same room." She swallowed. "It's the best thing I've ever written. It doesn't matter. It will never see print. But I needed you to know that when I finally used my own voice, that's what I chose to say."

Wren was quiet for a long time. Outside, a delivery truck groaned past, and the windows trembled faintly in their frames, and somewhere in the walls the old building settled with a sound like a sigh.

"You know what's funny," Wren said finally. "Theo's book. The chapter everyone's talking about — the lawsuit, the betrayal, all of it. He got so much wrong. Not the facts. The facts are mostly right. But he wrote it like Dad's bakery was a thing that was *taken* from him. Like inheritance is a trophy." They pushed off the counter and walked toward the ovens, running a hand along the cold steel. "Dad used to say the bakery wasn't ours. We were its. You don't inherit a thing like that. You get *kept* by it, if you're lucky, until it needs you again."

"And now it needs you?"

Wren turned. And for the first time since Petra had walked in, they smiled — small, tired, real. "Now it needs both of us. That's the part Theo still doesn't understand. That's why his book reads like a eulogy instead of a recipe."

* * *

They sat on upturned milk crates near the front window, sharing coffee from a thermos Wren had brought, because the building had no working plumbing yet and certainly no machine. The light came through the glass in long dusty columns, and Petra found herself cataloguing the room the way she catalogued everything — the mosaic tile floor, black and cream, cracked but intact; the marble counter scarred with a hundred years of knife work; the chalkboard menu still bearing a faded list in a dead man's handwriting. *Sourdough rye. Honey rolls. Wren's Wednesday special.*

"You were on the menu," Petra said softly.

"Wednesdays. I got to pick anything I wanted and he'd make it, and then he'd put it on the board like it was a real item, and people actually ordered it. I was nine. I picked chocolate croissants for like two months straight." Wren's voice did something complicated on the word *croissants*, bent between laughter and grief. "He charged full price for them, too. Said favoritism was bad for the dough."

Petra laughed — surprised by it, the sound coming out of her unbidden, and Wren laughed too, and for a moment they were just two people on milk crates in a ruined bakery, laughing about a dead man's integrity regarding children's pastry prices.

Then Wren set down the thermos, and the laughter drained away, and they looked at her with the expression of someone arriving at the thing they'd actually invited her here to say.

"I didn't clean this place up for therapy," they said. "I signed the lease yesterday."

Petra's breath caught. "Wren—"

"Not to compete with Theo. Not to prove anything. I met with him last week — really met with him, all night, we baked until sunrise and fought about Dad the whole time and cried into the proofing dough like idiots." Wren's mouth twitched. "We're not fixed. We might never be fixed. But we agreed on one thing: the original shop shouldn't stay dead. It should reopen. Under both names. Both names, Petra — Ashgrove back on the window where it always should have stayed."

"That's—" Petra's throat closed. "That's wonderful. You don't need me for that. You have Theo now, you have the ovens, you have—"

"I need a writer."

The words dropped into the room and rang there.

"Not a ghost," Wren said. "God, never a ghost again — I couldn't watch you disappear into someone else's sentences, I'd rather lose you to honesty than to craft. But the shop needs a story. Menus, the history wall, the book of Dad's recipes we're reconstructing — Theo has half from memory, I have half from muscle, and neither of us can write a sentence that isn't either a legal brief or a eulogy. And there's your own book. The one you've been not-writing for fifteen years. Your name on the cover. I've seen how you look when you talk about it, like it's a country you have a passport for and keep failing to board the plane."

"Wren, I have no money. I have less than no money. I owe—"

"I know what you owe. I did math too, once, about an inheritance." Wren reached into their apron pocket — they were wearing an apron, Petra realized, an actual work apron already broken in — and produced a folded piece of paper, creased soft from handling. "This is the partnership draft. Theo and I wrote it together, which means it took nine hours and one thrown dish towel. There's a line in section four for a third partner. Small share. Story and voice and — I don't know — the person who makes sure we don't turn the place into a shrine or a courtroom. We wrote your name in pencil. Because we figured you'd want to erase it yourself first, and then decide whether to write it back."

Petra took the paper. Her hands were shaking again, but differently now.

She read section four. *Third partner: editorial, narrative, and front-of-house voice. Terms negotiable. Name:* and there it was, in soft graphite, slightly smudged where a thumb had touched it — *P. Delacroix.*

"This isn't forgiveness," she said. Her voice came out rough. "This is a business document."

"It's absolutely a business document," Wren agreed. "Forgiveness isn't mine to hand out like a receipt. You'll earn whatever comes next the way anyone earns anything — by showing up, by doing the work, by being here on the bad days especially. Absolution is not a thing I sell." They looked at her, and their eyes were wet and steady at once. "But an invitation? An invitation I can give. Second chances aren't granted, Petra. They're proofed. You sit in the warm dark long enough, doing the slow work, and either you rise or you don't. Nobody can do it for you. But somebody can set the bowl in front of you and say: *here. Try.*"

Petra looked down at the paper. At her own name in pencil, waiting to be erased or inked.

She thought about every manuscript she'd ever ghosted — every life she'd written beautifully and then abandoned, every byline she'd given away like it cost nothing. She thought about her mother's voice on the phone last month, fierce and unexpected: *You tried, mija. Trying is the family trade; we just pretend it isn't.* She thought about a reunion she'd attend in the spring with no hired stranger on her arm, only herself, insufficient and unedited and finally, finally hers.

She dug in her bag for a pen. All she had was the cheap one from the hardware store, the one she'd bought to sign her resignation letter. Fitting.

"Ink or eraser," Wren said quietly. "Your call. No deadline. Well — okay, one deadline. We open in eight weeks and I refuse to open with a typo on the menu."

Petra laughed, wet-eyed. Then she smoothed the paper against her knee, found her name in its soft gray pencil, and pressed the pen to it.

She wrote slowly. Not because she was unsure — but because for the first time in fifteen years, every word she wrote was going to be signed.

* * *

Outside, the morning had committed fully to daylight. When they taped the paper to the inside of the front window — partnership draft, section four, her name in ink over pencil — the sun caught the glass and threw their doubled reflection onto the sidewalk, two figures leaning close over a shared page.

Wren flipped the old sign in the window. It still said CLOSED, paint flaking, but beneath it, on the reverse, in a child's careful hand preserved for nine years behind the glass, the other side read: BACK SOON.

"Eight weeks," Wren said.

"Eight weeks," Petra said. And reached for the broom.
