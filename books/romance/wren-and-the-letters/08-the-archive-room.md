## 8. The Archive Room

The archive room at the back of Marguerite Hale's house had once been a sunroom, and it still remembered. The windows ran floor to ceiling on two sides, and even in late September the light came through them thick and golden, catching the dust that rose whenever Wren moved a box, so that the whole room seemed to shimmer faintly, like something underwater. A wicker chaise sat in the corner under a sheet, and a potted fern had gone feral in the corner, its fronds reaching for the glass with the desperation of the long-neglected.

Marguerite had given her the key on her second day in Harborlight, pressing it into Wren's palm with the ceremony of a woman handing over state secrets.

"The papers are yours to sort," she'd said. "Everything from the last fifty years. Correspondence, drafts, contracts, grocery lists—I don't care. You'll know what matters. That's why I hired you." Then she'd paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and added, without turning around: "You'll find some things you weren't looking for. That's how archives work. That's how life works, if you're paying attention."

Wren had laughed politely and thought nothing of it.

Now, three weeks into the sorting, she thought about it constantly.

* * *

The work itself was a kind of fever dream. Marguerite Hale had published eleven novels across four decades, and she had kept *everything*. There were boxes of fan letters tied with ribbon gone brittle as autumn leaves. Boxes of royalty statements annotated in Marguerite's slashing hand—*robbery*, she'd written beside one figure, and beside another, *finally, the thieves blink*. Draft upon draft upon draft, sometimes seven versions of a single chapter, each one bleeding corrections in different inks, so that a page from 1987 might carry the archaeological record of an entire decade's reconsideration.

Wren had developed a system. She worked mornings, when the light was best and her mind was sharpest, moving chronologically backward through the years—the memoir was to be structured around the books, each novel a doorway into the life that produced it—and she took notes in a green marbled notebook that was already half full. She photographed everything fragile. She wore white cotton gloves until they made her feel like a surgeon performing an autopsy on a still-living woman, and then she stopped wearing them, because Marguerite, consulted by phone, had snorted audibly.

"Paper wants hands, girl, not gloves. It spent its whole life being touched. Let it rest in peace doing what it loved."

So Wren touched. And the papers told her things.

She learned that Marguerite's third novel, the one critics called her breakthrough, had nearly been abandoned—there was a letter from her agent, dated forty years back, urging her to cut the entire middle section, and Marguerite's reply, drafted and redrafted and finally sent, defending those hundred pages with a ferocity that made Wren sit back in her chair and press the letter flat against her heart like something warm. She learned that Marguerite had turned down a fortune to write a celebrity autobiography because, as she noted in the margin of the offer letter, *I have met my soul exactly twice and I am not selling either of them.* She learned that the novelist who survived scandal—who had weathered, twenty years ago now, a vicious controversy over a novel everyone insisted was a portrait of a real town, a real family, a real grief—had written in the front of her working copy of that book, in pencil, small enough to be erased: *They were right. It was all of them. It was all of me.*

Wren copied that line into her notebook and stared at it for a long time.

This was the strange gift of the work: it made her feel less alone. Here was a woman who had built a whole life out of sentences, who had been misunderstood and misrepresented and had come out the other side not hardened but *honed*, and every box Wren opened was another lesson in how a person could survive being known. She hadn't realized how much she needed the instruction. Eleven years in New York, editing other people's truths, curating other people's confessions, keeping her own story locked down so tight that sometimes she surprised herself with her own smoothness—*I'm from a small town on the coast*, she'd say, and leave it there, and no one ever asked what she'd left behind, because she'd learned to make leaving sound like ambition rather than escape.

But Harborlight was peeling her open anyway. The house on Larkspur Lane smelled like her grandmother again—camphor and lavender and the ghost of woodsmoke—and every drawer held a relic: her grandmother's reading glasses, still folded on the nightstand as if set down between chapters; the tin of lemon drops on the hall table; the crossword finished in two different hands, hers and her grandmother's, arguing amiably in graphite across the squares. Wren had unpacked only three boxes of her own. The rest sat stacked in the spare room like a decision she was postponing. As if fully unpacking would mean admitting she was staying, and admitting she was staying would mean admitting there was nowhere else she wanted to be, and admitting *that* would mean reckoning with the fact that she had come home partly because home was the last place Sebastian Iversen could follow her without her knowing.

Not that he would. Not that he was anywhere. Eleven years, and he had become a rumor, then a silence, then a scar that only ached when she let herself press on it—which was rarely, which was almost never, which was why she'd said yes to this job in the first place, because Marguerite Hale's world and Wren Calloway's old wound existed in entirely separate universes.

That is what she believed, on the morning of the fourth Tuesday, when she carried the next box in from the hallway and read the label.

* * *

The label was written in Marguerite's hand, in black felt-tip, the letters tall and unhurried: **HARBORLIGHT — FESTIVAL YEAR**.

And beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought, as if the pen had hesitated: **Do not open until you must.**

Wren stood in the gold light holding the box and felt the temperature of the room change. Not literally—the sunroom was warm, the fern swayed in a draft from the ill-fitting window—but something in her body understood before her mind did, the way you understand a storm is coming from the smell of the air.

Because she knew the festival year. Everyone in Harborlight knew the festival year. It was the year the autumn festival had nearly died, the year the plagiarism scandal broke over the town like cold water—the year a promising young writer named Sebastian Iversen was found to have lifted passages, whole luminous passages, from the unpublished manuscript of a dead local poet, and had stood in front of the festival committee with his head bowed while the town that had adopted him turned its face away. He'd left within the week. No forwarding address. No explanation beyond a statement his lawyer issued, three sentences long, taking full responsibility and asking for privacy.

Wren had been twenty-four. She had loved him. She had defended him for exactly one day, and then the evidence had been laid out on the bookshop counter where she worked—photocopies, side by side, his pages and the dead poet's, the echoes undeniable, the phrasing identical in places where coincidence could not reach—and she had stopped defending him, and he had disappeared, and she had spent eleven years learning to say his name without flinching.

*Festival year.* Her own handwriting wasn't on the box. But her history was inside it, or near it, or adjacent to it, the way everything in this town was adjacent to everything else, six degrees of separation collapsing into one.

*Do not open until you must.*

"Then I must," she said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how thin her voice sounded, and opened it anyway.

* * *

Inside: paper, of course. Reams of it. Newspaper clippings from the *Harborlight Courier*, the scandal splashed across the front page in a font size usually reserved for wars—GHOSTWRITER OF SHAME? someone had headlined it, and beneath, in smaller type, LOCAL WRITER ADMITS THEFT FROM ESTATE OF POET ELIN MARSH. There were committee minutes. There was a printed program from that year's festival, the corners soft with handling. There was a folder of correspondence between Marguerite and the festival board, Marguerite's voice rising off the page in indignant capitals—she had threatened, Wren saw with a start, to withdraw her lifetime achievement honor if they handled the matter "with anything less than due process and decency," and apparently they had listened, because there was a grudging apology on committee letterhead and a note in Marguerite's hand: *Cowards, but trainable.*

Wren read all of it kneeling on the floorboards, the box between her knees like a lapful of winter. The scandal had been worse than she'd let herself remember. The town had wanted blood. Elin Marsh had been beloved—a schoolteacher, a recluse, a woman whose poems circulated among maybe two hundred people who considered themselves lucky to know them—and the idea that a charming newcomer had rifled her posthumous papers for material was sacrilege of the highest order. Wren remembered the bookshop, the whispering customers, the way the owner had quietly removed Sebastian's self-published chapbook from the shelf and put it in a drawer, as if the object itself were contaminated.

She remembered, too—she could not stop herself from remembering—that Sebastian had never once defended himself. Not to the committee, not to the crowd outside the town hall, not to her. She had waited for him at the seawall for three evenings running. He never came. On the fourth evening she had stopped waiting, and something in her had closed like a shop pulling down its gate, and she had not fully reopened it since.

*He never explained,* she thought, smoothing a clipping flat with both hands. *Eleven years, and he never gave me one sentence.*

Her fingers found the bottom of the box.

Beneath the clippings, wrapped in a sheet of acid-free tissue that crinkled like frost, lay an envelope.

It was addressed to her.

**Miss Wren Calloway**, in blue-black ink, in handwriting she knew the way you know your own name in a stranger's mouth—instantly, totally, with a lurch that starts in the stomach and ends somewhere behind the ribs. The *W* with its deep initial stroke. The *y* that trailed off like a hook cast into water. The *ll* in Calloway, twin towers, precise and close together, the way he used to joke that everything he wrote looked slightly architectural because he'd wanted to be an architect before words got him instead.

Eleven years, and her body recognized his handwriting faster than her mind could form the thought *Sebastian*, and for one suspended moment Wren knelt in the golden dust-light of Marguerite Hale's sunroom and could not breathe.

The postmark read: HARBORLIGHT, MA — OCTOBER.

The year matched. The week of the festival. The week he vanished.

It had never been opened. The flap was still sealed, the gum gone amber with age, and the envelope had traveled—she could see it in the smudged cancellation, the softening of the corners—from here to wherever Marguerite had been living that autumn, and then back again, and then into this box, into the dark, into *do not open until you must*, where it had waited through eleven years of Wren's life like a landmine buried under a garden path.

Marguerite had it. Marguerite had *kept* it. Which meant—

Which meant Marguerite had received it, or intercepted it, or been given it, and had chosen silence. Had chosen a sealed envelope and a labeled box and a warning written in felt-tip, and had said nothing, for eleven years, and then had hired Wren Calloway to sort her archives, and had pressed a key into her palm and said, *you'll find some things you weren't looking for.*

"That's how archives work," Wren whispered. "That's how life works, if you're paying attention."

The light had shifted while she knelt; the sun had moved past the fern and now lay in a long bright bar directly across her hands, across the envelope, as if the room itself were insisting: *now. Here. In the open.*

Her thumb slid under the flap. The old adhesive resisted, then surrendered with a sound like a small sigh, and the letter came free—one page, folded in thirds, blue-black ink bleeding slightly at the creases where eleven years of pressure had pressed the lines into their opposite faces.

She unfolded it.

She did not read it. Not yet. She couldn't; her eyes skated off the first line the way a hand skates off a hot stove. What she did instead was look—really look—at the handwriting, at the architecture of it, at the *W* and the trailing *y* and the twin towers of the *ll*, and she let herself feel the full vertiginous weight of the recognition: *he wrote to me. He wrote to me and it never came. Or it came and was kept from me. Either way, eleven years, and the explanation has been sitting thirty feet from Marguerite's kitchen the whole time.*

Outside, a gull cried over the harbor, long and descending, the sound the town made when it was settling toward evening. Down the hill, the bookshop would be closing soon. The festival posters were already going up on the lampposts—this year's festival, the anniversary nobody had planned but everybody felt coming, the season turning back toward the place where everything had broken.

Wren looked down at the page in her hands, at the first line waiting there in ink the color of deep water:

*Wren—if you're reading this, then Marguerite finally did what I asked her not to do, and I find I'm glad.*

Her heart stopped, started again, wrong-footed, like a tide turning.

She began to read.
