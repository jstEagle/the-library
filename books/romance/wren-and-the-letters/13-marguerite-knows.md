## 13. Marguerite Knows

The house on Gullwing Lane smelled of bergamot and old paper, and it had opinions about him.

Sebastian felt that every time he climbed the front steps with his laptop bag over his shoulder—the house regarded him the way a cat regards a stranger who has overstayed his welcome. The shutters were painted a green gone soft with salt air. The garden was a riot of things Marguerite refused to prune because, she said, chaos had better stories than order ever would. And the porch boards announced him with a creak so particular that she always called out before he knocked.

"You're late, Mr. Iversen."

"I'm four minutes early," he said to the open door.

"Then I'm early too, and one of us is lying." Marguerite Hale stood in the hallway in a cardigan the color of storm water, her white hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils and a fountain pen. She was seventy-eight years old and moved like weather—slowly, and with the certainty that everything in her path would eventually accommodate itself. "Tea's been ready since noon. Sit down before you start apologizing. You apologize the way other men breathe."

He sat. He always sat. There was no version of himself, he had learned over these weeks of interviews, that could remain standing in Marguerite Hale's living room for longer than ninety seconds. The room did not permit it. The room had been arranged for confession: two armchairs angled toward each other like conspirators, a low table between them bearing the teapot and a plate of shortbread she never ate herself but watched him eat with an intensity he found increasingly unnerving.

Today there was a third object on the table.

A folder. Thin, manila, unremarkable—except that it sat squared to the table's edge with a precision that Marguerite, whose desk was an archaeology of forty years of drafts, had never once displayed toward anything else in his presence.

Sebastian poured the tea because his hands wanted something to do. Outside the window, Harborlight performed its October afternoon: gulls arguing over the harbor, a fishing boat coming in with its lights already on against the early dusk, the smell of woodsmoke beginning to thread through the salt. Somewhere across town, Wren was in the archive room at the historical society, or maybe at her grandmother's house, or maybe walking past the bookshop where she'd once shelved paperbacks and argued with customers about endings. He thought about her the way you think about a bruise—not constantly, but whenever anything pressed against it.

"The transcript from Tuesday," he said. "I've got the section on your first residency drafted. I think we've found the spine of chapter nine."

"Mm," said Marguerite, who was not listening, and who let him know she wasn't listening by the magnificent stillness of her attention elsewhere. She picked up the folder. Held it. Turned it over once, as if weighing it against some invisible alternative.

"I've been thinking," she said, "about how a man begins a sentence he's spent eleven years not saying."

Sebastian's hand stopped halfway to the shortbread plate.

* * *

She had been circling for days now. He'd told himself it was nothing—editors circled, subjects circled, old novelists especially circled, having learned that the direct approach wasted perfectly good suspense. But this was different. This morning she had asked him, apropos of her 1998 tour, whether he believed a person could build a life out of omissions and call it modesty. Last week she'd paused mid-anecdote to study his hands while he typed, as though checking them against some memory. And twice now she had used the phrase *that dreadful festival year* within earshot of the recorder, watching him with those pale, patient eyes while he kept his face arranged into professional blankness.

Professional blankness. That was the phrase he'd built his second life on. Eleven years of it. He could hold it through a video call with his own mother. He could hold it through contract negotiations, through cocktail parties where someone inevitably mentioned the scandal that had ended a promising young career, through the long gray afternoons when he ghosted memoirs for people whose names went on covers and whose truths went into print while his own stayed folded in a drawer in a rented flat in a city where nobody knew his face.

But Marguerite Hale had written nineteen novels. She knew what blankness looked like from the inside. She knew it was load-bearing.

"Chapter nine," she repeated now, pleasantly. "Yes. The residency. Where I learned that the story you tell about yourself is just the lie you've had the most practice telling." She set down her cup. The porcelain made a small, definitive click. "I want to talk about a different chapter today. One that isn't mine."

"Marguerite—"

"The festival year," she said. "Eleven years ago. Autumn. You'll remember it, I think, better than you'd like to."

The tea in his cup had gone still. Everything in him had gone still—that old, practiced stillness, the animal trick of becoming furniture. He had done it in a hundred rooms. He was doing it now, and hating himself for doing it, because he knew—he knew with the sick certainty of a man watching a trap close—that she had designed this afternoon the way she designed chapters. Nothing in her living room happened by accident. The folder had been squared to the table's edge on purpose. The shortbread was there so his hands would have somewhere to go when they started shaking.

They had started shaking.

"What is it you want to discuss?" His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it.

"A letter," said Marguerite.

* * *

She didn't open the folder. That was the cruelty of it—or the mercy; he couldn't decide which, and suspected she'd left the ambiguity there deliberately, the way she left gaps in drafts for editors to fall into.

"Eleven years ago," she said, "a young writer published a novel that contained passages belonging to another book. The scandal was small by the standards of the industry—no lawsuits, no criminal charges—but it was enormous by the standards of a town like this one. A town that had decided, years earlier, to be proud of its literary sons and daughters. A town that needed someone to be ashamed of." She looked out the window at the gulls. "And a young man stood up at the worst possible moment and said: it was me. I did it. Alone."

Sebastian said nothing. Saying nothing was the only move left that wasn't surrender.

"And everyone believed him," Marguerite went on, "because he confessed so completely. No hedging, no appeals. A full surrender. Do you know what I thought, reading the accounts? I thought: here is a man confessing to a crime the way a soldier falls on a grenade. I've read enough false confessions in my life—in fiction and out of it—to know their signature. They're too clean. Real guilt is messy. It argues with itself. It leaves fingerprints all over the place trying to prove it isn't guilt. But that boy's confession—" she tapped the folder once, lightly, "—was spotless. Not one thread of self-defense. And I thought: someone taught him to confess like that. Or something did. And I wondered what it cost him, and who paid the rest of the bill."

"Marguerite." His voice cracked on her name, and he hated that more than anything. "Whatever you think you know—"

"I don't *think*, Sebastian. Thinking is for people without archives." She leaned forward, and for the first time since he'd known her, the performance fell away entirely, and what was underneath was not cruel, only terribly clear. "Do you know why I hired you?"

"Because of my work. My samples. You said my prose had—"

"I said your prose had rhythm. Yes. I also said it had restraint. Do you know what I meant?" She didn't wait. "I meant I recognized the sound of a man writing around a hole in himself. Every ghostwriter has one. Most of them are ordinary—a failed marriage, a dead father, a book that never sold. Yours has a shape I recognized, because I've spent eleven years keeping a file on the shape of that hole."

"A file," he repeated dumbly.

"Clippings. Correspondence. The statement the publisher released. The statement *you* released—which, incidentally, was the finest piece of prose anyone produced that entire year, including my own novel, which won a prize that autumn and which I have never fully enjoyed since, because I kept thinking about the boy who wrote his own destruction in such beautiful sentences." She sat back. "When my editor proposed a ghostwriter for the memoir, I asked for three names. Yours was the only one I hadn't heard of, which interested me. Then I read your sample pages, and I knew."

"Knew what?"

"That the anonymous sample pages of Sebastian Iversen sounded exactly like the public apology of Sebastian Iversen. Same rhythms. Same restraint. Same terrible, careful music of a man who has practiced saying less than he means." Her eyes didn't waver. "You can change your name, dear. You cannot change your sentences. They're fingerprints."

The word *fingerprints* landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He waited a long time for the splash.

"So you hired me," he said finally, "to write your memoir. Because of a scandal that ruined me."

"I hired you," said Marguerite, "because I am dying."

The word entered the room quietly and rearranged all the furniture.

"Not immediately," she went on, brisk again, as if she'd mentioned a dentist appointment. "Months, probably. The doctors are admirably vague. But a woman in my position does not spend her last book settling scores for sport. I am spending it on truth, because truth is the only thing I've ever written that I couldn't invent, and I find I want to get it right while I still can." She lifted the folder at last, and held it out to him. "That dreadful festival year broke more than one person, Sebastian. It broke the boy who confessed. It broke the girl who was abandoned without explanation. It broke a mentor who has carried something she shouldn't have carried alone. And it broke me, a little, because I watched it happen and said nothing, and a novelist who says nothing while a story goes wrong is complicit in the worst way—we of all people should know better."

His hand hovered over the folder. "What's in it?"

"Correspondence. From the year of the scandal. Including letters between myself and a certain professor of literature at the college, discussing certain passages in a certain manuscript—passages whose true origin I believe you know better than anyone alive." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a woman holding a door open onto a room he'd sealed for eleven years. "Take it home. Read it tonight. And then come back tomorrow and tell me whether you intend to keep being a ghost, or whether you'd like, before this book ends, to have a name."

* * *

He walked home along the harbor road because he couldn't stand to be indoors, and the folder rode under his arm like something warm-blooded.

The light was going gold and then copper over the water. Fishing boats came home. Kids were stringing lantern wire along the festival poles in the square, preparing for a celebration that was still weeks away but already colonizing the town's imagination—and his. He passed the bookshop without stopping, mostly. He slowed, is what he did. He allowed his pace to degrade by perhaps ten percent, which was as much honesty as he could afford.

Wren had the letter. He was almost sure of it now. He'd seen it in the way she'd looked at him outside the shop three days ago—not hatred, which he could have survived, but recognition, which he could not. She had read something. She knew something. And Marguerite's folder was sitting under his arm containing, apparently, the rest of an architecture he'd spent a decade building in the dark.

Here was the arithmetic he could not make balance: the truth would free him and destroy the person he'd built the lie to protect. The lie had saved Wren's reputation and cost him everything, and he had never regretted it, not once, not in eleven years of cheap rented rooms and uncredited pages—except that lately, in this town, in this autumn, with her voice coming through frozen video calls and her handwriting surfacing in margins, he had begun to understand that the sacrifice had a second victim, and the second victim was her, and no amount of noble arithmetic could make that addition come out clean.

At his rented cottage he set the folder on the kitchen table and did not touch it for an hour. He made coffee he didn't drink. He stood at the window watching the tide do its patient, indifferent work. Finally, when the dark had come all the way down and the single lamp threw his shadow long across the floorboards, he opened it.

Letters. Dozens of them. Marguerite's handwriting, upright and severe. And interleaved among them, in a slanted, scholarly hand he recognized from the margins of every book that had taught him to write—

He stopped breathing.

There, on the third page, dated the spring before the scandal, a passage in Professor Eleanor Voss's hand: *the girl shows real promise, but the boy—there is something in the boy that frightens me, he gives his best work away.*

He read it four times. *He gives his best work away.* Eleven years ago, a mentor had seen the shape of the sacrifice before he'd made it, and had said nothing, and had let him fall.

And beneath that letter, clipped together with a rusted paperclip, a note in Marguerite's own hand, undated, addressed to no one:

*If I am right about the Iversen boy, then the whole town owes him an apology and a byline. Find out before I die. I refuse to leave this story unfinished—it's the only kind of ending I've never been able to forgive.*

Sebastian sat down slowly in the dark.

Tomorrow he would go back to Gullwing Lane. Tomorrow she would ask her question—ghost or name—and he would have to answer, knowing that whatever he chose, the choice had already been made for him, months ago, by a dying novelist with a filing cabinet and a conscience, who had hired a ghost precisely because she intended to give him back his name or bury him with the truth.

Outside, far off across the water, the first test string of festival lanterns flickered on—all down the pier, a line of small gold lights trembling above the black tide, like a fuse already lit, burning slowly toward him.
