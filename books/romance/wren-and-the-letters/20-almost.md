## 20. Almost

The storm had spent itself by midnight, but the sea hadn't gotten the message. It kept throwing itself against the pilings beneath the pier as if it had a grievance to settle, and the boards above it shivered with every blow. Sebastian stood at the far end of Harborlight's old fishing pier, where the planks gave way to rotted gaps and the town's new lanterns—strung up for the festival, two weeks away—couldn't quite reach. He had come out here to be alone with the rain still dripping off the eaves of his rented cottage three streets back. He had not expected her to follow him.

He should have known better. Eleven years ago she would have followed him anywhere, and apparently some things survived even the drowning he'd given them.

Wren came down the pier with her coat pulled tight and her hair half-ruined by the wind that still gusted off the water, and she stopped an arm's length away—the distance of colleagues, of strangers, of people who had once known each other's heartbeats—and said nothing at all.

That was the thing about Wren Calloway that had undone him from the very first day in the bookshop, all those years ago: she never filled silence just to fill it. She let it stand there between them like a third person, patient and enormous, until whatever was true finally climbed out on its own.

"I'm sorry about earlier," he said, because someone had to speak first and cowardice had always been fluent in him.

"You're not." Her voice was steady. The storm had washed something raw into it anyway. "You're sorry I asked."

He looked out at the black water rather than at her. A buoy bell clanked somewhere offshore, arrhythmic, like a clock losing its mind. "Maybe both things can be true."

"Everything with you is *maybe*," she said. "Maybe you'll stay through the festival. Maybe the memoir will tell the truth. Maybe you meant what the letter said. Do you know how exhausting it is to love a man made entirely of maybes?"

The word *love* landed between them like something dropped from a great height. Neither of them bent to pick it up.

"Wren—"

"No." She held up a hand, and he saw it wasn't quite steady. "I've been polite for weeks. I've been *professional*. I sat across from you in Marguerite's parlor and discussed chapter structure while my hands shook under the table. I read your letter eleven years too late and then I watched you walk past me in the square like I was a stranger holding a clipboard. And tonight, on this pier, in the rain, I asked you one question. One. If I'd asked you to stay—eleven years ago, that night, if I'd woken up and asked you not to go—would you have stayed?"

The buoy bell clanked. The pilings groaned. Somewhere behind them, up the hill, the town slept on under its festival lanterns, dreaming its small safe dreams.

"Yes," Sebastian said.

It came out of him before he could weigh it, before he could sand it down or hedge it or bury it in the conditional tense where he kept everything valuable. Just *yes*, naked and terrible, hanging in the salt air.

Wren's breath caught. He heard it. In eleven years of learning to control his voice for clients who paid him to disappear into their lives, he had never lost control of it until this woman stood in front of him asking questions with the rain coming down.

"Then why—" Her voice cracked and she hated it; he could see her hating it, see her jaw set against the crack. "Why did you make me find out from a letter in a box? Why did you let me spend ten years thinking I wasn't worth a goodbye?"

"Because if you'd asked me to stay, I would have." He turned to face her fully now, because there were some lies you couldn't tell looking away. "And staying would have destroyed you. That's the whole arithmetic of it, Wren. Every other version of that night ends with you ruined alongside me. Your name in the trade papers next to mine. Your father's business called into question because his daughter was the protégée of a plagiarist. You'd just signed your contract with Whitmore & Hale. You were twenty-six years old and the whole coast was talking about you like you were the future of the imprint. I was nobody. I was a ghostwriter. Nobody loses anything when a ghost disappears."

"That wasn't your decision to make."

"No," he said quietly. "It wasn't. It's the great sin of my life—not the lie, not the exile. The deciding. I decided for you what you could survive. And I've been doing it ever since, haven't I? I did it again tonight. You asked me a question and I answered it and now I want to take it back, because the honest answer is going to cost us both more than either of us budgeted for."

She stared at him. Rain dripped from the pier's rusted railing between them, counting seconds neither of them wanted counted.

"Don't take it back," she said.

* * *

They ended up walking, because standing still was unbearable and the pier ran out of planks. Down the ramp, along the seawall, past the shuttered chowder house where they'd eaten fried clams on their third date and she'd stolen food off his plate with the impunity of someone who already knew she'd be doing it for decades. He had thought, that night, *I will grow old with this exact feeling.* He had been right about the feeling and wrong about everything else.

The rain had stopped entirely now, but the wind kept coming in long shoves off the Atlantic, pushing them closer together on the narrow sidewalk than conversation required. He could smell her shampoo—something with rosemary in it, unchanged, absurdly unchanged—and underneath it the smell of the storm, ozone and kelp and wet stone.

"The letter," she said, after a block of silence. "You wrote it the night you left. You addressed it to me. And then you didn't send it."

"I couldn't."

"Why not? You'd already done the brave part. You'd already taken the blame. What did one letter cost you?"

"Everything," he said. "That's what it cost. Don't you see? The blame was clean. The blame was a sentence I could serve. But the letter—that was the truth, all of it, including the parts about how much I loved you and how leaving felt like cutting off my own hand. If you'd read it, you would have come after me. I knew you would. You're doing it right now, eleven years later, and I only sent it by accident of Marguerite's filing system. If you'd come after me then—" He stopped. Breathed. "I'd have let you catch me. And then we'd both have gone down together, and I'd have spent the rest of my life knowing I chose my comfort over your future."

"Your *comfort*?" She stopped walking. "You think leaving was comfortable?"

"I think being found was. There's a difference." He kept walking, because if he stopped he was afraid of what he'd do—reach for her, probably, and then never stop reaching. "Every year I told myself the same story. *She's better off. The wound is closed. Reopening it would be selfish.* It's a very flattering story when you're the one telling it. It turns cowardice into sacrifice. You can build a whole career inside a story like that. I did."

"And what's the story now?"

He looked at her. Under the streetlamp at the corner of Harrow Street her face was half gold and half shadow, and there was rainwater still caught in her lashes, and he thought: *this is the face I drew from memory for ten years, in the margins of other people's books, and none of the drawings got her right.*

"Now," he said, "the story is falling apart. Marguerite is going to publish the truth whether I corroborate it or not. My name is going to be in the memoir one way or another—either as the man who confessed or as the ghost who got exposed. And I keep sitting in that cottage at night trying to figure out which version of me survives that book, and the honest answer is *neither*. The man who took the blame is dead. The man who hid is about to be. And I don't know yet who's standing there when the smoke clears."

"So don't decide alone this time." She said it simply, without drama, the way she'd once told him a manuscript needed one more revision—stating a fact and trusting him to rise to it. "That's all I'm asking, Sebastian. Not forgiveness. Not yet. Just—don't decide for me anymore. Let me be in the room when you choose."

The wind shoved them again. Her shoulder brushed his arm, brief and electric, and he felt it all the way down through his boots into the old wood of the seawall, into the bedrock of the town, into whatever geological layer of himself still believed he deserved this.

They had reached the pier again without either of them deciding to turn around. It loomed ahead of them, black against the less-black water, the festival lanterns at its landward end glowing soft orange, moths stuttering against the bulbs. The tide was coming in. He could hear it filling the spaces under the boards.

"Walk out with me," she said. "One more time. We used to."

"We used to do a lot of things."

"We used to be honest at the end of the pier. It was the one place in this town where you talked like yourself. You told me once it was because the water didn't care who you were." She was already walking, and after a moment—because he had never once in his life successfully refused her—he followed.

* * *

The end of the pier was exactly as he remembered and worse. The planks were softer with rot. The railing leaned. Someone had carved initials into the post at the very tip—*S.C. + W.C.*, shallow now, weathered nearly to illegibility, but he found them with his thumb in the dark without having to look, the way you find a scar.

Wren saw him touch it. She didn't say anything. That was somehow worse than if she'd said everything.

"I used to come here," he said, "in Boston. Not piers exactly. Rooftops, bridges, the harborwalk. Anywhere I could stand over water. I told myself it was habit. Then I told myself it was grief. It took me about eight years to admit it was penance. Like if I stood close enough to the edge often enough, it counted for something."

"Did it?"

"No. Penance doesn't count unless someone accepts it. Otherwise it's just performance. Another kind of hiding—I could hide in guilt as easily as I hid in work. Easier, maybe. Guilt feels so much like virtue from the inside."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly swallowed. "God. Ten years and you got *more* articulate about your damage."

"You edited me for six weeks. Some of it stuck."

But she wasn't laughing anymore. She had turned to face him at the rail, and the nearest lantern threw its light across her at an angle that made her look the way she had at twenty-five, at the festival, in the blue dress, the night before everything broke. His chest did something structural and alarming.

"Ask me," she said.

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you've been not-asking since the day you walked into Marguerite's parlor and saw me sitting there. I've watched you carry it around for weeks like a stone in your shoe. Ask me, and I'll answer honestly, and then you owe me the same."

The tide surged beneath them. The whole pier flexed, gently, like something breathing.

"Do you hate me?" he asked. It wasn't the stone in his shoe. The stone was heavier than that. But it was a door he could open without dying.

"Some days," she said. "Mostly I hate the situation. Mostly I hate that you were right about the danger and wrong about the method, and I have to live with both of those facts at once. Mostly I'm furious that the letter was *good*, Sebastian. It was beautiful and honest and it was everything I needed to hear, and it arrived eleven years late in a cardboard box, and I read it standing up in the archive room and then I sat down on the floor among Marguerite's dead correspondence and cried like the building was on fire. So yes. Some days I hate you. Today I mostly just—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "Today I mostly remember what it was like before. And I hate how much I miss it."

There it was. The stone in his shoe, cracked open on the planks between them.

"Wren." His voice came out wrecked. He cleared his throat and it didn't help. "If I kiss you right now, it won't be fair."

"Excuse me?"

"It won't be fair, because I'd be kissing you as a man with an expiration date. In two weeks Marguerite takes that stage and everything I've built comes apart in public—my name, my clients, the quiet careful life I constructed out of small safe steps. And you'd be kissing a man mid-collapse. You'd be choosing me at the worst possible moment, and I would never be able to trust it. I'd spend the rest of my life wondering if you stayed out of pity, or out of anger at your family, or because the confession moved you the way tragedy moves everyone. I need—" He stopped. Made himself say it plainly, because plainness was the one currency he had left. "I need to be whole when you choose me. Or not choose me. Whatever you decide. I won't take your decision while I'm still made of omissions. You deserve someone whose yes doesn't have a body count attached."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and the look went through him like a sounding line dropped into deep water, measuring depths he preferred unmeasured.

"You self-sabotaging, self-flagellating, insufferably well-written fool," she said softly.

Then she kissed him.

Or almost—she rose onto her toes and her mouth was a breath from his, close enough that he felt the words more than heard them, close enough that the last eleven years funneled down to the width of a raindrop—

And he stepped back.

He felt it happen as if from outside himself: his weight shifting, his heel finding the gap between planks, the cold air rushing into the space where her warmth had been. She stumbled slightly, caught herself on the rail, and stared at him with an expression he had no defenses built for—not fury, not hurt, but a kind of devastated clarity, like a lighthouse sweeping over wreckage.

"You didn't just step back," she said. "You *decided* for me again. Right now. On this pier. You decided what I could survive."

"I know."

"You know." She laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard, because there was nothing in it but recognition. "Eleven years, and you've learned nothing except better vocabulary for it."

She pushed off the rail and walked past him, toward the shore, toward the lanterns, and he let her go because stopping her would have been one more decision made on her behalf, and because his hands were shaking too badly to trust them.

At the landward end she stopped. Didn't turn around. The lantern light turned her into a silhouette with a halo she hadn't asked for.

"For the record," she said, loud enough to carry over the tide, "if you'd kissed me, I'd have kissed you back. And I'd have known exactly what I was choosing. I always did. That's the part you never let me prove."

Then she was gone, up the ramp, past the moths and the bulbs, and the pier was just a pier again, rotting politely over the incoming tide.

Sebastian stood at the tip a long time, his thumb resting in the carved initials, watching the water rise. *Someone whole,* he had told her. As if wholeness were a prerequisite instead of a product. As if anyone arrived finished. As if she hadn't just offered him the one thing he'd spent ten years pretending he didn't want: to be seen mid-collapse and chosen anyway.

He'd have to live with the almost. He was good at living with things. It was the only skill his exile had actually taught him.

He turned to go, and that was when he saw it—caught under the railing bolt at the pier's tip, sodden but legible, a page torn from a legal pad in handwriting he recognized as Marguerite Hale's, the ink bleeding but the words sharp enough:

*I know what you burned, Sebastian. We should talk before the festival does.*

* * *

He read it three times under the weakest lantern, the tide climbing the pilings below him like something impatient, and understood that the choice he'd been rehearsing—the slow, dignified, two-week runway toward confession he'd been promising himself—had just been taken out of his hands.

Marguerite knew. And Marguerite, he was beginning to understand, had never once in her long career written a scene she hadn't already decided the ending of.
