## 5. The Torn Page

The ledger lived in the bottom drawer of my father's desk, under a dead flashlight, three rubber bands in various stages of decay, and a photograph of a boat I had never once seen him take out. I'd opened that drawer maybe six times since he died. It was the kind of drawer you open when you're looking for something else—a stamp, a screwdriver, an excuse—and close again quickly, the way you pass a room where someone used to sleep.

I was looking for the orchard deed. Or rather, for anything that explained why the county thought Eli Vance's name belonged on it next to mine. The clerk's letter sat on my kitchen table like a guest who wouldn't leave, and I'd spent two days telling myself there was a clerical error, that somewhere in a beige office a woman named Deb had transposed a name, and that by Friday everything would be beige and orderly again.

Instead I found the ledger.

It was the one he kept for the orchard—sales, pruning costs, the year the cider press ate itself. His handwriting was small and furious, numbers marching like soldiers who hadn't been fed. I flipped through 2014, the year Eli left, mostly to torture myself, which is a hobby of mine.

And there it was. A gap. Not a blank page—an absence with edges. The stub of a page, torn close to the spine, right at the end of October. The month Eli left. The page after it resumed in November with feed invoices, as if nothing had happened, as if October had simply been amputated.

My father was many things—stubborn, secretive, capable of silences that could fossilize—but he was not careless with records. He counted everything. He once itemized what I owed him for a phone call to Boston. A man like that did not tear pages out by accident. A man like that tore pages out the way you pull a tooth: because leaving it in hurts worse.

I sat on the floor of his old study with the ledger in my lap and felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when a house settles, or when your life does.

Here's the thing about suspicion: it's a boomerang. You throw it at someone else and it comes back and hits you in the hand holding it. All week I'd been rehearsing my hatred of Eli Vance—the way you rehearse a speech you'll never give, polishing each grievance until it gleamed. He left. He didn't call. He let me find out I was pregnant from my own body instead of his mouth. Ten years of that, kept behind the counter of the bookshop like a banned book I couldn't stop shelving face-out.

But now a different question was crawling up out of the drawer with the dead flashlight. *Why* did he leave? People don't vanish without a word. Even cowards send texts these days. And my father—who loved me in his locked-box way, who would have burned the town down before letting me be hurt—had torn out exactly one page, in exactly the right month, and then, apparently, gone on living beside me for nine more years without ever saying *Mara, about that boy.*

Unless he wasn't protecting me from Eli.

Unless he was protecting me from something else entirely.

I didn't sleep much. I reconstructed instead. That sounds more forensic than it was; mostly it was me, a pot of tea, and the ghost of my father's arithmetic. Because here's what I found when I stopped staring at the torn edge and started reading the pages around it: the November entries were wrong. Small wrongs. A payment to "R.C." for "consulting." Then another in December. Another in January. Monthly, like rent. Like a subscription to something. The amounts weren't huge—they were the size of a salary, actually, or half of one, the size of what a man might have made if he'd stayed in Salt Hollow and worked for someone.

The size, say, of what a nineteen-year-old might have needed to start over somewhere far away.

I told myself it was coincidence. R.C. could be anyone. Ruth Callahan who sold eggs. Robert Chen who did taxes. I wrote down every R.C. in the phone book from that era, which I still had, because my father threw away nothing except, evidently, single pages of his own ledger. There were eleven. I crossed off four who'd moved away before 2014. Seven remained, and I hated all of them equally and without evidence, which is the purest form of hatred there is.

The last payment was eighteen months ago. Two months before my father died. Whatever he'd been buying, he'd bought it for nearly a decade, and he'd stopped only when he was too sick to keep paying.

I closed the ledger and put my hand flat on the cover, the way you check whether something is still breathing.

"Dad," I said to the empty study, "what did you do?"

The empty study, being empty, said nothing. But the house creaked in its usual place above the stairs, and for one ridiculous second I heard it as an answer.

Wren came home from Delia's at four o'clock smelling of woodsmoke and other people's popcorn, dropped her backpack in the exact center of the kitchen floor—her signature move, a landmine for mothers—and announced, "He was at the hardware store."

I was elbow-deep in dishwater. "Who was?"

"You know who." She got the milk out and drank from the carton, watching me over the rim, which she does specifically because she knows I hate it. "Eli Vance. Mr. Deed. Hal Petrie told him the whole town history in one visit, probably. Hal tells me things too, Mom. I know about the time you put a firecracker in Coach Denny's tackle box."

"That was your aunt, and it was a flare."

"Uh-huh." She set the carton down and looked at me with her father's eyes, which is a sentence I try never to think in daylight. "So. Are we going to talk about it, or are we doing the thing where you alphabetize the poetry section for three hours?"

"The poetry section needs alphabetizing."

"Mom."

"Wren."

She leaned against the counter, thirteen years old and suddenly lawyerly. "Delia says he's staying at the Anchor until the county thing gets sorted. She says he barely talks. She says he tipped like he was apologizing to the money."

"Delia should write a novel."

"Delia says *you* barely talk too. About him. Ever." Wren picked at the label on the milk carton, peeling it in strips, and asked it casually, which is how I knew it wasn't casual at all: "Did you know him? Before?"

The dishwater had gone cold around my wrists. "Everyone knows everyone in Salt Hollow. That's the whole problem with Salt Hollow."

"I mean *know* know." She looked at me straight on. My daughter has never once flinched from eye contact; she inherited that from no one I'll admit to. "Like, before me. Did you know him before me?"

There are questions your child asks that come through the front door wearing ordinary clothes and turn out to be carrying dynamite. I dried my hands very slowly. "Where is this coming from?"

"He looked at me," Wren said. "At the hardware store. Through the window, I mean, I was outside. He looked at me like—" she searched for it, frowning, "—like I was a word he'd forgotten and just remembered. Delia says that's romantic. I think it's creepy. But also—" she shrugged, thirteen going on forty, "—he looked sad about it. Nobody looks *sad* about a stranger."

I could have ended it there. One sentence, motherly and bland: *He's just passing through, sweetheart.* I've built ten years out of sentences like that. They're load-bearing.

But the ledger was upstairs in my bag, and the torn page was in my chest, and some part of me that I didn't fully trust wanted Wren asking these questions—wanted them asked out loud in this kitchen where they'd been silent so long they'd grown mold.

"Yes," I said. "I knew him. Before."

Her eyebrows went up. "How before?"

"High school before. A long time ago."

"Was he your boyfriend?" She pronounced it with the clinical disgust of her generation, but she'd gone very still, the way she goes still when she's fishing.

"For a while."

"And then he left." Not a question. She'd assembled it already, my daughter, from Hal Petrie and Delia and the shape of my silences, the way kids assemble their parents' histories from weather patterns. "Before I was born."

"Wren—"

"It's fine." She peeled another strip off the label, not fine at all. "I'm just saying. He has my chin. I noticed at the funeral—no, wait, he wasn't at the funeral. Grandpa's funeral. Where *was* he?" She looked up sharply, and there it was, the terrible clarity children have, aimed at me like a flashlight. "Mom. Did Grandpa know him?"

The kettle chose that moment to shriek, and I have never been grateful for an appliance before or since.

"Tea?" I said.

"Mom."

"*Tea*, Wren."

She took the tea. She took it with the expression of a person accepting a bribe and noting it in the record. And later, when she'd gone up to do homework she was probably not doing, I stood at the sink and admitted the thing I'd been circling since midnight:

I didn't know which frightened me more—that Eli Vance had taken my father's money and run, in which case the man I'd mourned for a decade had never existed—or that he hadn't, in which case my father had spent nine years paying someone named Corliss to protect me from something so bad he'd rather tear out the page than say it.

Either way, somebody I loved had lied to me for years. Either way, the ledger knew.

I went upstairs, took out my phone, and scrolled to a number I'd sworn off using: Ruth Corliss, who ran the garden center out past the mill road, who'd known my father longer than I'd been alive, and whose initials were R.C.

She picked up on the second ring, cheerful as morning. "Blackwood Orchards! What can I—"

"Ruth," I said. "It's Mara. We need to talk about 2014."

The cheerfulness stopped like a clock.
