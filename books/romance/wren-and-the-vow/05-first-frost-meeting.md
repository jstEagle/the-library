## 5. First Frost Meeting

The county clerk had said the orchard was forty minutes from the records office. It took me two hours, because I stopped twice—once to be sick behind a gas station, once to buy a coffee I had no intention of drinking—and because my hands kept doing something strange to the steering wheel, gripping it like a railing over a long drop.

Twelve years is a long time to rehearse a conversation you'll never have. I'd done it in taxicabs and hotel lobbies, in the blue light of Marguerite's laptop at three in the morning while her voice came out of my fingers wearing someone else's clothes. In every version, Wren Ashby was reasonable. He listened. He understood that a nineteen-year-old girl with a father drowning in debt and creditors who'd already started asking questions about her boyfriend's family's land—that girl hadn't had options. In every version he said *I know, Si. I know.*

In no version did I actually believe it.

The road narrowed past the last strip of town until it was just gravel and frost-killed weeds, and then the trees began. Apple trees, hundreds of them, ranked along the hillside like an audience waiting for someone to say something worth hearing. Frost still clung to the grass in the shade, and the morning light came through it sideways and turned everything briefly, stupidly beautiful.

I parked behind a truck that had seen three decades of weather and sat there with my hands on the wheel, watching a man move through the rows ahead.

He was thinner than the boy I'd left. Harder through the shoulders, the way men get when they've spent years lifting things alone. He wore canvas work gloves and carried pruning shears even though the season for pruning was months gone, and he moved down the row touching each tree as he passed—a hand flat against the trunk, brief, almost formal, like a doctor taking a pulse.

My grandmother's lawyer had told me the orchard was failing. Standing there, I couldn't see it. The trees looked old but alive, patient, the way old things are when somebody keeps showing up for them.

Then I saw the other car.

It was parked on the far shoulder, a silver sedan too clean for this road, and near it stood a man in a fleece vest holding what was unmistakably a surveyor's tripod, talking to a woman with a clipboard. They were walking the orchard's boundary line, driving little stakes into the frozen ground at intervals, and the man in the vest had the particular cheerfulness of someone being paid by the hour.

Wren had stopped moving. He was standing ten rows out, very still, watching them the way you'd watch water rising around your ankles.

I got out of the car before I could talk myself back into it. This was the plan—I had a plan, I always have a plan; it's the only thing I've ever been able to sell. Walk up. Introduce myself as co-owner. Present the situation as a legal formality between strangers. Do not, under any circumstances, let him see that twelve years of silence had cost me anything, because if he saw that, he would know everything else, and everything else would ruin us both.

The frost crunched under my boots like breaking glass. Halfway across the field the surveyor's people noticed me and waved, friendly, assuming I was one of theirs. Wren didn't turn around until I was close enough to count the gray starting at his temples.

"Whatever they're offering," he said, without looking at me, "it isn't enough, and the answer's been no since March."

"I'm not with them."

"Sure." He turned then, and I watched recognition arrive in stages—the way it does, the way it must have arrived for me in the records office, a slow flood finding every low place. His face didn't do what I'd expected. It didn't soften and it didn't harden. It just went carefully, deliberately blank, which was worse, because I recognized the technique. I invented that technique. You keep your face empty so nobody can read what you're actually about to lose.

"Silas," he said.

"Wren."

We stood there. Somewhere behind us the surveyor drove another stake, the mallet striking twice, a small dull heartbeat in all that cold air.

"You look good," I said, because apparently twelve years of rehearsal had produced nothing better than a greeting card.

"Don't." He said it quietly, without heat, and it landed harder than shouting would have. He looked past me at my rental car, at my city coat, at the whole visible inventory of the life I'd built instead of his. "You're here about the deed."

"The lawyer sent letters."

"I got the letters." He pulled off one glove, finger by finger, a man buying time. "I didn't answer them."

"I noticed."

"What do you want, Silas?"

Straight to it. No *how have you been*, no *twelve years, Silas, twelve years*. And God help me, I was grateful, because I'd prepared for anger and I don't know what I'd have done with tenderness. Probably wept. Probably ruined everything right there among the trees.

"There's a sale agreement," I said. "The taxes are three years behind. If neither of us can cover them by end of next season, the county forces the sale, and given who's circling—" I nodded toward the surveyor's stakes "—we both know who buys it."

"I know how liens work."

"Then you know I can't carry half of this alone, and neither can you."

His jaw worked. "I've carried it alone for six years. I was going to keep carrying it."

"And now there are two names on paper, so you don't get to."

That landed. I watched it land. There's a particular cruelty available only to people who used to know each other completely—we know exactly where the soft tissue is, and we aim there without meaning to, or worse, meaning to.

"They should never have put your name on that deed," he said.

"No," I agreed. "They shouldn't have."

Something flickered across his face at that—not satisfaction. Something more wounded, like I'd confirmed a suspicion he'd been carrying a long time. "Your grandmother knew what she was doing," he said. "Putting us both on it. She always thought she could fix things after the fact with paperwork."

*She always thought she could fix things.* I filed that away, the way I file everything, some ghostwriter's reflex: *note tone, note grievance, deploy later.* Then hated myself for filing it.

"Look," I said. "I'm not here to take anything from you. I'm here because legally I don't have a choice. We share this. For one season. Then we sign whatever has to be signed and we're done."

"One season."

"One season. I'll pay my half of the taxes. I'll work. I don't know anything about apples, but I learn fast—it's practically my entire skill set."

He almost smiled at that. Almost. It died somewhere in transit. "And then?"

"Then we sell, if it comes to that. Together. Nobody gets ambushed."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I understood that whatever he was deciding, it wasn't really about the orchard. It was about whether he could stand to have me inside his days again, even at arm's length, even hostile. Anger, I realized later, was the one thing we could still safely give each other. Everything softer was radioactive.

"Fine," he said finally. "Rules."

Of course there were rules. There were always going to be rules; Wren Ashby had been building fences his whole life.

"One," he said, counting on gloved fingers. "You stay in the farmhouse. I stay in the cottage. We don't share walls." Two. "You want to know something about the orchard, you ask me, not the internet, not the lawyer, not—" a glance at the surveyors "—them." Three. "When the season ends, we settle it and you go back to wherever you've been, and we don't do this again."

"That's it? No rule against talking about the past?"

The blankness came down over his face again, deliberate as a shutter. "There's nothing to talk about. You left. That's not a conversation, that's a fact."

The stake-driver struck twice behind us. A crow went up off the far row, complaining.

"Okay," I said, and heard my own voice come out level, professional, dead. Twelve years of practice, right there. "Okay. One season."

He stuck out his hand. Bare now, the glove stuffed in his pocket, and I took it, and his grip was exactly as I remembered—warm, careful, a handshake from a man who'd learned early that hands could break things—and we shook on it like strangers closing a deal, and if either of us held on a half-second longer than the contract required, neither of us mentioned it.

"I'll drive you up," he said. "Show you the house."

"It's my house too."

"Yeah," he said, turning toward the truck. "That's the problem."

* * *

She looked different and she looked exactly the same, and I hated her for both.

That's the honest version. I've had all afternoon to draft a politer version and I'm throwing it out, because the politer version is how we got here—everybody softening the truth until it fits through the keyhole of what won't hurt anybody, and then everybody hurt anyway.

She looked different: shorter than I remembered, though I knew that was wrong, she'd always been short; it was me who'd grown around the absence. Her hair was darker, cut sharp, city-sharp. She wore a coat that cost more than my truck and she wore it like armor, buttoned to the throat in thirty-degree weather, and when she took my hand her palm was dry and steady and I thought: *she's practiced this.* Whatever this meeting was in her head, she'd run it a hundred times. I know what rehearsed looks like. I rehearse conversations with dead trees.

She looked exactly the same: the way she watched a person's face while pretending to watch the middle distance. The way she answered questions with logistics. Ask Silas Okonkwo how she feels and she'll tell you the tax schedule. I used to find that funny. Twelve years ago I found it charming, this girl who could turn any feeling into a filing system. Then she filed herself out of my life overnight—no note, no call, just gone, and her father's truck sold, and the house dark—and I learned that the filing system was load-bearing. It was how she kept from collapsing. It was also how she kept everyone, including me, from knowing where anything important was stored.

The surveyor people were still staking the east boundary when she walked up. I'd been ignoring them for an hour on principle. Hollis Development had been sending letters since spring, each one thicker and more polite, and now they'd graduated to sending actual humans onto my—onto *our*—land, driving stakes like little flags of conquest. I'd have run them off except my grandmother raised me to be civil to people just doing their jobs, and besides, part of me wanted to see how far they'd walk before somebody made them stop.

Then Silas said *I'm not with them,* and the ground did what ground does.

Here's what nobody tells you about twelve years: you build a person out of memory, and the memory stops updating, and eventually the person in your head is a fiction you maintain out of stubbornness. I'd maintained mine well. Nineteen years old, laugh like she was getting away with something, apple juice on her chin every September, always halfway out the door of anywhere she stood. And then the real one walked across my field in a thousand-dollar coat and said *I learn fast—it's practically my entire skill set,* and the fiction and the woman overlapped for one unbearable second, and I understood that I was in genuine danger.

Not of her. Of myself. Of the part of me that had spent six years keeping this place alive partly out of love for my grandmother and partly out of spite, and had told itself the spite was finished. Spite doesn't finish. Spite just waits for the person it's addressed to.

So I gave her rules. Three of them, clean and defensible, the kind of rules a reasonable man sets for a business arrangement. Stay in the farmhouse. Ask me, not the internet. When the season ends, we're done. All sensible. None of them the real rule, which was: *do not let her back in, because the last time she left, it took me four years to stop setting two cups out by habit, and I am not doing that arithmetic again.*

She agreed to all of it too fast. That's another thing about Silas—she agrees fast and decides slow, and by the time you notice she's decided something, she's already gone.

I drove her up to the farmhouse with the windows cracked because the cab smelled like her perfume and I needed air. She looked at the house the way you look at a photograph of yourself from before—recognizing it, auditing it. Paint peeling on the north gable. Porch step I keep meaning to fix. My grandmother's wind chime still hanging by the door, mute in the cold.

"You've kept it up," she said.

"Somebody had to."

It came out with an edge I hadn't planned. She flinched—actually flinched, small, quickly controlled—and I felt the edge of it turn back on me, because I hadn't wanted to wound her. I'd wanted to wound the version of her that left. They wear the same coat.

Inside, she walked the rooms with her hands in her pockets, touching nothing. Stopped at the kitchen window where my grandmother's geraniums used to sit. "It's smaller than I remember," she said.

"Everything is."

She turned and looked at me then, straight on, and for a second the filing system glitched and I saw something underneath—something that looked, absurdly, like fear. "Wren," she started, and I braced, because that tone was the opening move of a speech I'd imagined a thousand times, the speech where she explains—

Her phone rang. Of course it did. She glanced at the screen and something shut in her face, quick as a trap, and she said, "I have to take this," and stepped out onto the porch, and through the window I watched her pace the length of it with her free hand pressed over her eyes, saying very little, listening very hard, to whoever owned enough of her time to interrupt this.

I stood in my grandmother's kitchen with the kettle I hadn't filled and thought: *one season.* Two hundred and eighty days of sharing an orchard with a woman who takes calls she hides, who wears grief like a coat she's decided not to mention, who came back after twelve years with a lawyer's letter and a rehearsed smile and called it a property matter.

Fine. I could do one season standing on my head. I'd survived worse than her leaving. I'd survived her staying gone.

Outside, she ended the call and stood a moment longer on the porch, looking out at the trees, at the frost burning off the rows, at the whole stubborn living green of the place the developers had measured for death. Then she came back inside, and the smile she put on was flawless, and that was the thing that scared me most—because I'd known her at nineteen, before she learned to make it flawless, and I was probably the only person left alive who could tell the difference.

"Dinner?" she said. "I mean—I can cook. I assume there's a stove."

"There's a stove."

"Then I'll make dinner. Consider it rent."

I should have said no. I had a rule ready, rule number four, unwritten: *no meals, no firesides, nothing that looks like a life.* Instead I heard myself say, "There's cider in the cellar. Pressed it in October," and watched relief move through her like weather, and understood that I'd already broken my own rule, on day one, before dinner, over a bottle of cider.

One season. Two hundred and eighty days.

I went out to split wood I didn't need split, and behind me the farmhouse lit up yellow window by window, like something being carefully, dangerously, rekindled.
