## 6. Terms of Coexistence

I'd like to say I slept on the decision, weighed it like a man of sense, and came to the kitchen table in the morning with a clear head. What I actually did was lie awake until two in the morning cataloguing every reason to sell, then get up at dawn and catalog every reason not to, then drink coffee standing at the window watching her rental car sit in the frost like something that had wandered in from a different story and couldn't find its way out.

She'd slept in the farmhouse. My farmhouse. Our farmhouse, the deed said, and I was learning to hate that small conjunction.

She came down at seven, wearing yesterday's clothes, hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil, and looked around the kitchen the way a person looks at a museum exhibit of a life they once considered living.

"There's coffee," I said. "It's terrible."

"I've had worse."

"You haven't. I make it in a percolator older than both of us."

"Then it's earned its bitterness." She took the cup I poured, wrapped both hands around it, and sat down across the table from me like we were about to negotiate a hostage release. Which, in a sense, we were. I just hadn't decided yet who was the hostage.

"Here's how I see it," she said.

"I haven't agreed to see it yet."

"Silas." She said my name the way you'd set down a heavy box — carefully, and with visible relief at being done with it. "The taxes. I read the notices in the drawer by the phone. You're behind two years. The county's talking about a lien by spring, and the developer's letter is sitting right there on top of the breadbox like it lives here."

I moved the letter. Petty, but a man takes his victories where he finds them.

"So you've been reading my mail."

"I've been reading *our* mail," she said, and had the grace to wince at it. "That's the problem, isn't it. Everything here is ours now, whether we like it or not. Two names on paper."

"Paper can be changed."

"Paper can be changed," she agreed. "So let's change it. Or let's not. But either way, we should do it deliberately, not by you sulking behind the cider house until I give up and drive back to the city."

"I wasn't sulking."

"Wren." She looked at me over the rim of the cup. "You were sulking with a ladder. I watched you from the window. You pruned the same tree for forty minutes."

That tree had deserved forty minutes. I said so. She raised an eyebrow with the precision of a surgeon, and I understood that twelve years hadn't softened her, they'd just given her better instruments.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about a truce: it has to be built out of something, and all we had on hand was resentment and a shared mortgage-shaped problem. So we built with that.

We sat at the table with the tax notices spread between us like a losing poker hand, and I laid out my terms. I'd thought about them all night, and I want it noted that I thought of them as *terms of separation*, not terms of coexistence. A man tells himself what he needs to.

"One season," I said. "We run the orchard through the harvest. If the crop and whatever else we can scrape together covers the tax bill, we keep it. If it doesn't —"

"We both sign the sale," she finished. "Both names. No one sells out from under the other."

"That's the first term."

"And the second?"

I'd made a list. I'm not ashamed of the list. Well. I'm somewhat ashamed of the list.

"Term two," I said. "You don't touch my trees without asking. Term three, the farmhouse kitchen is mine until eight in the morning. Term four —" I hesitated, and she noticed, because she notices everything, which is the most exhausting thing about her — "term four, we don't talk about why you left. Not this season. There's work to do and I can't do it and also have that conversation."

The kitchen went very quiet. The percolator ticked as it cooled, counting out the silence.

"That's not a term," she said finally. "That's a wall."

"It's my wall. On my land."

"Half your land."

"Then it's half my wall, and I'm exercising my half." I gathered the tax notices into a stack and squared the edges against the table, which is what I do instead of shouting. "Twelve years, Silas. You don't get to walk back in and have that conversation on your schedule because the paperwork forced the issue. If we're going to be partners, we're going to be *partners*. Apples, taxes, frost dates. That's the whole country we share. You keep your borders and I'll keep mine."

She studied me for a long moment. I watched her decide not to fight it, and I watched what it cost her, and I want to be honest — some mean, small part of me was glad it cost her. That's the thing about armor. It doesn't just keep people out. It keeps you in, in all the worst rooms of yourself.

"Fine," she said. "Term five, then. I get the kitchen after eight."

"You get the kitchen after eight."

"Term six. We eat dinner together. Working partners eat. You look like you've been living on cider and spite."

"Spite has excellent caloric content."

"*Wren.*"

"Dinner," I said. "Fine. Term seven — you stay out of the west field. The Gravensteins in there are my grandmother's and they're finicky, and they don't need a stranger's opinions."

"Any more terms, or is that the whole treaty?"

"That's the whole treaty." I got up to rinse my cup, because the conversation had gotten to the temperature where standing up is easier than sitting in it. Behind me, I heard her say, quietly:

"You know I'd have agreed to any terms. Even the ones that are just walls."

I knew it. That was the problem with her. She'd always been the kind of person who'd agree to anything if she thought she deserved it, and I'd spent twelve years wondering what she thought she deserved, and I'd built a whole life on not asking.

* * *

The first week of the truce went like this:

She learned where everything was. The good shears, the bad shears, the ladder with the cracked rung that I'd been meaning to fix since before she left and now never would, out of some stubborn principle I can't explain. She kept the kitchen after eight like it was a lease clause, and I'd come in at eight-oh-five to find the table wiped down and a pot of coffee that was, infuriatingly, better than mine. She read my grandmother's orchard journal — I allowed it, since half of it was now legally her problem too — and started making notes in the margins in a small, exact hand.

She asked questions. That was the worst part. Not about us — she kept term four, scrupulously, so scrupulously it was its own kind of loud. About the trees. When to thin, how to read a leaf, why the Gravensteins sulked if you looked at them wrong. She wrote my answers down. She'd been a professional listener for twelve years — I learned that later, the shape of what she actually did for a living, and understood that I'd been dating a ghostwriter the whole time without knowing there was a ghost — and she brought that whole attention to bear on my apple trees like they were a memoir worth getting right.

I caught myself enjoying it. That was the second worst part.

* * *

The frost came early that year. I'd been saying so for a week, watching the sky do that flat, pale thing it does in October when it's deciding whether to be kind. On the third Thursday of the month it stopped deciding.

I was up before five, checking the thermometer nailed to the cider house — thirty-one and falling — and doing the arithmetic no orchard man wants to do. A hard frost this early, with the late blocks still hanging, could eat a third of what was left of our season. Our season. I was already saying *our* in my head and hating it.

I got the smudge pots out. There's a whole ritual to it — position, fuel, the particular way you light them so they catch instead of just smoking like a bad temper — and I was three rows in, hands black, eyes streaming, when I heard the farmhouse door and saw a flashlight bobbing down the row toward me.

"You're up," I said, which was stupid, since she was visibly up, wearing sneakers on frozen ground and a cardigan that wouldn't have kept a scarecrow warm.

"I saw the temperature from the window. What do you need?"

"You need to go back inside. This is two-person work and the second person is me."

"Then I'm the third person, and I'm free."

"Silas —"

"Tell me what to do or I'll start improvising, and you've seen my pruning."

She had a point. I'd seen her pruning. I put a pot in her hands and showed her the trick of the wick, and she knelt in the frost in that useless cardigan and lit them one after another down the row, and didn't complain once, and I was so busy not noticing how that looked — the small flames marching away into the dark, her breath hanging silver, the whole orchard suddenly populated instead of empty — that I didn't notice the shivering until her hands started missing the wicks.

"Your hands are shaking."

"It's cold."

"It's *freezing*, that's the entire point of tonight." I stood there holding a smudge pot and had a brief, vicious internal argument. Term two was about trees. Term four was about the past. There was no term against what I was about to do, which was either common decency or the first breach in my own wall, and I honestly couldn't tell which from the inside.

"Stay here," I said, and walked back to the farmhouse, and took my grandmother's coat off the hook by the door.

It's a heavy thing, wool and corduroy, patched at both elbows, smelling of woodsmoke and the particular ghost of her. I hadn't let anyone wear it. I hadn't let anyone touch it. There's a short list of things I kept of hers — the coat, the deed, the journal, a way of talking to trees as if they were difficult relatives — and I had built the same walls around all of them.

I carried it back down the row. She looked at it, then at me, and I saw her understand exactly what it was, because she'd known my grandmother, back when. Back when. Twelve years and the phrase still worked like a trapdoor.

"Put it on," I said. "You're no good to the harvest with hypothermia, and I'm not carrying you in. You'd note it in the margins of something."

She put it on. It swallowed her — my grandmother had been a broad woman with strong opinions about weather, and Silas disappeared into the coat like a letter into an envelope. She pulled the collar up and stood there in the smudge-pot light, and something in my chest did a thing I'm not going to describe, because this is a story about apples and taxes and I'm sticking to that.

"Thank you," she said.

"It's a practical decision."

"Of course."

"Term eight," I said. "You give it back when the frost lifts."

"Term eight," she agreed. "And term nine: thank you anyway."

I went back to my pots. We worked the rest of the night in a silence that had changed shape — not the armed silence of the treaty table but something with actual warmth in it, dangerous warmth, the kind that gets into a wall and rots the mortar. The frost burned silver on the grass. The little fires breathed. Somewhere in the dark the late blocks hung heavy with fruit we'd both, apparently, decided to save.

Near dawn the thermometer came back up to thirty-four and the sky went the color of a bruise healing, and we stood at the end of the last row looking at what we'd done.

"Saved," she said.

"Mostly."

"Mostly saved." She was still wearing the coat. She had her hands in its pockets, which felt like a violation of term eight that hadn't technically occurred yet, and she was looking at the orchard with an expression I recognized and resented, because it was the expression of someone falling for a place.

"The Gravensteins," she said. "The west field. The finicky ones."

"What about them."

"They'll need pruning before real cold. Won't they."

I looked at her. She looked back, entirely too innocent, a woman in a dead woman's coat proposing to violate term seven with a straight face.

"That's my field," I said.

"That's half your field. And I read the journal. There's a whole section on the Gravensteins. Your grandmother had, and I quote, 'a system.'"

"She did have a system."

"I want to learn the system."

I should have said no. I had a list of reasons as long as my arm and every one of them was term-shaped. But the frost was lifting, and she was standing in my grandmother's coat with her hands in its pockets and frost melting in her hair, and the wall I'd built had a hole in it the exact size of a wool coat, and I heard myself say:

"Tomorrow. Bring the bad shears. You're not touching the good ones till you've earned them."

She smiled. It was the first real smile she'd given me in twelve years, and it went through the mortar like water.

"Tomorrow," she said.

I turned back toward the cider house before she could see my face, because I was fairly sure it was doing something that violated every term on the list.

I was almost to the door when she called after me. "Wren. The coat — I'll return it. Term eight. I just — it's warm."

"Keep it till the frost lifts," I said, without turning around. "That was the term."

The frost, I noted with a kind of dread, wasn't forecast to lift for a week.
