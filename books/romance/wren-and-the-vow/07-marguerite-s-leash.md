## 7. Marguerite's Leash

The phone rang at 6:47 in the morning, which in Marguerite Vale's world was practically a courtesy.

I knew it was her before I looked. Nobody else called me before eight. Nobody else *could* — my number was unlisted, my existence deliberately frictionless, a woman built like a service entrance. The screen said UNKNOWN CALLER, and I thought, with the flat clarity of someone who has not yet had coffee: here it comes anyway.

I answered on the fourth ring, which is to say I let her wait just long enough to feel powerful and not so long that she'd escalate.

"Silas." Marguerite's voice came through warm as butter left out too long. "You're awake. Of course you're awake. You're always awake. That's what I've always admired about you."

"It's not seven yet."

"Which is why I'm calling now, darling, instead of at six." A pause with the texture of silk dragged over gravel. "How is the country? I hear you've been playing farmer."

My hand went cold around the phone. I hadn't told her about the orchard. I hadn't told anyone. I'd told the county records office, a lawyer in Kingston who billed by the syllable, and a man whose face I'd spent twelve years not thinking about, and none of them were in the habit of reporting to Marguerite Vale.

"I'm settling my grandmother's estate," I said. "It's not interesting."

"Nothing about you has ever been interesting, Silas. That's rather the point of you." She laughed, a small bright sound designed for rooms full of people who mattered. "But estates are interesting. Land is interesting. Land with *liens* on it, especially."

There it was. Not a guess. A fact, delivered with the casual cruelty of a woman who paid people to know things.

"What do you want, Marguerite?"

"To talk! Is that so suspicious? Two colleagues, catching up." In the background I heard ice shift in a glass, though it was barely seven her time, wherever she was — probably still California, where she'd decamped after the memoir tour, or possibly a hotel in Paris, since Marguerite treated geography as a wardrobe. "The book did beautifully, you know. Number one for nine weeks. The *Times* called it 'devastatingly honest.' Devastatingly honest." She let the phrase sit there between us like a cat she'd decided to keep. "And every word of it true, of course. Well. Every word of it *written*."

I sat down on the edge of the bed in the farmhouse, because my legs had opinions of their own. Through the window the orchard lay gray under a sky the color of dishwater, first frost silvering the grass between the rows. Somewhere out there Wren was already up — he was always already up — doing whatever it was he did to trees before dawn, and I was sitting in his grandmother's house holding a phone that felt like it had been dipped in ice water.

"Marguerite," I said carefully, "if this is about the acknowledgments—"

"This is about *clause fourteen*."

I closed my eyes. I could see it without opening the contract, which I had signed eleven years ago, in a conference room with bad lighting and better champagne: the standard work-for-hire language, the confidentiality provisions, the non-disparagement clause that read like a prenup drafted by someone who'd been burned. And buried in the middle, in type small enough to require faith rather than reading glasses, clause fourteen.

*First refusal.* Any original work of authorship created by the undersigned during the term of this agreement and for a period of twenty-four months following its termination or expiration shall be offered first to Vale House Publishing—

"The agreement terminated in March," I said.

"And March was," Marguerite said pleasantly, "twenty months ago. Do the arithmetic, darling. You always were better at it than me. That's why I kept you."

Twenty months. Which meant four. Four months until the leash came off, if it ever came off at all — because clause fourteen didn't expire quietly, it required notice, and notice required a counterparty, and the counterparty was a publishing house that Marguerite had spent thirty years making sure owed her everything.

"You can't claim a novel I haven't written," I said. "Clause fourteen covers works *created during* the term—"

"Created, commenced, or *contemplated*." I could hear her smiling. I could see it too — the way she smiled in photographs, the smile that made three generations of women feel flattered and four generations feel managed. "Contemplated is such a lovely word, isn't it? So roomy. And you, Silas. You've been contemplating something. I can always tell. You get this little line right here—" I imagined her touching the space between her own brows, the gesture she used on stage when she wanted an audience to feel seen, "—when you're writing something that belongs to you. You had it the whole last month of the memoir. I assumed it was guilt."

The frost on the window had begun to burn off, the orchard emerging from white into the bruised green of late October. Down the hill, faintly, I could hear the cough of Wren's truck turning over. He was up. He was always up. And I was sitting in his grandmother's kitchen learning that the woman I'd spent a decade serving had been keeping a file on me the whole time.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "If you have me leashed, why call and warn me?"

"Because I'm *generous*, Silas. Everyone says so. Ask anyone." Ice again, glass set down on wood. "And because I'd rather you heard it from me than from a lawyer with a subpoena. Here's my offer, and do listen, because I'll only make it once. You come back. One more book. Your best work — and we both know what your best work looks like, don't we, even if the world never will. Two years, standard terms, and in exchange I shred clause fourteen and we part friends. You go back to being invisible, which is what you're *good* at, and I go back to being brilliant, which is what I'm good at, and everyone goes home happy."

"And if I say no?"

Another pause. This one longer, and colder, and I understood suddenly that the warmth had been the performance and this — this patient, silken waiting — was the woman underneath.

"If you say no," Marguerite said, "then I suppose I'd have to enforce it. And enforcement is so *public*, darling. Depositions. Discovery. All those lovely drafts of mine in your handwriting, entered into the record. The world finding out that Marguerite Vale's devastating honesty was ghosted by a woman who won't even put her name on a grocery list." A soft laugh. "And then there's the land. Liens are such funny things. They attach to property, and property attaches to *people*, and people — well. People have pasts. I wonder what a title search would turn up about yours."

She knew. Not everything — not yet — but enough. Enough to find the rest. Marguerite Vale had built a career on other people's secrets the way other writers built careers on plot, and somewhere in her files there was a folder with my name on it, growing thicker by the week.

"I need to think about it," I said.

"Of course you do. Thinking is free. Everything else has a price." The glass lifted again; ice rang against crystal like a small bell tolling. "You have until the end of the week, Silas. Don't be dramatic. You never were any good at drama — that was always my department."

She hung up without saying goodbye. Marguerite never said goodbye; goodbyes implied endings, and endings implied that other people had the power to leave.

I sat in the farmhouse kitchen for a long time after the call ended, the phone dark in my hand, watching the frost finish its retreat off the window. The orchard stood in rows below, bare-limbed and patient, and beyond it the road where Wren's truck had gone, and beyond that the whole rest of the world, which apparently had never lost track of me at all.

I had thought invisibility was armor. Eleven years of it — no byline, no photo, no name on anything but bank deposits and legal boilerplate. I'd told myself it was strategy, discipline, the price of craft. Sitting there in my grandmother's kitchen with Marguerite's voice still ringing in my ears, I finally understood what it actually was.

A cage. Mine, custom-built, and I'd handed Marguerite the key years ago and thanked her for the privilege.

Because here was the thing about clause fourteen, the thing I'd signed without reading closely, the thing I'd let slide through eleven years of contracts like sand through fingers: it didn't just give Vale House first claim on books I wrote. It gave Marguerite first claim on *me*. On any future self I might have tried to become. I had spent a decade making myself nothing so thoroughly that when I finally wanted to be someone — when I sat down at night in this farmhouse with the draft of something that was actually mine glowing on the screen — the law itself rose up to say: *no. That belongs to her too.*

Even my escape had her name on it.

I got up and made coffee I didn't want, because my hands needed a job. The kettle rattled. Outside, the frost was gone entirely now, and the light came up hard and clear across the orchard, and I saw Wren's truck coming back up the road, and I turned away from the window before he could see me standing there.

Four months. That's what I had. Four months until the leash technically loosened — except it wouldn't loosen, would it, because Marguerite didn't offer deals she intended to honor. The offer wasn't an offer. It was a demonstration. Look what I can do to you. Look how far my reach extends. Even here, even in the middle of nowhere, even in the one place you thought was yours.

And underneath the dread, quieter and worse, another thought, the kind that arrives uninvited and stays:

*What would Wren think?*

Not about the clause — Wren didn't live in the world of clauses. About the rest of it. The eleven years. The bylines that weren't mine. The woman he'd loved, who turned out to be a professional ghost, a person who had made an entire career of being exactly the kind of absence she'd once been to him. He'd asked me once, twelve years ago, what I wanted to write, if I ever got to choose. I'd laughed and said I'd know it when I found it.

I'd found it. It was sitting in a folder on my laptop titled *Untitled*, and it belonged to Marguerite Vale, and I had until Friday to decide whether to hand it over or watch her take it apart in public, piece by piece, along with the orchard, along with whatever fragile thing was growing between me and the man down the hill who still wouldn't look at me directly.

I poured the coffee out. It had gone bitter while I wasn't drinking it.

Then I opened my laptop, and I looked at the folder marked *Untitled*, and I did the only brave thing available to a coward at seven in the morning.

I renamed it.

*Wren.*

One word. My name on nothing yet, but his on something. If Marguerite wanted to claim everything I wrote, fine. Let her try to claim this. Let her come to the orchard, to the farmhouse, to the kitchen table where I'd written it, and explain to a judge how a book about a man who stayed belonged to a woman who couldn't bear to be known.

Outside, the frost had burned off completely, and the day had begun whether I was ready for it or not.

Down the hill, the sound of the tractor started up, and I closed the laptop, and I went to get dressed, because the trees weren't going to prune themselves — and neither, it turned out, was I.

* * *

Marguerite called again Thursday.

This time I let it ring. I watched the phone buzz itself silent on the kitchen table, next to the tax documents and the sale agreement and the cup of coffee I was actually drinking, and when it stopped I felt something I hadn't expected: not relief. *Defiance*. Small, stupid, premature defiance, the emotional equivalent of a match struck in a rainstorm, but mine.

She left a voicemail. I listened to it twice, because the first time I was too busy hating her to absorb the content.

"Darling. Discretion was always your finest quality; I'm sorry to see it failing. Friday, Silas. End of business. After that I stop being generous and start being thorough. And do give my regards to Mr. Ashby — I understand he's handsome in a rustic way. Photographs are so much more reliable than memory, aren't they?"

Photographs. She had photographs. Which meant she had someone, which meant she'd had someone for longer than a week, which meant the folder with my name on it had been growing since long before the lawyer's letter arrived in Manhattan. Since maybe before I even knew the orchard existed. Marguerite Vale did not stumble into information; she commissioned it, the way she commissioned everything else, including me.

I deleted the voicemail. Then I sat very still and did the math I'd been avoiding all week.

Option one: sign. Go back to New York, write one more masterpiece under her name, collect the shredding of clause fourteen like a ransom receipt, and spend the rest of my life knowing I'd traded the orchard and Wren and the folder marked *Wren* for continued safety. Continued nothingness. The cage, repainted.

Option two: refuse. Watch her enforce. Depositions, discovery, the drafts in my handwriting entered into the record — eleven years of my best work dragged into daylight under someone else's name, and me exposed as the invisible woman, the ghost, the fraud. And the liens, the title search, whatever trap she'd hinted at around the orchard. She'd take everything, and she'd do it smiling, and the worst part — the genuinely unbearable part — was that some large fraction of the world would side with *her*. Marguerite Vale, national treasure. Me, a footnote with a law degree's worth of liability.

There was supposed to be an option three. There's always supposed to be an option three. Publish independently, expose the clause, rally public sympathy — the fantasy every ghostwriter nurses at three in the morning. But fantasies require leverage, and leverage requires a name, and a name was precisely the thing I'd spent eleven years not having.

Except.

I looked at the laptop. At the folder I'd renamed five days ago and hadn't opened since, because opening it meant admitting it existed, and admitting it existed meant admitting I intended to fight.

Eighty pages. Rough, uneven, half of it written in the margins of nights when I couldn't sleep for wanting a man who wouldn't speak to me. Eighty pages about an orchard and a vow and a woman who learned too late that silence is also a choice. It wasn't finished. It might never be finished. But it was *true*, and truth was the one currency Marguerite had been trading in counterfeit for thirty years.

And I thought: she keeps saying *enforcement is public*. As if publicity were a threat.

As if I hadn't spent my whole life wishing someone would look.

I picked up the phone and called the lawyer in Kingston, the one who billed by the syllable.

"How solid is clause fourteen," I asked him, "if the work in question was created substantially after termination, in a different state, on equipment she never paid for?"

A pause, the sound of billing. "Define *substantially*."

"Every word."

Another pause. Longer. When he spoke again, I could hear him trying not to sound interested, which from him was the equivalent of a war whoop. "Send me the contract. Send me the metadata. And Ms. Okonkwo—" a breath, the sound of a man deciding to say something he'd normally charge extra for, "—who else knows about this book?"

"No one," I said.

"Keep it that way," he said, "until I tell you otherwise."

I hung up and sat in the quiet kitchen with my heart going like a bird in a box. Outside, the afternoon light was going gold and low across the orchard, and somewhere down the hill Wren was doing whatever Wren did at the end of a working day, and he didn't know yet that his name was in a folder on a lawyer's desk, that the woman he could barely stand to look at had written eighty pages about him and named the file after him and was now, God help me, preparing to commit literature.

Friday came. End of business came. My phone stayed silent, which frightened me more than ringing would have, because Marguerite Vale never went quiet — she went *strategic*.

On Saturday morning I walked down to the orchard to tell Wren something — I still don't know what; the truth, probably, or the shape of it — and found him standing at the end of the row nearest the road, holding an envelope with a developer's letterhead, his face unreadable as winter.

He looked up. Saw me coming. And put the envelope behind his back.

That's when I understood the week had only been the beginning, and that Marguerite's leash had more than one collar in it — and that some of them, the ones that mattered most, we were fastening on each other.
