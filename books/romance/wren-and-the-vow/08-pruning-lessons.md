## 8. Pruning Lessons

There is a particular way Silas Okonkwo holds a pair of pruning shears that tells you everything you need to know about her: like the shears might bite her, or like she might bite them, or like both of them are waiting for the other to make the first mistake.

I'd been watching her from across the row for ten minutes before I said anything, which was nine minutes longer than my grandmother would have tolerated.

"You're strangling it," I said.

She didn't look up. "I'm holding it."

"You're strangling it. The tree can't feel anything through your hands, but if it could, it would be filing a complaint."

She set her jaw in a way I remembered from twelve years ago and had spent twelve years trying to forget — the jaw of a woman who has decided to be right about something before anyone's told her what. "You said cut at an angle above the bud."

"I said *above* the bud. You're cutting at the bud. There's a difference between giving something room to grow and taking its head off."

"Those seem like the same operation from where I'm standing."

"They're not." I crossed the row, because apparently I was going to do this, whatever this was. "Here." I held out my hand for the shears.

She hesitated. Of course she hesitated. Then she handed them over handle-first, which was either courtesy or surrender, and with Silas it was usually impossible to tell the difference, and usually both.

* * *

Let me back up, because I can hear you asking how we got here, a man teaching his co-owner — his abandoned co-owner, his ghost, his twelve-year-old wound with legs — how to prune apples on a gray Tuesday in early spring.

The answer is: badly, and on purpose.

The truce we'd signed in the kitchen three weeks ago was four sentences long and legally meaningless, but it had one clause that mattered: *both parties will work the season*. Her lawyer wrote it. My lawyer — okay, fine, the same lawyer, old Hargrove, who charges us both and presumably laughs all the way to the bank — read it aloud like scripture. Silas signed. I signed. And then I discovered that a woman who writes books for a living does not know what to do with her hands when there's no keyboard under them.

She'd been trying anyway. That was the thing I hadn't expected. I'd expected Manhattan, weekends only, a woman who showed up in impractical shoes and took photographs of herself among the trees for some audience I couldn't imagine. Instead she showed up Monday morning in boots that had clearly been bought new and already broken in by someone who knew what breaking in meant, and she asked me where to start.

So I put her on the Macintosh row, because the Macs are forgiving, and I said prune the dead wood, and I walked away, and then I came back and found her strangling a perfectly healthy water sprout like it owed her money.

Which brings us to now. To my hand closing over hers on the shears.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of the season whether I like it or not: I did not need to guide her hands. I could have demonstrated on the next branch. I could have talked her through it. There were approximately forty ways to teach a woman to prune an apple tree without touching her, and I chose the one way that involved my palm against the back of her hand and her breath going very slightly uneven under mine.

I'm not proud of it. I'm also not sorry. A man can hold two things at once; it turns out that's mostly what a man is.

"Angle," I said, and my voice came out lower than I intended, so I cleared my throat and tried again. "Forty-five degrees. Away from the bud. The water runs off, the bud doesn't rot. It's not complicated."

"Nothing's complicated," she said, "until someone explains it."

The cut went clean. Even I had to admit it — the kind of cut that heals in a season, callus rolling over neat as a stitched wound. She looked at it the way she looked at everything, like she was taking dictation.

"Again," I said, and didn't move my hand.

* * *

We worked the row like that for an hour. Not touching, after the first few cuts — I made myself step back, made myself stand with my hands in my pockets like a man with no interest whatsoever in the back of anybody's hand — but close. Closer than the rules allowed. My rules. Rule three, specifically, drafted by me, for me, in the first week: *no unnecessary proximity.* I'd written it down, which should tell you something about how necessary I considered it.

Rule one was *no questions about the past.* Rule two was *no meals together longer than twenty minutes.* Rule four was *she leaves when the papers are signed and you don't watch her go.*

I had a lot of rules. Men who've been left once tend to build a lot of fences afterward, and mine were load-bearing.

"Tell me what I'm looking for," she said, standing back from the tree, shears lowered, actually asking instead of assuming. That was new. Twelve years ago Silas Okonkwo never asked directions from anyone, including God, including me, especially me.

"Dead wood," I said. "Crossing branches. Anything growing inward."

"Inward?"

"A tree wants light more than it wants size. Everything that grows toward the middle is choking something else out. You take it off even if it looks healthy. Especially if it looks healthy."

She was quiet a moment. "That sounds like a metaphor."

"It's an orchard."

"It's a metaphor wearing an orchard costume."

"Everything's a metaphor if you're a writer."

"Everything's a *tree* if you're a farmer," she shot back, but there was no edge on it. That was the strange new weather between us — the hostility hadn't gone, exactly, but it had gone seasonal. Frost in the mornings, thaw by afternoon. I kept checking myself for signs of hope and finding them, and hating them, and keeping them anyway, the way you keep a stray that showed up in a storm.

She cut a crossing limb. Clean angle, right distance from the collar. I felt something in my chest do a thing I refused to name.

"Huh," she said.

"Huh what?"

"It's satisfying. Nobody told me it would be satisfying. You just — decide what the tree gets to keep."

"That's the job," I said. "Deciding what gets to keep growing."

And then, because I am apparently a man who steps on his own rakes, I heard myself say, almost gently, "You used to be good at that too."

The shears stopped.

I stood there in the cold with my stupid mouth open, watching twelve years of rule number one lie broken on the ground between us like a pruned limb. Because it was true, is the thing. She used to be ruthless about it — cutting away what didn't serve her, what threatened the whole. Her father's debts. Her family's slow-motion collapse. Me. She'd looked at our life the way she was looking at this tree, and she'd made the cut, and she'd never once explained where the blade went or why.

I could have asked. Right then. The question was right there, fully formed, warm in my throat: *Why did you leave? What did I do? What did they have on you?*

Twelve years I'd wanted to ask it. Two winters I'd waited by a phone, and then I'd stopped waiting, and then I'd stopped wanting, or told myself I had, and the question had gone down into me like a stone into a well — still there, still sinking, never landing.

Her head turned. Just slightly. Enough that I saw her profile against the gray sky, the line of her jaw doing that thing again, and her eyes when they found mine were not defensive. That was the worst part. They were *waiting.* Like she'd been standing next to this question her whole life and had only been waiting for me to pick it up.

My heart was going like a thrasher in May.

"Don't," she said. Quietly. Not a warning. Almost a plea. "Not unless you're going to finish it."

And I — well. I looked at the tree. At the clean cuts, the open center, the light getting in. At all the growth I'd chosen to remove so the rest could live.

"Not yet," I said.

Something went out of her shoulders. Relief or disappointment; with her, same problem as always, impossible to tell, probably both.

"Okay," she said. "Not yet."

We went back to work. Neither of us mentioned it again. Both of us carried it.

* * *

By noon the sky had gone the color of dishwater and the wind had picked up out of the northwest, the kind of wind that means business later. My grandmother would have called it a coat day. I called it a coat day. Silas, who had come out in a canvas jacket roughly the weight of a napkin, called it nothing, because she was too stubborn to admit she was freezing, and I could tell because she'd started making her cuts twice as fast, which is how people freeze.

"Take a break," I said.

"I'm fine."

"You're shivering so hard you're going to take off a branch we need."

"I am *not*—"

A gust came down the row and settled the argument for me. She hugged the jacket around herself and glared at the horizon like it had done this personally.

I should note, for the record, that I did not plan what happened next. I want that on the record, somewhere, anywhere, because I have a feeling it's going to come up in evidence later. I simply walked to the barn, took my grandmother's coat off the peg where it had hung for eleven years — wool, dark green, smelling faintly of cedar and apples and a woman who'd been dead since before Silas ever heard of Marguerite Vale — and brought it back down the row and held it out.

Silas looked at it. Looked at me.

"That's yours," she said.

"It's nobody's. It's a coat."

"It was your grandmother's."

"It was. Now it hangs in a barn. Coats don't grieve, Silas. People do, and people get cold."

"I'm not—"

"If you say *fine,*" I said, "I'm going to start explaining bud unions again, at length, with diagrams."

There was a pause in which I believe she genuinely weighed frostbite against my diagrams and found the contest closer than flattering. Then she took the coat. She put it on slowly, and it swallowed her — my grandmother had been a big woman, big hands, big laugh, the kind of person who filled a doorway and a silence — and Silas stood there drowning in green wool with her wrists sticking out, and something in my chest cracked along a fault line I'd thought was set.

"It smells like cedar," she said.

"And apples. And forty years of pipe smoke she swore she'd given up."

"She sounds—" Silas stopped. Started over. "You loved her."

"Everybody loved her. It was infuriating." I picked up the shears, busied my hands. "She'd have liked you. God help you."

That got a sound out of her, half laugh, half something else, quickly folded away. We stood in the wind a moment, the two of us and the row of trees, and I watched her pull the coat tighter and disappear further into it, into my family's smell, into a warmth she had no legal claim to and was accepting anyway, and I understood with sudden terrible clarity that rule six — *no lending her things, nothing that has to be returned* — had just died quietly, unmourned, and that I was going to keep breaking every rule I'd written until there weren't any left, and that the sale agreement sitting in the farmhouse drawer had started to look less like an exit and more like a countdown.

"Your hands," she said suddenly.

"What about them?"

She nodded at them. I looked down. In all my demonstrations I'd forgotten gloves, and the cold had opened the old splits along my knuckles, the ones that come back every winter like bad relatives. Nothing dramatic. Just blood, a little, at the edges.

"You're bleeding."

"It's February. I'm always bleeding somewhere."

"That's—" She crossed the space between us before I could decide whether to allow it, took my hand in both of hers, turned it over like a page she was proofreading. Her fingers were cold as river stones and her frown was fierce and focused and entirely, catastrophically familiar, and I stood there like a fence post while a woman I had sworn to keep at arm's length examined my damage with an expression I last saw on her face at nineteen, bandaging a cut I got falling out of her grandmother's apple tree, saying *hold still, you're impossible, hold still.*

"Hold still," she said now. "You're impossible."

I held still.

She let go before anything could happen, which was wise, and stepped back, which was wiser, and pulled my grandmother's coat around herself like armor, which was wisest of all, and none of it helped, because the damage was done. The air between us had changed composition. Any fool could taste it.

"We should get the rest of the row before the weather," I said, voice steady, heart not.

"Yes," she said. "The row. Weather. Good."

Great conversation. Two adults. Very articulate.

* * *

We worked until the light went thin and blue. She got faster. By the end of the row she was reading the trees herself — spotting the dead wood, the inward growth, the suckers stealing strength from the crown — and once she made a cut I wouldn't have made differently, and I caught myself watching her instead of the branches, and made myself stop, and failed.

At the end of the row she stood back and surveyed the work. Bare trees, open centers, light getting in everywhere. It looks like violence, good pruning. It looks like you've destroyed the thing. Come summer, it's the reason there's anything to pick.

"It looks awful," she said.

"It'll thank us in July."

"People say that about a lot of painful things."

"Yeah," I said. "They do."

She glanced at me sideways, quick, and I couldn't read it, and I hated that I still couldn't read her, and I hated more that part of me — the part that had waited two winters by a phone — was still fluent in her, still translating every glance, and the translation of this one was: *ask me.*

I didn't. Rule one. Though by now the rules were less a fence than a memory of a fence, posts and wire scattered across a field, and I knew — walking back toward the farmhouse in the failing light, her a few steps behind me wrapped in my dead grandmother's coat, the wind rising behind us out of the northwest with something heavier than snow in it — I knew I wasn't going to hold much longer.

Inside, she hung the coat on the peg by the door. My peg. Then she checked the time on her phone, and I watched her face do a small complicated thing — a calculation, a wince, a decision postponed — and she said she should review the tax documents tonight, and I said fine, and neither of us mentioned that the last train east left at 9:40, and neither of us mentioned that she'd missed it twice this week already, and I certainly didn't mention that I'd noticed.

I made coffee. She spread papers across the kitchen table. The wind came up hard against the north side of the house, rattling a loose shutter my grandmother had been meaning to fix since 1998, and outside, in the dark, the orchard stood open and pruned and waiting, all its dead wood gone, everything living turned toward the light.

Around ten, I pretended to go to bed. Around eleven, I heard the front door open softly, and the coat come off its peg, and footsteps going out onto the porch — and then not going any farther. Just staying there. Standing in the cold in a dead woman's coat, looking at the trees.

I lay in the dark listening to the wind and did not sleep, and did not ask, and understood that tomorrow — or the day after, or the day the train schedule finally won — one of us was going to stop pretending we had time.
