## 2. Terms of Engagement

Rafael Amberdale had rehearsed the pitch eleven times in the taxi, and it fell apart on the twelfth, which was the one that mattered.

The problem was Xanthe's office. He'd expected something like a wedding — soft light, tulle somewhere, a faint smell of sugar. Instead he'd walked into a room that looked like a general's war tent: color-coded binders, a wall calendar dense as a medieval manuscript, and a whiteboard reading *DO NOT PROMISE THE BRIDE'S MOTHER ANYTHING* in letters that suggested someone had learned this the hard way.

Xanthe sat behind a desk that had clearly been chosen for its ability to survive sieges. They didn't stand when he came in. They looked at him the way airport security looks at a suspiciously shaped carry-on.

"Mr. Amberdale," they said. "You called twice yesterday and once at seven this morning."

"I did."

"At seven in the morning."

"It felt urgent."

"It wasn't." They gestured at the chair across from them. "Sit. You have twenty minutes, and I'm billing you for all of them whether you use them or not."

He sat. The chair was less comfortable than it looked, which he suspected was also policy.

* * *

Here is what Rafael knew about himself: he was good at leaving. Twenty-nine years old, and he'd gotten so fluent in departures that his family had stopped saying goodbye to him at train stations and started saying *see you, if we see you*. His grandmother Odile had once told him, over Christmas goose, that the Amberdales measured love in attendance the way other families measured it in words or money. "You can tell me you adore me until Easter," she'd said, carving with terrifying precision, "but if you're not at my table, I'll believe the goose more than you."

He'd laughed then. He was not laughing now, because last week she'd called him and said, in a voice like a closing door: *The reunion is in three weeks. Come home for the whole of it, bring someone who looks like they intend to stay, or don't bother coming back at all — I'm signing the estate over to your cousin Priya.*

Priya, who remembered birthdays. Priya, who had attended every harvest festival since birth, including the year she had chickenpox and came anyway, spotted from across the orchard like a small contagious comet.

So here he was, in a siege-proof chair, about to ask a stranger to lie beautifully for him.

"You want me to pretend to be your partner," Xanthe said, when he'd finished talking. They said it without inflection, the way one might say *you want me to pretend this invoice is accurate*.

"For one weekend. Two nights. My family's reunion."

"And why me? There are agencies for this. There are apps. There are, I assume, ambitious cousins of friends who'd do it for free wine."

"There are," Rafael admitted. "But they'd be nervous. Or hopeful. They'd want it to become real, or they'd want something from me afterward. You—" he gestured vaguely at the whiteboard, the binders, the entire fortress of competence— "you do feelings for a living. Professionally. You'd treat it like an event."

Something flickered across Xanthe's face — too fast for him to name it, gone before it landed. "Go on."

"You plan weddings. You know exactly how couples look at each other when it's real. You could manufacture it. Choreograph it. Nobody would question it because nobody questions you."

"That's true," Xanthe said, with the calm of someone stating the boiling point of water. "Nobody does."

* * *

The offer was thirty thousand pounds. He'd decided on the number in the taxi, between rehearsals six and seven, telling himself it was generous rather than desperate.

Xanthe wrote it down on a legal pad, looked at it for a long moment, and then did something he hadn't expected: they turned the pad around and crossed out half of it.

"Twenty thousand," they said. "And I keep the difference in dignity."

"I— what?"

"I'm not taking thirty thousand pounds to hold your hand at dinner. Thirty thousand is a price for something else, and whatever that something else is, I'm not selling it." They clicked the pen shut. "Twenty. Flat fee. Travel covered separately. And I have terms."

"Terms," Rafael repeated. He'd come prepared for haggling upward. He had not prepared for being negotiated downward by someone with a legal pad.

"Rule one." Xanthe held up a finger. "No touching beyond necessity. Hand-holding at dinner if the audience requires it. An arm around shoulders if your grandmother is watching. Nothing else. We are colleagues performing a scene, and colleagues do not improvise stage business."

"Necessity," Rafael said carefully. "Who decides what's necessary?"

"I do. I've been deciding what's necessary at events for nine years. It's the whole job."

"Rule two?"

"No real names in bed." They said it flatly, and watched him very closely while they said it, and Rafael understood two things at once: first, that this rule had history behind it; second, that he was not going to get the story today.

"We're sharing a bed?" he asked, instead of asking the other thing.

"My grandmother will check," he said. "She checks everything. She once opened a guest's suitcase to confirm the man owned more than one shirt."

"Then yes. Sharing a bed. Which brings me to rule three: separate sides, a border of pillows if you like, and nobody crosses it. Out by Sunday. I leave after breakfast, you drive me to the station, and whatever happened at the reunion stays at the reunion. No follow-up calls. No 'that was fun, let's do it again.' No—"

"Loose threads," Rafael said quietly.

Xanthe paused. For a moment the professional mask slipped just enough that he could see the person underneath it — tired, watchful, younger than the armor suggested. "Loose threads unravel things," they said. "I run tight operations, Mr. Amberdale. That's why people hire me. Everything ends where it's supposed to end, on schedule, with everyone believing it was beautiful."

"And has that worked?" he asked. "For you. The tight operations."

The look they gave him could have chilled champagne. "That's outside the scope of this engagement."

* * *

They shook on it — a handshake conducted at arm's length, brisk, contractual, the kind of handshake that leaves no fingerprints. Rafael found himself oddly disappointed by it, which was ridiculous. He'd gotten exactly what he came for: a professional, a script, a weekend with clean edges.

Except.

Except that as Xanthe walked him to the door, past the wall calendar with its dense beautiful machinery of other people's happiest days, they said, without turning around: "One amendment. Free of charge."

"Yes?"

"If anyone in your family asks how we met, we met at a wedding I planned. Yours, ideally — no. Not yours." A beat. Something moved behind their eyes. "A friend's. You caught the garter, which embarrassed you, and I complimented your tie to make up for it. Keep the tie story simple. Complicated cover stories collapse under grandmothers."

"You've done this before," Rafael said. It wasn't quite a question.

Xanthe opened the door. Grey daylight fell into the hallway, and they stood in it with their hand still on the frame, and for one unguarded second they looked less like a fortress and more like a person standing at the edge of a pool they'd sworn never to enter again.

"No," they said. "I've never done this before. That's why there are rules."

Then the door closed, polite and final as a period at the end of a sentence, and Rafael stood on the pavement outside with a signed agreement in his pocket and the strange, unwelcome feeling that he had just hired a bodyguard for his heart — and that the bodyguard was better armed than he was.

Three weeks later, the taxi turned through the rusted gates of Amberdale, and Xanthe looked up at the crumbling house with its festival scaffolding clinging to one wing like a splint on a broken leg, and said, in a voice pitched only for him:

"Remind me of the tie story on the way in. And Mr. Amberdale — if your grandmother asks me one question I haven't been prepped for, I'm improvising, and I warn you, I improvise *beautifully*."

Odile was already waiting on the steps.
