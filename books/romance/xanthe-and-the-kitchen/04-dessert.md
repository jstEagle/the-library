## 4. Dessert

The dining room at Amberdale had been built to intimidate, and after two hundred years it had gotten very good at its job.

Rafael counted the ways he knew it: the table long enough to seat twenty-two and currently seating fourteen, so that conversation happened in islands and everyone strained slightly toward the middle like plants toward a sun that wasn't there. The portraits of dead Amberdales lining the walls, each one painted with the same expression of mild disappointment, as though the artist had said *think of your taxes*. The chandelier that his grandmother refused to have rewired because "candles are more honest," which was Odile's way of saying she liked watching people squint.

He sat with Xanthe on his left — a strategic choice he'd made at the door and immediately regretted, because now he could feel the warmth of their arm through two layers of sleeve — and across from his cousin Marcus, who had the face of a man who'd never once wondered if he was the problem.

"So," Marcus said, carving the roast with the confidence of someone who had never been asked to stop. "Rafael. Still in— what is it you do? Events?"

"Weddings," Rafael said.

"Right." Marcus smiled at Xanthe with the particular kindness people reserve for those they've decided to pity. "And you're in the business too? How did you two meet?"

"A wedding," Xanthe said smoothly. "I planned it. He caught the bouquet."

"It was thrown directly at my head," Rafael said. "I have the reflexes of a startled deer."

Xanthe's mouth twitched. It was a small thing, barely a centimeter of movement, but Rafael had spent three days learning their face the way you learn a piece of music — not the notes, but the spaces between them — and he knew a suppressed laugh when he saw one. Something in his chest loosened by a degree he hadn't authorized.

"And what do you make of all this?" Aunt Verity gestured at the room with her wine glass, a sweep that took in the cracked plaster, the taped window in the far hall, the general air of a great house quietly running out of money. "The old place. Be honest."

"I think it's beautiful," Xanthe said.

Everyone looked at them. It was the wrong answer and they all knew it; the correct answer at an Amberdale table was something wry and faintly mournful, delivered while looking at one's plate.

"The east wing is falling down," Verity said.

"The light in the long gallery this morning was extraordinary," Xanthe said. "Whoever built those windows understood exactly where the sun would be at eight o'clock in October. That's not luck. Someone stood here for years watching the light before they cut the glass." They paused, then added, gently, "Falling-down things usually mean somebody stopped paying attention, not that nobody ever cared."

The silence that followed had texture. Rafael's grandmother set down her fork with a small click, and he braced himself, because Odile Amberdale's silences were ordnance awaiting coordinates.

"Hm," she said, which from her was a standing ovation.

Marcus recovered first, as bullies do. "Well. It's romantic, I suppose. Though I hear the estate's future is rather up in the air." He said it lightly, but his eyes went to Rafael, and Rafael felt the trap close around him with the familiar click of family machinery. "Grandmother's been telling everyone the reunion would settle things. Attendance, legacy, all that. I assume you'll be staying on, Rafe? Taking some responsibility for once?"

There it was. Twenty-nine years of it, served with roast potatoes. *Taking some responsibility for once.* As though leaving at eighteen had been a moral failure rather than the only sane response to a house that measured love in attendance records. As though he hadn't sent money home every quarter, answered every call, come back every Christmas and left every Boxing Day feeling like a ghost haunting his own life.

"I haven't decided," Rafael said, which was true, and insufficient, and he watched Marcus register the insufficiency with relish.

"Not decided." Marcus turned to the table at large, a man presenting evidence. "He hasn't decided. Grandmother opens the house, half the county comes, and the heir treats it like a hotel checkout."

"Marcus—" his mother began.

"No, it's fair, it's fair." He raised his hands, all innocence. "I'm just saying what everyone thinks. The estate will be sold within the year, we all know it, and Rafael will be off to wherever he goes, and poor Xanthe here will discover what the rest of us already know — that Rafael Amberdale doesn't stay anywhere. Not for school, not for work, not for—"

Rafael didn't decide to take Xanthe's hand. That was the thing he would turn over later, lying awake in the too-soft bed: there was no decision. There was only Marcus's voice doing what it had always done, carving him up at the table with the good knives, and then his own hand moving across the linen and finding Xanthe's — cool-knuckled, steady, a hand that had clearly held a hundred clipboards against the wind — and closing around it.

"*That's enough,*" Rafael said.

His voice came out lower than he intended. The room went still. Somewhere down the table a spoon was set down with exaggerated care.

"My relationship is not a topic for debate," he said. "Neither am I. And if you want to know whether I'll stay, Marcus, ask me like a person instead of performing me like a eulogy."

The silence rang. Verity studied her wine. Marcus opened his mouth, found nothing in it, and closed it again — a small miracle, possibly historic.

It was Odile who spoke next, into the ringing quiet, in a tone of devastating mildness: "Is the soufflé ready? It's been ready for ten minutes and soufflés wait for no one."

* * *

They escaped through the garden door after dessert, which was a pear tart that Rafael did not taste even slightly, because his hand remembered being held the way a bell remembers being rung.

The orchard sat behind the walled kitchen garden, older than the house itself, rows of apple trees gone crooked and silver-limbed with age. The festival crew had strung test lights between two of them that afternoon — a single strand, warm and low, left glowing over the grass like a mistake someone had decided to keep. The harvest moon was up, fat and orange, doing its annual impression of a lantern.

Xanthe walked ahead of him with their hands in their pockets, and Rafael realized he was following them without any plan at all, which was becoming a pattern he should probably examine.

"You didn't have to do that," Xanthe said, without turning. "In there."

"Yes, I did."

"You had rules. We had terms." They stopped under the lit tree and faced him, and the string lights caught the edge of their jaw, the line of their throat. "Rule four. No improvisation. You agreed to rule four."

"I don't remember agreeing to rule four."

"You initialed it. There's a document. I'm a professional, Rafael, I don't improvise."

"No," he said. "You don't. I've noticed. You plan everything — the seating chart, the exits, the weather contingencies. You had a contingency for my aunt asking about your childhood, which, by the way, who has a contingency for that?"

"People who've been asked about their childhood at dinner parties," Xanthe said, and something flickered across their face, quick as a fish under ice, there and gone.

Rafael stepped closer. The lights hummed faintly overhead. A late apple dropped somewhere in the dark rows with a soft thud, and neither of them flinched, and that shared non-flinch felt suddenly enormous.

"What I meant," he said, "in there. When I took your hand. It wasn't—"

"Don't." The word came out fast, almost sharp. Xanthe's chin lifted. "Don't explain it. If you explain it, then it becomes a thing we discuss, and if it becomes a thing we discuss, then it becomes a thing, and we have one weekend, and the fee was agreed, and on Sunday I drive home, and none of this—" they gestured at the orchard, the lights, the space between their bodies, which had shrunk to something scandalous without either of them authorizing it, "—none of this survives Sunday. That's the arrangement. Arrangements work because they end."

"Does it work?" Rafael asked quietly. "The arrangement. Is it working?"

Xanthe looked at him. And Rafael watched something happen in their face that he suspected very few people had ever been permitted to see — the mask not dropping, exactly, but going translucent, the way frosted glass shows you a shape without giving you the detail. Wanting, maybe. Or fear wearing wanting's coat.

"No," Xanthe said.

The word landed between them like the fallen apple. Neither moved to pick it up.

"No?" Rafael managed.

"It's working perfectly," Xanthe said, with terrible precision, "and that's the problem. I'm very good at this, Rafael. I'm good at making rooms look loved. And tonight at that table, when you took my hand, for about four seconds I forgot it was a job, and I have not forgotten a job in eleven years, and I would like to go back inside now and review tomorrow's schedule."

They turned. Rafael caught their wrist — gently, a question asked in pressure — and Xanthe stopped, and for one suspended moment they stood in the pool of lamplight close enough that he could smell the woodsmoke in their hair, and the whole orchard seemed to lean in, every crooked tree a witness.

If he kissed them now, he thought, it would be a violation of the contract. If he kissed them now, it would also be the first honest thing he'd done in this house in a decade.

He didn't. He let go of their wrist, finger by finger, like untying a knot he might need later.

"Goodnight, Xanthe."

"Goodnight, Rafael."

They walked back toward the house separately, ten feet apart, like strangers arriving at the same party — and Rafael lay awake afterward staring at the ceiling, cataloguing the damage. His hand still held the memory of theirs. Four seconds, Marcus had cost him four seconds, and the bill had come due instantly: because the terrifying truth, the one he'd taken Xanthe's hand to escape and walked into instead, was that it hadn't been acting.

And worse — far worse, the kind of worse that rearranges furniture in a man's chest — he was fairly sure that for four seconds, it hadn't been acting for Xanthe either.

Which meant the rules weren't protecting either of them anymore.

Which meant Sunday was coming.
