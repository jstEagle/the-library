## 22. Nesting Season

The truck came up the spit road at half past four in the afternoon, kicking up a rooster tail of salt dust that I could see from the hatchery window before I could hear the engine. I knew the sound of it anyway. Ten months of that engine, arriving on Fridays and leaving on Sundays, and then — three weeks ago — not leaving at all, because Julian had finally done what he'd been circling toward all spring: he'd driven to the mainland ferry office and sold his apartment over the phone while standing in my kitchen, holding a mug of coffee like it was a contract he was about to sign.

But today was different. Today the truck was full.

I watched it bounce down the last stretch of shell road with its suspension complaining in a language only old trucks and old men understand, and I saw the boxes stacked in the bed — his books, his good microscope wrapped in a moving blanket, the blue ceramic pot with the rosemary plant that had somehow survived both his neglect and mine. And riding shotgun, seatbelted with the fastidiousness of someone who considers safety a moral position, was Xanthe Voss.

She'd written to tell us she was coming. Of course she had. Xanthe did nothing unannounced; her letters arrived on cream paper with a return address that was just a post office box in a city she never named, and her handwriting looked like it had been typeset. *I find myself with a fortnight between depositions,* she'd written, *and a curiosity about whether you two have managed to build anything habitable out of all that weather. Also, I owe your generator an apology.*

That last part was very Xanthe. She had spent one night of the storm apologizing to no one for anything, and had apparently decided the generator was the exception.

By the time the truck crunched to a stop outside the station, I had my hands in the sink and was pretending I hadn't been watching from the window for ten minutes. Julian came in carrying two boxes at once, set them down, kissed me — briefly, thoroughly, the way he did everything once he'd finally made up his mind about it — and said, "She brought a filing system."

"I heard that," Xanthe said from the doorway. She stepped inside carrying her document case, the same locked case from the storm, though these days it held nothing more incriminating than her own correspondence and a paperback romance she would deny owning if asked. "And it's not a filing system. It's an archive. There's a difference, and the difference is that an archive is meant to be read."

"By anyone?"

"By whoever earns it." She looked around the main room of the station — the map wall, the kettle, the couch where Julian had slept that first night with his back turned to me like a man guarding a wound — and something moved through her face too quick to name. "It's smaller than I imagined."

"You imagined it?" I said.

"I've imagined every place you've described to me since March. You write long letters, Mara Ravensworth. You always did. You just used to send them to the wrong person." She set the case down on the table with a click that sounded, after everything, almost gentle. "Now. Show me where this generator lives so I can make my amends, and then someone will explain to me why there are seventy-one stakes with little flags in the sand north of the dune line, because I counted them from the air and I have questions."

"Ninety-three," I said. "And they're nests."

"They're *hope* with flags on them," she said, "but we can use your word."

* * *

We walked the beach at golden hour, the three of us, and I let them fall behind me by twenty paces so I could watch them together without either of them knowing.

They were an odd pair and had been from the first night, when they'd stood in my doorway soaked through and glaring at each other across a decade of professional rivalry. Julian argued in circles, building his case brick by brick until you found yourself standing inside it. Xanthe argued in straight lines — she would simply state the thing that was true and then wait, with terrible patience, for you to catch up. For months after the storm I'd assumed they tolerated each other the way rival nations tolerate a border. But somewhere between the affidavit and the trial and the slow-motion collapse of Aldous Wren's empire — fourteen counts, three resignations, one very public apology drafted by lawyers who billed by the syllable — the two of them had become something I didn't have a clean word for. Colleagues was too small. Friends was too easy. They were, I think, the only two people alive who fully understood what it had cost each other to stand in that lamplight and read those letters aloud.

Julian was saying something now that made her laugh — actually laugh, a short surprised sound like she'd been ambushed by it — and I felt my chest do the thing it had been doing all year, this unfamiliar expansion, like a room whose furniture had finally been arranged correctly.

Ten years ago I had believed love was a door you either walked through or didn't, one night, one decision, one dock where a man waited three hours in the rain. I had built my whole life on the idea that I'd missed my chance and that missing was permanent, the way a nest abandoned past incubation is simply empty forever. It had taken a snowstorm, an archivist, ninety-three flagged nests, and one sentence spoken at sunrise to teach me otherwise: that some doors don't close. They just wait. They stand there patient as tide lines, holding their shape, until you're finally standing still long enough to walk back through.

"H Mara!" Julian called. "Xanthe wants to know if the turtles have names."

"The turtles do not have names," I called back.

"She says the one with the chipped scute looks like a Gerald."

"The turtles *definitely* do not have names."

"Gerald it is," Xanthe said serenely, and wrote something in her notebook, and I knew with absolute certainty that somewhere in that archive there was now a card catalog entry for a sea turtle named Gerald, cross-referenced, dated, and meant to be read.

* * *

The emergence happened on her fourth evening, which felt like the island showing off.

I'd been watching Nest 41 for days — the depression in the sand going soft and dimpled, the ghost-crab tracks circling like nervous sentries. On the morning of the boil I woke at five, checked the light, woke the others, and by six we were crouched behind the driftwood line with coffee going cold in our hands as the sand began to shiver.

They came up all at once, the way they always do — no warning, just the surface erupting into dozens of small flailing lives, loggerhead hatchlings no bigger than bottle caps, scrambling over each other in their panic and purpose. The gulls wheeled in immediately, screaming their objections, and Julian stood up shouting and waving his arms like a man defending a border crossing, and Xanthe — dignified, archival Xanthe — dropped to her knees in the wet sand and began, with great seriousness, to escort individual hatchlings toward the water, herding them with cupped hands and murmured encouragement that I am fairly sure included the word *Gerald*.

"Let them run," I told her, laughing. "They have to make it on their own. That's the whole — "

"I know what the whole point is." She didn't look up. A wave slid in and took three of them at once, and she watched them go with an expression I recognized from the night she'd read Wren's letters aloud: the look of someone measuring a cost against a truth and finding both worth paying. "I'm allowed to want things to survive. That's not interference. That's testimony."

The waves kept coming and taking them, dozens of small dark shapes riding the foam out into the gold-lit water, and the odds being what they are — one in a thousand, maybe one in ten thousand — most of them wouldn't come back to this beach. Most of them would never come back anywhere. The ocean doesn't promise returns. It only promises that the ones who make it will carry the memory of this shore in their bones, imprinted, and that years from now some female among them will haul herself up onto sand very much like this one and dig.

I sat down next to Xanthe in the sand. Julian dropped beside us a moment later, breathless from gull duty, his shoulder warm against mine.

"One in a thousand," he said quietly. He'd learned the numbers by heart without meaning to, the way he'd once learned case law.

"About that," I said.

"And you do this anyway. Every season."

"Every season."

He was quiet for a while, watching the last stragglers make their dash — one stubborn hatchling taking the scenic route, correcting course twice, finally caught by the foam and carried out sideways like a small drunk sailor. "You know," he said, "for ten years I thought you'd chosen the work over me. Over us. Over testifying. I had it all sorted — you, the career, the cowardice, the whole taxonomy. And then I got here and watched you spend a night in a storm saving eggs you'll never meet again, for a return rate that's basically an insult, and I thought — oh. She wasn't choosing against me. She was choosing the only thing she could still protect."

"That's generous," I said.

"It's accurate. I'm an expert witness now. I only say accurate things."

"He's insufferable," Xanthe said. "It's his best quality."

I looked out at the water, at the glittering line where the last hatchlings disappeared, and I tried to explain the thing I'd been turning over all year, the thing I wanted to get exactly right because I suspected I would only ever say it once.

"For a long time I thought second chances were about timing," I said. "That if I'd sent the letter a week earlier, or testified a month later, or come back to the dock an hour sooner, everything would have worked out. I wasted years doing that math. But it was never the math. The letter was ready. The truth was ready. *I* wasn't — I couldn't stand still. I kept moving because moving felt like trying, and staying still felt like failing. And second chances aren't fast. They're not even patient, really. They just sit there. They wait for you to stop running long enough to pick them up."

Julian reached over and laced his fingers through mine, sand and all.

"You stopped running," he said.

"I stopped running," I agreed. "Turns out everything I was running from was just waiting to be loved properly."

"That's going in the archive," Xanthe said, already writing.

"Absolutely not — "

"*Second chances are not about better timing but about standing still long enough to take them.*" She read it back in her flat archival voice, then looked up, and for one unguarded moment her eyes were bright and young and completely undefended. "Do you know how many records I've handled that end too early? Depositions cut off. Letters returned unopened. Whole lives archived mid-sentence. I spent eleven years building a case because I believed the record could be corrected. And it could — Wren is finished, and it mattered, and I'd do it again. But this—" she gestured at the beach, the truck, the two of us, the churned sand where ninety-three nests waited under their flags— "this isn't correction. This is continuation. Nobody taught me how to file that."

"So don't file it," Julian said. "Just be in it."

She considered this the way she considered everything, thoroughly and slightly suspiciously. Then she closed her notebook.

"A fortnight," she said. "I'm staying the fortnight. And I want it noted that I expect to see at least one more emergence, and that if any hatchling requires escorting, I will be escorting it, and that neither of you is permitted to find this sentimental."

"Noted," we said together.

* * *

That evening the light came in low and honeyed off the water, and the three of us sat on the porch steps with the last of the wine Julian had hauled over in the truck, watching the tide come in over the flats. Somewhere north of the dune line, ninety-two flags stood in the fading light, waiting. Somewhere out past the breakers, a few dozen small survivors swam east with a beach's worth of memory in their bones.

Xanthe went in early — she claimed exhaustion, but we both heard her in the guest room a while later, typing, and we chose to believe it was the romance novel. Julian's hand found mine in the dark, thumb tracing the scar on my knuckle from the oyster rake, the one he'd bandaged badly and tenderly our first week.

"Mara," he said.

"Mm."

"Next season, I want to build the new shade frames for the hatchery. Proper ones. I drew plans."

"You drew *plans*."

"I'm thorough. It's my best quality."

"Xanthe says it's insufferability."

"We contain multitudes." He was quiet a moment, and then, in the voice he saved for true things: "I keep thinking about the dock. Three hours in the rain. How sure I was that was the ending. And now I'm sitting on a porch with you and there's a woman inside archiving a turtle named Gerald, and the mentor who ruined us is finished, and tomorrow there's data to log and frames to build and — Mara, it doesn't feel like an ending. It feels like the beginning of a very long, very ordinary life, and I have never wanted anything so much."

I leaned my head on his shoulder. Out over the water the first stars were coming up, patient as tide lines.

"Good," I said. "Because I'm not going anywhere. I live here. You live here. We're standing still."

"Standing still," he agreed, and squeezed my hand.

From inside, faintly, came the sound of Xanthe's typing stopping, followed by the unmistakable click of a notebook opening — and then, drifting out through the screen door, her voice, dry as sand and soft as foam:

*"For the record: nesting season, year one. Status: ongoing. Subjects: three. Prognosis: excellent."*

And for once, neither of us objected to being filed.
