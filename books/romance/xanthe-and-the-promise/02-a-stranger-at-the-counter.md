## 2. A Stranger at the Counter

The bus from the city smelled of diesel and other people's grief, and Xanthe Reyes spent the whole two hours of the ride pretending hers was smaller than it was.

Aunt Rosa had been dead for nineteen days when the lawyer's letter finally reached her in Marisol's bakery kitchen, flour-dusted and coffee-stained, the way all her mail arrived now. *Regarding the estate of Rosa Reyes-Villanueva, and certain real property of interest to you.* She had read it four times at the stainless-steel counter while the proofing dough rose behind her like a slow, patient tide, and each time the sentence landed the same way — not as news, but as a summons. *Come home. There are things here with your name on them.*

Home. The word sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed at twenty-four and never quite digested.

She had told herself the trip would take a week. Sign the papers, empty the house on Alder Street, put the little yellow bungalow on the market, and be back in the city before the sourdough starter she'd left with Marisol could turn feral. Quiet. Efficient. A funeral's unfinished business, nothing more. She had even rehearsed it on the bus, the way she rehearsed everything now: *I'm here about my aunt's estate. No, I won't be staying long. Yes, it's been a while. No — * and here her rehearsal always snagged, the way a hem catches on a nail — *no, I don't know what Casper's been up to.*

Because of course she knew. You couldn't not know, in a town the size of Alder Creek. The bookshop had come up in every conversation she'd had since Rosa's funeral, which she had attended in the back row with sunglasses and a borrowed black coat, slipping out before the reception. *The Okafor boy turned the old Hartwell place into a bookshop, did you hear? Right on Main. He does a story hour for the children on Saturdays.* People said *the Okafor boy* the way they said *the Hendersons' orchard* — as if he were still a fixed feature of the landscape, a fact of the town, while she had become a rumor with a forwarding address.

She had not planned to go into the bookshop. That was the thing she would remember later, turning it over like a coin she couldn't stop flipping: she had *not planned to.* Her plan had been the county records office, ten minutes of microfiche, and out. But the records office didn't open until Monday, and the letter from the lawyer had mentioned — in that bland, devastating legalese — *the property known as the Okafor-Reyes orchard, currently in the possession of* — and she had needed, with an urgency that alarmed her, to see it. To see *him*, maybe, though she'd have bitten through her own tongue before admitting it. To see whether eleven years had changed his face, or only hers.

So she stood on Main Street on a gray Thursday morning in late September, in front of a storefront she had walked past a thousand times as a girl — the old Hartwell hardware store, with its ghost of a painted sign still visible above the awning — and read the new lettering in gold leaf: *IVERSEN & DAUGHTER BOOKS.*

& Daughter. Something in her chest went tight and strange. He had a daughter.

The window display was better than it had any right to be: a pyramid of clothbound classics, a hand-lettered card reading *BANNED AND BELOVED*, and a fat orange cat asleep in a ceramic bowl labeled DO NOT DISTURB (HE WON'T). Through the glass she could see shelves where the hardware aisles used to be, and deeper in, warm lamplight, and a small cluster of children sitting cross-legged on a braided rug near the back, and a man's voice — low, unhurried, doing a voice, doing *the wolf's* voice — rolling out over their heads like weather.

She knew that voice. She had spent six years of her life learning its every register: sleepy, teasing, furious, tender, the way it went rough when he read aloud, the way it broke once, only once, on the night she left.

Xanthe Reyes was thirty-five years old, and she stood on the sidewalk of her hometown with her hand on a door she had no business opening, and she opened it anyway. The bell above the door rang — the same bell, she realized with a lurch, the same tarnished brass bell from the hardware store, from their childhood, from the summer they'd stolen penny nails from the bin by the register and Casper had gotten caught and hadn't ratted her out — and eleven years collapsed like a folding chair.

* * *

He was at the front counter when she came in, and for one merciful second he didn't see her. He was crouched, hunting through a drawer of paperbacks, a pencil tucked behind his ear, and he looked — God help her — he looked *good*, which felt like a personal affront. Broader through the shoulders than the boy she'd left. Gray coming in early at his temples, threading the close-cropped black. Reading glasses pushed up on his head. He surfaced with a battered copy of something and said, without looking up, in his bookseller voice, "If it's the wolf book you're after, we're out until Thursday, but I can order—"

He looked up.

The paperback hit the counter with a slap that made the sleeping cat in the window crack one eye open in protest.

For a long moment — three seconds, five, an entire geological age — neither of them moved. Xanthe had imagined this meeting on the bus, in the shower, at 3 a.m. in eleven different years, and in none of those imaginings had she been struck quite so dumb. He was looking at her the way you look at a door opening in a wall you thought was solid. His mouth opened. Closed. The pencil slid from behind his ear and clattered to the floor, and he didn't pick it up.

"Hi, Casper," she said, because someone had to, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I need to talk to you about the orchard."

"The orchard," he repeated. Flat. As if she'd said *the moon* or *the ocean floor*.

"The deed." She set her bag on the counter between them like a barricade. "The lawyer sent me a letter. About my aunt's estate. Apparently Rosa—" Her throat did something traitorous. "Apparently my aunt's name is on it. Along with your grandmother's. And apparently—" she made herself say it, made the words come out in a businesslike clip, "—along with *mine*."

"Yours," he said.

"Mine. And yours. Both of ours. Which is why I'm here." She heard herself accelerating, the words tumbling out with the momentum of a cart on a downhill grade. "I want to understand the situation. Legally. I'm not — I'm not here for anything else."

"Right," Casper said. And then, because he was Casper, because eleven years had apparently sanded nothing off him at all, he said: "You look good, Xanthe."

The nerve of it. The sheer, unmitigated nerve. Something hot went off behind her ribs — not the soft ache she'd braced for, not the grief she'd rehearsed, but *anger*, clean and sudden, and she seized it the way a drowning woman seizes driftwood. Anger was useful. Anger had a shape you could hold.

"Don't," she said. "Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That. The small talk. The — you look good. Eleven years, Casper. You don't get to open with *you look good* like I just ran to the store for milk."

"I didn't—" He dragged a hand down his face. Behind him, the children on the rug had gone quiet in the way children do when the adults' weather changes; one small girl with two tight braids was watching them with open, forensic interest. "Okay," Casper said. "Okay. You're right. I'm — it's a shock. Seeing you. I didn't know you were coming."

"Why would you?" She kept her voice low, for the children's sake, which somehow made it worse, made it intimate, made it the old voice, the one she'd used across pillows and kitchen tables. "You haven't returned a letter in eleven years."

His face did something complicated. "I could say the same to you."

"You could *what*?"

"You stopped writing first." He said it quietly, but there was iron under it, and it knocked her back half a step. "August. You wrote in June, I wrote back in July, and then nothing. I wrote twice more. Nothing came back. So no — I didn't write in September. I learned."

"That's not—" The floor seemed to tilt a degree. *You wrote back in July.* She had never — she had written in June from the city, one long letter she'd agonized over, and then she had waited, and the silence had grown so loud so fast that by October she had understood it as an answer. Hadn't she? Hadn't the silence *been* the answer? "I never got anything in July."

"Postmarked and everything." His jaw was tight now. "Grandma Ede was there when I mailed it. She said she'd walk it to the box herself."

Something cold slid down Xanthe's spine, some small animal instinct, a thread of wrongness she couldn't pull free — but the moment was already being swept away by the present tense, because a door banged open at the back of the shop and a woman with a stroller came in, and a boy of about six peeled off the rug and came barreling toward the counter shouting "Mr. Casper, is story hour still going, my mom said—" and the ordinary machinery of a Thursday morning closed over their conversation like water over a stone.

"Story hour," Casper said to the boy, with the visible effort of a man hauling himself up a rope. "Yeah. Five more minutes. Go pick your seat." The boy tore off. Casper looked at Xanthe, and something in his face had shut — not coldly, but carefully, the way you shutter a window before a storm. "Can this wait? Twenty minutes? I've got twelve kids expecting a wolf."

Xanthe should have said yes. She should have said *I'll come back*, should have retreated to the sidewalk, to her rehearsed script, to the clean distance of lawyers and paperwork. Instead she heard herself say, "I'll wait," and then, before she could stop herself, "I'll listen. If that's — I'll just sit."

He hesitated. Then he nodded once, jerkily, and picked up the wolf book, and walked back to the rug.

* * *

She took the chair by the window, half behind the pyramid of banned books, where she could watch without being watched, or told herself she could.

And then Casper Iversen read *The Three Billy Goats Gruff* to twelve children on a Thursday morning, and Xanthe Reyes's armor developed its first crack.

He was good. That was the unbearable part. He didn't just read; he *performed*, dropping his voice into the troll's gravelly rumble, letting the smallest goat's voice go squeaky until the children shrieked, pausing at the exact right beat before *who's that trip-trapping over my bridge* so that a boy in a dinosaur shirt could yell the answer. And the children — they were putty, they were his congregation, they leaned toward him like plants toward light. The girl with the braids, sitting front and center, kept tugging his sleeve to whisper things, and he'd nod gravely as if receiving intelligence of national importance.

*He has a daughter,* Xanthe thought. *IverSEN & DAUGHter.* She found herself scanning the rug for a child with his eyes, his stubborn chin, and couldn't tell which one, and hated herself for looking.

The story ended. The children clapped with the unselfconscious violence of the very young, and Casper sent them off to browse with a promise — *next week, the one with the tiger who eats everything, tell your mothers* — and then the shop was theirs again, and the bell over the door rang for a departing customer, and the silence between them had weight and texture, like wet wool.

"Coffee?" he said. "There's a machine in the back. It's terrible."

"God, yes."

* * *

The back room had been the stockroom in Hartwell's day; now it was a kitchenette and a desk buried under review copies, a wall of children's drawings taped up in a riotous grid, a corkboard with a leak in the ceiling above it — a brown water stain shaped, Xanthe thought uncharitably, like a lung — and a rota in Casper's handwriting, which she recognized instantly and refused to be moved by. He poured two mugs of coffee that smelled like a burnt tire and handed her one, and they stood a careful four feet apart among the boxes, and the forty minutes began.

It went like this:

The deed, it emerged, had been redrawn fourteen years ago, when Ede Okafor's health first failed. Rosa and Ede — Xanthe's aunt and his grandmother, the two women who had run the town's garden club like a benevolent cartel, who had taught Xanthe grafting and Casper pruning in the same summers, who had sat together at every harvest festival on the same folding chairs — had gone to the lawyer together and put four names on the orchard instead of two. Ede's, Casper's, Rosa's. And Xanthe's.

"She never told me," Xanthe said. Her coffee was untouched, warming her palms. "Rosa never said a word. Not when I visited at Christmas, not when I called every Sunday. Nothing."

"Grandma Ede didn't tell me either." Casper was turning his mug in slow circles on the desk. "I found out the same way you did, presumably. The lawyer's letter, after the funeral." He looked up, and his eyes were the same dark, level eyes she'd drowned in at nineteen, and she made herself hold the look because looking away would feel like losing. "You want to know the part that doesn't make sense to me? She put your name on it eleven years after you left. She could have left it to me alone. She had years to change it. She didn't."

"Maybe she forgot."

"Ede Okafor didn't forget her own name."

"Then maybe she was sentimental. Old women do that."

"Maybe." He said it in a tone that meant *I don't believe you and neither do you.*

The conversation circled, tightened, frayed. She learned in pieces, the way you learn a town's news when you've been gone: that his grandmother had died in the spring, that the orchard had gone untended for two seasons and half the trees were blighted, that the bookshop was hers — *& Daughter*, Amara, seven years old, in the second grade, *she's the one with the braids, she was sitting front row* — and that Casper had a roof that leaked and a bank that called. She gave him nothing back. When he asked — carefully, sideways, the way you approach a dog that's been kicked — *so you're in the city now, you're — what are you doing?* — she said "I bake," and left it there, and watched him decide not to push.

But the anger was still in her, banked and glowing, and every few minutes it found a vent.

"You could have found me," she said at one point. "It's not hard, in this century. People have addresses."

"I wrote," he said. "Three times. I'm not doing this in front of the espresso machine, Xanthe."

"Then when? Because that's the thing, Casper, that's the thing I keep—" She stopped. Breathed. The children's drawings on the wall — crayon dragons, a wobbly rainbow, one that just said MRS. CASPER in enormous letters, which meant *Mr. Casper*, which meant a five-year-old had made it, which was somehow the most devastating object in the room — swam in her vision. "I came here for a deed. One signature, one sale, one week. That was the plan."

"Whose name do you think is on the roof leak?" he said, and it was almost a joke, and it almost worked, and then his face did the thing where the humor didn't quite hold, and he said, quieter: "You could have found *me* too. That's the part I keep."

The clock over the door ticked. Somewhere out front the orange cat knocked something off a shelf with the deliberate malice of its kind.

"I should go," Xanthe said. "I have a rental car and a house full of my aunt's things and a lawyer to see Monday. I'll have my lawyer contact yours. About the deed."

"Fine," Casper said.

"Fine."

Neither of them moved.

"You could stay for the harvest," he said suddenly, and then looked as surprised by it as she was. "I mean — practically. There's a month of work before anyone should decide anything. The trees, the appraisal, the — a sale price depends on the state of the orchard, and the orchard is a disaster. Any lawyer will tell you that. It's just — practical."

It was not practical. It was the least practical sentence ever spoken over burnt coffee. They both knew it. Xanthe looked at him — at the gray in his hair and the pencil back behind his ear and the water stain on the ceiling above his head, at the whole improbable life he had built in the town she'd fled — and felt the anger shift under her like ice on a pond, groaning, unreliable.

"I'll think about it," she said, which was a lie, because she had already decided in the space between one tick of the clock and the next, and she did not know yet whether what she'd decided was to come or to run, only that her whole body had voted on something.

She got as far as the front door. Her hand was on it. The bell waited above her, brass and tarnished and patient.

"Xanthe." His voice behind her, low. "The letters. The ones I wrote in July and August. If they'd reached you—" He stopped. Started again. "Did anything else ever reach you? Anything from anyone? That summer?"

She turned. He was standing by the children's rug with the wolf book still in his hand, and he was looking at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before — not anger, not the old hurt, but something sharper and more frightened. A man checking a floorboard for rot.

"What kind of anything?" she said.

He opened his mouth. And from the back of the shop came a crash, a yowl, and a small voice calling "Daddy! The cat's in the window display *again*," and the moment shattered like a dropped mug, and by the time he'd dealt with the cat and she'd dealt with her own heartbeat, she was on the sidewalk with the bell's ring still fading behind her, and the question — *did anything else ever reach you* — was sitting in her chest beside the swallowed stone, cold and heavy and impossible to put down.

She got three steps toward the rental car before she stopped, in the middle of Main Street, in front of the whole town's quiet watching windows, and thought: *Rosa. What did you do?*

The letter from the lawyer was in her bag. She took it out and read it again, standing right there, and this time she noticed the sentence she'd skimmed past twice before, the bland legalese that had seemed like nothing:

*Enclosed you will find a partial inventory of the estate. Certain correspondence, held in trust, remains to be catalogued.*

Held in trust. Certain correspondence.

Xanthe stood in the middle of Alder Creek with the paper trembling in her hand, eleven years of silence rearranging itself around her like furniture in the dark — and for the first time since the bus, she was not sure which of them had been lied to, or by whom, or how much.
