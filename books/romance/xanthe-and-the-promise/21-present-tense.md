## 21. Present Tense

The orchard smelled like forgiveness.

Casper noticed it first thing every morning now — that green, sweetish breath rising off the rows as he walked them with his coffee, the scent of sap running and blossom buds swelling tight as secrets on a thousand branches he'd once believed were dead. Three weeks of pruning had transformed the place from a graveyard into something closer to a promise. The blighted wood was gone, burned in the big pile behind the cider house; the surviving trees stood in their long ranks with their canopies opened to the light, looking less like patients than like people who had finally exhaled.

He walked past row nine, where Xanthe had taught Amara the difference between a water sprout and a fruiting spur, and row fourteen, where they'd both gotten stuck arguing about whether the old Gravensteins wanted a harder cut than the Pippins. (They did. She'd been right. He'd written it down.) The grass between the rows was still winter-brown at the roots but greening fast, and the whole hillside hummed with bees already scouting the early plums.

At the top of the slope stood the oldest tree — the one his grandmother had called the Grandmother Tree, because of course she had — its trunk wide enough that three children holding hands couldn't circle it, its branches heavy this year with more bud clusters than Casper had seen in a decade. Xanthe said it was because of the pruning. Amara said it was because the tree knew. Casper privately suspected both.

Below it, the café was taking shape.

The old packing shed had been gutted down to its bones and reframed over two weekends by what felt like half the town — Deacon Marsh on the saw, the Okafor cousins on the roof, Mrs. Adeyemi's sons running electrical, and a rotating cast of bookshop regulars who showed up with hammers and opinions and thermoses of terrible coffee. The frame stood open to the morning now, studs and rafters pale as fresh paper, waiting for walls. Xanthe had taped her bakery floor plan to the inside of what would be the front window: ovens there, counter here, a little reading nook in the corner that connected — through a planned gate in the fence — directly onto the orchard path and, beyond it, to the bookshop.

Bookshop, orchard, café. Three doors into the same life.

Casper stood at the edge of the frame with his coffee going cold and watched his daughter teach someone else's child to climb.

* * *

Amara had appointed herself head of instruction, which surprised no one who knew her. She stood at the base of the Grandmother Tree with her hands on her hips in a posture so exactly Xanthe's that Casper's chest ached pleasantly, addressing her student with the gravity of a mountain guide.

"Rule one," Amara said. "You always tell somebody you're climbing."

"I'm telling you," said Marisol Reyes, age six, Xanthe's niece, solemn as a small judge. She had arrived three days ago with a duffel bag and an unshakable conviction that she lived here now, and nobody had yet found reason to correct her.

"Good. Rule two." Amara pointed up into the low fork where the first handholds waited, worn smooth by forty years of children. "You go where the tree says. Not where you say. The tree knows better."

"Does it know my name?"

"It knows everybody's name," Amara said, with total certainty. "It's really old."

Marisol considered this, nodded, and began to climb — slow, deliberate, tongue between her teeth, following the girl eight years her senior as though following scripture. And behind them, carrying a ladder she absolutely did not need for anything, came Xanthe.

Casper watched her the way he had been trying not to watch her for weeks and had recently given up pretending otherwise. She wore her hair tied back with a strip of flour-sack cloth and one of his grandmother's old flannel shirts with the sleeves rolled, and she moved through the rows like someone returning to a language she'd been made to forget — touching bark as she passed, tilting her head to read a branch, murmuring to the trees under her breath. When she reached the Grandmother Tree she set the ladder down, looked up at the two girls tangled in the low branches, and put two fingers in her mouth and whistled the sharp ascending note that meant *careful* in every orchard in the county. Both girls froze. Both girls grinned.

"Casper," she called, without turning around. "Your daughter is teaching my niece to be feral."

"Our daughter is teaching your niece to be an arborist," he called back. "There's a difference. It's about four hundred dollars an hour."

She laughed — head back, unguarded, the laugh he'd spent eleven years forgetting and three weeks relearning — and crossed the grass toward him, and something in his chest performed its now-familiar maneuver of leaping and then steadying itself, like a bird landing.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning."

"You're staring at the café frame again."

"I'm assessing structural integrity."

"You're staring."

"I'm staring," he admitted. "It doesn't look real yet. It looks like a drawing of a thing we decided to want." He turned to face her fully. Sunlight through the budding canopy fell across her face in shifting coins. "Do you ever wake up and think we imagined all of it? The land trust, the town meeting, the eighty-three families who signed on?"

"Every morning," she said. "And then I come out here and Marisol is in a tree and you're lying about coffee, and I think, well. Either it's real or I've finally gone completely mad, and honestly either way I'm staying."

The word landed in him and kept ringing. *Staying.* She said it easily now, the way she said *row nine* and *Saturday*, and every time it sounded to him like a bell rung in an empty church.

"The bank paperwork cleared," he said. "Final signatures Monday. After that the deed belongs to the trust and neither of us can sell it even if we lose our minds separately."

"I heard. Ruth called me at six a.m. to tell me, which means Ruth has been awake since four, which means Ruth is excited, which frightens me."

"She knitted the trust a banner."

"A banner."

"It says THE ORCHARD REMEMBERS. It has apples on it. There are pom-poms, Xanthe."

Xanthe pressed her hand over her mouth, laughing again, and above them Amara shouted down — "Are you two being weird? You're being weird!" — and Casper looked up at his daughter hanging from a limb of the oldest tree in the county, flushed and certain and happy in a way that had taken eleven years and a dead woman's meddling deed to make possible, and felt the thing rise in him that had been rising for days now, patient and enormous, asking to be said.

Not yet, he told it. Saturday.

* * *

Saturday came gray-gold, with a wind off the ridge that carried the smell of rain not yet committed to falling. Story hour filled the shop the way it always did — rugs dragged into a semicircle, toddlers stacked on laps, Mrs. Adeyemi in her corner chair with her knitting, Deacon Marsh pretending he was only there to shelve returns while standing where he could hear everything. Rain drummed softly on the roof that no longer leaked, thanks to a sale that never happened and a town that had refused to let it.

Xanthe read.

She'd been doing it for three Saturdays now, officially invited after the second, and she read the way she did everything — with her whole body, doing voices, letting the children finish the rhymes, pausing at the good parts to make them beg. Today it was a book about a bear who planted a garden for someone he missed, and Casper stood at the register pretending to reconcile receipts and listening to every word, watching her hands turn pages, watching Amara lean against her knee as though she'd always belonged there, watching Marisol asleep against Xanthe's other side with her thumb in her mouth.

When the last page turned, applause — toddler applause, sincere and percussive. Xanthe bowed. Children swarmed her with requests. And Casper set down his pen, because the thing that had been rising in him for days had stopped asking.

He walked to the front of the room. Someone — he suspected Ruth, who had materialized in the doorway with the pom-pom banner and the expression of a woman who knew exactly what was about to happen — turned the music down.

"Xanthe," he said.

She looked up, a child still tugging her sleeve, flour dust on the flannel shirt, and something in his face must have told her, because she went very still.

"Eleven years ago I had a speech prepared," he said. His voice behaved better than he expected. "I wrote it out. I was going to ask you to stay, and I had reasons, and arguments, and a whole future laid out like a floor plan. And then I didn't say any of it, because I listened to the wrong person and my own stupid pride, and you left, and I let the silence do my talking for a decade." A ripple went through the room. Somewhere Mrs. Adeyemi's needles stopped. "So today I'm not giving a speech. I'm just going to say it plainly, in present tense, because we've both had enough of the past tense to last several lifetimes."

He took a breath.

"Xanthe Reyes. Stay."

The word hung in the dusty gold light of the shop.

"Not because of the deed — the deed's done, it belongs to everyone now. Not because of the café, though God knows I want to see what you do with that corner nook. Not because of Amara, though she has informed me that if you leave she's going with you and she has packed a bag twice already. None of it. No conditions, no terms, nothing to sign." He smiled, and his eyes were burning, and he didn't care. "Just this: I love you. I loved you at twenty-four and I love you at thirty-five, and I would like to spend whatever years we get loving you out loud instead of in unsent letters. So — stay. Here. With us. With me. That's the whole question. That's all of it."

The silence lasted three full seconds, which in a room containing forty children and Ruth is a geological era.

Then Amara broke it, because of course she did, standing up from the rug with her fists clenched in agony: "*Say yes, Xanthe!*"

Laughter everywhere, and Xanthe laughing too, crying and laughing, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, walking toward him through the scattered rugs and the crumb-strewn semicircle of the entire town's smallest citizens until she was close enough that he could see the flour in her hair and the tears on her lashes and the answer arriving in her face before her voice caught up.

"Yes," she said.

"Yes?"

"Yes, Casper. Yes, obviously, yes, you enormous dramatic man, yes—" and then he kissed her, in front of everyone, in the middle of story hour, and the room detonated into cheering, and Ruth unfurled the banner over the biography section, and Deacon Marsh was openly weeping into a stack of returns, and Amara jumped up and down shouting "I KNEW IT, I WROTE IT IN THE TREE FIRST," which was a sentence Casper decided to investigate later, much later, possibly never.

Xanthe pulled back just far enough to speak against his mouth. "For the record," she whispered, "I was going to ask you the same thing tomorrow. I had a whole plan. There was going to be a cake."

"There's always a cake."

"There was going to be a *specific* cake—"

"Marry me properly later," he said. "Right now just stay."

"I'm staying," she said, and kissed him again, present tense, no conditions, while the rain finally arrived on the new roof and found nothing to ruin, and outside the wet windows the orchard stood in its long green rows, budding, remembering, beginning.

Above them, unnoticed by anyone but Amara, who smiled and said nothing — for now — a single white petal from the plum branch in the story-hour vase drifted down and settled, lightly, on the open ledger of the community land trust, right beside the line where two names had once refused to sign.

Amara looked at it. Then at her father. Then at the wishing tree visible through the window, far up the hill.

"Huh," she said, in the tone of a girl whose work here was finished — almost.
