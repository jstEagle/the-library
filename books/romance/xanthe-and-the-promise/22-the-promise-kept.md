## 22. The Promise Kept

The oldest tree bloomed like it had something to prove.

Casper stood beneath it at seven in the morning, the day of his wedding, holding a ladder he no longer needed and looking up into a canopy so white it seemed lit from within. A year ago this tree had been half dead — bark split, one limb black with blight, its fruit small and bitter as regret. Now every branch had gone to blossom overnight, the way apple trees do when they've finally decided to trust the season.

"You're going to be late," Amara said from behind him. She was nine now, or nearly — she'd started announcing the countdown to her birthday in February — and she wore her flower-girl dress over rain boots because she'd declared that mud was "basically guaranteed" and she refused to be a casualty. Her hair was braided with white ribbon. She held two cups of coffee like a tiny, imperious waiter.

"One of those better be for Xanthe," Casper said.

"Duh. I'm not bringing you *both*." She handed him his anyway — black, too hot, made exactly wrong the way he liked it — and then stood beside him, craning her neck at the blossoms. "Papa. Do you think Grandma Okafor can see this?"

Casper considered the question the way he'd learned to consider all of Amara's questions: seriously, without flinching. His grandmother's grave was on the hill behind the cider house, under a smaller tree, one Xanthe had insisted they plant rather than buy a stone marker for. There had been a long conversation about that — about forgiveness, about whether grief could coexist with anger, about what you owed the dead when they'd broken your heart on purpose. In the end they'd planted the tree and let the grass grow soft around it, and neither of them had said everything they felt about her, and both of them had decided that was allowed.

"I think," Casper said, "that if she can see anything, she can see this."

Amara nodded, satisfied, and drank half his coffee when he wasn't looking.

* * *

Xanthe woke before dawn in the farmhouse, which was no longer really hers and not really not-hers, which was the whole point of everything they'd built.

She lay in the gray light listening to the orchard wake up — birds first, then the creak of branches, then, distantly, the sound of folding chairs being opened by volunteers who had shown up at six a.m. because that was what this town did now. The Blossom Festival had doubled in size since last spring. There were three food vendors, a string quartet composed entirely of high schoolers, and a waiting list for the cider press demonstrations that Priya ran with terrifying competence. The land trust board met monthly in the bookshop's back room, where the deed still hung framed on the wall — two names on one page, signed in different-colored ink because they'd each grabbed whatever pen was closest.

Xanthe had wanted to frame the quitclaim they never signed instead. Casper had outvoted her. *People should see the thing that almost happened,* he'd said, *so they understand the thing that did.*

She got up and put on the dress hanging on the closet door — simple, blue, chosen in ten minutes at a shop in the city while Amara supervised via video call with the ruthlessness of a seasoned stylist — and went downstairs to the kitchen, where her niece Sofia was already sitting on the counter eating bread dough.

"You're not supposed to be here yet," Xanthe said. "You're supposed to arrive dramatically later."

"Tía Marisol said I could help." Sofia was eight, dark-eyed, fearless in trees, and had attached herself to Amara within four minutes of their first meeting last summer, an alliance that had restructured the entire social order of the orchard. "Also there's a bee in the pantry and I'm not dealing with it alone."

There was, in fact, a bee. Xanthe caught it in a jar and carried it outside, and stood for a moment on the back step with the jar in her hands, looking down the rows of trees going white in the early light.

A year ago she had driven through a storm clutching a letter she'd found in a lawyer's box — the final letter, the one Elena Okafor had intercepted on the morning Xanthe left town, the one that would have changed everything eleven years earlier if a proud old woman hadn't decided she knew best. Xanthe had read it three times in the car with the wipers going and her chest cracking open. Then she'd driven to the bookshop, soaked through, and put it in Casper's hands, and watched him read his own young voice saying *come back, I don't care why you left, come back* — words written to a girl who never received them, kept from her by someone who loved them both in the most destructive way love knows how.

They didn't talk about Elena much anymore. But every year on the anniversary, Xanthe walked up the hill and stood under the young tree for a while, and last fall she'd finally said, out loud, to the grass: *I understand why you did it. I still wish you hadn't. I think that's the truest prayer I know.*

The bee buzzed against the glass. Xanthe unscrewed the lid and tipped it toward the blossoms, and the bee rose into the white light and was gone.

*Okay,* she thought. *Today.*

* * *

By ten o'clock the meadow under the oldest tree looked like a wedding had been planned by people who owned a bookshop and a bakery, which it had.

Folding chairs in uneven rows. An aisle of scattered petals that Amara and Sofia had laid down with the precision of bomb technicians. The string quartet tuning in a key none of them fully agreed on. Folding tables at the edges bearing Xanthe's pastries — almond croissants, honey cakes made with last autumn's cider, a tower of something involving apricots that had taken three attempts and one small kitchen fire. And above it all, the tree, so heavy with bloom that petals came loose in the breeze and drifted down through the whole ceremony like slow, generous snow.

Casper stood at the front in a suit his mother had altered twice, watching the chairs fill. The whole town was there, it seemed — Mrs. Adeyemi from the bank, who had fought for the land trust loan through four rejections; old Tomás Reyes, Xanthe's uncle, who had spent thirty years telling anyone who'd listen that the orchard was doomed and had wept openly at the first blossom festival; the story-hour parents, the cider-press volunteers, the teenagers who'd built the café porch as a summer project and now treated it as personal property. Even Mr. Halloran, who had once tried to sell them out to developers and had since donated, with visible discomfort, the use of his surveying equipment for the orchard expansion.

And there, in the second row, sat a woman Casper hadn't seen in twelve years: Ms. Whitfield, retired now, who had run the town library and had been the first person, a year ago, to say *you know, Elena Okafor used to bring me her letters to post, back in the day — strange woman about her mail.* It had been Ms. Whitfield's offhand remark, over borrowed library books, that had sent Xanthe digging into the estate correspondence in the first place. Casper had thanked her once, formally. Today he crossed the aisle and hugged her, and she patted his arm and said, "About time, dear. Though frankly I always thought you were too stubborn."

"He is," said Amara, passing by with the ring pillow. "Both of them. That's the whole problem. That's why I wrote the vows."

* * *

Xanthe came down the aisle on her uncle's arm, and Casper forgot every word he'd rehearsed.

It wasn't the dress, though the dress was lovely. It was that she was laughing — at something Tomás had whispered, at the quartet's heroic struggle with the processional, at life itself, that laugh he'd spent eleven years remembering and one year relearning — and the petals were coming down around her, and the oldest tree stood over her like a witness that had waited a very long time to give testimony.

She reached him. The officiant — Priya, deputized for the occasion after a town vote that was less a vote than a landslide — cleared her throat.

"Dearly beloved," Priya began, "borrowed persons, and everyone else—"

"Priya," Xanthe warned.

"—we are gathered under a tree that has died twice and come back twice," Priya continued serenely, "which makes it, in my professional opinion as a cider maker, uniquely qualified to speak at a wedding."

Then it was time for the vows, and Amara stepped forward.

She smoothed the paper in her hands. She looked out at the crowd. She took a breath with the gravity of a supreme court justice, and Casper realized, with a lurch, that she had not actually shown him the final draft.

"These vows," Amara announced, "were written by me, but approved by Xanthe, so if Papa doesn't like them that's too bad." A ripple of laughter. She glared it into silence. "Number one. You have to share the coffee machine even when someone makes it wrong on purpose, which is a real thing that happens in this family."

"That's slander," Casper murmured.

"It's documented," Amara said. "Number two. Nobody gets to leave without saying goodbye. Not to the orchard, not to the town, and definitely not to each other. If you're mad, you say you're mad. If you're scared, you say you're scared. This rule exists because of a letter, and everyone here knows about the letter, so I don't have to explain the letter."

"We do not all know about the letter," said a man near the back.

"The letter," Amara explained patiently, "is framed above the counter at the café. Go look at it after. Number three." She paused, and her voice, which had been performing, suddenly wasn't. "This one's mine. When I wished on the wishing tree last spring, I asked for Xanthe to stay forever. And then I felt bad about it, because wishes are supposed to be private, but also because I thought maybe it was too big a thing to ask for." She looked up at them both, and her chin trembled just slightly, and she mastered it, because she was her father's daughter. "But it wasn't too big. It came true. So my vow is: I promise to keep asking for big things. Both of you have to promise that too."

There was a silence in which several adults pretended to have something in their eye.

"We promise," Xanthe said, and her voice cracked clean through the middle of it.

"We promise," Casper said.

"Good." Amara folded her paper with tremendous dignity and returned to her seat between Sofia and Ms. Whitfield, who was already crying.

The rings were passed forward. The quartet played something soft. And then it was just the two of them under the tree, with the petals falling, and Casper found that all the rehearsed eloquence had burned away and only the truth was left.

"Eleven years ago I wrote you a letter and didn't send it," he said. "I told myself it was pride. It was fear. I've had a year of practice saying things in the present tense, so here goes." He took her hands. "I love you. Not the girl who left, not the memory, not the letter. You. The woman who prunes better than I do and won't admit it. Who reads to children like they're the most important audience in the world. Who drove through a storm to hand me the truth when she could have kept it as a weapon." His throat tightened. "I love you. Today. Tomorrow. Out loud. In present tense. Forever, if you'll have it."

Xanthe was crying and laughing at once, which he would learn was simply how she did important things.

"My turn," she managed. "I spent eleven years building a life in a city that never fit, because I thought going back meant admitting I'd failed. Then I came back for a funeral and found out I'd inherited half an orchard with a man I couldn't stand." Laughter moved through the chairs. "I couldn't stand him," she confirmed. "He laddered terribly. He was smug about books. He lied badly to his daughter about who I was." She squeezed his hands. "And then we planted things. And somewhere between the grafting and the cider weekend and the night we read that letter aloud in the rain, I stopped being afraid of coming home. So here's my vow: I'm not leaving. Not when it's hard, not when we fight, not when the roof leaks again — and it will leak again, Casper, I've seen the roof. I choose this. Present tense. Every morning. Forever."

Priya pronounced them married before either of them remembered to wait for her, and the kiss happened under a sudden thick drift of falling petals, and the quartet gave up on the sheet music entirely, and Amara and Sofia threw petals with the aim of artillery officers, and the oldest tree held all of it — the noise, the joy, the two names spoken together at last — inside its white and patient branches.

* * *

The party lasted until the light went gold and then blue.

There was dancing on plywood over the grass. There was a moment when Tomás Reyes danced with both nieces at once and wept again and pretended it was allergies. There was a toast from Mrs. Adeyemi about interest rates and love, which should not have worked and did. At dusk, the wishing tree at the orchard's edge — hung now with hundreds of paper wishes from two festivals' worth of children — flickered with the little lanterns Amara had strung, and Casper saw Xanthe slip away toward it and followed.

She was reading the wishes. Most were small and perfect: *I wish for a pony.* *I wish the cider never runs out.* *I wish Ms. Priya teaches me the press.*

One, in careful nine-year-old handwriting, read: *I wished this last year and it came true, so this year I wish for everyone to get what I got.*

Below it, in different ink, newer: *I wish Sofia's family moves here too. (Working on it. — A.)*

"She's running a campaign," Xanthe said.

"She's running the town," Casper said. "We're just staff."

They stood together in the lantern light, husband and wife, the word still new enough to glow. Down the slope, the café windows were dark and friendly, and above the counter inside — visible even now, if you pressed your face to the glass, as Amara was currently doing with Sofia — hung a plain wooden frame. Inside it, a water-stained page in a young man's handwriting, unfinished, unsent, its last line trailing off where pride had dropped the pen:

*Come back. I don't care why you left. Come back, and I'll spend the rest of—*

Visitors asked about it sometimes. Xanthe had written the little card that hung beside it: *Some words take eleven years to arrive. Read yours out loud.*

"Look at that," Casper said quietly, nodding toward the hill behind the cider house, where the young tree on his grandmother's grave had put out its first blossoms this spring — sparse, tentative, but blooming. "She's flowering."

Xanthe leaned into him. "Everyone blooms eventually," she said. "Even the ones who broke our hearts. Especially maybe them."

They stayed there until the lanterns burned low and the quartet packed up and Amara fell asleep across three folding chairs with a croissant in one hand like a scepter. And when they finally walked back through the rows of trees, past bud and blossom and the green promise of fruit to come, Casper noticed that his wife had stopped at the oldest tree and laid her palm flat against its trunk, the way you greet an elder.

"What did it say?" he asked.

Xanthe smiled up into the branches, into the dark full of white flowers and next year's harvest already forming, patient as a promise.

"It said," she told him, "*keep going.*"

And so they did.
