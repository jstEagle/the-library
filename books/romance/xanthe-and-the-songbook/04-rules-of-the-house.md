## 4. Rules of the House

The generator was where the solicitor's notes said it would be: a red steel box squatting in the woodshed like an animal that had been left out too long. Elias Vane crouched beside it, sleeves rolled, hands black with old fuel, and thought about how many times in his life he had stood in front of an orchestra and told them, with total authority, *follow me*.

The generator did not care about his authority. It coughed, caught, coughed again, and died.

"Choke," said Juno Reyes from the doorway, arms crossed, snow melting on her shoulders. "It's flooded. You're flooding it."

"I know how a generator works."

"You clearly don't."

"I've conducted in halls older than this machine," Elias said, which was not actually a defense of anything and they both knew it. He pulled the choke anyway, waited, tried again. The engine turned over, sputtered, and then — grudgingly, the way all things in this house seemed to work — caught, settling into a ragged but steady thrum that made the lightbulb overhead flicker to life like something waking from a long sleep.

Juno didn't say *I told you so*. That was somehow worse.

They had been in the house for six hours. Six hours since the snow had closed the lane behind them like a held breath, since Astrid Underhill had opened her grandmother's door and found the two of them standing on her porch with their luggage and their histories, looking like exactly what they were: two people who had no right to be there, summoned by letters neither of them had questioned hard enough.

Six hours, and the three of them had moved through the house like planets avoiding collision — polite, orbital, precise. Juno had claimed the east wing without asking. Elias had taken the room over the kitchen because it was the farthest from both of them. And Astrid had disappeared into the depths of the house with a lantern and come back an hour later having apparently decided nothing at all, which was its own kind of answer.

Now the generator ran, and the pipes would eventually thaw, and there was nothing left between them and each other but the talking.

Elias wiped his hands on a rag and stood. "We need rules," he said.

Juno raised one eyebrow. "Rules."

"Terms. A truce. Whatever you want to call it." He kept his voice level, the voice he used for difficult soloists, the one that made people feel heard while he quietly took control of the room. "Three of us. One roof. Unknown duration. If we go into this without structure, we will tear each other apart inside a week, and I include myself in that estimate."

"You're giving yourself a week?" Juno said. "Generous. I'd have given us four days."

"Juno."

"I'm listening." She leaned against the doorframe, and he saw her do the thing she always did — the thing she'd done in every composition seminar, every argument, every silence across fifteen years — assess him, catalog him, file him somewhere. "Go on, Maestro. Propose your terms."

He hated being called Maestro by her. He hated that she knew it.

"The final performance," he said. "October 2014. We don't discuss it. Not obliquely, not accidentally, not at three in the morning when the walls are thin and the wine is open. It doesn't exist in this house."

Something moved behind Juno's eyes — quick, unreadable, gone. "That's your first rule? The thing we all have most in common is the thing we're banned from saying?"

"It's the thing we're least capable of saying well."

"That's not the same as not saying it."

"No," Elias admitted. "But it's what I can offer tonight."

Juno looked at him for a long moment. Snow dripped off her coat onto the woodshed floor, ticking like a metronome. Then she nodded, once, sharp. "Fine. Rule one. What else?"

"Separate wings. We've already done it, so let's make it official. Your territory is yours; mine is mine. No midnight wandering, no accidental encounters engineered to look accidental."

"I would never engineer anything," Juno said, with a straight face that fooled absolutely no one, had never fooled anyone, least of all him.

"Shared labor. The house needs wood, water, cooking, digging out if the plow ever comes. We split it. Three ways."

"And the third rule?"

"That's the third rule."

"I meant—" Juno stopped. Recalibrated. "You said three of us, one roof. You counted chores as two rules."

"I count what keeps the peace."

"You count what keeps you in control," Juno said, but she said it lightly, almost fondly, and Elias felt the strange vertigo of realizing that after everything — after the hall, after the downbeat, after ten years of not speaking — he still knew the exact pitch of her teasing, and some traitorous part of him had missed it.

They carried the argument back into the kitchen, because the kitchen was where Astrid was, standing at the counter with three mugs lined up in front of her and a jar of instant coffee open like a small surrender. The power from the generator reached this room first; a single bulb burned above the stove, and under it Astrid looked pale and composed and entirely elsewhere.

"We have terms," Elias announced, before he could stop himself sounding like a board meeting.

Astrid looked up. For a moment — just a moment — he saw something flicker across her face, something old and tired and almost amused, as if she were watching two children negotiate custody of a playground. Then it was gone, smoothed away, and she was only Astrid, careful and quiet, the woman who had once played like sunlight through water and now held herself like glass someone might knock over.

"All right," she said.

"Rule one," Juno said, sliding onto a stool. "We don't talk about October 2014."

"Agreed," Astrid said.

Too fast. Elias heard it land wrong even as she said it — the word *agreed* arriving before any part of her had visibly considered it, the way you'd agree to a seat assignment, a delivery window, a thing that didn't matter. He watched her hands wrap around the mug of hot water she hadn't yet put coffee into, watched her thumbs move against the ceramic in small circles, and thought: *she's rehearsed this*. She has been agreeing not to talk about it for ten years. We are late to our own embargo.

"Rule two," he pressed on, because stopping would mean examining that. "Separate wings. Shared work. Chores split three ways — wood, water, cooking, the cellar if it needs it."

"Agreed," Astrid said again, and this time she even smiled, a small administrative smile, and put a spoonful of instant coffee into her hot water and stirred it like it was a ritual from a country she no longer lived in.

"And rule three," Elias said, and here he hesitated, because the third rule had assembled itself in his chest sometime during the drive up, somewhere between the last radio signal and the first wall of snow, and he wasn't sure until he said it whether it was mercy or cowardice. "No one leaves. Whatever happens — however bad the weather gets, however bad *we* get — nobody walks out into that. Nobody drives anywhere. Nobody disappears. We stay until the road opens, and when it opens, we decide together what happens next."

Silence. The generator thudded away below them like a second heartbeat for the house. Outside the dark window, snow came down with the patience of something that intended to keep coming for days.

Astrid looked at him across the counter. Her eyes were the same. That was the unbearable part — ten years, a collapsed career, a frozen stage, and her eyes were exactly the same, and they were looking at him the way they had looked at him from the third row of the conservatory library, waiting for him to finish whatever sentence he was too much of a coward to start.

"Agreed," she said.

Third time. Third time, same speed, same flat gentle tone, and Elias felt something cold slide down his spine that had nothing to do with the weather. Anger he could have worked with. Anger was a live wire; anger meant the past still had current running through it. But this — this smooth, immediate, costless consent — this was the sound of a person who had made her decisions years ago and was simply letting them play out. As if the verdict had come down long ago and she was just waiting for the rest of them to catch up.

As if she had already forgiven them, or already condemned them, or already done something more complicated than either, and none of it required discussion.

"There," Juno said softly, watching Astrid too, and Elias realized the composer had heard it as well. "That's settled, then."

"That's settled," Astrid echoed, and drank her terrible coffee, and asked, in a voice pitched carefully at the neutral register of a hotel receptionist, whether either of them had eaten, because there was bread in the freezer and soup on the shelf and she supposed someone should figure out dinner.

* * *

Dinner was soup, made by committee, which meant made mostly by Astrid while the other two hovered uselessly and were assigned tasks to keep them occupied — Elias peeling carrots with the concentration of a man defusing ordnance, Juno finding the good salt within four minutes of entering a stranger's pantry, because of course she did. They ate at the big table under the one working bulb, and the soup was fine, and nobody talked about anything that mattered, and it was, Elias reflected, the most civilized catastrophe he had ever attended.

Afterward the truce revealed its daily architecture. Juno washed dishes and claimed it was because she liked to; Elias split kindling on the back step until his shoulders burned; Astrid went through the house turning off lights they'd turned on, a curator closing a museum. At nine o'clock they reconvened in the sitting room without discussing reconvening, the way musicians find a downbeat without counting one, and discovered that none of them knew how to spend an evening together anymore.

Juno found a deck of cards in a drawer, missing the sevens. They played something half-remembered from conservatory common rooms, arguing about the rules, and Astrid won three hands in a row with a face like still water, and Elias laughed — actually laughed, short and surprised — when Juno accused her of cheating with the missing sevens hidden somewhere on her person.

Then Astrid set down her cards and said, "I'm for bed," and the evening ended the way a movement ends when the player decides it ends, not when the score says so.

Elias lay awake in his room over the kitchen listening to the house tick and settle, the generator's distant pulse, wind testing the shutters like a cautious hand. He ran the day back through his mind the way he'd run a rehearsal recording, hunting for the flaw.

She'd agreed too fast. All three times. He kept circling it. He built scenarios and dismantled them: she wanted them gone and was being polite; she wanted them close and was being careful; she had known about the letters all along; she knew nothing and suspected everything. Every scenario ended the same way — with Astrid Underhill holding some piece of information or intention behind those unchanged eyes, patient as snowfall, letting him and Juno orbit her while she decided — what?

What was she deciding?

At some point near three in the morning he gave up on sleep and went down to check the generator, because checking things was what he did instead of feeling them. The woodshed was bitter cold, his breath ghosting silver in the bulb's weak light. The machine ran on, faithful and ugly. He stood there longer than the task required, hands in his pockets, and became gradually aware of a sound underneath the engine's thrum.

Faint. Distant. Coming through the floorboards of the house above him.

Notes. Long, slow, deliberate. A scale — no, not a scale. Something simpler. An arpeggio, climbing and falling, climbing and falling, careful as a person testing ice.

A cello.

Elias stood very still in the freezing woodshed and did not move toward it, because rule one said the past didn't exist in this house, and whatever was happening in the ballroom above him at three in the morning was either the present or the past, and he was suddenly terrified of finding out which.

He listened until the notes stopped. Then he went back to bed and lay staring at the ceiling, and the last thing he thought before sleep finally took him was that Astrid had agreed to all three rules too quickly —

— and that somewhere in the dark above him, she had just broken one.
