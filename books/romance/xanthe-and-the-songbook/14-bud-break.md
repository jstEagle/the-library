## 14. Bud Break

The vines woke before any of us did.

I came down the cellar steps at dawn with two buckets of water for the pressing room and stopped on the last stair, because the light through the vent had changed. It was the same gray it had been all winter, the same thin slice of morning the window allowed — but something in its quality had shifted, some green undertone I couldn't name. Then I stepped outside and saw it: along the rows, at the joints of every bare cane, small swollen buds like held breath finally released.

I stood in the frost with my hands going numb around the bucket handles and felt something in my chest crack open that I'd been bracing shut since December.

Spring doesn't ask permission. That's what Marguerite's margins said, back in February, when Juno read them aloud over breakfast in a voice pitched to sound casual and wasn't. *Pruning is not punishment,* she'd written, decades ago, in ink gone brown. *It is the gardener admitting she cannot protect everything and choosing, anyway, to help the plant become what it can bear.* Astrid had left the table when Juno read that one. I'd told myself it was the coffee, or the cold, or any of the hundred small excuses we'd all gotten fluent in.

But standing there among the waking rows, I understood why she'd fled. Some truths arrive too early. They need the right season, the way buds need a specific number of cold hours before they'll trust the warmth.

Astrid had had ten years of cold hours. I didn't know if the warmth would come as mercy or as fire.

* * *

She found me in the east rows an hour later. I heard her before I saw her — the crunch of her boots deliberately loud, announcing herself the way you announce yourself to a horse that startles. She'd been doing that since the silence broke, since those three days in March when she worked the vines alone and Juno and I kept the house running around her grief like blood around a wound. Announcing herself. Giving us time to arrange our faces.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning." I didn't look up from the cane I was tying. "Your roses are budding."

"I know. I saw." A pause. "Elias."

There it was. The tone. I set down the twine and turned.

She looked different in spring light. Not softer — Astrid Underhill was never soft, not even at nineteen, not even the night she played my heart out of my chest and into her cello and I never told her so. Sharper, maybe. Like the season had honed her. Her hair was tied back with a strip of red cloth I recognized as one of Juno's scarves, and there was dirt under her fingernails from three weeks of honest work, and she was looking at me with the expression of someone who has decided to have a conversation whether or not the other party survives it.

"You've been circling something for weeks," she said. "Since the letters. Since before the letters. You do this thing where you start sentences and then conduct them away."

"Conduct them away," I repeated.

"You know what I mean. You give the orchestra four bars of your attention and then you cut them off and move to the cellos. You've been cutting yourself off for weeks." She crossed her arms. "I'm asking you to stop."

The frost was melting off the trellis wires in bright drops. Somewhere behind the house, Juno was burning toast — I could smell it, could hear the smoke alarm's distant complaint, could hear Juno arguing with it, which was a whole conversation Juno always lost. Ordinary sounds. The soundtrack of a life I'd stumbled into by way of a forged letter and a snowstorm, and which I now could not imagine leaving.

"Astrid," I said. "Sit down."

"It's thirty-eight degrees."

"Then stand. But stand still."

She stood still. She'd always been good at that, at the terrible patience of waiting for a downbeat. God, the memory of it — her in the practice rooms at conservatory, waiting for my count-in with that half-smile, trusting me absolutely. Trusting me the way you can only trust someone before they've failed you.

I had rehearsed this apology for eleven years. In hotel rooms in Vienna. On trains through the Alps. In the greenroom bathroom in Prague the night I conducted Bruckner's Seventh and thought about her hands for the entire adagio. I had rehearsed it in every register, every tempo, every dynamic from pianissimo to the full cry of a hundred players. And every version I'd rehearsed had been wrong, because every version had been built to make *me* feel forgiven rather than to make *her* feel heard.

So I threw out the score. I conducted nothing. I just spoke.

"The night of the premiere," I said, "when you froze — I want to tell you what actually happened from the podium. Not what you've imagined. What happened."

Her jaw tightened. "We agreed—"

"We agreed in December. Under a generator and instant coffee, with two cars buried in snow and no way off this property. We agreed because we were strangers sharing a roof, and neutrality was the only furniture we had." I took a step toward her. "We're not strangers anymore. And it's April, and the vines are awake, and I am done hiding behind rules I made to protect myself."

"Elias—"

"Let me finish. Please. Because if I stop now I'll never start again, and I've been starting again for eleven years."

The toast smell had faded. The property was very quiet, the way it only is in early spring, before the birds fully commit. Just wind in the bare upper canes, and the drip of meltwater off the trellises, and Astrid breathing.

"You came in on the solo," I said. "Bar 214. And it was — Astrid, it was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard anyone play. I don't say that as flattery. I say it as a conductor who has stood in front of orchestras on four continents. Bar 214 was transcendent. And then bar 215 came, and you stopped."

"I know what I did."

"Do you know what *I* did?" My voice cracked and I let it. "I panicked. That's the truth. The hall was full, the critics were in row F, Juno was in row G looking like a man watching his own execution, and my first chair cellist — my *first chair*, who had rehearsed your part in secret because he didn't trust you, because I'd let him think he shouldn't — leaned over and asked if I wanted him to take it. And I raised my baton and gave the downbeat."

Her face had gone very still. Stillness, in Astrid, is not calm. I knew that then and I know it now. Stillness is the eye of whatever storm she's carrying.

"You cut me off," she said quietly. "In front of nine hundred people."

"I cut you off. Yes." I made myself hold her eyes. "And here is what I have never said to anyone, including myself, until this exact moment, standing in your grandmother's vineyard with my hands shaking: I didn't do it to save the performance. I did it because I was a coward. Because I loved you — I was twenty-four years old and in love with you and terrified of it, terrified of what it would cost me, what it would look like, what *you* would do to my carefully planned life — and in that moment, watching you freeze, some vicious part of me thought: *this is the exit.* This is how I get to be Elias Vane, conductor, instead of Elias Vane, the man who loves Astrid Underhill and has to build a life around that fact. So I took the exit. I gave the downbeat. And I spent eleven years telling myself it was professionalism."

The wind moved through the rows. A bud, somewhere near us, chose that moment to split its casing — I swear I heard it, a tiny sound like a knuckle cracking, though I know that's impossible.

Astrid hadn't moved. "You're saying you ended my career on purpose."

"I'm saying I ended it out of cowardice, which may be worse than purpose. Purpose at least requires conviction." I laughed, and it came out broken. "I could have held the tempo open. Four bars, six, ten — halls will wait for a soloist, Astrid, audiences *ache* to wait for a soloist, it's the oldest contract in music. If I had held the silence open, you might have come back. Or you might not have. But either way, the choice would have been yours. Instead I made it mine. I decided your failure for you, in public, in under two seconds, because deciding it was faster than feeling it."

"Why are you telling me this now?"

"Because Juno told you about the review. And because you went silent for three days, and I watched you work the vines alone, and I realized I was doing it again — arranging things around your pain instead of walking into it. Pruning you. Cutting back the parts of our history that were inconvenient for me, so that what remained would be easier to bear." I shook my head. "Marguerite's book says pruning isn't punishment. But I lied to myself about that too, because I have been pruning *you* since bar 215, and it was punishment, and it was mine."

She was quiet for a long time. Long enough that I counted the drips off the trellis wire — fourteen, fifteen, sixteen — long enough for the sun to clear the ridge and lay a blade of gold across the row between us.

"Say the rest," she said finally.

"There isn't a rest. That's all of it. I chose the orchestra over you. Over us. Before the premiere, after the premiere, every day since. Every city I took, every season I built, every time I told interviewers that music comes first — I was describing a cowardice and calling it devotion." I spread my hands. "That's the apology. There's no elegant version. I ruined the thing I loved to avoid loving it, and I called the ruin leadership."

Astrid looked at me for a long moment. Then she walked past me — past, not away — down the row to the end vine, the old one, the grandmother vine with its trunk twisted like a question mark. She put her palm flat against the bark.

"My grandmother planted this the year my grandfather left," she said, without turning around. "Did you know that?"

"No."

"She told me once that heartbreak is a pruning decision other people make about you. That the only power you get back is deciding what grows next." She turned. Her eyes were dry, which frightened me more than tears would have. "I have spent eleven years thinking the worst night of my life was the night I froze. It wasn't, was it. It was the night the man I loved decided I was finished before I got to decide."

"Yes," I said. "And I would give every concert I've ever conducted to unmake it."

"I know." She said it simply, without cruelty, which somehow landed harder than cruelty. "I believe you, Elias. I believe every word. And I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't know yet what I'm going to do with it. Forgiveness isn't a downbeat. I can't just — pick it up because you've counted me in."

"I'm not asking you to."

"Aren't you? Some part of you is. Some part of you has been standing in this vineyard since December waiting for absolution with your baton raised."

The accuracy of it took my breath away. I nodded. "Yes. That part exists. I'm trying to starve it."

"Keep trying." She picked up the twine I'd dropped and handed it back to me, handle first, the way you hand back a weapon you're not ready to be given. "Accountability isn't a gift I owe you thanks for. It's just — true. And true is a place we can start."

She walked back up the row toward the house, toward the smell of Juno's second attempt at breakfast, and at the gate she paused.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "bar 214 was the best thing I ever played. I'm glad somebody heard it."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with the vines and the sun and the enormous, unfinished quiet of being forgiven nothing and offered everything.

* * *

Juno found me an hour later, sitting on the cellar steps with a glass of the bad rosé Marguerite had left behind, the kind that tastes like apology fruit.

He sat down beside me without a word. He'd gotten good at that, at the wordless seating arrangements of this strange household. For a while we just watched the rows.

"You told her," he said.

"I told her."

"And?"

"And she heard me. And she didn't forgive me. And both of those things are exactly right." I drank. "How did you know to come find me?"

"Because you look like a man who just performed his own autopsy and survived it." He knocked his shoulder against mine. "Also because Astrid came inside, put down the secateurs, and said, and I quote, 'Elias said the true thing. Make sure he doesn't drown in it.'"

I laughed — wetly, embarrassingly. "She said that?"

"Word for word. Then she asked me what bar 214 sounded like from row G." He was quiet a moment. "I told her it sounded like the future arriving early. She didn't ask what I meant. But she wrote it down. In the songbook margin. In pencil."

My chest did something complicated. "She's writing in the songbook."

"She's been writing in the songbook for a week. Little notes. Questions. One of them, Elias—" He pulled out his phone, where he'd photographed it, because of course he had; we catalogued each other's healing like stock traders. The margin note, in Astrid's careful hand, read:

*Grandmother finished nothing on purpose. Maybe finishing is a thing you do together or not at all.*

Below it, smaller, newer:

*Ask them about the finale. Ask them tonight.*

Juno pocketed the phone. "Tonight," he said. "After dinner. She wants all three of us at the piano bench."

The sun was fully up now, the frost burned off, the whole hillside green-tinged and trembling with new growth. Eleven years I had waited for a season warm enough to say what I said this morning, and I had said it, and it had not killed me, and it had not fixed me, and Astrid had taken it the way soil takes rain — slowly, deeply, on her own schedule.

Now she was summoning us. To the bench. To the unfinished finale, the one Marguerite had left *to be completed by whoever stays.*

"Juno," I said, standing, brushing off my trousers, "do you suppose she knows what she's about to ask us?"

He grinned, and it was the grin from the photograph in the cellar, 2009, young and unbroken. "Elias. She's an Underhill. Of course she knows."
