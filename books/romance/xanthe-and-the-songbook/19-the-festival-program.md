## 19. The Festival Program

The village hall smelled of dust and old varnish and, faintly, of the decade it had spent forgotten. Elias stood on the stage where the community orchestra had once rehearsed on Thursdays, and he let the silence of the room tell him what it needed.

A new roof, mostly. The floorboards could be sanded. The piano — God, the piano — had been left uncovered against the north wall, and its soundboard had cracked along the bass bridge like a river delta seen from altitude. It was unplayable. It was also, he thought, running his thumb along the split wood, exactly the kind of thing that could be rebuilt. He had rebuilt worse. He had, if he was honest, been rebuilt worse.

The hall belonged to the village, but the village had stopped using it the year the harvests stopped, and the harvests had stopped the year Marguerite Underhill died. Everything in this valley was one long sentence with the clauses removed. He was beginning to understand that his whole life had been the same — a sentence he'd cut short with a downbeat, and had been pretending ever since was finished.

He took out his phone and called the number the solicitor had given him, a man named Prewitt who spoke as though every sentence were a legal document being read aloud at dictation speed.

"Mr. Vane. To what do I owe the — "

"I want to put on a concert," Elias said. "Harvest festival. Here, in the hall. And I want the vineyard's first harvest in ten years to be the occasion for it."

A pause long enough to contain an entire probate process. "The hall is not, strictly speaking, certified for public assembly."

"Then we'll certify it."

"The piano is — "

"I know what the piano is. I'm having it rebuilt. There's a man in Ashford who owes me a favor and a man in London who owes me two."

Another pause. Then, with the first note of warmth Elias had heard from him: "Marguerite would have liked you, Mr. Vane. She liked people who argued with paperwork."

"Did she?"

"Oh, she *loved* them. She argued with mine for forty years. I have letters." A rustle of paper, actual paper, held to a phone. "She left instructions, you know. In the event of a concert."

Elias went very still. "What kind of instructions?"

"Program notes," said Prewitt. "She wrote them years ago. She said, and I am quoting, *if they ever find their way back to the music, tell them I said the hall holds two hundred and the acoustics forgive everything.*"

Elias sat down on the edge of the stage, his legs suddenly untrustworthy. Outside, the September light was doing something gilded and shameless to the vine rows, and the grapes hung heavy as held breath.

"Mr. Prewitt," he said. "I'm going to need those letters."

* * *

He told them at dinner, because dinner had become the place where things were told. It had happened without anyone deciding it — Juno cooked badly and competitively, Astrid set the table with the ceremony of someone relearning domestic ritual, and Elias washed dishes because it gave his hands something to do that wasn't conducting the conversation. Three people who had spent ten years as headlines to each other had become, over a winter and a spring and a summer, a household. He still didn't have a word for it. He suspected the word was *family* and suspected even more that saying it aloud would break it.

"So," he said, when the plates were cleared and Juno had stopped threatening the bread machine. "The harvest festival. I want to end it with a concert. In the village hall. The finale."

The word *finale* landed in the room like a glass set down too hard.

Astrid's hands went still around the dish towel. She had taken up drying plates as a form of meditation, and Elias had learned to read her through it: the tempo of her hands was the tempo of her heart. Right now the tempo was gone entirely. Rest. A fermata held so long it stopped being music and became a held breath.

"The hall," she said, "isn't — "

"It will be. I'm having it restored. Roof, floor, piano. Prewitt has your grandmother's program notes; she wrote them for this. Years ago. She *expected* this, Astrid. She left instructions like a woman leaving a light on."

"That's not the same as — " Astrid set the towel down with terrible precision. "You're asking me to perform. Publicly. On a stage."

"I'm asking you to consider it."

"No." Not angry. Worse than angry — administrative. The voice she used for cataloging. "No, Elias. You were there. You know what a stage does to me."

"I know what *that* stage did," Elias said, and heard his own voice go careful, go conductor-precise, the way it did when he was terrified. "And I know this isn't that stage. Different room. Different decade. Different piece — *our* piece, one we built with our own hands, wine stains and all. There is no version of this where the music ambushes you. You know every bar. You *wrote* half of them."

"I froze on music I knew every bar of."

The sentence dropped into the kitchen and lay there. Juno, who had been leaning against the counter with the studied stillness of a person trying to be furniture, stopped pretending.

"Astrid," Juno said quietly.

"Don't." Astrid held up a hand. "Please. Both of you. I need — I'm going to check the east rows. The pinot's ready a week early and nobody's netted it."

She left. The door didn't slam. Astrid never slammed doors; she closed them the way she ended phrases, cleanly, with the sound carefully swallowed. Elias stared at the towel she'd folded into a perfect rectangle and felt the old guilt arrive on schedule, punctual as a metronome.

"You pushed," Juno said.

"I offered."

"You *programmed*. There's a difference, and you know it, because you've been programming other people's courage your whole career." Juno crossed to the window, watching Astrid's figure shrink down the row of vines. "She's not one of your soloists, Elias. You can't cue her in."

"I know she's not." Elias stood. "That's rather the point. She's not anyone's anything. That's what we spent the spring teaching her, and what she taught us, and I will not stand here and be lectured about — " He stopped. Breathed. "I'm asking her to come back to the thing that was taken from her. Not by us. By her own belief that we — " He couldn't finish it. Even now, even after everything, the shape of that night was a room he could only enter alone.

Juno's expression did something complicated. "Ask her again," Juno said finally. "Not tonight. But ask her again. And when she says no the second time, Elias — don't you dare take it for an answer, and don't you dare make it a demand. There's a third thing, and you're going to have to find it."

* * *

He found her in the east rows at golden hour, netting the pinot with the economy of someone who had learned the work properly. She didn't look up when his boots crunched the cover crop. She always heard him coming; she'd told him once that he walked like a downbeat, and he had never recovered from the accuracy of it.

"Two hundred," she said, before he could speak. "The hall holds two hundred. She wrote it down."

"She did."

Astrid tied off a net corner. "She wrote program notes. For a concert that didn't exist. For a granddaughter who hadn't played in — " Her voice did something and she let it. "Ten years, Elias. I haven't played for anyone in ten years. I played for *you* in the ballroom, in February, and my hands shook for an hour after. And that was two people and a locked door."

"The festival crowd won't be two hundred strangers. It'll be the Coopers, and the Hollis family, and Prewitt, and the man who delivers the propane, and Mrs. Adeyemi from the farm shop who has been asking me since April when you'll play for her." He took the netting shears from her hands — she let him, which was its own small miracle — and held them hostage against his chest. "It'll be people who watched you prune their vines. Who ate your bread. Who are going to be there regardless, because it's the first harvest in a decade and this valley has been starving for a reason to celebrate. The only question is whether the concert happens with you or around you."

"That's a cruel way to put it."

"It's an honest way. I've been crueler." He handed the shears back. "I was crueler, once, in the worst possible way, on the worst possible night, and I have spent ten years conducting other people's orchestras trying to conduct my way out of it. It doesn't work, Astrid. There's no tempo fast enough. The only way through that night is *back through it*, and this — " he gestured at the rows, the hall's roofline visible past the winery, the whole small golden architecture of the life they'd built — "this is as close to a fair fight as the world is going to offer you."

Astrid was quiet for a long time. A crow argued with another crow somewhere over the treeline. When she spoke, her voice was level and it cost her visibly.

"Twice," she said. "You've asked me twice now. And I'm going to say no a second time, and I need you to hear the no, Elias, because it isn't a no to you and it isn't a no to the music. It's a no to the version of me that stood on that stage and died in front of nine hundred people while the man I loved cut her off with a flick of his wrist." She met his eyes. "So. No. Ask me again and I'll still say no. That's my answer."

"Understood," Elias said, and his chest felt like the cracked soundboard. "Then I'll program the concert around you. The finale stays on the program. I'll play the piano part myself, and Juno can — "

"Juno can *what*?"

They both turned. Juno was standing at the end of the row, work jacket over one shoulder, having clearly heard more than either of them wanted. Juno's face had the expression Elias had learned to fear slightly more than anger: the composer's expression, the one that meant a structure had been seen and was about to be revised.

"Around her," Juno repeated. "You're going to perform her finale *around* her. At her grandmother's harvest. In her grandmother's hall. With her grandmother's program notes." Juno walked down the row slowly, gravel to grass to gravel. "No. Absolutely not. I'm not letting either of you do this."

"Do *what*?" Astrid's voice cracked upward. "There's no *this*, Juno. I said no. It's settled."

"It's not settled, because a no that has to be said twice isn't a no, it's a *wound with a door in it*, and I have spent ten years watching both of you nail that door shut from opposite sides." Juno stopped between them, close enough that Elias could see the tremor in the jaw that Juno would deny to the grave. "You want to know what I did after that night? I didn't write for four years. Four years, Elias. You at least got to keep conducting. I sat in a room in Berlin with the knowledge that my piece had broken the best cellist I ever heard, and I couldn't put two notes together without hearing her stop." Juno turned to Astrid. "And you. You think the stage is what broke you. It wasn't the stage. It was the *silence after* — nine hundred people not breathing, and the review you never read, and the two of us gone by morning. The stage didn't do that. *We* did that, by leaving you alone inside the moment. So no. I will not stand in that hall and perform your finale as a trio of two while the person it was written for nets grapes fifty yards away. I refuse. That's not a concert. That's a reenactment."

The rows went very quiet. Somewhere a tractor started, absurdly domestic.

"Then what," Astrid said, very softly, "do you want, Juno?"

"I want," Juno said, "the third thing." And looked at Elias. "You remember. I told you to find it."

"I remember."

"Then find it *now*, because I'm out of patience and she's out of nets to hide behind, and I have carried this piece for ten years and I will not watch it premiere as a eulogy." Juno's voice finally broke, and Juno let it, standing there in the golden light with grapes hanging heavy on either side like an audience holding its breath. "Elias. The third thing. *Now*."

And Elias Vane, who had conducted symphony halls on four continents, who had held two hundred musicians in the palm of one hand, stood in a vineyard row between the two people who had broken him and been broken by him, and understood that the third thing was not a compromise, not a smaller stage, not a private performance with the doors locked.

The third thing was that she had to *want* it — and that wanting it was allowed. That the stage was not a debt to be paid or a sentence to be served. That the finale was not an examination she could fail.

He opened his mouth to say so.

And Astrid, who had not touched a stage in ten years, who had said no twice, looked past both of them toward the village hall — toward the roofline and the light on the windows and the piano waiting inside it with its broken heart — and said, in a voice none of them had heard before:

"The hall holds two hundred," she said. "She wrote it down."
