## 14. Meeting His Mirror

The girl came up the vineyard road at half past four on a Thursday, when the light had gone long and gold and the rows threw shadows like piano keys.

Julian saw her from the trellis where he was working—thinning the canopy on the old Chardonnay block, his hands sticky with sap and green fruit—and at first he thought nothing of it. Children cut through Halcyon all the time; it was a shortcut between the village and the reservoir path, and he'd learned in his first weeks back that a fence meant nothing to an Aldercombe kid who'd grown up treating the vineyard as common ground. He kept pruning. Then he looked again.

She was walking the rows like she was reading them. Not wandering—*reading*. Her head tipped slightly, her eyes moving from vine to vine with the particular attention of someone cataloguing, comparing, filing something away. She wore a canvas satchel across her body and her hair was dark, dark as his had been before the sun of three continents bleached it, and she walked with her chin up and her shoulders square in a way that made something in Julian's chest go strange and cold.

He knew that walk. He'd seen it in old photographs of himself that his mother kept in a drawer he was never supposed to open.

She stopped six feet away, at the end of the row, and looked up at him without a trace of shyness.

"You're thinning," she said. "Not pruning."

Julian set down his shears slowly. "That's right."

"My mum says you can tell everything about a grower by whether they thin or just prune. Pruning is what you do for looks. Thinning is what you do so the rest survives." She said it evenly, factually, the way she might recite a line from a book she'd memorized. "You're doing it right. The clusters are too heavy on the east rows. They'd ripen unevenly."

"Who are you?" he asked, though some part of him—the part that had gone cold and ringing since the moment she'd come up the drive—already knew, already knew with the terrible certainty of a man hearing his own voice played back through a child's mouth.

"Wren Amberdale." She said it plainly. No flourish. And then, because she was clearly not a girl who wasted motion: "I have questions."

* * *

He should have sent her home. That was the thought that ran on a loop beneath everything else, even as he led her to the bench outside the winery door and got her a glass of water from the kitchen and sat down across from her in the slanting light. She was twelve years old. She had walked two miles alone to ask a stranger questions, and every adult instinct he possessed said *call her mother, call someone, this is not yours to handle.*

But he looked at her face—at the set of her jaw, the directness of her eyes, the way she held her water glass with both hands like it was something precious—and he understood that sending her home would be the one unforgivable thing. She hadn't come to be managed. She had come because no one would tell her the truth, and she had decided to collect it herself.

"I know who you are," Wren said, before he could find a word. "Obviously. You own the vineyard now. You're Julian Voss. Your family used to own it before, and then you left, and then your father sold it, and then you bought it back." She paused. "You also wrote a letter to my mum in March. I read it over her shoulder. She didn't know."

Julian's stomach dropped. "Wren—"

"I'm not finished." She took a sip of water. "In the letter you said you hoped Aldercombe remembered you kindly, which is a weird thing to say unless you did something wrong. And you said—" here she closed her eyes briefly, retrieving it, and Julian realized with a lurch that she had the whole letter by heart— "you said, 'There are things I should have said twelve years ago, and I intend to say them.' Twelve years ago I was born. So." She opened her eyes. "Is he my dad?"

The question landed in the courtyard like a stone into still water, and for a long moment Julian could not move. A bee droned somewhere in the lavender by the step. Down the hill, the vines went on breathing their green breath, indifferent.

Every answer he owned was a lie or a theft. *No* was a lie. *Yes* was a theft—it would take from Tamsin the telling, the one thing she might still want to own. He sat with that for five full seconds, and Wren watched him do it, and he could see her counting, could see her deciding what his silence meant.

"The honest answer," he said finally, "is that I don't know. But I believe so. And I think your mother believes so too, and has believed it since the day you were born, and that she has raised you magnificently and alone and that it is not my place—or yours—to decide this today."

Wren absorbed this. He watched her turn it over, watch her test each clause for softness, for evasion. She found none, apparently, because after a moment she nodded once, sharply, like a judge accepting testimony.

"That's the most honest thing any adult has said to me in about two years," she said. "Most of them either lie fast or lie slow. You lied slow, but you didn't actually lie." She set down the glass. "Why did you leave? Before I was born. Mum won't say. She just does this thing where she gets very busy with the stockroom whenever anyone mentions the Voss family, which is basically never, because everyone in town treats them like a ghost story."

Julian looked out at the rows. The truth was a country he'd been exiled from for twelve years, and here was a girl holding the gate open, and she deserved better than the version he told at dinner parties—which was no version at all.

"Your grandfather—Tamsin's father—ran the bookshop," he began. "And my father ran this place. And my father believed, with his whole being, that a Voss married a Voss's worth of land, and that anything else was a phase. When I was twenty-four he gave me a choice. Stay in Aldercombe and be disowned—no inheritance, no name, no future in wine, which was the only future I knew how to want—and watch this place rot under his spite. Or take the posting in Bordeaux he'd arranged, learn the business properly, and come back in five years with his blessing and the estate intact." He heard his own voice go flat and careful. "I told myself I was choosing the responsible thing. I told myself five years was nothing. I told your mother—I didn't tell her anything, that's the truth of it. I wrote her a coward's letter and I got on a train, and I let myself believe that distance would make it clean."

"Did it?"

"No." He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "It made it filthy. It made it twelve years of filth."

Wren was quiet for a while. She looked out at the vineyard the way he'd seen her look at it from the road—reading it—and then she said, "Mum works fourteen-hour days during festival season and she never complains, but sometimes I catch her doing sums on the back of receipts, and she stops when she sees me looking. Is that about money?"

"It's about money," Julian said. "Among other things."

"And the man Hale. Marcus Hale." Her mouth did something complicated around the name. "He comes into the shop and buys books he never reads—he buys first editions and leaves them in the car, I've seen them—and he asks Mum questions that sound friendly but aren't. Last week he asked how long we'd had the lease. Nobody asks that unless they already know the answer." She turned those eyes on him, his own eyes, and Julian felt the floor tilt. "What does he want?"

"He wants leverage," Julian said. "Over me. Over the town. Possibly over your mother most of all, because—" he stopped himself, but too late, and Wren caught it instantly.

"Because of me," she said. "Because whatever I am to you, I'm also a secret, and secrets are worth money to people like him."

Julian felt something rise in his throat that was equal parts fury and awe. Twelve years old. Twelve years old and she had mapped the shape of the trap faster than the adults caught inside it.

"Wren," he said, leaning forward, "listen to me very carefully. What Marcus Hale suspects, he cannot prove, and what he cannot prove cannot hurt you. But if he ever approaches you—if he says anything to you about your mother, about me, about money—you walk away and you tell her immediately. Do you understand? You don't answer him. You don't negotiate. You come get your mother."

"I'm not scared of him," Wren said, with the magnificent stupidity of the young.

"You should be. Not because he'll hurt you—because he'll *use* you, and you won't see it happening until it's done." He held her gaze until she dropped it, which took longer than it would have taken an adult, and cost her visibly more. "Promise me."

"I promise I'll tell Mum," she said, which was not quite the promise, and he decided to take it.

They sat in the gold light while the shadows lengthened across the yard. Somewhere behind the winery, the fermentation tanks ticked as they cooled. Wren finished her water and then asked, quieter, "Do you want to be? My dad?"

It was the only question all afternoon that sounded like a child asking it, and it undid him more thoroughly than all the forensic ones combined.

"Yes," Julian said. His voice cracked on it and he let it. "More than I have wanted anything in a very long time. But wanting isn't the same as deserving, and deserving isn't mine to declare. That's a conversation I need to have with your mother—with both of you—and it needs to happen the right way, not on a roadside because a twelve-year-old did better detective work than three adults."

"She's going to be furious," Wren said. There was no fear in it. If anything, there was a kind of grim satisfaction, the satisfaction of a scholar whose thesis has been confirmed. "She hates being managed. She calls it 'being handled.' If she finds out you knew I figured it out and didn't tell her—"

"Then we tell her together," Julian said. "Tonight. Tomorrow morning, at the latest. We walk into the shop and we put everything on the counter—everything—and we let her be as furious as she needs to be, and we don't defend ourselves, and we don't rush her. Agreed?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a treaty negotiation. "Agreed. But I get to say my part first. I did the work. I earned it."

"You did the work," Julian agreed, and something in his chest ached so badly he nearly put his hand over it. "You earned it."

She stood, brushing off her skirt, and shouldered her satchel, and for one absurd moment Julian wanted to say *stay, stay for dinner, stay forever,* and understood that he had no right to any of it yet. She was halfway down the row when she turned back.

"For what it's worth," she said, "you thin grapes correctly. Mum would say that matters more than the other stuff."

Then she was gone, small and straight-backed down the golden rows, and Julian stood in the emptying courtyard with his heart hammering like a fist on a locked door, knowing that tomorrow everything changed, and fearing it, and—for the first time in twelve years—not dreading it.

* * *

He spent the evening preparing, because preparation was the only prayer he knew. He wrote three drafts of what he would say to Tamsin and burned none of them, simply left them in a drawer, useless. He cleaned the winery tasting room though no one was coming. At nine he stood at the window of the old house and looked down at the village lights—Aldercombe strung along its valley like embers—and rehearsed the sentence that mattered: *I believe Wren is my daughter, and I have known since the day I saw her in your shop, and I stayed silent because I was afraid, and I am done being afraid.*

He was still standing there when he saw the car.

It came up the valley road slow, headlights sweeping the stone walls, and turned in at his own gate without hesitation. Julian knew the car—had seen it parked outside the bookshop twice, had watched Marcus Hale unfold from it with that unhurried grace of a man who had never once been kept waiting. Julian went downstairs and opened the front door before Hale could knock.

"Voss." Marcus smiled in the porch light. He was immaculate even at nine at night, even in the country—charcoal coat, no scarf, as if weather were a courtesy extended to lesser men. "Forgive the hour. I was driving back from Ashcombe and saw lights. Thought I'd offer a courtesy."

"What courtesy?"

Marcus's eyes moved past him, taking inventory of the hall with a connoisseur's flick—a ledger behind the pupils. "A professional one. Man to man." He folded his hands. "I'm going to make you an offer on the development parcel next week, publicly, at the number you'll refuse. And then I'm going to make the town an offer it will accept. None of that requires you. But I've come to believe, lately, that you and I share a certain... interest. In the bookshop. In its proprietor."

Ice moved down Julian's spine. "Say what you came to say."

"Oh, I intend to." Marcus's smile deepened by a single degree, and Julian understood, with the clarity of a man watching a blade leave its sheath in slow motion, that whatever came next had been composed in advance, polished, timed. "I only wanted to give you the chance to hear it from me first. As a courtesy. Since we're going to be such close neighbors."

Behind Marcus, far down the valley, the lights of Aldercombe burned on—among them, surely, the lamp in the bookshop window that Tamsin left burning every night, the lamp Julian had watched from this same window for weeks like a man watching a lighthouse from a wreck he couldn't swim away from.

Marcus stepped closer, and lowered his voice as though confiding something almost kind.

"Twelve years is a long time, Voss. Long enough for a great many things to happen that a man wouldn't know about. Unless he looked."
