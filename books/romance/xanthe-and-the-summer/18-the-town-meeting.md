## 18. The Town Meeting

The village hall smelled of floor polish and nervousness, the way it always did before anything important happened in Aldercombe. Tamsin stood at the back with Wren's hand in hers, feeling her daughter's pulse tapping against her palm like a second heartbeat, faster than her own.

The hall was full. That was the first surprise. She had expected maybe forty people, the usual civic core—Edith Marsh, the vicar, a scattering of farmers who came for the free tea. Instead every folding chair was taken and people stood along the walls, three deep by the doors. The Hollis brothers from the garage. Mrs. Penderel, who hadn't left her cottage since her hip went bad two winters ago, seated near the front with her stick across her knees like a drawn sword. Even the Delgados had driven over from the far side of the valley.

They had all come because of Marcus Hale.

He stood at the front beside the chairman's table, immaculate in charcoal grey, hands clasped loosely as though he were about to deliver good news. Behind him, a screen displayed an artist's rendering: Halcyon transformed into *Hale Aldercombe Estates*—glass-fronted tasting rooms, a spa carved into the hillside, forty holiday lodges fanned out among the vines like white teeth.

"Friends," Marcus began, and his voice carried the way it always did, warm and measured, a voice built for boardrooms that had learned to sound like kitchens. "I won't take much of your evening. You know what I'm proposing. You've seen the plans. Jobs. Investment. A future for this valley that doesn't depend on one harvest going well or one family's luck holding."

Tamsin's stomach tightened. Beside her, Wren whispered, "Mum, why is everyone looking at us?"

"Because we're late," Tamsin said, which was true, and not the reason.

She had spent the day deciding. All through the morning rush of customers, all through the afternoon while she alphabetized a shelf no one had touched, she had turned it over: let Marcus speak first, control nothing, or stand up before he could shape the story of her life into something unrecognizable. Julian had called at noon—she'd heard the resolve in his voice, the way he said *whatever happens tonight, I'm with you*—and she had told him only, *Don't fight my battles for me.* He'd gone quiet, then said, "I never learned how to do anything else," and hung up.

Now Marcus was scanning the room, and his eyes found her, and there it was—the flicker, the blade being unsheathed behind the smile. He had told her what he would do. He had given her until six o'clock to sign. It was nearly seven.

"I want to address one thing before we open discussion," Marcus said, "because I believe this community deserves honesty. There have been... private arrangements made between certain residents and Mr. Voss's operation. Arrangements that may have influenced opinions here tonight." He paused delicately. "In particular, I think it's fair to ask whether the voice of the bookshop—that beloved institution—is entirely its own."

A murmur moved through the hall like wind through wheat.

Tamsin felt Wren's grip tighten. "Mum," she breathed, "what's he doing?"

"What I should have done weeks ago," Tamsin said, more to herself than to her daughter. She squeezed Wren's hand once—a question, a permission—and Wren, twelve years old and braver than anyone in this room, nodded.

Tamsin walked down the aisle.

It was thirty feet of worn carpet and it felt like crossing a frozen lake, every step testing whether the ice would hold. Faces turned. Edith Marsh's mouth formed a small O. Somewhere near the back, someone stopped whispering mid-word.

Marcus watched her come with an expression she could almost admire—surprise quickly folded away into courtesy. He extended a hand toward the microphone as if offering her the stage graciously. "Ms. Amberdale. We're honored."

She didn't take the microphone. She stood beside the chairman's table, facing the room, and found Julian near the side wall. He looked like a man watching someone walk into deep water, ready to dive in after her whether she wanted him to or not. She gave him the smallest shake of her head. *Stay.*

"My name is Tamsin Amberdale," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; inside, everything was shaking loose. "I've lived in this village my whole life except for four years, and I've run the bookshop for fourteen. Most of you have bought books from me. Some of you—" she looked at Edith Marsh, at old Tom Hollis, at Mrs. Penderel—"some of you sold me your houses' worth of patience when I was twenty-two and terrified, and I never asked any of you why you were so kind."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

"So I'm going to be kind back, and tell you the truth, all of it, before Mr. Hale tells you his version."

She saw Marcus shift. Just slightly. A man recalculating.

"Twelve years ago I had a daughter," Tamsin said. "Her father was Julian Voss. His father threw him out of this valley for loving me—for choosing a bookseller over the vineyard—and Julian left, and I didn't tell him I was pregnant, because I was proud, and hurt, and twenty-two, and I thought love that leaves doesn't deserve to be informed." Her throat caught on the words but she pushed through them. "That was my choice and my silence and I own it. Wren is his daughter. He found out this summer. Whatever else happens tonight, that part is nobody's business but ours—but I'm telling you because Mr. Hale has been using it against me, and I refuse to let him spend my daughter like currency."

The hall erupted—not into noise exactly, but into a collective intake of breath, a hundred people inhaling at once. Edith Marsh pressed a hand to her chest. Tom Hollis said, loudly, "Well, I'll be damned," and his brother elbowed him.

"And here's the rest," Tamsin went on, louder now, finding her feet under her. "Mr. Hale holds the mortgage on my building. He bought it quietly, through intermediaries, along with debts from half this valley—I've seen the ledger. Last week he offered to forgive mine if I'd stand up tonight and support his development. And when I wouldn't sign, he threatened to tell this room about Wren—his version of it. A version where I hid a child to extract leverage from Julian Voss. Where my silence was a scheme."

She turned to face Marcus directly. Up close she could see the composure holding, barely, like ice over moving water.

"You wanted to play this card," she said. "So I played it first. That's the difference between us, Mr. Hale. I can afford the truth. You can't."

For a moment, the hall was absolutely still.

Then Mrs. Penderel's stick cracked against the floorboards. Once. Twice. The sound rang through the room like a bell, and every head turned to her.

"Well," the old woman said, in the voice she'd presumably used on misbehaving farmhands for sixty years, "that explains a great deal." She looked at Marcus with eyes like flint. "Young man, do you know who held the note on my cottage before you? Because I do. And the year my husband died, that note was quietly forgiven, and I was told never to ask by whom. This valley has been carrying each other's debts since before you were born, and we don't make weapons of them."

"We knew," Edith Marsh said, standing now, her voice trembling but upright. "About Wren. About whose eyes those are. Did you think we were blind, Tamsin? That girl has been the image of the Voss men since she could walk. We kept quiet because it wasn't ours to tell."

Tom Hollis got up too, slow and deliberate. "And I'll say this for the record. Hale's people offered me forty percent over market for the garage last spring. Through a solicitor in Exeter, so I wouldn't know it was him. When I said no, my fuel supplier suddenly got difficult. Coincidence?" He spat the word.

One by one, they rose. Not all at once—nothing in Aldercombe happens all at once—but steadily, the way morning comes. Farmers and shopkeepers, the vicar, the Delgados, people Tamsin had served tea to and argued with about parking for fifteen years, standing up in the yellow light of the village hall, turning their faces toward the front of the room where Marcus Hale stood alone with his rendering of glass and white lodges glowing behind him.

Marcus looked out at them, and Tamsin watched something extraordinary happen: the charm drained out of his face and left behind something older and barer. Not defeat—not yet. Recognition. As if he were seeing, for the first time, exactly what kind of town this was, and understanding that he had been trying to buy a thing that had never been for sale.

"The vote," the chairman said faintly, "the vote on the development proposal—"

"Is dead," said Tom Hollis flatly. "Bury it."

Marcus gathered his papers with unhurried precision, but his hands weren't quite steady. At the edge of the stage he paused beside Tamsin. For a moment she thought he might say something cruel, and she braced for it.

Instead he said, very quietly, "You played it well."

"It wasn't a game," she said.

"No," he agreed. "That's rather the point." And he walked out through the doors he'd entered like a conqueror, alone.

* * *

Afterward, the hall emptied slowly, reluctantly, everyone wanting to touch her arm, to say a word. Edith Marsh hugged her hard enough to creak ribs. Mrs. Penderel gripped her wrist and said, "Your grandmother would have stood up exactly like that," which undid Tamsin more than anything else that night.

Wren found her last, pushing through the thinning crowd, and threw her arms around her mother's waist in front of everyone, which she hadn't done in public since she was nine.

"You told everybody," Wren said into her coat.

"I told everybody."

"Even the part where it was your fault?"

"Especially that part." Tamsin tipped her daughter's chin up. "Are you all right?"

Wren considered this with the gravity she gave all important questions. "Everyone already knew," she said finally. "Mrs. Marsh used to call me 'little Voss' when I bought sweets. I thought she meant the shoes." A pause. "Can we go home? I've got homework."

And just like that, the vault was empty, the secret was weather, and her daughter needed to conjugate French verbs. Tamsin laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, the first in weeks—and kissed the top of Wren's head.

Julian was waiting by the doors. He didn't approach. He simply stood there with his hands in his pockets, watching mother and daughter together, and when they reached him he said, "You were magnificent."

"I was terrified."

"Yes," he said. "Both things can be true. I'm learning that." He hesitated, and she saw him wrestling with something. "There's something you should know. Marcus came to see me this afternoon, before the meeting. He gave me an ultimatum—abandon the project, or he'd release documents painting you as a schemer. I told him to do his worst." His jaw tightened. "But that's not the part that matters. The part that matters is that he mentioned something else. Something about the original sale of Halcyon. About my father's ledger. He said, 'Ask your son what his leaving actually bought.' And Tamsin—he was smiling when he said it. Like he knew something I don't."

Outside, the June night had gone cool and clear, stars sharp over the dark line of the vineyard hills. Somewhere up there, in the old house, a ledger waited in a drawer—or didn't. Tamsin took Julian's hand, because some things you did without deciding.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Tonight, we go home."

But she felt it already, the ground shifting again beneath them both, and wondered what other truths this summer intended to dig up before it was done.
