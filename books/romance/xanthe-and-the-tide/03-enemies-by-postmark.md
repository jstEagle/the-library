## 3. Enemies by Postmark

The blue box sat on my counter like a confession nobody had made yet.

Wren Alcott had left it there the previous afternoon, along with a clipboard, three pens that didn't work, and an expression of such hopeful authority that I'd agreed to judge the festival letters before I understood what I was agreeing to. That was her gift, I think. She could make surrender feel like volunteering.

"Two evenings," she'd said. "You read, you score, you seal them back up. No names — they're anonymous by design. You're judging the writing, not the writer."

"The writing is the writer," I'd said.

"That's very romantic for a man who alphabetizes his grief."

I hadn't answered that, mostly because I couldn't tell if it was an insult or a compliment, and partly because Juniper was watching me from behind a shelf with the particular intensity of a child cataloguing evidence.

So now it was Tuesday night, the shop was closed, the CLOSED sign turned, and the blue box held two hundred and eleven envelopes that belonged to strangers. The rules were simple: score each letter one to five on voice, honesty, and craft. Disqualify anything that broke anonymity. Return everything to Wren by Friday.

I made coffee I didn't need and cleared a space among the returns pile, and I began.

* * *

The first hour was easy. Small town, small letters. A fisherman writing to his brother about the price of crab and the silence of their dead mother's kitchen. A widow describing her garden to someone she suspected was twelve years old. A teenager who wrote entirely in questions, which should have been exhausting but was instead the most honest thing in the box so far. I gave it a five on voice and felt something in my chest unclench slightly, the way it always did when language worked.

This was the part of the festival I'd never admitted to liking. For one evening, Xanthe stopped being a town where everyone knew everyone's business and became a town where everyone told the truth. Anonymity did that. Take away the face and the history and the pity — God, the pity, the way people still looked at me in the grocery store, ten years on, like grief was a coat I couldn't take off — and what was left was just a person saying a true thing to another person.

My father would have hated it. He believed words were contracts. He signed his name to everything, even his complaints, even his apologies. Especially his apologies.

I scored the teenager a four on craft because the questions circled without landing, and moved on.

* * *

Envelope forty-seven stopped my hand before my brain caught up.

It wasn't the handwriting — the festival letters were all handwritten, and half of Xanthe wrote in the same careful schoolteacher cursive. It was the first line:

*"There is a specific loneliness in being the person who asks the questions. Everyone thinks you're brave for asking. Nobody wonders whether you already know the answers."*

I read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you reread a sentence when you suspect it's been written about you.

I knew this prose. Not the way you know a stranger's face — the way you know a room you've argued in. I'd spent three weeks annotating returned books with pencil marginalia aimed at whoever kept checking out Neruda and returning him dog-eared, and I'd know that rhythm anywhere: the long clause that pretends to be casual, then the short sentence that lands like a dropped glass. The parenthetical asides that were never really asides. The habit of ending on a question that wasn't rhetorical at all but a genuine door left open, which was somehow more disarming than any statement would have been.

Wren Alcott.

Of course. She ran the festival; nothing in the rules said she couldn't enter it. She'd probably entered it every year since she invented it. And technically — I flipped through the rule sheet she'd left, hoping for an out — technically she hadn't broken anonymity. She'd written under no name. She'd broken nothing except my ability to pretend I didn't recognize her.

By the rules, I should have set her aside. Judge conflict of interest, disclose, recuse. I'm a bookseller, not a judge, but I understand conflicts. I've lived inside one for a decade; it has my father's name on it.

Instead I scored her letter. Voice: five. Honesty: five. Craft: five. Then I sat there looking at the scores like a man who'd just caught himself doing something he'd have to explain later.

There were more of hers in the box. Pen-pal festivals run on correspondence, not single letters — each participant writes three over the season, delivered through the box, responses routed by number rather than name. I found her second letter twenty minutes later, matched to a correspondent whose replies were all enthusiasm and no punctuation. She was patient with him. She asked real questions. In the margins of her own letter she'd written *(I hope this finds you — I hope everything finds you, that's the whole trouble)* and I read that line four times and then put the envelope down and walked to the window and stood there like an idiot looking at the dark square.

Here is what I will admit, since I'm apparently keeping a ledger: I liked her letters. I liked them the way you like a fire in a room you meant to leave. And liking things, in my experience, is how buildings fall down.

* * *

Envelope one hundred and nine.

The handwriting was expensive. That sounds like a judgment, and it is — some hands are trained into people by schools where tuition is a rumor you don't repeat. Slanted, confident, the loops closed tight like fists. I almost set it aside as another tourist entry — the festival drew a few every year, people who summered in Xanthe and thought our little ritual was quaint — and then I read the opening line.

*"My family owns most of the hill above this town, and I have come to learn that owning land is not the same as belonging to it."*

The coffee went cold in my cup. I don't mean it cooled gradually. I mean I looked down and it was undrinkable, and I had no memory of the time passing between the first line and that moment.

Cassian Alcott.

Not Wren's husband, not her brother — the town had speculated for years about the exact shape of that connection and gotten it wrong in every direction; they shared a surname and, as far as anyone could prove, nothing else. Cassian Alcott, who had been sent away at nineteen after the foreclosure, who came back once for his father's funeral and left again before the reception ended. Cassian Alcott, heir to Alcott Vineyards, which sat on the hill above Xanthe like a hand resting on the back of your neck.

He shouldn't be in the box. He hadn't lived here in a decade. But the rules said residents and former residents, and former covered him, and besides — I remembered now, with the sour clarity of things remembered too late — Juniper had mentioned it weeks ago, chattering about harvest season, the vineyard hiring, *Ms. Alcott says the Alcott son is coming home for the first pressing.* I'd filed it under things I would deal with when they arrived.

Well. One of them had arrived, in envelope one hundred and nine, written in a fist like a locked gate.

I should have disqualified him. There was no rule against it — I was a judge, judges have discretion, and discretion is just prejudice wearing a clean shirt. His presence in the festival offended me on procedural grounds I could have invented in thirty seconds.

Instead I read the whole letter.

That's the part I keep circling back to, the part I'll be honest about because dishonesty is what ruined my family in the first place. I read all four pages. And the worst of it — worse than recognizing him, worse than the hand on the neck feeling of his prose — was that the letter was good.

Voice: the man could write. Honesty: that was the trap. Because the letter was about guilt. Not named guilt — he was too careful for that, too trained — but guilt the way you smell smoke two rooms away. He wrote about inheritance as a debt structure. He wrote about a father who taught him accounting the way other fathers teach fishing, and about ledgers that balance on paper and nowhere else. He wrote: *"There is a field I think about more than I think about my own reflection. I have never gone back to it. I am afraid that if I stand on it, I will finally have to decide what I owe."*

I knew which field. I could draw the fence lines from memory. My father had died owing money on it, and the money had been owed because of numbers that Cassian's father's accountants had moved like furniture, and the lawsuit had died on a technicality while my father died of a heart that had simply gotten tired of carrying the arithmetic.

And I sat in my closed bookshop at nearly midnight, scoring the son of the man who killed my father, and my hand wrote: *Honesty: 5.*

I stared at that number for a long time.

* * *

Here is the creed I actually live by, the one I don't say out loud because it sounds either too soft or too strange depending on the listener: hearts are trainable muscles. Grief is a repetition. Resentment is a repetition. You do them enough and they stop being feelings and become form — the shape your chest holds when nothing is happening to it. Ten years of mornings opening this shop, packing Juniper's lunch, nodding at the pity in the produce aisle: that wasn't healing. That was training. I had built a heart that could carry this weight indefinitely, and I was proud of it the way you're proud of a scar.

But muscles have a failure mode, and it isn't weakness. It's the moment you use them for something new.

Reading those letters was new. Reading Wren's letters was new. And somewhere around envelope one hundred and forty, when I found the third exchange — hers to a shy retired ferryman, his answer, her reply — I realized I'd stopped judging and started eavesdropping, and that the distinction mattered enormously, and that I was going to keep reading anyway.

* * *

It got worse. Of course it got worse.

Because the festival routes correspondents by number, and because Wren had been running this thing for years and evidently enjoyed her own game, somewhere in the middle of the box there was a pairing I hadn't expected: her number against his. Letter to letter. Wren Alcott and Cassian Alcott, corresponding anonymously across the same blue box I was holding, neither knowing — or both knowing? — who was on the other end.

Her first letter to him was the one I'd already read, the loneliness-of-questions one. His reply was envelope one hundred and nine. And then there was her second letter to him, and his second to her, and I sat with the growing stack in front of me like a man holding evidence in a trial that hadn't been scheduled yet.

Their correspondence was a fencing match. She probed; he deflected with elegance. She asked what he owed; he answered with a story about a horse his grandfather sold that he'd never seen. She wrote: *"You describe everything beautifully and nothing honestly. What's under the beautiful?"* And he wrote back four pages about light on water, and at the bottom, in smaller script, as if the pen had acted without committee approval: *"Under the beautiful there is a field."*

I put that one down too.

The clock said 1:40 in the morning. I had judged maybe sixty letters properly and read perhaps thirty the way a starving man reads a menu — not for nourishment but for the fantasy of it. The blue box was supposed to take me two evenings. I had one evening left, and I'd spent it learning the secret prose styles of the two people in Xanthe least likely to forgive me for knowing them.

Because that was the real problem, the one underneath the problem. It wasn't that I'd recognized them. Recognition happens; I recognized the fisherman's grief and the widow's begonias and nobody was harmed. The problem was what recognition does to power. Wren had handed me two hundred sealed envelopes and trusted the process, and I had opened — not the envelopes, I want to be clear, the seals were intact, I'm not a monster — but something worse. I'd seen through the paper. I now knew things about Wren Alcott that she had written believing no one who knew her would ever read them. Things about loneliness. About asking questions all day and never being asked one. About a line I keep returning to: *"Some days I teach thirty children and go home to a house that doesn't know my name."*

And I knew things about Cassian Alcott that I had no right to know and every reason to want. He had written about the field. He had written about ledgers that balance on paper and nowhere else. He had written, in his second letter, a sentence I copied onto a scrap of the scoring sheet because my hand did it before I could stop it: *"Guilt is just memory with the interest still accruing."*

Ten years I'd wanted a sentence like that out of an Alcott mouth. Ten years I'd rehearsed mine — one sentence, one accusation, saved for a confrontation I both dreaded and kept polished like a tool. And now the universe, in its usual tasteless humor, had delivered his half of the conversation to me in a blue box, anonymously, with a scoring rubric.

I should disqualify them both. That was the clean move. Set the envelopes aside, write Wren a note — *conflict of interest, recused myself, sorry* — and let someone else's clean hands sort the rest.

I stacked their letters separately. Hers in one pile, his in another, the exchanges paired and squared like documents for a filing I had no authority to make.

Then I poured the cold coffee down the sink, took out a fresh scoring sheet, and began again at envelope one — because if I was going to do the wrong thing, I intended to do it thoroughly, and because I needed until Friday to figure out how to look Wren Alcott in the eye on Monday morning knowing what her lonely looked like on paper.

The last thing I read before I turned off the lamp was her third letter to him. One paragraph. It said:

*"You keep telling me about the field. Here is my question, and please answer it plainly, because I am tired of beautiful men who mistake scenery for confession: if you went back to it, what would you plant?"*

Below it, in his hand, the reply — one line, the loops closed tight:

*"Something that would feed people who hate me."*

I sat in the dark for a while after that.

Friday, I thought. I have until Friday.
