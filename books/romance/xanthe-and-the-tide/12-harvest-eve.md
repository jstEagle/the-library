## 12. Harvest Eve

The square had never held this many people. Wren knew because she'd counted chairs — three hundred, then gave up when the folding seats ran out and people started standing along the arcade, hanging off the balustrade of the clock tower, perching on the sea wall with their shoes off like it was a beach day instead of the last night of September. Strings of bulbs swung between the lampposts. Someone had nailed a banner over the fishmonger's awning that read THE TENTH ANNUAL LETTERS OF XANTHE in paint still tacky enough to shine.

She stood at the microphone stand with her hands flat on the crate they'd set it on, and she could not stop looking for one particular head in the crowd.

"He's here," said Marisol, appearing at her elbow with two paper cups of the vineyard's free pour. "Your judge. Third row, end seat, arms crossed like he's grading us."

"I wasn't—"

"You were absolutely scanning." Marisol pressed a cup into Wren's hand. "Drink something. You've reorganized that clipboard four times."

Wren drank. The wine was young and bright and slightly too cold, and it did nothing for the thing sitting under her sternum, which was not nerves about public speaking. She'd taught thirty eleven-year-olds through a production of *Charlotte's Web*. She could read aloud in front of a town.

It was the other thing. The marginalia thing. The stack of books sitting in her classroom cabinet right now, each one carrying his handwriting in the margins like a second author had moved in without asking — wry, dry, occasionally so funny she'd laughed out loud alone at her kitchen table, which was how she'd known she was in trouble.

Sable Lockridge sat exactly where Marisol said, dark jacket, no smile, watching the stage with the expression of a man doing jury duty. Juniper beside him in a dress Wren had never seen, swinging her legs, waving when she spotted Wren. Wren waved back and felt Sable's gaze flick to her for half a second — half a second, that was all he ever allowed — before returning to the middle distance.

Ten days since the argument outside her classroom. Ten days since she'd said *I tutor her because she asked, and because she's good, and because you were too proud to notice* and he'd gone white around the mouth and said, very quietly, *You don't know what my pride costs* and walked away. Since then: nothing. No notes in returned books. She'd left *The Blue Castle* on the shop counter with a question written inside the cover — *do you think Valancy ever regrets waiting?* — and it had come back three days later wiped clean, every pencil mark erased, even hers. Even hers. As if he'd decided their whole correspondence had been a mistake and tidied it out of existence.

So. Festival night. She would do her job.

"Welcome," she said into the microphone, and the feedback squealed, and the crowd laughed kindly, and she found her footing the way she always did, by treating three hundred strangers like one large class. "Welcome to the tenth Letters of Xanthe. For those who don't know the rules: all year, anyone in town could write to anyone else anonymously, sealed, sorted by volunteers, delivered blind. Tonight we read a few aloud — with permission, always with permission — and we honor the idea underneath it all, which is that we are all braver on paper than we are face to face."

Scattered applause. A whistle from the fishermen's table.

"We received four hundred and six letters this year," she went on. "Some were complaints about parking. Some were love letters. One was an extremely detailed recipe for cioppino addressed to 'the person who keeps stealing my crab pot,' which I'm told resolved the dispute entirely."

Laughter. Good. Warm room, warm night, tide audible beyond the seawall like slow breathing.

"The judges selected twelve letters for tonight. I want to thank them — Marisol Ferro, Pastor Ede, and Sable Lockridge, who owns the bookshop and who I'm told read every single letter twice."

She looked at him when she said it. She couldn't help it. He inclined his head a fraction, acknowledging the applause without accepting any of it, and Wren thought: *there is a man who has practiced being unreadable until it became a language.*

The readings began.

A boy of maybe nine read his letter to the lighthouse keeper, asking if the light ever got lonely; the keeper, a huge gray-bearded man, stood up from the crowd and answered aloud, voice cracking, that yes, but now he had four hundred witnesses to say otherwise. An old woman read a letter she'd written to her late husband and never sent, and the square went silent as a held breath, and somewhere in the third row Wren heard a woman weeping openly and didn't have to look to know it was Mrs. Okafor from the bakery. A teenager read a letter apologizing to his best friend for the winter he'd stopped speaking, and the best friend stood up mid-reading and hugged him in front of everyone, and the crowd roared.

Letter after letter. Truth after truth, spoken into a microphone by people who had spent all year hiding behind envelopes.

Wren watched Sable through all of it. He didn't laugh easily, but he laughed — once, at the cioppino follow-up letter, a real laugh that escaped before he could catch it, and it transformed his whole face for two seconds, and Wren felt those two seconds somewhere below her ribs like a struck bell.

Then it was time for the last letter, and Cassian Alcott took the stage.

He did it smoothly, of course. He came up during the applause for the teenagers as if he belonged there, as if the stage were simply where he stood, in a linen shirt unbuttoned just enough, holding a glass of the family wine. He kissed Wren's cheek — she turned too late to make it land anywhere but near her ear — and leaned toward the mic.

"For those who don't know me, I'm Cassian Alcott. My family's been growing grapes above this town for sixty years, and tomorrow we press our first harvest under the Alcott name in a decade. So tonight feels like a homecoming." He smiled the smile that had charmed the chamber of commerce, the harvest committee, half the women at the tasting room opening. "And I requested permission to read one more letter. Mine."

Wren's stomach dropped. He hadn't shown her this. He hadn't told her there *was* this.

"It's addressed," Cassian said, scanning the crowd with theatrical slowness, "to a certain schoolteacher who has spent a year writing anonymous letters better than anyone in this town — including, I suspect, the ones she wrote to me."

The crowd made the sound crowds make: delighted, hungry. Somewhere Marisol said, "Oh, *honey*, no," under her breath.

"I know it's you, Wren," Cassian read, from a card he didn't need. "I knew by the third letter. Nobody else uses the word 'liminal' correctly. I know your handwriting from the permission slips. And I've been waiting all year for the festival, because I wanted to answer you in front of everyone: I'm done being anonymous too. Ask me anything you wrote and wanted to ask. Starting tomorrow, I'll be in town through the whole harvest. I'd like to take you to dinner. Publicly. On purpose. No envelope required."

He turned to her, hand extended, in front of three hundred people and a string of swaying bulbs and the whole listening tide.

Wren's mind went white. Not with flattery — with arithmetic. She counted, in one lightning flash, everything wrong with the moment: that he'd taken her private words and used them as a prop; that he'd cornered her in public so refusal would cost her; that he'd timed his claim to the hour his vineyard needed goodwill most; that the card in his hand was a performance of vulnerability containing none.

But mostly, in the white space, she saw one image: Sable's face in the third row.

He wasn't angry. That was the terrible part. He was *composed*. He was wearing the look of a man confirming a fact he'd already filed — the teacher and the vineyard heir, of course, obviously, case closed — and he was already standing, already murmuring something to Juniper, already lifting the canvas tote from the seat beside him.

"No," Wren said into the microphone.

The word came out small. She gripped the stand and said it again, properly.

"No, Cassian. Thank you, but no."

The crowd's delight curdled into delicious discomfort. Cassian's smile held, barely, a hairline fracture running through it. "Wren—"

"That letter was meant to be private," she said, and her voice steadied as she found the teacher register, the one that stops playground fights. "That was the entire point of the festival. You don't get to spend someone's courage on your own announcement." She turned back to the crowd, cheeks burning, pulse slamming. "And that concludes our readings. Please — the band starts in ten minutes, the wine is free, and I need a drink."

Applause, scattered then fuller, the crowd mercifully eager to move on. Cassian stepped back from the mic saying something low and quick — *you're making a mistake, you know I'm good for it, everyone can see it* — and Wren walked off the stage without answering, straight past him, straight into the crowd, scanning—

Third row. Empty.

Juniper was there, though, standing alone by the chair, looking lost. Wren crouched to her.

"Hey. Where'd your dad go?"

Juniper's face did something complicated. "He said it was time to go. He does that." She picked at the strap of the tote he'd left behind — no, he'd given it to her, Wren realized; he'd handed his daughter the bag and walked off alone. "Ms. Alcott? Are you okay?"

"I'm fine, sweetheart." She wasn't sure it was true. "Did he say where?"

Juniper shook her head. Then, quieter, with the terrible precision of children: "He looked like he does when he talks about Grandpa."

* * *

Wren found Marisol first, commandeered her keys, drove the four blocks to the shop with the windows down and the festival noise fading behind her like a party on another continent.

The lights were off. But the door was unlocked — Sable never left it unlocked — and inside, in the smell of paper and dust and the lemon polish he used on the counter, she saw it immediately.

On the counter, in a neat stack, tied with the twine he used for parcels: her books.

All seven of them. Every novel she'd bought or traded or borrowed across the year, the ones that had come back annotated, the ones whose margins had become, without either of them naming it, the truest conversation of her adult life. *Middlemarch.* *The Blue Castle.* *Gilead.* *Possession.* The poetry collection she'd pretended to buy for her class.

Untied, she knew what she'd find, and her hands shook untying it anyway: blank margins. Erased. All of them. His voice scrubbed out, and hers with it, graphite ghosts and dented paper where strong words had been pressed hard and then rubbed away with such thoroughness it could only be called deliberate. Discipline. He'd erased them like a man sweeping a floor he intended never to walk again.

There was no note. Of course there was no note. A note would be a sentence, and sentences were the one thing he'd apparently decided she couldn't have.

Wren stood in the dark shop holding seven mute books and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that she had just watched a man choose a story over a person. He had seen Cassian's hand extended toward her and read it as an ending — the teacher and the heir, the tidy narrative, the town's favorite romance — and instead of asking, instead of writing even one line in one margin, he had closed the book.

Her eyes stung. She let them, briefly, in the dark, where no one could file it.

Then she pulled out her phone, opened the group chat for the festival committee, and typed with cold, steady thumbs:

*Marisol. The last letter tonight — Cassian's — was read without the consent of the person it was about. That violates the festival rules, section four. I'm invoking my right as organizer to respond in kind at tomorrow's closing reading.*

Send.

Marisol replied in four seconds: *OH HONEY. YES. What are you going to read??*

Wren looked down at the erased books. At the ghost-margins where the best parts of her year had lived and been rubbed out by a man too armored to fight for them.

"Not his letter," she typed. "Mine. The one I never sent."

She put the books in her car — she was not leaving them in his shop to be resold as clean copies, stripped of their history — and drove home past the square, where the band played and the string lights swung and Cassian Alcott held court at the vineyard table, telling the story of his public proposal to anyone who'd listen, already rewriting the night in his favor.

Tomorrow the whole town would gather again for the closing reading. Tomorrow she would stand at that microphone and say, out loud, in front of everyone, the things she'd only ever written in margins and sealed envelopes — and if a certain bookseller was in the crowd, he was going to learn what happened to men who erased her.

At home, she cleared her desk, found a fresh sheet of paper, and began to write.
