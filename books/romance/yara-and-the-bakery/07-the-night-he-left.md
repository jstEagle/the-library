## 7. The Night He Left

The rain had a smell that summer, the night everything ended. Wet dust and hot stone, the orchard breathing out its green exhale after the first real storm in weeks. Dominic remembered it because he had spent ten years trying to forget it, and the body keeps what the mind refuses—the ozone bite of it, the way the gravel crunched under Gran Wren's boots as she came up the orchard road with her lantern held high, the light swinging like a pendulum counting down.

He was nineteen. Yara was eighteen. They were lying in the grass between the third and fourth rows of Gravensteins, her head on his chest, both of them soaked through and laughing about it, because they were young enough that being drenched in a summer storm felt like a gift rather than an omen.

Her hair smelled like rain and apple blossom. His shirt was stuck to his back. Somewhere down the slope, the irrigation ditch ran loud and brown with runoff, and the whole world was the sound of water and the two of them, hidden in the dark rows like the trees had grown up around them on purpose, a cathedral built for exactly this.

"I'm going to tell her," Yara had said, drowsy, her fingers tracing the seam of his shirt. "About us. After the harvest. I'm tired of sneaking around my own grandmother's orchard like a thief."

"She'll take it fine."

"She'll take it like a heart attack, and then she'll take it fine." Yara laughed into his collarbone. "You don't know her."

I know her, Dominic had thought, and said nothing. Because he did know her. He knew that Agnes Wren had buried a husband and a son—his father—on that hill, and that grief had calcified in her into something with rules. Montroses leave. That was the family proverb, worn smooth as a river stone. His father had left. His grandfather had left. The Montrose men were a lineage of slammed doors, and Gran Wren had raised Yara alone in the wreckage of one of them.

The lantern light found them before he heard the boots.

It swung up the row, and the shadows of the trees lurched, and then Agnes Wren was standing at the end of the grass aisle with her lantern raised and her face doing something Dominic had never seen a face do—going through three expressions in the space of a second, shock and fury and then a terrible, settling cold, like water finding its level.

"Yara." Just the name. Just the name, and Yara sat up so fast she nearly knocked the wind out of him.

"Gran—"

"Get inside."

"Gran, let me explain, we were going to tell you—"

"*Inside.*" The word cracked like a branch. And then the lantern turned, and the light found Dominic's face, and Agnes Wren looked at him the way you look at weather you can't stop. "And you. You get off my land."

"Mrs. Wren—"

"You get off my land and you don't come back." Her voice wasn't loud anymore. That was the worst part, the part that lived in him like a splinter for a decade: she had gone quiet. Quiet the way people go quiet when they've already decided everything. "You know what I'll do, boy? You know what I'll do if you don't walk away?"

Yara was on her feet, dripping, furious. "Gran, you can't—"

"I can cut you out of this orchard so fast your head will spin. Every acre. Every tree. The house you were born in." She said it to the dark, to the rain, but her eyes never left Dominic. "He knows what his family does. He knows what Montroses are. Ask him."

And Dominic—nineteen, soaked, in love for the first and only time in his life—looked up at this woman who had lost everything twice, who had one thing left in the world and it was this stubborn hillside and the girl standing in front of him defending him, and he understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she meant it. That she would do it. That Yara would lose the only home she'd ever known, the trees her grandfather planted, the whole architecture of her life, because of him. Because loving him had a price and the price was written in a dead man's will.

"Yara," he said. "Go inside."

"Not until—"

"*Go inside.*"

She went. He heard her boots on the gravel, heard the farmhouse door, and then it was just him and Agnes Wren and the rain coming down soft and relentless on ten thousand leaves.

"You'll tell her nothing," Gran said. "If you tell her why, she'll fight me. She'll choose you over this land, and then I'll have to choose whether to mean it, and I am too old and too tired to find out. So you'll go, and you'll let her hate you, because hate heals. It's love that festers."

He could have argued. He could have said *she's eighteen, she's not property, this is her choice too.* He has thought of a thousand things to say in the ten years since, entire speeches, delivered to ceilings at three in the morning. But that night, standing in the rain with a dead man's daughter holding a lantern over him, he said the only thing that was true:

"If I go, you take care of her."

"I have always taken care of her."

And he left. That was the whole of it. No note, no explanation, no dramatic scene at the bus station. He packed a duffel bag while the storm passed, and at dawn he walked down the orchard road with wet shoes, and he did not look back at the farmhouse because if he looked back he would not leave, and if he did not leave she would lose everything.

He let her hate him. Hate heals. It's love that festers.

He was twenty-nine years old before he understood that Gran Wren had been wrong about that too.

* * *

Present day. The cottage kitchen, eleven at night, the lamp turned low so the room was all amber and shadow. Yara sat across the table from him with her hands wrapped around a mug of cider she hadn't drunk, and between them lay the photograph he'd found tucked inside the ledger—him at nineteen, caught mid-laugh in the packing shed, Yara's hand on his shoulder, both of them lit up like the answer to a question nobody had asked yet.

She had asked him, finally, the question she'd been carrying for ten years. *Why did you leave? Not the café. Not the city. Why did you leave me without a word?*

He had been avoiding the answer for two days, hiding in pruning and pastry and the honest labor of his hands. But the photograph sat on the table like a witness, and Yara was looking at him with those eyes that had never learned to lie, and Dominic understood that the truce they'd built—one season, one harvest, one grudging bargain—would not survive another silence. Whatever this was between them, growing in the cracks like grass through pavement, it would die of the same disease it died of before.

So he told her. All of it. The storm, the grass, the lantern coming up the row. The words Gran had said, delivered flat and exact, because he had rehearsed them so many times in the dark that they'd worn grooves in him. *I can cut you out of this orchard so fast your head will spin.*

He watched her face while he talked. He watched the confusion, then the disbelief, then something worse—the slow, dawning horror of a woman doing arithmetic with her whole life. Her grandmother's fierce love. The locked drawer in the office he'd never been allowed to open. The way Gran had gone strange and soft in her last year, pressing the deed into probate with instructions that made no sense until they made every sense.

"Fix it or lose it," Yara said quietly, when he finished. "That's what her note said." Her thumb moved over the rim of her mug, back and forth, back and forth. "She wasn't talking about the orchard."

"No."

"She was talking about us." The mug came down. Her hands were shaking, and she pressed them flat to the table like she could pin the trembling there. "She spent ten years watching me be angry at you. She watched me build my whole life around a wound, and she—she let me. She let me hate you, Dominic, because she couldn't admit what she'd done. And then she died and made us fix it from beyond the grave because she didn't have the courage to do it while she was alive."

"I know."

"Do you?" Her voice cracked and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "Because I have spent a decade being angry at a man who was protecting me. I built a whole self out of being left. And it turns out you didn't leave me. You *sacrificed* me. You let me think I was unlovable so I could keep my inheritance, and I don't—" She stood up, pushed back from the table, stood at the dark window with her arms crossed tight. "I don't know what to do with that. I don't know who I am if the story was never true."

Dominic stayed in his chair. He had learned, in ten years of silence, that the worst thing you can do to a wound is crowd it.

"She was wrong," he said. "I've had time to think about it. She was wrong about me, and she was wrong about you, and she was wrong that hate heals. It doesn't. It just gets comfortable. You build a house in it." He looked at his hands, flour in the knuckle creases, calluses from her loppers that still surprised him every morning. "I let you hate me because she asked, and because I was nineteen and terrified, and because it was easier to be your villain than your cost. But I'm not nineteen anymore, and I'm done being easy."

Yara turned from the window. The lamp caught the wet on her cheeks, and she didn't wipe it away, and something in his chest came loose—some clamp he'd fitted around his own heart the morning he walked down that road, rusted shut, finally giving way.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is you're not saying. Say it."

"I never stopped—" His voice failed on the first try. He started again, slower, the way you'd walk out onto ice. "I never stopped loving you. Ten years. Two hundred miles. I could recite your grandmother's threat word for word but I couldn't make myself stop loving you, and I have hated myself for the cowardice of it every single day, and I am so sorry, Yara. For the silence. For letting you believe the worst. For being the thing that made you build a house in the hate."

The clock on the shelf ticked. Outside, the orchard breathed in the dark, ten thousand trees holding their ripening fruit like held breath, and somewhere down the slope the irrigation ditch still ran, brown and loud, the same sound as that night, as if the land had been waiting all this time for the sentence to finish.

Yara crossed the kitchen. She didn't touch him—she stood close enough that he could feel the warmth of her, close enough that the space between them was its own kind of confession—and she looked at him for a long, long moment.

"Ten years," she said, and her voice was wrecked and steady at once. "You let me hate you for ten years, and now you tell me it was love the whole time." A breath that was half laugh, half sob. "Dominic. I have spent a decade being angry at the wrong man, and I don't know how to stop on a dime. But I need you to know—" her hand came up, hovered, landed against his jaw like it had never left, "—I never stopped either. Not for one day. And that makes me angrier than anything else."

"Then be angry," he said, turning his face into her palm. "Be angry here. Just—be here."

She was still holding his face when the kitchen window lit up—headlights swinging up the orchard drive, slow and wrong for that hour, and Yara went still against him.

"Who comes up this road at midnight?" she whispered.

The car stopped. The engine ticked. And through the rain-streaked glass, a figure got out and stood looking at the cottage, and Dominic felt the whole fragile night tilt beneath them.
