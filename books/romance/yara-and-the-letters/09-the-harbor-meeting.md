## 9. The Harbor Meeting

The news arrived the way bad news always arrived in Saltmere Harbor: not by phone, not by letter, but by Mrs. Aldous Pemberton standing on a chair in the middle of the Hartwell reunion luncheon and announcing it to the room at large.

"There's a survey boat," she said, with the gravity of a woman reading a war dispatch. "In the harbor. Since Tuesday. Taking measurements."

Aunt Prue lowered her fork. "Measurements of what?"

"Of *our harbor*, Prudence. What else does one measure?"

The dining room — which had been humming along pleasantly on potato salad and second helpings — went quiet in that particular way rooms go quiet when everyone is doing arithmetic at once. I felt Yara go still beside me, her knee pressed to mine under the table, and I remember thinking, absurdly, *she has lovely instincts for stillness.* Later I would understand why. That morning I only reached for her hand because the room had begun to tilt.

"Survey boats come through all the time," said Uncle Gerald, who believed this about most things, including weather he didn't like.

"Not taking *soundings*," Mrs. Pemberton said, savoring the word like a hard candy. "My nephew works the fuel dock. He says they've been mapping every berth from the breakwater to the cannery pier. Every dock. Every mooring." She paused, letting the room catch up to her. "And yesterday a man came into the chandlery asking after title records. Harbor parcels. He was very polite." Another pause, longer, crueler. "He had an Okafor Maritime business card."

I did not know yet what that name meant. I want to be honest about that, because for years afterward I lied to myself about it — told myself I'd sensed something, that some lighthouse-keeper's instinct had flickered. It hadn't. The name meant nothing to me. But beside me, Yara's hand turned cold as harbor stone in mine, and she withdrew it so smoothly, so gently, that I thought she was simply reaching for her water glass.

She drank half of it in one swallow.

"Okafor Maritime," repeated Grandmother, from the head of the table. She said it the way she said everything — flatly, without inflection, a woman who had spent eighty-one years refusing to be surprised by anything before lunch. "That's a mainland shipping concern."

"It's a *buying* concern," said Mrs. Pemberton darkly, "if you ask me."

Nobody had asked her, but everybody listened anyway, because Mrs. Pemberton had been right about the cannery closing in '09 and right about the storm surge in '14, and being right twice in a decade buys you a lifetime of unearned authority in a town of four hundred people.

* * *

By three o'clock the reunion had stopped pretending to be a reunion.

It happened the way these things happen — one conversation migrating to the porch, then two more joining it, then someone dragging folding chairs out of the boathouse, and by the time the fog started coming in off the water there were forty-odd Hartwells and neighbors arranged in the yard in the loose, aggrieved semicircle of a town preparing to fight something. Someone had made coffee in quantities suitable for a natural disaster. Uncle Gerald had produced a legal pad. Dana Voss — my old rival, my old wound, still sharp-eyed and sharper-tongued at thirty-four — stood at the center of it with her arms crossed, running point, because Dana had never met a crisis she didn't want to organize.

"The way these things work," she was saying, "is they come in quiet. They buy up parcels one at a time, cheap, from people who don't know what they're sitting on. Then once they've got enough of the waterfront, they announce a marina development, or a resort, or some nonsense with the word 'destination' in it, and suddenly your grandfather's mooring is a condo parking structure." She flipped a page on Gerald's pad. "We need to know who they've already talked to. Who's signed anything. Who's been approached."

"I heard the Kesslers were offered money for their boatyard," someone offered.

"The Kesslers would sell their own shadows if the light hit them right," said Aunt Prue.

"That's exactly the problem, isn't it," said Dana. "They don't need everyone. They need a third. A third is enough to make the rest of us look like we're holding out over sentiment."

I stood near the back with Yara, close enough to feel the tension running through her like current through wire. She had barely spoken since the announcement. She'd helped carry chairs — she carried chairs beautifully, I noticed, with the competence of a woman who'd grown up around staff — and now she stood with her arms folded, watching the crowd with an expression I couldn't read. Not boredom. Something closer to grief, though I couldn't have said why, and wouldn't learn until much later.

"You okay?" I murmured.

"Fine," she said. Too fast. Then, softer: "This happens everywhere, you know. Towns like this. They come in, they map, they offer, and half the time nobody even notices until the first demolition permit goes up." Her voice had a strange, practiced fluency, like she was reciting something she'd read rather than lived. "You're smart to get ahead of it."

"We're not smart," I said. "We're just angry. There's a difference."

"There isn't, actually," Yara said quietly. "Anger organized is just strategy wearing its heart on its sleeve."

I looked at her. She looked at the harbor, where the survey boat sat at anchor, white and patient as a gull waiting for a dropped chip.

* * *

Grandmother let the war council run for an hour before she ended it, the way she ended everything — by standing up without asking anyone's permission.

The yard hushed. It always hushed. Edith Hartwell had that effect on rooms, porches, churches, and once, according to family legend, a funeral.

"You're all talking about lawyers and petitions," she said. "Good. Do both. But you're missing the thing that matters." She looked out at the assembled faces, unhurried. "Okafor Maritime doesn't send survey boats to harbors they can't use. Which means somewhere there's a plan with our harbor's name on it, drawn up in an office none of us will ever see. And plans like that have a schedule. So the question isn't whether they're coming." She folded her hands. "The question is who in this town is going to stand up and say no loudly enough that the answer gets written down somewhere permanent."

Silence. The foghorn sounded far off, low and patient.

Dana opened her mouth — I could see her gathering herself to volunteer, because Dana volunteered for things the way other people breathed — and I heard myself speak first.

"I'll do it."

Every head turned. Forty faces, several of them belonging to people who had watched me leave this town ten years ago with my tail between my legs and a plagiarized-manuscript scandal trailing behind me like a torn hem. I felt the heat climb my neck. Somewhere in the crowd, I caught a flicker of Dana's expression — surprise, quickly filed away, quickly assessed.

But I kept going, because apparently once the door was open, ten years of held breath came out all at once.

"They took my voice once," I said. My voice shook, and I let it; I was done pretending steadiness was the same thing as strength. "Not this company — people. People I trusted. And I left, and I stayed gone, and I told myself I was building a new life, but mostly I was just... hiding in a smaller and smaller room." I looked at Grandmother, who watched me with those pale, unreadable eyes. "I'm done hiding in rooms. If Okafor Maritime wants this harbor, they're going to have to go through somebody who grew up keeping a light in it. Somebody who knows every rock in that channel by heart. Somebody with nothing left to lose except—" I faltered, just slightly, "—except the thing I'm trying to protect."

"Well," said Aunt Prue, into the silence, "that was dramatic."

"Dramatic and correct," said Mrs. Pemberton, "which is rare."

There was laughter — small, surprised, warming — and hands went up around the semicircle, one after another, the way hands do when someone has finally said the thing everyone was thinking. Gerald wrote my name on his legal pad at the top of the page, underlined it twice, and looked at me with an expression I'd never seen him wear before, which I eventually identified as respect.

Yara squeezed my arm. "You were good," she whispered.

"I was terrified."

"Yes," she said. "That's usually how you can tell."

* * *

The council broke up at dusk, dissolving into smaller, hungrier conversations — who'd talk to the Kesslers, who'd call the county, who'd dig through the land records at the courthouse Monday morning. Dana cornered me briefly on the porch steps, and I braced for a knife, because Dana's knives were traditional at gatherings like this.

"You know they'll use it against you," she said instead, without malice, which was somehow worse. "Your history. The plagiarism business. Companies like this don't fight fair — they find the person leading the opposition and they dig until they find something rotten, and then they hold it up like a lantern." She studied me. "You've got something they can find. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you're volunteering anyway."

"I'm volunteering anyway."

Dana nodded slowly, and for one strange moment she looked almost like the girl who'd once been my best friend, before the competition curdled. "Then you'd better make sure the truth comes from you first," she said. "Before anyone else gets to tell it." And she walked off down the lane toward her car, leaving me with that sentence turning over like a stone in my pocket.

Behind me, the screen door creaked. Grandmother came out onto the porch carrying two cups of tea, handed me one, and settled into the rocking chair that had been hers since before I was born.

For a while neither of us spoke. The harbor darkened. The survey boat lit its anchor lamp, a single small eye watching us across the water.

"Your young woman," Grandmother said at last. "Miss Okafor."

"What about her?"

"She didn't seem surprised today."

My tea stopped halfway to my mouth. "What do you mean?"

"I mean most people hear a name like that for the first time and ask questions. *Who are they? What do they want?*" Grandmother rocked once, twice, the chair creaking its old complaint. "Your Yara went very quiet and drank her water. Like a person hearing a family name mentioned in a room where she wasn't expecting it." She took a sip of tea, unhurried. "Or maybe I'm an old woman who reads too much into nothing. I've been wrong before." A pause, dry as driftwood. "Twice, I believe."

"Grandmother—"

"I'm not saying anything, Sable." She set down her cup with a small definitive click. "I'm saying that when the time comes, you should ask her where she learned to carry chairs like that. Nobody carries furniture that gracefully by accident." She rose, patted my shoulder once — the closest thing to open affection she permitted before witnesses — and went inside.

I sat on the porch a long while after, listening to the water, holding my cooling tea, telling myself that Grandmother was old and suspicious and had distrusted every stranger since 1987.

Across the harbor, the survey boat's anchor lamp burned steady and patient, and upstairs in the guest room, the woman I'd met four days ago at a ferry terminal was making a phone call I was not meant to hear — her voice low through the window screen, saying words I couldn't make out, in a tone I'd never heard her use with me.

I told myself it was probably just family.

Everyone tells themselves something, the night before the knife turns.
