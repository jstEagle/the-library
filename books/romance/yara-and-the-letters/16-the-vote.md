## 16. The Vote

The harbor meeting was held in the fire hall, which was the only building in Saltcrest large enough to hold the town's opinions and old enough to have survived most of them.

Sable arrived early to help set up folding chairs, and by eight o'clock there were more chairs than the hall owned, dragged in from porches and church basements, arranged in crooked rows that faced a card table draped in a blue tablecloth. On the table sat a coffee urn, a stack of ballots printed on the back of old tide charts, and a shoebox that had once held Sable's grandmother's winter boots and now served as the ballot box. Democracy, in Saltcrest, was a homemade thing.

She should have felt triumphant standing there. Three weeks ago she had volunteered to lead the resistance, and she had led it — petitions signed, letters written, the *Coastal Register* running her op-ed under the headline *THE LIGHT IS NOT FOR SALE*. Her voice had come back to her like a tide returning something it had borrowed.

But this morning her hands would not stay steady, because at nine-oh-four, a black car with mainland plates pulled up outside the fire hall, and out of it stepped a tall man in a coat too fine for salt air, flanked by two lawyers carrying briefcases like weapons they hoped not to use.

Emeka Okafor had come to close the deal personally.

And behind him, half a step back, hands in her pockets, face carefully arranged into nothing at all, came Yara.

Their eyes met across the parking lot for one second. One second was enough. Sable saw the apology in it, and the fear, and beneath both something that looked terribly like love — and then Yara looked away, because her father was speaking to her, low and quick, and whatever he said made her spine straighten like a mast taking wind.

"Miss Hartwell." Emeka Okafor extended his hand on the fire hall steps. His smile was warm the way heat lamps are warm. "I've heard so much about you. My daughter speaks of this town like a novel she's fond of."

"I'll bet," Sable said, and shook his hand anyway, because her grandmother was watching from the doorway and Grandmother Hartwell believed in civility the way other people believed in scripture: absolutely, and as a weapon.

Inside, the hall filled fast. Fishermen in their good sweaters. The Bremers from the chandlery. Dana Voss near the front, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had delivered her ammunition and was waiting to see where the shrapnel landed. Sable still didn't know exactly what Dana had found or who she'd given it to — only that Dana had been smiling differently all week, like someone holding a lit match behind her back.

Grandmother Hartwell took the front row and said nothing, which from her was louder than testimony.

The moderator was old Tom Pell, retired harbormaster, who banged a gavel that was actually a boat cleat bolted to a handle. "This meeting is called to order," he said. "Question before the town: whether Saltcrest accepts the offer of Okafor Maritime for the harbor parcels, including the Hartwell light. Mr. Okafor has asked to speak first. Then we vote. Simple as that."

Nothing in Saltcrest was simple as that, but everyone let him say it.

* * *

Yara's father spoke for eleven minutes, and they were very good minutes.

He talked about investment and preservation, about how Okafor Maritime did not want to change Saltcrest but to save it. He talked about the ferry line's declining ridership, the cannery closure, the young people leaving. He said the word *heritage* four times and the word *future* six times, and he said them in an order designed to make heritage sound like a thing you could keep only if you sold it first.

"We are not buyers," he said, spreading his hands. "We are stewards. And I understand there are those in this room who distrust us. That is fair. Trust must be earned." He paused, and Sable felt the pause like a change in current. "But I would ask you to consider the character of the opposition's leadership. Miss Hartwell."

The hall went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when everyone already knows what's coming.

"You may know her name," Emeka Okafor continued gently, "from literary circles. Ten years ago, Miss Sable Hartwell published a celebrated debut manuscript — celebrated until it was discovered that substantial portions of it were lifted, nearly verbatim, from another writer's work. The publisher withdrew it. The awards were returned. She left this town rather than answer for it."

Sable stood very still. Ten years fell away in a single breath, and she was twenty-six again, standing in a bookshop while a man read her stolen sentences aloud and asked if she had anything to say for herself.

"I mention this," Okafor said, "not to wound, but because judgment matters. This town is being asked to entrust its future to its past. And its most prominent voice —" he turned, regretful, toward Sable — "has already demonstrated what happens when her judgment is tested."

Dana didn't even look surprised. That was the worst part. Dana had known, and had handed it over, and now sat watching Sable with the patient satisfaction of a woman watering something she'd planted.

Sable opened her mouth. Closed it. Her voice — the one she'd spent three weeks reclaiming — was nowhere in the room.

It was Grandmother Hartwell who rose, unhurried, one hand on the pew-back chair.

"Mr. Okafor," she said, "that girl wrote every word of the letters her grandfather taught her to write, and if you knew anything about this family you'd know we don't measure worth by our worst season." She sat down. The hall murmured. But the murmur had a different texture than it would have three weeks ago — doubt had been planted, and doubt grows fast in shallow soil.

Okafor smiled his heat-lamp smile. "Of course. I meant no harm. Now — shall we hear from the opposition?"

Sable walked to the front on legs that belonged to someone else. She had prepared remarks. She had statistics about ferry schedules and easements and the structural report — *the structural report*, and the thought snagged in her chest like a fishhook, because she knew things about that report she shouldn't know, things whispered across a pillow, and using them would mean admitting where she'd learned them.

She looked out at the town. She looked at Yara, standing at the back beside the door, positioned — Sable realized — exactly where she could leave fastest.

"The lighthouse isn't a parcel," Sable said. Her voice came out rough but it came. "It's a promise my grandfather kept for forty years, for people he never met, in weather he didn't cause. That's what belonging looks like here. It's not ownership. It's keeping." She talked about the survey, about access clauses buried in the offer's appendix, about the ferry dock lease that would let Okafor Maritime control who came and went. She watched faces turn. She felt her voice hold.

And through all of it, Yara stood by the door with her father's shadow falling across her, and did not say one word.

* * *

Yara counted the exits the way she'd been trained to count them — two doors, the windows were painted shut, the crowd density made movement slow — and hated herself for counting, because counting exits was her father's habit living in her body like a tenant who paid no rent.

Her father had found out everything, of course. He always found out everything; it was the one inheritance guaranteed to transfer. He'd known about the fake date within days. He'd known about the lighthouse letters, the structural report she'd refused to falsify, the letter she'd sent refusing him. He'd flown in not despite her disobedience but because of it, the way you don't bother disciplining a dog until it bites.

"You will stand beside me," he'd said in the car, "and you will say nothing. When the vote passes, you will shake her hand. And then you will come home, and we will discuss your future, which currently consists of a trust fund and a surname, in that order."

So she stood. And Sable spoke, and Yara listened to the woman she loved defend a lighthouse with her grandfather's stubbornness in her spine, and every word was a small mercy Yara hadn't earned, and every minute Yara stayed silent was a small betrayal she was adding to the pile.

Then Tom Pell called for the vote, and the trap closed around her throat.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn: the vote would be close. She'd seen the petition numbers. Forty-one households against, thirty-eight leaning yes, the rest swaying like kelp. If the town voted no, her father would not walk away — he'd escalate, litigation, pressure on the ferry company, a war Saltcrest couldn't afford. If the town voted yes, the light would belong to a company whose reports bent like reeds, and Sable would spend the rest of her life fighting a fortress Yara had helped design.

Either way, her silence was a vote. Silence was always a vote. Her grandfather's letters — the ones in the lighthouse, the ones she'd read by flashlight while Sable slept — said so. *A man who keeps his mouth shut in a storm has chosen the rocks,* Elias Hartwell had written to a woman named Adaeze, in 1961, and Yara had read that line forty times without understanding it was addressed to her.

Tom Pell shook the shoebox. "All in favor of accepting the Okafor offer, drop a white stone. Opposed, blue."

The lines formed. Sable watched hers — the blue line was longer, but not long enough, and she could see people counting the way she was counting. Dana dropped a white stone, naturally. Old Bremer hesitated between boxes like a man choosing between two bad anchors.

Yara moved.

Not to the boxes. To the front of the room, past her father, whose hand came up half an inch — a gesture that in any other family might have been a reach for support and in hers was a leash — and she stepped around it.

"My name," she said, and her voice carried better than she expected, "is Yara Nkemdirim Okafor. I'm not a consultant. I'm the heir to Okafor Maritime. I've been in this town under a false name since June, and I was hired — by Sable Hartwell, who didn't know who I was — to pretend to be her partner at a family reunion."

The hall detonated into sound. Somewhere a chair went over. Dana's face performed shock so badly it was almost honest.

"And I am going to tell you," Yara continued, louder, "what the survey really says—"

"*Yara.*" Her father's voice cut the room like a hull through chop. He didn't shout. He never shouted. "Sit down."

She looked at him. Twenty-nine years of looking at him. And she thought of a letter written in 1961 by a man who chose the woman over the family, and lost the family, and gained everything else.

"No," Yara said.

* * *

From the front row, Sable watched the daughter of the enemy stand in the middle of the fire hall with her whole inheritance balanced on her tongue, and understood — with the terrible clarity that arrives one moment too late for everything except the next moment — that she was watching the same choice her grandfather had made, replayed sixty years later in a different coat.

Yara drew breath to speak.

And Emeka Okafor did the one thing Sable would never have predicted, the move of a man who had closed a hundred hostile rooms: he turned to the town, spread his hands in sorrow, and said, "You see what I've endured. My own child, compromised. Whatever she says next, ask yourselves who put her up to it — and remember that this town's leading voice against us is a proven plagiarist, and her new ally is a proven liar. Two forgeries, ladies and gentlemen. Vote accordingly."

The words landed like ballast dropped from height. Sable saw it happen — saw the doubt ripple through the blue-stone line, saw old Mrs. Pelletier drift from one box toward the other, saw the arithmetic tilt.

Yara stood frozen, her confession strangled in her throat, discredited before she'd spoken it.

And Sable Hartwell, who had rehearsed one sentence for ten years and never said it aloud, who had just been called a forger in front of everyone she'd ever loved, felt something in her chest stop being afraid.

She stepped forward, to the card table, to the shoebox, to the cleat-handled gavel, and picked up a blue stone.

"Tom," she said, "I'd like to say something before anyone votes. And this time—" her eyes found Yara's across the crowded hall, and held them, "—I'm going to say the true thing. All of it. Even the parts that cost me."

The hall went quiet. Her grandmother leaned forward. Dana's match finally caught.

Sable opened her mouth, and the sentence she had carried for a decade rose in her throat like a tide coming home.
