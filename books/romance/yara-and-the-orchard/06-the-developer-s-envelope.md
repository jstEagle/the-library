## 6. The Developer's Envelope

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which Yara would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day the orchard most liked to remind her of everything she didn't know.

She had been living at Bramble Hollow for three weeks by then — long enough to learn that the water heater sang a low, mournful note before it scalded you, and that the third apple tree in the east row dropped its fruit early out of what she could only assume was spite. Long enough to have filled two notebooks with her aunt's habits: the way Marigold Adeyemi had labeled her jam jars with dates *and* weather (*"plum, overcast, good for grief"*), the way she'd left one chair pulled out from the kitchen table as though she expected someone to sit down any minute.

Yara kept not moving that chair back.

The morning of the envelope, she was up before six, because the trees apparently held meetings at dawn and she was expected to attend. She stood in the wet grass with her tea going cold in her hand, surveying the orchard the way a new teacher surveys a classroom full of children who already know more than she does. Forty-one apple trees. Six pears. One plum tree near the fence line that leaned toward Emrys's vineyard like an old woman eavesdropping over a garden wall.

She had named four of them so far. It felt presumptuous, naming trees she hadn't planted, but the silence demanded *something*, and names were cheaper than conversation.

"You're all going to die without me," she told them cheerfully. "I just want to be honest about our situation."

The plum tree said nothing, which she chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

The car came up the gravel lane at nine-forty, silver and gleaming and deeply wrong, the way a tuxedo is wrong at a picnic. Yara watched it from the porch, tea forgotten, as a man stepped out who looked like he had been assembled from a brochure about success. Charcoal suit. Watch that probably cost more than the tractor rusting behind the barn. A smile he deployed before he'd even reached the gate, the way other people checked the weather.

"Ms. Adeyemi?" He extended a hand across the fence — her aunt's fence, the one Emrys had offered to mend just last week, standing there with his sleeves rolled and sawdust on his forearms, saying *no charge, I owe your aunt about forty years of borrowed tools.* "Daniel Osei, Meridian Development Group. Do you have a moment?"

Yara looked at his hand. Then at the orchard. Then at his hand again.

"I have several moments," she said, "but they're mostly spoken for." She opened the gate anyway, because her mother had raised her to be polite to strangers and suspicious of them in equal measure, and the two instructions cancelled each other out into something like curiosity.

He followed her to the porch, where she pointedly did not offer him a chair, partly because there were only two and one belonged to a ghost, and partly because she suspected Daniel Osei had never once been made to stand while talking and might benefit from the experience.

"I'll keep this brief," he said, producing a leather folder from under his arm with the smoothness of a magician. "Meridian has been acquiring parcels in this valley for some time. We're planning a resort community — vineyard views, tasting rooms, luxury cottages. Very tasteful. Very much in keeping with the character of the area."

"The character of the area," Yara repeated.

"Heritage aesthetics," he confirmed, nodding as if this settled something. "We've been in discussions with the neighboring property."

Something shifted in Yara's chest, small and cold, like a stone dropping through still water. "Emrys's vineyard."

"We're not at liberty to discuss—" He caught her expression and recalibrated, the smile adjusting itself by degrees, a thermostat responding to a draft. "Mr. Okafor's land is part of the vision, yes. But frankly, Ms. Adeyemi — and I say this with respect — yours is the jewel. Forty acres of established orchard, mature trees, water rights. The developer across the valley got half this for twice the price last year."

He laid the folder on the railing between them and opened it, and there it was: a number with so many digits that Yara's first instinct was to laugh, because surely this was a joke, surely somewhere a camera crew was hiding behind the plum tree waiting for her reaction.

Her second instinct was to do the math. The number, multiplied against the debts folded inside the estate — the back taxes, the equipment loan Marigold had quietly taken out in her final year, the roof that needed replacing before winter — left a remainder that Yara had never once seen attached to her own name. A remainder with room in it. Room for a small house somewhere with working plumbing. Room for time to write. Room to stop counting.

"How long," she said, "have you been sitting on this?"

"We move quickly when we find the right parcel." He tapped the folder. "There is, of course, a condition. The offer is contingent on consolidation. We need both properties — the orchard and the vineyard — or neither. A resort hemmed in by a working farm is a resort with a view of a problem."

"And if Mr. Okafor says no?"

"Then the offer expires." He said it gently, the way a doctor delivers a diagnosis he's delivered a hundred times. "I understand you've only recently arrived. You may feel a certain... attachment forming. To the land, to the neighbors. That's natural. But attachment isn't a business plan, Ms. Adeyemi. This money could set you up for life. And honestly?" His voice dropped into something almost kind, which was somehow worse. "From what we understand of the Okafor situation, you'd be doing him a favor too. That property is drowning. Sometimes the kindest thing is to let everyone stop pretending."

Yara looked at the number again. It sat on the page, patient and enormous, like a well-fed cat.

"Thank you," she heard herself say. "I'll need time."

"Of course. Take the week." He shook her hand — hers was cold; she noticed distantly — and walked back to his silver car, which turned around in her aunt's yard with the delicacy of a large animal trying not to crush anything, and failed slightly, leaving twin ruts in the grass by the gate.

Yara stood on the porch for a long time after the sound of the engine faded.

Then she went inside, moved the ghost's chair back in at the table, and immediately pulled it out again.

* * *

She didn't tell Emrys that afternoon. She told herself this was because she wanted to read the documents properly first, and this was true, but it was also true that every time she imagined his face when she mentioned Meridian's name, her throat did something complicated.

Instead she spent the day doing what she'd been doing for three weeks: learning the orchard badly. She pruned a pear tree with more enthusiasm than skill and produced what could charitably be called modern art. She found, in the barn, a crate of her aunt's grafting knives wrapped in oilcloth, their handles worn smooth as river stones, and held one for a long moment without knowing why it made her eyes sting.

And she thought about the number.

That was the thing no one warned you about temptation — how reasonable it was. How it arrived dressed in your own voice, saying all the sensible things. *You didn't plant these trees. You don't know these trees. You killed a succulent in college by watering it with iced coffee. What exactly are you protecting here?*

But then there was the other voice, quieter, older, wearing her aunt's cardigan. *Forty-one trees, Yara. Count them again.*

She counted them again. Forty-one.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when she finally walked down to the fence line, the developer's folder under her arm like a summons. The light was doing that thing the valley did in the evenings — pouring gold over everything indiscriminately, making even the dead plum tree look like it had been touched by a Renaissance painter.

Emrys was at the far end of the vineyard rows, and she watched him for a minute before calling out, because watching him work had become, without her permission, one of her favorite things. He moved through the vines the way other people moved through familiar houses in the dark — no wasted motion, no looking, just knowing. His hands found problems before his eyes did. A torn shoot. A cluster of grapes gone soft. He touched each one gently, the way you'd touch a bruise to check how bad it was.

"Yara." He'd sensed her, or heard her, or simply known — he had that quality, of always being aware of exactly where she was, which she tried not to read anything into and failed at regularly. He came down the row wiping his hands on his thighs. "You look like you've been fighting something."

"A pear tree. We've agreed to disagree." She held up the folder. "Actually, I need to show you something, and I need you to not react until I'm finished, because I've been rehearsing this since lunch and I only get through it if nobody interrupts."

His eyebrows rose, but he nodded, leaning his forearms on the fence post. Sawdust still on him, she noticed. From her fence. Her chest did the complicated thing again.

So she told him. All of it — the silver car, the heritage aesthetics, the number, which she said out loud and watched him flinch at, actually flinch, like the digits were thrown stones. And then the condition, and the word *consolidation*, and the sentence she'd been circling all day: *they need both properties, or neither.*

When she finished, the evening was very quiet. Somewhere in his vineyard, irrigation clicked on with a sigh.

"He came to you first," Emrys said finally.

"This morning."

"And you've been carrying this around all day." Not a question. Something moved through his face — hurt, maybe, or its cousin — and then settled into something wearier. "You should have brought it straight to me."

"I know. I was—" She stopped. Started again, because lying to him had become strangely difficult, like trying to lie to a mirror. "I was afraid of what your face would do. And it did that."

"My face did nothing."

"Your face just did it again."

A breath escaped him that might have been a laugh in better circumstances. He looked past her, out over her orchard, at the forty-one trees going amber in the last light. "Do you want to sell?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Yara set the folder on the fence post between them, a border crossing document. "Here's what I keep landing on. That money fixes everything I came here to escape. The debts, the taxes, the roof. I could walk away whole. For the first time in my adult life I could be *whole*, Emrys, and all it costs me is forty-one trees I've known for three weeks and a neighbor I've known for—"

She stopped herself. Three weeks. It felt like a lie to say three weeks. It felt like a lie in the same direction as *ten years of letters* felt like a truth, and she wasn't ready to stand in either place yet.

"A neighbor," Emrys finished quietly, for her.

"A neighbor," she agreed, and pretended her ears weren't hot.

He was silent for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — flattened out, careful, the voice of a man reading terms and conditions aloud. "You should take the offer."

Yara stared at him. "Excuse me?"

"If I sign, you're free regardless. If I don't sign, you lose the option entirely, and you'd be losing it for my sake, and I won't—" He stopped. Restarted, the words coming slower now, like a man walking uphill in wet boots. "Yara, that vineyard has been failing since before I owned it. My father mortgaged it twice. There are days I calculate whether the harvest will cover the interest and the answer is *maybe*, and *maybe* is not a foundation to ask anyone to build on. You inherited forty acres of living trees. Don't sink them with a drowning man next door."

"Drowning man," Yara repeated. "Is that what you think you are?"

"It's what the bank thinks I am. The bank's opinion tends to prevail."

"Well, the bank can—" She pressed her fingers to her temples. "Emrys. Listen to me. I did not come here to be rescued by a man in a charcoal suit. I came here because my aunt died and I owed her something I can't pay anymore, and those trees are the only currency I have left. So don't you dare stand there and hand me a tidy exit like it's a gift. I've had enough people decide what was best for me from a safe distance. I'm *done* being managed from a safe distance."

The words came out harder than she intended, edged with something older than this argument, and she saw him register that — see him file away the crack in her voice for later, the way he filed everything, patient as soil.

"I'm sorry," he said, and meant it, and it was worse than if he hadn't. "But you should know what you'd be staying for. Or rather—" his mouth twisted, "—what you'd be staying *with*. Because if you turn down that money and I lose the vineyard anyway, you'll have traded a fortune for a view of my failure. That's the arithmetic, Yara. That's the honest arithmetic."

"Then let me see the rest of the ledger," she said. "All of it. Every debt, every deadline, every *maybe*. If I'm going to be reckless, I intend to be recklessly informed."

He looked at her for a long time. The irrigation sighed off. In the deepening dusk his face was half shadow and half gold, and she watched him wrestle with something — pride, fear, the habit of a lifetime of carrying things alone — and she watched the wrestling go on longer than it should have, longer than made sense for a simple question about numbers.

Because it wasn't a simple question about numbers. She understood that suddenly, standing at the fence with the envelope between them like a landmine. There was something underneath his refusal, something structural, load-bearing, some wall built years ago out of materials she hadn't seen yet.

"Come by tomorrow," he said at last. "In the morning. I'll show you the office. The real ledger." A pause, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "You should know what you're standing next to before you decide to stand there."

"I already know what I'm standing next to," Yara said.

He glanced up sharply.

"The trees," she added, after a beat. "Obviously. The trees, Emrys. Keep up."

But he was still looking at her, and the look had layers, and she collected her folder from the fence post and walked back up the hill toward the dark orchard feeling like a woman who had just declined a fortune and been handed, in exchange, a locked door and a key that didn't fit yet.

Behind her, in the vineyard, Emrys stood at the fence a long while, watching her go, one hand resting flat against the post her aunt had mended twice and he had repaired once, as if the fence were the only thing holding any of it together.

Inside the house, Yara set the developer's envelope on the kitchen table, weighted it down with a jar of her aunt's plum jam — *overcast, good for grief* — and slept badly, dreaming of numbers that turned into roots that turned into hands.

In the morning, she would knock on his door and ask to see the real ledger.

She had no idea yet what she was asking to see.
