## 18. The Night Before the Sale

Yara had spent the afternoon pretending to pack.

She'd folded the same three shirts twice, refolded them, and then abandoned the suitcase open on her aunt's bed like a confession she couldn't finish writing. The deed signing was at ten tomorrow morning. The developer's lawyer had called it "a simple formality," which was the kind of phrase that made Yara want to bite something. Her money was already half-wired, sitting in escrow like a hostage. Emrys's signature was all that remained.

And Emrys had gone quiet.

Not cold — never cold — but quiet in the way of a man standing at the edge of a high dive, recalculating. All week he'd been cheerful in a brittle, overwatered way, making jokes about lawyers and paperwork and how at least the developer's contract had better coffee in his office than the bank did. And every joke landed with the soft thud of something being buried alive.

So when the light turned gold and long across the orchard, Yara closed the suitcase, left it where it was, and went out to find him.

She found him where she knew she would: under the oldest tree, the Gravenstein her aunt had called *the Matriarch*, its trunk so wide that two people could lean against opposite sides and never touch. He sat on the ground with his back against the bark, hat off, hands loose in his lap, looking out at the rows of apple trees going amber in the evening light.

"You're sitting on my inheritance," Yara said.

"I'm sitting on your aunt's tree." He didn't look up. "You only inherited it yesterday."

"Semantics." She lowered herself down beside him, shoulder to shoulder, close enough that she could feel the warmth coming off him even through the cooling air. For a while neither of them said anything. Somewhere down the slope, one of the farm's old dogs barked twice at nothing and gave up on it.

"I packed today," Yara said finally.

Emrys went very still beside her. "I know."

"You *know*?"

"Your curtains were open. You held up the same green sweater for about four minutes and then put it back in the drawer." He paused. "It's a good sweater."

"It's an excellent sweater. It deserves better than a developer's condo tower." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. "I don't want to go, Emrys."

"I know that too."

"And yet tomorrow morning we're both going to sit at a table and sign away—"

"Don't." His voice came out rougher than he meant it to. She heard him take a breath, heard him try again, gentler. "Don't say it out loud yet. Let me have tonight before it becomes real."

Yara turned her head and looked at him — this man who had written her ten years of letters without ever once asking for anything, who had carried his father's debts like a coat he couldn't take off, who had refused her money not out of pride but out of terror, because he loved her too much to let her sink with him. She had been furious at him for weeks. She had cried into her pillow about it. But fury, she was learning, was just love wearing work clothes.

"I brought something," she said instead.

He glanced over. "If it's the green sweater, I approve."

"It's better than the sweater." She reached into her jacket pocket and drew out a small notebook, bound in cracked blue leather, its corners worn round as river stones. "Aunt Adaeze's journal. The last one. I found it in the attic last month, but I haven't been able to read the final entry. I've read everything else — every page, some of them three times — but not that one." She weighed it in her palm. "I think I was saving it. Which is ridiculous, because it isn't a chocolate. It doesn't spoil."

"What does it say?"

"I don't know. That's rather the point of reading it aloud, isn't it?" She shifted closer, until their shoulders pressed together properly, and opened the cover. The pages smelled of dust and cedar and something faintly sweet, like apples left too long in a drawer. "I thought — under the Matriarch. Where she used to read to me. It seemed right."

The sun had dropped behind the western ridge now, and the orchard filled with that particular blue-gold dusk that photographers chase and never catch. A breeze moved through the rows, carrying the smell of ripe fruit and turned earth. Yara found the last written page by feel more than sight, her thumb knowing the thickness of what remained.

"'Final entry,'" she read. "'Written in September, the year before I got sick.'"

Her voice caught slightly on *sick*. Emrys's hand found hers without either of them deciding it should. She held on and kept reading.

---

*Dear Yara,*

*You will never read this while I'm alive, because you are too busy being brilliant somewhere far away, and I am too proud to make you come home. We are alike in that way, you and I — we would rather bleed quietly than inconvenience anyone.*

*But I want the record to show certain things.*

*I planted this orchard the year you were born. Your mother thought I was mad — forty acres of apples in soil everyone said was better suited to grapes or grazing. But I wanted you to have a place that grew while you grew. Every tree here is named. Not after saints or presidents, the way sensible farmers do it, but after versions of you. The stubborn one by the creek is Yara-the-Defiant. The crooked little one near the gate is Yara-Who-Leaves. I pruned that one hard every year hoping it would learn to stay rooted, and every year it tried to grow sideways toward the road. I let it, eventually. Love is mostly letting things grow the way they insist on growing.*

*The Matriarch is Yara-Who-Stays. She has never borne heavily, but what she bears is the sweetest fruit on the land, and she holds the whole hillside together with her roots. When you finally come home — and you will, child, even if it takes my dying to bring you — sit under her. She'll tell you what I can't.*

*Here is what I can't: that I forgive you. That there is nothing to forgive. That leaving is not a wound unless we decide to call it one, and I have decided otherwise. You were always going to be a bird. I built the orchard anyway. Trees are for the birds that return, and for the ones that only pass through, and for the ones that never come at all — they shade all of it, every version of us.*

*One more thing, and then I'll let you get back to your life, wherever and whoever it contains. The neighbor boy — Emrys, the Okafor boy — watches this orchard from his fence line the way I once watched your mother from mine. If you are reading this, and he is anywhere within shouting distance, do me a favor and shout.*

*That's all. Be brilliant. Come home sometimes. Eat the apples before the wasps do.*

*With all the love I never managed to say out loud,*
*Auntie Dede*

---

The silence afterward was enormous.

Yara realized she was crying — not the ugly, wrenching crying of the attic, but something quieter, tears simply arriving and sliding down her face while she watched the orchard turn silver-blue in the last light. Emrys hadn't moved. His hand still held hers, and his thumb was moving slowly across her knuckles, back and forth, like he was smoothing a page.

"The neighbor boy," he said at last, and his voice was wrecked. "She *knew*. She stood at that fence and talked to me about soil pH for six years and she *knew*."

"She knew everything. She knew about the letters, probably. She knew about you before I did."

"That's mortifying."

"It's Aunt Dede. Mortification was her love language." Yara laughed wetly and wiped her face with her free hand. "She told me to shout. If you were within shouting distance."

Emrys looked at her then, fully, and whatever he saw in her face made something in his own expression crack open — the careful, weathered composure he wore like bark splitting to show the pale wood underneath.

"Yara." He said her name the way he wrote it in letters, carefully, like something breakable and precious. "Tomorrow—"

"Don't," she whispered. "You said let tonight be before it becomes real. So let it be tonight. Just tonight."

He shook his head slowly. "No. I've spent my whole life letting nights be just nights. My father taught me that wanting nothing keeps you safe, and I believed him for twenty years, and it nearly cost me the vineyard and it very nearly cost me you, and I am done." He turned to face her fully, cross-legged, taking both her hands in his. "I'm done being safe."

* * *

Emrys had rehearsed speeches all week. In the truck, in the cellar, in the shower — whole paragraphs, well-reasoned, structurally sound, the kind of arguments he'd have respected in a business meeting. *The numbers don't work. I won't drag you under. You deserve a life that isn't mortgaged.* He'd polished them until they shone.

Every one of them evaporated now, sitting under a tree named Yara-Who-Stays, holding the hands of the woman he'd loved through paper and ink for a decade before he ever learned her face.

So he said the true thing instead, the unpolished thing, the thing that had been sitting in his chest since the day she'd handed him a box labeled KITCHEN, FRAGILE, PROBABLY and he'd recognized her handwriting like recognizing his own heartbeat in a crowd.

"Ask me not to sign," he said.

Yara blinked. "What?"

"Ask me. Tonight. Right now." His heart was hammering so hard he was sure she could hear it. "Because I've been trying to be noble for months, and nobility is just fear in a nicer shirt, and I can't do it anymore. If you ask me not to sell — if you tell me you'll stay, that we'll figure out the ruin together, that you'd rather drown next to me than live dry and safe in some city — then I'll tear up that contract myself and feed it to the compost. But I can't ask you for that. I've watched one person I love ruin herself for this land already." His voice broke on the last words. "I won't be the reason it happens twice."

The orchard held its breath. Somewhere above them, the Matriarch rustled her leaves like an old woman shifting in her chair to hear better.

Yara stared at him. Then she laughed — a startled, broken, luminous laugh — and squeezed his hands so hard his fingers ached.

"You absolute *idiot*," she said, and her eyes were shining. "I offered you my inheritance. Six weeks ago. In your kitchen. Over terrible coffee. I said, *take the money, save the vineyard,* and you said no, and I have been furious with you ever since, and you're telling me you've been waiting for *me* to ask *you*?"

"I'm telling you I'm a coward with good penmanship."

"You're telling me we've spent two months refusing each other out of love and calling it protection." She was laughing and crying at once now, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen. "Emrys. Listen to me. Listen very carefully, because I'm only saying this once and then I'm going to kiss you, and after that neither of us will be capable of speech."

He waited. The whole world waited.

"Don't sign," she said. "Stay. Ruin and all. Debt and all. I have an orchard full of trees named after women I might have been, and every single one of them — the defiant one, the crooked one, even the one who leaves — every one of them would tell me to stay. My aunt forgave me before I ever asked. She planted forgiveness forty years ago and told it to wait for me." She lifted his hands and pressed them against her heart. "I'm done running, Emrys Okafor. Ten years of letters, and I finally understand what we were writing. We weren't writing to strangers. We were writing home."

For a moment he couldn't move. Then he pulled her to him — not gently, not carefully, but with the full clumsy desperate force of a man setting down a weight he'd carried for twenty years — and kissed her under the oldest tree in the orchard, while the first stars came out over the ridge and the Matriarch dropped a single late apple into the grass beside them, like a benediction, or a punchline, or both.

When they finally broke apart, foreheads touching, breathless, Yara murmured against his mouth:

"So. What exactly happens if we don't sign tomorrow?"

Emrys laughed — really laughed, deep in his chest, for the first time in longer than he could remember. "Then by nine a.m., a very expensive lawyer has a very bad day. And by noon, I owe a bank more money than either of us has ever seen. And by harvest—" he cupped her face in both hands, thumbs tracing her cheekbones like he was memorizing them, "—by harvest, we press the vintage ourselves, sell it ourselves, and gamble everything on whether the world wants wine grown by two fools in love."

"Fools in love," Yara repeated, tasting the words. "That's a terrible brand name."

"It's a *fantastic* label. I've already sketched it."

"You have *not*."

"I have absolutely — there's a drawing in the cellar next to the fermentation logs. There has been a drawing for three weeks." He grinned, and it transformed his whole tired face into something young. "I was waiting for you."

They gathered themselves slowly, reluctantly, the journal tucked safe in her jacket, the night settling cool around them. As they walked up through the rows toward the lights of the farmhouse, Yara stopped at the crooked little tree by the gate — Yara-Who-Leaves — and laid her palm against its leaning trunk.

"Not this year," she told it softly.

Beside her, Emrys slipped his arm around her waist, and together they walked home through the darkening orchard, past forty trees growing the way they insisted on growing — and tomorrow, God help them, they would fight.
