## 19. One Last Harvest

The frost cloth came down at noon on the third of October, and Emrys Okafor stood in the middle of Block Four with his hands full of netting and his heart full of something he hadn't felt in years — which was to say, anything at all.

Half the crop had survived. He kept repeating it to himself like a prayer he didn't quite believe: *half the crop, half the crop.* The lower rows, tucked against the slope where cold air drained away instead of pooling, had come through the frost with their fruit intact. The upper blocks were a graveyard of blackened clusters that smelled, when the wind shifted right, like a wine cellar's grief.

But half. Half was not nothing. Half was a chance, if you squinted at it sideways, if you held your mouth right, if you did everything else perfectly from this moment forward.

"Em." Yara appeared at the end of the row, her hair escaping its braid in a way that suggested she'd been fighting both the wind and the netting all morning. She had dirt on her cheekbone and a scratch on her forearm and she was, he thought not for the first time, the most beautiful thing this land had ever produced, including the land itself. "The press is dead."

He closed his eyes. "Dead dead, or dramatic dead?"

"It made a noise like a wounded animal and then it made no noise at all. I'd call that dead dead."

Of course it was. The press was older than he was, inherited along with everything else — the debts, the mortgage, the name Okafor stenciled on equipment that predated color television. His father had bought it secondhand in 1987 and maintained it the way he maintained everything: reluctantly, and only when failure was imminent.

Emrys set down the netting. "Right," he said. "New plan."

"You've had four new plans since breakfast."

"And this one is the best one, because I've had the most practice." He started walking toward the barn, and she fell into step beside him, matching his stride without effort, the way she matched everything now. "We don't have a press. So we press by hand."

Yara stopped walking. "By hand."

"By hand."

"You want to make wine by stomping grapes. Like a Renaissance fair. Like a cartoon."

"Like every winemaker on earth for five thousand years," Emrys said, "until some genius invented machinery, and machinery turned out to be expensive, fragile, and mortgaged." He heard himself and laughed — actually laughed, a short startled sound that surprised them both. "Look at me. Making debt jokes. That's growth."

She was smiling at him, though, that crooked smile that undid him more efficiently than any frost ever could. "You're serious."

"I'm completely serious. We have maybe two tons of good fruit. A commercial press run would cost money we don't have and take time we don't have. But two tons is small enough for open fermentation, basket pressing by hand, and honest labor." He gestured at the vineyard around them, at the workers moving through the rows — Marta and her crew, who had stayed through the frost scare without being asked twice, because Marta had worked for his father and knew exactly what kind of man had replaced him, or so she claimed, though Emrys suspected her loyalty was simpler than that and warmer. "It's small enough that people can do it. That's the whole point. This vintage was never going to be made by machines. It was always going to be made by us."

*Us.* The word sat in the air between them, and neither of them rushed to pick it up or put it down.

"The bank gives you twenty-six days," Yara said quietly.

"Twenty-five now. Thank you for the reminder, I was having a nice moment."

"Someone has to keep the books." She bumped her shoulder against his arm. "Okay. Hand pressing. What do we need?"

"Crates. Buckets. A food-grade barrel or six, which I may be able to beg off old Tomás at the co-op. Cheesecloth. Yeast, if we're being scientific about it, which we are, because I refuse to let my one great gamble taste like vinegar out of romanticism." He counted on his fingers. "And hands. As many hands as we can get."

"I'll write the letters," Yara said. "I'm very good at letters."

He looked at her. She looked back, entirely unrepentant.

"That was one letter," he said. "One. Ten years ago."

"It was a very good letter. It got you to answer."

* * *

They told the workers that evening, standing in the barn among the crates and the surviving harvest, and Emrys braced himself for the polite silence of people listening to a doomed man describe his doom.

Instead, Marta crossed her arms and said, "Basket pressing. My grandmother pressed by hand in Portugal until she was seventy. You'll do it wrong unless someone shows you."

"Marta—"

"Sixty percent of the harvest share, deferred to next season, when you can pay." She said it like a businesswoman, because she was one, and had been quietly running half his operation for years while letting him believe otherwise. "And I want it in writing, because your father promised things too."

"He did," Emrys said. "In writing. That was the problem."

That got the laugh he was fishing for, and after the laugh, something loosened in the room. Old Dele, who pruned the eastern rows and had once, memorably, told Emrys's father to his face that he was ruining good land, raised a gnarled hand. "I'll stomp grapes. I've been wanting to stomp something on this farm for thirty years. Never thought I'd get permission."

"It's not stomping, technically, it's—" Emrys began.

"It's stomping," said Dele, "and I'm doing it barefoot, and nobody's stopping me."

By the time the meeting broke up, they had eleven volunteers, three borrowed barrels, a promise of cheesecloth from the co-op, and a plan scrawled on the whiteboard in Yara's handwriting — that handwriting, still, after everything, capable of making his chest do something embarrassing whenever he saw it. She'd written *ONE LAST HARVEST* across the top and underlined it twice, and beneath it, a list that looked less like a plan and more like a dare.

He stood looking at it after everyone left, and Yara came to stand beside him, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"My father would have hated this," Emrys said finally.

"Because it's risky?"

"Because it's *small*. He wanted big. Big loans, big expansion, big plans he never finished. He wanted the vineyard to be impressive before he wanted it to be good." Emrys touched the edge of the whiteboard. "I spent my whole life trying to want nothing, because wanting things is how he destroyed us. And now here I am, wanting this — this ridiculous, handmade, two-ton vintage — more than I've ever wanted anything except—"

He stopped. Yara waited. She was very good at waiting; ten years of letters had taught her the shape of his silences.

"Except you," he finished, quietly. "Obviously."

"Obviously," she agreed, and her ears went pink, and he fell a little further in love with her, which was becoming a daily hazard of employment.

* * *

His sister arrived on Thursday.

Emrys saw the car coming up the drive — a silver sedan too clean for these roads — and his stomach performed its familiar Adaeze-related maneuver, dropping several floors at speed. They had spoken twice since she'd signed with the developer. Both conversations had been short. Neither had ended well.

But she got out of the car carrying a cooler, and she was wearing work boots, actual work boots, mud-flecked ones, and that combination of facts did not fit together in any way he understood.

"If you're here about the sale," he said, "the answer hasn't changed."

"I'm not here about the sale." She set the cooler on the tailgate of his truck and opened it, revealing sandwiches — dozens of sandwiches, wrapped in wax paper, labeled in her precise handwriting. "I'm here because Mama called me and asked what I was doing while my brother gambled the family farm on stomping grapes, and I said watching television, and she said, *Adaeze, go help your brother,* in the voice. You know the voice."

Emrys did know the voice. It had ended arguments between nations, presumably.

"So you're here under duress."

"I'm here under duress," she agreed. "Also—" She paused, and he watched his sister, who had inherited their mother's spine and their father's stubbornness and none of either parent's talent for pretending, struggle visibly with a sentence. "Also I read the thing Yara posted. Online. About the harvest day. Open to the community, bring gloves, etcetera." Her jaw tightened. "Two hundred people signed up, Em. Two hundred. People from town. People from the co-op. Someone offered to bring a drum."

"A drum."

"A drum, Emrys. For grape stomping. Apparently that's a thing people do."

"It is a thing people do," he said faintly. "At festivals. Which we are apparently having."

"We are apparently having a festival." She picked up the cooler again. "I still think selling is smarter. I still think you're gambling with our father's mistakes instead of burying them. I reserve the right to say I told you so, extensively, if this fails." She marched toward the barn, then stopped, not turning around. "But if it doesn't fail — if you pull this off — then it won't just be a farm saved. It'll be proof that he was wrong. That the land could be loved instead of leveraged. And I want to be here for that." A beat. "Also Mama sent jollof. Don't let Yara eat it all before lunch."

"She already knows about the jollof, doesn't she."

"She texted her from my phone. They're conspiring. I've lost control of the conspiracy."

Emrys watched his sister walk into the barn, shoulders squared like a woman reporting for a duty she'd never admit to wanting, and he stood in the driveway for a long moment, blinking at the sky, doing absolutely nothing about the fact that his eyes had gone hot.

* * *

Harvest day arrived gray-gold and cold enough to see breath, and the vineyard filled with people the way a church fills — gradually, then all at once, then overwhelmingly.

They came in trucks and on foot. Marta's crew, who needed no instruction and gave plenty. Dele, barefoot as threatened, standing in the first crate of Pinot Noir with an expression of profound spiritual satisfaction. The co-op crowd, forty strong, who'd driven over en masse because Tomás had declared it "a matter of regional honor." Neighbors whose names Emrys only half knew. A family of four whose youngest daughter took one look at the stomping vats and had to be physically restrained from diving in headfirst. Two hundred signatures on Yara's list, and it seemed like most of them had actually come, drawn by a flyer that read, in her handwriting, *Help save the vineyard next door. Bring gloves. Leave with wine (eventually).*

Yara ran the whole operation like a general who happened to be in love with the battlefield. She'd organized picking teams by block, assigned crate runners, set up washing stations, negotiated a truce between Dele and the drummers over volume disputes. When Emrys tried to thank her, mid-morning, she pressed a sandwich into his hand and said, "Eat. Generals who faint are bad for morale."

Adaeze worked the sorting table beside their mother's best friend, rejecting damaged berries with ruthless precision, and when Emrys passed, she said, without looking up, "Your fermentation temperatures better be planned."

"They're planned."

"Show me later."

It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even peace. But it was his sister asking to see his work, and after twenty years of silence shaped like family, Emrys would take it.

By late afternoon, the surviving half of the harvest sat in open fermenters in the barn — crushed by two hundred feet, carried by two hundred hands, smelling of fruit and yeast and something harder to name. Emrys stood at the edge of it all, exhausted past speech, and watched Yara teach the smallest child how to punch down the cap of grape skins with a wooden paddle, gently, like stirring a giant pot of soup.

Marta found him there. She handed him a cup of tea — she always seemed to produce tea from nowhere at critical moments, a skill Emrys suspected was the actual source of her power — and stood beside him surveying the barn.

"Your father," she said, "would have charged admission."

"Probably."

"This is better." She sipped her own tea. "This tastes like wine already. The rest is just chemistry."

The light was going amber outside the barn doors. Yara caught his eye across the room, and held up the paddle, and grinned at him, flour — no, grape must — smeared across one cheek, and Emrys thought: *whatever happens in twenty-four days, whatever the bank says, whatever becomes of this land — this happened. These people came. This vintage exists because love showed up in work boots.*

Then his phone buzzed in his pocket, and he almost didn't check it, and then he did, because hope makes you careless.

An email. From a wine writer whose column he'd subscribed to for years and never once dared contact. Subject line: *Heard about your harvest day.*

*Okafor — Tomás mentioned you're doing a field blend, hand-pressed, community harvest, the works. If it's true, I want the story. And if the wine's any good, I want to taste it. Send me two bottles when it's ready. I can't promise coverage. But I can promise I'll drink them honestly.*

Emrys read it three times. Then he walked across the barn, tapped Yara on the shoulder, and handed her the phone without a word.

She read it. Read it again. Looked up at him with her eyes gone wide and bright.

"Emrys," she said. "This is — if she writes about it—"

"Small distributors will return my calls," he said. "Restaurants. Direct sales. Enough to restructure. Maybe enough to survive." His voice cracked on the last word, and he didn't care. "It's not a miracle. It's a door. It's one door, and we still have to walk through it, and the wine has to be *good*, and the fermentation could still fail, and—"

Yara kissed him.

In front of two hundred people, in the middle of the barn, with grape must on her face and a wooden paddle still in her hand, she kissed him, and the barn — because the universe has a sense of humor — erupted into applause, whistles, and one triumphant drum solo from the corner.

When she pulled back, breathless, she said, "Sorry. You were spiraling. It's my one proven technique."

"Noted," Emrys managed. "Effective."

Above them, unnoticed by anyone, the evening star came out over Block Four — over the blackened rows and the living ones, over the graves and the cradles of this stubborn, borrowed, beloved ground — and somewhere in the dark fermenters, the yeast began, quietly, patiently, to work.
