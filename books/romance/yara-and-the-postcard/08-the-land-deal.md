## 8. The Land Deal

The Haven's End Historical Society occupied the ground floor of the old cannery office, a building that smelled of brine and toner, run by a retired schoolteacher named Dot Pemberton who had known Yara since she was small enough to be bribed with butterscotch.

"Research," Dot repeated, when Yara explained what she wanted. "For the memoir. How exciting."

"It's not exciting. It's mostly about my grandmother."

"Even better." Dot was already leading her past the microfilm machine toward a back room where the town's real records lived—deed books, council minutes, tax rolls, all of it unsorted and under-loved. "Nobody comes in here. You'll have the place to yourself. There's coffee if you don't mind it being terrible."

Yara had told herself she was only here for texture. Color. The kind of small-town detail Marisol wanted—the smell of the fish plant in August, the year the ferry schedule changed, whatever filler could pad out a memoir that was, at its honest core, just a woman writing about her grandmother to avoid writing about herself.

That was the story she'd been telling herself for two weeks, and she'd almost made herself believe it.

But she'd brought her grandmother's name with her, written on the inside cover of a notebook like an accusation: *Ingrid Lindqvist.* And when Dot left her alone among the boxes, Yara did not pull the files about the fish plant or the ferry. She pulled the deed books.

She told herself it was diligence. A memoirist should know what her family owned and when. That was all.

* * *

The lighthouse parcel sat on a promontory at the north end of the harbor, eleven acres of wind-scoured grass and granite, and Yara had grown up believing it belonged to her family the way blood belongs to a body—absolutely, invisibly, without question. Her grandfather had kept the light for thirty-one years. Her grandmother had kept him. When the Coast Guard automated the beacon in 1989, the house went with the job, or so Yara had always understood it: the family moved down into town, the light burned on without them, and that was simply how time worked.

The deed book told a different story.

She found it on her second afternoon, cross-referencing the 1974 council minutes against a transfer record that didn't make sense. The parcel had never been sold. It had been *conveyed*—that was the word, sitting there in careful typewritten font, so clean and bureaucratic it took her a moment to feel its weight. Conveyed from Ingrid and Henrik Lindqvist to the Town of Haven's End, consideration: one dollar.

One dollar. For eleven acres and the most valuable view on the coast.

Yara sat very still in the cold back room and read the page three times, as if the words might rearrange themselves if she gave them the chance. They didn't. She turned to the council minutes from the same season, and there they were, the names, listed like a cast of characters in a play she'd unknowingly lived inside her whole life: Selectman Aldous Eriksen. Councilwoman Ruth Vane. Selectman Peter Halloway. Five men and one woman, voting four to two to accept the conveyance, with a handwritten note in the margin of the minutes—someone's pen, someone's conscience—that read: *Mrs. L. not present. Was told meeting moved.*

*Was told meeting moved.*

Yara's hands were cold. She noticed this distantly, the way you notice weather happening to someone else. Her grandfather had died by then—1973, the winter she'd never known because no one talked about it—and her grandmother had been left alone with a lighthouse, a debt-ridden parcel, and a town full of helpful neighbors. And the neighbors had come. Of course they had. Someone had told Ingrid Lindqvist the meeting date changed, and Ingrid—who trusted people, who fed strangers, who taught Yara to always leave the porch light on for whoever might need it—had believed them.

And while she waited for a meeting that wasn't happening, the men of Haven's End voted her family's land into their own keeping.

Yara closed the book. Opened it again. Made herself copy the page numbers into her notebook in her neatest hand, because some part of her brain, the professional part, the part that had survived eleven years in publishing, was already working even as the rest of her stood in the wreckage. *Document everything,* that part said. *Get copies. Get dates. Find the second dissenting vote.*

The second dissenting vote. She flipped forward through the minutes, looking for the record of who had voted no, and found it appended to the next month's session: Councilwoman Ruth Vane had formally objected, citing inadequate compensation, and had been overruled. And beneath her objection, in the flat language of small-government defeat, a single line:

*Mr. Eriksen notes for the record that the keeper's widow has agreed to terms and that further delay serves no one.*

Agreed to terms. Yara stared at the sentence until it blurred. Her grandmother had agreed to nothing. Her grandmother had been lied to about a meeting and then described, in the public record, as a willing seller—a fiction so tidy, so useful, that it had hardened into fact and sat there for fifty years, waiting for a granddaughter with a library card.

Aldous Eriksen. Gideon's grandfather. Gideon's father, Torben, would have been a young man then—old enough to know, surely, or to learn later. Old enough to inherit the story along with the money the Eriksen boatyard had built on the harbor front, land that had appreciated, land that connected, land that—Yara pulled the harbor district plat map off the shelf with suddenly steady hands—yes. The Eriksen yard sat on parcels that had passed through the town's hands in the same decade, acquired cheap, consolidated fast, the whole waterfront quietly reassembled around a family that had arrived in Haven's End with a fishing boat and left it owning the shore.

She copied everything. She photographed pages with her phone until the battery died, then borrowed Dot's charger and kept going. Dot came in once, looked at the spread of documents, and asked no questions—which was its own kind of answer, because Dot Pemberton had taught fourth grade in this town for thirty-five years and knew exactly which families' names were best left unspoken in certain rooms.

At five o'clock, Yara walked out into the gray afternoon holding a folder that weighed almost nothing and felt like it contained a live thing.

* * *

She didn't go home. She drove north instead, up the coast road to the lighthouse, because her body made the decision before her mind caught up—the same way, she supposed, her grandmother's feet must have carried her up that hill a hundred times, toward a light she no longer tended, on a piece of land that was no longer hers.

The gate was chained now. Town property. Yara had walked past that chain her entire life without once wondering why a lighthouse her grandfather had kept should be closed to his granddaughter. She'd thought it was liability insurance. Bureaucracy. The ordinary indifference of towns toward their own history.

She stood at the fence in the wind and understood, with a clarity that felt less like discovery than like a bone setting, that the chain had been put there by the same vote that had taken the land. Everything she'd accepted as background—the modest house in town, the family stories that stopped at 1974, her grandmother's strange silence whenever anyone mentioned the old days at the light—it all rearranged itself around this new center of gravity. Her grandmother hadn't been sentimental about the lighthouse. She'd been *silent* about it. There was a difference, and the difference had a price, and the price had been paid across two generations by people who never saw the bill.

Yara leaned her forehead against the cold chain-link.

Because here was the thing she couldn't get past, the splinter working deeper the harder she pushed: her grandmother had known. Not suspected—known. Yara had spent two weeks in the attic boxes after finding the postcard, cataloguing letters for the memoir, and she remembered now—remembered with horrible precision—a letter from Ruth Vane to Ingrid Lindqvist, dated the spring after the conveyance, its language careful and grieving, an apology without the word apology in it. Yara had skimmed it, filed it under *town correspondence, miscellaneous,* and moved on. She'd held the evidence in her hands and set it aside, because she'd been looking for love letters and material, not for this.

Her grandmother had known what was taken from her. And she had stayed. Kept the little house on Pilgrim Street, raised her daughter, spoiled her granddaughters, baked for the church fair, waved to Aldous Eriksen on the street—Yara was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of things you've seen your whole life without seeing. Had she smiled at him? Had she bought bread from the store his money helped build?

Why would anyone do that?

The wind came off the water and pushed against her like a hand. And Yara heard her own voice in her head, answering the question, in the voice she used for excuses: *Because what else do you do? Because he was dead and the fight was lost and she had a child to feed. Because a town is a small place and a woman alone in a small place learns which hills are worth dying on.*

It was a good answer. It was probably even true.

It was also, Yara realized, standing at the fence of stolen land in the last light of the afternoon, exactly the kind of answer she'd been giving for her own life for eleven years. *What else do you do. The fight is lost. Learn which hills are worth it.* She had built a whole philosophy out of her grandmother's silence, called it wisdom, called it staying small, called it knowing your size—and all along it hadn't been wisdom at all. It had been a wound, dressed up and handed down, and she'd worn it like inheritance.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. Marisol. Yara looked at the screen and let it ring out, then watched the voicemail badge appear, then watched a text follow it: *Thinking about structure. Call me when you can. I think we lead with the lighthouse.*

*I think we lead with the lighthouse.* As if Marisol could smell it through the phone. As if the manuscript already knew what Yara had only just learned.

She got in the car and sat with the engine off while the dark came down over the water. The lighthouse beam swept overhead every twelve seconds—automated, faithful, keeping watch over a family it no longer belonged to—and each pass lit the interior of the car white and then released it, white and then released it, like a slow interrogation.

Gideon's face rose in her mind, unbidden: Gideon at seventeen, laughing on the breakwater; Gideon at twenty-eight, the night she left, standing in the rain outside the bus station saying *go, then, if that's what you want* in a voice that meant the opposite. Gideon at thirty-nine, three weeks ago in the fish market, polite and careful and watching her like a man watching weather.

He hadn't done anything. He'd been nine years old when the vote happened. Whatever the Eriksen fortune was built on, it had been built before him, around him, underneath him—he'd inherited the harbor the way she'd inherited the silence, without ever being shown the deed.

But he was about to marry Celeste Vane. And Ruth Vane—the lone dissenting vote, the one conscience on that council—had been a Vane too. Which meant the Vane family knew both sides of it: the theft and the objection. Which meant Celeste's family name was braided through this every bit as tightly as Gideon's, and the engagement announcement at the lantern festival would unite the two oldest names in Haven's End over ground that had been taken from Yara's grandmother with a lie and a one-dollar bill.

Yara started the car. Her hands were steady now, and that frightened her more than the shaking had. Steady hands were the hands she wrote with. Steady hands were the hands that had signed contracts before, that had learned long ago that other people's pain made excellent material if you could hold them still enough.

She drove home through the dark and did not turn on the radio.

* * *

The attic boxes were still stacked in her spare room where she'd left them, and she went through them that night like a woman searching a burning house, pulling letters, journals, photographs, laying everything on the bed in chronological order. It was nearly two in the morning when she found the journal she was looking for—she'd seen it before, a slim cloth-bound book with a cracked spine, and had assumed it was recipes, because the early entries were recipes, herring in dill sauce, cardamom buns, her grandmother's handwriting looping and sure.

The recipes ended in March of 1974.

Yara turned the page and her grandmother's voice changed mid-book, the way voices do when no one is listening anymore.

*They tell me the meeting was moved and I missed my chance to speak. Ruth says this is not so, that the meeting was held as scheduled and I was expected. One of us is mistaken. I do not believe Ruth is mistaken. I do not know what to do with what I know. Henrik is eight months gone and the bank wants its money and Aldous stood on my step today with his hat in his hands and I could not hate him, which may be the worst thing of all. A person should be able to hate a man who robs her. I will write it all down here so that someday someone knows I knew.*

*Someday someone knows I knew.*

Yara sat on the floor of the spare room with the journal open in her lap and the streetlight coming through the curtains, and she read the entry again, and then the ones after it—weeks of them, months, her grandmother circling the wound, deciding, undeciding. *If I fight them I lose the house too. If I fight them the town will close around me like water. If I fight them, Astrid grows up the daughter of the crazy lighthouse woman and no boy will dance with her and no shop will trust her credit. So I will keep the peace. God forgive me, I will keep the peace, and I will bake for the fair, and I will smile, and I will write the truth here where it cannot hurt anyone, and I will call that survival.*

There it was. The whole architecture of her childhood, load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall. The silence hadn't been weakness. It had been a decision, made by a woman with no good options, and then maintained for fifty years at the cost of everything it was supposed to protect. Her grandmother had traded truth for safety and called the trade love, and Yara had inherited the ledger without ever seeing the accounts.

And now Marisol wanted the story. Now the publisher wanted *The Lighthouse Keeper's Granddaughter* with the land deal in it, the scandal, the buried crime, the famous family names—an advance big enough to erase Yara's debts, a book big enough to finally matter, and all of it priced in the currency of Gideon's name and Celeste's family and a town's comfortable lies.

Yara had thought, driving home, that she'd already decided. That the research was the decision, that she'd crossed some line at the fence and there was no uncrossing it.

But sitting on the floor with her grandmother's confession in her hands, she felt the other pull, the older one, the family gravity: *keep the peace, bake for the fair, write the truth somewhere it cannot hurt anyone.* Burn it. File it. Call it survival.

Her phone glowed on the floor beside her. A new message. She picked it up expecting Marisol, another nudge, another deadline dressed as enthusiasm.

It wasn't Marisol.

It was a number she didn't have saved, and the message was short, and friendly, and landed in the dark room like a dropped glass:

*Hi Yara — Celeste Vane here! Gideon mentioned you're back in town. I'm chairing the festival committee and we'd LOVE to feature a local author. Coffee this week? Would mean so much. 🌊*

Yara looked at the message for a long time. Then she looked at the journal, open to a page where a robbed woman had chosen silence to protect the children of the men who robbed her—one of whom had been named Eriksen, and one of whom, in the way of small towns and long marriages, had very likely been named Vane.

She typed back: *I'd like that. Thursday?*

Then she set the phone face-down on the floor, next to fifty years of everything her grandmother had never sent, and did not sleep.
