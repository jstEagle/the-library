## 9. A Drink Neither Planned

Gideon told himself it was a harbor matter.

That was the story he'd constructed on the walk over, and he'd built it well, with load-bearing details: the workshop flyer tacked to the notice board by the chandlery listed Yara Lindqvist's Thursday session, *Writing the Place That Made You*, open to the public, held in the back room of the Salt & Anchor. A town event. Harbormasters attended town events. It was practically in the job description.

The fact that it was raining sideways and the workshop had ended forty minutes ago was a wrinkle in the narrative he chose not to examine.

He stood under the Anchor's awning longer than any man should stand under an awning, watching rain gutter off the edge and thinking about Celeste's voice on the phone that morning—*the caterer confirmed, the lantern order came in, everyone's so excited, Gid*—and how each sentence had landed like a stone dropped down a well he couldn't find the bottom of. He was getting married in June. He was content. He had arranged his life the way you arrange a good mooring: checked lines, proper tension, nothing left to weather.

And then he pushed open the door.

The Salt & Anchor at nine on a Thursday was half empty, smelling of fryer oil and old varnish, the jukebox playing something mournful that nobody had chosen. Yara sat at the far end of the bar with a notebook open and a glass of wine she wasn't drinking, and when the door's bell announced him she looked up, and Gideon watched eleven years of careful distance fail to survive the crossing of that room.

"Harbormaster," she said.

"Lindqvist." He took the stool two down from hers. Not beside her. There were protocols. "Your flyer says the workshop runs till nine."

"It does. I let them out early. Marta Reyes was writing about her late husband and I figured the room needed air more than craft."

"That was kind."

"It was cowardice," she said, and almost smiled. "Kindness is just cowardice that went to finishing school."

He laughed before he could stop himself—a real laugh, surprised out of him—and saw her register it, the way her pen stilled against the page. Eleven years, and apparently they still spoke the same language. That was the trouble with knowing someone young. You learned their dialect too well to ever fully unlearn it.

"So," he said, and ordered a beer from Denny behind the bar because standing at a bar without ordering was loitering, and harbormasters didn't loiter. "You're teaching now."

"I'm teaching. And writing." She closed the notebook, which told him everything: whatever was in it was not for him. "A book."

"What kind of book?"

"The kind publishers buy."

"That's not an answer."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

Denny set the beer down and retreated with the discretion of a man who'd poured drinks in Haven's End long enough to know which conversations required deafness. Gideon turned the bottle a quarter turn on the coaster. Turned it back.

"I read your last one," he said.

Yara looked at him properly for the first time. Her eyes did the thing they'd always done—went very still, very direct, like water over deep ground. "You did not."

"The lighthouse keeper who loses his light in the storm. The daughter who comes home too late." He drank. "I recognized the lighthouse."

"It was fictionalized."

"Gideon Eriksen is not a subtle name to give a character, Yara."

"You're right," she said quietly. "It isn't. I've never been subtle about anything except the things that mattered."

The jukebox changed songs without anyone touching it. Somewhere in the back a pool ball clicked into a pocket. Gideon understood, distantly, that he had come here to ask a question and that every instinct he owned was now advising retreat, and that he was going to ignore all of them, because he was thirty-four years old and tired of being a man who managed things.

"Why here?" he asked. "Why come back? You had New York. You had—" he gestured vaguely at her, at the whole published, reviewed, photographed fact of her, "—all that."

"All what?" She turned on her stool to face him, and up close he could see the years on her—not as damage, exactly, but as weathering, the way driftwood weathers, grain showing where softness used to be. "Say it. You've been building up to saying it since you walked in. All *that*. The career I chose instead of—"

"Don't."

"Instead of staying," she finished anyway, because she had always run toward the cliff edge while other people were still warning her about it. "Yes. I had all that. And it turns out success is a room you build around yourself one brick at a time until one day you look up and there are no windows and you can't remember why you started building."

"That's the saddest description of publishing I've ever heard."

"It's the honest one." She wrapped both hands around her wineglass but still didn't drink. "I came back because my grandmother died, Gideon. That's the whole answer. Everything else is decoration."

"I'm sorry. About her. She was—" He stopped, recalibrated. "She used to slip me cinnamon buns when I fixed her dock line. Told me I was the only Eriksen worth the flour."

Something moved across Yara's face, fast, gone before he could name it. "She said that?"

"Every time."

"Huh." Yara stared at her wine. "She never said that to me."

"Maybe she thought you already knew."

"Maybe," Yara said, "she knew I didn't deserve to hear it."

The words dropped between them with a weight that made no sense—light, offhand, and yet the whole bar seemed to tilt a degree. Gideon set down his beer.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing. Forget it." She was already rebuilding the wall; he could see the mortar going up. "So. How's—Celeste, right? I saw the announcement in the paper. Congratulations. The festival, the engagement, the whole—" 

"We haven't set a date."

"You will."

"Probably."

"Probably," she repeated, and there was something in her voice he refused to identify, because identifying it would require him to do something about it, and he was an engaged man sitting two stools away from the woman who had gutted him at twenty-three, and identification was a luxury he could not afford.

But he stayed.

That was the thing he would think about later, in the dark, with the honesty that only arrives after midnight: he stayed. Denny brought another round without being asked. The rain kept up its static against the windows. And somewhere between the second drink and the third, the conversation stopped being a conversation between two careful adults and became something older, something from before, the way water finds an old channel.

She asked about his mother, and he told her about the winter she died, three years ago now, and how he'd found her reading glasses still folded on top of a library book she'd never finish, and how he'd kept the book on his nightstand for a year without opening it because finishing it felt like closing an account. Yara listened the way she'd always listened—completely, as if he were the only sound in the world—and when he finished she said, "You never told anyone you kept it."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're telling me. That's how I know."

He asked about New York, and this time she answered, really answered: the agent who dropped her after her second novel underperformed, the year she wrote greeting-card copy under a pseudonym, the apartment with the radiator that clanked like a ship's engine so that some nights she could pretend she was near water. "I wrote my best scene in that apartment," she said. "Chapter nine of the second book. The one reviewers called 'hauntingly authentic.' It was the harbor. I was homesick so bad I could taste it, and I wrote the harbor, and everyone said how real it felt, and I sat there thinking: *you have no idea what it cost.*"

"And now you're writing another one."

"Now I'm writing another one."

"About Haven's End."

"About Haven's End."

"About—" he hesitated, and then, because the third drink had dissolved the last of his diplomacy, "—about the lighthouse parcel?"

Her face did something complicated. "What do you know about the lighthouse parcel?"

"Nothing. Less than nothing. My father gets a certain look whenever it comes up, which means it's a subject, which means it's a wound, which means in this town it's probably a crime." He shook his head. "Forget it. Why are you asking me?"

"Because you're the only person in Haven's End who might tell me the truth about your own family," she said. "And I hate that I need to ask."

"Yara." He leaned in, close enough to see the lamplight caught in her eyes, close enough that the old gravity took hold of them both whether they willed it or not. "Whatever you're writing—if it touches my family—I'm asking you, before you print a word, to talk to me first. Not Celeste. Not the council. Me."

"Why me?"

"Because I remember the night you left."

The bar noise receded. Or maybe it didn't; maybe it was just that everything else got quiet inside him.

"You don't want to talk about that night," she said.

"I've spent eleven years not talking about that night. It's the longest conversation I've ever had." He turned the beer bottle, quarter turn, back again. "You stood at the end of my dock with your bags packed and you told me the town was too small for you, and I said go, then, if it's too small, and you said—that's the part I've never gotten straight—you said, *it's not the town that's small.* And then you got on the bus and I stood there like an idiot in the rain and I have spent eleven years deciding what you meant."

"Do you want to know what I meant?"

"No," Gideon said honestly. "Yes. God. Both."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the rain eased, and the harbor lights came out doubled on the wet black water, and somewhere a boat horn sounded once, low, like the town clearing its throat.

"I meant," Yara said slowly, "that I was the small one. That I wanted the big life so badly I was willing to leave the true one behind, and I dressed it up as ambition because ambition sounds brave and fear doesn't. I meant I'm sorry. I meant I've been sorry since the bus pulled out. I meant—" she stopped, pressed her lips together, and he watched her decide, deliberately, to say less rather than more, and recognized the gesture because he'd seen it once before, eleven years ago, at the end of a dock. "I meant what I said. That's enough for tonight."

"It's not."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. But it's what I've got."

Gideon paid for both rounds over her objection. At the door, with the bell above them and the whole wet street waiting, he paused.

"Thursday workshop," he said. "Do you take drop-ins?"

"It's a writing workshop, Gideon."

"I have things I've never written down. Eleven years' worth." He pulled his collar up against the drizzle. "Seems like a waste, letting them rot."

He walked out before she could answer, which was either courage or cowardice—he genuinely could no longer tell the difference—and made it four steps down the sidewalk before he realized he was smiling, and made it four more before he realized he was smiling the way he hadn't smiled since he was twenty-three, and stopped dead under the flickering streetlamp outside the chandlery with the cold truth finally catching up to him:

One drink had become three hours. Three hours had undone eleven years. And in the morning he would wake up engaged to Celeste Marsh, in a town that watched everything, while the woman he'd just spent the evening with was writing a book about Haven's End that she wouldn't show him, that she flinched from naming, that made her ask questions about the lighthouse parcel with guilt written all over her face.

He should have felt guilty. He searched for it and found only the hairline crack, widening.

Behind him, through the Anchor's fogged window, he could see Yara still at the bar, notebook open again, writing fast, and he stood in the rain watching her write whatever she was writing—about him, about the night, about the parcel, he couldn't know—and the not-knowing felt less like ignorance than like a countdown.

* * *

Yara wrote until Denny called last call, and even a little past it, and what she wrote was not the memoir chapter she owed Marisol. It was a letter-shaped thing, addressed to no one, beginning *Dear Gideon, what I didn't say at the door was*—

She stopped. Read it. Tore it out.

Some confessions, she was learning, kept. Like the postcard in her coat pocket, they waited eleven years without complaint, patient as tide. She folded the torn page into quarters and put it in the box where unsent things lived, and told herself the box was closed, and knew, walking home through the dripping streets with the harbor breathing behind her, that boxes like that always open again—usually at the worst possible moment, usually in front of witnesses.

Across the harbor, a light burned late in the harbormaster's office. She watched it a moment too long.

Then she went home to a manuscript that was starting to feel like a lit fuse.
