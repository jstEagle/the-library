## 22. Yara and the Promise

The promise hangs above my bed in a black frame that Papa Theo made from an old bakery shelf, because he said promises should live somewhere sturdy. It's just a piece of paper with my handwriting on it, the letters wobbly at the bottoms because I was eight when I wrote it and now I'm nine and my handwriting is much better, but Hazel says we don't fix it.

"You don't retouch a promise," Hazel said. "It's not a manuscript."

I didn't know what retouch meant then. Now I do, because I read everything in the bookshop when story hour is slow, which is how I learned that retouching is what you do to photographs to make them look like something they're not. Which means Hazel was saying our promise looks exactly like what it is. Which is true. It says:

**NOBODY LEAVES WITHOUT SAYING GOODBYE.**

And under it, smaller, because I made them sign it separately on two different nights so neither one could see the other's face while they did it: *Yara Ishikawa, witness.* Then Hazel's signature, neat like typing. Then Theo's signature, big and loopy with a little flour smudge he refused to wipe off.

"That's authentication," he said. "Any document worth signing should have flour on it."

One year later, the flour is brownish and the frame glass has a fingerprint in the corner that is mine, from the night I hung it, and nobody has cleaned it because everybody in this house agrees that some things you leave alone.

* * *

Our house — well, it's not really a house, it's the bookshop with stairs now, but before I explain that I have to explain the door.

The door is the best part.

Last spring, when the zoning board finally said yes — after Hazel stood up in front of all those folding chairs and talked until their voice shook and Mrs. Okafor from the garden society switched her vote like flipping a pancake — a construction crew came and cut a doorway right through the wall between the bookshop and the bakery. Hazel supervised the whole thing holding a clipboard even though they weren't in charge of anything, and Theo brought the crew cinnamon rolls every morning until one of the workers, a big guy named Denny, said if the project took any longer he was going to have to buy new pants.

Now there is no wall between the books and the bread. There is a wide doorway with no door in it, which Theo says is the point. "A door you can close is a door you can lose," he told me once, dusting his hands on his apron. "An opening is just an opening."

On Saturday mornings, story hour happens in the bookshop side, and the smell comes through the opening — cinnamon, mostly, and sometimes cardamom, which is my favorite word to say out loud even though I used to think it was a bird. The babies sit on the braided rug and chew their fists, and the moms drink coffee Theo sends over on a tray, and I do the voices for the picture books because I'm good at it. I can do a bear voice that makes toddlers laugh so hard they tip over sideways like bowling pins.

Hazel reads the actual words. Hazel's voice does the steady part, the part that holds everything up. That's how it works. Theo is the smell and the tray and the laughing, and Hazel is the voice that keeps going even when a baby starts crying, and I am the bear.

Nobody planned it this way. It just happened, the way the opening happened, the way families happen when you stop building walls.

* * *

Here is what I know about the year between the promise and now, which is everything, because I keep a notebook.

In my notebook it says:

- **June:** Hazel tore up the landlord's paper in front of me. I helped. I got to hold one corner while it ripped. It sounded like a snowstorm.
- **July:** Grandma came to dinner without being asked twice. She brought a lemon cake and she put it on the counter next to Theo's bread and she said, "Your crust is better than mine," which Theo says is the nicest thing anyone in this town has ever said to him, including Hazel.
- **August:** Theo stayed the night. Then the next night. Then I stopped counting, and then I noticed nobody else was counting either, and that's when I knew it was real. Real things don't get counted. Counting is what you do when you're scared something will run out.
- **September:** School started. Theo walked me the first day and Hazel walked me home and both of them waved from opposite ends of the block like idiots.
- **October:** The lease got signed. The REAL lease. With Theo's name on it and Hazel's name on it, together, co-something-or-other, which means partners in paper as well as everything else. Grandma watched Hazel sign it and she cried a little and pretended it was about the pen.
- **November:** Daniel sent a card. Hazel read it, laughed once — a surprised kind of laugh, like a hiccup — and put it in the recycling. I fished it out later because I wanted to see. It was very boring. Some people are just boring, even when they're trying.
- **December:** We hung the promise frame. That was my job. I picked the spot above my bed where I can see it lying down, because promises work better if they can watch you back.
- **January through now:** Everything good, mostly. Some hard parts too, but the hard parts had people in them, and that's the difference.

That last line is my favorite thing I ever wrote. I'm saving it for something.

* * *

Tonight is closing night, which is my favorite kind of night, better than birthdays almost.

Closing the shop takes all three of us and follows a list that lives inside Theo's head instead of on paper, which drives Hazel crazy, but Theo says a list you write down is a list you can lose, same as doors. First Theo banks the ovens and wipes down the counters until they squeak. Then Hazel walks the shelves, straightening spines, finding the books that wandered into the wrong sections during the day — there's always a cookbook hiding in poetry, always, like it's embarrassed about being practical. Then I do the floor with the big push broom, which is technically against child labor laws, Hazel says, but only technically, because I insist.

Then we turn off the lights in order: bakery first, then the front of the shop, then the lamp over the register last, so the dark rolls toward us instead of chasing us.

And then we stand in the middle, all three, in the half-dark, and Theo says, "Locks?" and Hazel says, "Locked," and I say, "Broom's away," and that's it. That's the whole ceremony. It takes eleven seconds and I would fight anybody who tried to skip it.

Tonight, after the lights, Hazel says, "Hot chocolate?" and Theo says, "I'll make it properly, not from the powder," and I say, "With the real marshmallows, not the lies," and we go up the stairs to the apartment that used to be storage and is now rooms, mine with the framed promise, theirs with two toothbrushes that stopped being remarkable sometime around February.

From the kitchen I can hear them talking low, the way they talk when they think I'm not listening. I am always listening. This is called gathering intelligence and my friend Priya says it will be useful when I'm a spy or a journalist, whichever pays better.

"—board meeting went long," Hazel is saying. "Okafor wants to talk about the mural."

"The alley mural? The one with the kid reading to the dog?"

"She says the dog looks like it's judging everyone."

"It is. I painted it."

I come around the corner with three mugs before they remember to lower their voices, and they do the thing they always do, which is move apart about four inches and then move back together when they realize moving apart is ridiculous, because I've known about them since before they knew about themselves. I have a notebook. I have evidence.

"Spy," I say, handing out mugs.

"Journalist," Hazel corrects, which is what they always say, and which is its own kind of spy work if you ask me.

We sit on the couch, Theo in the middle because he's warm like an oven even when the ovens are off, and outside the window the street does its nighttime sounds — the bus sighing at the corner, someone's dog making its opinion known, the clock tower doing its slow count of hours that nobody needs counted anymore.

"Story hour tomorrow," Theo says. "Denny's bringing his niece. She's two and she bites books."

"A connoisseur," Hazel says.

"I'll set out the board books. Sacrificial literature."

I sip my hot chocolate and look at them over the rim, the marshmallow mustache Theo has already earned, the way Hazel tucks their feet under them like a bird on a branch, and I think about the promise on my wall and how it doesn't say anything about staying. Everybody thinks that's what it says — *nobody leaves* — but that's not it. It says goodbye. It says: whatever happens, however far anybody goes, we say it out loud, to each other's faces, with the lights still on.

Because the worst thing isn't leaving. I figured that out when I was eight, sitting on the stairs listening to Hazel rehearse a lie about meeting Theo at a farmers market. The worst thing is disappearing mid-sentence. The worst thing is a person becoming a closed door.

Nobody here closes doors anymore. We cut openings in walls instead.

* * *

Later, tucked in bed, I hear the last sounds of the house settling: Hazel's footsteps crossing to the bedroom, the creak of the floorboard by the bathroom that I know how to avoid and they never learn, Theo laughing at something — a real laugh, the kind that comes all the way up from his stomach, the kind he didn't have the first month I knew him, when his laughs were polite and small like samples at the bakery counter.

I look at the frame. In the dark I can't read it, but I don't need to. I wrote it. I know every letter, including the flour smudge.

Then I hear something new, and I sit up.

It's coming from downstairs — the shop, after locks, after lights, after the whole ceremony. A soft sound, like a page turning. Then another one.

I should wake somebody. That's what the rules say about noises in the night. But I'm nine and a half and I gather intelligence, so instead I pad down the stairs in my socks, past the opening in the wall where the bakery breathes its sleeping-bread smell into the dark, and I stop at the edge of the reading alcove.

Hazel is there. Just Hazel, in pajamas, sitting cross-legged on the floor in the moonlight from the front window, with a book open on their knees. Not reading it. Looking at it. And when I lean closer I see why they're not reading: it's a photo album, old-fashioned, with corner pockets, and the page open in front of them is a photograph of the three of us taken at the reunion — no, not that reunion, the one from last summer, the second one, the good one — me on Theo's shoulders, Hazel laughing with their head tipped back, Grandma in the background pretending not to smile and failing completely.

There are more pages behind it. A lot more. The album is thick.

"Hazel," I whisper.

They jump about a mile. Then they see it's me and put their hand on their chest. "You're supposed to be asleep."

"So are you." I sit down next to them. "What is it?"

Hazel looks at the album for a long moment. Their thumb moves along the edge of the page, back and forth, the way it does when they're deciding whether to tell the truth. I wait. I'm good at waiting. It's the family skill.

"It was your grandma's idea," Hazel finally says. "She started it. She said—" and here Hazel's voice does something funny, goes soft and rough at the same time, "—she said we spent too many years acting like remembering was dangerous. So. We remember now. On purpose."

I turn the page. There's the bakery, the morning the opening in the wall got finished, Denny in the background eating a roll. There's the zoning board room, blurry, Mrs. Okafor mid-pancake-flip of her vote. There's me, asleep on Theo's shoulder at the first reunion, drooling slightly, which I have decided to forgive.

Page after page. All of us. All of it kept.

"You're not dangerous," I tell Hazel, because it seems like the thing they need to hear, even though I don't fully understand why. "Neither is he. Neither is any of it."

Hazel puts their arm around me and pulls me in, and we sit there in the moonlight among the unsold stock and the sleeping ovens, looking at pictures of ourselves being happy on purpose, and after a while Hazel says, quietly, like a promise being renewed:

"No. We never were."

Upstairs, the floorboard creaks — Theo, awake, coming to find us, because in this house nobody disappears mid-sentence, not even at midnight, not even for a minute. His footsteps come down the stairs and pause when he sees us, and then he doesn't ask questions, he just sits down on my other side, so I'm in the middle, warm, held on both sides, and the album opens to a blank page near the end.

"Room left," Theo says, tapping the empty pockets.

"For what?" I ask.

Hazel and Theo look at each other over my head, the way they do, having a whole conversation in four inches of air.

"Everything true," Hazel says. "However much fits."

Outside, far away, a train sounds its horn at the edge of town, going somewhere, carrying people, running on schedule — and none of us even look up.
