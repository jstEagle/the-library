## 9. The Songbook

The box had lived on the top shelf of my closet for six years, which was four years longer than it had lived anywhere else. Before the closet it had been under my bed in the apartment on Delmore Street, and before that it had been in the trunk of my car for eleven months, because eleven months was how long it took me to be able to carry it from my mother's house to somewhere with a roof I paid for myself.

It was a shoebox, technically. Or it had started life as one — a wide brown thing that had once held men's oxfords, size ten and a half, though my mother wore a seven. The label on the end read MARSH in her handwriting, blocky and sure, the way she wrote everything, as if the alphabet owed her money. Inside was not shoes. Inside was the songbook.

I want to say I thought about it every day. That would be more romantic, and this whole arrangement of ours was supposed to run on romance, the fake kind, the kind you can invoice. But the truth is I thought about it the way you think about a tooth that needs filling: constantly in the sense that it was always there, and never in the sense that I ever wanted to look at it directly.

So when Mara Pemberton stood in my kitchen on a Tuesday evening holding two mugs of tea I hadn't offered to make and didn't need but drank anyway, and said, "So. Terms and conditions," I heard myself say something I had not planned to say.

"Come upstairs."

* * *

Here's what you should understand about the deal we'd struck, because I've had six chapters' worth of people ask me — Priya mostly, over text messages she thought were subtle — why a man would agree to be a fake boyfriend for a woman he'd known for the length of one broken violin and three Sundays of cataloguing.

The answer is: I wasn't agreeing to be her fake boyfriend. I was agreeing to have a reason to open the box.

I hadn't told her that. What I'd told her, three weeks ago, standing in the doorway between our shops with flour — no, sawdust; I don't bake, that's a different neighbor — with sawdust on my sleeves and my heart going like a cheap metronome, was: "Not money. You help me catalogue my mother's songbook. Every page. And I'll be your date."

She'd blinked at me. "A songbook?"

"She wrote music. She never published any of it. It's all handwritten." I had kept my voice very level, the way you keep your voice level when you're walking across ice that has already cracked once. "I can't read it alone."

Mara, to her enormous credit, did not do the face people do when they hear something like that — the softening, the head-tilt, the gentle *oh, Theo* that makes you want to crawl into the soundhole of a cello and live there. She just nodded once, businesslike, and said, "Then we'll need better coffee than whatever you're drinking," and shook my hand, and that was that.

Which is how, on a Tuesday in October, I ended up leading the woman I was pretending to date up the stairs of my own house to show her the one thing in the world I actually couldn't bear.

* * *

The stairs creaked in a pattern Lily had once memorized and performed for me like a drum solo, so I knew exactly where we were — fourth step, seventh step, landing — without looking back. Mara carried her tea with both hands, careful, the way she handled everything that belonged to other people. She'd taken off her boots at the door without being asked. She noticed things. That was the trouble with her. That was, I was beginning to suspect, going to be the entire trouble with everything.

"My room's through there," I said, pointing, then immediately regretted the sentence's entire architecture. "The spare room. My room is also through there, in the sense that it's a room and I go through it. What I mean is—"

"Theo." She was smiling into her mug. "Breathe."

"I know where my own rooms are," I muttered, and opened the closet.

The box was where it always was, behind the winter sweaters I never wore because this town has maybe nine days that require wool. I took it down with both hands. It weighed almost nothing. It had always weighed almost nothing. That was the part nobody warned you about grief — they tell you it's heavy, and it is, but the object itself, the actual physical remains of a person, weighs less than a bag of groceries. My mother fit in a shoebox. Sixty-one years of her, and the part she'd left behind in ink weighed less than the sweater I moved aside to reach it.

Mara had gone quiet behind me. Not awkward-quiet. Witness-quiet. There's a difference, and I felt it land between my shoulder blades like a warm hand.

We sat on the floor of the spare room, backs against the bed, because chairs felt too formal and the floor felt honest. I set the box between us and did not open it. For a while neither of us spoke. Downstairs, the shop clock ticked. Somewhere outside, Mrs. Okafor's dog registered its ongoing objections to the concept of squirrels.

"You don't have to narrate," Mara said finally. "In case you're worried I'm waiting for context."

"I'm always worried you're waiting for context."

"Well, stop. I'm a professional. I catalogue things for a living. You'd be amazed what people hand me in boxes." She tucked her legs under herself. "Last month a man brought me his father's entire library and said, 'I don't know what any of this meant to him, but it must have meant something.' That's the job. Meaning something. You don't have to explain it first."

I looked at her. She was watching the box, not me, giving me the privacy of an unobserved face, and I understood in that moment that she had done this before — sat on floors with people and their dead — or if she hadn't, she'd sat on enough floors with her own dead to know the choreography.

"Her name was Eleanor," I said. "Everyone called her Nell. She wrote songs the way some people doodle. Margins of grocery lists. Backs of envelopes. Napkins, once, at my brother's graduation dinner, which caused a scene with the waiter." I smiled despite myself. "She taught piano out of the front room until I was twelve. Then she got sick the first time, and after that she wrote instead of teaching, and after the second time—" I stopped. Restarted. "After the second time, the writing got faster. Like she was racing something. She finished forty-one songs in the last two years. I counted them once at three in the morning and then never counted them again."

"Forty-one," Mara repeated softly.

"Forty-two, if you count the one that's just a title and eight bars. 'For When You're Ready.' That's all it says. Title, eight bars, and then nothing." I turned the box over in my hands. "I've read the title about four hundred times. I've never managed page two."

"And tonight you're going to."

"Tonight *we're* going to. That was the deal. You read. I—" I gestured vaguely at my own chest. "I provide emotional ballast."

"Ballast," she said. "Is that what I am to you? Ballast?"

"It's a nautical term. It means the heavy thing at the bottom that keeps the boat upright."

"Mm." She set down her tea. "And here I thought I was the fake girlfriend."

"You're both. It's a competitive market."

That got a laugh out of her, quick and low, and the laugh did something to the air in the room, loosened it, and I thought — *now, do it now, before you think again* — and I lifted the lid off the box.

* * *

The smell hit me first. It always does. Paper and pencil shavings and a faint ghost of the lavender lotion she used, the kind from the drugstore, the kind she bought in bulk because she went through it rubbing her hands after playing. Six years sealed in cardboard and the smell was still there, patient as anything, waiting for me like it had all the time in the world, which, for a smell, I suppose it did.

"Oh," Mara breathed.

The top layer was loose sheets — staff paper in uneven stacks, some photocopied, some torn from a spiral notebook, corners soft with handling. Beneath those, a proper binder, green, one of the ones with the ring mechanisms that ate paper, stuffed past capacity. On the spine, in masking tape gone amber with age: **THE BOOK**. Her name for it. She'd called it that the way other people call their cars by names — *don't touch the Book, Theo, the Book stays open on the stand.*

Mara reached out, then paused, her fingers hovering. "May I?"

"It's yours to read. That's the arrangement."

She lifted the loose sheets as carefully as if they were made of frost. I watched her hands — small, quick, ink-stained at the side from the day's receipts — and I watched her read the first page, and I watched her face do the thing faces do when they meet my mother through her handwriting: a startle, then a softening, then a smile that arrives slightly too late to hide itself.

"She numbered them," Mara said. "All of them."

"In order of composition. She was very firm about it. If you played number nineteen before number eighteen at Christmas she'd make you go back and do it properly. She said they were conversations and you don't walk into a conversation mid-sentence."

"That's—" Mara stopped, laughed a little, shook her head. "That's exactly what you do. With the inventory. In your shop. You told me the violins are shelved chronologically by acquisition."

"They're conversations too."

"Uh-huh." She was grinning now, and it should have been annoying, being seen like that, mapped onto my mother like that, but instead it felt like being introduced. Like my mother, six years gone, reaching through a shoebox to tap me on the shoulder and say *this one, Theo. Look at this one.*

"Read one," I said. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Out loud. I've only ever read them silently. I think — I think they're meant to be in the air. Songs. That's the whole point of a song, it doesn't count until someone hears it."

Mara looked at me for a long moment. Whatever she saw in my face made her nod slowly, and she smoothed the first sheet flat on her knee, and she began.

"'Blue Hour,'" she read. "Number one. *Lyrics and melody, E. Marsh, written on the porch, June.*" She squinted. "There's a note here in the margin — it says, '*Theo helped with the bridge. He was nine. He was right.*'"

"I remember that," I said, and my chest did something complicated. "She'd been stuck for a week. I said the second chord should go down instead of up. She looked at me like I'd handed her a winning lottery ticket."

"Of course she did." Mara cleared her throat gently and read on:

> *"The day goes blue the way a bruise goes gold,*
> *slowly, and then all at once,*
> *and I sit on the steps with the day getting old*
> *and practice the art of the long exhale.*
> *Nothing is wrong. Nothing is wrong.*
> *That's the strange part — nothing is wrong,*
> *and still the light leaves like it's leaving home,*
> *and still I wave, and still I hold on."*

The room was very quiet when she finished. Even the dog downstairs seemed to have resolved its squirrel situation.

"She wrote that the summer before she got sick the first time," I said. "I used to think it was about the weather."

"Do you still?"

"No," I said. "I think it was about practicing. For later. For all the blue hours that came after." I picked up my tea and found it cold and drank it anyway. "Next one."

* * *

We worked through numbers two through nine that way, Mara reading, me supplying footnotes — *that one's about her sister Ruth, that one she wrote the week I broke my arm, that one the church asked her to tone down and she refused, obviously* — and it was going well, it was going almost pleasantly, the ice holding, the words staying in the safe shallow water of anecdote. Number ten was a lullaby she'd written for me. Number eleven was funny; Mara laughed at the right places, and my mother's humor landed in a new mouth and survived the trip, which undid me a little, quietly, in a way I filed away to feel later.

Number twelve was titled "The Long Way Home."

I knew it was coming. I could see it in the stack — I'd seen the title a hundred times over six years, fanned at the edge of the pile like a card I refused to draw. And I'd decided, somewhere in the last twenty minutes, in the warm fog of the blue hour and the lullaby, that I was fine. That I was ballasted. That tonight was the night the boat held.

Mara smoothed the page. "'The Long Way Home,'" she read. "*E. Marsh, March.*" And then, because she was reading faithfully, because she didn't know, because there was no possible way she could know —

> *"Take the long way home, my darling,*
> *take the road along the shore,*
> *and if you're driving after midnight,*
> *roll the window down for me.*
> *I'll be in the wind, I'll be in the pines,*
> *I'll be every mile marker sign —*
> *so take the long way home, my darling,*
> *there's no hurry coming back to me.*

> *And when the road bends toward the river,*
> *and the headlights sweep the rain,*
> *keep both hands upon the wheel, my love,*
> *and count the bridges as they pass —*
> *one for sorrow, two for mending,*
> *three for all the roads not ending,*
> *four for coming home at last,*
> *coming home at last."*

She read it beautifully. That was the problem. She read it the way it deserved, with the melody implied in her cadence, with the tenderness intact, and I could hear my mother singing it, I could hear her humming it in the kitchen with her back to me, and I could hear — underneath it, underneath everything — the other thing, the true thing, the thing the song was really about, the thing I had never said out loud to anyone, not to Priya-not-my-sister, not to the grief counselor the label recommended, not to God, whom I mostly addressed these days in the form of complaints about shipping costs for instrument parts.

*Keep both hands upon the wheel, my love.*

March. She died in April. She wrote that song in March, five weeks before the road, five weeks before the rain, five weeks before my hands — one of them, one hand, for four seconds, four seconds reaching for a phone that lit up on the passenger seat — left the wheel.

"—Theo?" Mara's voice, from far away. "Hey. Hey, look at me."

I couldn't. Looking at her meant being in the room, and the room had become unbearable, it had become full of my mother and full of the song and full of the specific mercy in Mara Pemberton's face, and I had learned six years ago that mercy was the thing that finished me off. Anger I could carry. Pity I could dodge. But kindness, freely given, with no transaction attached — that went straight through every defense I owned.

"I'm fine," I said, which was the traditional lie, the ancestral lie, handed down through generations of Marsh men.

"You're not."

"I need—" I stood up too fast and the tea slopped over my knuckles and I didn't care. "I need a minute. I'm sorry. Keep — you can keep reading, I'll be right—"

"Theo—"

But I was already through the door, down the hall, into the bathroom, where I locked the lock out of pure animal habit and put both hands on the sink and looked at myself in the mirror, thirty-four years old, eyes wet, jaw shaking, a grown man undone by a piece of loose-leaf paper.

*Keep both hands upon the wheel.*

She'd known. That was the thing I could never get past, the reef I wrecked on every time. Some part of her had known — not the accident, not specifically, but the shape of it, the way mothers know — and she'd spent her last good month writing her son a song about keeping his hands on the wheel, and he hadn't listened, and she'd died anyway, and the song had been sitting in a shoebox ever since like a letter returned to sender, marked *undeliverable*, marked *he can't bear it yet*.

I turned on the tap and let cold water run over my wrists, which is a trick she taught me, actually, for panic — *cold on the pulse points, Theo, it tells your body the emergency is over* — and I stood there dripping and breathing and hating the mirror, and after a while — I don't know how long, five minutes, ten — there was a knock. Soft. Two knuckles, once.

"I'm not going to come in," Mara said through the door. Her voice was steady and unhurried, no pity in it, which I loved her for and would deny loving her for. "I just want to say two things and then I'll shut up. One: I put the song back in the exact order. Numbered. I checked twice."

A breath.

"Two: she wrote *four for coming home at last*. Theo. She wrote the ending first. Whatever happened after — and I don't know what happened after, and I'm not asking, that's rule two — she already wrote you the ending. Coming home at last. That's in her handwriting. It counts."

I pressed my forehead against the cool of the mirror and closed my eyes.

"I'll be out in a minute," I said, and my voice only broke a little.

"Take two minutes," she said. "I'll make more tea. Yours is a crime scene." A pause, and then, gentler, almost to herself, almost not meant for me: "God, she was good. Your mom. She was *really* good."

Her footsteps went down the hall, light on the stairs, and I stood in my bathroom with my mother's cold-water trick running over my wrists and understood, with the slow dread of a man watching a tide come in, that the deal was supposed to protect us both — her heart, my grief, everything fenced and labeled and paid for in full.

And that somewhere between number one and number twelve, without either of us signing anything, the arrangement had stopped being fake — and I had no idea what I was going to do about it.

Downstairs, I heard her filling the kettle, and humming.

It took me a moment to place the tune. Then I did, and my whole chest went tight and bright at once.

Number one. "Blue Hour." *He was nine. He was right.*

She was learning my mother's songs by heart, and she didn't even know yet what she was carrying up those stairs — and neither, God help me, did I.
