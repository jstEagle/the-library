## 19. The First Kiss

### Mara

The caseworker's car was a gray hatchback that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated both cars and people, and it sat outside the bookshop for forty minutes while Ms. Okafor drank tea Mara didn't remember offering her and asked questions Mara had rehearsed answers to for three weeks.

Lily was at Priya's — a strategic deployment Mara had planned like a military operation. The shop was clean in the way shops are clean when you've scrubbed things that were never dirty. The back room, where Theo had been teaching Lily chords on Saturday mornings, smelled faintly of lemon polish and guitar strings. On the little table by the window, someone — Theo, she suspected, though he'd never admit it — had arranged three children's instruments in a careful row: a half-size ukulele, a tambourine, and a recorder that Lily had declared "a weapon" and refused to touch.

Ms. Okafor looked at all of it. She looked at the reading nook Lily had claimed as her own, with its crooked cushions and its shelf of dog-eared paperbacks. She looked at the photograph wall above the register — Lily at four, missing a front tooth; Lily at seven, holding up a fish she'd caught and immediately released; Lily last spring, mid-laugh at the kitchen table, blurred because she'd never once been still in her life.

Then she wrote something in her notebook, and Mara's heart performed a maneuver that should have required a permit.

"You've built something here," Ms. Okafor said, capping her pen. "It's not fancy. But it's real, and it's hers." She stood, buttoning her coat. "I'll be honest with you, Ms. Pemberton. I visit a lot of homes. Most of them are bigger than this one. Very few of them feel more like one."

Mara walked her to the door on legs made of borrowed scaffolding. She said thank you. She said something about calling if there was anything else needed. She smiled the way you smile when you're holding a full cup of very hot tea and someone tells a joke.

And then the door closed, and the gray hatchback pulled away, and Mara stood in the middle of her own bookshop and discovered that her knees had resigned without notice.

She gripped the counter. She breathed. Somewhere upstairs, the radiator knocked twice, the way it always did, and the familiarity of it nearly undid her.

*It went well.* Three words, and she wanted to shout them at the ceiling, call Priya, run into the street. Instead she did what she always did: she reached for the phone to tell the one person who would understand exactly what this meant, who had been there through every lawyer's letter and every 2 a.m. spiral, who had offered — God help her — to be her stability on paper, and who had somehow become so much more than paper that she'd stopped noticing where the arrangement ended and the truth began.

He answered on the second ring, which meant he'd been waiting, which meant he'd been as nervous as she was, which meant—

"Well?" Theo said.

"It went well."

There was a pause, and then a sound she'd never heard from him before — a whoop, unguarded and slightly off-key, followed by what she strongly suspected was him knocking something over in his shop. "Sorry," he said. "That was a chair. The chair is fine. I'm fine. That's — Mara, that's *wonderful*."

"I know." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she laughed at herself, wet-eyed behind the register at ten forty on a Tuesday morning. "I keep waiting to feel relieved and instead I just feel — I don't know. Like I've been holding my breath since September and someone finally told me I'm allowed to exhale."

"So exhale."

"I'm working on it."

"Come over," he said, and then, quickly, "for coffee. To celebrate. Properly. I have that terrible cinnamon bread you pretend not to like."

"I genuinely do not like it."

"You eat entire slices of it."

"That's called politeness."

"Mara." His voice did the thing it did sometimes, dropped down into something warm and certain, a low note held just for her. "Come celebrate."

She looked around the shop — at the reading nook, the photo wall, the row of small instruments catching the morning light — and for once in her carefully managed life, she didn't calculate anything first.

"On my way," she said.

* * *

### Theo

He'd bought the cinnamon bread on purpose, obviously. He'd also, in the twenty minutes between her call and her arrival, straightened his workbench, hidden the invoice pile under a drop cloth, and changed his shirt, which was ridiculous, because she'd seen him covered in sawdust and tuning-grease approximately nine hundred times. But today wasn't a sawdust day. Today felt like it might be a different kind of day entirely, and Theo Marsh, who did not believe in jinxes but absolutely believed in preparation, had put on the blue shirt.

She arrived still in her work clothes, hair escaping its clip, carrying a bottle of cider she'd grabbed from the corner shop "because coffee felt insufficient," and when he opened the door she was smiling — really smiling, the unguarded version, the one that rearranged her whole face — and something in his chest turned over like an engine catching.

"To Lily's caseworker," she said, raising the bottle, "who has excellent taste in bookshops."

"To Ms. Okafor," he agreed, "patron saint of good news."

They drank cider out of mismatched mugs among the half-repaired instruments, sitting on stools like teenagers, and she told him all of it — the notebook, the photograph wall, the thing about houses feeling like homes — and he listened the way he always listened to her, fully, leaning in, and somewhere in the middle of her impression of Ms. Okafor saying *"it's real, and it's hers"* (which was, frankly, devastatingly accurate), Theo realized he was in trouble.

Not new trouble. Old trouble, finally admitting itself. The kind of trouble that had been building since a broken violin came through his door with a determined eight-year-old attached to it, and that had gotten worse every Sunday since, every shared songbook page, every time she laughed at his jokes like they were actually funny, which they mostly weren't.

She finished her story. She was glowing. And the relief coming off her was so bright and so earned that he thought, briefly, of doing nothing — of pouring more cider and keeping the blue shirt a secret forever.

But she looked at him then, really looked, and whatever was on his face must have shown, because her smile faltered into something softer and more dangerous.

"What?" she said.

"Nothing," he said. "I'm just glad. About the home study. About—" He gestured vaguely at everything, which was cowardly, and she knew it, and he knew she knew it.

"Theo."

"Mm?"

"Why are you looking at me like that?"

And here is the thing about relief: it dissolves things. Rules, especially. All those lines they'd drawn over coffee months ago — *no real feelings, no past questions* — written in her looping handwriting on the back of a receipt, taped inside his cabinet like a contract. Relief dissolved ink. Relief dissolved sense. Relief, in that moment, in that room full of wood shavings and cider and her, dissolved everything except the fact that she was right there, and that he had wanted to kiss her since approximately the Mesozoic era.

"I think," Theo said carefully, "that I'm going to do something, and I'd like you to stop me if—"

She didn't stop him.

Later, neither of them could say who closed the distance. It didn't matter. What mattered was that her mug got set down on the workbench with a clack, and his hand found the side of her face, and the kiss was soft and brief and utterly annihilating — the kind of kiss that makes every previous kiss in your life retroactively look like rehearsal.

When they broke apart, she kept her eyes closed for a second longer than necessary. He watched her eyelashes and thought, distantly, *oh no.*

"Oh," Mara said.

"Yeah," Theo said.

"That was—"

"Yes."

"A violation of Rule One," she said, and her voice had gone bright and brittle, the way voices do when they're holding up a ceiling. "Technically. Rule One was no real feelings. I'm not sure a kiss counts as feelings, legally speaking, but a reasonable person could—"

"Mara."

"—interpret it that way. And we agreed. We *wrote it down*. There's a receipt involved. A contractual receipt—"

"Mara." He caught her hand, gently, and she stopped, breathing fast, staring at their fingers like they'd betrayed her personally. "Hey. Breathe. We can — we can call it a celebration. People kiss when they're relieved. It's practically medical."

"Is it."

"Historically documented."

She laughed — a short, startled sound — and then pressed her free hand over her eyes. "God. I'm spiraling. You can see me spiraling. This is me, spiraling in front of you."

"For the record," Theo said, "I don't want to take it back."

Her hand lowered slowly. She looked at him, and there it was again — that war behind her eyes, hope and fear fighting over the same territory, and he hated that he knew her well enough to see it and couldn't do anything about it.

"I don't either," she admitted, barely above a whisper. "That's the problem."

* * *

### Mara

The problem, as she explained it to herself on the walk back next door — a walk of eleven steps that she stretched into a full minute of loitering by the lamppost like a woman waiting for a bus that would never come — was arithmetic.

Daniel was still out there. The hearing hadn't happened yet; the home study was one battle, not the war. If she let herself want Theo — really want him, not fake-date him, not paper-stabilize her household with him — then she was handing the universe another thing to take. And the universe had form. The universe had taken Daniel, and her twenties, and her ability to trust her own judgment, and it had done it all while she stood there holding a baby and a set of house keys, wondering how she'd let it happen.

You don't reach for the good thing while you're falling. Everyone knows that. You hold onto the railing until your feet are under you, and only then—

Only then what? Only then did she get to be happy? Who had signed off on that schedule?

She went back into the shop. Lily's reading nook sat in its corner, cushions askew, a library book open face-down on the armrest. Above the register, the photo wall. *It's real, and it's hers,* Ms. Okafor had said, and she'd been talking about the shop, but standing there Mara understood she'd been talking about more than the shop.

Her phone buzzed. Theo:

**For the record, the blue shirt was for you.**

Mara stared at it. Laughed despite herself, one hand over her mouth in her empty shop. Then typed, deleted, typed again:

**Rule One is suspended pending review.**

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared.

**Pending review by whom?**

She looked at the message for a long time. Outside, October light moved across the sidewalk; somewhere down the block the bakery bell rang; the world continued being the world, indifferent and generous at once.

**By me,** she sent. **Give me a few days. Don't be weird about it.**

**I am constitutionally incapable of not being weird about it. But okay. A few days.**

And then, a minute later, before she could put the phone down:

**Mara. For what it's worth — nothing about today felt like pretending.**

She read it twice. She put the phone face-down on the counter, the way you do with a letter that's too true to answer yet, and stood in the middle of her shop surrounded by books and light and the ghost of a kiss, and told herself: *a few days. Just a few days to get your feet under you.*

Which was when the bell over the door rang, and Lily came barreling in from Priya's, backpack first, shouting "AUNT PRIYA SAYS DID IT GO OKAY AND ALSO CAN WE GET PIZZA," and Mara caught her daughter mid-flight and held on, and over Lily's shoulder her eyes found the window — and Theo, next door, watching through his own glass with an expression she could read across eleven feet and an entire wall of unresolved rules.

He raised his mug to her. She raised Lily, who waved wildly, and Theo waved back, and Mara stood there in the doorway of her own life thinking: *a few days. I just need a few days.*

She had no way of knowing that Nashville was already three days away.
