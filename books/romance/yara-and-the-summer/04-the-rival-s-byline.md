## 4. The Rival's Byline

Elias Marchetti had learned to read people the way he read weather—by pressure, by the weight of the air before a storm broke. It was a skill his father had drilled into him over thirty harvests: *watch the sky, boy, not the almanac.* The almanac lied. The sky never did.

Ulyana Reyes was a clear morning with frost underneath it.

He'd noticed it the second evening, when she'd asked him about the year he'd spent in Tuscany and her pen had stopped moving—not paused, *stopped*, as if the question had cost her something to ask. He'd noticed it again when she'd flinched, barely, at the word *abandoned*, though he'd been talking about a vineyard parcel lost to phylloxera. And he noticed it now, on the fifth night of interviews, when he set down his glass of the '19 Sangiovese and said, without planning to, "There's something I should tell you before we go any further with this book."

The guest house study was warm, lamplit, smelling of pencil shavings and the faint mineral breath of old wine. Ulyana sat across from him with her recorder between them like a small black altar. She went very still—the stillness of prey, or of someone listening for a sound they already dreaded.

"I didn't agree to write this memoir for posterity," Elias said. "Or for legacy, or any of the other words my editor uses when she wants me to feel important. I'm writing it because my story was taken from me."

"Taken," she repeated. Her voice was level. Her hands were not; she folded them in her lap like closing a book.

"You've heard of *A. Wren*."

Something crossed her face then—fast, gone before he could name it. "Everyone's heard of A. Wren. *The Vintner's Daughter* sold two million copies."

"*Three* million," Elias said, and heard the bitterness in his own voice, raw as a fresh-cut vine. "Three million copies of my life, told by someone who was never in it. Or—" he laughed, short and joyless, "—who was in it, and left, and decided to make money off the leaving."

Ulyana did not move. The lamp hummed. Somewhere outside, the irrigation pumps cycled down for the night, and the silence that followed had the texture of held breath.

"The novel," she said carefully. "It's fiction. It says so."

"It's fiction the way a map is fiction." He stood, because sitting still had become impossible, and walked to the window where the vineyard lay silvered under a rising moon. Row after row of vines, each one trained along wires his grandfather had strung, each one carrying fruit that would become this year's wine—this year's story, pressed into glass. "Change the names, soften the geography, call the winery something else—and every reader in the world knows exactly whose heartbreak they're reading. My mother's illness. My father's bankruptcy. The girl who came here one summer and vanished before the first frost." He turned back to face her. "A. Wren wrote my grief so beautifully that strangers weep over it in airports. And I couldn't even claim it. Do you know what that's like? To watch your own life become someone else's masterpiece?"

He expected sympathy. That was what ghostwriters traded in, after all—a professional tenderness, an ear for pain. What he saw instead frightened him more than indifference could have. Ulyana's face had gone white beneath its olive warmth, and her eyes—those dark, watchful eyes he'd begun, dangerously, to look forward to—were fixed on him with an expression he could only describe as *recognition*. As if he were describing her wound, not his own.

"Elias," she said quietly. "I'm sorry. That's—that's an unforgivable thing, what was done to you."

"It gets worse." He came back to his chair but didn't sit. "My editor believes Wren plagiarized. Not just borrowed—*plagiarized*. There are passages in that book, scenes, details, that were never public. Things only someone who'd been inside this house, inside this family, could have known. She thinks Wren stole letters. Journals. Something." He dragged a hand through his hair. "And maybe she's right. Maybe the woman I loved kept copies of everything I ever gave her and sold them to a publisher while I was still grieving her disappearance. Eleven years, and I find out my summer wasn't even mine to keep."

The recorder's red light glowed between them like a small accusing eye.

"So that's why you're really here," Ulyana said. "Not a memoir. An investigation."

"A reclamation." He met her eyes. "I want my name on my own story. And I want the world to know who A. Wren is—not to destroy her, whatever my editor thinks. But because a person shouldn't be able to live off someone else's heart and stay invisible forever." He paused, and something gentler moved through him, unwelcome as rain during harvest. "You of all people understand that. Invisibility. You've built a whole career on it, haven't you? Writing other people's truths so you never have to risk your own."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—he thought she might cry. Her jaw tightened; her throat worked. Then she smiled, and it was the most convincing lie he had ever witnessed, which was itself a kind of confession.

"Someone has to hold the pen," she said. "It doesn't have to be the same person who lived the story."

"No," Elias agreed softly. "But it helps if they're honest."

She gathered her recorder, her notes, her careful composure, and wished him goodnight in a voice that only shook once. He watched her cross the courtyard toward the guest house, her silhouette thin against the moonlit rows, and felt the strangest sensation—as if he'd just shown a scar to someone and watched them bleed from it.

* * *

Ulyana did not sleep.

She sat on the narrow bed in the guest house with her laptop open and *The Vintner's Daughter* glowing on the screen—her book, her byline-less book, the work that had made her reputation and her fortune under the pseudonym the whole industry whispered about. Three million copies. Translation deals in nineteen languages. A film option she'd never signed, because signing meant exposure, and exposure meant the end of A. Wren, and the end of A. Wren meant the end of the only self she'd trusted in eleven years.

She had written it in six months of fevered grief, holed up in a rented flat in Lisbon, turning their summer into architecture: every conversation rebuilt, every touch transposed into prose so luminous that reviewers called it "devastatingly true." She had told herself it was tribute. She had told herself he'd never know. She had told herself a great many things, and the telling had worked right up until tonight, until Elias Marchetti stood in front of her with his father's vineyard at his back and described her betrayal in his own voice—*the girl who came here one summer and vanished before the first frost*—and she had felt the sentence go through her like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

*Plagiarized,* his editor believed. Stolen letters. Stolen journals.

No. Worse. She hadn't stolen anything. She had *been* the source. Every detail in that novel was hers because it was theirs—his mother's hands in the soil, the argument in the cellar the night before she left, the letter she'd written and slipped under the door of the estate office and never confirmed arrived. The letter. God, the letter—she'd found it three days ago in the cellar catalog, water-stained, unopened, postmarked eleven years too late, and she had hidden it in her room like contraband and told herself she'd decide what to do with it later.

Later was running out of road.

She thought about her career the way she imagined a gambler thought about his last chip. Invisibility had saved her. That was the truth no one wanted spoken aloud: anonymity wasn't cowardice, it was survival. She'd come up through publishing watching talented women get chewed into content, their faces attached to their failures, their private lives strip-mined for publicity. A. Wren had been armor. A. Wren had let the work speak and kept the woman safe. And the work had been *good*—she refused to kneel before the altar of guilt and pretend otherwise. She had made something beautiful out of the worst summer of her life, and millions of readers had found their own losses inside it. Wasn't that worth something? Wasn't that, in its crooked way, a kind of honesty?

But Elias wanted his name back. And his editor wanted a scalp. And Ulyana Dravenmoor was sitting in the guest house of the man she'd wronged, being paid to write his truth while concealing hers, holding an undelivered letter that proved the whole architecture of her double life was founded on a message that had never arrived.

If he'd read it, eleven years ago—she pressed her palms against her eyes. If it had reached him, would she have come back? Would there have been no Lisbon, no pseudonym, no novel, no wound? One envelope. One stamp. The entire divergence of two lives, resting on a piece of paper that had slid behind a cabinet in a cellar and stayed there through eleven harvests.

Her phone buzzed. An email from her agent, subject line: *Wren 2.0 — publishers asking.* They wanted a follow-up. Of course they did. The machine she'd built demanded feeding; the invisible woman was only valuable as long as she stayed invisible and kept producing.

She looked at the email. She looked at the letter, wrapped in cloth in the bottom of her suitcase. She looked at the dark window, where the vineyard waited under the moon like a held breath.

Then she opened a blank document and began, mechanically, professionally, to draft tomorrow's interview questions—questions about his mother, his grief, his lost girl—and her hands shook so badly she had to stop after the third one.

Because she knew now what she'd only suspected since the first night: that she wasn't writing Elias Marchetti's memoir. She was sitting across from the man she'd turned into fiction, helping him hunt the author who'd done it, and every honest answer she gave him was another brick in the wall that would eventually seal her inside.

Tomorrow he would ask her, trusting her, to help him find proof against A. Wren.

And she would have to decide whether loyalty to the life she'd built was worth more than the truth—or whether it had ever been worth anything at all.
