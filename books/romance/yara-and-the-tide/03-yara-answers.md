## 3. Yara Answers

The brochure had arrived, as most dangerous things did at Voss & Daughters, in a manila envelope with no return address.

Yara Voss found it on a Tuesday morning, wedged between a galley proof of *The Salt Widow's Daughter* and a handwritten note from her father's nurse reminding her that he'd slept badly again and that the cardiologist wanted to move up his appointment. The envelope sat there among the wreckage of her desk—contracts flagged in yellow, a cold cup of tea she'd microwaved twice, three different drafts of a statement to the board—all of it the paper architecture of a life she was supposed to want.

She almost threw it away. That was the honest truth of it. She picked it up, felt the weight of it, registered the words **MERIDIAN LITERARY AGENCY — CORRESPONDENCE PROGRAM** embossed in gray on the front, and very nearly dropped it into the recycling bin beside her desk, where it could keep company with the rejected manuscripts and the expired parking validation stubs.

But then her father's voice drifted up through the floorboards of the townhouse—not literally, of course; Edmund Voss hadn't climbed a staircase unassisted since March—but figuratively, in the way his voice lived permanently inside her head now, narrating her choices like a particularly critical audiobook.

*You'll need to present a coherent public narrative, Yara. The board will want coherence.*

Coherence. As if she were a manuscript with structural problems. As if thirty-one years of being Yara Voss could be revised and resubmitted.

She opened the envelope instead.

Inside was a single sheet, heavy cream stock, printed in that deliberate old-fashioned serif that expensive institutions used when they wanted you to feel you were joining something exclusive rather than something strange.

*The Meridian Correspondence Program pairs writers and readers in anonymous exchange. No names. No meetings. No photographs. Letters are routed through our intermediaries and delivered monthly. Participants are asked only this: to write honestly to a stranger who cannot see them, judge them, or use what they learn against them.*

*Enclosed: your first assignment letter, written by your correspondent. Reply via the enclosed prepaid postmark within fourteen days to continue the exchange.*

There was a second sheet beneath it. Her correspondent's first letter.

Yara read it standing up, one hand braced on the edge of her desk, and by the third paragraph she was making a sound low in her throat—the sound she made when a submission was *almost* good, when a writer had talent but was hiding behind technique the way shy people hid behind furniture.

The letter began:

*Dear Correspondent,*

*I hope this letter finds you well. I'm told we're not to exchange personal details, so I'll confine myself to general observations: the weather here has been mild, the coffee adequate, and the pigeons outside my window engaged in their usual campaigns of petty extortion. I understand the purpose of this program is honest exchange, though I confess uncertainty about how much honesty is appropriate between strangers. I've drafted this letter four times. This is the version that seemed least likely to alarm anyone.*

*I work with words for a living, which means I spend my days making other people's thoughts sound like they came from somewhere more interesting than an office. In my spare time I walk, I read, and I maintain a small, doomed garden that the pigeons regard as a buffet.*

*I look forward to learning whatever you choose to share.*

*Sincerely,*
*Your Correspondent*

Yara set the letter down on her desk and stared at it for a long moment.

Then she laughed. Out loud, alone, in her office at nine-forty in the morning—a laugh that startled the intern passing in the hallway enough that she heard his footsteps quicken nervously past her door.

It wasn't a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of recognition. She knew this letter. She had *been* this letter. This was the letter of someone so practiced at being acceptable that they'd forgotten how to be anything else. Every sentence was a closed door painted to look like an open window. *The coffee adequate.* *Least likely to alarm anyone.* Four drafts, and the winner was the one designed to be forgotten by the time it reached the bottom of the page.

She'd received ten thousand letters like this. Query letters, cover letters, letters from authors apologizing for missed deadlines, letters from authors *not* apologizing for missed deadlines. The whole publishing industry ran on correspondence exactly this bland, this careful, this determinedly inoffensive—and Yara had built half her reputation on writing back, *No. Try again. Tell me what you actually mean.*

She pulled a legal pad toward her—she still wrote first drafts by hand, a habit her father called affectation and she called survival—and clicked her pen.

Because here was the thing nobody at Voss & Daughters knew, the thing she hadn't said aloud to another living soul: she had applied to the Meridian program herself, six weeks ago, at two in the morning, in the window seat of her apartment with a glass of wine and the particular despair that arrives when you realize every person in your life has a stake in who you're supposed to become.

Her mother wanted her fierce. The board wanted her docile. Crane—Uncle Aldous, who sent birthday cards with investment tips tucked inside them—wanted her grateful. Her father, who had built the whole edifice and was now watching it slip from his hands one atrium at a time, wanted her *ready*. Everyone wanted a different Yara, and the terrible arithmetic of it was that if you added up all the versions everyone wanted, there was nothing left over for the original.

So she'd filled out the form. *Anonymous correspondence,* the ad had said, buried in the back pages of a literary journal she read mostly for the obituaries of authors she'd once published. *Write honestly to someone who cannot see you.*

She hadn't told anyone. Not Priya, who knew everything else. Not even the pen itself, really, until now, holding this stranger's careful, bloodless letter in her hands and feeling—unexpectedly, absurdly—relieved.

Because a person who wrote four drafts to avoid alarming anyone was a person who would never, ever come looking for anything from her. No favors, no leverage, no angle. Just a pigeon-besieged garden and adequate coffee and a loneliness so well-mannered it had learned to introduce itself as contentment.

She knew that loneliness. She'd been having lunch with it for years.

* * *

She wrote her reply that night, after the board call—which had run ninety minutes over because Gerald from Finance had discovered a new way to say "we have concerns about readiness" without technically saying it—and after she'd checked on her father, who was propped up in bed reading proofs with a magnifying glass and pretending his hands didn't shake.

"How's the list looking?" he'd asked, meaning next season's titles, meaning the future, meaning *are you going to be able to hold all of this when I let go.*

"Strong," she'd said. "Two lead titles we can build a season around."

"That's my girl." And then, softer, with the terrible tenderness of a man handing over a weight: "You'll be fine, Yara. You just have to want it cleanly."

*Want it cleanly.* As if ambition were a kitchen surface you could wipe down. As if she hadn't spent her whole life wanting things in ways that left fingerprints.

So: the letter. She sat at her kitchen table with the legal pad and the stranger's four-drafted masterpiece in front of her, and she decided—deliberately, with the same instinct she brought to a promising but timid manuscript—that the kindest thing she could do for this person was refuse to let him hide.

*Dear Correspondent,*

*Your letter arrived today, and I want to begin by saying that I admire it enormously. It takes real discipline to write four drafts and choose the one with no pulse.*

*You told me the weather is mild, the coffee adequate, and the pigeons are extorting you. You told me you work with words, which in my experience covers everyone from poets to people who write cereal box copy, and that your garden is doomed. What you did not tell me is a single true thing about yourself, unless the pigeons count, and I suspect even they were included for charm rather than accuracy.*

*I don't say this to wound you. I say it as a professional courtesy, because I happen to know a great deal about letters, and yours is wearing a disguise. It's a good disguise—well-tailored, pleasant, forgettable in the way expensive furniture is forgettable—but I grew up in a house full of words, and I can always tell when a sentence is standing guard in front of something.*

*So here is my counteroffer. I'll go first.*

*My name doesn't matter—you're not allowed to have it, and frankly I've watched too many people use it against each other to miss it. But here is what's true: I am about to inherit something enormous that I'm not sure I want, from a man I love who never asked me whether I wanted it. There are people circling that inheritance the way your pigeons circle your garden, except mine wear better tailoring and their extortion comes with PowerPoint presentations. I joined this program because I wanted to say one true thing per month to someone who couldn't do anything with it. That's the whole reason. That's the entire sordid motive.*

*Now you. One true thing. It doesn't have to be large. Tell me why the garden matters to you when you already know it's lost—I find that people reveal themselves entirely in the answer to that question. Or tell me what the four discarded drafts of your letter said. I think about them more than I should.*

*Fail at this, and I'll understand. Succeed, and I promise not to be gentle about it.*

*Yours in merciless honesty,*
*Your Correspondent*

She read it over twice. The old reflex flared—*too much, too sharp, they'll withdraw, you've frightened them off*—the same reflex that had made her soften her notes to authors for years until her father caught her doing it and said, *Yara, you're editing yourself out of your own letters. If you respect someone, tell them the truth and let them decide what they're worth.*

She sealed the envelope anyway. Dropped it in the Meridian postbox on her walk to work the next morning, and spent the rest of the day waiting to regret it.

* * *

Regret did not arrive. Something else did instead: a lightness she distrusted immediately, the way she distrusted all good feelings before verifying their references.

Priya noticed. Of course Priya noticed; Priya had worked the front desk at Voss & Daughters for six years and had graduated from reception to rights management by virtue of being the only person in the building who could read Yara's moods faster than Yara could file them.

"You're humming," Priya said, appearing in her doorway Thursday afternoon with a stack of contracts and the expression of a woman preparing to open negotiations.

"I don't hum."

"You're humming right now. Something smug. It sounds like a hymn for people who've won arguments." She dropped the contracts on the desk. "What happened?"

"Nothing happened."

"Mm." Priya settled into the chair across from her, which was not a request. "Gerald from Finance says you smiled during the budget review. He used the word 'unsettling.' Two separate interns have reported that you complimented a submission. Unprompted. One of them cried in the stairwell."

"They were happy tears, presumably."

"There's no way to know. We don't have a baseline." Priya leaned forward. "Yara. What did you do?"

And here was the moment where Yara could have told her. Priya was her closest friend; Priya had held her hand through the diagnosis announcement last spring; Priya was constitutionally incapable of betrayal and constitutionally incapable of letting a secret die of loneliness either. If Yara said the word *Meridian*, Priya would extract the whole story in under four minutes and then bring her soup.

But the program's rules sat in her mind like a stone in a shoe: *no names, no meetings.* And more than the rules—more than any rule some agency printed on cream stock—there was the raw, childish, magnificent selfishness of it. This one thing was hers. One small, sealed room in the house of her life with no board members in it, no expectations, no father's legacy balanced on its rafters. A stranger who knew her only as a voice on a page, and who owed her nothing, and whom she owed nothing, and in whose presence—for one letter a month—she did not have to be *ready*.

"I reorganized my desk," Yara said.

Priya looked at the desk, which was a crime scene. "Sure you did." She stood, gathered her dignity, and paused at the door. "Whatever it is, keep doing it. But if it's a man, I get veto power."

"It's not a man."

It wasn't, quite. It was a door, maybe. A door she'd knocked on in the dark, and heard someone on the other side pretending not to be home.

* * *

The reply took eleven days.

She knew the exact number because she'd stopped pretending she wasn't counting. Eleven days of telling herself she didn't care, that the silence was fine, that she'd probably frightened the poor man into withdrawing from the program entirely and he was currently drafting a polite note to Meridian requesting a new correspondent, someone gentler, someone who didn't open with *your letter has no pulse.*

On the eleventh day, the cream envelope appeared in her mail slot at home—Meridian routed everything through intermediaries, so the postmarks were meaningless, the handwriting disguised by a forwarding service—and she stood in her hallway holding it for a full minute before opening it, the way you hold a submission you've been anticipating, afraid the ending won't hold.

It was longer than his first letter. His handwriting had started controlled and had progressively surrendered, the sentences tilting downhill like a sled losing its argument with the slope.

*Dear Correspondent,*

*You were right. All of it. I've read your letter perhaps forty times, which I recognize is either flattering or alarming, and I haven't decided which.*

*Here is the true thing you asked for, or the beginning of one, because it turns out honesty is less like a faucet and more like a dam, and you've put a crack in mine.*

*The garden matters because it's the only thing I've ever grown that I chose for myself. Everything else in my life was assigned. The work, the city, the personality—I inherited all of it the way you inherit furniture from a previous tenant, and I arranged it neatly and lived around it. But the garden I planted on a whim, badly, with no plan, and it fails every year, and every year I plant it again. I think I love it precisely because it can't be done correctly. There's no draft of it that will be least likely to alarm anyone.*

*As for the four discarded drafts: the first was funny, and I deleted it because funny felt like showing off. The second was sad, and I deleted it because sad felt like asking. The third was angry, and I deleted it because I've been trained out of anger so thoroughly I no longer trust my own aim. The fourth was the one you received: nothing, carefully arranged.*

*You asked me to succeed at this and promised not to be gentle. So—one more true thing, larger than the garden: I am lonely in a way I have never said out loud, because saying it out loud would make it real, and because the people around me prefer the version of me that isn't. You are the first person in years who has looked directly at me, and you did it through an envelope, which tells you something about the state of my life that I probably shouldn't commit to paper.*

*Tell me about the inheritance. Not the facts—I don't need the facts. Tell me what it costs you to carry it. I've decided I want to know you, and I've noticed that wanting things is easier the second time.*

*Your Correspondent*

Yara read it twice, standing in her hallway with her coat still on.

Then she went to the kitchen, poured a glass of wine she didn't strictly need, and sat down at the table with the legal pad, and discovered that her hands were not entirely steady.

Because this was the dangerous part. Not the sharpness—sharpness she could handle; sharpness was armor she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it clanked. But this. A stranger admitting, in tilted handwriting, that he was lonely, and that she had seen him, and that being seen had not killed him. A stranger asking her what her inheritance *cost* her, which was a question no one at Voss & Daughters had ever thought to ask, because they were all too busy calculating what it would cost *them*.

She thought of her father upstairs with his magnifying glass, wanting her ready. She thought of the board, wanting her coherent. She thought of Uncle Aldous's birthday cards, wanting her grateful.

And she thought: *this one just wants to know me. That's all. That's the whole transaction.*

She pressed her pen to the paper and began:

*Dear Correspondent,*

*It costs me my reflection. But let me start properly...*

Outside, the city went on with its evening—traffic and lit windows and ten thousand people not knowing each other—and inside the kitchen of the Voss townhouse, Yara wrote until midnight, filling three pages with truths she had never said to anyone with a face, and when she finished she read the letter over and felt the strangest sensation, like a tide going out and leaving her standing on ground she'd never seen before.

She sealed it, addressed it to the intermediary, and set it by the door for morning pickup.

Then she paused, pen still in hand, and added one line to the back of the envelope, small, in the corner, where only a careful reader would find it:

*P.S. — The fourth draft was my favorite. Don't ever arrange nothing for me again.*

She didn't know yet that somewhere across the city, in an apartment with a doomed view and a typewriter, a man who erased himself for a living was reading her first letter with his heart going like a trapped bird.

She didn't know that the memoir contract sitting in his inbox bore the name VOSS in the subject line.

All she knew was that for the first time in months, she fell asleep easily—and that somewhere, a tide was turning, patient and enormous, toward a shore neither of them had chosen yet.
