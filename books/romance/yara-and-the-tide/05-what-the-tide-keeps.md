## 5. What the Tide Keeps

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Yara would later decide was significant, though she couldn't have said why. Tuesdays had a certain honesty about them. Not the false promise of a Monday, not the giddy surrender of a Friday. Just a day that showed up, did its work, and left.

She opened it at her desk in the third-floor office of Voss & Daughters, between a meeting about the fall catalog and a meeting about the spring catalog, which were, as far as she could tell, the same meeting wearing different clothes.

*Dear Correspondent,* it began. *You asked what I do when I can't sleep. I've decided to answer, though I want it noted that this constitutes a significant breach of my personal security policy.*

Yara smiled at the page the way she hadn't smiled at anything all week.

*When I can't sleep, I reorganize bookshelves. Not mine — I've given up on mine, they've achieved a kind of entropy that I've decided to respect — but imaginary ones. I take the books I've loved and I shelve them in order of how they hurt. It's a useless exercise. It changes nothing. But at three in the morning, it feels like architecture.*

*You will notice I have not asked you a question in this letter. That is deliberate. I'm told the surest way to make a person talk is to stop asking. Consider this a silence with a stamp on it.*

*— C.*

He still wouldn't give more than an initial. She'd tried, in her last letter, a kind of gentle siege: *At least tell me what the C stands for. I need something to call you besides "Correspondent," which sounds like a disease.* He'd written back, *C stands for C. It's a very common name. You've probably met several.*

She'd laughed out loud alone in her office, which the junior editor across the hall had almost certainly heard, and which Yara had chosen not to care about.

She got out her good pen — the one her father had given her at sixteen, engraved with her initials, back when he still believed she'd use it to sign great things — and began to write on the agency's plain cream stationery.

*Dear C,*

*I see your silence-with-a-stamp and I raise you a confession.*

*Here is what I did not do this week: I did not tell the board of my family's publishing house that I read their memo calling me "untested" eleven times. I did not tell my father's nurse that I've started memorizing the sound of his breathing when I visit, the way you memorize a tide chart, looking for the turn. I did not tell anyone that I sat in the parking garage of our own building for forty minutes on Thursday because it was the only room in my life with no one waiting inside it.*

*You're a stranger. That's the entire point of you. You are the one person I can hand the ugly draft to.*

*You should know that my father built this company out of a rented storefront and a stubbornness so dense it had mass. He used to say a book was a door and we were in the business of hinges. I believed him so completely that I never asked whether I wanted the business, or only wanted him to look at me the way he looked at a first printing. Now he's declining — you'll forgive the euphemism; the doctors use longer words — and the board is circling, and I have discovered that when I argue with them, I use his voice. Not his words. His voice. The cadence, the certainty, the way of ending a sentence like a door closing.*

*I am becoming my father's ambition wearing my mother's patience, and I don't know how much of either is actually mine.*

*There. That's the ugly draft. You may shelve it wherever it hurts.*

*— Y.*

*P.S. I've decided the C stands for something absurd. Caspian. Cornelius. I'm workshopping it.*

*P.P.S. Your imaginary shelves — where do you put the books that hurt in a way you're grateful for? I ask because I suspect that's the whole shelf.*

She read it over twice. The old Yara — the Yara of six weeks ago, the Yara who chaired meetings and answered emails within the hour and never said anything in a letter she wouldn't say in a boardroom — would have cut half of it. Would have softened the garage into something wry and survivable.

But the whole premise of this arrangement, the thing the Meridian Agency had promised in its stiff, notarized way, was that the letters went nowhere. That they were read by exactly one person who had no face, no name, no leverage, no seat at any table that mattered. A confessional with a postmark.

She sealed it and dropped it in the box on Fifth Avenue on her way home, and felt — she was almost embarrassed by it, alone in the evening crowd — lighter.

* * *

Her father had good days now. That was the cruel arithmetic of it. In the bad stretch, last spring, he'd been uniformly fogged, and grief had at least been simple. Now the fog lifted and settled like weather, and on the good days he was so entirely himself that Yara would find herself laughing at his terrible jokes and then, twenty minutes later, standing in the hallway of the house on Delancey Street with her hand pressed flat against the wall, mourning a man who was still upstairs asking for tea.

On Sunday he was having a good day. He was in the study, in the good chair, with the tide almanac open on his knee — he still read it like scripture, though the house hadn't been near the coast in thirty years — and when she came in he looked up with the old sharpness and said, "There she is. The heir apparent. Sit. Tell me you've fired someone interesting this week."

"We don't fire people for being interesting, Papa."

"We should. It would save time." He patted the arm of the sofa. "Sit. I want to talk about the hearing."

"Papa—"

"Six weeks." He said it the way other men said *checkmate*. "They'll want a narrative from you. A coherent public narrative — I've read the memo, Yara, I still read everything, the fog is not a lobotomy. They want to know if you're a steward or a squanderer. So tell me. Which are you?"

The question landed in the room and just stayed there, the way his questions always did. He had never once asked a question he didn't already think he knew the answer to, which Yara had decided long ago was either supreme confidence or a profound loneliness, and possibly both.

"I don't know," she said, and watched something flicker across his face — disappointment or relief, she genuinely could not tell.

"Good," he said finally. "Don't know yet. Knowing too early is how men like Crane happen." He turned a page of the almanac with a papery sound. "Your uncle has been very attentive lately. Calls. Flowers for the nurse, which the nurse found unsettling. When a man is attentive to a dying brother's household, he is measuring it for a coffin."

"Aldous is family."

"Aldous is a tide chart," her father said. "He tells you exactly what the water's doing and never once why. Sit with that. And take a book home — the new Voss & Daughters poetry list. I hear it's mediocre. I'd like to know if it's mediocre on purpose."

She took two books and kissed his forehead, and in the car she thought: *I told a stranger about the parking garage before I could say a true sentence in this house.* And then she thought, with a small, startled tenderness: *I told a stranger, period.*

* * *

That night she wrote again. She hadn't meant to — the rhythm they'd settled into was roughly one letter each, a polite tennis match across whatever anonymous middle distance the agency maintained — but the words had been queuing up all week, patient as people outside a bakery.

*Dear C,*

*You said you'd stop asking questions, so I'm going to abuse the opening.*

*Today my father asked me who I am. Not in those words — he asked whether I was a steward or a squanderer, which in our family is the same question with better tailoring. And I realized, sitting there, that I've spent my whole life answering questions he was only asking himself. His ambition. His hunger for the company to outlive him. I inherited it the way you inherit a coat: it fit, so you stop wondering whether you'd have chosen it.*

*Here's the thing I can't say to anyone with a face: I'm good at this. That's the trap. If I were bad at it, I could walk away clean. But I can walk into a boardroom and dismantle a man's argument in four sentences and enjoy it, C. I *enjoy* it. And every time I do, I hear his cadence in my mouth, and I can't tell if I'm using the voice or the voice is using me.*

*My mother used to take me to the coast, before she died. There was a cove where the tide went out so far at low water that you could walk on sand that spent most of its life underwater. She told me: everything the tide takes, it keeps. It doesn't destroy anything. It just holds it where you can't see it, and brings it back when it's ready. I used to think that was comforting. Now I think it might be a warning. All the things I've hidden — the doubt, the wanting, the parts of me that don't fit the narrative the board is drafting — the tide has them. And six weeks from now, at this hearing, it's going to give them all back at once, in front of everyone.*

*I would like, very much, to choose what I surface. I've never once been allowed.*

*— Y.*

*P.S. You never answered the shelf question. Where do the grateful hurts go? I've decided this is the only question that matters and I will not be dissuaded.*

*P.P.S. I've settled on Caspian. You have the energy of a man named Caspian who goes by something else to avoid being teased.*

* * *

The reply came four days later, and Yara, who had begun to know its weight through the envelope the way you know a footstep on a stair, took it to the cove.

Not the actual cove — the city had no coves, only a riverfront with ambitions — but her apartment's narrow balcony, which she had privately named the cove, and where she kept a chair and a blanket and a small defiance of the building's no-plants policy in the form of one extremely smug fern.

*Dear Y,*

*Noted: I am Caspian now. I fought it for a full day and then caught myself signing a grocery list with it, so the war is lost.*

*You asked where the grateful hurts go. Top shelf. Eye level. I'm not proud of how much of the shelf they take up.*

*And since you've abused your opening so thoroughly, I'll abuse mine. You wrote that you've never been allowed to choose what you surface. I want to push on that, gently, the way you'd test ice. Allowed by whom? The board is six weeks old as a threat. Your father's expectations are older. But I suspect — and tell me if I've overstepped, I've been told I have a gift for it — that the strictest gatekeeper is you. That you surface only what's useful. That you've gotten so good at being the coherent narrative that you've forgotten you were ever a rough draft.*

*If I'm wrong, throw this letter in the river. If I'm right —*

He'd crossed something out there, a full line, inked over so heavily she held the page up to the lamp. She could just make out the ghost of it: *if I'm right, then I know the feeling, and —*

Then nothing. The sentence abandoned like a car at the roadside.

*If I'm right,* he'd resumed, in his smaller, more guarded hand, *then I'll say only this: rough drafts are where the truth keeps house. The finished version is just where it gets photographed.*

*You asked me once what the C stands for. Here is a trade. I'll tell you one true thing about myself, and you'll tell me one true thing about yourself, and we'll call the names question closed forever. My true thing: I have not been called by my full name in a room full of people in nine years. I chose that. It felt like safety at the time. Lately it feels like the tide.*

*Your turn.*

*— C.*

Yara read the letter four times. The crossed-out line she read perhaps twenty times, tilting it against the light like a jeweler with a stone of dubious provenance. *I know the feeling.* What feeling? The hiding? The gatekeeping? The nine years?

She should have been unsettled. Some prudent, boardroom Yara in the back of her skull noted that this stranger was getting closer to the bone than anyone had a right to, that the anonymity ran both directions and she knew nothing verifiable about him, that her father — her father, who read everything — would call this arrangement reckless.

But the fern swayed in its pot, and the river did its evening glitter, and Yara found that what she felt was not alarm. It was the feeling of a door she'd kept locked for so long she'd forgotten it was a door, swinging open onto a lit room.

He'd said *lately it feels like the tide.* As if something were changing for him too. As if the safety were eroding from the inside, grain by grain, and he could feel it going.

She got her pen.

*Dear Caspian,*

*My true thing, as agreed: I have never told anyone the following, including my father, including my dead mother, including the fern, who is discreet.*

*When I was nineteen, I wrote a novel. It was bad — genuinely, structurally, unforgivably bad, the kind of bad that has good sentences in it like raisins in a scone, which is somehow worse — but it was mine. I finished it. I hid it in the bottom drawer of my desk at the office, under the tax files, because if my father found it he would either publish it out of love or never mention it out of policy, and I couldn't survive either. I have moved that drawer three times, to three offices, and I have never once opened it to reread it, because I'm afraid the badness would have spread to the memory. That the girl who wrote it would turn out to have been a rough draft I was right to abandon.*

*There. The tide has given that back. It's yours now. Everything the tide takes, it keeps — so be careful what you accept, Caspian, because I intend to hold you to the keeping.*

*And now the dangerous question. You may not answer it. I want that on the record — you are permitted silence, permitted the stamp, permitted the whole armored repertoire. But I'm going to ask it anyway, because I've noticed that I trust you, a man with no name and no face, more than I trust anyone with both, and I'd like to know if that's wisdom or just loneliness wearing a good coat.*

*Who are you really?*

*Not the name. I've made my peace with C. I mean — what's under the armor? What did the nine years hide? What does the tide have of yours?*

*You can throw this letter in the river if you like. But I suspect you won't. I suspect you're exactly the kind of man who reads a dangerous question three times and then answers it at two in the morning, and then reorganizes an imaginary bookshelf to calm down.*

*— Y.*

*P.S. If you don't answer, I'll understand. But I'll also shelve you at eye level, and you should know that's where the grateful hurts go.*

She sealed it before she could lose her nerve, which took four seconds, and walked it to the box on Fifth Avenue in the rain without an umbrella, which took nine minutes and soaked her thoroughly and felt, absurdly, like the bravest thing she'd done all year — braver than the boardroom, braver than the hearing loomed, braver than any door she'd closed with her father's cadence.

The box swallowed the letter with its small brass mouth.

Yara stood on the wet sidewalk a moment longer, watching the rain come down on Fifth Avenue, and thought: *I've just handed a stranger the bottom drawer.*

She had no way of knowing that across the city, in a quiet apartment full of alphabetized shelves, a man was at that very moment holding her earlier letter up to a lamp, reading the line he'd crossed out, and drafting — for the first time in nine years — something true.
