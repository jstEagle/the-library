## 10. First Bloom

August had counted turtles for eleven years. He knew the number the way other men knew their own pulse: 214 green sea turtles tagged in the cove since his first season, 61 nests documented, 9 females he could identify by the notched pattern of their shells alone. Numbers were how he loved things—safely, at a distance, without ever having to say so out loud.

He had never once tried to count blossoms.

Now, standing at the head of the east row on a morning in late March, he understood why. There were too many. The trees that had stood gray and silent for a decade had erupted overnight—or so it seemed, though Yara would correct him, laughing, and say *nothing blooms overnight, August, it took us all winter*—into clouds of white and faintest pink. The petals caught the early light and held it, so that each tree looked lit from within. The air smelled like honey and cold water. Somewhere down the slope, a bee was already working, and the sound of it was so small and so enormous that August had to stop walking just to listen.

"Stop counting," Yara said beside him. She'd come up the path with two thermoses, her hair tied back with the same frayed blue ribbon she'd worn at fifteen, and she was watching him watch the trees with an expression he was still learning to read—not guarded anymore, but something open where the guard used to be. "You get that look. Like you're afraid you'll lose track."

"I will lose track," he said. "That's the point. You can't count this."

"You can try." She handed him a thermos. "That's what makes it worth counting."

The coffee was terrible—the way it always was when she made it, too much chicory, a habit from her father—and August drank it anyway, because some things you didn't fix. Some things you kept exactly as they were.

* * *

They had signed the papers on a Tuesday in February, in the same county office where August had once come intending to sell his half and be done with the whole Solano question forever. The clerk—a woman named Delia who remembered both of them as children, who remembered Yara's mother buying stamps and August's father arguing about drainage easements—had pulled the old deed from the archive and laid it on the counter between them like something rescued from a fire.

The covenant was still there, in faded ink: *neither party shall transfer without the consent of the other.* But beneath it, in the margin, someone had written a note decades ago, small and slanted: *This land is meant to be held together or not at all.*

Yara had read it twice. Then she'd looked up at August, and whatever she saw in his face made her laugh—a wet, broken kind of laugh—and say, "Your uncle's lawyer wrote that. Can you believe it? The man who stole everything wrote one true thing before he died."

"He didn't steal everything," August said. "He gave back what he could. And we're here."

So they signed again. Not to divide, not to release, but to hold—together, as partners, the word written plainly into the new deed where anyone could see it. Delia stamped the page and slid it across the counter, and outside the courthouse window the winter light came down flat and silver on the street, and August felt something in his chest unclench that had been clenched for three years, maybe longer, maybe since he was seventeen years old under a fig tree making promises neither of them had known how to keep.

Yara had squeezed his hand once, hard, and let go. That was enough. That was everything.

* * *

The town came for the bloom the way water comes into a dry channel—slowly at first, then all at once.

Marisol Vega arrived first, because Marisol arrived first everywhere, hauling folding tables in her truck bed and directing traffic that did not yet exist. Then the Okafor brothers, who had spent the winter helping clear storm-fall from the north grove. Then old Tomás Reyes—no relation, though he claimed otherwise every time—which everyone allowed because his stories were better than genealogy. By noon there were forty people in the orchard, and by two there were eighty, and someone had strung lights between the fig tree and the old well even though it was hours until dark, because the lights were not for seeing. They were for saying: *we expected to stay.*

Yara moved through the crowd like the orchard itself had grown her—greeting people whose names August only half-knew, accepting casseroles and cuttings and unsolicited advice about thinning the plum rows. He watched her from a distance and felt the strange double vision of loving someone he had also lost: every gesture carried its ghost. At fifteen she had moved through these same rows the same way, barefoot in June, telling him which trees were which as if introducing friends.

"You're doing it again," said Tomás, materializing at his elbow with a paper plate of empanadas. "Counting."

"I'm not counting anything."

"You're counting her," Tomás said, unbothered. "I watched your father do the same thing to your mother for forty years. It's a family condition." He pushed the plate into August's hands. "Eat. Love makes men stupid and hungry. Usually in that order."

* * *

At golden hour, the town gathered under the fig tree.

It had been Yara's idea, and she'd been shy about it in a way August had never seen her be shy—fidgeting with the ribbon, saying *it's probably silly, it's just a tree*—and he'd understood then that she wasn't asking them to gather under a fig tree. She was asking them to witness a vow being finished. Fifteen-year-olds don't know what they're promising. They only know they mean it.

She stood with her back against the trunk, in the exact spot where she'd stood at fifteen, and the whole town went quiet the way crowds do when they sense they've stumbled into something older than themselves.

"When I was fifteen," she said, and her voice carried clean across the rows, "I made a promise under this tree. I promised that if this orchard ever needed saving, I'd save it. And then my family left, and I spent eleven years believing I'd broken that promise. Eleven years believing the leaving was mine—that I chose it." Her eyes found August's. "I didn't choose it. My father's debts weren't his failure alone. Someone bought them, and used them, and took our land, and I was a child who couldn't stop it and couldn't speak it either, because speaking it would have hurt someone else I—" her voice caught, steadied— "someone else I loved."

August stepped forward. Not because he'd planned to. Because standing apart from her while she told the truth had become unbearable.

"For three years I built a life with walls around it," he said. "I told myself it was discipline. Science. Containment." A ripple of laughter—he'd talked about his methods often enough that the whole town knew his vocabulary. He let them laugh. "But walls don't keep the heart safe. They just keep it small. And the truth is—" he turned to face the crowd, because this part wasn't only for Yara; it was for the town that had treated him like a ghost returning wrong, and for himself, most of all— "the truth is my uncle took this land from the Solanos, and my silence protected him, and Yara's silence protected me, and between the two silences we lost eleven years. I'm done with silence."

He pulled the folded paper from his jacket pocket—the copy of the letter his mentor had found among the uncle's effects, the deathbed document proving the return of the land had been attempted, blocked, attempted again. He'd shown it to the town already, weeks ago, in the church hall, in the ugly fluorescent light of full disclosure. But he unfolded it again now and held it up.

"This is finished history. What isn't finished is the vow." He looked at Yara. "You asked me once, under this tree, if I'd help you save the orchard. I said yes. I'm saying it again. Not for a season. Not for a harvest. For as long as the trees stand."

Yara crossed the space between them—three steps, no more—and took the letter from his hands and set it on the low stone wall, deliberate, like laying something down.

"I never signed the release," she said quietly, for him alone, though the whole town leaned in. "In February. When I said I would. I sat in my truck outside the county office with the pen in my hand and I couldn't do it. So I drove home and I burned the papers in the woodstove and I told myself I'd find the courage to tell you eventually." She smiled, and it was the smile from the fig tree, from fifteen, from before everything. "Turns out I didn't need courage. I needed you to go first."

"I usually do," August said. "Turtle surveys start at dawn."

"Turtle surveys," she repeated, and kissed him, and the town cheered, and somewhere in the cheering Marisol Vega was already crying and denying it.

* * *

Later—much later, after the lanterns were lit and the music started and Tomás Reyes had told the story of the flood of '89 with entirely new embellishments—August walked the east row alone.

Not alone, really. Yara was twenty feet behind him, talking with the Okafors about irrigation, but he walked slowly enough that she'd catch up, and she always did. That was another thing he was learning: you could walk ahead of someone and still be walking with them.

He stopped at the third tree. It was the one they'd grafted together in the first week, back when they were strangers wearing familiar faces, back when their hands had remembered rhythms their hearts hadn't dared to. The graft had taken. Of course it had. The scion and the rootstock had recognized each other the way he and Yara had—as if eleven years were a season of dormancy, nothing more, and everything beneath the bark had stayed alive, waiting.

He counted the blossoms on one branch. Lost track. Started over. Lost track again.

"You'll never finish," Yara said, arriving at his shoulder.

"No," he agreed. "I won't."

"Good." She leaned into him, her head against his arm, and together they looked down the row—all those trees in their white eruption, all those neighbors under the string lights, all that life insisting itself out of ground that everyone had written off. "A scientist should know better than to count what can't be counted."

"A scientist knows the difference between data and wonder," August said. "I'm working on the second one."

Above them, the first stars were coming out, and the bees had gone home, and the orchard breathed its honeyed breath over everything it held. August thought about the number 214, and how he'd wear it like armor for eleven years, and how numbers hadn't saved him from anything—they'd only delayed him. What saved him was a covenant in a margin. A woman who burned the papers. A tree that bloomed after ten years of nothing, because someone finally stayed long enough to feed its roots.

"Come on," Yara said, tugging his hand. "Marisol's starting the dancing, and if we don't get there first she'll make us lead."

They walked back toward the lights, and behind them the blossoms went on opening in the dark—more than could ever be counted, more than either of them would ever try—and August Reyes, who had spent a lifetime observing life without joining it, let himself be counted among the living, and found the number fit.
