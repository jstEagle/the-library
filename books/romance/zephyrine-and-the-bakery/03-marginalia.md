## 3. Marginalia

The book came back on a Tuesday, which was the day Mirabel least expected anything.

Tuesdays were for inventory — the slow arithmetic of stock and dust, the radio murmuring weather to an empty shop. She was halfway up the ladder with a stack of paperbacks when the bell over the door chimed and Mrs. Okonkwo from the flower stall came in holding a novel at arm's length, the way one holds something that has begun to smell.

"Mirabel. This came through the donation box last month. I took it home." She set it on the counter with a certain ceremony. "I want you to look at what someone's done to it."

The book was *A Field Guide to Edible Wild Plants*, which was odd enough on its own — nobody donated field guides, and nobody bought them either; it had sat spine-out on the nature shelf for three years like a hermit crab in the wrong shell. But it wasn't the title that made Mirabel's hands go still as she opened it. It was the margins.

Pencil, small and slanted, filling every white space the way ivy fills a wall.

*Chickweed — Mama called this poor-man's salad. We ate it out of the drainage ditch behind the church and she pretended it was a delicacy until I believed her.*

*Elderberry. The year of the fever I made wine from these and gave it away because keeping sweetness felt greedy.*

*Mushrooms: never eat what you cannot name. A rule for more than mushrooms.*

Mirabel read standing very still, the ladder forgotten behind her, the shop going quiet around her the way a room goes quiet when someone says your name in another room. She knew this voice. Not the words — she'd never read these particular words before — but the voice underneath them. The dry tenderness. The way grief was folded into a joke so you'd only find it later, like a coin sewn into a coat hem. The habit of turning every fact into a small confession.

Ten years of unsigned letters had taught her this voice the way you learn a language by drowning in it.

"Mirabel?" Mrs. Okonkwo said. "Are you all right? It's just vandalism, really, I thought you'd want to pull it—"

"No," Mirabel said, too quickly. Then, softer: "No. I'll take care of it."

She wrapped the book in brown paper like contraband and carried it home in her bag, where it sat on the kitchen table all evening radiating a low heat while she made dinner and pretended not to look at it. Odette looked at it instead. Eleven-year-olds miss nothing.

"What's in the package?"

"A book."

"You already have those."

"Mm."

Her daughter rolled her eyes with the full-body commitment of her age and went to do homework, and Mirabel finally opened the field guide again under the lamp and read every marginal note twice, hunting for a name, a date, a slip. There was none. Of course there was none. This was a person who had written unsigned letters for a decade; anonymity was not a habit but an architecture, load-bearing, built into everything they touched.

But the letters had stopped coming to her. That was the thing she kept circling. The last one had arrived eleven months ago, and before that they had come every spring without fail, always on the anniversary, always saying just enough. Whoever wrote in margins had not stopped writing. They had only changed address.

Or — and here the thought arrived with its little cold feet — they had moved next door.

Mirabel set the book down and pressed her palms flat against the table until the wood's grain printed itself into her skin. Don't. She knew exactly what hope did to her; she'd watched it work on her for ten years, patient as water. Hope found the crack in everything and widened it.

Still. When she slept that night, she dreamed in pencil.

* * *

Zephyrine Okafor knew something was wrong the moment Mirabel Delacroix walked into the bakery at seven-forty in the morning carrying a paper-wrapped parcel and wearing the expression of a woman arriving at a duel.

For six weeks Zephyrine had perfected the art of being unremarkable. She rose at four, baked until the street smelled like forgiveness, sold bread to Mirabel's customers, and exchanged with Mirabel herself a currency of small civilities — change counted precisely, weather discussed moderately, croissants recommended with the neutral enthusiasm of a stranger. Every morning she performed the stranger flawlessly. Every morning it cost her more.

Because she knew the woman across the counter down to the marrow. She knew about the hospital corridor and the vending machine coffee and the year Mirabel had eaten nothing but whatever Odette left on the plate. She knew the exact sentence that had once kept a drowning woman above water, because Zephyrine had written it, and rewritten it four times, and mailed it unsigned because signing it would have made it about her, and it could never be about her. It had to be a door Mirabel could walk through without seeing who held it open.

And now Mirabel was crossing the bakery floor with a parcel, and Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the floorboards into the proofing room.

"Good morning," Mirabel said. Her voice was level. Her eyes were not. "Could I trouble you for two coffees?"

"Of course." Zephyrine turned to the machine, grateful for the noise, the steam, the ritual. Behind her she heard the parcel's paper being unfolded — deliberately, unmistakably, the sound of evidence being produced.

"I found something," Mirabel said, "in a returned donation. A field guide. Someone's written all through the margins."

The espresso machine hissed. Zephyrine watched the dark thread of coffee fall and told her hands to be steady. "People do that. Write in books. Some of us think it improves them."

"Do you?"

"Do I what?"

"Think it improves them." Mirabel came around the end of the counter — not close, but closer, into the territory where the smell of butter and yeast lived. "There's a note about elderberries. About making wine the year of a fever, and giving it away." A pause, calibrated. "*Keeping sweetness felt greedy.*"

Zephyrine set down the portafilter with exaggerated care. Somewhere behind her ribs, a decade of discipline stood at attention.

"That's lovely," she said. "Whoever wrote it sounds like my aunt. She used to say things like that. Give away the jam, keep the jars." She smiled — warm, rueful, entirely fictional. "You think it might be someone local?"

"I don't know." Mirabel's gaze stayed on her a beat too long, searching the flour-dusted apron, the forearms, the face, for a handwriting she'd only ever touched with her eyes. "It's a beautiful voice, whoever it belongs to."

"Then I hope you find them." Zephyrine slid both coffees across the counter and added, on instinct, on some deeper current she didn't examine: "Though sometimes it's kinder to let people stay unnamed. Some people write better in the margins than they ever could in the text."

Something flickered across Mirabel's face — disappointment, or its twin, relief. "Yes," she said quietly. "Maybe they do."

She paid, gathered her parcel, and paused at the door. "Your croissants sold out by nine yesterday. Odette says your morning buns are the best thing on this street, and she is a harsh critic."

"Tell her the harsh critics get first pick tomorrow. Baker's privilege."

And then she was gone, the bell chiming, and Zephyrine stood alone in her own bakery with her heart going like a stand mixer on high, thinking: *she knows. She doesn't know, but she knows.* And beneath that, unwelcome as warmth in winter: *she quoted me back to myself and looked at me like that.*

She should have felt caught. What she felt, God help her, was seen.

* * *

Odette Delacroix had a system, and the system was this: homework happened wherever the best light and the best snacks converged, and as of six weeks ago that meant the corner table of the bakery next door, where the window seat caught the afternoon sun and Zephyrine Okafor dispensed lemonade and opinions in equal measure.

"It's not fair," Odette announced on Thursday, stabbing her pencil at a math worksheet. "Mrs. Brennan marked the whole problem wrong because I didn't show my work. But I *did* the work. In my head. Where work happens."

"Ah," said Zephyrine, wiping down the counter. "Invisible labor. The oldest injustice in the world." She came over and looked at the worksheet upside down, which was how she preferred all mathematics. "Here's the trick they don't tell you. The answer was never the point. The showing is the point. They want to see inside your head, because most people can't trust a thing they can't watch happening."

"That's stupid."

"It's human." Zephyrine tapped the page. "Show them anyway. Not because they're right. Because sometimes letting people see the work gets you something the answer alone won't."

"Like what?"

Zephyrine thought of a hospital waiting room, ten years and a lifetime ago. A woman with chapped hands and a sleeping daughter, reading a letter over and over like it was bread in a famine.

"Like being understood," she said. "Sometimes."

Odette considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "Did you show your work when you were a kid?"

"I showed my work so much they gave me a scholarship to stop."

Odette laughed — the big, unguarded laugh she seemed to save for this room — and Zephyrine felt the laugh land somewhere under her sternum and stay there. This was the part she hadn't planned for. She had prepared for Mirabel: for the awkwardness, the danger, the exquisite torture of proximity. She had not prepared for a child who did her homework in flour-light and asked questions like small knives, who had started calling her Zeph without asking permission, who had informed her last week, apropos of nothing, "My mom reads your letters."

Zephyrine had gone very still and said, "Sorry — my letters?"

"The ones that come in spring. No return address. She keeps them in a tin under the register. I'm not supposed to know." Odette had shrugged, merciless. "I know everything."

That had been eight days ago, and Zephyrine had been carrying it around like a stone in her shoe ever since. *A tin under the register.* So the letters weren't ash after all. They were kept. Archived. Treasured, maybe, or held as evidence — with Mirabel, lately, it was getting harder to tell the difference.

The bell rang at five-fifteen and Mirabel came in for her daughter, and mother and baker looked at each other across the warm room with the careful blankness of people guarding the same secret from opposite sides.

"Ready, monster?"

"One minute." Odette shoved worksheets into her bag. "Zeph, can I come back Saturday? I want to learn the braiding thing."

"If your mother permits it."

Both of them looked at Mirabel. Mirabel looked at Zephyrine — at the flour on her cheekbone, at the pencil-sharp kindness she wore like an apron — and something in her chest tilted off its axis. Suspicion and hope had become, over these weeks, difficult to tell apart; both made the heart race; both made her look too long.

"Saturday's fine," Mirabel said. "If it's no trouble."

"No trouble at all," Zephyrine said, and meant it so plainly that it frightened her.

They left together, mother and daughter, the bell chiming them out into the blue evening. And through the glass Zephyrine watched Mirabel glance back once — quickly, guiltily — at the bakery, at her, before turning away.

That night, closing up, Zephyrine found Odette's forgotten pencil case under the corner table. Inside, along with the pencils, was a folded scrap of paper — a page torn from a notebook, and on it, in an eleven-year-old's careful hand, copied out like a spell:

*Never eat what you cannot name. A rule for more than mushrooms.*

Zephyrine stood in her empty bakery holding her own sentence in a child's handwriting, and understood with terrible clarity that the margins were closing in — that sooner or later, one of them was going to have to write in the text.

Across the alley, the light in the bookshop's back room burned late. Mirabel was awake too, she realized. Awake, and probably reading.

Probably reading him — the tin, the letters, the voice.

Zephyrine turned off her lights and stood in the dark, deciding nothing, deciding everything.
