## 11. Amara Says Yes for the Wrong Reasons

The press died on a Tuesday.

Not dramatically—Amara would have preferred dramatically. A catastrophic failure she could have raged at, could have thrown her whole body against the way she'd once thrown herself against a sous-chef who salted her caramel wrong. Instead it simply slowed, that morning, the way old things do: the screw press groaning through its rotation half a beat late, then two, then stopping altogether with a sound like a tired man sitting down.

She stood in the pressing shed with juice still dripping off her knuckles and listened to the silence where the orchard's heartbeat used to be.

"Could be the main bearing," Gus said behind her, in the tone of a man delivering a diagnosis he'd already priced out in his head. He wiped his hands on a rag that had been gray since before Amara was born. "Could be the worm shaft. Either way, we're talking parts nobody makes anymore. Somebody'd have to machine them custom."

"How much?"

Gus looked at the press instead of at her, which was answer enough. "Machinist up in Corvallis quoted Theo four thousand for the last job like this. That was six years ago."

Four thousand. Amara did the arithmetic without wanting to—the harvest coming in three weeks, forty acres of Gravensteins and Jonagolds and the late pears nobody else in the valley bothered with anymore, all of it useless as decoration if they couldn't press it. The cider and the pear butter and the small-batch vinegars were the only part of Zephyrine that actually made money. The restaurant was a dream; the weddings Noor planned for other people were someone else's dream; but the press, the press was real. The press was her grandmother's handwriting on the recipe cards. The press was the reason the workers got paid in October.

"We can't lose it," she said, and heard how young her voice sounded, and hated it.

"No," Gus agreed mildly. "We can't."

* * *

That night she sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open under the good lamp, the one with the green glass shade that had survived every renovation and every disaster the house had thrown at it. The numbers hadn't improved with darkness. They never did. She added the column again anyway, because hope is just arithmetic performed by a stubborn person.

The buyer's card sat where she'd left it three weeks ago, weighted under the sugar bowl like something venomous pinned under a glass. Hollis Development Partners. R. Calloway, Acquisitions. He'd come by twice more since, both times wearing the same pleasant expression of a man who knew exactly what he was holding. The second time, he'd brought a folder.

"The east field," he'd said, tapping the aerial photograph. "Prime road frontage. We'd structure it as a separate parcel sale—clean, fast, no impact on your operations here." His finger had moved across the photo to the block of old-growth Gravensteins, eighty years in the ground, gnarled and heavy-hipped and still bearing fruit like it was nothing. "You understand those trees are past commercial prime anyway."

"They're not," Amara had said.

He'd smiled the way people smile at dogs that bark at mail carriers. "Miss Vale. I've done this twenty years. I know what an orchard looks like when it's finished."

She had shown him the gate with a politeness so glacial it had taken him a full minute to register it as a door closing.

Now she turned the card over in her fingers. The number on the back, written in his own hand, was generous. Insultingly, transparently generous—a number designed to make refusal feel like insanity rather than principle. And it wasn't even the full offer. It was the *advance against* the full offer, payable immediately, no conditions except the one written into the parcel agreement in language she'd have to hire a lawyer to fully unpack: *buyer to assume site preparation costs upon execution.*

Site preparation. Clearing. Demolition.

The east field gone. Her grandmother's favorite walk, the row of trees she'd named after each of her grandchildren—Amara's tree was the fourth one in, split-crotched from lightning, still giving the best Gravensteins on the property. Gone for a cul-de-sac or a tasting room or whatever Hollis built when it ate places like this.

She put the card down. Picked it up again. Put it down.

"You're going to wear a hole in it," Theo said from the doorway. He had flour on his forearms and the particular exhaustion of a man who'd been laminating croissant dough since four a.m. He leaned against the doorframe and looked at the ledger, then at the card, and his face did something careful. "Don't," he said.

"I haven't decided anything."

"You've decided. You're just waiting for me to say it out loud so you can fight about it instead of doing it."

Amara pushed back from the table. "We need four thousand dollars by Friday, Theo. Maybe five. The harvest starts in nineteen days. If the press isn't running by then—" She stopped, because finishing the sentence made it true.

"I'll take a second job."

"On top of the sixty hours you already work? You'll fall asleep in a fryer."

"Then we sell the truck."

"The truck IS the second job."

They stared at each other across the wreckage of the family finances. Outside, the September dark pressed against the windows, and somewhere out there the east field stood in it, patient and unaware, doing the only thing it knew how to do, which was grow.

"There's another way," Theo said finally, and Amara felt it coming the way you feel weather change. "You heard him. Delacroix. Two hundred guests, destination event, and they want local catering. Local *prestige* catering. He said the bride's people called three farms before us."

"He said that to get in my door."

"And did it work?"

Amara said nothing, which was its own admission.

"Catering fee alone covers the press twice over," Theo went on, relentless in the gentle way he'd perfected growing up as the younger sibling of a person who bit. "Plus the venue fee. Plus exposure—you cater one Delacroix wedding and every planner on the coast has your number. You've been saying for months we need to build the restaurant's reputation before we even have a restaurant. This is that. Just... earlier. And bigger. And for terrible people."

"They're not terrible. They're rich. Those are different diseases."

"Fine. For rich people." He came into the kitchen properly now, pulled out the chair across from her, sat. "Amara. I know what you're not saying. I'm asking you to say it anyway, because keeping it in your mouth will rot faster than saying it."

She looked at the ledger. At the card. At the window, and the dark beyond it, and the field inside the dark.

"The venue fee," she said. "The way Hollis structured the parcel deal—if I take their money for the wedding, if I sign anything that puts their name on our grounds, it activates the option clause. They get first position on the east field sale. Not automatic. But first position, at their price, which means nobody else can bid." Her voice stayed level. She was proud of that, later, that it stayed level. "I'd be handing them the field with a bow on it."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs a pipe ticked as it cooled—one of the pipes Noor had fixed, she thought absurdly, one of the pipes Noor had fixed with his sleeves rolled up and plaster dust in his hair.

"And Noor?" Theo asked. "Does he know any of this?"

"No."

"Are you going to tell him?"

"It's his account, Theo. His boss. His big partnership. He's been walking around for two weeks like a man carrying a live grenade with the pin halfway out—he wants this wedding so badly he's stopped pretending he doesn't. If I tell him the money that pays for his triumph also sells the field—" She laughed, short and ugly. "What do I do with that? Make him choose? Between the thing he's wanted his whole career and the thing we—"

She stopped. Theo waited with the patience of a brother.

"The thing we haven't named," Amara finished flatly.

"Yeah," Theo said softly. "That."

* * *

She told herself she'd sleep on it. She told herself that for three nights, while the machinist in Corvallis confirmed the quote—four thousand seven hundred, eight-day turnaround, payment upfront—and while the first Jonagolds began dropping in the night wind, unpressed, wasted, a soft thud she heard through her window like a clock ticking backward.

On the fourth morning she found Noor in the greenhouse kitchen, standing at the long worktable with a binder open and his reading glasses on, and the sight of him did something unfair to her resolve. He'd started leaving the glasses on lately, forgetting them on counters, hooking them in his collar like a man becoming comfortable in a place. There was a smudge of candle wax on the far sill from the storm night that neither of them had cleaned, and neither of them spoke about, and which Amara had begun to think of as a landmark.

"Delacroix confirmed the date," he said, looking up, and his face did the thing it did when he talked about this wedding—that bright, armored enthusiasm she'd learned to read underneath. Underneath it he looked like a man asking permission for something he'd already stolen. "Third Saturday of next month. Two hundred and ten guests. They want the ceremony on the lawn, dinner in the restored kitchen, fireworks over—" He caught himself. She watched him catch himself, watched his eyes flick toward the east-facing windows and away. "Fireworks later. Anyway. I told them we'd need to discuss catering separately, that the kitchen had its own—"

"I'll do it."

The words came out of her before she'd decided to release them. Noor blinked. Took off the glasses.

"You'll—"

"Cater it. The Delacroix wedding. Me. My kitchen." She kept her voice brisk, professional, the voice she'd used in Michelin kitchens when service was collapsing and the only way through was forward. "Two hundred and ten covers, plated, farm-driven menu, your client's people were apparently already sniffing around. I'll need their dietary requirements, the timeline, and a deposit by end of week."

Noor stared at her. And here was the thing she hadn't prepared for: the joy. It broke over his face like light through a cracked door—all of it, unguarded, the enthusiasm and the relief and something rawer underneath, gratitude maybe, or the specific happiness of a man watching two halves of his life agree to touch. He came around the table and for one vertiginous moment she thought he might hug her, and some traitorous part of her braced for it, and then he didn't, and she couldn't decide whether she was relieved or bereft.

"Amara, this is—I can't tell you what this means. The Delacroix matriarch has rejected every caterer on the coast. If she tastes your food—" He shook his head, laughing a little. "This could save everything. The press, the—everything."

*Everything.* She held his eyes and smiled and felt the lie go down like a stone swallowed whole.

"There are terms," she said.

"Name them."

"My kitchen, my menu, my staff. You handle the client, I handle the food, and neither of us crosses the line. And the venue fee comes to the orchard operating account, not to either of us personally. Agreed?"

"Agreed." He put out his hand, formal, ridiculous, and she shook it, and his palm was warm and dry and lingered a half-second past business, and she withdrew her hand first because if she didn't she was going to do something with it that would complicate an already unforgivable situation.

He turned back to his binder, already talking timelines, seating charts, the matriarch's allergy to lilies. Amara nodded in the right places. Through the greenhouse glass the east field lay in the morning sun, the Gravensteins heavy with almost-ripe fruit, her grandmother's namesake tree fourth from the fence with its lightning-split crown, and she thought: *I am selling you. I am selling you to pay for the machine that turns you into cider, and I am going to stand in the kitchen and cook beautifully while they cut you down, and I am going to let him believe—*

"Amara?" Noor was looking at her. "You went somewhere."

"Just planning the menu," she said. "It's a lot of covers."

It was the first outright lie she'd told him since the roommate agreement, and it slid out so easily it frightened her. That was the thing about betrayal, she was learning. Everyone imagined it arrived with drama, with slammed doors and discovered letters. Nobody warned you it could arrive as competence—as doing your job well, smiling correctly, shaking a warm hand—while the worst thing you'd ever done sat quietly in your chest like a swallowed stone, getting heavier.

* * *

She called Calloway that afternoon from the pressing shed, where the dead press loomed over her like a monument to the price of things.

"The Vale girl," he said warmly. "I wondered when I'd hear from you."

"I want the advance. Today. Wire transfer, close of business."

"A wise woman." Papers rustling. "And you've reviewed the option provision?"

"I've reviewed enough."

A pause, brief, satisfied. "The east field won't be touched until after your event, of course. We're not barbarians. Site prep wouldn't begin until spring regardless." His voice took on the confiding tone of a man offering her a kindness. "For what it's worth, Miss Vale, most people wait too long. They hold on until there's nothing left to sell. You're being practical. The grandmother, I'm sure, would understand."

"Don't," Amara said quietly, "talk about my grandmother."

The line went politely silent, then: "The wire will be initiated within the hour. Congratulations on the Delacroix booking, by the way. Big account. Your planner friend must be thrilled."

She hung up and stood in the shed with the phone hot against her ear and the smell of old apples and old oil all around her, and above the workbench her grandmother's recipe box sat on its shelf—smoke-stained now, rescued, scarred, saved. Everything in this room had been saved from something.

At dusk she walked out to the east field alone. The fourth tree was loaded so heavily the split bough nearly touched the grass. She picked one apple, polished it on her sleeve, and ate it standing up, right there, juice running down her wrist, sweet-tart and perfect and utterly uncompensated for.

Behind her, up at the house, the kitchen light came on, and she saw Noor's silhouette move past the glass, and she stood in her dying orchard eating her grandmother's apple and did not go inside for a long time.

The wire confirmation buzzed in her pocket. Four thousand seven hundred dollars. Enough for the press. Enough, God help her, for the beginning of the end.

She was still standing there when she heard footsteps on the gravel path—Noor, jacketless, squinting against the dusk, holding something in his hand. An envelope. Lawyer's cream.

"Amara," he said, and his voice had a strange, careful weight she'd never heard from him before. "There's something I need to tell you. About the Delacroix contract."
