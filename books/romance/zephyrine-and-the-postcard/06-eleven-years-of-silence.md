## 6. Eleven Years of Silence

I locked the front door at 6:04, which was four minutes later than I'd ever closed Pages & Tide in eleven years, and I did it because Jonah Reyes was still standing at the poetry shelf like a man who had forgotten how doors worked.

"Shop's closing," I said, though he was looking directly at me and the CLOSED sign had been flipped for ten minutes.

"I know." He turned a paperback over in his hands. Neruda. He didn't read poetry—I remembered that with a vicious little clarity, the way you remember everything about a person you've spent a decade trying to forget. He read maps and engine manuals and once, memorably, a tide chart for pleasure. "I'll buy this."

"You don't want that."

"Maybe I've changed."

"People don't."

The words came out sharper than I'd intended, and something flickered across his face—not hurt exactly, more like a man acknowledging a hit he'd been expecting. He carried the book to the counter and set it down, and I rang it up because the alternative was standing there with my hands full of nothing, and I have never in my life been good at empty hands.

Wren had gone home twenty minutes ago, sent ahead with Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery, who had taken one look at Jonah's face and one look at mine and departed with the speed of a woman who could smell a story she'd be telling for weeks. My daughter had looked back at me from the doorway with those eyes—his eyes, God help me, his exact eyes—and mouthed *are you okay*, and I'd nodded the way mothers nod when they are lying.

Now it was just us. The shop ticking as the radiator cooled. Rain starting against the windows, soft, apologetic rain, the kind Zephyrine does instead of apologizing out loud.

"You have her eyes," Jonah said.

"Don't."

"Coral—"

"*Don't.*" I heard my own voice crack on the word and hated it, hated him, hated the eleven years that had made my voice a thing that cracked. I pulled open the drawer beneath the register—the drawer where the bank notice lived unopened, where pens went to die—and took out the postcard.

I'd carried it all day. In my apron pocket while I shelved. Against my ribs while I made change. It had sat there like a coal, burning through the fabric, through the skin, down to whatever I keep in place of a heart these days.

I set it on the counter between us.

"That's your address," I said. "That's your handwriting—" I stopped. Corrected myself. "*My* handwriting. Addressed to you. Postmarked the third week of September, eleven years ago. Returned to sender. Do you know what return to sender means, Jonah? It means it never reached you. It means someone gave it back."

He picked it up the way you pick up something that might be alive. His thumb moved over the water stain that had bloomed across the corner, eating half my sentence, and I watched him read the surviving words upside down before he turned it right side up.

*I have to tell you something and I don't know how except to say it flat: I'm pregnant.*

His face did something complicated. I watched it happen the way you watch weather come in off the water—one moment clear, the next a whole gray system moving through. His jaw worked. His hand, holding the postcard, trembled slightly, and I noticed his hands were different than I remembered, older, a scar across one knuckle I didn't know the story of and refused to ask about.

"I never got this," he said quietly.

"No," I agreed. "You didn't. That's rather the point."

"Coral, I swear to you—"

"Oh, we're swearing now." I laughed, and it came out wrong, high and brittle, a stranger's laugh. "Eleven years too late but sure. Let's swear things. Swear them at me. Go on."

"I never got any letter." Each word came out flat and separate, like stones laid in a row. "I got nothing. Not a letter, not a call, not a note in a bottle. I left because—" He stopped. Started again. "I left because you told me you were pregnant and then you told me to go."

"I told you no such thing."

"You said—" His voice caught. He pressed his palm flat on the counter, steadying himself against it. "You said you'd figure it out alone. You said you'd always figured things out alone. You said it like a door closing, Coral. I stood in your grandmother's kitchen and you looked at me like I was already gone."

* * *

Here is what memory does. It takes one night and files it under different headings depending on whose head it's stored in.

In my filing system, that night went like this: I told him. I waited. He went pale in a way I'd never seen a man go pale, and he said he needed to think, and he walked out into the dark, and he never came back. Three days later his room above the hardware store was empty. A week after that, his mother Marisol stood in my doorway with her kind, terrible eyes and said, *He's gone, mija. He took the morning boat.* And I understood that I had shown him a door and he had walked through it, and I built my entire life on the foundation of that understanding.

But now here was Jonah, standing in my shop with rain in his hair, saying: *you looked at me like I was already gone.*

"You said you'd handle it," he said. "You said your grandmother would help. You said—" He laughed, and his laugh was broken too, and for one ugly second we were two people laughing at the wreckage of the same night from opposite ends of it. "You know what I heard? I heard a woman telling me she didn't need me. Politely. Thoroughly. Like a rejection letter. And I thought, well. She's given me the out. And I was twenty-three and scared out of my mind and I took it. I took it and I hated myself for it before the boat even cleared the harbor, and by then it felt too late to turn around, because what would I say? *Sorry, I panicked, please let me un-leave?*"

"So you stayed gone. For eleven years."

"Yes."

"You could have written."

"I DID write!" It burst out of him, loud enough that a book seemed to flinch. He lowered his voice. "First month. From up the coast. I wrote that I was sorry, that I'd been a coward, that if she—if the baby—I wrote asking if there was a baby, Coral. I asked. I begged. I mailed it to your grandmother's house because I didn't know where else. Nothing came back. No answer. No *return to sender*. Just nothing. So I thought—" His throat moved. "I thought you'd decided. And I decided that a man who runs from a woman carrying his child doesn't get to argue with her decision."

The rain picked up outside. Somewhere down the street a shutter banged, once, twice, the town settling itself against the weather.

"And I wrote again," he said. "Every year. For the first five years. Birthdays. Did you get those?"

"No."

"Nothing?"

"Jonah, do you honestly think—" I gestured at the shop, at the window seat, at the whole small architecture of my life. "Do you think if I'd known you were writing, I'd have—what? Filed them? You think I wouldn't have burned them or answered them or *something*? I am not a woman who leaves letters unanswered. I own a bookshop. Words are the only thing I've ever trusted."

"Then someone—" He stopped. I watched him arrive at it, the same cliff edge I'd gone over two days ago behind the poetry shelf. "Someone kept them apart."

Neither of us said anything for a long moment. The radiator ticked. The rain wrote its long gray paragraph against the glass.

"Who?" he finally asked. "Who, Coral? Who had access to your mail and mine and hated me enough—or loved you enough—to do that?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for two days like a bird over deep water, afraid to land.

Because I knew. Of course I knew. I'd known since the moment I read the postmark, some part of me, the part that never lies even when the rest of me begs it to, had whispered the name. But saying it out loud meant tearing down the last wall between the life I'd chosen and the life that had been chosen *for* me, and I wasn't ready. I stood there with my hand on the counter and my whole body shaking and said:

"I don't know."

It was a lie. We both knew it was a lie. Jonah looked at me with those eyes my daughter shares, and he saw straight through me the way he always could, back when we were young and stupid and I thought being seen was the same as being safe.

"You know," he said softly.

"I don't—"

"Coral." He set the postcard down between us, carefully, like evidence at a trial. "Look at the return stamp. Look at the date. Someone brought this back to you and never said a word. Someone intercepted five years of my letters. That's not an accident. That's a person. A person who is probably still here. Still in this town. Still—" He hesitated, and I watched him not say it: *still feeding you soup, still watching my daughter grow up, still sitting at your table.*

"The postmistress retired years ago," I said, grasping at straws, drowning gracefully.

"It wasn't the postmistress."

"How do you know?"

"Because whoever it was," he said, and his voice went very quiet, very steady, the voice of a man walking toward something he doesn't want to see, "had to love you an awful lot to do something this cruel."

* * *

I made tea because I didn't know what else to do with my hands. He sat in the window seat where Wren does her homework, absurdly too big for it, knees up near his chest, and held the mug I gave him without drinking, and we talked for three hours while the rain came down and the shop grew dark around us and neither of us moved to turn on more than the lamp by the register.

We laid the night out piece by piece, like divers mapping a wreck. His version. My version. Where they matched—her kitchen, the words *I can do this alone*, the door—and where they diverged, wildly, catastrophically, like two roads that had once been the same road.

"You really thought I was rejecting you," I said at one point, and it wasn't a question so much as a wound being re-opened to check for infection.

"What else was I supposed to think?"

"I was twenty-two and terrified! I'd just found out I was pregnant by a man whose entire plan for the future was a boat! I said I could handle it alone because I was trying to sound brave, Jonah, not because I wanted you gone. There's a difference. There's a whole ocean of difference."

"I know that *now*."

"Well, I know that *now* too, and look at us. Two people who spent eleven years building lives on top of a misunderstanding like it was bedrock." I set my mug down harder than necessary. "Do you know what I did, the week after you left? I wrote you another letter. A furious one. Told you exactly what I thought of men who run. Marisol—"

I stopped.

The name sat in the air between us like a struck bell.

Marisol, who had come to my door with her kind terrible eyes. Marisol, who had told me he took the morning boat. Marisol, who had brought me soup every Tuesday for the first year of Wren's life, who had knitted the yellow blanket Wren still slept under, who had stood godmother-adjacent at the edges of my daughter's childhood without ever once asking why Coral never spoke of her son.

Marisol, who had died three weeks ago, whose estate her son was here to settle, whose house was full of boxes that hadn't been opened yet.

"Coral." Jonah had gone very still. "What is it?"

"Nothing." I stood up too fast. "It's late. You should go. The inn on Harbor Road has rooms."

"Coral, what is it?"

"Nothing. It's nothing. It's—" I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "She knew, Jonah. Whatever happened to the letters—she knew. She had to have known. She watched me raise your daughter. She knitted her blankets. She *held her*, and she never once said a word, and either she was part of it or she spent eleven years dying inside, and I don't know which one is worse, and she's dead, and I can't ask her, and you're going to go through her house and find—"

I couldn't finish. Because I didn't know what he'd find. Because whatever it was, it was waiting for him in a shoebox or a drawer or a bundle tied with string, in a house on Cypress Lane, and there was nothing I could do to stop it.

Jonah set down his mug. He crossed the small space between us, and for one disorienting second I thought he was going to touch me, and every nerve in my body woke up after eleven years of sensible sleep—but he didn't touch me. He stopped just short, close enough that I could smell salt and old paper and the particular cold of a man who'd walked in the rain.

"We're going to find out," he said. "Whatever it is. Whoever it was. We're going to open every box in that house together if we have to."

"Together," I repeated. The word tasted foreign. Eleven years of practice and I'd nearly forgotten how to pronounce it.

"Together." He picked up the Neruda from the counter and held it out to me. "Keep this. Consider it a down payment."

"On what?"

"Honestly?" He almost smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that used to make me stupid, worn thin now, rusted at the edges, but recognizable. "I have no idea. On eleven years of silence. On however many letters are sitting in some drawer that nobody ever gave me. On finding out who decided our lives for us." He tucked the book into his jacket. "Goodnight, Coral."

"Goodnight, Jonah."

He let himself out the back, into the rain, and I stood in the dark shop listening to his footsteps fade down the wet street, and I thought about my grandmother's house on the hill, dark now, empty now, full of her furniture and her recipes and her forty years of secrets.

I thought about the letter she must have intercepted, and the ones after it, and the quiet, ferocious love that would have been required.

And I thought: tomorrow he starts on those boxes. Tomorrow the truth comes out of that house whether I'm ready or not.

Tomorrow, one of us is going to have to say her name out loud.
