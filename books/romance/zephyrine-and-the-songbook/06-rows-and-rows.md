## 6. Rows and Rows

The vines did not care that Noor Yarrow had once made a concert hall hold its breath.

Zephyrine understood this by the second morning, watching them stand at the end of Row Fourteen with a pair of secateurs held like something surgical, as though the wood might object to being cut. The sun was already climbing over the Luberon ridge, the mist had burned off the valley floor, and the grapes hung heavy and dusty-blue in their rows, indifferent to reputation. Grenache didn't know about cadenzas. It knew about sun, and water, and whether your hands were steady.

"Like this." Zephyrine reached past Noor's shoulder—close enough to smell the cedar of whatever soap they used, a detail she catalogued with irritation because it meant she'd been close enough to notice—and took the cane in one hand, the blade in the other. "You cut here, above the bud, angled away from it. You're not pruning the plant. You're choosing what it becomes next year."

Noor's jaw tightened. "I'm choosing what it becomes," they repeated, flat, the way someone repeats instructions in a language they resent needing.

"You wanted the consulting job," Zephyrine said. "This is the consulting job."

"I wanted the estate solvent so I could sell it."

"And I want the harvest in before the first frost, so we're both going to be disappointed together." She stepped back, wiped her blade on her jeans, and moved down the row. Behind her, after a long enough pause that she almost turned around, she heard the snick of steel through cane. Then again. Then again, faster, less careful.

Good, she thought. Anger was easier to work with than grief. She'd spent eleven years learning the difference.

* * *

She had taken the contract for reasons she could not say aloud even to herself, which was unlike her; Zephyrine Aubert had always been able to justify anything, had built a whole career on the architecture of defensible decisions. The Lyon position was respectable. The pay was better. The vineyard needed someone who knew old-vine Grenache and Syrah on limestone, and Odile Joubert's blocks were older than either of them, planted in years when the village still argued about electricity.

None of that explained why she'd said yes within four minutes of hearing the name Yarrow.

The call had come on a Tuesday, from a lawyer named Perrin whose voice had the dry patience of a man who handled other people's inheritances the way a dentist handles other people's teeth. *Domaine Joubert, near Vaison-la-Romaine. The heir is Noor Yarrow.* And Zephyrine had stood in her kitchen in Lyon with a cooling cup of coffee and felt eleven years fold up like a map.

Noor. Who had frozen mid-cadenza in front of two thousand people and never played publicly again. Noor, whose silence had ended things between them—or rather, whose silence had *been* the ending, the unanswered letter, the closed door, the whole weather system of rejection she had lived inside since she was twenty-two.

She had believed, for eleven years, that she knew exactly what had happened. That was the thing about believing something: you built your house on it, furnished it, paid rent.

Now she was going to spend six weeks working beside the person who had done it, pruning vines, testing sugar levels, sleeping forty meters from their window across the courtyard. She had told herself this was professional. She had told herself a great many professional things while packing her car.

* * *

By the fourth day she had learned Noor's rhythms the way you learn a piece of music: where they rushed, where they hesitated, which silences were tiredness and which were walls. They worked the morning rows in silence. They ate lunch on the low wall outside the press house, bread and cheese and tomatoes that tasted like actual tomatoes, and spoke only about the fruit. In the afternoon, when the heat pressed down and the rows shimmered, Noor's armor thinned. Not cracked—thinned. Like fabric worn soft.

It was in those hours that Zephyrine began her experiments.

She told herself they were experiments. Small provocations, calibrated, each one a probe sent into the dark to see what echoed back. Because beneath the expensive restraint and the careful posture there was supposed to be a person—the person who, at nineteen, had climbed the scaffolding behind the conservatory practice rooms to leave a peach on her windowsill. The person who had laughed so hard at a bad recital in Avignon that they'd had to leave the hall, both of them wheezing in the stairwell like criminals. The person who wrote *you are the only true thing* in the margin of a score and then underlined it twice.

She wanted to know if that person was dead or merely buried. There was a difference. She needed to know which.

Experiment one: she mispronounced a wine term deliberately—called the *vendange entier* process "whole-bunch fermentation" with an English accent so exaggerated it was practically a costume—and waited. Noor corrected her automatically, precisely, and then, catching the trap a half-second too late, went very still. Their ears went faintly pink.

"Noted," Zephyrine said solemnly. "I'll defer to the expert on all matters of fermentation."

"You're the consultant."

"And you're the heir apparent to a woman who won a medal at the Paris agricultural fair in 1987, so frankly we're both out of our depth."

Something moved at the corner of Noor's mouth. It wasn't a smile. It was the ghost arguing with the door. But it moved, and Zephyrine logged it like a data point, and hated herself a little for how carefully she was logging anything at all.

* * *

Experiment three happened on the sixth day, in Row Nine, and it was not planned, which is how the best ones always worked.

A tractor had come through the neighboring parcel that morning, the Delacroix boy driving it too fast, kicking up a rooster tail of dust that settled over everything like gray snow. By noon they were both filthy, forearms streaked, hair stiff. Zephyrine looked over at Noor—who was crouched under a vine, squinting up into the canopy with a leaf stuck to their cheekbone, looking for all the world like a very elegant child playing at farming—and said, without deciding to say it:

"You have a leaf on your face."

"I know."

"It's been there for an hour."

"I'm aware of its presence," Noor said, with tremendous dignity, not removing it. "We've reached an understanding."

And Zephyrine laughed—a short, surprised bark of a laugh, real, ugly, nothing performed—and Noor looked at her, and for one full second the wall wasn't there. For one full second Zephyrine saw the nineteen-year-old underneath, the one who used to look at her like she was a room with all the lights on, and the sight of it hit her somewhere below the ribs, in the place where she kept the letter she had written and never had answered.

Then the second ended. Noor turned back to the vine. The leaf stayed on their face another hour, out of what Zephyrine suspected was pure spite toward her, and she found she respected that enormously.

* * *

That evening she sat on the stone steps of the guest cottage with her notebook, doing the numbers she was actually being paid to do—crop estimates, acid retention, the question of whether Block Six would make weight before the rain came—and found herself writing other things instead, small observations in the margin like a schoolgirl:

*D. 6 — almost laughed. Actually laughed at me, maybe. Or near it.*

*D. 4 — asked about Odile unprompted. First voluntary sentence of the day.*

*D. 2 — flinched when I hummed. HUMMED. Four notes of the Fauré. Flinched like I'd struck them.*

She stared at that last one for a long time. Everyone in her world had some relationship to music; most people flinched at nothing. But Noor Yarrow hadn't just stopped playing. From everything the papers said, from everything the gossip columns had chewed for a season and spat out, Noor had stopped *hearing*. Refused concerts, refused recordings, refused the radio in cars. A cellist who couldn't bear sound—that wasn't stage fright. Stage fright was a fear of failing. This had looked like a fear of succeeding, which made no sense, unless—

Unless the collapse itself had been the point.

She put the pen down. That thought was a door she wasn't ready to open, and she had learned, expensively, what happened when she walked through doors marked with Noor's name without checking what was behind them.

* * *

The week turned. The grapes sweetened. The mornings grew a degree cooler, and the light came lower and more golden across the rows, and the two of them fell into a wordless choreography that Zephyrine distrusted precisely because it felt so easy—one at the head of the row, one at the foot, meeting in the middle, trading the crate, hands brushing on the handle and neither of them acknowledging it, which was its own kind of acknowledgment.

On the ninth day, she pushed harder.

They were in the far block, the old Syrah, where the vines were gnarled and low and needed hands more than tools. The work was slow. The silence had gone on long enough to become a third presence, and Zephyrine had never in her life met a silence she couldn't provoke.

"Do you remember," she said, eyes on the vine, voice casual as weather, "the summer we drove to Cassis?"

She felt Noor go rigid three meters away. Felt it the way you feel a string detune—not seeing it, knowing it.

"No," Noor said.

"We got lost outside Aubagne. You insisted you knew a shortcut. We ended up in a quarry parking lot eating a baguette that had mostly turned to gravel, and you said—" she paused, savoring it, cruel and tender at once, "—you said, *if this is failure, it tastes better than success ever has.*"

The secateurs stopped. Somewhere up the slope a cicada sawed away at the afternoon, indifferent. When Noor finally spoke, their voice was level in the way that ice is level.

"People change, Zephyrine."

"Yes," she agreed. "That's generally the rumor." She cut a cane, dropped it in the pile. "I just like to verify these things personally. Professional habit. You test the soil before you trust the harvest."

"Is that what I am? Soil?"

"You tell me." She straightened, finally looking at them directly, letting the whole eleven years sit in her gaze without apology. "What are you, Noor? Because I've spent a week trying to figure out what's buried here, and honestly, I can't tell if it's a person or a grave."

The words landed. She watched them land—watched Noor's breath catch, watched their hand tighten on the cane until the knives of tendons stood out on the back of it. For a moment she thought they would answer. For a moment the whole row seemed to lean in, the old vines holding their fruit and their breath.

Then Noor set down the secateurs with exaggerated calm, picked up the crate of cuttings, and said, "Block Six needs checking before the wind picks up," and walked away down the row with their spine straight as a staff.

Zephyrine stood alone among the Syrah and exhaled slowly.

Not a grave, then. Graves don't walk away that fast.

* * *

But it was the tenth day that mattered. The tenth day she would think about later, turning it over like a stone found on a path.

It started stupidly, the way important days do. The irrigation line in Block Three had clogged—old calcium scale, a problem Odile had apparently solved for decades with a length of wire and profanity—and Zephyrine spent the morning elbow-deep in the pump housing while Noor held the flashlight and handed her tools, and somewhere in the middle of it, soaked to the elbows in ditch water, swearing in French and English alternately, she heard a sound behind her.

She turned. Noor was looking at the sky with an expression of profound betrayal.

"What?"

"A bird," Noor said. "It's been imitating my ringtone for ten minutes. I keep checking my phone."

"That's a hoopoe. They do that."

"They do *not*—"

"There's a nest in the plane tree by the well. It's been tormenting Odile's cat for years, apparently. Perrin mentioned it. The cat has developed a complex."

And Noor Yarrow—cellist, recluse, owner of the most controlled face Zephyrine had ever studied—looked at the plane tree, and then at the muddy consultant kneeling in their irrigation ditch, and made a sound.

It wasn't a laugh. It was the first syllable of one, strangled at birth, a single helpless note escaping through the nose before Noor clamped down on it with visible force, pressing their lips together, appalled at themselves. But it had happened. Zephyrine had heard it. Eleven years of silence, and she had heard the first note of a laugh, and it wrecked her more thoroughly than any performance ever had.

"There it is," she said softly.

"There's nothing anywhere." Noor turned away, but not fast enough. "Hand me the wrench."

"You laughed."

"I made a noise. Birds make noises. It's a natural environment."

"You *laughed*, Noor."

"The wrench, Zephyrine."

She handed them the wrench. Their fingers touched around the handle and neither of them pulled away, and the ditch water dripped, and the hoopoe sang its stolen ringtone from the plane tree, and for a moment—just a moment—they stood there in the sun like two people who had never been broken at all.

* * *

That night, Zephyrine lay awake in the cottage listening to the wind move through the vines, and admitted the thing she'd been refusing to admit since Lyon.

She still loved them.

Not the way she had at twenty-two—that had been love as fire, love as certainty, love with a whole unwritten future stacked behind it like cordwood. This was worse. This was love as archaeology: patient, methodical, brushing dirt away from something precious with no guarantee it was intact underneath. She had come here telling herself she was finished, that the letter and the silence had closed the account, that she was a professional taking a professional contract. And instead she was keeping notebooks full of marginalia about Noor's almost-laughs like a lovesick intern.

Eleven years ago, she had written that letter—pages of it, honest past the point of dignity—and sealed it, and mailed it, and waited. And the silence that came back had taught her the lesson she'd built her adult life on: that Noor Yarrow, given the choice, chose the career. Chose Marguerite Vasseur's gilded machine. Chose everything over her.

She believed that. She had furnished a house with it.

So why, she wondered now, staring at the ceiling, did the person in the next building over flinch at four notes of Fauré? Why did they wear their composure like a cast on a limb? Why did they look at the vineyard sometimes—at Odile's rows, at the old house—with an expression that wasn't grief exactly, but recognition, like someone reading a letter addressed to them in handwriting they'd been told to forget?

Why had they really come here?

The wind shifted. Down the slope, in the big house, a light was still burning in what she'd begun to think of as Noor's window—third from the left, the one facing the courtyard. Burning late, night after night. Cataloguing, presumably. Sorting a dead woman's life into boxes labeled *keep* and *sell*.

Zephyrine rolled onto her side.

Tomorrow she would push again. Tomorrow she would find the seam in the armor and worry it, gently, the way water worries stone, because she had six weeks left on this contract and eleven years of questions, and because the person she loved was in there—she'd heard the laugh, she knew it—in there, alive, buried under something that had a shape she was beginning to recognize.

And it was the next morning, coming down the cottage steps early with two coffees, that she saw Noor standing in the open doorway of the cellar, motionless, pale as the milk-fog still lying in the rows—saw the shape of something long and dark and wooden in the shadows behind them, and heard, faintly, from the depths of the earth, the memory of a cello's strings being tuned.
