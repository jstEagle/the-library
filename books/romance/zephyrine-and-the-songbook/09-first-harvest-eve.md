## 9. First Harvest Eve

The village of Saint-Aubin-le-Vieux did not have a square so much as a widening of the road where three streets gave up arguing and became a place. Zephyrine had been there four days when she understood that this was where the village kept its heart: a fountain with a chipped stone nymph, two plane trees older than the republic, and the Café des Vendangeurs, whose awning had faded from red to something like the memory of red.

On harvest eve, the café spilled its tables into the street, and the street filled with people, and the plane trees were strung with paper lanterns that someone—Zephyrine suspected old Bastide from the co-op—had climbed a ladder to hang with the gravity of a man defusing ordnance.

She arrived early, out of habit, and took a table at the edge of the light.

This was not avoidance, she told herself. This was strategy. She had a contract to finish, a crop to bring in, a client she was not thinking about, and the smartest thing she could do was sit where she could see the whole room and no one could see her watching it.

Except that she was watching it. She knew exactly what she was doing.

* * *

Noor Yarrow came down the rue de l'Église at half past eight, and the whole street turned like a field of sunflowers.

Zephyrine saw it happen in real time—the heads lifting, the pause in conversation, the small collective intake of breath that villages reserve for strangers and returning dead. Noor wore what they always wore now: dark clothes, sleeves pushed up, forearms still faintly stained purple from the sorting table. They looked, Zephyrine thought, less like a celebrated cellist than like someone who had been working. Which, for eleven years, was the most radical transformation Zephyrine could imagine for them.

Then Madame Roux got there first.

Roux owned the bakery and had known Odile Joubert for fifty years, and she crossed the street with the unhurried certainty of a woman who has never once been stopped by traffic. She planted herself in front of Noor, looked them up and down, and said something too quiet for Zephyrine to hear.

Noor's face did something complicated. Zephyrine watched it happen: the reflexive composure, the professional mask sliding into place—and then failing, because Madame Roux reached up and took Noor's chin in one flour-dusted hand like they were nine years old, and said something else, and whatever it was, it broke something open.

Noor laughed. Not the polished laugh Zephyrine remembered from conservatory corridors, deployed like currency. A startled, unguarded sound, half a cough, as if the laugh had escaped custody.

And then the street closed around them. Bastide clapped Noor on the shoulder hard enough to stagger them. The mayor's wife pressed a glass of something into their hands before they'd agreed to hold it. Children—actual children—orbited Noor's elbows, and one small girl with a ferocious fringe demanded something, and Noor crouched down to her level, and Zephyrine saw them listen. Really listen. Head tilted, whole attention given, as if a six-year-old's opinion of lanterns were the most important information gathered all day.

*You used to do that,* Zephyrine thought, and then made herself stop thinking it.

Belonging arriving unasked. That was the thing she couldn't look away from. Noor hadn't performed for this welcome, hadn't earned it, hadn't even sought it—they'd spent three weeks being brusque and grief-stricken and half-hidden behind inventory lists, and the village had simply decided, the way villages decide, that Odile's grandchild belonged to them. The welcome wasn't a transaction. It was weather.

Zephyrine had spent eleven years believing she'd been rejected by this person. Watching them be loved by an entire street for free did something corrosive and clarifying to that belief. Because she had seen Noor perform belonging before—at galas, at donor dinners, at Marguerite Vasseur's salons where affection was a line item—and she had never once seen them receive it like this: clumsily, gratefully, with purple hands and no idea what to do with it.

The waiter came. She ordered wine she didn't taste.

* * *

The evening assembled itself around her whether she participated or not. Tables were dragged together until the café's furniture formed one long broken chain down the street. Someone produced an accordion, and someone else produced an argument about the accordion, and eventually both were playing. The food was the kind that exists only in villages on the night before work: enormous, unapologetic, free. Aïoli in a bowl the size of a hubcap. Daube that had been thinking about itself since morning. Bread by the armful, because it was Roux's bread and Roux would personally pursue anyone who left some.

Zephyrine ate alone and told herself she preferred it.

It was almost true. She had built a life on almost-true things: a career teaching teenagers in Lyon who would never love the cello the way she'd loved it, a flat near the park that she kept immaculate because mess reminded her of wanting things, a reputation for being excellent and slightly cold, which was easier than being excellent and wounded. She had gotten very good at tables at the edge of rooms.

But then Noor looked up.

Across forty feet of lantern light and accordion noise, across eleven years and one unread letter, Noor Yarrow found her the way water finds a low place—without decision, without effort, just gravity. And held her gaze for one second too long to be accidental, and one second too short to mean anything she could act on.

Zephyrine raised her glass an inch. A neutral gesture. Diplomatic. The gesture of a consulting vintner acknowledging a client at a public event.

Noor started toward her.

Panic arrived, fully formed, wearing its oldest clothes. Zephyrine looked down at her plate with tremendous interest, rearranging a fork, buying herself three seconds, and by the time she looked up Noor had been intercepted by Bastide, who wanted to discuss the parcel above the chapel and whether the Syrah in it was ready, and Zephyrine felt relief and disappointment arrive simultaneously, twins, indistinguishable.

*You are thirty-four years old,* she informed herself. *You survived Marguerite Vasseur's entire apparatus without knowing you were surviving it. You can survive one dinner.*

But the letter sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Eleven years ago she had written it—she could still recite it, which was its own humiliation—and she had walked to the post office in Lyon in the rain, and she had posted it, and she had waited. And the silence that came back had been so complete, so total, that she had done the only reasonable thing: she had believed it. Believed Noor had read every word and chosen the career, chosen Marguerite, chosen the gilded cage over the girl with the bad car and the scholarship and the plan to drive south someday.

Three weeks ago she'd taken this contract partly out of spite—a private, shameful spite, the desire to stand in front of the person who'd discarded her and be indispensable, be brilliant, be fine. She'd told the agency it was because the Joubert vineyard was a technically interesting challenge. Both things were true. That was the trouble with her; everything was always two things.

And now here was Noor, crouched in the street listening to a child, looking like someone had let them out of somewhere.

*If they were engineered into that cage,* whispered a thought she'd been refusing for days, *then who engineered me out?*

She drank her wine and didn't answer.

* * *

Later, the dancing started.

It was not really dancing; it was the village occupying space rhythmically. Bastide's grandson played guitar badly and with total conviction. Couples who had been married forty years danced the way old trees lean—slowly, inevitably, having long since stopped deciding to. The lanterns swung. Somewhere a dog sang along.

Zephyrine stayed at her table through two songs, hating herself with great efficiency, and then Noor appeared beside her chair.

"Madame Roux says I have to dance," they said. "She said it's not optional. She said Odile never missed a harvest dance and neither would I."

"And do you always obey bakers?"

"No." Noor's mouth did something at the corner—not quite a smile, more like the ground where a smile might build. "I'm told I'm very bad at obedience. Historically."

The word landed between them with a weight Noor couldn't know it carried. Zephyrine set down her glass carefully, because her hand wanted to shake and she refused to permit it.

"I don't dance," she said.

"I know," Noor said. "I remember."

Two words. Four syllables. And Zephyrine's whole careful architecture wobbled, because *I remember* meant there was a shared archive, meant the conservatory rooftop party in second year when Zephyrine had stood against the wall all night with a cup of warm punch and Noor had come to keep her company without asking why, meant that the archive existed on both sides, intact, catalogued.

"The music's terrible," Zephyrine said.

"It is," Noor agreed. "That's rather the point."

They stood there in the lantern light, and the distance between the table and the dancers was perhaps six meters, and it might as well have been the width of the Rhône. Zephyrine could smell the crush on them—grape must and sulfur dust and underneath it something clean, soap maybe, the ordinary human smell of a person who had showered after work. Eleven years ago she'd have catalogued that scent as home. She made herself breathe normally.

"One dance," Noor said. "For Odile. You're here for her vineyard. It's practically contractual."

"You're abusing your position as client."

"Flagrantly."

And God help her, Zephyrine stood up.

* * *

They were terrible at it, which saved them. Noor danced the way they presumably did everything now—carefully, slightly out of practice, watching their own hands as if the hands might defect. Zephyrine had two left feet and no patience. They collided twice, apologized twice, and on the third collision Noor said "sorry" and Zephyrine said "stop apologizing" and Noor laughed—that same startled laugh from earlier, the escaped-custody laugh—and something in Zephyrine's chest cracked along a fault line she'd thought was fully healed scar tissue.

The song changed. Slower. The accordion player, sensing either romance or an audience, leaned into it shamelessly.

And then they weren't colliding anymore. They were close. Closer than the dance required, closer than the contract allowed, and Noor's hand settled at her waist with a hesitation so profound it was nearly a question—*may I, is this, are we*—and Zephyrine, who had rehearsed cruelty for this moment for a decade, who had planned to be ice and invoices, put her own hand on Noor's shoulder and did not remove it.

The lanterns turned overhead. The village danced around them, uninterested, mercifully incurious. Noor smelled of the harvest. Their eyes in the paper light were the color they'd always been, dark amber, wine-colored, and Zephyrine thought: *I am going to ruin everything, I am going to kiss them in front of the entire village and the letter will come up and the truth will come up and it will be a disaster*—

—and she found she didn't care. That was the terrifying part. Eleven years of grievance, and one slow song, and the grievance was losing.

Noor's head tipped down. Hers tipped up. The space between them went soft and charged, the last centimeter, the point of no return—

A phone buzzed.

Not hers. She felt Noor feel it—a vibration through the hand at her shoulder, through the whole body, a small electric shudder. Noor went rigid. The eyes that had been half-closing against her opened all the way, and Zephyrine watched the lantern light go out of them like a lamp turned down.

"Don't," Zephyrine said quietly. It came out before she could decide whether she had the right to say it.

"I have to." Noor's voice was flat again, the performance reassembling itself piece by piece, and Zephyrine hated how fast it happened, how practiced. "It could be the lawyer. The estate—"

"At ten o'clock at night?"

"Lawyers don't sleep. That's why they charge so much." The joke landed wrong, airless. Noor stepped back. The absence of their hand at her waist was a specific coldness, a shape drawn on her body in negative. "I'm sorry. One minute."

They turned away to answer it, and Zephyrine stood alone in the middle of the dancers, heart slamming, dignity in ruins, and heard—because the music had a gap, because the universe was cruel in an organized way—one side of a phone call.

"Noor Yarrow." A pause. "Marguerite."

The name went through Zephyrine like cold water through cloth.

She hadn't heard that name spoken aloud in years, but her body recognized it instantly, the way a dog recognizes a whistle pitched below hearing. *Marguerite.* The tower of the conservatory. The office that smelled of lilies and ambition. The woman who had once, in Zephyrine's third year, looked at her across a desk and said, *You have talent, Aubert, but talent needs protecting from itself,* and had thereafter made sure Zephyrine's auditions were scheduled against impossible conflicts, that her scholarship renewals came back flagged, that every door she reached for had already been quietly locked by a hand she never saw.

She had blamed bad luck for years. Then she'd blamed herself. It had never once occurred to her to blame Marguerite Vasseur, because Marguerite's cruelty was upholstered, invisible, deniable—because Marguerite only ever destroyed people through paperwork.

"No, I haven't signed anything." Noor's back was to her, shoulders squared, voice pitched low and pleasant—the client voice, the student voice. "It's harvest, Marguerite. I've told you, I'll be here through—" Pause. "That's not—" Pause, longer. "I understand. Of course. Yes. I'll call you tomorrow."

The call ended. Noor stood motionless for a moment, phone still in hand, a silhouette against the lanterns, and Zephyrine watched them press the heel of their free hand briefly against their sternum—an unconscious gesture, self-bracing, the gesture of someone keeping something caged inside their own ribs.

Then they turned, and the smile they put back on was flawless and dead, and Zephyrine understood suddenly, completely, with the clarity that arrives only at the worst moments, that she was looking at a person who had been trained. Trained the way she herself had been trained, by the same trainer, to smile while being leashed.

"Sorry," Noor said. "Where were we?"

Zephyrine looked at them—at the smile, at the hand that had almost touched her face, at the leash lying slack and gleaming in the lantern light—and every question she had been swallowing for three weeks rose at once. *Why did you never write back? What did she say to you? What did she take?*

What she said was: "We were finishing the dance."

And she stepped back into Noor's arms, and let the song carry them, and under the turning lanterns she smiled her own flawless, dead smile, and began—quietly, methodically, with the patience of a woman who had waited eleven years—to plan how she was going to find out everything.
