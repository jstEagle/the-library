## 18. The Songbook's Last Page

The songbook had been sitting on Noor's desk for three days, and they had been avoiding it for all of them.

It was the one thing left in Odile's house that they hadn't catalogued, hadn't photographed for the lawyer, hadn't touched except to move it from the shelf in her bedroom to the desk by the window where the morning light came in slantwise over the vines. A slim green volume, cloth-bound, its spine cracked into pale threads. *Chansons populaires de Provence*, printed in 1961, the paper gone soft as cloth. Zephyrine's letter had come out of its hollowed center eleven years ago; Noor had put the book back on a shelf in Lyon and never opened it again until now.

But that wasn't quite true either, and Noor was trying, these days, to stop lying to themselves in small ways first. The truth was they had carried the songbook through four apartments in three cities. They had never unpacked it anywhere. It lived at the bottom of a box marked MISC., which was as close as Noor could get to throwing away a thing they couldn't explain why they kept.

Now it sat in the light, and outside the window the rows ran gold-green toward the horizon, heavy with fruit, two days from harvest, and Noor sat down across from it like sitting down across from a person.

"Fine," they said aloud, to the empty room, because talking to objects was apparently something they did now. "Let's see what else you're hiding."

* * *

They went through it properly this time. Not the way they had at twenty-four, hands shaking, pulling out the letter like a splinter and slamming the covers shut before anything else could escape. Now they turned every page. They read the songs they'd half-remembered from childhood summers — the ones Odile used to hum while she pruned, off-key and unbothered, her secateurs keeping time. *La Marcho dei Rei.* The May song. The one about the girl who goes down to the river and comes back changed.

Noor's own handwriting appeared in the margins — they'd forgotten this too, or buried it: a child's careful pencil marks, age eight or nine, sounding out the words phonetically, *la marcho dei rei* spelled with an i because no one had corrected them. On the facing page, in a different hand entirely, rounder, more confident, someone had written a translation. Odile's hand. Noor knew it now from the ledgers, from the labels on the old bottles in the cellar. She had translated the songs for a visiting grandchild who didn't yet speak Provençal, and Noor had never known, had filed those summers under *nothing important*, had let Marguerite's version of their childhood overwrite the actual one: the summers were a distraction, Odile was a difficult woman, the vineyard was a burden you'd escaped.

Page by page, the book gave up its small archaeology. A pressed sprig of lavender, brown as old blood, marking the harvest song. A grocery list on the flyleaf — *oil, yeast, stamps* — in Odile's hand. And then, near the end, the last page before the back cover, Noor felt it before they saw it.

A thickness. A wrongness in the binding.

They set the book flat on the desk and ran their thumb along the inside edge of the back cover, where the cloth met the endpaper. There was a ridge there, a seam that shouldn't exist, faint as a scar. For a long moment Noor just looked at it. Eleven years ago they had been too young and too shattered to look closely at anything. They weren't either of those things anymore.

They got the small knife from the kitchen drawer — the one Zephyrine used for grafting, actually, borrowed without asking, which would have been unthinkable a month ago — and slid the blade along the seam.

The endpaper lifted. Beneath it, folded into a recess cut into the board itself, deliberate, patient, waiting: an envelope. Heavy cream stock, unsealed. On the front, in Odile's upright hand:

*For Noor. When they come home.*

* * *

Noor did not open it immediately. That surprised them. They sat with the envelope in both hands and felt their own pulse in their fingertips, that old performer's awareness of the body as an instrument being played by something larger, and they thought: *this is what the silence felt like. This exact weight. The moment before the downbeat when you know whatever comes next will divide your life in two.*

Outside, a tractor coughed somewhere down the valley. The light moved. A bee bumped against the glass twice and gave up.

They opened it.

The letter inside was dated fourteen months before Odile's death. Her handwriting was steadier than Noor expected, though the lines drifted slightly downward toward the bottom of each page, the way writing does when the hand tires.

*My Noor,*

*If you are reading this, then you came home after all, and later than I hoped but sooner than I feared. I have left instructions everywhere — in the ledger, in the cellar, in this book, which I hollowed myself with a razor blade and a great deal of swearing, so if you found nothing else, know that your grandmother swore like a vintner and meant it as a compliment.*

*I am going to tell you what I know, and I am sorry for how long I have known it, and sorrier still that I chose to wait rather than act. You may not forgive me. I have made my peace with that. Old women are allowed one cowardice.*

*Eleven years ago — you will know the date better than I — a woman came here. Elegant. Parisian shoes ruined in my mud, and she wore them anyway, which told me everything about her before she said a word. She said her name was Vasseur. She said she was calling about you.*

*She sat at my table and drank my wine and told me a story. That you were fragile. That there was a girl — she named no names, but she described her well enough that I knew — who had been bad for you. That letters were coming to my house because the girl believed you were here, and that for your sake, for your career, for your *protection*, those letters should not reach you. She asked me to hold the mail. She offered me money.*

*I want you to understand that I refused her.*

*I want you to understand also that I understood her perfectly, because I had seen her kind before. In 1954 a man came to this valley buying up vineyards from widows at half their worth, and he wore the same face — the face of a person doing you a favor. So I said yes to her face, and I wrote to the postal clerk in Rasteau, Marcel, whose mother I had nursed through the winter of '62, and I paid him — my own money, never hers — to keep copies of everything that passed through addressed to you, and to send them to me. And I hid them. All of them. Because I did not know then what I know now, which is that I was dying slower than I thought, and that you would not come home while she still had her hooks in you, and that the truth would keep better than grapes.*

*There is a tin in the cellar, behind the third barrel from the door, under the false floorboard. Everything is there. The letters the girl wrote you — there were nine, Noor. Nine. Copies of the money order Vasseur sent to Marcel, which he returned to her, untouched, because he was an honest man and I had asked him only to watch, not to take. The hotel receipt from Avignon where she stayed two nights. The note she left me, written in her own hand, saying she trusted my discretion. I kept it all. I am not a sentimental woman, but I am a thorough one, and I knew that someday someone would need to be thorough on your behalf.*

*Here is the part I cannot make gentle. I believe she broke you. I believe the concert — the one everyone talks about, the one you will not speak of — was not your failure. I have watched musicians my whole life, my dear, and I married one, and I know what fear looks like from the stage and what sabotage looks like from the wings. A person does not simply stop. But a person can be stopped — worn down, isolated, convinced that their own hands belong to someone else. She spent ten years building a cage and calling it care. And when the girl threatened the cage, she removed the girl, and then she waited for you to break, and when you broke she called it proof that she had been right all along.*

*I do not ask you to believe me. I ask you to look in the tin. Then I ask you to decide what you are going to do about it.*

*And one more thing, since I am dead and allowed honesty: I left you this house not because I loved you most — though I did — but because I thought you might need a place to stand when the ground gave way. Land is truth, Noor. It doesn't flatter you and it doesn't lie. The vines come up every spring whether anyone applauds or not. I hoped you would learn that here, if you ever came.*

*Come home. You did. Good boy. Now finish it.*

*Your grandmother,
Odile Yarrow, née Aubert*

*P.S. The girl's name is Aubert too. I checked. I am thorough. Whatever you decide, decide quickly — life is short and harvest waits for no one.*

* * *

Noor read it twice. Then a third time, standing up without noticing they'd stood, the pages held at arm's length as if distance might change the words.

*Née Aubert.*

They looked at the name for a long time. Odile Yarrow, née Aubert. Their grandmother had been born an Aubert. Which meant — and the arithmetic arrived slowly, stupidly, one obvious step at a time, the way the largest truths always did —

Zephyrine Aubert. From Rasteau. Whose family had farmed the next valley over, whose grandmother and Odile had, according to the village women at the festival, been inseparable once, *like sisters*. Who had grown up hearing the same songs. Who had come to this vineyard as a child the way Noor had, and learned these same pages, and —

Noor sat down again, hard, the chair scraping.

It wasn't a coincidence. None of it had ever been a coincidence. Two children from two branches of the same scattered family, thrown together at Odile's table summer after summer, sharing a songbook, sharing a language neither of them fully spoke, falling into the particular gravity of people who recognize each other. And Marguerite had known. Of course Marguerite had known. Marguerite knew everything; it was her profession. And she had come here, to this kitchen, in her ruined Parisian shoes, and called it protection.

Nine letters. There had been nine. Noor had found one — the last one, the desperate one, the one that ended *please, just tell me you're alive* — and had built an entire decade on top of it. One letter, unanswered, and Noor had concluded: *she stopped. She gave up. I was not worth the trouble.* Eight more had been sitting in a tin behind a barrel forty meters below this room the whole time.

And the money order returned. Returned untouched. Meaning Zephyrine had never taken a centime — meaning whatever Marguerite had told Noor afterward, whatever version of events had been fed to them in that velvet voice (*she took the settlement, Noor, she moved on, she's happier, some people aren't built for our life*) — had been a fabrication stitched from whole cloth. Zephyrine hadn't sold them out. Zephyrine had been punished for them. Dropped auditions, lost scholarships — Noor remembered the fragments Zephyrine had let slip across the harvest table, casual as passing salt, and now every fragment snapped into place like notes resolving into a chord.

The rage came up through Noor's chest slowly, coldly, nothing like the hot flash they'd expected. It felt more like tuning. Like every string of their life being loosened and retightened to a pitch they'd never been allowed to hear.

*She broke you,* Odile had written. *I believe the concert was not your failure.*

Fourteen months before her death, Odile had written that. Had known it. Had hidden the proof and died with it, trusting a grandchild she'd barely seen to eventually come home and dig. And Noor had nearly sold the place. Had stood in the notary's office with papers drawn up, had priced the vines per hectare like inventory, had almost handed the truth to strangers to be bulldozed into holiday rentals.

The tin. Behind the third barrel. Under the false floorboard.

Noor was moving before they finished the thought — down the stairs, through the kitchen, out into the late afternoon, the gravel hot through their shoes, the cellar door cool under their hand. Inside it smelled of earth and oak and the ghost of last year's crush. Third barrel from the door. They knelt. The floorboard came up easier than it should have, oiled recently, maintained — Zephyrine must have found it during the inventory, must have puzzled over it, must have left it, respecting a mystery that wasn't hers to open.

The tin was a biscuit tin, rusted at the corners, heavier than it looked.

Inside: eight letters in Zephyrine's handwriting, postmarked across eighteen months, each one a little shorter than the last, hope thinning page by page like a candle burning down. A bank slip showing a returned payment. A hotel folio from Avignon. And a note on cream cardstock, in a hand Noor knew better than their own — Marguerite's hand, the hand that had written *bravo* on a thousand programs and *again* on a thousand practice-room doors:

*Mme Yarrow — Thank you for your discretion. Some loves are a luxury our artists cannot afford. I trust we understand each other. — M.V.*

Noor knelt on the cellar floor with the evidence spread around them like cards from a deck, and above them, faintly, they heard footsteps crossing the kitchen — Zephyrine, back from the north rows, calling their name, the sound coming down the stairwell and landing among the letters like light.

They gathered everything up. Every page. They were not going to hide in a cellar with this. They were done with cellars, done with hollowed books, done with truths that waited politely in the dark for permission.

But halfway up the stairs, envelope in hand, Noor stopped. Because there was one thing Odile's letter hadn't answered, one question the old woman — thorough, magnificent, swearing-like-a-vintner Odile — had somehow not anticipated:

Marguerite had come here eleven years ago. And Marguerite had called yesterday, silk-voiced, pressing about the foundation, the tour, the comeback — and when Noor had mentioned the vineyard, Marguerite had said, smooth as poured wine, *"That place was always beneath you, darling."*

Beneath them. As if she knew it. As if she'd stood in its mud herself.

She was coming. Noor understood it suddenly, completely, the way you understand a modulation two bars before it lands. Marguerite Vasseur did not leave loose threads, and Noor had just spent a week pulling at them — the lawyer's questions, the ledger, the neighbors' long memories. Somewhere between Lyon and this valley, silk was already being packed.

Noor climbed the rest of the stairs, into the kitchen light, where Zephyrine stood with dirt on her hands and a question already forming on her face — and held out the tin like an offering, like a confession, like a downbeat.

"We need to talk," Noor said. "All of it. Right now. Before she gets here."

Zephyrine went very still. "Before *who* gets here?"

Noor set the tin on the table between them, next to the songbook with its split binding, its last page finally empty of secrets.

"My grandmother," Noor said, "was born an Aubert. And Marguerite is on her way."
