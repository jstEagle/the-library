## 21. Vendange

**Noor**

The trucks came before dawn.

Noor heard them from the window of Odile's bedroom—the room they'd slept in since the storm, since everything changed—headlights sweeping the courtyard walls like slow searchlights. By the time they got downstairs, the village had already arrived: tractors idling in the lane, crates stacked in pyramids against the press house, and forty-some people moving through the blue half-light with the easy efficiency of people who had done this every September of their lives.

"You're late," said Marcel Bonnet, handing them a pair of secateurs as if conferring a knighthood. "Vendange waits for no one. Not even cellists."

"I'm not—"

"You are now." He nodded toward the rows, where Zephyrine stood at the head of the first vine already, hair tied back, sleeves rolled, a crate at her feet. She didn't look over. She didn't have to. Noor felt her awareness of them the way you feel a low note through a floor.

They walked out together into the vines.

* * *

Picking, Noor discovered within the first hour, was nothing like playing. It was worse and better. The shears wanted precision; the grapes wanted judgment—a twist of the wrist, a reading of the skin's bloom, a refusal of anything underripe or split. Their hands, which had once frozen in front of two thousand people, moved without permission now, cutting and passing, cutting and passing, and somewhere around the fourth row Noor realized they had stopped monitoring themselves entirely.

"Your left hand is better than your right," Zephyrine said beside them, not looking up. "You favor it. You always did."

"You've been watching my hands all morning?"

"I've been watching your hands for twenty years." She dropped a cluster into the crate. "It's a hard habit to break."

Noor let that sit between them like warm air. Eleven days ago—was it only eleven?—they had told her everything: the letter, the hollowed songbook, Marguerite's voice on the recording admitting, in her too-fluent way, to details only the letter could have supplied. Zephyrine had listened with her whole body still, and then she had said, *I don't forgive you yet,* and then, after a long moment, *but I believe you.* It was not absolution. It was something rarer: a door left unlocked.

"Row six is yours," Marcel called down the alley. "The old lady's favorites."

Odile's favorites. The vines nearest the house, planted—according to the ledger—in 1974, the year she'd bought the place with money nobody in the family ever explained. Noor cut the first cluster and held it up to the graying sky. The grapes were small, thick-skinned, dark as bruise.

"Grenache," Zephyrine said. "She'd start every tasting with these. I found her notes in the cellar office—she called this row *le cœur*. The heart." A pause. "Sentimental woman."

"You liked her."

"I never met her." Zephyrine's shears snipped. "I liked her handwriting."

By nine the sun had cleared the ridge and the heat came up off the stones, and the village worked in its rhythms—Marcel shouting about sugar levels, his wife Pascale distributing watered wine nobody drank until ten, children running the rows with empty crates they filled mostly with grapes they ate. Noor's back ached by eleven in a way that felt honest, earned, entirely unlike the ache of a concert hall chair. When they straightened to stretch, they caught Zephyrine watching them—not their hands this time, their face—and neither of them looked away, and neither of them said anything, and it was somehow the most complete conversation they'd had in weeks.

At noon the whole operation broke for lunch at long tables under the plane trees, and Noor sat among farmers and their grandchildren and the postal clerk's daughter—who avoided their eyes, still, after what the ledger had revealed about her father—and ate daube and bread and tomatoes that tasted like actual tomatoes, and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that this was the thing Marguerite Vasseur had spent twenty years making sure they would never want.

Because wanting it meant needing nothing from her.

* * *

**Zephyrine**

The press house smelled of must and cold stone and, by mid-afternoon, of fruit—hundreds of kilos of Grenache going through the old vertical press Odile had refused, on principle, to replace with anything pneumatic. Zephyrine had pressed grapes in three countries and knew every modern efficiency, and none of them produced what this stubborn machine produced: juice with a kind of gravity, a seriousness. She ran the press while Noor hauled crates, and the pink-gold must ran down into the vat, and she thought: *this is what eleven years should have tasted like.*

Not the Lyon conservatory, where she'd taught teenagers who feared her because she was famous for being passed over. Not the dropped auditions, the rescinded scholarships, the quiet phone calls Marguerite Vasseur had made to half the classical music world—*Zephyrine Aubert is difficult, Zephyrine Aubert is unstable, best to wait a season*—a season that became a decade. She'd pieced that together from Noor's confession and her own memory, the pattern snapping into focus like a score finally read correctly. Every closed door had had the same elegant hand behind it.

And still. And still the woman who did it had come here, to this valley, and picked grapes beside her, and laughed yesterday when the hail netting collapsed on both of them, soaked and swearing, and Zephyrine had thought: *oh no. Oh no, it's happening anyway.*

"Noor!" Marcel appeared in the doorway, waving an envelope like a man waving down a plane. "The notary's clerk. Papers for the signature—you said today, before the fête, yes?"

Noor set down the crate. Wiped their hands on their jeans with unnecessary care, and Zephyrine realized they were stalling, and realized why, and set down her press bar.

"I'll come," she said.

* * *

They signed at the kitchen table, the same table where the letter had been read aloud, where the ledger had lain open like a wound. The clerk spread the documents: the withdrawal of the sale listing, the transfer of full title, the business registration in two names—because Noor had insisted, three days ago, standing in this exact spot, that the estate be registered jointly if Zephyrine would agree to stay past the crush. *Past the crush,* Zephyrine had repeated. *That's a very specific horizon.* And Noor had said, *it's the only one I'm asking for.*

Now the pen hovered.

"The tour contract," the clerk said gently, "requires a separate letter of declination. To Madame Vasseur's foundation. You're certain?"

Zephyrine watched Noor's hand. The hand that had frozen mid-cadenza in front of two thousand people. The hand that had played four bars alone in a dry cellar at midnight, shaking. It was steady on the table.

"She'll call," Noor said. "When the story breaks properly. She'll call and offer me the world again, and it will be a cage with better lighting." They signed the declination first—three strokes of the pen, quick, like pulling a splinter—and then the title papers, slower, and then slid both across the table. "There. I own a vineyard I don't know how to run."

"You know how to run one now," Zephyrine said. "Badly. But you know how."

The clerk gathered the pages, wished them luck with a formality that had gone soft at the edges—he'd known Odile, everyone here had known Odile—and left. The kitchen was suddenly very quiet. Through the window, the last light lay along the rows like poured honey.

"Joint registration," Zephyrine said, when she trusted her voice. "That's a legal document, Noor. That's not *past the crush.*"

"It's whatever you want it to be." Noor turned from the window. In the amber light their face was open in a way Zephyrine had learned to distrust and was learning, slowly, at great cost, to trust again. "I spent eleven years believing I chose my cage. Then eleven days finding out it was built around me. I'm not going to build one around you. So—it's whatever you decide. After the crush. After the bottles. Whenever."

Zephyrine looked at the signature drying on the page. Two names, side by side, above the name that had started everything: *Domaine Odile Yarrow.*

"Come on," she said. "The press won't run itself. And there's a fête tonight, and you owe the entire village a speech, which should be excellent."

"I hate speeches."

"I know," she said, and handed them their jacket. "It's one of my favorite things about you."

* * *

**Noor**

The first bottle came off the line at dusk.

It wasn't much of a line—Pascale's cousin with a hand filler, Marcel working the corker like a man defusing bombs, Zephyrine applying labels with a precision that made Noor suspect she'd labeled things before, carefully, in another life. The wine was young and rough and would need months in the vat before bottling proper, but Odile's ledger had recorded a tradition: one bottle drawn off on vendange day, corked green, drunk at the harvest festival in honor of the dead. Marcel had insisted. *The old lady would haunt us otherwise,* he'd said, and nobody had laughed, because everyone knew he was right.

The label was simple cream stock, and Zephyrine had printed it herself that afternoon on the ancient printer in the cellar office:

**DOMAINE ODILE YARROW — VENDANGE**
*Cuvée du Cœur — Première Récolte*

Below it, small: *Elle revient à la maison.* She comes home.

Noor held the bottle under the cellar lamp and could not speak.

"It's just a label," Zephyrine said quietly, beside them.

"No, it isn't."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

The festival took over the square the way festivals do—lanterns strung tree to tree, tables dragged out of every kitchen in the village, the band setting up under the plane trees with an accordion player who had clearly been waiting all year for this. Someone had put flowers in Odile's honor on the fountain's rim. Children were already feral in the best way. The green-corked bottle sat at the center of the head table like a relic.

Noor gave the speech. It was four sentences long. The village applauded anyway, warmly, the way you applaud someone who has stopped performing and started belonging, and Marcel toasted *à la petite Yarrow*—to little Yarrow, though Noor was nearly forty—and Noor drank, and the wine was rough and alive and tasted like the press house and the storm and the four bars played alone at midnight.

Later, when the band hit its stride and the dancing started, Zephyrine pulled them to the edge of the square, into the lantern shadow between the fountain and the church wall.

"Look at them," she said. The whole village turned in the lantern light, sweat and dust and joy. "Eleven years ago I was in Lyon thinking you'd chosen all this over me. The halls, the career, her." Her jaw tightened on the pronoun. "And you were in a gilded cage being told you'd chosen nothing at all. We were both wrong about everything except each other."

"That's very generous. I was also a coward about the letter for a week."

"A week. I'm counting it." But she was smiling, and she turned to face them fully, and the accordion swelled into something slow, and Noor thought: *now, maybe now—*

"Before you say anything," Zephyrine said, "there's something you should see. I found it this morning, in the cellar office, behind Odile's tasting notes. I was going to show you tomorrow but—" she exhaled. "After tonight, after the signing, you should have it now."

She reached into her jacket pocket and brought out an envelope. Old, brittle, addressed in a hand Noor knew intimately by now—Odile's hand—to *Mlle Z. Aubert, Conservatoire de Lyon.*

Unsent. Postmarked by nothing. Never posted at all.

"Odile wrote to me?" Zephyrine's voice was careful. "Eleven years ago? What—Noor, why would she write to me and never send it?"

Noor took the envelope. The paper whispered. Inside, a single page, folded twice, and even before unfolding it they saw the date in the corner, and the blood went strange in their ears.

Dated the day after the collapse.

*Chère Mademoiselle Aubert — I know what Marguerite Vasseur did to my grandchild, and I know what she will do to you. Come to me. I can protect you both, but only if you come before she—*

The rest was torn away. Ripped cleanly, deliberately, a strip of paper missing from the bottom third of the page, as if someone had removed the final lines—the address, perhaps, or the instructions, or the proof.

"Zephyrine." Noor's voice came out very flat. "This was in the songbook's binding. With her letter. Someone tore it."

They looked at each other across the lantern light, the music swelling, the village laughing, and both of them arrived at the same thought at the same moment, and Zephyrine said it aloud:

"Marguerite never came here for the mail." Her eyes had gone bright and hard. "She came here for *this.*"
