## 7. Mothers and Militias

The report reached Ilsa Voss at 0400, which was the hour the settlement kept for bad news. She had learned that over thirty years of command: good news came at supper, delivered with the bread and the gossip; bad news came in the dark, carried by someone who had been standing outside your door working up the nerve to knock.

She opened the door before Corporal Adeyemi-Okafor — no relation to anyone important, she reminded herself, though the name still snagged something in her chest every time — could finish raising his fist.

"Commander. It's the Okonkwo children."

"There are no Okonkwo children," Ilsa said, because it was true and because saying true things sometimes bought you another minute before the world changed.

"The dreamers, ma'am. All eight of them woke up at the same time. Same minute. Parents are saying they were talking in their sleep. In unison." He swallowed. "Saying numbers."

Ilsa dressed without turning her back on him, an old habit from the rebellion years when the people closest to you were the ones most likely to put a bolt through it. She buckled the sidearm out of ritual rather than expectation; nobody had shot at her in eleven years, and the pistol's charge cell had probably degraded past usefulness anyway. But a commander of New Meridian did not walk into a crisis unarmed, even if the only enemy was the dark.

"Numbers," she repeated.

"Yes, ma'am. Ama Ekwueme's mother wrote them down before the girl stopped. Nine digits, repeating."

"What do they decode to?"

"That's the thing, Commander. We ran them through the old cipher wheels from the war. They don't decode to anything." He hesitated, and Ilsa watched him decide whether to say the rest. "They decode to coordinates. The vault."

* * *

By dawn she had seen all eight children, or as much of them as their frightened parents would allow, which meant she stood in doorways while mothers hovered and fathers held onto small shoulders as if the wind might take them.

They looked ordinary. That was the worst part. Ilsa had spent her life reading faces for the lie underneath — she'd interrogated loyalists and collaborators and once, briefly and disastrously, her own brother — and these children showed her nothing but the blank exhaustion of kids who hadn't slept properly in two weeks. Two weeks. That was how long the dreams had been going on, it turned out, once she started asking instead of being told. Two weeks of a garden growing inside a star, shared across eight households that had nothing in common except proximity to the mountain.

Except one thing more, she realized, sitting in the schoolroom with the attendance ledger open in front of her. Proximity to the mountain, and age. Every dreamer had been born after the Silence. Not one child who remembered Earth dreamed the dream. Not one adult.

Whatever it was, it was speaking a language only the post-Silence generation could hear.

"Show me what they say," she told Teacher Nkechi, a gray-braided woman who had taught three generations of New Meridian's children and whose hands shook only slightly as she passed over a slate covered in chalk.

The words were always the same, Nkechi said. *The garden is planted. The garden is waiting. Come and see.* And then the numbers. And then, last night for the first time, a new phrase, spoken by all eight children simultaneously at 0357:

*The door is not locked.*

Ilsa read it four times. Outside the schoolroom window, the mountain sat under its morning haze, the vault a gray tooth in its flank, and thirty years of silence pressing down on it like snow.

Her daughter had been born six years after the Silence. Her daughter was fourteen now, and had not dreamed the dream, and Ilsa found herself absurdly, shamefully grateful, and then furious at herself for the gratitude, because it meant she had already decided the other children were the ones in danger, and decisions made that fast were made badly.

"Nobody talks about this outside this room," she said. "No transmissions. No notes. If the fleet is listening—"

"Is the fleet listening?" Nkechi asked quietly. "After thirty years?"

That was the question, wasn't it. Ilsa folded the slate into her jacket like it might burn through the fabric.

"I'm going up the mountain," she said. "And until I know what's up there, the dreamers stay where their parents can see them. For their protection."

Nkechi looked at her the way teachers look at soldiers, which is to say with a patience that felt like judgment. "Protection from what, Commander?"

Ilsa didn't have an answer yet. She hated how honest her silence was.

* * *

The climb took four hours and she made it alone, which her militia captain, Dagny Reyes-Holt — everyone on this rock was named for somebody dead or somebody famous, it was how colonists mourned — had called insane and then volunteered for anyway, and whom Ilsa had ordered to stay behind with a single sentence: *"If I don't come back, seal the settlement and don't let anyone follow me."*

Dagny had understood. That was the inheritance of the rebellion: everyone over forty knew how to be left behind, and how to leave.

The vault's outer door was exactly as she remembered it from the one other time she'd come here, twenty-two years ago, as part of the delegation that told Bram Okonkwo the settlement would no longer send supplies. She had been younger then, and angrier, and the Archivist had stood in his doorway with dust on his sleeves and accepted the news the way a man accepts weather. She had hated him a little for that calm. She had thought about him more than she'd ever admitted, all these years. The last man who talked to Earth, talking to no one.

Today the door was already open a hand's width.

Her sidearm was out before she'd finished deciding to draw it. She pushed through into the entry chamber, boots loud on the grating, and the smell hit her first — cold stone, machine oil, and underneath it something organic and faintly sweet, ten million seeds breathing out their slow chemistry in the dark.

"Bram Okonkwo," she called. "Commander Ilsa Voss. I'm coming in. Don't make me shoot a seventy-year-old man."

"No danger of that," came the voice from deeper in, unhurried, familiar from broadcasts half the settlement secretly listened to and pretended they didn't. "Guns are against my religion. So are visitors, generally, so we're both compromising."

She found him in the archive proper, in a canyon of server stacks older than the colony itself, kneeling at an open panel with a diagnostic wand in his hand. He didn't stand. He didn't seem afraid. He looked tired in a way that went down to the geology of him.

"You've been transmitting," Ilsa said.

"I've been recording. Thirty years. There's a difference, though I understand it's subtle from down there." He set the wand down and finally rose, joints cracking like green wood. "You've come about the children."

The floor tilted slightly under her certainty. "How do you know about the children?"

"Because they've been accessing my archive." He gestured at the open panel, at a cascade of access logs glowing amber on the ancient display. "Not deliberately. Not physically. Their sleep cycles have been running queries against my broadcast index for nineteen days. Compressed neural handshake, pattern-matching against stored voice data. I've never seen anything like it, and I built half of this system." His eyes, pale and steady, held hers. "Someone is using my voice to reach them, Commander. Someone with very deep access to very old code."

"Earth," Ilsa said.

"Earth went silent thirty years ago."

"Earth went silent *publicly* thirty years ago." She stepped closer, and the gun was still in her hand, and she noticed she was pointing it at him and made herself lower it, because he watched her do it without flinching and that was worse. "You think I climbed four hours to trade theories? Eight children, Okonkwo. Eight children dreaming the same dream, waking at the same minute, reciting coordinates to *your vault*. You want me to believe that's an accident of signal propagation?"

"I want you to believe what the logs say."

"The logs say someone is inside our children's heads." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she let it. Fear was a tool too; she'd learned that in the war. "And everything that reaches them comes through this mountain. Through you."

Something moved behind his face — hurt, maybe, or the ghost of it, worn smooth like everything else about him. "Ilsa Voss. Your mother was Marta Voss."

The name landed like a dropped weight. "What does my mother have to do with anything?"

"She has dispatch number 2,206. Recorded eleven years ago, the day before she died. She walked up here, sat in that chair—" he pointed, "—and talked for forty minutes about you. About the rebellion. About how proud she was and how sorry. I keep every voice anyone gives me. That's the whole job. That's all the job has ever been." He said it without drama, without appeal, and somehow that made it unbearable. "If you want to arrest me for it, the chair is right there. But the children aren't being attacked. They're being *addressed*. And I need to find out by whom before whoever it is finishes."

Ilsa stood in the cold humming dark with her mother's name sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, and made herself think like a commander instead of a daughter.

"If it's Earth," she said slowly, "if the fleet is coming back, and this is how they announce themselves — through our children, through a backdoor in colonial infrastructure — then every hour we spend being curious is an hour they spend positioning. You understand that. Whoever built this key built it to be used, and things built to be used get used *on* us."

"Or it's a message," Bram said. "Thirty years is a long time to compose an apology."

"Apologies don't require decryption keys in sleeping children."

"No," he agreed quietly. "But surrender demands do. And ultimatums. And I've been wondering, these nineteen nights, why a fleet that wanted to hurt us would bother with a garden."

She had no answer for that either. Two silences in one morning; her record was deteriorating.

She gave the order anyway, because orders were the one language she spoke fluently when everything else failed.

"The vault is sealed as of now," she said. "My garrison takes the approaches. No traffic up or down the mountain. The dreamers stay confined with their families until I know what's assembling itself in their heads. And you—" she looked at the old man, at the canyon of servers holding thirty years of voices including, apparently, her mother's, "—you'll come down to the settlement. For questioning. For your protection."

Bram Okonkwo smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen since the war.

"For my protection," he repeated. "Yes. Everyone says that, right before they lock the door."

* * *

She posted the garrison at dusk: six soldiers, a generator, a jamming rig cobbled together from war surplus to cut the mountain off from any signal going down. Dagny Reyes-Holt commanded it, which Ilsa regretted immediately and couldn't undo without explaining why, and Dagny looked at the sealed vault doors with an expression Ilsa chose not to examine.

In the settlement, the confinement order went out at 1900. Eight houses. Eight families. Armed patrols on the streets outside them, officially a quarantine against fever, unofficially what everyone knew it was, because New Meridian had lived through martial law before and recognized its posture.

Ilsa walked the circuit herself that night. At the third house, Sefu Adisa's mother met her on the step, arms crossed, and behind her in the lamplight the girl sat at the table, twelve years old and hollow-eyed, watching Ilsa with an attention no child should own.

"She asked for paper," Sefu's mother said. "She's been writing the numbers down all evening. Different ones each hour. Says the dream is getting faster."

"Faster toward what?"

The mother just looked at her. Inside, Sefu lifted the slate she'd been writing on, and turned it around, and Ilsa felt the night air go cold against her neck.

It wasn't numbers this time. It was a sentence, in a child's careful chalk hand:

*Tell the Commander the door was never locked. Tell her the garden knows her mother's voice.*

Ilsa stood on the step for a long moment, long enough for the patrol to notice and pretend not to, long enough for her own heartbeat to become audible.

Then she turned and walked back toward the command post, and gave the order she'd been dreading since dawn: double the guard on the mountain road, and wake her at 0300 — an hour before the children slept — because whatever was coming out of that vault, or coming *down* it, was no longer willing to wait for her permission.

Behind her, in eight lit windows across the settlement, eight children bent over their slates and began, in unison, to write.
