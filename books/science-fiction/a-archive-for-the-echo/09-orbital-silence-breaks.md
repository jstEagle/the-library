## 9. Orbital Silence Breaks

The coffee on the *Retribution* had not been real in thirty years, and Captain Ngozi Adeyemi had long ago stopped pretending to miss it. She drank it anyway, every morning at 0540 ship-time, standing at the viewport of her ready room with both hands around the bulb of the cup as if it were something that could be warmed by holding. Below her, turning slow and green-brown and indifferent, hung Meridian.

Thirty years she had looked at that planet. First from a distance of eleven light-years, through relayed telemetry and the long silence of lightspeed; then from high orbit aboard the first survey wing; now from here, geostationary over the northern temperate band, close enough that on clear days — and every day was clear, this high above weather — she could see the river deltas silvering in the dawn line, and the dark scatter of the settlement they called New Meridian, and, far to the south, the white scar of the seed vault pressed into the mountainside like a tooth.

She had been thirty-four when the *Retribution* left Earth. She was sixty-four now, and the ship's medical officer assured her she would see seventy-five if she kept off the stimulants and let the centrifuge do its work. Seventy-five. Long enough, perhaps, to finish what Earth had started. Not long enough to forgive herself for it.

"Captain." The voice of Lieutenant Okonjo, her watch officer, came through the bulkhead speaker with the flat courtesy of someone who has learned not to startle his captain before coffee. "Priority traffic from Command. Flash precedence."

"Route it to my display," she said, and did not move from the window.

The message assembled itself on the screen beside her in characters of cold green light. She read it twice. Then a third time, more slowly, because the third reading was where the trap always showed itself, if there was one, and after thirty years in the service Ngozi Adeyemi had learned that there was almost always one.

*FROM: FLEET COMMAND, EXPEDITIONARY GROUP SEVEN*
*TO: CO, FCS RETRIBUTION*
*CLASSIFICATION: OBSIDIAN — EYES ONLY*

*1. SIGNAL ANALYSIS CONFIRMS ACTIVE DECRYPTION OF EMBARKED PAYLOAD IN PROGRESS ON SURFACE. KEY ASSEMBLY ESTIMATED COMPLETE WITHIN 14 STANDARD DAYS.*

*2. UPON COMPLETION, TRANSMIT ULTIMATUM AS PER ATTACHED TEXT. COLONY WILL SURRENDER ALL ARCHIVE ASSETS AND ACCEPT ORBITAL ADMINISTRATION.*

*3. IF ULTIMATUM REFUSED OR IGNORED, YOU ARE AUTHORIZED TO EMPLOY LANDING FORCES TO SECURE THE VAULT AND ITS CONTENTS INTACT.*

*4. INTACT. COMMAND STRESSES: THE ARCHIVE IS THE OBJECTIVE. COLLATERAL DAMAGE TO SETTLEMENT INFRASTRUCTURE IS REGRETTABLE BUT PERMISSIBLE. LOSS OF ARCHIVE MATERIAL IS NOT.*

*5. ACKNOWLEDGE BY FLASH.*

She stood very still for a moment. Outside the glass, Meridian turned, patient as an egg.

Fourteen days. The decryption deadline — which meant someone down there had found the payload, or the payload had found them, which amounted to the same thing and was worse. And the timetable attached to the ultimatum matched the decryption estimate to within hours, which meant Command had known about the key's assembly before she had, which meant either their signals intelligence was better than hers — possible — or someone in the expeditionary staff had known exactly what that payload was going to do and when, because they had helped write it.

She thought about that last possibility the way you probe a sore tooth. Gently. Once. Then she stopped, because today was not the day, and the coffee was finished, and the ship needed its captain upright and certain-looking.

"Acknowledge receipt," she said to the bulkhead. "And set Condition Two throughout. I want the landing wing briefed by thirteen hundred."

* * *

The briefing room of the *Retribution* held forty officers and smelled of recycled air and the faint ozone tang of overworked displays. Adeyemi stood at the head of the table with the planet rotating behind her on the wall screen, and she gave them the speech she had given at every briefing for three decades, polished smooth as river stone: duty, discipline, the mission, the mission, the mission.

She watched their faces while she spoke. Major Tuller, commanding the marine landing contingent, taking notes in his precise engineer's hand, nodding at the right intervals. Lieutenant Commander Vashti, intelligence, whose eyes never quite settled on anything, including her. The young ones — there were always young ones, born aboard or decanted from the frozen embryo banks somewhere past the halfway mark, children of the voyage who had never seen a sky that wasn't manufactured — the young ones watched her the way they always did, hungry for certainty, ready to be filled with it.

That was the part no one warned you about, she thought. Not the killing. The filling. You stand up in front of people who have entrusted you with their lives and their consciences, and you pour certainty into them like water into vessels, and whatever is left over when they're full — doubt, mostly — you are expected to drink yourself.

"The colony will be presented with terms," she said. "I expect compliance. These are farmers, not soldiers. But we prepare as though they will refuse." She touched the display, and New Meridian bloomed across it in tactical overlay: the settlement's low sprawl along the river, the militia armories flagged in amber, the winding road south into the mountains, and at the end of it, ringed in red, the vault. "Primary objective here. The archive comes out of that mountain whole. Nothing else matters. Questions?"

Vashti raised a hand, which was unusual; Vashti preferred ambushes to questions. "Captain, the decrypt timeline. Fourteen days assumes the key completes assembly without interference. If the colonists attempt to destroy the payload rather than surrender it—"

"They won't destroy it," Adeyemi said. "They don't know what it is yet. That's our advantage, and it expires the moment they do." She let her gaze travel the table. "Which is why nothing leaves this room. Dismissed."

They filed out. Tuller lingered, as he sometimes did, a broad gray man with a face like a closed ledger. "Ma'am," he said quietly, when the room had emptied. "The rules of engagement annex. Paragraph nine."

"I've read it."

"It authorizes preparatory fires against settlement communications infrastructure. That includes the settlement's civilian broadcast tower. It includes—" He paused, choosing the words with visible care. "It includes the school."

"I said I've read it, Major."

"Yes, ma'am." He did not move. Thirty years had taught him that she noticed things, and that some things were worth the risk of noticing aloud. "The children of the settlement attend that school. My marines have been training for this deployment since before most of them were born. They'll follow orders. I'd just... prefer the orders didn't require them to become something else afterward."

Adeyemi looked at him for a long moment. Through the viewport behind him, Meridian's terminator crept westward, pulling night across the settlement like a blanket drawn up by a careful hand.

"Noted, Major," she said. "Off the record: if it comes to firing on that school, I'll give the order myself, and I'll carry it, so your people don't have to. Now get your wing ready."

He saluted and went. She remained alone in the briefing room, watching the planet turn, and permitted herself — briefly, the way you permit yourself one breath underwater — to feel what she actually felt.

* * *

Her cabin was small and she kept it deliberately so: a bunk, a desk, a shelf of paper books that had crossed eleven light-years in a waterproof case and were worth more than the ship's drive. She sat at the desk and opened the drawer beneath it and took out the thing she took out perhaps twice a year.

A photograph. Actual photographic emulsion on actual paper, developed in an actual darkroom on Earth before departure. A woman in a garden — a real garden, soil and bees and everything — kneeling among tomato vines, laughing at whoever held the camera. Her mother. Dead now for nineteen years, though the news had taken eleven of those years to reach the ship, arriving as a single line in a compressed family packet: *MOTHER PASSED PEACEFULLY MARCH 3. SHE ASKED ABOUT YOU.*

Her mother had opposed the expedition. Not loudly — her mother had never done anything loudly — but at the farewell dinner she had set down her fork and said, *Ngozi, they're sending you to guard a pantry, and calling it destiny. Guard it well, but don't ever confuse the pantry with the people.* And Adeyemi, thirty-four and glorious in her new uniform, had kissed her mother's cheek and said something dutiful and forgettable, and gone.

Now she sat in the dark of her cabin with the photograph in her hands and read the ultimatum text again on her display, because she had made herself memorize it and wanted to test the memory.

*TO THE INHABITANTS OF MERIDIAN: YOUR REBELLION AGAINST LAWFUL AUTHORITY HAS BEEN NOTED AND JUDGED. THE SEED ARCHIVE CONSTITUTES STRATEGIC PROPERTY OF THE TERRAN CHARTER AUTHORITY AND WILL BE SURRENDERED UNCONDITIONALLY...*

Strategic property. She mouthed the words without sound, tasting them. Ten million seeds, gathered and cryopreserved by the finest hands of a desperate century, sent across the dark so that human beings might eat on another world — and thirty years later, a committee on a world those seeds had never fed had ruled them *strategic property*, and built a warship's worth of law around keeping them from the mouths they were meant for.

And the timetable. She kept circling back to the timetable. Ultimatums were supposed to be instruments of decision, blunt and simple: comply or suffer. This one was threaded through with something finer. The surrender demand would transmit the moment the key completed assembly — not before, not after. As though the message and the gun were meant to arrive together. As though someone wanted the colonists to hear the demand *while* the payload was opening in their minds, so that fear and wonder would braid together into something pliable.

Or — and here the sore tooth again, probed deeper this time — as though someone wanted the demand to *justify* whatever happened next. To make the record read: *we asked, they refused, we acted.* A clean ledger. A tidy file for some archive on Earth, in some vault of its own, full of documents that had never once been fertilized by rain.

Her comm chimed. Watch change report; all stations nominal; the landing wing would be ready ahead of schedule. She acknowledged it and did not move.

On the shelf above her desk, between two paper books, stood a small object she had carried for thirty years: a seed pod, dried and hollow, from a tree that grew in the courtyard of her childhood home. Her mother had put it in her pocket at the spaceport, wordlessly, and Adeyemi had found it during the first week of the voyage and never surrendered it to the quarantine regulations she herself enforced.

Seeds. Everything came back to seeds. Earth had spent its last great fortune buying them a future, and then, having bought it, had spent thirty years pretending the purchase had never cleared.

*Don't confuse the pantry with the people,* her mother had said. And she hadn't. She was quite sure she hadn't. It was only that lately, looking down at the green-brown turn of the world below, she had begun to suspect that someone above her — someone with a flash-precedence cipher and an obsidian classification — had confused them very much indeed, and had done it on purpose, and had arranged the arithmetic so that by the time anyone noticed, the guns would already be warm.

* * *

At 0300 ship-time, unable to sleep, she walked the ship.

This was an old habit, older than the mission. Captains who walked their ships at night learned things that reports never told them: which compartments hummed wrong, which crews were tired beyond rotation, which silences were peaceful and which were the held-breath kind. Tonight the *Retribution* felt like the second kind. Word of the flash traffic had moved through the decks the way water moves through cloth, invisible and total, and everywhere she walked she found the same thing: people working carefully, speaking quietly, polishing equipment that was already polished.

In the marine embarkation bay, she found a young corporal — one of the shipborn, twenty-two at most, with the long bones and slightly widened eyes of zero-g childhood — cleaning a rifle that gleamed like a chapel icon.

"Corporal."

He came off the bench so fast he nearly left the deck. "Captain."

"At ease. What's your name?"

"Osei, ma'am." A pause, and then, because shipborn children had not yet learned the officer's art of not answering: "My grandmother was from the colony, ma'am. Before the rebellion. Her sister stayed behind."

Adeyemi regarded him. Somewhere below them, under forty kilometers of turning atmosphere, that sister or her daughter or her granddaughter was asleep in New Meridian, dreaming whatever colonists dreamed — or, if the intelligence was right, dreaming something very specific, something planted, something that was even now assembling itself into a key.

"Then you understand," Adeyemi said, "that everyone down there is somebody's family."

"Yes, ma'am." He hesitated. "Will we... I mean, is it true we're going down?"

"In fourteen days, if the terms fail." She said it evenly, and watched the sentence land in him like a stone in still water, ripples spreading outward through everything he had believed the deployment would be. "Get some rest, Corporal Osei. You'll want it."

She walked on. In the observation blister aft, she stopped and stood a long time at the glass, alone in the dark, with the planet filling half the sky.

From here, Meridian looked unbroken. That was the obscenity of altitude: at this distance, no fences showed, no graves, no militia checkpoints, no scarred fields from the rebellion's brief and bitter fighting. Just weather, and rivers, and the slow glitter of the settlement's lights coming awake along the dawn line. A living world. A world that had spent thirty years proving it could live without Earth, and had succeeded so thoroughly that Earth had finally noticed — and sent her.

*Ngozi,* her mother said, from nineteen years dead, in the voice she used for truths too large for emphasis, *they're sending you to guard a pantry.*

She keyed her comm. "Bridge. Captain returning. Have Major Tuller meet me in my ready room at 0600. We'll begin finalizing drop trajectories." A breath. "And get me everything signals has on the southern highlands. There's a facility up there — a vault. I want to know why, in thirty years, one man has been broadcasting to a planet that never answers."

A pause on the line. "Ma'am? Broadcasting how?"

"That's what I'm asking you to find out."

She cut the channel and stood a moment longer, watching the dawn come up over New Meridian, over the school, over the small stubborn lights of people who had no idea what was falling toward them out of the silent sky — people, and somewhere among them, if the analysts were right, children who had begun to dream in a voice they shouldn't know.

Fourteen days, she thought. Whatever was buried in that mountain had waited thirty years to speak, and Earth had timed its gun to the exact moment the words would come.

That was not security. She had served long enough to know the difference, and the knowing sat in her chest like swallowed ice.

That was fear. Someone, somewhere in the chain of command, was afraid of what the archive had to say.

And Ngozi Adeyemi, captain of the *Retribution*, keeper of a dead woman's seed pod, turned from the viewport and went to prepare her ship for war — while privately, in the locked room behind her ribs where captains keep the things they cannot say, she began, for the first time in thirty years, to wonder which side of the muzzle she was standing on.
