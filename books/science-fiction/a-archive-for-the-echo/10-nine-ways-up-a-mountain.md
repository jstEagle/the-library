## 10. Nine Ways Up a Mountain

The mountain had two faces, and Bram had spent thirty years learning only one of them.

The southern face was his — the switchback trail he'd worn smooth with his own boots, the cable-car he'd repaired a hundred times and ridden down twice a year for supplies, the long grey scree slopes that gave way to the terraced fields of New Meridian like an old man easing into a chair. He knew every stone of it the way he knew the vault's corridors: by heart, by habit, by the slow accumulation of decades.

But the northern face was a stranger. It fell away from the vault in a sheer tumble of broken basalt and frozen seep, unmarked on any map the colony still possessed, because no one had climbed it since the rebellion — since before it, maybe. The old survey charts in the archive showed a maintenance route, a line of rusted anchor points drilled into the rock by engineers who had never imagined their work would one day be used by a seventy-year-old man with bad knees descending toward a war.

He went over the edge at dusk, when the light failed on the southern trails and any watcher below would be squinting into the glare of the setting sun.

"You're insane," Sefu said inside his head.

Not inside his head, he corrected himself, for the hundredth time that day. Not exactly. The dream-link — whatever it was, whatever the fleet had seeded into his archive all those years ago — did not carry words the way a radio carried words. It carried *certainty*. When Sefu spoke to him through it, he didn't hear her voice so much as feel her meaning arrive whole, the way you feel heat from a fire without touching it. Her fear arrived first, always. Then her stubbornness, which was considerable. Then, finally, the shape of what she wanted to say.

*Insane*, she meant. *You are an old man going down a cliff in the dark.*

"I've been alone for thirty years," Bram murmured, testing his weight against the first anchor point. The rope hummed. "Insanity would be staying put."

Below him, the settlement of New Meridian was lighting its evening lamps, and the sight of it stopped his breath the way it always did — not with beauty but with grief. Thirty years ago there had been ten thousand people down there, and the lamps had come on in patterns, districts waking in sequence, a constellation with a logic to it. Now there were perhaps three thousand, and the lights came on at random, each household deciding for itself, a sky full of stars that had forgotten their constellations.

And somewhere in that scatter of yellow points, eight children were dreaming of a garden inside a star, and waiting for him.

*Third anchor is loose,* Sefu sent. *I can see it. Left side.*

He couldn't see anything; the dark was total now, and he'd left his lamp off. But he reached left, and his fingers found the corroded bolt head rocking in its socket, and he bypassed it without thinking about how she could possibly know.

"How can you see it?" he asked anyway, because some questions refused to stay buried.

A pause. Then an image arrived, unbidden: the mountain's north face rendered in dream-logic, glowing faintly, the anchors marked in gold like the illuminated manuscripts in the archive's historical collection. She was seeing the mountain the way the dream saw things — or the way the message saw things, the vast compressed intelligence slowly unpacking itself across eight sleeping minds, using them as lenses.

*I don't know,* she admitted, and her honesty was a small cold comfort. *It's getting stronger. Ama drew the whole tunnel system under the eastern granary yesterday. She's six. She's never been in a tunnel.*

Bram descended another length of rope, joints complaining, heart hammering against ribs that felt suddenly fragile. The fleet's deadline hung over everything — four days now, maybe three, the ultimatum broadcast on every open channel: surrender the archive, surrender the dream-children, or the Retribution would begin reducing settlements from orbit. Voss had sealed the children in the militia barracks under guard. Adeyemi's drop-ships were already in high atmosphere, running descent drills that rattled windows across the valley.

And between those two forces, one old man on a rope, trusting a twelve-year-old's dreams to guide him.

*You're smiling,* Sefu observed. *That's grim.*

"It's not a smile. It's wind."

*It's a smile.*

* * *

He reached the valley floor two hours after full dark, and the last hundred meters nearly killed him — a scree field that shifted underfoot like living things, each step a negotiation. He came down onto the old service road behind the eastern granary with his palms scraped raw and his left knee singing a high note of pain, and lay flat on his back in the ditch for a full minute, looking up at unfamiliar stars, letting his body file its complaints and then dismissing them all.

*Turn left at the culvert,* Sefu sent. *There's a patrol on the main road, but they rotate past the granary every eleven minutes. You have time if you don't limp.*

"I'm limping."

*Then limp faster.*

The culvert was exactly where the dream said it would be — a concrete throat beneath the road, dry this season, big enough for a man to crouch-walk. He emerged on the granary's blind side into a yard gone to weed, and there, crouched in the shadow of a collapsed drying shed, was a small figure with her grandmother's jaw and her own fierce eyes.

Sefu looked at him, and he looked at her, and for a moment neither of them moved. He had imagined this meeting a dozen times on the climb down — imagined what he would say, some words about courage, about the weight of what they were carrying. What actually happened was simpler. She crossed the distance between them and hugged him hard around the middle, her cheek pressed against his coat, dust transferring from her hair to his chest, and he held on and thought: *thirty years, and this is the first person who has touched me without being paid to bring me supplies.*

"You smell like the recordings," she said into his coat. "Dust and old paper."

"That's the vault. It gets into everything."

"Good." She stepped back, businesslike again, twelve years old and commanding. "Come on. We don't have much time."

* * *

They moved through the back lanes of New Meridian like ghosts, and Sefu navigated them not by sight but by the dream. She would stop mid-stride, tilt her head, and say, "Two men at the corner ahead, bored, one eating," or "The barracks gate changes shift in four minutes," and every time she was right. Bram followed in her wake feeling like a man walking inside someone else's prophecy.

She talked while they walked, low and fast, filling him in on the siege of small rooms.

"They moved us into the east barracks three days ago. Eight of us in one room, guards at the door. Commander Voss comes every morning and asks us questions we can't answer — she wants to know what the dream wants, and I keep trying to tell her it doesn't *want* anything, it's a *message*, but she hears 'fleet' and stops listening." Sefu's jaw tightened. "My mother cries at the window. Ama's father tried to take her out and got arrested. They think they're protecting us."

"Maybe they are," Bram said gently. "In their understanding."

"Their understanding is going to get everyone killed." She glanced up at him. "The garden is dying, Bram. In the dream. Every night it's a little more wilted, the star a little dimmer. It's counting down. Whatever the message is, it has a shelf life, and the fleet knows it — that's why the deadline. They want us to fail."

"And your plan?"

She stopped in the lee of a water tower and turned to face him fully, and in the thin lamplight he saw how tired she was — the bruised shadows under her eyes, the tremor in her hands she kept fisted to hide. Twelve years old, carrying eight minds and a planet's inheritance.

"The tunnels," she said. "Under the eastern granary. Ama mapped them — the storm drains run all the way to the aqueduct, and the aqueduct runs uphill to within two kilometers of your vault. The guards watch the roads and the gates. Nobody watches under their feet." She pulled a scrap of paper from her pocket — a child's drawing in charcoal, precise as an engineer's blueprint, the tunnel system rendered in confident strokes. "Tomorrow night there's a supply delivery to the barracks. Trucks at the loading door, engines loud, everyone distracted. We go out through the floor drain. All eight of us. You meet us at the aqueduct junction with food and water and whatever that rope situation you did tonight was, because there's a ladder section that's missing rungs."

Bram stared at the drawing. Then at her.

"When did you plan this?"

"Last night. In the dream." A ghost of a smile. "We had a meeting. All nine of us. You were there too — well, a version of you. You mostly listened." She folded the paper away. "The dream says the message needs all of us at the vault. It says the key doesn't turn unless every mind is in the room. So we go to the vault. All of us. Before the deadline."

"Sefu." He crouched to her level, took her shoulders. "If Voss catches you—"

"If Voss catches us, we're locked in a room until the fleet either bombs us or takes us, and either way the message dies and Earth keeps lying forever." Her eyes were steady and ancient. "You spent thirty years talking to nobody. I'm not going to let the answer die in a barracks."

For a moment he couldn't speak. Somewhere in the archive of him, thirty years of dispatches stirred like leaves in wind.

"Aqueduct junction," he managed. "Tomorrow night. I'll be there with food, water, and rope."

"And don't limp."

* * *

He made the return climb in the small hours, and it nearly broke him — the knee first, then the hands, then something in his chest that felt less like muscle and more like hope straining against its stitching. He hauled himself over the vault's northern sill at 3 a.m., lay gasping on the familiar concrete, and laughed once, quietly, at the absurd arithmetic of it: an old man scaling cliffs by night, taking marching orders from a sixth-grader's charcoal map, and believing — actually believing — that it might work.

He spent the day preparing. Rations from the emergency stores: protein wafers, dried fruit, electrolyte salts. Water bladders. A coil of climbing line. He packed it all into a frame pack and set it by the north door, and then, because the waiting was worse than the work, he sat down at the receiver console and listened to the fleet's deadline broadcast again, the fourth time that day.

*"To the settlers of New Meridian: you have ninety-six hours to surrender the archive facility and the minors designated as key-assets. Compliance will be met with amnesty. Resistance will be met with—"*

"With what?" Bram asked the speaker. "With what, exactly?"

The broadcast didn't answer. Broadcasts never did. He had thirty years of experience with voices that didn't answer, and he was beginning to develop opinions about them.

*Ninety-six hours.* And tomorrow night, nine people would slip into the dark under a granary and start climbing toward the only place on the planet where the truth could still be spoken aloud.

He slept two hours. He dreamed of nothing at all, and woke grateful.

* * *

The second night, the sky over the valley was clear and cold, and the trucks came to the barracks right on schedule.

Bram watched from the ridge above the aqueduct junction, pack on his back, night glasses borrowed from a crate of pre-rebellion survey equipment. Through the lenses he saw the loading bay floodlit, marines' worth of activity — no, militia, Voss's people, hauling crates — engines idling loud enough to cover a great many sins. He saw nothing of the children. That was the point.

*We're in the drain,* Sefu sent, at 21:40. *All eight. Ama's scared but quiet. Tesfaye cracked his elbow on a pipe and didn't even cry, which I told him was very cool.*

"Tell him the old man says cool counts double when it's silent."

A flicker of her laughter, warm as a hand on the back of his neck. Then movement below: small shapes detaching from the culvert mouth one by one, crossing the dead ground to the aqueduct in a disciplined scatter — Sefu last, counting them through, her lips moving. Eight. All eight.

They came up the maintenance ladder to the junction chamber where he waited, and it was like watching a constellation assemble. One by one they crested the lip of the access hatch and looked at him — eight children aged six to thirteen, dusty, wide-eyed, terrified and resolute in equal measure. Little Ama, six years old and solemn as a judge, stared up at Bram and said, "You're taller than in the dream."

"In the dream I'm younger too," Bram said, kneeling. "I'm sorry about that."

"You talk the same, though." She considered him. "You always say sorry when it isn't your fault."

Behind him, Sefu snorted. "Told you."

There was no time for more. He distributed water and wafers, checked Ama's small boots and doubled her socks, and got them moving along the aqueduct's service catwalk — a narrow stone channel running uphill through the dark, the water long diverted, the walkway crumbling but passable. Two kilometers of it. Then the switchback trail, then the vault.

They made good time for the first kilometer. The children were quiet, quick, bound together by something invisible and taut — he could feel it even without the link, the way they moved as one organism, the older ones flanking the younger, hands passed back along the line over bad footing. Nine ways up a mountain, he thought. Eight children and one old man, and the mountain didn't know yet what was coming.

The betrayal, when it came, came from the most ordinary thing in the world: a cough.

Little Tesfaye, seven years old, dust in his throat from the tunnels, stifled it badly — a sharp bark swallowed too late, echoing off the aqueduct walls. And from the darkness ahead, where the catwalk passed under the old road bridge, a flashlight snapped on and a voice said, calm and terrible:

"Stand where you are. Militia. Everyone stand where you are."

Four of them. Bram counted the silhouettes behind the light — four armed militiamen in Voss's grey-and-green, rifles leveled, and he understood in one cold instant that this wasn't a roving patrol. This was an ambush. Someone had talked. Someone always talked. A parent, a guard, a frightened child who wasn't one of the nine — the plan had leaked, and Voss had known, and here they were.

"Down," Bram breathed. "Everyone behind me."

The children clustered. Ama's small hand found his coat hem and gripped it. Ahead, the lead militiaman advanced, boot-steps deliberate on the stone, and Bram recognized the walk before the light found the face — young, square-jawed, one of Voss's corporals, a man named Denash whom Bram had seen at the settlement market years ago buying flour.

"Old man Okonkwo." Denash's voice was tight with adrenaline and something else — reluctance? "Commander Voss has orders. The children come with us. You—" the rifle barrel lifted a few degrees, "—you come too, or you don't come at all."

"Denny." Bram raised his hands slowly, palms out, an old man's empty hands. "You know me. Thirty years I've brought my cart down to your market. I fixed your mother's radio in the winter of '19, do you remember? No charge."

"I remember." The barrel didn't waver, but the voice did, just barely. "That's why you get to live. Step aside from the children."

Behind Bram, he felt rather than heard Sefu move — a shifting of weight, coiling. He knew what she was thinking. He could feel her plan forming through the link, half-formed and desperate: scatter, run for the switchbacks, some of them might make it.

Some of them might make it. Four rifles in the dark. Children ranging from six to thirteen.

No.

"Corporal," Bram said clearly, pitching his voice to carry, to hold every eye and muzzle on him. "Let me tell you what happens if you take these children back to the barracks. In three days, the fleet's deadline expires. If the message they carry dies in a locked room, the Retribution opens fire on this valley — and I have heard their orders, boy, I have read what they intend, and it is not amnesty. Every soul in New Meridian burns because we were afraid of eight children and a story." He took one slow step forward, away from the kids, putting his body squarely between the light and the small shapes behind him. "Or: these children reach the vault, and the truth reaches Earth, and maybe — maybe — everyone lives. Those are the options. There are no others."

"Commander Voss says—"

"Voss is a good woman making decisions out of fear. I've done the same thing for thirty years." His voice cracked, and he let it. "Denash. Look at them. Look at Ama. Does that look like a weapon to you?"

The light swung, briefly, and found Ama's face — round, dirty, tear-tracked, staring at the soldier with the terrible frankness of the very young. Something moved in Denash's jaw. For one suspended second, Bram believed.

Then the radio on the corporal's shoulder crackled: *"Patrol Four, report. Do you have them?"* — Voss's voice, clipped, exhausted, afraid — and the moment closed like a door.

"Sorry, old man," Denash said, and he sounded like he meant it. "Patrol Four has them. Bringing them in." He gestured with the rifle. "All of you. Walk."

They walked. Bram kept himself between the soldiers and the children the whole way down the catwalk, and behind him he felt Sefu's fury and terror churning through the link like weather — and underneath it, faintly, the dream itself: the garden inside the star, petals browning at the edges, dimming.

At the point where the aqueduct met the service road, a militia transport waited with its engine running and its rear doors open. Denash motioned the children toward it, and Ama began, very quietly, to cry, and Tesfaye took her hand, and Sefu stood rigid, glaring at Bram with an accusation he accepted completely: *you should have had a better plan, you should have—*

"Sefu." He caught her eye and held it, and through the link he pushed everything he had into one simple transmission, the clearest thing he had ever sent: *Listen to me. They only need one hostage to make the arrest look complete. They cannot search what they do not catch.*

Her eyes widened. *What are you—*

"The corporal needs a prisoner," Bram said aloud, to Denash, stepping forward, away from the children, arms still raised. "Here's one. The archivist. The man who built the signal, the man who planned all of it — I'll give you everything, names, codes, the vault's defenses. Take me, and write your report tonight: target apprehended attempting to flee with the children recovered unharmed. Full credit. Voss will have no choice but to log it."

"Bram, *no*—" Sefu's voice broke clean in half.

"Corporal." Bram did not look at her. He could not look at her, or he would stop. "Arrest me. Quickly, before I change my mind."

There was a long moment in which the night held its breath — four rifles, eight children, one old man standing in the open with his hands up and his heart going like a drum. Then Denash exhaled, lowered the rifle halfway, and made the calculation that tired men make at the end of long shifts.

"Cuff him," he said. "Kids in the transport. Move."

They put Bram against the transport's flank and bound his wrists, and as the plastic bit down he turned his head and found Sefu over the soldiers' shoulders — furious, devastated, alive, free — and sent her the last thing he had:

*The vault. North door. The pack by the door is yours. Get them up the mountain, girl. Finish it.*

*You come back,* she sent, ragged, blazing. *You hear me? You come BACK—*

The soldiers loaded the children into the transport. They loaded Bram into a separate vehicle, alone, and the doors shut, and the last thing he saw through the wire-glass window was Sefu's face pressed to the rear glass of the other truck, mouthing words the glass wouldn't let him hear — though he felt them arrive anyway, through thirty years of silence and one impossible dream, clear as a bell:

*I'm coming for you. All of us are coming for you.*

The engines started. The convoy pulled out onto the dark road toward New Meridian, toward the barracks, toward the interrogation rooms where Commander Ilsa Voss was waiting with her questions and her grief — and Bram Okonkwo, hands bound, sat in the dark of the moving truck and began, quietly, to rehearse what he would say to her.

He had thirty years of recordings in his head.

He only needed to find the one that could save them all.
