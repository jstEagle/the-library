## 12. Marines in the Wheat

The dropships came down through a sky the color of gunmetal, and Captain Naledi Adeyemi watched them from the *Retribution*'s command deck with her hands clasped behind her back so that no one would see them tremble.

Twelve birds, forty-eight marines each, falling toward the green smear of the colony's northern farmland. Toward the mountain. Toward the vault.

"Wave One away," Lieutenant Okoro reported from the drop bay feed. "Atmospheric insertion nominal."

Nominal. Adeyemi turned the word over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. Thirty years of silence between Earth and this world, thirty years of dead channels and unanswered dispatches, and now the fleet had come back not as family but as a fist — and everything about it was *nominal*.

"Captain," said Commander Reyes at her shoulder, his voice smooth as polished bulkhead. "You'll want to hear the ground net come up. It's always the best part."

She did not look at him. She had learned, in eleven days of sharing a command deck with the political officer, that looking at Reyes too long felt like looking into water and finding something already looking back.

"Put it up," she said.

* * *

The landing zone was a wheat field.

Adeyemi saw it in the first drone feed: golden grain standing chest-high under a low sun, terraced down the valley slopes in curves some colonial engineer had drawn by hand a lifetime ago. The dropships flared their drives above it and the downdraft flattened the wheat in great spiraling circles, stalks whipping, seed heads shattering into chaff that caught the light like snow. Then boots hit soil that someone had plowed, weeded, harvested for three generations without asking Earth's permission.

Sergeant Vashti Iwu's squad spread out along the field margin, weapons up, moving in the textbook wedges Adeyemi had drilled a thousand times in a thousand simulations. The feed showed no resistance. Just wheat, and wind, and the distant geometry of settlement rooftops two kilometers south.

"Contact left!" someone shouted, and then the feed bloomed with muzzle flash.

It wasn't an army. It was farmers. Adeyemi could see it even through the drone's flat eye — figures in canvas work coats dropping behind irrigation channels, firing salvos that were wild and desperate and aimed with more courage than skill. A militia, exactly as intelligence had described: three hundred rifles at most, most of them older than the marines carrying them, many of them held by people who had been children when the rebellion happened.

"Hold fire on noncombatants," Adeyemi ordered. "Iwu, push them back, don't break them. We want the vault, not a massacre."

"Aye, Captain." A pause. "Captain — they're not retreating."

She watched. She saw what he meant. The militia line bent under the marine advance but did not shatter; the farmers fell back in short bounds, covering each other, dragging their wounded, and every time the marines paused they stopped too, holding their ragged line like a fence against a flood. They were not trying to win. They were trying to buy time.

"They know where we're going," Reyes said softly. "They're defending the archive."

"The archive is a seed bank, Commander."

"The archive is the single most valuable strategic asset on this planet, and you know it." Reyes moved to the tactical table, its hologram spinning with the vault's schematics — stolen, decades old, annotated in a hand Adeyemi didn't recognize. "Thirty years of genetic library. Ten million cultivars. Whatever Earth wants done here, Captain, it begins and ends inside that mountain."

*Whatever Earth wants done here.* The phrase snagged in her like a fishhook. Because she had read the mission orders — all of them, including the annexes, because she was not a captain who let other people decide what she didn't know — and there was a phrase in Annex Seven that had been sitting in her chest for six days like swallowed glass.

She hadn't told anyone. Not yet. Not until she understood it.

"Status on Wave Two?" she asked instead.

"Dropping in eight minutes. Vault perimeter assault scheduled for 14:40 local."

Below, in the wheat, the skirmish crackled on. A marine went down clutching his thigh; medics swarmed. A farmer — a woman, gray-haired, firing from the doorway of a pump house — took a stun round and folded like laundry. The marines advanced. The wheat burned in one corner where a flare had caught it, a small orange stain spreading across the gold, and Adeyemi found herself thinking, absurdly, of her grandmother's garden in Lagos Habitat, of the way the old woman had guarded her tomatoes from the neighbor boys with a broom and a stream of curses, and how everyone had laughed, and how no one had ever sent soldiers against her.

"Captain." Reyes again. "A word in private?"

* * *

He led her to the briefing room, which was empty, which was the point. He sealed the door and stood with his back to it, and for a moment they regarded each other across four meters of deck plate like duelists who hadn't agreed on weapons.

"You've read Annex Seven," Reyes said.

"I've read everything."

"Then you understand the operational reality." His voice stayed pleasant, conversational, the voice of a man discussing cargo manifests. "The decryption window closes in fifty-one hours. If the payload cannot be recovered and transmitted before then, the contingency protocols apply."

"Contingency protocols," Adeyemi repeated. "Say it plainly, Commander. I find I trust plainness more than I used to."

Something flickered behind Reyes's eyes — surprise, maybe, or recalibration. "Sterilization of the settlement," he said. "New Meridian and its satellite hamlets. Orbital assets are already tasked. Precision kinetic strikes on population centers, followed by biological neutralization of the surrounding agricultural zones. Total projected casualties—" he consulted nothing, recited from memory, which was worse, "—eleven thousand, four hundred. Give or take."

The number hung in the air. Eleven thousand. Children among them. Children who had been dreaming, according to the intercepts, of gardens growing inside stars.

"And if the message succeeds?" Adeyemi asked. "If the payload transmits?"

Reyes smiled. It was a good smile. It had been designed, somewhere, by professionals. "Then the contingency lapses and everyone lives happily ever after. You see? There's mercy built into the architecture. All we require is success."

"All we require." She walked to the viewport. Below, the planet turned, blue-green and indifferent, its northern valleys stitched with fields. "Who wrote Annex Seven?"

"The order carries Fleet Command authentication."

"I didn't ask whose cipher. I asked who wrote it. Authentication codes can be inherited, Commander. Stolen. Forged." She turned. "Eleven thousand people, Reyes. On the strength of an annex nobody briefed me on until after we made orbit. On a deadline that appeared out of nowhere attached to a payload nobody can explain. Have you actually looked at the payload telemetry? Because my signals officer has, and she says the compression architecture doesn't match anything in current Fleet doctrine. It matches something *older*. Something Earth buried."

For the first time, Reyes's composure shifted — a hairline crack, quickly plastered. "Captain, I'd advise you to be very careful about the direction of your suspicions. This ship has been at sea a long time. Crews get strange ideas. Captains especially."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a reminder," Reyes said pleasantly, "that Annex Seven is lawful, authenticated, and countersigned at the highest level. Your duty is to execute it. Mine is to ensure that you do." He unsealed the door. "14:40, Captain. Do give the marines your best."

* * *

She gave the marines her best. That was the horror of it — that she could stand on the command deck with the glass in her chest and still do her job flawlessly, because thirty years of service builds a person who functions regardless of what's rotting underneath.

By 15:10 local, the marines held the mountain's lower slopes. The militia had fallen back to a ridge line two kilometers from the vault entrance and dug in with a competence that surprised Adeyemi's tactical officers. Drone feeds showed sandbagged positions, interlocking fields of fire, camouflaged supply caches — positions that had been prepared *in advance*, quietly, patiently, by people who had apparently spent thirty years expecting Earth to come back with guns.

They weren't wrong, Adeyemi thought. That was the worst part. Every paranoid trench those farmers had dug was justified by the order sitting in her own safe.

"Captain," Lieutenant Okoro called. "Ground team reports the vault's outer doors are sealed. Manual locks, pre-rebellion pattern. They're cutting."

"How long?"

"Ninety minutes. Maybe less. But sir—" Okoro hesitated, glancing at his board. "There's something else. Signals picked up local transmissions during the fighting. Encrypted, low power, settlement-to-settlement. And one transmission from the vault itself, aimed at the settlement. Very brief."

"Content?"

"That's the thing, Captain. Our linguistics AI can't parse it. It's structured like language but the entropy profile is wrong — too dense, too layered. Analysts think it might be some kind of key. Or a code being exchanged between the vault custodian and someone below."

Someone below. Adeyemi stared at the tactical display, at the small blinking marker that represented the vault, and thought about the intercepts from the first night in orbit — the ones describing children who shared a dream. Eight children, sleeping in identical nightmares, dreaming of a garden growing inside a star. Intelligence had filed it as hysteria. Folk panic. Colonial superstition.

But Adeyemi had served long enough to know that when a military operation's timeline happens to coincide with something inexplicable happening to children, the coincidence is never a coincidence. The deadline, the payload, the dreams — they were all threads of the same rope, and someone in the fleet knew exactly how they were tied together, and it wasn't her.

"Okoro. Pull the full mission authorization chain for me. Every signature, every countersignature, back to origin. Quietly."

"Sir, Commander Reyes keeps those files—"

"I know what he keeps, Lieutenant. That's why I said quietly."

Okoro held her gaze for a moment, and in it she saw the same question she was afraid to ask aloud, reflected back at her from younger eyes. Then he nodded. "Yes, Captain."

* * *

At 16:22, Sergeant Iwu's squad breached the vault's outer airlock, and Adeyemi watched the interior feeds come alive: long corridors of pale light, frost-rimed racks stretching away into darkness, row upon row upon row of sealed drawers, each labeled in careful handwriting. Seeds. Ten million futures in paper envelopes, tended by hand for three decades by a man the files listed only as CUSTODIAN, B., SOLE OCCUPANT.

The vault was empty of people. But it was not empty of presence. The feeds showed signs of recent habitation everywhere — a cot, a kettle, a wall covered in handwritten tallies. And on a worktable near the archive core, someone had left a recorder running, its little red light pulsing, mid-broadcast, abandoned in haste.

"Play it," Adeyemi said.

The audio channel opened into the command deck, and an old man's voice filled the room — warm, unhurried, worn smooth as river stone:

"—dispatch four thousand three hundred and eighty-one, to whoever is still listening. The rains came late this year, but the graves are weeded, and the wheat on the north terrace came in better than last season. I counted forty-one new roofs in the settlement today. Forty-one families who have never known anything but this valley, and who deserve to know that someone remembers they exist. So: I remember. I remember all of you. Even the ones who stopped writing. Especially the ones who stopped writing—"

"Cut it," Reyes said sharply.

But Adeyemi raised a hand, and the voice went on, and around the command deck the crew had gone very still. She watched their faces in the low light. Watched a young sensor tech bite her lip. Watched Okoro look away. Thirty years of silence, and here was the sound of the other side of it — patient, forgiving, addressed to *them*, to the fleet that had never answered — and it was doing something to her people that no briefing or ultimatum could undo.

"—if anyone receives this," the old man said, "know that the seeds are healthy. Know that the children are healthy. Know that we kept faith, even alone. End of dispatch."

Silence on the command deck. Somewhere below, in the wheat, smoke rose from the burned corner of the field.

"Captain," Reyes said quietly, "Annex Seven's clock is running. Fifty hours."

"I heard you the first time."

"Did you?" He stepped closer, and dropped his voice so only she could hear. "Because I'm starting to wonder, Captain, whether you've decided what kind of officer you are. The kind who executes lawful orders. Or the kind who reads old letters and gets sentimental, and forces me to do what needs doing without you." He smiled again, that professional smile. "I'd hate for that to happen. You seem like a good officer. Don't make me find out what else you are."

He walked off the deck. Adeyemi stood at the viewport a long time, watching the planet turn, feeling the glass in her chest finally begin to move — not dissolving, but shifting, sharpening, becoming something with an edge.

Her terminal chimed. A private message, routed through channels that would leave no log: *CAPTAIN — AUTH CHAIN PULLED. ORIGIN SIGNATURE ON ANNEX SEVEN DOES NOT MATCH FLEET COMMAND ARCHIVE. IT MATCHES AN INTERNAL RETRIBUTION AUTHORITY. SUGGEST WE SPEAK. NOT HERE. — O.*

Adeyemi closed the message. Below, the marines were digging in around a mountain full of seeds, guarding them for men who intended to burn the people those seeds were meant to feed.

Fifty hours. She keyed her chair's console and began, very quietly, to plan a different kind of operation.
