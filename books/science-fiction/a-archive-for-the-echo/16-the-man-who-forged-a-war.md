## 16. The Man Who Forged a War

The Retribution's flag deck smelled of recycled air and old ambition.

Captain Naledi Adeyemi stood at the viewport with the planet turning beneath her — a blue-green marble stitched with cloud, so beautiful it felt like an accusation. Somewhere down there, in a wheat field outside a settlement called New Meridian, her marines were trading fire with colonists who had been children when Earth last spoke to them. Somewhere down there was a vault full of seeds and a message she was beginning to suspect she did not understand.

She held the datapad against her thigh like a weapon she hadn't decided to draw.

On it: the ultimatum. *Surrender all colonial assets within seventy-two hours or face decisive orbital action.* Transmission origin logged as Fleet Command Relay, Sol. Authentication codes valid. Signed with the seal of the Admiralty Collegium.

And beneath it, pulled from the ship's own deep logs by a junior signals officer who would probably never know what he'd found: the raw routing header of that transmission, timestamped to the second.

It had never touched the relay at Sol.

It had been composed aboard this ship. Eleven days ago. In the political officer's quarters.

* * *

Commander Elias Reyes kept his office too warm, the way men did when they wanted you uncomfortable before they wanted you convinced. Adeyemi had served with him for six years and had never once seen him cold. He sat behind a desk salvaged from a pre-Flight liner, hands folded, face arranged into the patient expression of a man listening to a child explain why the stove wasn't hot.

"Say it again," he said. "Slowly. I want to make sure I understand what you're accusing me of."

"You forged the ultimatum." She set the datapad on his desk between them, screen up, headers glowing. "You spoofed the Sol relay signature using the authentication suite in your own compartment. You timed it to the decryption deadline because you needed the colony hostile before anyone down there could read what the payload actually contains."

Reyes looked at the pad for a long moment. Then he laughed — not cruelly, which was worse. It was the laugh of a man watching someone finally arrive at a party already in progress.

"And what does the payload contain, Captain? Have you decrypted it?"

"Partially. Enough."

"Enough," he repeated, tasting the word. "Enough to commit treason over? Because that's what this is, Naledi. What you're describing — if I took it seriously — is a captain of the Terran Fleet accusing a commissioned political officer of forging Admiralty orders. There are cells at Meridian Station for people who say less."

"The codes were yours."

"The codes were issued to me. That's what issue means." He rose, walked to the small porthole behind his desk — a vanity, technically unauthorized, framing the planet like a portrait he owned. "Do you know what the charter is, Captain? The original colonization charter for Meridian? Not the summary in your briefing packet. The actual document."

"I'm asking you."

"It's a deed." Reyes turned. His face had lost its patience; something older and colder had come up underneath, the way bedrock shows through topsoil. "Full inheritance. The seed vault, the archive, the colony itself — deeded to the settlers and their descendants in perpetuity, signed by the last Secretary-General before the Long Silence. Earth gave them everything and then Earth went dark for thirty years, and do you know why it went dark? Because the Collegium that inherited the government decided the gift was a mistake. A colony that owns its own food supply doesn't need Earth. A colony that owns its own future doesn't obey it."

Adeyemi kept her voice level. "That's a political theory."

"That's a *fact*, buried under thirty years of classification, and you're standing in the only ship in orbit that knows it." He came around the desk slowly. "I didn't forge a war, Captain. I prevented one. A quiet one. The kind where a colony slips loose generation by generation until one day Earth looks up and there's a sovereign nation on its frontier with ten million heirloom genomes and no reason ever to answer another transmission. I made it loud instead. Loud wars end quickly. Quiet ones fester for centuries."

"You killed people to make it loud."

"I *will* kill people to make it loud, if that's what the arithmetic requires, and the arithmetic has always required it. That's the job. It was the job when you fired on the militia convoy at Hesper's Gap, and you slept fine afterward. Don't develop a conscience at me now. It's unbecoming, and it's late."

The words landed harder than she expected, because they were true-shaped. Hesper's Gap. Fourteen dead. A report filed, a commendation received. She had slept. She thought of the sterilization order nested in her mission parameters — *in the event of decryption failure, deny the asset to all parties* — and felt the floor of her certainty tilt.

"If the charter is real," she said, "then the order to suppress it isn't from Earth either. Is it. Show me the directive authorizing the suppression. The genuine one. Sol-signed, Sol-routed."

Reyes smiled, and it was the first honest expression she'd seen on him in six years.

"There isn't one," he said. "There never was. The Collegium couldn't put it in writing — charters of this vintage have sympathizers even at home. So they sent me. One man, one ship, plausible deniability wrapped in a captain who follows orders." He spread his hands. "Congratulations, Naledi. You've just reasoned your way into the same cell as me. The difference is that I built the room and you merely discovered it."

* * *

She should have gone straight to the bridge. She knew that later, replaying it, cataloguing the failure like a damage report. She should have sealed his compartment, raised the executive officer, taken the ship.

Instead she said, "I'm ordering the marines withdrawn."

"No," Reyes said pleasantly, "you're not."

He touched nothing. He didn't need to. The door opened behind her and two Marines of the ship's security detail stepped through — young, apologetic-eyed, rifles slung but hands near them. Lieutenant Okafor, her own former tactical student, stood between them wearing the particular misery of a good soldier handed a bad order.

"Commander," Okafor said, not looking at Adeyemi. "By authority of Fleet Special Directive 9, I'm relieving Captain Adeyemi of command pending review. She's to be confined to her quarters."

"Naledi." Reyes's voice softened into something almost gentle, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing on the whole flag deck. "Sit down. Grieve it properly. In seventy hours this will all be settled — the vault seized, the archive burned, the charter a rumor no one can prove — and you'll be restored with a commendation and a story about temporary instability. Everyone gets their career back. That's the offer. It's the only offer, but it's a real one."

Adeyemi looked at him for a long time. At the porthole behind him, where the planet turned, patient and green and utterly indifferent to the arithmetic of men.

"You said the quiet war festers," she said. "You're right about that much. But you've got it backwards, Elias. The quiet war isn't the colony slipping away. The quiet war is the lie. Lies fester. Truths just sit there, waiting to be read aloud."

She let them take her arm. She didn't resist, because resistance now meant a scuffle and a sedative and a locked medical bay, and confinement to quarters meant a terminal, a door lock she could defeat, and — if her signals officer was half as brave as she suspected — an open channel to the surface.

As they walked her out, Reyes called after her, mild as weather:

"Seventy hours, Captain. Pray the message fails. It's kinder for everyone if it fails."

She did not pray for that. She had stopped praying somewhere over Hesper's Gap, and what she felt now, walking the corridor of her own ship as a prisoner, was not fear.

It was relief — terrible, clarifying relief — that the thing she had been serving for thirty years had finally shown her its face, and she could hate it without doubting herself.

In her quarters, the door sealed. The terminal woke at her touch. And already queued in the buffer, sent from below, unsigned, was a single line of text:

*We have the message. We need the sky.*

Naledi Adeyemi began, very quietly, to plan mutiny.
