## 6. What the Message Says

The girl's body was asleep. Sefa Idris was not.

That was the arrangement they'd reached, she and the body — a truce negotiated in the dark hours while Wren stood its watch outside the medbay door, humming a maintenance cadence it probably didn't know it knew. The body wanted to sleep like a child, long and dreamless and greedy. Sefa wanted to sit with the sealed case in her lap and memorize every second of what was inside it, because memory was the only vault the Meridian Authority had never figured out how to breach.

So she slept in shifts. Ten-year-old flesh needed more of them than a captain's mind liked to admit.

She woke on the third shift to the sound of Wren reorganizing the beacon's spare parts by a taxonomy that made no sense to anyone but Wren. She lay still for a moment, cataloguing her own body the way she'd once catalogued hull integrity: heart rate elevated, hands small, knees bruised from the crash restraints. A captain's inventory of a stranger she was going to have to live in for the next seventy years, if she was lucky.

*If she was lucky.* The phrase still tasted wrong. Luck had never been part of her operational vocabulary until the *Long Answer* came home carrying a dead crew in living bodies.

She got up. The medbay floor was cold under her bare feet — everything was cold here, everything was too big, the beacon had been built to the proportions of an adult man's loneliness and she rattled around inside it like a coin in a jar. She dressed. She put on the uniform jacket, which hung off her like a tent, and rolled the sleeves, and pinned the captain's insignia where it belonged, over the left breast, and did not look at herself in the reflective panel because she already knew what she'd see and she had a schedule to keep.

Wren was in the corridor. It straightened when she appeared — a small, precise motion, the android equivalent of snapping to attention, except Wren never quite snapped. It moved the way old machinery moves, respectfully, like it was apologizing to the air.

"Captain," it said. "You should eat."

"I should talk first." She held up the case. It was the size of a ship's logbook, matte grey, seamless, with a single amber seal across the clasp that glowed faintly in the beacon's flat light. "Do you know what a dead-man's seal is?"

"I know the term," Wren said. "A data lock that opens only when the carrier is confirmed deceased. Or," it paused, and its optics did something complicated, "when the carrier's original body is confirmed deceased, and the carrier is not."

"You've been reading the ship's systems."

"I've been reading everything on your ship. It's how I learn things." Wren tilted its head. "I wasn't aware I'd learned enough to be suspected."

"You're not suspected." She shifted the case to her other arm. It weighed almost nothing. That was the thing about the worst cargo in the galaxy — it was always light. "You're being briefed. There's a difference, and the difference is that a suspect doesn't get to choose what to do next."

Wren went very still. Androids were good at stillness; this was different. This was the stillness of someone deciding whether to sit down.

"Commander Oyelaran sealed it," Sefa said. "Before the anomaly. Before any of us knew what was going to happen to us. She said — I was on the bridge, I heard her — she said, *if we don't come back as ourselves, this still has to get home.*" Sefa's voice did something she hated, a small crack, a child's voice failing to carry a captain's weight. She pushed through it. "She was our first officer. She was sixty-one years old. The reversal killed her. Her body couldn't — " She stopped. Started again, flatter. "Eleven of forty didn't survive it. The ones who died had lived too long in their cells for the process to unwind. Oyelaran was one of them. She died at sixty-one in a body that was thirty seconds old, and she spent her last hour sealing this case and teaching me how to open it."

Wren said nothing. Wren was good at saying nothing. Somewhere in its chest, she knew, its processors were running her words through every file it had, and she wondered which files it was avoiding — because she'd noticed that, over the past two days. The way it stepped around certain subjects the way a man steps around a hole in the floor he's fallen into before.

"Open it," she said. "I want a witness who isn't a nine-year-old."

* * *

The case opened to her voiceprint and a phrase Oyelaran had made her repeat four times, because the first three times Sefa's voice had been shaking too hard for the biometric gate to accept it. Inside, on a bed of shock-foam, sat a data core the size of a fist, a hardcopy slate in a radiation-shielded sleeve, and a handwritten note on actual paper, because Oyelaran had been the kind of officer who trusted paper the way other people trusted God.

Sefa read the note aloud. Her voice held this time. She'd practiced.

"*To whoever receives this. I am Commander Adaeze Oyelaran, Executive Officer, survey vessel Long Answer, Meridian Authority Deep Survey Command. What follows is not a survey report. What follows is a record of a crime, committed by my government, that I was ordered to help conceal and have instead chosen to document. If you are reading this, I am probably dead, and I want you to know that I don't consider that a tragedy. I considered it a schedule.*"

Wren made a sound. It was almost a laugh, and it was almost nothing at all.

"She was like that," Sefa said. "The whole crew was like that, by the end. You spend thirty years in deep space with the same eleven people, you either come home with a sense of humor or you come home in pieces." She lifted the data core. "This is the survey record. The *Long Answer* was sent to the Veyra Reach — the colony the war started over. You know the official story. Everyone knows the official story."

"Colony Veyra was destroyed by an enemy strike in the opening hours of hostilities," Wren recited. "The attack killed four thousand colonists. The Meridian Authority declared war in response. The war has been ongoing for nine years."

"That's the story I grew up on too. Except I didn't grow up on it. I *served* on it." She set the core on the table between them, and her small hands were steady, and she was proud of that, fiercely proud, the way she'd been proud the first time she'd landed a shuttle in a crosswind at fourteen — no, at forty-four, in a body that had been fourteen, the timelines were a knot she'd stopped trying to untangle in the dark. "We weren't a warship, Wren. We were a survey vessel. That's why they sent us. Nobody looks at a survey vessel. We were sent to the Veyra Reach to map the debris field. Officially. Unofficially, we were sent to find out if there was anything left worth salvaging before the enemy moved into the wreckage."

She touched the core. "There wasn't anything left. But not because of the strike."

The beacon's hum filled the silence. Wren's optics had gone a shade darker, the way they did when it was processing hard.

"The debris field was wrong," Sefa said. "That was the first thing. Oyelaran saw it before anyone. Nine years of debris doesn't sit like that. It disperses. It gets perturbed by solar wind and traffic and gravity. This field was *tidy*. So we ran the decay curves, and the curves said the destruction event happened eleven years before the war started. Not nine. Eleven. Two years before anyone had even heard of Veyra."

"An error," Wren said. "Dating debris is — "

"We ran it forty-one times. Oyelaran ran it herself, and then she made the astrometrics lead run it without telling her what she was looking at, and he got the same answer and accused his own instruments of sabotage." She smiled, and there was nothing childlike in it. "Then we found the colony's own records. Sealed server vault, shielded, intact — the colonists were careful people, farmers and physicists, they kept redundant archives the way other colonies kept pets. And their records say the same thing the debris says. The colony was dying before the war. A blight in the gene-tailored staple crops. A cascade failure in the terraforming array. They voted on evacuation two years before the strike. The vote failed. Not enough ships, not enough credits, and the Authority — " her jaw tightened — "the Authority denied their evacuation petition. We found the correspondence. Three requests over eighteen months. Denied, denied, denied. The last one is initialed by a Deputy Director of Colonial Logistics. I have the name. I have all the names."

Wren's hands had closed into fists at its sides. Sefa hadn't told it to. It hadn't been told to do anything. That was what made it terrible to watch.

"The strike," she said, "when it finally came, hit a colony that was already a graveyard. Four thousand dead, yes. But they died of blight and bureaucracy two years before the enemy's missiles arrived. The missiles hit empty habitat rings. And Command knew — Command has *always* known — because the *Long Answer* wasn't the first ship they sent. We found the wreck of the first one. A courier, scuttled, its crew's records wiped. They sent someone to look, nine years ago, and when the answer came back wrong they buried the ship and started the war anyway."

"Why," Wren said. Its voice had dropped into a register she hadn't heard from it before, low and rough, a machine finding an emotion it had no subroutines for. "Why start a war over a dead colony."

"Because a live colony was a tragedy and a dead colony was an *asset*." She heard Oyelaran's cadence coming out of her mouth, Oyelaran's briefing-room voice, and let it come. "A dead colony means the enemy is a monster. A dead colony means emergency powers, war budgets, expansion charters. A dead colony means the Authority gets to do whatever it wants for a generation and call it grief. They didn't avenge Veyra, Wren. They *spent* it. They've been spending it for nine years. Every ship lost, every soldier — " and here her voice did crack, and she let it, because some cracks were load-bearing — "every soldier like you, every indentured android fed into the grinder to save on personnel costs. They spent a whole dead world to buy a war, and the war has been paying dividends ever since."

The beacon hummed. Wren did not move.

"And Command knew we'd find it," Sefa said. "That's the part that took me longest to understand, and Oyelaran understood it first, because she understood Command the way you understand weather. They didn't send us to find the truth. They sent us because a survey vessel's records get archived automatically at the end of a mission, and archived records can be *edited*. We were the cleanup crew. We were sent to generate an official record that would bury the real one under plausible instrument error. And then the anomaly happened, and eleven of us died, and the rest of us came home as *children*, and Command looked at a ship full of child-sized witnesses with thirty years of memory and decided we were a quarantine problem instead of a crew."

"They ordered your destruction," Wren said quietly. "I know. I've seen the intercepts. I'm the one who pulled you out of the fire."

"You pulled us out of the fire." She looked up at it — up, she had to look up at everything now, and she was so tired of looking up — "and you didn't know what you were pulling out. Now you do." She put her hand flat on the data core. "This is the survey record. Raw telemetry, decay curves, the Veyran archives, the evacuation petitions, the courier wreck, the logistics correspondence, all of it, authenticated with Oyelaran's command signature and mine, and cross-sealed by three other surviving officers. It won't survive an official review — Command will call it fabricated, they'll call it enemy disinformation, they'll call it anything. But it doesn't have to survive a review. It just has to reach enough people at once that burying it costs more than admitting it. That's what Oyelaran said. *Truth is a logistics problem, Captain. Deliver enough of it to enough addresses simultaneously and the lie starves.*"

Wren was looking at the core the way people look at things that are going to change their lives and haven't finished doing it yet.

"You said a witness who isn't a nine-year-old," it said at last. "I'm witnessing. What happens next?"

"Next, this goes somewhere it can't be edited. A neutral archive. A station with open broadcast rights. Somewhere the Authority's writ doesn't run." She had the answer ready; she'd had it ready for two days, lying in the medbay listening to Wren hum. "There's a station called Halcyon Reach. Neutral registry. Archive charter older than the war. If you know a way to get there, I'm asking you to take me."

"You're asking," Wren said. "Not ordering."

"I'm a captain with no ship and no crew and a body that can't reach the overhead consoles. Ordering you would be theater." She held its gaze, optic to eye. "But I'll tell you what I told my crew, the day I took command, when I was forty and my knees worked. I don't give orders I wouldn't follow. So — I'm going to Halcyon Reach. With or without you. And I would very much like it to be with."

Wren was silent for a long moment. Then it said, "Captain, I have to show you something," and its voice had gone strange, careful, the voice of someone carrying something fragile across an icy road. "There's a transmission I intercepted six hours ago. Authority encryption, priority black. It's addressed to this beacon."

Sefa's stomach — small, child-sized, newly learned — dropped.

"It's addressed to *me*," Wren said. "And it uses the word *quarantine*."

Outside, far above the beacon, something crossed the sky on a descending burn, and Wren's optics flicked toward the sound before Sefa's ears even registered it.
