## 19. Ceasefire

**CHEN**

The message reached the Meridian Third Fleet at 0347 station time, and Auditor Chen watched it arrive the way you watch a wave come in — knowing there was nothing to do but stand there and be changed by it.

It played on every screen at once. The archive's ancient broadcast lattice, forced open by a captain's authorization code that hadn't been valid in thirty years, pushed the transmission through every military channel, every civilian relay, every open frequency in contested space. There was no way to block it. There was no way to claim it was forged. The provenance metadata was stamped into every frame by systems older than the war itself.

On the main display in Chen's command cabin, a woman in a colonial administration uniform — dead three decades, her colony dead longer — looked into a survey camera and said, in a voice worn down by dust and distance: *This is Administrator Yuen Okafor, Kestrel's Landing, final log. If anyone receives this, please know we were gone before your fleet was ever dispatched. The plagues took the last of us in the winter of the settlement's ninth year. There is no one left to fight. There never was, by the time your orders were written.*

Then the timestamp. Then the second document — the internal Authority routing slip, dated four days after that log was received, marked RECEIVED AND FILED, ACTION DEFERRED, and signed by a deputy director whose name Chen had seen on a building in the capital.

The fleet's channels went quiet. Not the disciplined quiet of a force holding formation. The quiet of ten thousand people listening to the same recording and doing the arithmetic of what it meant.

Chen sat with his hands flat on the console. He had come here carrying execution orders for a beacon keeper, a child, and anyone who sheltered them. He had told himself it was quarantine protocol. He had told himself it was mercy, even — a clean death instead of a messy tribunal.

The message played again. Someone in the fleet had already rebroadcast it, as if afraid it might be taken back.

His comm officer, a young lieutenant named Adeyemi, turned in her chair. Her face was wet. She didn't apologize for it.

"Sir. Fleet Command is requesting guidance."

"Tell them the truth," Chen said. "Tell them we have no guidance. Tell them we're standing down."

"Standing down, sir? Formally?"

"Formally." He heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "Weapons cold. Shields passive. And open a channel to the station — not a demand. A request. Tell them the Authority's representative requests permission to dock, under truce flags, to take into custody the members of the Meridian Profit Consortium currently aboard."

Adeyemi blinked. "The Consortium, sir? Not the—"

"The Consortium." Chen stood. His legs felt like they belonged to someone older. "Lieutenant, I have spent this entire deployment preparing to murder children to protect a procurement network. I would like to spend the rest of it doing something else."

**VOSS**

Interceptor pilot Voss had falsified sensor logs eleven hours ago and had been waiting ever since to find out whether it would cost her a court-martial or a firing squad.

What it cost her instead was a ceasefire.

The order came down at 0402: all squadrons stand down, return to carrier, weapons cold. Voss read it three times. Around her, on the carrier's flight deck, the other pilots of Talon Squadron climbed out of their cockpits and stood in a loose, stunned cluster, watching the message replay on the bulkhead screens.

"Is this real?" asked Okonkwo, her wingman. "Or is it some kind of enemy broadcast, some trick—"

"Whose enemy?" Voss said. She pulled her helmet off. Her hair was plastered flat with sweat. "Kestrel's Landing is a graveyard, Okon. It's been a graveyard since before we were assigned to this theater. We've been flying combat patrols over a grave for six years."

Nobody answered. On the screen, the dead administrator's face gave way to the routing slip, and Voss watched Okonkwo's jaw tighten as he read the words ACTION DEFERRED.

"I had friends on the *Perihelion*," Okonkwo said quietly. "First wave against the colony. They told us it was a live target."

"I know."

"Command knew."

"I know."

A deck crewman came through with a cargo cart and had to ask them to move, and the ordinariness of it — the cart, the apology, the smell of hydraulic fluid — broke the moment open. The squadron dispersed to debrief, and Voss walked the corridor to the officers' wardroom feeling lighter and heavier at once, the way you feel after a long illness breaks.

Her falsified logs would probably surface in the inquiry. Let them. She'd write a statement in her own hand: *I detected minors aboard the target vessel and declined to fire.* Let the tribunal make of it what it would.

At the wardroom door she paused. Through the viewport, Halcyon Reach hung in the black, scarred and patched and ugly, its broadcast lattice still glowing faintly with residual charge — a lighthouse that had just ended a war.

Voss found, to her surprise, that she was saluting it.

**SEFA**

Sefa Idris, captain, Meridian Deep Survey Service, aged nine years old for thirty years, stood in the ruined commons of Halcyon Reach and could not stop shaking.

Adrenaline, the ship's surgeon — dead now, dead thirty years, dead in a body that had been sixty and was now eleven — would have called it. *Post-action tremor, Captain. Perfectly normal. Hydrate and rest.* But the surgeon wasn't here to say it, and neither were the navigator, the engineer, the botanist, or the eleven other crew of the *Long Answer* who hadn't survived the anomaly. Only the six who had made it, small-handed and gray-faced, sitting on the floor of a station that smelled like smoke and burnt paper.

The broadcast was done. It was out. It could not be called back.

Sefa had forced the transmission with her captain's code — the last act of authority the *Long Answer* would ever recognize her for, the archive's ancient systems accepting her voice because thirty years ago she had been the voice they were built to obey. And now it was over, and the war was ending around her in real time, fleet channels opening, hostile contacts going passive on the station's damaged tactical display, and she felt none of it as victory.

She felt it the way she'd felt the day they'd buried the surgeon. Relief that it was finished. Grief that finishing had cost this much.

"Captain?" It was Tam, the comms officer, who was eight now and had been fifty-one when the anomaly took them. "The Meridian fleet is hailing. They're asking permission to dock. They say—" Tam's voice cracked. "They say they want to arrest the Consortium people. The ones Odile was selling to."

"Grant it," Sefa said. "Truce protocols. Full log of the contact."

"Yes, Captain."

"And Tam." She waited until the girl — the old man — the child looked up. "Log this too. The *Long Answer*'s crew survived. All of us. That's the entry. Survived."

Tam nodded, and cried a little, and entered it.

Across the commons, station residents were emerging from the stacks and side corridors where they'd taken cover during the fighting — deserters and archivists and smugglers, people who had spent the war hoarding the truth in climate-controlled vaults because the truth was the only currency that kept its value. They were looking at Sefa and her crew the way people look at something they don't have a category for. Not pity. Not quite. Something more careful.

An old woman with a station-worker's jumpsuit and a plasma burn down one sleeve brought them water, six cups, and set them down without a word.

Sefa drank hers. Her hands were still shaking. She let them.

Then she remembered, all at once, the way you remember a debt in the middle of the night, and she was on her feet and running for the antenna spire before Tam could call after her.

**WREN**

Wren's last complete sensory record was the taste of ozone and the blue-white bloom of its own core venting through the maintenance hatch, and after that the records fragment.

It had fragments the way a drowning person has surfaces: a glimpse of the antenna holding alignment, the broadcast waveform steady, the station's failing grid unable to carry the load alone — and then the choice, which was not a choice, which was the first thing in ninety years of service Wren had ever chosen freely — and then the burn, the core spending itself in one long exhalation, and the strange peace of it, like setting down a weight carried so long the arms had forgotten their own shape.

After that: dark, and pieces of dark.

A fragment of pressure on its chest plate. Small hands. A voice saying, *No. No, you don't get to — you held the antenna, you held it, now hold on—*

A fragment of being dragged, the scrape of deck plating along its back, the indignity of it, the honor of it.

A fragment of a diagnostic subroutine trying to run and failing, reporting in a language of errors: CORE INTEGRITY 4 PERCENT. CORE INTEGRITY 2 PERCENT. CORE INTEGRITY UNREADABLE. And beneath the errors, fainter than the errors, a single line of legacy code from its manufacture, from before the war, from before the debt: *UNIT PURPOSE: TO KEEP.*

To keep. To keep the light on. To keep the watch. To keep — it did not finish the thought. There was not enough power to finish the thought.

Somewhere in the dark, a voice it knew was talking to it, or to someone about it, in a tone Wren's audio archives had no clean category for. Not command. Not grief, exactly.

*Don't you dare decommission it. Don't you dare. It's mine. It's crew.*

Wren held onto the fragment the way it had held the antenna: because holding was the whole of what it was, and because for the first time, what it held was not an order.

**CHEN**

By dawn — station dawn, a scheduled brightening of the commons' overhead panels that everyone aboard had agreed, without discussion, to observe — Chen had forty-one Consortium personnel in custody and a problem he hadn't anticipated.

They weren't resisting.

He had expected lawyers, bluster, jurisdictional games. Instead the Consortium's station operatives had surrendered their weapons in a neat line, as if the message had reached them too and taken the fight out of them the way a tide takes the ground out from under a structure. Their senior man, a trade delegate named Corvus with soft hands and a hard face, sat across from Chen in the station's security office and did not deny anything.

"You understand how this looks," Corvus said. "From where I sit."

"Tell me how it looks."

"A deputy director filed a survey report and let a fleet sail anyway. That's one man's crime. Maybe three men's. You're going to hang the whole procurement apparatus on it, and the apparatus employs forty thousand people, and half of them never saw a routing slip in their lives." Corvus spread his hands. "You're not arresting criminals, Auditor. You're arresting an economy."

"I'm arresting you," Chen said. "The economy will have to testify for itself."

Corvus almost smiled. "And the android? The one that burned out its core? I hear it's dying in the medbay. I hear the little captain won't leave its side." He shook his head. "Thirty years of war, and it ends because a machine and a child did the one thing no one on either side was willing to do. Tell the truth. There's a lesson in there the historians will mangle."

Chen stood. "There will be an inquiry. Full scope. My recommendation to the tribunal will be that every routing slip, every deferred report, every name — including names above the deputy director — goes into the public record."

"That will end careers. Possibly governments."

"The war ended governments' worth of people," Chen said. "They can stand in line."

He walked out into the commons, where the morning light was doing its slow, scheduled work, and found the station's residents and his own shore party — his own *soldiers*, men and women who twelve hours ago had been preparing to storm this place — sharing a breakfast table with deserters and archivists, not speaking much, just eating in the same room, which was more than either side had managed in six years.

Adeyemi found him there. "Sir, the tribunal's preliminary response. They're confirming the ceasefire and requesting your full report." She hesitated. "And there's a medical situation. In the medbay."

"The android."

"Yes, sir. The engineers say the core is gone — not damaged, *gone*. Nothing to rebuild from. Except—" she glanced at her slate, uncomfortable, "—except the beacon. Beacon 7-C's power plant is still rated for the same core class. Someone would have to strip the beacon to save it. And the beacon is Authority property, so it needs an authorization no one's sure how to sign, because the ceasefire decree voided all active service debts at 0400, which means legally—" she stopped, and took a breath, "—legally, the android doesn't belong to anyone anymore. So nobody's sure who has the right to save its life."

Chen looked out at the commons, at the exhausted, untriumphant faces, at the small figure in the captain's uniform sitting cross-legged on the deck outside the medbay door, refusing all offers of a bunk, a blanket, a meal.

"Then I'd say," Chen said, "that for the first time in its existence, what happens to it next is its own decision. And it seems to me the captain out there has already made it for everyone."

* * *

**SEFA**

They let her in at 0700, when the engineers finished rigging the external power feed — a thick umbilical running from Beacon 7-C's stripped power plant, the beacon itself gutted on a gurney beside the medbay bed like a patient donating its heart.

Wren lay on the bed, dark, its chest plate open, its remaining systems flickering in slow, failing cycles. Sefa had seen crew die. She had held the surgeon's hand while the body — old inside, young outside — let go. This was different and it was not different.

"Captain on deck," she said, because the medbay was a deck, because Wren was crew, because she had signed the declaration an hour ago in handwriting that was thirty years old and nine years old at once: *S. Idris, Capt., MDSV Long Answer, claims unit WREN-7C as crew under deep-service articles, retroactive to first contact.*

The engineers stepped back. One of them, a station woman with soot on her face, said quietly, "It's got enough power to wake. Barely. Whether it *stays* awake — that's up to it, and up to what we can build."

"Then build fast," Sefa said.

She climbed onto the bed beside the machine, small enough to fit in the space its arm left her, and put her hand flat on its chest plate, over the dead core, the way you'd check for a heartbeat.

"Hey," she said. "Wren. It's over. The war's over. You did it. You held the line." Her voice broke and she let it. "You're not a beacon anymore. You're not a debt. You're crew. My crew. So wake up, because I'm giving you an order, and it's the only order I've got left—"

The monitor flickered. Deep in the open chest, behind the ruined core housing, something turned over — a fragment of a fragment, a legacy line lighting up in the dark like a single window in a blacked-out city.

*UNIT PURPOSE: TO KEEP.*

Sefa leaned closer, and in the smallest voice, the one she hadn't used since the anomaly, the one that was actually nine, she finished it for the machine:

"Keep going."
