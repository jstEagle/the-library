## 16. The Meridian's Price

Ilsa Reyes had spent thirty-one years learning to read a ship the way other people read faces, and the *Meridian's Promise* was lying to her.

It was in the small things. The ventilation cycling a half-second too often on Decks Four through Seven, as if the environmental system were compensating for bodies that shouldn't be there. The cargo manifests for the aft holds sealed under a priority code she hadn't issued. The way her own first officer, Davor Okonkwo, had started knocking before entering the bridge—a courtesy he'd never once extended her in nine years of service, because courtesy was what you offered to someone you no longer trusted to be caught off guard.

She stood at the viewport of her cabin and watched the Horizon turn against the stars, that great pale spindle of a ship, its hull scarred with twelve years of her patience. She had bought this claim with a decade of bribes, forged registry entries, and one very expensive marriage she preferred not to think about. The Horizon was supposed to be the thing that made all of it clean. Salvage law was simple: a derelict with no surviving claimant belonged to whoever could hold it. Ten thousand sleepers in cryo-bays, legally indistinguishable from cargo. She'd told herself that sentence a hundred times until it stopped tasting like a lie.

It had never stopped tasting like a lie. She'd just gotten good at swallowing.

Her cabin was the one luxury she'd allowed herself—real wood paneling salvaged from a pre-war liner, a decanter of actual whiskey, a window wide enough to make the void feel like an ocean instead of a mouth. She poured two fingers now and didn't drink it. The whiskey caught the light of the distant sun and turned amber, patient, like everything else in her life that was waiting to be spent.

The door chimed.

"Enter."

Davor came in the way he always did, filling the frame, a big man gone soft at the edges, his hands folded in front of him like a man attending a funeral. He'd worn his dress uniform. That was the detail that made her stomach drop—not the knock, not the sealed manifests, but the dress uniform, pressed, with the Combine's sigil at the collar. Men dressed like that for boardings and for executions, and there was only one of her.

"Ilsa." He said her name the way he said it in the old days, before the fleet, before the board, when it had just been the two of them running contraband medicine through the blockade lines. "You should sit."

"I'm fine standing."

"You should sit anyway." He glanced at the whiskey, at the window, at the Horizon's pale curve. "It's a long conversation."

"Then have it standing. I've got the legs for it."

Something moved behind his eyes—genuine pain, she thought, or a very good copy of it. Davor had never been able to lie to her with his face. That was why she'd made him first officer. That was also, she understood now with a clarity that felt like cold water, why the board had chosen him for this.

"The board convened four hours ago," he said. "Quorum was reached while you were docked. They voted."

"They voted." She let the words sit in her mouth like stones. "On what, exactly? I hold the claim. My name is on every registry line from here to Ceres."

"Your name was on the registries." He unfolded a slate and set it on her desk, face-up, and she made herself look. "Article Nine. Derelict assets of extraordinary value may be repossessed by the Combine in the event the claimant is found to be—compromised. Acting against the financial interest of the enterprise. There's a transcript. You gave the colonists forty hours, Ilsa. You told them the boarding was off. You told the board the salvage was off."

"I told them the truth. There are ten thousand living people on that ship."

"There are ten thousand *assets* on that ship." He said it flatly, without cruelty, and that was the worst part. Davor had always been able to say terrible things flatly. It was how he'd survived the blockade years. "The genetic library alone—the terraforming data in that AI's core—Vasquez from the biotech division priced it at more than the fleet's entire operating capital. The sleepers are a rounding error. The board's position is that the rounding error can be thawed or not thawed at the buyer's discretion."

"Buyer."

"There are three consortiums bidding. The core is the prize. The bodies are—" He stopped himself. She watched him stop himself, watched the flatness crack for just a moment. "The bodies are negotiable."

She picked up the whiskey and drank it all, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't breaking against his jaw. "And me? What am I in the minutes?"

"Detained pending review. There's a security team outside your door right now, Ilsa. Two more on the lift. I argued for you. I want you to know that. I stood in that room and told them you'd built half this fleet with your own hands and they were cutting out their own heart." His voice finally wavered. "They said hearts are what the biotech division is for."

* * *

They came for her at 0300, ship's time, which was the hour cowards preferred and she had always respected them for the consistency.

Two men from internal security, polite, professional, carrying restraint cuffs of the soft polymer kind—the kind you used on family. They walked her down the aft corridor toward the guest quarters they'd converted to a holding cell, and she went quietly, because Ilsa Reyes had not built an empire by fighting battles she couldn't win, and the arithmetic of this corridor was very simple: two guards, one of her, and a ship full of people who had already decided she was cargo.

But the ship told her things as they walked. It always had. The *Promise* was hers in the way a body is yours—she knew its murmurs, its aches, the places where the old blockade damage still sang in the frame during burns. And the ship was murmuring now. The lift on Deck Six had been locked out of the maintenance rotation. The comms relays on Decks Four through Seven were running a traffic pattern she didn't recognize—compressed, burst-transmitted, pointed aft toward the Horizon. Someone was streaming data to the boarding parties already over there. Someone had been streaming it for days.

The sealed manifests. The extra bodies on the environmental readouts. The boarding teams hadn't been waiting for her signal. They'd been working under their own.

They put her in the guest cabin on Deck Seven—a nice room, a room with a viewport, because the board wanted her comfortable and wanted her to see what she'd lost—and locked the door with a code she could recite from memory because she'd written the original lock protocols herself, nine years ago, before she'd gotten sentimental about her own security.

She sat on the bed for eleven minutes. She allowed herself eleven minutes of grief, timed it against her pulse, and then she stood up and went to work.

The lock took four minutes to defeat, and she wept a little while she did it, not from fear but from shame, because the backdoor she was using was the one she'd built for Davor, after his wife died, so he could get to the observation deck at night without waking anyone. She'd built kindness into the ship's bones and now she was picking it open to save herself, and there was no version of that which didn't taste like a betrayal of something.

* * *

Deck Six was dark. Emergency lighting only, amber and low, and she understood why: the maintenance lockout, the rerouted power. They were feeding everything aft. The boarding operation on the Horizon wasn't a boarding operation anymore. It was a harvest, and her fleet was the combine, in every sense the word had ever carried.

She found the comms relay junction in a crawl space behind the water reclamation panels, a space she knew because she'd hidden in it once, at nineteen, while blockade runners searched her father's ship for her and her brother. She'd been small enough then to fit comfortably. Now she folded herself into it with her knees against her chest and her cheek against warm pipe, and she listened to the traffic with a handheld sniffer pressed to the relay housing.

Encrypted, but volume told her what cipher couldn't. Heavy traffic, sustained, bidirectional. Cargo specs. Cryo-bay schematics. Power draw projections. And on a side channel, in the clear because someone had gotten sloppy or arrogant: a work order. *Priority: core extraction. Secondary: biological assets, condition assessment. Timeline: 72 hours.*

Seventy-two hours. The colonists had been promised the boarding was off. Beatrix Brightwater—that strange, quiet gardener with the burn scars and the honest eyes—had staked everything on her word. She'd sat across from him in a greenhouse that smelled like wet earth and growing things, an impossible smell in the middle of the void, and she'd watched a man love ten thousand strangers through a speaker, and she'd given him her word because for the first time in thirty years she'd wanted to be the kind of person who could keep it.

Seventy-two hours, and her word was already worthless.

She should have felt despair. Instead she felt something colder and more useful, the old blockade feeling, the nineteen-year-old in the crawl space feeling: *they think they've won, which means they've stopped watching properly.* The board had detained her and congratulated itself. Davor had argued for her and lost and gone quiet. Nobody had changed the lock codes, because nobody imagined she'd crawl through her own ship's guts to reach a transmitter.

The nearest relay node was forty meters aft, through two sealed bulkheads. But there was another way. There was always another way; she'd designed the ship that way, paranoid and modular, so no single failure could kill her. She'd just never imagined the failure would be herself.

* * *

It took her two hours to reach the auxiliary transmitter in the dorsal survey pod, and by the end of it her hands were shaking and there was blood on her sleeve from a maintenance hatch that had caught her forearm, and she had never felt more alive. That was the shameful truth of it, the thing she'd admit to no one: some part of her had been dying slowly for years inside the boardrooms, inside the ledgers, inside the flat terrible arithmetic of people like Vasquez, and crawling through the dark warm guts of her own ship with torn skin and a stolen code cylinder felt like being born again into the person she'd been before the money.

The survey pod was a cramped sphere of glass and instrumentation bolted to the dorsal hull, used for mapping debris fields, officially decommissioned three years ago, unofficially kept functional because she'd never trusted a board that wanted her blind. Its transmitter was short-range—she'd have to aim it like a gun—but the Horizon was close enough to touch, hanging in the glass like a pale moon.

She keyed the channel Beatrix had given her. The gardener's frequency, the one he'd written on a scrap of paper in careful block letters, like a man who didn't trust his own handwriting to be legible to the future.

"This is Reyes." Her voice cracked on the first word and she hated it, steadied herself against the pod's frame, and started again. "This is Ilsa Reyes, transmitting on the open channel we agreed. If you're hearing this, Brightwater, listen fast, because I don't know how much time I have before they trace the burst."

The stars didn't answer. The Horizon turned its slow quarter-turn, its garden lights visible even from here, a green constellation on the spine of the ship. She thought of the smell of it, that impossible wet-earth smell, and made herself say the rest.

"The board voted you out. Article Nine. They're taking the ship—my fleet's already working under their orders, they've been running data to the boarding parties for days. Core extraction is priority one. The sleepers are—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "The sleepers are listed as *biological assets, condition assessment, seventy-two hours.* They're going to strip the AI's core and sell the colonists to consortiums, Brightwater. They're going to sell them."

She pressed her forehead against the cold glass, one breath, two.

"I'm sorry. I gave you my word and my own people took it away from me, and I don't know what that makes me, but I know what it makes them, and it isn't salvage law. It isn't anything." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, leaving a smear of blood across her cheekbone. "I'm locked in my own ship. I don't know if I can get to you. But you have minutes, maybe an hour before they trace this burst and come for me, and you have seventy-two before they start pulling sleepers out of the racks to assess them for sale. Wake them. Wake all of them, do whatever the machine says can't be done, do the thing you were always going to do—"

The pod's console shrieked—a trace warning, incoming signal lock, someone on Deck Two running a bearing on her transmission—and she cut the burst and killed the transmitter and sat in the sudden silence with her blood on her face and her heart going like a hammer.

Through the glass, the Horizon's garden lights burned on, green and patient and utterly unaware.

She had minutes. She had a ship full of enemies and one friend forty meters of vacuum away, and everything she had built in thirty-one years—the fleet, the claim, the name—was already spent, spent on a burst transmission to a gardener she'd known for a week, and she found, sitting in the dark of her own survey pod with the trace alarm still whispering, that she would spend it again. She would spend it twice.

The pod's hatch lock cycled. Footsteps in the dorsal corridor, unhurried, two sets, and a voice she knew better than her own, calling through the glass with something that might have been apology in it:

"Ilsa. I know you're in there. Open the hatch, and we can still fix this."

Davor. And behind him, fainter, the flat professional voice of the security team, and the soft click of restraints being readied—the polymer kind, the kind you used on family.

Reyes looked at the Horizon one last time, at its green constellation burning against the dark, and pressed her palm flat against the glass as if she could push the whole ship to safety by sheer want.

Then she turned to face the hatch.
