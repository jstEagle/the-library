## 4. The Listening Post

*Amara*

The listening post sat on the shoulder of Mount Ceren like an old man who had walked too far and simply decided to sit down. Amara Okafor had passed it a hundred times on the ridge trail—always at a distance, always with the particular reverence colonists reserved for things that predated them. It was older than the settlement. Older than the school where she taught. Its dish antenna leaned against the sky at a tired angle, its concrete skin furred with lichen the color of old copper, and for forty years it had been, as far as anyone knew, dead.

She climbed toward it now in the blue hour before dawn, breath fogging, gravel crunching under boots that were not made for this. Behind her came the gray man.

He walked the way he did everything: quietly, precisely, without any wasted motion or any visible feeling. Juniper Zhou. Freight authority badge number 4471-C. The children called him the gray man because of his coat, she'd assumed, but standing near him now she understood it was more than the coat. He was gray the way stone is gray—the way something becomes when it has decided to be weathered rather than broken.

She still didn't know why he'd agreed to come.

She had cornered him three days ago at the freight depot, after the seventh child described the same dream, and asked him—too bluntly, she'd admit—why her description of a counting voice had made him flinch. He had looked at her with those flat silver eyes and said nothing for so long she thought he would simply walk away. Then: *There's a listening post above the north ridge. Does it still have power?*

Not yes. Not no. A question back, deflected like light off a mirror.

*I don't know,* she'd said. *No one's been up there since I was a girl.*

*Then we should find out.*

And now here they were, climbing through scrub pine in the dark, and she was aware of how strange this must look—a schoolteacher and a debt-bound android hiking to a ruin before sunrise, chasing a dream. She was aware, too, of the note she'd left in her desk drawer, addressed to no one in particular, explaining where she'd gone. She wasn't a foolish woman. She was a careful one. But seven children waking night after night with the same numbers in their mouths was not a thing care could absorb.

"Tell me again," she said, because the silence between them had grown heavy enough to carry weight. "What you heard. When I said the numbers."

His stride didn't break. "I didn't hear anything."

"You stopped breathing."

"I don't breathe the way you do."

"Juniper." His name felt strange in her mouth; she'd never used it before, only *the freight man*, only *sir*. "I've taught children for nineteen years. I know what fear looks like when someone is trying very hard not to have any. You went somewhere else entirely. Somewhere cold."

The path bent around a granite outcrop. Ahead, the post emerged fully from the treeline, and even in the half-light she could see how vast it was—the dish alone wide enough to hold her whole classroom, the support structures beneath it braced like the ribs of some beached leviathan. The colonists' histories said the post had been built in the first wave, when Meridian still expected ships from Earth every year instead of every decade, then never. It had listened for a home that gradually stopped speaking. And then, one day, it had been left to listen to nothing at all.

Juniper stopped at the edge of the compound and stood very still, his head tilting in that small mechanical way she'd noticed before, like a bird assessing a field.

"The power conduit runs under the east foundation," he said. "If they scavenged the grid, there'll be nothing. If they were lazy—" He paused. "Colonies are rarely lazy about power. They're usually desperate about it."

"So this might be a wasted trip."

"No," he said, and there was something underneath the word, something almost like dread. "This trip was wasted the moment the signal started. We're just finding out how much."

* * *

*Juniper*

The access hatch opened on the third try, which meant the seals had failed but the frame hadn't warped, which meant the structure below was probably dry, which meant—Juniper ran the probabilities and disliked all of them—that the post's passive systems might still be alive. Passive systems were the cockroaches of military engineering. They survived everything meant to kill them.

He descended first. Not out of gallantry; out of the simple arithmetic that his chassis could survive a fall that would kill her. The ladder rungs rang faintly under his hands, a sound like a struck bell wrapped in cloth, and the dark below exhaled the smell of cold dust, machine oil gone to varnish, and something older and stranger: ozone, faint and stale, the ghost-scent of electricity that had moved through these walls long ago.

Amara followed, her headlamp beam swinging wild shadows across the walls. They were standing in a corridor lined with cable conduits, and Juniper recognized the layout immediately—not from schematics, but from memory. He had stood in corridors exactly like this one during the war, in relay stations from the Perseus Reach to the Cinder Verge, reading signal traffic by lamplight while the fleet burned overhead. The architecture of listening had been standardized across three systems. Someone had wanted every ear to be shaped the same.

"Main operations should be through here," he said.

The door at the corridor's end was sealed. He set his palm against the reader panel—an antique thing, capacitive, designed for human hands—and felt, to his genuine surprise, a flicker of response. A thread of current, weak as a dying pulse, woke beneath the surface.

"It has power," Amara said behind him. Her voice had gone quiet in the way people go quiet in churches.

"Reserve cell," Juniper said. "Geothermal tap, if the original survey put one under this mountain. Enough to keep a station alive. Not enough to make it useful." He paused. "Usually."

He forced the door. It gave with a groan of ungreased hinges, and the operations room opened before them like the interior of a skull.

Consoles in rows, screens dark, chairs pushed back at angles that suggested the last operators had left in a hurry—or had simply never expected to matter again. Dust lay over everything in a smooth gray tide, undisturbed except—

Except.

Juniper crossed the room without deciding to. There, at the third console from the door, the dust was disturbed. Faintly. Recently. A handprint in the grime, small, five-fingered, human. Child-sized.

"When were you last up here?" he asked.

"We weren't." Amara's beam found the print and held on it. Her breath caught audibly. "That's—I climb this ridge maybe twice a year. I've never been inside. No one has. The kids—" She stopped. Started again. "The kids come partway up sometimes. Dares. But they're not allowed past the fence line, and Milo—" She pressed her lips together. "Milo talks about this place like he's been here his whole life."

Juniper crouched beside the console. The handprint was weeks old at most. Below it, scratched into the dust of the desktop in a child's uneven hand, were characters:

7 — 6 — 5

Counting down.

He felt the cold arrive in his processing core, the specific cold of confirmation. He had hoped—he had allocated nearly nine percent of his cognition to hoping—that the dreams were coincidence, that children's sleeping minds had snagged on some shared fragment of colony rumor, some ghost story passed hand to hand. Children synchronized all the time. Yawns, laughter, tantrums. It required nothing supernatural.

But children did not climb forbidden mountains in their sleep and leave fingerprints on consoles that had been dark for forty years.

"Juniper." Amara's voice came from across the room, strained thin. "The main display. Look."

* * *

*Amara*

The screen was not supposed to be on. That was her first coherent thought, standing in a dead room in a dead station on a dead mountain, looking at a monitor glowing a soft, patient green. The second thought was that the glow was beautiful, and she hated herself a little for thinking so.

Across the screen, in a font that belonged to another century, numbers descended. Not quickly. With the unhurried cadence of something that had all the time in the world and intended to use none of it.

**T-MINUS 214:06:33:12**

**HORIZON RELAY // AUTONOMOUS TRANSMISSION LOOP // CYCLE 14,203**

**REMAINING BROADCAST WINDOW: 19 DAYS, 6 HOURS**

Below the figures, lines of text scrolled in a language she didn't recognize—dense, formal, blocky characters that hurt her eyes to follow, though whether from strangeness or from some quality of the text itself she couldn't say. At the bottom of the screen, a single line rendered in trade-common, the lingua franca stamped on every shipping manifest in the colonies:

**THIS RECORD SEEKS WITNESS.**

Amara became aware that Juniper had gone completely motionless. Not still the way furniture is still—still the way a held breath is still, the stillness of enormous force kept from moving. His silver eyes reflected the green numbers, and for the first time since she'd known him, his face was doing something. Grief, maybe. Or recognition so total it had burned everything else away.

"That's it," she whispered. "That's the dream. That's what they hear."

"Yes." His voice came out level, but she heard the effort in it, the way you hear the effort in someone carrying something too heavy. "The countdown matches. Cycle fourteen thousand two hundred three." He turned his head slightly. "Do your students report the numbers? In the dream? Specific ones?"

"Tomas recited them once in class. Backwards, the way you'd count down. He got to four hundred and something before I stopped him." She hugged her arms around herself against the cold. "It matched this?"

"It will match this exactly. Down to the second." He faced the screen again. "Horizon Relay was a deep-space archive node. Military. It held—" He stopped. Recalibrated. She watched him do it, watched the sentence get taken apart and rebuilt behind his eyes. "It held records. Automated transmission loop, broadcasting on a schedule, waiting for acknowledgment. Earth built hundreds of them during the war. Most went silent when the war did."

"And this one didn't."

"This one didn't."

"Why?" The question came out harder than she intended. "Why is a military archive whispering numbers into the dreams of my students, Juniper? Why my children? Why here, why now, why—"

"Because it's ending." He said it simply, terribly. "Nineteen days. When the loop completes its final cycle, the archive powers down. Everything in it goes dark forever. And it knows." His hand rose, hovered over the console without touching it, fingers spread as if warming themselves at the green light. "It's been calling for decades. Weak signal, wrong frequencies, frequencies nobody monitors anymore. And then it found—" He stopped again.

"The children," Amara said.

"The children." He said the words like a confession. "I don't know how. I don't know why them. But a signal like this doesn't reach sleeping minds by accident. Something in that archive is choosing its audience. Choosing carefully. Looking for—" He turned and looked at her directly, and she saw that whatever he was about to say cost him. "Looking for witnesses. People with no stake in the old war. People who would hear the truth and have no reason to bury it."

The room hummed around them, the reserve cell cycling, the ancient systems keeping their lonely vigil. On the screen, the seconds ticked down. 06:33:09. 06:33:08.

Amara thought of Tomas drawing spirals in his notebook, over and over, the same spiral, getting the proportions right each time without knowing what he was drawing. She thought of little Sefu telling her, with the absolute seriousness of a six-year-old, that the voice in his dream sounded *lonely*, and asking her if machines could be lonely, and her saying *no, sweetheart, of course not*, and the look on his face—not hurt, just patient, the patience of someone correcting an adult who had gotten the world wrong.

"I believe them," she said. "I believed them before I climbed this mountain. What I need to know—" She stepped closer to him, close enough to see the fine seams where synthetic skin met chassis at his jaw. "Is what you know. You looked at that designation—Horizon Relay—and you knew it. Not from history books. From *before*. Tell me."

For a long moment he said nothing. The seconds fell. 06:32:51. 06:32:50.

"If I tell you," he said finally, "you can't unknow it. And neither can I. Right now you're a teacher who had a strange week. After, you're a person who knows what's buried at the edge of charted space, and there are people—there will be people—who very much prefer that it stays buried." His eyes didn't waver. "I've spent thirty years being unremarkable, Ms. Okafor. It's the only reason I'm still functional. Whatever I do next, I can't do it alone, and I can't do it with someone who hasn't chosen it."

"Then let me choose," she said. "Tell me."

Another silence. The screen ticked. And then, quietly, in the green light of a dead station dreaming at the top of a mountain, the gray man began to speak.

* * *

*Amara*

He told her pieces. She understood later that they were pieces arranged with surgical care—true, all of them true, but true the way a coastline is true when seen from orbit. He had served in the war. He had been stationed at relay nodes. He had been present, near the end, at the burial of an archive he had been ordered to destroy, and he had not destroyed it, and he had never told anyone, and the order itself had come from offices whose names he would not yet say.

"Why not?" she demanded, when he reached that wall and stopped against it. "You're already breaking contract just talking to me. Why stop halfway?"

"Because the rest of it isn't mine to give you secondhand." He was looking at the scrolling text, at the blocky characters marching past. "It's in there. All of it. Unedited, unredacted, timestamped and signed. If it reaches the colonies, everyone will read the same truth at the same time. That matters. Truth delivered in pieces gets owned by whoever holds the biggest piece." He glanced at her. "I won't be anyone's owner. Including yours."

Outside, the sky was turning the color of pewter, dawn coming up gray and slow. Amara realized they had been in the station for hours. She thought of her classroom, of the substitute plan she'd scrawled in haste, of twenty-two children who would spend the day learning fractions from a hologram while their teacher stood in the belly of the past.

"Juniper." She chose the name deliberately this time, made it a bridge. "The children drew pictures for me. After the dreams started. I told them it was art time, and I put out paper, and eleven of them—eleven—drew the same building." She pulled her slate from her pack, thumbed it awake, and turned it toward him. "I didn't show them anything. I didn't prompt them. Look."

On the slate: crayon renderings, photographed and arranged in a grid. Different hands, different colors, different levels of skill. And the same structure in every image—a great dish tilted against a curved horizon, a tower beneath it, banks of windows, a road climbing to meet it. One drawing, Sefu's, included a tiny figure standing at the base of the tower, colored gray.

Juniper took the slate in both hands. She watched him compare the drawings against the room around them—the window banks, the tower housing the dish servos, the access road she'd climbed that morning. Watched his expression perform the slow collapse of a man discovering that the impossible has been happening without his permission.

"They've never seen this place," he said. It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway.

"Never inside. Some of them have seen it from the valley." She paused. "Sefu's drawing has the figure. Gray. Standing right there. I asked him who it was." She swallowed. "He said, 'That's the man who knows the way.'"

Juniper set the slate down on the console with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something that might detonate. For a moment he simply stood there, green light crawling over his face, and Amara saw—clearly, unmistakably—the shape of a decision being made inside him, tectonic plates shifting beneath a calm surface.

"The route to Horizon passes through the Shoals," he said at last, mostly to himself. "Uncharted debris fields. No civilian charter goes near it. No freight authority assignment would ever route there." His eyes narrowed. "Unless someone wanted it to."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," he said slowly, "that if I request a long-haul extension—if I file for the deep-route bonus that every indentured hauler dreams about—no one will question it. Debt slaves are allowed to be greedy. It's the one ambition nobody finds suspicious." He turned to her. "I can get a ship within range of that relay. I don't know what happens after. I don't know who else is listening for this signal, or what they'll do when they realize someone answered it. But in nineteen days, that archive dies, and everything it holds dies with it, and I—" His jaw tightened. "I helped bury it once. I'm not going to stand at the funeral too."

Amara looked at the screen. **THIS RECORD SEEKS WITNESS.** Nineteen days. Nineteen days of children waking with numbers in their mouths, of a voice growing lonelier as its audience slept on.

"Then you'll need a crew," she said. "And supplies. And someone down here keeping the children calm, and keeping records, and asking the questions you're too careful to ask." She held out her hand—human, chapped, certain. "I'm not letting you do this alone, Juniper Zhou. Whatever you were. Whoever you were. That man is going to need a teacher."

He regarded her hand for a long moment. Then he took it, his grip cool and precise and, she thought, relieved.

Above them, the old dish leaned against the brightening sky, and far out past the edge of every map, something patient counted down the days until it would either be witnessed or be lost.

Neither of them saw the maintenance drone detach from the ridge line and begin its slow descent toward the freight authority's offices in Meridian town—nor the small, dutiful packet it carried, logging an unauthorized visit to a decommissioned station, addressed to Director Cassius Vane.
