## 9. Dead Space Navigation

Rhea Okonkwo had once flown a courier skiff through the burning atmosphere of a gas giant to win a bet she couldn't remember making, and she had never been afraid in a cockpit since. Fear was a thing that happened to other people, in other places—on the ground, mostly, where the ground could reach you.

She was afraid now.

Not of the dark ahead. The dark ahead was just the dark; she'd grown up inside it, on a station whose windows faced nothing but the long black between stars. What frightened her was the paper.

Milo's charts were drawn on actual paper—the twins had scavenged a ream of packaging liner from the hold, and the boy had covered sheet after sheet with colored pencils Amara Okafor had pressed into his hands at the last minute, back when this was still supposed to be a delivery run and not a pilgrimage. The drawings were child-things: wobbly circles for suns, spirals for something Milo called *the loud place*, a long gray line he'd labeled, in careful block letters, THE WAY THAT IS NOT A WAY.

And every one of them was right.

* * *

Rhea discovered it on the third day out from the Shoals, past the last buoy that any registered vessel had ever logged, in a stretch of space the freight authority's atlases simply left blank—a pale rectangle on the chart with the words UNCHARTED: NAVIGATION PROHIBITED stamped across it like a warning nailed to a door.

Prohibited. She'd laughed at that word when Juniper first showed her the route. Nobody prohibited anything out here. There was no one out here to do the prohibiting.

Except there were things. That was the problem. Uncharted didn't mean empty. It meant rocks you couldn't see coming, gravity wells with no name, debris fields from collisions older than the colony itself, drifting in silence with all their murderous patience intact. Every pilot knew the stories. Ships went into the blank rectangles and did not come out, and the insurance consortiums paid out quietly and the atlases stayed blank, because blankness was cheaper than surveying.

So Rhea flew slow. She flew defensive, eyes flicking between the passive array and the old collision-avoidance scope she'd personally rebuilt twice. And on the third day, forty minutes before the scope showed her the debris field, she looked down at Milo's drawing of *the loud place*—a spiral of black pencil with small red marks scattered along its outer arm like seeds—and understood that the boy had already told her exactly what she was about to see.

The red marks were rocks. Eleven of them. The scope counted eleven returns.

She sat very still in the pilot's chair for a long moment, listening to the ship breathe around her, and then she called Juniper forward without quite knowing what she was going to say to him.

* * *

He came the way he always came, moving through the corridors of the *Long Haul* like a man trying not to disturb the furniture of someone else's house. He stood behind her chair—not touching it, never touching anything—and read the scope over her shoulder.

"Eleven," Rhea said. "Count them."

He counted. She watched his reflection in the canopy glass do it, those pale optical surfaces flicking through some internal arithmetic no human eye could follow.

"The drawing has eleven," she said. "Drawn by an eight-year-old who has never been off Meridian. Who has never seen a debris field. Who drew this—" she fanned the sheets across the console, "—three days before we launched."

Juniper said nothing. He was good at saying nothing. She'd noticed that about him early on, back when he was just the gray man who hauled freight and never made conversation: he wore silence like other people wore coats, fitted to him, broken in, comfortable. But this silence had a different texture. This silence was load-bearing.

"What else is he receiving?" Rhea asked.

It was the question she'd been carrying since the Shoals, since the night she'd found Milo asleep in the cargo bay with his pencil still in his hand and a half-finished chart under his cheek, and Juniper standing over him with an expression she had no name for. Not tenderness. Something older than tenderness. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing a door he'd sealed decades ago standing open.

"I don't know," Juniper said finally.

"You know more than that."

"I know the signal is real." His voice was low, pitched beneath the ambient hum of the deck so that only she could hear. "I know it's military in origin. I know it shouldn't be transmitting anymore." A pause, long enough for the scope to cycle twice. "I don't know why it would choose children. I don't know why it would choose *him*."

"Maybe it didn't choose him. Maybe children are just the ones who answer."

Juniper turned that over. She could see him doing it, some vast slow machinery turning behind the flat gray calm of his face. "That would be worse," he said.

"Yeah," Rhea agreed. "I thought so too."

* * *

The debris field took six hours to thread.

By rights it should have taken sixteen, or should have ended them. It was a graveyard of some ancient collision—hulls and rock and frozen volatiles ground together into a rotating belt of shrapnel, tumbling fragments the size of buildings crossing paths the size of continents. Rhea flew it the way she'd flown everything since they'd lost their cargo and their cover at the Shoals: stripped down, running dark, reactor at minimum, the *Long Haul* reduced to its bones and then to less than bones, because the twins kept finding more things to pull apart and feed to the drive.

But she flew it following a child's drawing.

She held the sheet against her knee on the descent leg—Milo had drawn the field as seen from above, which made no sense, because no one had ever seen this field from above, but the geometry held anyway. The safe corridor wound through the belt in a lazy S, and where the drawing showed a gap, there was a gap. Where the drawing showed a cluster marked with a small anxious scribble—*this part bites*, Milo had written—there was a cluster of fragments tumbling in resonance, a natural grinder that would have chewed the *Long Haul* into confetti if she'd trusted her own instruments instead of the paper.

Her own instruments, as it happened, agreed with the paper. That was the thing she couldn't stop thinking about afterward, sitting in the galley with her hands wrapped around a bulb of recycled tea while the twins argued cheerfully about coolant routing. The instruments agreed. The passive scopes agreed. The mass sensors agreed. Everything human-made and rational and calibrated agreed with a colored-pencil spiral drawn by a boy who talked in his sleep in a language nobody on the ship spoke.

Impossibly accurate wasn't even the phrase anymore. There was nothing impossible about it if you accepted one premise: that somewhere out past the edge of everything, something was looking at this same debris field and telling a sleeping child about it. Something with eyes. Something patient enough to teach.

Wonder, she thought, was supposed to feel warm. This felt like standing on ice over deep water.

* * *

On the fifth day, Milo came to the cockpit.

He didn't ask permission; children stowaways operated on a different legal framework entirely, one in which every door was theirs. He climbed into the second seat—the twins had rigged it with cushions and a folded blanket until it looked almost like a throne—and sat with his legs swinging, watching the blank dark with the untroubled attention of someone watching a familiar room.

"It's quiet here," he said.

"That's dead space, kid. Quiet's the whole product."

"No." He shook his head, solemn. "It's quiet like after singing. When everybody stops but the room's still full of it."

Rhea glanced at him. In the low light of the cockpit his face was all shadows and shine, and she was struck again by how ordinary he looked—scrawny, gap-toothed, a smudge of graphite still on his jaw from falling asleep on his drawings. Nothing about him said *receiver*. Everything about him said *kid who needs a bath*.

"Do you hear it now?" she asked. "The counting voice?"

"Not counting anymore." He frowned, working at something. "Counting's almost done. Now it's more like... showing. Like when you close your eyes and press them and see colors? It shows me colors and I draw what they are." He pointed at the canopy, at a patch of darkness off the port bow where, as far as her scopes could tell, there was absolutely nothing. "There's a door out there."

"There's nothing out there."

"There's a door," Milo repeated, with the mild immovability of the truly certain. "A big one. It's cold. It's been closed a really long time and it wants to be opened but it's scared of who opens it." He swung his legs. "It likes Juniper."

Rhea's tea went cold in her hands.

"Why does it like Juniper?" she asked, keeping her voice light the way you keep a rope light when you're walking someone across a ravine.

Milo considered the question with the seriousness it deserved, whatever it deserved. "Because he remembers," he said. "Everybody else forgot on purpose. He forgot on purpose too, but it didn't take. It never takes with him." He yawned enormously, suddenly eight years old again and done with metaphysics. "Can I stay here till sleep-time? The twins snore."

"They're your brothers."

"That's why I know they snore."

She let him stay. She watched him fall asleep in the co-pilot's seat with his cheek against the harness strap, and she watched the dark beyond the canopy, and she thought: *a door that likes Juniper.* She thought: *he forgot on purpose, but it didn't take.*

She thought about the way Juniper had gone still, back at the listening post on Meridian, when Amara described the dream. She'd assumed then that it was fear. Flying beside him these five days, sharing watches, trading silence—she was beginning to suspect it was something closer to homesickness. The terrible kind. The kind you feel toward a place you burned down yourself.

* * *

They found the beacon on the seventh day.

Or rather, Milo's chart found it, and Rhea confirmed it, and the whole crew crowded into the cockpit to look at a point of light so faint it barely qualified as a fact. A relay transponder, pinging on a frequency band so old that the twins' modern receivers initially classified it as instrument noise. Juniper identified it in four seconds flat, reciting the designation like a prayer he'd been forced to memorize: *Horizon Deep Relay, colonial network node seven, decommissioned—* and then he stopped, because the word *decommissioned* clearly tasted wrong in his mouth, and everyone heard it stop.

"Decommissioned relays don't transmit," said Odile, the taller twin, from the doorway.

"This one disagrees," said her brother Ansel.

Rhea ran the numbers while they argued. The beacon put them nine days from the relay at their current burn—nine days through progressively worse nothing, with fuel margins that would have made her old flight instructor weep openly. Behind them, somewhere in the dark they'd crossed, was an interdiction fleet that had known their route before they'd flown it. Ahead was a machine that had been talking to children in their sleep for who knew how long, teaching an eight-year-old to draw maps of places no mapmaker had ever surveyed.

She looked at Milo's latest sheet, pinned above the nav console where she'd started keeping them, in order, like scripture. The newest drawing showed the relay itself: a spindled structure like a seedpod, half of it scribbled over in heavy black—*the broken part*—and a single figure standing at its base, drawn in gray pencil. Small. Patient. Holding something up.

She hadn't shown that figure to Juniper yet. She wasn't sure she ever would. Some things you let a man discover on his own schedule.

"Nine days," she said aloud, and the cockpit went quiet around the number. "Fuel says we can make it if we strip everything that isn't thrust. Reactor says maybe. The kid's chart says yes." She tapped the paper, the wobbling circles, the long gray line labeled THE WAY THAT IS NOT A WAY. "I've flown on worse recommendations."

"And the fleet?" Juniper asked quietly.

Rhea looked at the passive display, at the enormous blank dark behind them, and felt the ice creak under her feet.

"The fleet," she said, "is going to be the least of our problems. Because somebody sold us once already, and whoever bought us knows exactly where this door is—and I don't think they're coming out here to knock politely."

Behind her, in the co-pilot's seat, Milo stirred in his sleep and murmured something in that language none of them spoke—and then, clear as a bell, in plain colonial English, right on the edge of waking:

*"Nine days. Hurry."*
