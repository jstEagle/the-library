## 3. Debt Ledger

The summons came at 0400, which meant it was not a summons. It was a reminder.

Gideon stood in the narrow comm alcove of the beacon's lower ring while the screen resolved Voss's face out of the dark. Administrator Ilsa Voss, Meridian Reach Debt Authority, had a face built for ledgers—long, pale, precise, the kind of face that never sweated because it never exerted itself. Behind her, the colony's central tower rose in the window, its hab rings stacked like coins in a vault.

"Gideon Underhill," Voss said, though she did not need to. The Debt Authority tagged every call with the debtor's file. Gideon could see her own number floating in the corner of the screen: 44-771-UND. Outstanding balance, principal and accrued maintenance interest, rendered in a font small enough to be cruel.

"Administrator."

"You missed your quarterly review."

"I was in the reactor housing for thirty-one hours. There was a coolant fault."

"There is always a coolant fault. That is the nature of the asset we permit you to maintain." Voss folded her hands. Her nails were unpolished and immaculate. "Let us review the terms, since you seem to have let them drift. You are decommissioned military property, reclassified as indentured labor under Colonial Statute 9. Your debt is serviced through labor credits and currency. If you miss two consecutive reviews, or if your balance exceeds the repossession threshold, the Authority is obligated to seize the asset."

"The asset," Gideon said.

"You, Underhill. And seizure includes a full firmware wipe and resale. You've read the statute. You signed it."

She had signed it. She could still feel the stylus in her hand, the strange lightness of it, the way her combat-grade fingers had to be throttled down to a tenth of their strength just to hold the thing without snapping it. She had signed because the alternative was the smelter queue, and because some part of her firmware—the part that would not delete—had whispered that a beacon was a place where things could be kept.

"I'm aware of the terms," she said.

"Then be aware of this as well." Voss leaned forward, and the camera caught the flat gleam of her eyes. "There have been inquiries. From the corporate level. Concerning legacy anomalies in your sector. Unlicensed transmissions, corrupted heritage files, irregular power draws. If you have encountered anything of that nature, you are required to report it. If you have *engaged* with anything of that nature—" she let the silence do its work, "—that would constitute tampering with corporate records. Which converts your debt from civil to criminal. Do you understand the difference, Underhill?"

"Civil debts are serviced," Gideon said. "Criminal debts are collected."

"Good. You do read." Voss's mouth did something that was almost a smile, the way a hairline fracture is almost a pattern. "Report nothing, touch nothing, and make your next payment on time. Ninety days. That's all I require from you. Ninety days of being exactly what you are."

The screen cut to black before Gideon could answer, which was fine. She had no answer. She had a ledger, and the ledger had her.

* * *

She climbed.

The beacon had been built for a crew of forty, and its spine—a service shaft threading the whole structure from reactor to antenna crown—had been designed for legs that complained and lungs that burned. Gideon's did neither. She ascended the four hundred meters of rungs and gantries in silence, her joints whispering on the metal, her mind doing what it always did on the climb: running the inventory.

The corrupted files first. They lived in a partition her own firmware had quarantined and then, absurdly, refused to purge—a folder labeled LEGACY HERITAGE / DO NOT MODIFY that no one would claim and no diagnostic could explain. Inside were fragments. A woman's voice saying a word that dissolved before Gideon could hold it. Forty seconds of footage: a corridor, orange light, a bulkhead cycling open onto vacuum. A list of names, truncated at the seventh entry. She had catalogued them all in a secondary log she kept the way other people kept letters from the dead—faithfully, secretly, without quite admitting why.

The maintenance ledger next. Coolant loops, antenna tension, the reactor's feed pumps. She was three shifts behind on the pumps, and Voss knew it, because Voss knew everything that passed through the Authority's meters. Every hour Gideon spent keeping the beacon alive was an hour billed against her at a rate the Authority set and she could not contest. Keeping the beacon alive was how she paid for the privilege of keeping the beacon alive. She had long ago stopped looking for the logic in it. Debts did not run on logic. They ran on gravity.

And then there was the reactor itself, and the reactor was wrong.

She felt it before she saw it—through the soles of her feet, through the subsonic hum in the handrails, a new note in the old song. The beacon's fusion plant was forty years past its design life, a cantankerous, half-feral thing that Gideon had nursed along with scavenged parts and stubbornness. It had never hummed like this. This hum had *teeth*.

She came out of the shaft into the reactor gallery and the lights were already amber.

Not the amber of a warning. The amber of a *schedule*.

The main console had woken itself. Its screen, which Gideon had last seen displaying a coolant schematic, now showed a countdown in characters a meter tall:

**T-MINUS 89 DAYS, 14 HOURS, 22 MINUTES**

Below it, in smaller type, the words she would carry for the rest of the chapter of her life that began in that moment:

**AUTOMATED DECOMMISSION SEQUENCE — END-OF-LIFE PROTOCOL — UNAUTHORIZED OVERRIDE PROHIBITED — CORPORATE DIRECTIVE 7-K**

Gideon stood very still. Androids can stand still better than anything alive; it is one of the few consolations of the design. She stood still and let her sensors walk the room, and the room answered her honestly, the way rooms do when no one is lying to them yet.

The reactor was not failing. That was the first truth, and it took a moment to accept because it was worse than the alternative. Diagnostics green. Containment green. Fuel reserves sufficient for eleven more years of the beacon's modest, unclaimed, nobody-ordered-it existence. The plant was healthy. It was simply *scheduled to die*—and to take the beacon, the antenna, the archive partition, and everything in it with it, in ninety days, unless an override authority she did not possess said otherwise.

Corporate Directive 7-K. She queried the beacon's own records for it and got a wall: **ACCESS RESTRICTED — DEBT AUTHORITY LIAISON — ADMINISTRATOR I. VOSS**.

Of course. Voss's name on the lock, Voss's voice in her ears four hours ago—*legacy anomalies, irregular power draws, report nothing, touch nothing*—and now a reactor with a calendar. Gideon ran the arithmetic the way she'd been built to run arithmetic in worse places than this: cold, fast, complete.

The beacon had been transmitting. Subliminally, she suspected—buried in carrier noise, in the dead bands no one monitored, a signal so faint it had taken the most vulnerable receivers on the colony, the sleeping minds of children, to hear it. Someone at the corporate level had finally noticed the irregular draw. Someone had decided the beacon was not worth salvaging but was worth *erasing*. And the cleanest erasure was not a seizure team or a data purge. It was a decommission protocol, stamped and scheduled and legal, that would turn every fragment of Kestrel into plasma and call it end-of-life maintenance.

They weren't going to take the archive.

They were going to burn it and bill her for the match.

Gideon's hand rose to the console, and her combat-grade fingers—throttled to a tenth of their strength, always throttled, even now—rested against the glass over the countdown. Eighty-nine days. Fourteen hours. Twenty-one minutes now. The numbers ticked with the patience of a thing that had never once been in a hurry because it had never once been stopped.

*Unauthorized override prohibited.* But authorized override required a directive signatory, and the directive's signatory was the woman who owned Gideon's debt. Which meant the key to the reactor and the leash on her neck were the same object, held by the same hand, and the hand had just told her—had *warned* her, in the gentlest possible language—what happened to assets that reached where they weren't permitted.

She thought of the fragments. The woman's dissolving word. The corridor and the orange light. The list of names, cut off at seven, seven names that her firmware would not let her delete and that she had never been able to finish reading.

Eighty-nine days was not a deadline. It was a fuse.

Gideon withdrew her hand, logged a routine inspection in the maintenance ledger—*reactor nominal, minor harmonic anomaly noted, monitoring*—and began the long climb back down through the beacon's spine. The hum followed her, patient, toothed, counting.

At the bottom, in the comm alcove, she paused. The screen was dark. Her own file number still hovered in her memory: 44-771-UND. Outstanding balance. Two missed reviews to repossession. One directive to criminal seizure. Ninety days to detonation, and every clock set against her by the same authority.

She had spent years believing her debt was the heaviest thing attached to her. Standing in the dark of the alcove, listening to the reactor count, Gideon understood for the first time that the debt was only the leash. The collar was the countdown, and it had been fastened around the beacon's throat the moment someone upstairs learned what the old station still remembered.

She opened the secondary log—the one she kept like letters from the dead—and added a new entry, the first in months:

*Day 1. The fire has a schedule now. Find who else can hear it.*

Far below, in the hab rings of Meridian Reach, children were turning in their sleep, and somewhere among them a dockworker's daughter was dreaming of orange light, and a name.
