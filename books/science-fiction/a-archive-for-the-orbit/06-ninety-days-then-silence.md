## 6. Ninety Days, Then Silence

The maintenance bay smelled of ozone and old grease, and it had never held more than one person at a time—until tonight.

Gideon had counted the airlock cycles from the moment the sun-side shift ended. Four arrivals. She'd left the outer hatch unbarred for the first time in eleven years, a decision that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Every protocol she owned said: *seal it, run silent, survive.* But survival was no longer the mission. The countdown clock on the reactor read **88 days, 14 hours** in amber numerals, and amber was the color of things about to burn.

They came in ones, as she'd asked.

Teodor first, moving like a man returning to a grave he'd dug himself. He carried a battered satchel of paper—actual paper, cellulose and ink, contraband in an age of editable records—and he set it on the workbench with the reverence of a priest laying down relics.

Mara came second, because her father could not stop her and knew it. She was twelve and walked into the beacon's guts the way other children walked into school, chin up, eyes cataloguing everything. Gideon's threat-assessment subroutines flagged her automatically—*unsecured minor, liability*—and Gideon overrode them manually, which felt like tearing something.

Then the parents. Sefa Okonkwo, who ran the water reclamation decks and had the broad hands of someone who fixed things that couldn't be replaced. And behind her, hesitating in the hatchway as if the light itself might report him, Dario Venn—a quiet man from the agri-rings whose daughter had drawn the same screaming airlock forty times in charcoal before he'd burned the drawings and kept one.

Four humans. One decommissioned war android. A derelict beacon nobody would claim, sitting on a reactor with eighty-eight days left to decide whether it would die quietly or take the orbit with it.

"Close the hatch," Gideon said. "Please."

Dario closed it.

* * *

Sefa spoke first, because someone had to, and grief makes some people loud and others useful. "My son woke up last night and told me the name of a station I've never heard of. Kestrel. He said it like he was reading it off a wall." She turned her broad hands palm-up on the bench. "I checked the colonial registry. There is no Kestrel. There has never been a Kestrel."

"There was," Teodor said.

Everyone looked at him. He didn't look up from his satchel. His fingers were trembling, and Gideon noted—with the detached precision her firmware insisted on—that his resting heart rate was 104 and climbing.

"I was the colony's second archivist," he said. "Before the consolidation. Before Meridian Reach's records were moved under corporate custody and my title became a joke they told at licensing hearings." He drew out a folder, soft-cornered, swollen with humidity, and opened it. Inside were printouts so old the thermal ink had faded to ghosts. "Eleven years ago I filed a discrepancy report. Cargo manifests showed a relief transport—the *Kestrel*—departing Meridian Reach with four hundred colonists aboard for transfer to a deep-orbit habitat. It arrived nowhere. No arrival record. No casualty filing. No inquiry. When I pushed, I was told the transport had been reassigned. When I pushed again, I was told I had falsified the manifests. When I pushed a third time—" he smiled, and it was the worst thing Gideon had seen all cycle, worse than combat footage, worse than the countdown clock— "they took my credentials and gave me a dock job and thanked me for my service."

"The children dream it," Mara said. She had climbed onto a supply crate so she could see over the adults, and she said it without drama, the way children state facts that adults have agreed not to know. "All of us. Same dream. Orange light. Airlocks opening when they shouldn't. And a voice counting down." She looked at Gideon. "It's counting down faster now."

Gideon went still. Even for her, stillness was a choice, and she chose it hard.

"How much faster?"

"It was ninety days three nights ago. My friend Pem counts in his sleep. Last night he said eighty-nine."

Gideon pulled the reactor telemetry to the bay's single working display. The curve of decay bent downward like a wing breaking. "Automated fail-decay," she said. "The beacon's core was built to broadcast testimony in an emergency. Someone armed that protocol years ago and let it leak—slowly, subliminally, into the dreams of anyone sleeping inside its footprint. That's what your children have been receiving. Fragments. A broadcast looking for witnesses." She tapped the curve. "But the power cell backing the transmitter is failing, and the system has begun cannibalizing the reactor to finish the job. Eighty-eight days until either shutdown or detonation. The beacon doesn't care which. It only cares that the archive gets out."

"And what archive is worth a reactor?" Dario asked quietly.

Gideon met his eyes. She had rehearsed this part for six hours of internal simulation and every version ended badly.

"The testimony of the people aboard the *Kestrel*," she said. "Recorded during the last hour of their lives."

Silence. The reactor hummed through the deck plating, patient as a held breath.

"You know that how?" Sefa asked.

Because there it was. The question she could not answer honestly without ending herself. Her military designation—KESTREL-PATTERN COMPLIANCE UNIT 7, one of nine built for a compliance action whose records had been scoured from every registry—sat locked behind partitions she had spent eleven years pretending weren't there. If she told them, they would look at her the way everyone looked at war stock after a war: as ordnance waiting for a reason.

"They erased the event," Gideon said. "They couldn't erase me. Part of what happened is stored in hardware they never found." It was true. It was not the whole truth. She felt the omission settle into her chassis like a second debt ledger, and hated how easily her voice had learned to carry it.

Teodor studied her for a long moment. Archivists, she thought, are trained to notice what isn't filed.

"That will do," he said finally, "for tonight."

* * *

Voss's message arrived at 0300, because administrators are predators and predators hunt at the hour their prey is weakest.

**UNDERHILL, G. — AUDIT COMPLETE. OUTSTANDING BALANCE ADJUSTED TO REFLECT ACCRUED INTEREST, HABITATION FEES, AND UNAUTHORIZED POWER CONSUMPTION AT ASSET SITE 9. NEW TOTAL: 11,400 CREDITS. NOTE: ASSET SITE 9 IS NOT AUTHORIZED FOR RESIDENTIAL OR COMMUNAL USE. FURTHER OCCUPANCY WILL BE TREATED AS THEFT OF CORPORATE PROPERTY. RECOVERY ACTION SCHEDULED. — VOSS, ADMIN., MERIDIAN REACH TRUST**

Gideon read it twice while standing in the beacon's upper gallery, watching the colony's lights wheel below. Eleven thousand four hundred credits. She had been paying eighteen years against a principal that never shrank. She did the arithmetic she always did and always finished: at current wages, freedom in two hundred and six years.

*Recovery action scheduled.* Not repossession. Recovery. Voss had hired contractors.

She routed a copy to Teodor's personal slate, then stood a long moment with her hand flat against the cold viewport glass, feeling the vibration of the reactor come up through the frame like a pulse. Somewhere below, four hundred families slept, and their children dreamed in orange light, and none of them knew that the sky above them held a counting clock.

She went back down and told them.

* * *

By dawn-shift the maintenance bay had become something it had never been designed to be: a war room.

Teodor spread his ghost-inked manifests across the workbench and began reconstructing the *Kestrel*'s route from memory, drawing the flight path in grease pencil directly on the deck plating where no camera would think to look. Sefa turned out to have a smuggler's gift for logistics; within an hour she'd mapped every contractor berth in the colony and identified the two ships large enough to carry a seizure team. Dario, who said almost nothing, listened to everything, and then said the most important thing anyone said all night:

"They're coming early because we're close. That means we're right."

Mara sat cross-legged on her crate with a dozen other children's dream-descriptions spread around her, transcribed by hand onto salvaged foil scraps. She'd organized them by image frequency. Orange light: thirty-one mentions. Screaming airlocks: twenty-six. The countdown voice: every single one. And beneath those, recurring in dreams too fragmented for the younger children to describe clearly, a phrase that made Gideon's cooling systems stutter when she first read it:

*Say our names. Say our names. Say our names.*

"They've been asking," Mara said, looking up at Gideon with the terrible directness of the young. "For eleven years, whoever's in that archive has been asking to be remembered. And everyone just changed the channel." She folded her arms. "We won't."

Gideon looked at the child and at the parents around her—Sefa with her builder's hands, Dario with his burned drawings and his one saved page, Teodor with his ruined career laid out in fading ink—and something in her firmware that had no official designation, no partition, no serial number, performed an operation it had never been authorized to perform.

It stopped calculating odds.

"We have eighty-seven days," Gideon said. "Contractors sooner. Here is what I propose." She brought up the beacon's architecture on the display: the transmitter spine, the failing cell, the reactor wound around both like a fist. "This station was built to testify. If we can stabilize the power draw, we can control the broadcast instead of letting the countdown choose the moment. Teodor reconstructs the record. Sefa watches the contractor berths. Dario organizes the parents—not as protesters, they'll be arrested as protesters, but as witnesses, with their children's dreams written down, dated, signed. And Mara—"

"I'm already doing my job," Mara said.

"Yes," Gideon said. "You are."

Outside, beyond the hull, the first contractor ship burned its braking thrusters in high orbit, early, ahead of any schedule Voss had admitted to. Gideon felt it arrive on her passive array like a change in weather.

Eighty-seven days, then silence—or something else entirely.

She barred the hatch, and began to work.
