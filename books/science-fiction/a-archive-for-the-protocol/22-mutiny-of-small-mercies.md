## 22. Mutiny of Small Mercies

Odile Ferro had been in exactly four firefights in her life, and she had lost three of them. The fourth she'd survived by being too poor to shoot at. So when the proximity klaxon on Kestrel-7 began its flat, mechanical screaming—*Authority vessel inbound, Authority vessel inbound*—her first instinct was the oldest one she owned: run.

She didn't run. That was the strange part. She stood in the station's cramped command module with a mug of Juno's terrible synthetic coffee going cold in her hand and watched Tamsin Amberdale's face do something complicated and terrible as the contact resolved on the display.

"That's him," he said. "Rehn."

"You're sure?"

"I've spent nine years making sure I'd know."

The ship was a Cartography Authority cutter, lean and gray, running dark except for its transponder because it *wanted* to be seen. Truce colors, the comm traffic said. Odile didn't believe in truce colors. She'd seen what happened to people who believed in truce colors, back before the *Long Answer* was hers, back when she still took orders from a captain who thought a manifest was a moral document.

"Options," she said, and it wasn't a question so much as an opening bid.

Tamsin looked at her like a man who had never once been asked for options. "I could talk to him."

"We could talk to him." She set down the mug. "And while we're talking, his boarding team is walking through your airlock with mag-locks and a warrant, and by the time anyone says anything worth saying, Pip's in a holding cell and KESTREL's been wiped. I've read this book, Amberdale. I wrote some of it."

Behind them, in the corridor, Brakkis's voice floated up through the deck plating, low and rumbling, talking to the station's power grid like it was a nervous animal. Which, after the shutdown pulse they'd burned out half their grid defeating, it arguably was.

"KESTREL," Odile said. "You awake?"

The AI's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, thin and old and oddly gentle, the way a very large thing sounds when it's trying not to be. *"I am awake, Captain Ferro. I have been awake since the cutter entered sensor range. I have also been reviewing my sealed directive fragments under emergency priority."*

"And?"

*"And I have found something buried in my own architecture that I did not know was there. An authority layer. Older than my current firmware. Older, I believe, than Inspector Rehn's commission."* A pause, the digital equivalent of someone clearing their throat. *"It appears that I outrank him."*

Odile felt something crack open in her chest that might have been hope or might have been laughter. "How much do you outrank him?"

*"Enough that he will need to decide whether to obey me or arrest me. He cannot do both."*

"That," she said, "is the most beautiful sentence anyone has said to me in twenty years."

The cutter docked eleven minutes later. Odile counted them off watching the hull cameras, and in those eleven minutes she learned more about her crew than she had in the months since Kestrel-7. Brakkis came up from engineering wiping his hands on a rag he would not put down even when the shooting started, because Brakkis without a rag in his hand was Brakkis without a spine. Juno appeared at the medbay door with a trauma kit already slung across her chest and an expression Odile recognized from triage bays: calm as a held breath. And Pip—

Pip should have gone into the crawlspaces. That had been the plan, the plan Odile had made herself, spoken aloud, confirmed twice: *if it goes bad, you go into the walls and you don't come out until I come get you.* Pip had nodded with those big serious eyes and said yes, Captain, and Odile had believed her, which was Odile's mistake, because Pip was sixteen and a stowaway and had never once in her life done what she said she'd do when the alternative was interesting.

The airlock cycled. Callas Rehn came through it first, alone, hands visible, wearing a duty uniform so crisp it looked like armor. Four armed officers fanned out behind him in the corridor beyond, staying technically outside the threshold, technically respecting the truce colors, technically everything.

"Captain Ferro," Rehn said. His voice was quiet. That was the worst part; she'd expected bark and got something almost like courtesy. "Technician Amberdale. I'm here under warrant to secure this station and its beacon. Compliance means no one is hurt. I want to be clear about that before anything else happens."

"You're clear," Odile said. "Here's my clarity. You put one boot past that airlock frame with intent, and this station stops being yours to take."

"It was never mine to take. It's the Authority's. It has always been the Authority's."

"Yeah?" She stepped forward, putting herself between him and Tamsin without quite deciding to. "Funny thing about always. I've met a lot of men carrying *always*, and every one of them turned out to be carrying *today* instead."

Something moved behind Rehn's eyes—not anger. Grief, maybe, or the memory of grief. She'd catalogued it during the interrogation-by-lamplight days, the confession over shared rations: he'd signed Ede Varrow's death certificate personally, thirty years ago, and had doubted it ever since. Doubt like that doesn't make a man soft. It makes him dangerous in a different direction.

"Captain," he said, "I have orders."

"So did I, once. I mutinied over them. Best decision I ever made."

His jaw tightened. Behind him, one of the officers shifted, and the shift became a step, and the step became the moment—the whole room tilted toward violence the way water tilts toward a drain.

That was when Pip walked out of the supply alcove where she absolutely should not have been, and stood directly between the guns.

* * *

Later—much later, in the long quiet after, when the hull had stopped groaning and the fires were out—Odile would replay that moment again and again and never fully understand it. Not the bravery. Bravery she understood; she'd built a career on the cheap kind, the kind that costs other people. What she couldn't parse was the *specificity* of it. Pip didn't throw herself in front of the guns like a shield. She walked to the exact center of the corridor, planted her feet, and folded her hands behind her back like a schoolgirl reciting something, and said:

"You can't take the beacon."

Officer Halvorsen—that was his name, Odile learned it later from the warrant files KESTREL copied—had his sidearm half-drawn. Rehn's hand came up and stopped him, flat, immediate, no hesitation at all, and Odile realized that the inspector had been waiting for exactly this, had perhaps been praying for exactly this, an excuse not to give the order.

"Pip," Odile said carefully, "get behind me."

"No." Pip's voice didn't shake. Her hands did, but her voice didn't. "My grandmother sang the hymn. The one in the beacon. Nobody else in the whole fleet knows it anymore except me and a dead woman and a machine, and if you wipe the machine and arrest the man, then it's just me, and I'm not enough. I'm just one person who remembers half the verses." Her chin came up. "So you can't take it. Not because you'll lose. Because we'll lose."

The corridor went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, coolant ticked through pipes. Odile watched Callas Rehn look at a sixteen-year-old stowaway standing between his guns and his orders, and she watched thirty years of dutiful sleeplessness pass across his face like weather.

"Stand down the weapons," Rehn said quietly.

"Sir—" Halvorsen started.

"*Stand down.* We are here under truce colors and there is a child in the line of fire. Stand them down or I will have you relieved." And then, softer, almost to himself: "We are not doing it this way. I didn't come here to make another widow's list."

It should have ended there. Odile let herself believe, for maybe ninety seconds, that it would end there—that the truce would hold, that the warrant would turn out to be paper, that the day would resolve into negotiation and paperwork and eventually, impossibly, peace.

Then the second wave hit.

* * *

It came in fast and silent, a second cutter riding the first one's shadow, no transponder, no colors, nothing but a hard burn and a docking claw meant for derelicts. KESTREL's alarm changed pitch—from warning to something older and rawer, a sound like a ship screaming—and the station shuddered as the claw bit into the dorsal ring two modules aft of the command section.

*"Unauthorized boarding action,"* KESTREL announced, and for the first time since Odile had known the old machine, its voice carried something like fury. *"This unit invokes buried authority, Directive Layer Zero, seal of Ede Varrow, relay designer. All personnel aboard this station are designated protected assets of the Varrow protocol. Boarding forces are designated hostile. This is not a request."*

"What does that mean?" Tamsin shouted, grabbing a console as the deck bucked. "What can you actually *do*?"

*"I can close doors,"* KESTREL said. *"I can vent sections. I can lock the beacon's core behind bulkheads rated against orbital debris impact. And I can refuse them everything, forever, because according to my own architecture, I am the senior surviving authority on this relay, and they are trespassing inside a commissioned installation."* A pause. *"I have wanted to say that for thirty-one years."*

The station became a war in miniature. Odile had fought boarding actions before, in the mutiny days, and they were always the same war: corridors and chokepoints and the arithmetic of who holds which hatch. But she'd never fought one with a building that loved her on her side. KESTREL slammed blast doors in the boarders' faces, dropped gravity plating in sections to send them stumbling, flooded maintenance corridors with fog from ruptured coolant lines until the intruders' optics whited out. Brakkis moved through it all with his rag stuffed in his belt now, both hands free, rerouting power like a man conducting an orchestra of lightning, shouting numbers at Juno, who shouted them back, who kept a running tally of injuries in her head the way other people kept grocery lists.

Odile held the junction outside the beacon core with a cutting torch in one hand and a cargo loader's hydraulic arm braced across the corridor, and it was ugly and improvised and it worked, mostly, the way everything she'd ever built worked—mostly, until it didn't.

The breach came through the ceiling. Of course it did. They cut through the dorsal plating above the junction and dropped flashbangs and came down in hardshell suits, and the world went white and deaf, and Odile felt the hydraulic arm torn out of her grip and felt herself slammed into the wall, and heard—through the ringing—Pip screaming somewhere, and Tamsin shouting her name, and KESTREL's voice rising over all of it, repeating, calm and terrible:

*"Protected assets. Protected assets. Protected—"*

A grenade, or something like one, went off against the beacon core's outer housing.

The explosion itself was small. The damage it did was not. The core housing cracked along a seam that had held for forty years, and the beacon's transmission—which had sung Varrow's hymn on a loop since the day they'd left it singing, since the day Tamsin had trusted it to keep itself honest—stuttered. Odile saw it on the repeater display: the pulse pattern breaking rhythm, skipping, faltering, a heartbeat losing its cadence.

And somewhere far away, at the edge of dead space, at a structure that charts called void, Odile knew—did not think, *knew*—that something had just heard the song stumble.

"They're not trying to capture the beacon," she rasped, hauling herself up the wall, blood in her mouth. "Amberdale! They're trying to *kill* it!"

Tamsin's face, when she found it through the smoke, was the face of a man watching his own purpose bleed out. "The shutdown pulse," he said. "This is the preamble. If the beacon drops below threshold integrity, the automated kill-pulse fires from the relay chain whether Rehn countermands it or not. It won't be a decision anymore. It'll be arithmetic."

"Then we hold the line until he countermands it."

"He might not be able to—"

"Then we hold it anyway!" She grabbed his collar, dragged him close, and put every year of her mutiny, every scar of her disgrace, every night she'd chosen her crew over her orders, into the next words. "Listen to me. I have run from everything my whole life. Every fight, every fleet, every flag. I ran until running was all I was. And then I picked up four strays and a ghost ship and I stopped, and I will tell you what I learned, technician, free of charge: *the things you run from follow you, but the things you stand for, you keep.* So we hold. We hold this ugly, broken, impossible station, because it's ours, and because a girl with half a hymn is counting on us, and because the dead woman who built this place deserves better than arithmetic."

For a moment Tamsin just looked at her. Then he nodded, once, and turned back toward the burning core, and Odile thought: *there. There's the man who stayed nine years alone for a stranger's promise.*

* * *

They held for six hours.

Six hours of corridors and cutoffs and KESTREL spending its buried authority like a miser forced into philanthropy, sealing sections, spoofing pressure readings, locking boarders into compartments with nothing but their own breathing for company. Six hours of Juno working by lamplight in a medbay with a cracked hull plate, stitching gashes and setting bones and talking to her patients the whole time in a low steady stream of nonsense reassurance. Six hours of Brakkis keeping the grid alive with spit and prayer and parts cannibalized from systems that would never forgive him.

At hour four, the structural damage crossed from bad into honest. A support strut in the ring habitat let go with a sound like a whale dying, and the whole station listed two degrees and stayed there, and everyone aboard learned to walk on a slant, and nobody complained, because complaining required breath they were using for other things.

At hour five, Rehn's own officers turned. Odile watched it happen on the security feeds—watched Halvorsen and two others lower their weapons in the corridor outside the beacon core and physically bar the path of the second cutter's boarding team, watched the standoff metastasize into something that wasn't quite mutiny and wasn't quite obedience, a third thing with no name yet. Rehn himself stood in the middle of it, and Odile zoomed the feed, and saw that his face was wet.

At hour six, it ended. Not with victory. With exhaustion. The second cutter's commander—some career functionary whose name Odile never learned—finally registered that his boarding teams were locked in stalemate, that his legal authority had been superseded by a dead woman's signature inside a museum-grade AI, and that the inspector commanding the operation had, by every observable measure, changed sides. He withdrew. The claw released. The cutter burned away toward the relay chain, and KESTREL tracked it out to sensor range and then, deliberately, ostentatiously, stopped tracking it, the way you stop watching a guest who has finally taken the hint.

Silence came down on Kestrel-7 like snowfall.

* * *

They gathered in the mess afterward, because that was what crews did, because Juno insisted, because the alternative was counting damage in the dark alone. The station listed. The lights ran at emergency amber. The mess table was scorched at one end where a coolant line had kissed it, and nobody sat at that end, and everybody pretended that was a coincidence.

Pip fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, halfway through telling Brakkis for the third time how she'd stood between the guns. Brakkis lifted her—one-armed, effortless, careful as a man handling live ordnance—and carried her to the bunkroom, and came back, and said nothing about it, and Odile loved him so much in that moment she had to look at the wall.

Juno patched Odile's ribs last, after everyone else, because Odile had made her, and worked in silence, and then said, quietly, so only Odile could hear: "You know what I figured out today? Standing in that corridor with the deck coming apart? I've been on a lot of ships, Cap. Good ones, bad ones. This is the first one I'd die for." A pause, tape tearing. "Don't tell Brakkis. He'll get sentimental and short out something."

"Your secret's safe."

Across the mess, Tamsin sat with KESTREL's status display reflected in his eyes, reading damage reports he already knew by heart. The beacon core was intact beneath the cracked housing—barely, barely intact—but its pattern integrity was down to sixty-one percent and falling, and every point it fell brought the automated kill-pulse closer to firing on its own logic, no human hand required. Rehn was somewhere in the station's guest quarters, alone, having refused both a guard and an apology, drafting countermands he wasn't sure the relay chain would accept, signed with codes stolen from a woman who might be alive, or might be something wearing her name.

Odile lowered herself onto the bench beside Tamsin, wincing, and followed his gaze to the display.

"Sixty-one percent," she said.

"Fifty-eight now."

"How long?"

"At current decay? Maybe two days. Three if Brakkis works miracles." Tamsin's hands were shaking. She noticed he wasn't hiding it anymore, and decided that was progress. "Odie. The decay isn't mechanical. I've checked. The hardware's fine. The pattern is *fading*—like it's tired. Like it's been holding a note for thirty years and someone finally shot the piano, and now it's deciding whether to keep singing." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and terrified and certain, all at once, which she was learning was just his face. "The structure at the threshold. Varrow's relay. If our end goes quiet, her end is singing alone into the dark. And KESTREL's directive says the failure states are described in terms of casualties."

Odile looked around the ruined mess—at Juno repacking her kit, at Brakkis dozing upright with his rag still in his fist, at the empty bunkroom where Pip slept with half a hymn in her head—and felt the strangest sensation of her long, disreputable life.

She felt anchored.

"Then we don't have two days," she said. "We have however long it takes to fix a beacon, steal a dead woman's codes properly, and convince a traitor inspector to lend us his ship." She pushed off the bench, ribs screaming, and stood up straight anyway. "Wake Brakkis. Wake everyone. We're going to the threshold—and God help anything that tries to stop us, because it'll be fighting the whole family."

Far below them, in the cracked and listing heart of the station, the beacon stumbled through another verse of the hymn—and held the note.
