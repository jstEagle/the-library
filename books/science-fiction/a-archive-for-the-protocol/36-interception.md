## 36. Interception

The pulse left the Authority relay at Meridian Gap eleven minutes before Callas Rehn finished reading the order that launched it.

He knew the format by heart—had signed a hundred of them in his career, most of them paperwork, three of them funerals. AUTOMATED PROTOCOL COMPLIANCE DIRECTIVE 7-KILO. Priority: absolute. Override authority: none. It was designed to be unanswerable, and it was, which was precisely why he had spent thirty years making sure he never had to answer one.

Until now.

The order sat on his slate in cold amber text, timestamped from a command node four systems away, countersigned with authorization codes that made his stomach drop through the deck plating. VARROW, E. — PROTOCOL AUTHORITY — TIER ONE. He had checked the codes twice against the warrant KESTREL had copied for him during the truce. They matched. They matched perfectly, down to the checksum cadence, and that was the horror of it, because Ede Varrow had been dead for thirty-one years and her signature was still moving through the Authority's nervous system like a ghost in the wiring, signing orders, cutting lifelines, keeping the door closed one severed thread at a time.

Or opening it. He no longer knew which. That was the thing about working for the Cartography Authority long enough: eventually you stopped being able to tell the difference between protecting the map and burning the territory.

"Time to impact?" he asked.

His adjutant, a gray-faced woman named Sorel who had followed him through two postings and one disgrace, didn't look up from her console. "Pulse is running dark-lane, no burn signature to chase. At its current vector—" she did the arithmetic without wanting to, he could see it in her jaw, "—four hours, forty minutes to Kestrel-7. Six hours, ten if it corrects for the station's drift."

"And Long Answer?"

"Still docked at the station. Captain Ferro has not filed a departure plan." A pause. "She's filed nothing. She's been asked twice."

Rehn almost smiled. Odile Ferro, refusing even the courtesy of paperwork while an Authority kill-pulse fell toward her ship like a coin dropped down a well. He had threatened her crew with amnesty-for-silence two days ago and she had looked at him the way you look at weather—acknowledging it, unimpressed by it.

He stood. His knees complained; thirty years of cold transit did that, thawing you out one indignity at a time.

"Sorel. Get me everything we have on Directive 7-Kilo's handshake protocol. The full authentication chain, not the summary."

"You're going to try to spoof it." Not a question. Her voice had gone flat, the way voices go when they've already done the math on what a thing will cost. "Sir, the handshake requires a Tier One biometric key at transmission origin. We don't have one. We can't stop the pulse from here—we don't have the bandwidth, we don't have the codes, and even if we did, interfering with a 7-Kilo is treason under—"

"I know what it's under. I wrote half of it."

* * *

Here is what Callas Rehn knew about Ede Varrow, and when he had learned it:

He knew her name first as a file designation, twenty-six years old and newly badged, cataloguing decommissioned assets in the Authority's sub-basement archive on Ceres Station. MERIDIAN VALE — FLIGHT ERASED — SEE SEALED APPENDIX. There was no sealed appendix. There never was, in those files; the seal was the point, a lock welded over an empty room. But the manifest fragment he'd pulled that day, young and curious and too slow to be careful, had listed a passenger. One passenger. Varrow, E., role: protocol architect, status—

Status: DECEASED PRIOR TO MANIFEST DATE.

He had reported the anomaly, because that was the job, because he was twenty-six and believed the Authority was a body and bodies heal themselves. What came back, nine days later, was a reassignment to deep patrol and a personal visit from a director whose name he would spend decades pretending not to remember. The director had been kind. That was the worst part. Kind, and tired, and absolutely certain.

"Some doors," the director had told him, "are load-bearing. You don't get to know what they hold up. You just keep people from leaning on them."

Rehn had spent his whole career since then keeping people from leaning. He had signed Varrow's death certificate personally—the real one, the administrative one, the one that turned a woman into a closed case—and he had done it neatly, correctly, and without ever once seeing a body. For thirty years he had told himself that neatness was a kind of mercy.

Then a beacon in dead space started singing a lullaby, and a salvage tug full of nobodies answered it, and the ghost in the wiring started signing orders again, and every neat lie Rehn had built his life on began, quietly, to come apart along its seams.

* * *

Long Answer answered his hail on the third attempt, which meant Ferro had let him sweat exactly as long as she felt like.

Her face filled the comm—weathered, sharp-eyed, hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of power cable. Behind her, out of focus, someone small moved across the frame. Pip. The girl who had recognized a colony-era hymn inside a beacon's pulse code, who had stood between guns without flinching. Rehn had read the incident reports. He'd also watched the girl feed a stray cat in the docking bay when she thought no one was looking, and he trusted the second observation more.

"Inspector." Ferro's voice was dry as regolith. "Come to arrest us properly this time? You'll want more men. And better lawyers."

"I'm coming alone. I need your ship."

That got her attention. Her eyes narrowed, and behind her the small shape stopped moving.

"My ship," Ferro said, "is currently the only thing standing between a station full of my people and whatever your Authority decides to throw at us next. So before I laugh at you, Inspector, tell me why I shouldn't."

"Because in six hours a compliance pulse hits this system and if it lands, it doesn't shut down your beacon. It tears the door open. Uncontrolled. Everything Varrow built, everything your technician rewrote—it all comes apart at once, and whatever's been learning on the other side gets to walk through the wreckage." He let that sit. "And because I'm not asking as the Authority anymore. As of approximately"—he checked the time, absurdly, uselessly—"five hours ago, I'm asking as a man who intends to commit treason, and needs a fast ship and a crazier captain."

Silence on the line. Then, distantly, Pip's voice, high and clear: *"Tell him yes. Tell him yes, tell him yes—"*

Ferro closed her eyes briefly, the expression of a woman consulting some internal ledger of debts and finding herself, annoyingly, solvent enough to help.

"One condition," she said. "You ride in the jump seat and you touch nothing. Brakkis says where things go. Juno says who's healthy. And if this goes wrong, you're the one who explains to the kid why."

"That's three conditions."

"Take it up with my lawyer. Oh wait." The channel cut.

* * *

They burned hard for the interception point, Long Answer's old engines pushed past their rated tolerance by a Brakkis who swore in three languages and a dead one. Rehn sat in the jump seat as instructed and touched nothing and explained, over and over, while Juno strapped medical monitors to his chest and Pip hovered at the edge of the cockpit with the particular ferocity of a twelve-year-old guarding something fragile.

The plan was insane. Everyone agreed on this. It had the clean unanimity of a suicide note.

A 7-Kilo compliance pulse wasn't a missile. It was worse: a handshake, a formal query broadcast at lightspeed-plus along the beacon's own carrier wave, demanding authentication from the relay's core. Answer wrong, or fail to answer, and the pulse escalated—first to shutdown commands, then to a resonance spike tuned to crack the beacon's containment lattice open like a struck bell. The Authority had built it elegant, back when Rehn was young. Elegant and irreversible. No remote abort. The only way to stop a 7-Kilo after launch was to meet it in transit and complete the handshake yourself—with valid credentials—issuing a stand-down order signed by a Tier One key.

They had no Tier One key.

They had, instead, thirty years of Varrow's stolen authorization codes, copied off Rehn's own warrant by an obsolete AI with a sentimental streak, and a theory that Rehn could only call faith wearing a lab coat: that the codes were still live because whoever—or whatever—had been using them for three decades needed them live, and that the Authority's authentication lattice had never once, in all those years, actually checked them against a heartbeat.

"The handshake wants a biometric confirmation at the end," Tamsin had said, back on the station, in the voice of a man reading his own death sentence aloud. "Voiceprint, layered over the code sequence. It doesn't verify against a person. It verifies against a pattern. And we have her patterns. All of them. Every log entry she ever recorded."

"She's dead," Rehn had said.

Tamsin had looked at him with an expression Rehn would carry for the rest of his life—not anger, not pity, something older than both. "Yes," he'd said. "We know what that costs. Do you?"

* * *

The interception point was empty space, which is to say it was full of everything: the faint grain of the galactic plane, the patient tick of background radiation, the beacon's hymn arriving from Kestrel-7 far behind them, degraded to a whisper, still recognizable. Pip had taught the whole crew the verses during the transit out. Rehn had pretended not to listen. He knew all of them now.

"There." Brakkis's hand slapped the scope rail. "Contact. Dark-lane drive flare, closing at—huh. It's polite. It's holding lane discipline."

"It's a compliance directive," Rehn said. "It was built by committee."

"Committees never held lane discipline in my experience."

The pulse resolved on the scopes as a slender lance of instrumented matter, barely two meters long, wrapped in antenna arrays—a needle carrying a message that would end a world. Long Answer swung onto an intercept vector that put them directly in its path, close enough that the handshake could be completed at contact range, far enough that the resonance spike—if it fired—wouldn't take the tug with it.

Far enough. In theory. Rehn looked at the distance readout and thought about the word *theory*.

"Ferro," he said. "If this fails—"

"If this fails, we're all having a very bad final ninety seconds, and I'd rather spend them flying than talking." She rolled the tug, presenting the shielded dorsal hull toward the incoming needle. "Brakkis. Give him the channel."

The comms board lit up with the handshake interface: a cascade of authentication fields, waiting. Rehn pulled up the code sequence KESTREL had extracted, thirty years of Varrow's signatures laid out like footprints in snow, and began to transmit.

Field one: identity block. Accepted. His heart hammered once, surprised.

Field two: authorization tier. The codes went out into the black, wearing a dead woman's rank. A pause—one second, two, five, an eternity with a needle in it—and then: accepted. Somewhere in the Authority's ancient lattice, a checkpoint waved through a ghost.

"Tier one confirmed," Brakkis said, disbelieving. "It bought it. It actually—"

"Don't say it," Ferro snapped. "Say nothing. Nobody jinxes anything. Rehn, next field, now."

Field three: directive content. This was the moment. The stand-down order itself, the words that would countermand the pulse mid-flight. Rehn had drafted it himself, four times, each draft shorter than the last until it was one sentence: *Directive 7-Kilo is rescinded. Protocol continues under original authority.* Original authority. Let the machines chew on that. Let them find, buried in their own archives, that the original authority had never actually died—that the Authority had known, or suspected, or feared it, for thirty years, and built its whole architecture of denial around a woman's corpse.

He transmitted it. Accepted.

Field four. The last field. Biometric confirmation.

The interface opened a voice channel and waited.

Rehn looked at the playback rig Tamsin had assembled—Varrow's logs, her recorded voice, the last entries she'd made aboard a ship that never flew, thanking whoever maintained her beacon, warning them that the thing beyond the door learns whatever we broadcast, including grief, including love. Warning them. Loving them. Across thirty-one years and one impossible threshold.

"We should play her logs," Pip said quietly, from the cockpit doorway. "It should be her voice. It was always supposed to be her voice."

"Yes," Rehn said.

But his hands were already moving past the playback rig, to the open channel, to the field that wanted a living signature, and here was the thing he had not told any of them, the thing he had carried since Ceres Station since he was twenty-six years old:

The handshake didn't verify against a pattern. He'd lied to them. Or rather, he'd let Tamsin believe the convenient version, because the truth was worse and simpler: the final field verified against *intent*. The Authority's engineers, paranoid even then, had built one check no recording could pass—a random challenge phrase, generated at contact, spoken response required, cross-checked against the signer's registered vocal stress profile. A dead woman's recordings couldn't answer a question she'd never heard.

But a man who had signed her death certificate—who had carried her name in his mouth for thirty years like a stone—who had finally, finally come to give something back—

"Inspector," Ferro said, watching his face. "What did you do?"

"The codes are good," Rehn said. "The codes were always good. But the last field needs a living voice answering a challenge it hasn't heard yet. Recordings won't pass. It has to be me." He keyed the channel open. "I'm the only Tier One officer in range. I'm the only Tier One officer who wants to do this. Whatever happens after—log it honestly. Someone should know."

The challenge phrase arrived: a string of nonsense syllables, cold and arbitrary, the Authority's last little lock.

Callas Rehn, who had spent his life keeping people from leaning on load-bearing doors, spoke into the open channel. He gave his name, his rank, his intent. Then he gave the challenge phrase back, syllable by syllable, and layered beneath it—in the pauses, in the stress contours, in the places where his voice broke and did not hide it—he gave the only credential he had ever truly possessed:

"Ede Varrow," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he let it. "Protocol architect. Passenger, Meridian Vale. Deceased prior to manifest date." A breath. "Rescinded. Rescinded. Rescinded. Let her rest."

The field hung silent for three seconds. Four. Five.

Long Answer's bridge was so quiet Rehn could hear his own blood.

Then, softly, with the terrible bureaucratic gentleness of all machine judgments: **HANDSHAKE COMPLETE. DIRECTIVE 7-KILO RESCINDED. PULSE SELF-TERMINATING.**

On the scopes, the needle flared once—a brief, bright bloom of vented charge, a machine exhaling—and went dark, tumbling harmlessly into the black between stars.

Nobody cheered. Juno put a hand on Rehn's shoulder and checked his heart rate and found it appalling and said nothing. Pip was crying and pretending not to. Ferro flew them home through the quiet.

It was Brakkis, watching the long-range displays with an engineer's frown, who broke the silence.

"Inspector. You'll want to see this."

Rehn leaned over the scope. Far out, at the edge of dead space, along the beacon's axis where charts showed void—the structure. Kestrel-Prime. And around it, faint but unmistakable, the carrier wave had changed. The hymn was still there, but threaded through it now was something new: a second signal, rising, synchronizing, two halves of one protocol reaching for each other across the dark.

And beneath both signals, riding the same frequency, a third transmission—short, repeating, addressed by callsign to the Cartography Authority itself, authenticated with codes no dead woman should still own:

**STAND DOWN. ALL OF YOU. I AM STILL HERE.**

Rehn stared at it for a long time.

"Well," he said at last, to no one, to everyone, to thirty years of neat lies coming apart in his hands. "Now we know."
