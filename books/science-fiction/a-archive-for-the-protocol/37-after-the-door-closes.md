## 37. After the Door Closes

The first thing Juno noticed was the quiet—not the silence of vacuum, which she had lived with her whole career, but a silence that felt *finished*. The kind of silence after a fever breaks, after the last guest leaves, after a monitor flatlines and the family finally exhales.

She was in Long Answer's medbay, which had become, over the past three days, the de facto heart of the ship. Pip sat on the exam table with her legs swinging, refusing to lie down, refusing to sleep, refusing to be anywhere but within reach of Juno's hands. Brakkis had come through an hour ago with a laceration along his forearm from a conduit panel—shallow, clean, already closing under dermal adhesive. Odile had a bruise blooming across her ribs the size of a dinner plate that she refused to let Juno image properly. And Callas Rehn, the Authority inspector who had ridden a kill-pulse's trajectory into the black on a stolen handshake, was sitting in the corner chair with second-degree burns on both hands, letting Juno treat them in silence because he had nothing left to bargain with and no reason left to be proud.

Everyone had come through her medbay. Everyone had let her touch them. Juno had spent eleven years on this crew patching people who flinched, who lied about their pain, who treated her bay as a toll booth between themselves and the next job. In the last seventy-two hours they had come to her like it was the most natural thing in the world, and she understood that this, too, was part of what the door had changed.

The threshold had closed at 0417, station time, which was a fiction they maintained because human bodies needed a clock even out here where no sun rose. She had been awake for it. Everyone had. They had gathered on the bridge in a huddle of blankets and borrowed jackets, watching the long-range feed that Odile had angled toward the structure—the sister relay, Kestrel-Prime, hanging at the edge of honest charts like a lantern at the end of a drowned road.

At 0417 the beacon's light—the actual light, the pulsing signature that Tamsin had spent nine years tending and three weeks dying to protect—had changed. Not stopped. *Changed*. It slowed, the intervals stretching, the hymn's rhythm dissolving into something longer and simpler, like a sentence ending. Like a hand letting go.

And then the entity's voice had come through one last time.

Juno would spend the rest of her life failing to describe it accurately. It was not sound; the feed carried no audio. It was the hymn rendered in the pulse code itself, the whole song, every verse, including the ones Pip's grandmother had never taught her because they belonged to a colony fleet that had scattered and died before Pip was born. The entity had learned them all. It had learned them from Varrow, or from the archive, or from thirty years of listening to a door it could never walk through. It sang the hymn once, complete, in the language of light intervals that Ede Varrow had built into a beacon before Juno's parents were born.

Then it stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, the way a singer stops at the end of a song, with the last note held and released cleanly, on purpose, with the full knowledge that the song was over.

Pip had cried. Juno had held her and cried too, quietly, in the way medics cry—efficiently, with their hands still steady, because someone might need them. Brakkis had turned away toward the viewport. Odile had said nothing at all, which from Odile was a eulogy. And Rehn had stood at the back of the bridge like a man at a funeral for someone he'd helped bury, and Juno had watched him mouth the final verse along with the pulse pattern, and she understood that he had known the hymn too, had known it for thirty years, had carried it through cold transit and warrants and orders he never believed in, the way you carry a stone in your shoe because putting it down would mean admitting you'd been walking on it.

Afterward there was nothing to do, which was its own kind of shock. For weeks there had always been something to do: a pulse to dodge, a system to bypass, a decision to make with insufficient information. Now the door was closed, the archive was sealed, the entity had its bounded memory and the dead had their rest, and the crew of Long Answer was adrift in a dead space that was, at last, honestly dead—and the only task remaining was the oldest one.

Going home.

* * *

They burned for Kestrel-7 on a lazy trajectory, because there was no longer any reason to hurry and every reason to conserve what remained of their fuel margins. Brakkis had rebuilt the drive from salvage and stubbornness, and it ran rough, with a harmonic shudder in the second stage that he swore was within tolerance and everyone else pretended to believe.

Juno fell into the rhythm of a long transit the way she always did: rounds, meals, sleep, small repairs. But the rhythm had changed key. Pip had appointed herself ship's morale officer, a position she invented and no one contested, and her primary duty was teaching the crew the hymn. She had the notation—the folk-hymn notation her grandmother had used, the one she'd recognized in the beacon's pulse pattern back when they were still strangers deciding whether to trust a station that shouldn't have existed—and she had transcribed the full song from the entity's final transmission, working by ear off the pulse intervals, humming, arguing with herself, filling in verses from memory.

"It's a work song," she explained one evening, in the galley, to an audience of four who had not asked. "That's what nobody gets. It's not a lullaby, it's a *work song*. The fleet crews sang it while they were calibrating the first relay chains. It's got counting in it—listen—" and she sang the third verse, and Juno heard it for the first time, the numbers folded into the melody, one through nine and then back to one, the way you count a cycle, a shift, a watch. A song for people keeping something running alone in the dark, so they'd know the count was still true.

Tamsin had gone very still when she said that. Juno had watched him from across the galley, this thin, quiet man who had spent nine years as the song's only audience, and she had seen him understand, all at once, that he had never been alone in the dark. He had been the latest in a line. The count had been kept for him before he ever arrived, and he had kept it for whoever came after, and now the count was finished, and he did not yet know who he was without it.

Juno knew something about that. She had been a fleet medic before she was a salvager, before the mutiny, before Odile. She had spent four years on a station not so different from Kestrel-7, tending a crew of forty, and when the station decommissioned she had discovered that she didn't know how to be a person without patients. That was why the medbay had become the heart of the ship. That was why she kept it warm, kept the lamp on, kept the kettle where people would find it. Not because people were always hurt. Because a medbay with a lamp on was a promise: *someone is here, someone is watching the count.*

She made tea. She always made tea. Brakkis came in with his soldering smell and his burned knuckles and drank it without being asked. Pip did homework—actual fleet academy prep coursework, downloaded from a courier buoy three weeks ago, which Odile pretended not to have paid an obscene fee for. Rehn sat in the corner and, on the ninth evening of the transit, without preamble, asked Juno a question.

"You were fleet. Before."

It wasn't a question, but she treated it as one. "Four years. Medical corps, relay support division."

"Then you remember the decommission orders. After the Vale." He said it carefully, the way a man handles a thing that might still be armed.

"I remember the fleet getting smaller. Nobody told us why."

"Nobody told us either." He turned his burned hands around his cup—she had grafted new skin over them; they would scar, and he seemed to want them to. "I signed her death certificate. Varrow. I was a junior records officer. The file came down sealed, and I signed, and I told myself for thirty years that the seal meant someone above me had verified it. And then I intercepted a kill-pulse with her authorization codes and I understood that the seal had never meant anything at all. It just meant *stop asking*." He looked up at Juno, and she saw that his grief was not for Varrow, exactly, and not for himself. It was for the version of him who had signed. "I keep thinking about what she said. That the thing beyond the door learned whatever we broadcast. Including grief." His mouth twisted. "Thirty years of my grief, leaking through a beacon I was trying to kill. It probably knows me better than my own file does."

"It knows the hymn," Juno said. "It sang it whole. Whatever else it learned, it learned that." She refilled his cup. "That's not nothing, Inspector."

"Callas," he said. "I'm not an inspector anymore. I burned the career."

"Callas, then."

Pip looked up from her coursework, and Juno caught the girl's eye, and something passed between them that needed no words: *he's one of us now, or he's trying to be, and that's more than any of us expected of him.*

* * *

On the nineteenth day of the transit, Juno had a nightmare.

It was not unusual—she'd had them since the structure, since the shadow-debris that rearranged itself when unobserved, since the night the entity had broadcast fragments of their own voices back at them. But this one was different. In the dream she was back on her old fleet station, in the medbay, and the lamp was on, and the bay was full of patients, and every one of them was someone she had failed to save, and they were all singing the hymn, and the count was wrong. The count was wrong and no one would stop singing to fix it.

She woke in the dark of her bunk with her heart hammering and lay there doing the thing she told patients to do: name five things you can hear. Drive shudder. Air recycler. Pip's music, faint, through the bulkhead—she was humming the hymn in her sleep, Juno realized, the count coming out true even unconscious. Brakkis snoring. Odile's boots, somewhere above, walking the night watch she still insisted on taking even though there was nothing left to watch for.

Nothing left to watch for. Juno lay in the dark and turned the phrase over and found that it didn't frighten her. For eleven years, *nothing to watch for* had meant *no work, no pay, no next job*. Now it meant something else. It meant the dark was just dark. It meant the thing moving at sensor range was a rock, and would always be a rock, and would never rearrange itself when she blinked.

She got up and went to the medbay, because that was where she went, and found she wasn't the only one who couldn't sleep. Tamsin was there, sitting on the exam table in the lamplight, and he looked at her with the particular guilt of a man caught in someone else's sanctuary.

"Couldn't sleep either," he said.

"Nobody can." She put the kettle on. "It's the quiet. Everyone's waiting for the other shoe."

"There isn't another shoe."

"I know. You know. Their bodies don't know yet." She handed him tea. "Give it a month. Bodies are slow learners. That's the whole reason my job exists."

He drank. For a while they sat in the lamplight, two insomniacs in a ship full of sleeping people, and then Tamsin said, quietly, "I keep hearing it. The last verse. The way it ended—clean, you know? Not cut off. *Finished.*" He stared at his cup. "Nine years I thought I was maintaining a beacon. Turns out I was keeping someone company while she died. Or while she lived. I still don't—" He stopped. Started again. "She crossed over so the door could close right. She asked me to hold my end. And now it's held, and it's done, and I keep waiting to feel like it was enough, and instead I just feel—"

"Grief," Juno said. "That's what it feels like. It feels like nothing was enough, because nothing was ever going to bring them back. That's not failure. That's just the shape of it."

He looked at her with wet eyes and didn't wipe them, and Juno understood that this was why she'd become a medic, really become one, in the way that outlasted the fleet and the pay and the work. Not to fix people. To sit with them while they learned the shape of what couldn't be fixed, and to make sure they learned it in a warm room with a lamp on and tea in their hands.

"Varrow knew," Tamsin said at last. "In her last log. She said the thing beyond learns grief, and she said it like a warning. But it wasn't a warning, was it. It was—" He searched. "It was a request. *Learn this. Learn what we do with it.*"

"Learn that we keep the count," Juno said, "even when the count is sad."

Tamsin laughed—a short, broken, real sound—and drank his tea, and the drive shuddered its rough harmonic, and somewhere above them Odile walked her unnecessary watch, and the dead space around them was dead, and it was enough.

* * *

They made orbit-of-sorts around Kestrel-7 on the thirty-first day.

There was no orbit, technically—the station hung in a gravitationally indifferent patch of nothing, and Long Answer simply matched velocity and held station, the way you hold a vigil. But Juno stood at the viewport with the rest of them and thought *orbit*, because the word had the right weight.

The station looked the same. That was the first shock. After everything—the shutdown pulse, the mutiny of small mercies, the structural damage, the flooding module where Tamsin and Rehn had talked their way into an alliance—the old relay hung in the black with its scars facing away, its beacon dark, its hull catching the distant starlight like a bone on the floor of a deep sea.

"Beacon's off," Brakkis said, at his console. "Full dark. KESTREL's answering my ping, though. Systems nominal. Well—nominal for Kestrel-7, which is its own category."

"She's waiting," Pip said.

"She's been waiting nine years," Odile said. "She can wait another hour while we dock like professionals instead of drifting in like ghosts."

But it was Tamsin whom Juno watched. He stood at the viewport with his hands at his sides, looking at the station he had left three weeks and a lifetime ago, and Juno saw the grief and the vertigo of it—the homecoming of a man who had left the only place he trusted in order to close a door, and was now returning to a station that was no longer a prison or a post or a hiding place, and who did not yet know what it was. What *he* was.

"You'll figure it out," she said, coming to stand beside him.

He glanced at her. "Did I say something?"

"You didn't have to. You've got the look. Same look Brakkis had after his last engine fire, same look Odile had the day she bought this hull. The 'I don't know who I am without the emergency' look." She bumped his shoulder with hers, gently, medic-gentle. "It passes. What's on the other side is better."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then I'll write you a prescription." She smiled. "Tea, twice daily, with company."

KESTREL's voice came through then, thin and old and unmistakably glad, patched through to the whole ship because the AI had apparently decided that nine years of standby had earned it the right to skip protocol: *"Long Answer, this is Relay Station Kestrel-7. Docking ring is pressurized and lit. I have—"* a pause, a hesitation no one had ever heard from the machine before— *"I have kept everything. The logs. The hymn. Her voice. I wasn't sure anyone would want it, but I couldn't make myself delete it, and I have run the contradiction eleven thousand times, and I have concluded that this is what it means to be a station. You keep things. Come home."*

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Odile, gruff, wet-eyed, hands steady on the thrusters: "Coming home, Kestrel. Good station."

Long Answer slid toward the docking ring, and the beacon—dark for thirty-one days—flickered once, twice, a soft pulse like a lamp turned on in a window. Not a summons. Not a lure. Just a light, left on for people who might need it.

And that was when Brakkis, watching his console out of habit, went rigid.

"Juno." His voice was careful, the way it got when the news was strange rather than bad. "You're going to want to see this. There's a signal coming out of the archive channel. From the far side." He looked up, and his face was doing something Juno had almost never seen it do. "It's clean. It's lawful. It's *human*. And it's one word long."

Juno crossed to the console. On the screen, decoded from the sealed archive's far end, in the pulse notation of a hymn that had finally finished singing, was a single word from a woman who had crossed beyond a closed door so that everyone else could stay.

*Stay.*

Behind her, Juno heard Tamsin's breath catch—and heard, in it, not grief, and not fear, but the sound of a man who had just been asked the only question that mattered, and who already knew his answer.
