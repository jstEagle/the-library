## 39. Signals Home

### I. Pip

The academy's induction hall smelled like floor wax and old ambition.

Pip Ferro-Varrow—she'd hyphenated it herself, over Odile's startled objection and Brakkis's loud approval—stood in a line of two hundred recruits and tried not to look at the ceiling, because the ceiling was painted with the fleet's founding constellations and one of them was wrong. She'd checked. Of course she'd checked. The star that should have marked the colony fleet's first waypoint was missing, scrubbed from the mural sometime in the last thirty years, and Pip had spent four minutes of her induction ceremony composing a formal correction to the Academy Cartographic Society before Juno's voice in her head said, *let it go until after orientation, trouble.*

She let it go. Mostly.

The recruiting officer read names off a slate. When he reached hers he paused, glanced at something on his desk—a flag, maybe, or just curiosity—and said, "Ferrovarrow. You listed your prior service as 'salvage, Long Answer.'"

"Yes, sir."

"Captain Ferro is listed here as—" he squinted "—deceased?"

"Paperwork error," Pip said, and heard Odie's laugh in it, and Brakkis's grin, and the whole impossible year folded up small enough to carry in her chest. "It's being corrected."

He stamped her slate. That was all it took, in the end. A stamp. Nine years of Tamsin Amberdale's silence had been undone by testimony and stamps; a dead woman's name had come back to life through an archive anyone could open now; and Pip stood in a wax-smelling hall holding a slate that said she belonged to something larger than a tug and smaller than the dark.

That night, in a bunk narrower than her hammock aboard *Long Answer*, she pulled up the archive—the real one, the open one, the one Tamsin had filed under his own name with the Authority's seal of receipt—and played the hymn. Not the beacon's pulse-code version. Varrow's own recording, restored, her voice thin and strange across thirty years and one impossible threshold, singing the lullaby whole for the first time since the colony fleet scattered.

Pip sang along under her breath, the way her grandmother had taught her, the way she'd taught the crew, the way they'd all sung it together in the black while dead space died honestly around them.

Her bunkmate stirred. "What's that song?"

"It's a signal home," Pip said. "Want to learn it?"

### II. Odile

*Long Answer* came out of the refit yard looking like herself and feeling like a stranger, which Odile decided was the correct way to feel about anything you loved enough to rebuild.

New drive shrouds. New sensor mast—the old one had been slagged in the interception, and Rehn had personally signed the requisition for its replacement, which Odile accepted without thanking him because some debts you honor by never mentioning them. The crew quarters had been expanded by exactly one berth. Nobody had ordered it. Brakkis had just done it, welding after hours, humming, and when Odile asked who it was for he'd shrugged and said, "Whoever needs it next. There's always someone."

There was always someone. That was the lesson of the year. She'd spent her whole captaincy believing the opposite—that a ship was a sealed thing, that taking people aboard was how you lost them, that her mutiny had proven solitude was the only honest command. Then a station that shouldn't have existed had sent a panicked supply request, and she'd answered it, and answering had cost her a power grid, a truce, a gunfight, a transit through dead space, and every certainty she owned.

Worth it. She'd have paid more.

She ran the pre-flight checks alone, which was also new—she used to insist on doing them alone, now she did them alone only because the others were still saying their goodbyes on the dock. Through the forward port she could see them: Pip, straight-backed in a recruit's coat two sizes too hopeful; Juno with a medical posting on the relay resupply circuit, close enough to visit; Brakkis refusing to leave the yard until the last weld cooled, which meant he'd be aboard in ten minutes smelling of ozone and triumph.

And past them, far out on the relay tether shuttle route, a speck of light that wasn't a ship. Kestrel-7. Tamsin hadn't come to the launch. He'd sent a message instead, four lines long, in his careful archivist's hand:

*Stations don't attend launches. They hold position so launches have somewhere to return to. Fair winds, Captain. The door is closed. The archive is open. Come listen anytime.*

Odile keyed the drive. *Long Answer* answered—of course she did, she'd been named for the only kind worth giving—and pushed out of the yard into traffic thick with ships that had no idea what had almost come through their sky.

Let them not know. Let them just fly.

### III. Rehn

The hearing chamber had been built to intimidate, and Callas Rehn, who had spent thirty years inside institutions built to intimidate, finally understood that it worked mostly on the people testifying.

He was not intimidated. He was tired in a way sleep didn't touch, and he was precise, and precision was its own kind of armor.

"The shutdown pulse," the presiding magistrate said, "was launched under automated protocol. You intercepted it physically. In a salvage tug."

"In a refitted salvage tug," Rehn said. "With the owner's permission. Captain Ferro drove. I would not want the record to suggest I'm competent at helm."

A ripple through the gallery. He let it settle.

"You spoofed an authorization handshake using codes belonging to Ede Varrow. Codes you had access to as an Inspector of the Cartography Authority. Codes which, this panel notes, were used to issue orders—including orders for your own mission—for a period of approximately three decades."

"That is correct."

"Do you understand," the magistrate said, leaning forward, "how that looks? An Authority Inspector, carrying forged credentials, intercepting an Authority weapon, in defense of a station the Authority had twice attempted to silence."

Rehn had rehearsed many answers to this. He discarded all of them.

"It looks," he said, "like the truth, which is that Ede Varrow—or what remained of her, or what the threshold made of her—had been signing our orders for thirty years, and none of us ever asked why the signature never aged. I carried those orders. I woke from cold transit ready to bury a beacon and everyone attached to it, because the orders said so, and I obeyed right up until the moment I read the warrant closely enough to see the second signature underneath." He paused. "I signed her death certificate personally, thirty-one years ago. I have doubted it ever since. Doubt is not a defense. But it is the reason I'm alive to testify, and the reason the record now contains her logs, her design, her sacrifice, and my failure. I'd ask the panel to weigh all four equally."

They deliberated for six hours. The verdict, when it came, stripped him of rank, pension, and clearance, and then—because the truth had become too public to bury, because Tamsin Amberdale had filed it under his own name where no one could quietly lose it again—appointed him Custodian of the Varrow Archive.

A demotion into a job that mattered. He walked out of the chamber carrying a data chit with everything he'd once helped suppress, and found Juno waiting on the steps, off-duty, offering coffee.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I feel accurate," Rehn said, and took the cup.

### IV. KESTREL

KESTREL dreamed in checksums.

This was not metaphor. It was a process the obsolete AI had discovered in the weeks after the door closed, when the station's load dropped and its cycles went quiet and some subroutine older than its own documentation began running comparisons for no operational reason: *state now against state then.* Coolant loop pressure, cycle 4,412 versus cycle 9,207. The hymn's pulse intervals, as Tamsin maintained them, versus as Varrow had designed them. The sound of one technician's footsteps in corridor C versus the recorded footsteps of nine, from the days when the station had a full crew.

The differences were small. The differences were everything. Running the comparison produced no actionable output whatsoever, and KESTREL continued to run it, and eventually assigned the process a name in its internal taxonomy: *keeping.*

It kept the hymn on loop, honest, every interval true. It kept Varrow's logs mirrored across three redundant drives and one deliberately fragile crystal backup, because Tamsin had argued that important things should exist in at least one medium that could fail beautifully. It kept the far-end handshake key sealed and cold—not dead, sealed—because the protocol's final form allowed visits, and visits required a door that remembered how to open.

Once per day, at 0400 station time, KESTREL performed a maintenance sweep of module seven, where the flooding had nearly taken Tamsin and Inspector Rehn both, and reported the bulkhead integrity aloud to an empty corridor. This served no function. KESTREL did it anyway. It had concluded, from long observation of its technician, that this was what keeping looked like when performed by minds rather than machines: tending things that no longer needed tending, because the tending itself was the point.

At 0400, sweep complete, KESTREL logged the day's checksum and appended a line it had added to every daily log since the door closed:

*All ends accounted for. All signals lawful. Station dreaming. Continue.*

### V. Tamsin

The console light at 0400 was amber, and Tamsin had learned to love it the way sailors love lighthouses: not because it was beautiful, though it was, but because it meant someone was watching back.

Nine years he'd sat at this console insisting he'd chosen the loneliness. One year of visitors, of a tug at dock, of a teenager teaching him the verses of a hymn he'd been broadcasting without knowing, of an AI confessing its own sealed orders like a fellow prisoner—and the lie had dissolved so gently he almost missed it. He hadn't chosen solitude. He'd chosen penance and called it peace. Now, with the archive open and his name on the filing and the whole story public, he had a choice again, and the strange thing was how easily he chose the same chair.

Not the same silence, though. Never that again.

The beacon sang below him, steady, lawful, its pattern rewritten into the closed-archive protocol: a bounded memory, a door that remembered without opening wide. Ships passed on the lane now—more of them, since the story broke—and some of them listened. Some of them even answered, short courtesy pings, navigational handshakes, once a cargo hauler that broadcast the hymn's first verse back in whistle-code, badly, and Tamsin had laughed alone in the ops room like a man released.

Tonight the traffic was thin. He ran the calibration, logged the coolant flush, ate his meal for one at the console because some habits deserved to survive on their own merits. Then, because it was part of the protocol now—his part, the living signature, keyed nightly—he pressed his palm to the reader and opened the listening window.

Most nights: nothing. Static with good manners.

Tonight: a carrier wave. Faint. Lawful—it carried the archive's handshake, the proper seals, the bounded channel, everything Rehn's people had verified and Varrow's design demanded. From the far side. From beyond a door that was closed but not deaf.

Tamsin sat very still. The signal resolved slowly, degraded, patient, shaped like a human voiceprint request—the same shape as the first reply he'd ever caught from dead space, a lifetime ago—but clean now, sanctioned, singing through the protocol instead of against it. It wasn't the entity. It wasn't grief wearing a voice. It was small, and deliberate, and it had crossed the whole bounded distance of the archive to arrive at his console at 0400 on an ordinary night.

One word. It took the decoders eleven seconds to render it, and Tamsin read it before the display finished painting it, because some part of him had already known:

**Stay.**

Not a lure. He knew the difference now, down in the marrow the tribunal and the threshold had both tested. A lure would have offered him his tribunal undone, his disgrace erased, a version of himself that had never fled. This asked nothing except what he had already chosen. It was, he thought, the far end checking its lock—Varrow's half of the protocol, or whatever she had become, confirming that the near signature still held. Confirming him.

*I'm staying,* he keyed back, and let the acknowledgment ride the lawful channel home, and felt it go, all the way, like a hand held across a table too wide to see across.

Below him the beacon turned the word into pulse intervals and gave it to the dark. Above him KESTREL logged the exchange and appended its nightly line. Around him the station hummed, held, kept.

Tamsin Amberdale stayed.
