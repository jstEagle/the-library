## 40. Chapter 40

The third year after the door closed, Kestrel-7 grew a garden.

It was Brakkis's idea, originally—an offhand complaint that the hydroponics bay had been sitting empty since before Tamsin's arrival, a coffin-sized room of dead trays and mineral smell that the engineer couldn't abide leaving idle. *Waste is a sin,* Brakkis said, in the way he said most things, as though reading from scripture no one else had been given. So they seeded it. Juno brought cuttings from three different colonies in sealed gel packs; Odile traded two crates of salvage-grade alloy for a vat of heirloom soil from a farming barge out of Meridian Reach; Pip, home on academy leave, planted something she refused to identify and labeled the tray only with her grandmother's name.

Tamsin tended it now the way he had once tended the beacon: daily, without ceremony, without expectation. The tomatoes came in crooked and small. The beans climbed a lattice welded from old conduit brackets. The unnamed plant in Pip's tray turned out to be a night-blooming vine that opened its white flowers only when the station dimmed to sleep-cycle lighting, filling the bay with a scent like cold honey, like something remembered rather than smelled.

He stood there some evenings, after the calibration rounds, after the log entries, and let the growing things be the whole of his accomplishment. Nine years alone had taught him how to keep a machine alive. The garden was teaching him something slower—that keeping things alive and watching them change were not the same discipline, and he had never once practiced the second.

The beacon sang overhead through the deck plating, a vibration more felt than heard. Varrow's hymn, on loop, lawful and open, keyed to the new protocol's bounded frequency. Anyone could listen. Nothing could cross. And twice a day, at the intervals written into the archive he'd built with Ede Varrow's half-remembered hands guiding his own, the far end answered: a single pulse, clean and small, like a knock from inside a closed room.

*Still here,* the pulse said, in the language of intervals.
*Still here,* the beacon replied.

That was all either of them needed it to say.

* * *

Pip arrived on the autumn rotation aboard a fleet courier too new for its own paint, wearing a cadet's uniform that fit her like a borrowed future. She'd grown four centimeters. She'd also grown a habit of standing at attention that Odile spent the first hour trying to beat out of her with mockery and the second hour secretly admiring.

"You filed your thesis," Tamsin said. They were in the archive module, which Pip had insisted on visiting before docking, before greetings, before anything. Her fingers hovered over the reader where Varrow's logs sat unencrypted, open to any query, any historian, any grieving descendant who might come looking.

"Colonial Fleet Hymnody as Navigational Protocol." Pip shrugged, but her ears went pink. "Rehn testified for my citation access. He wrote—" she stopped, restarted. "He wrote that the primary source material was authenticated by the man who maintained the original relay. That's you. You're a citation now."

"I'm a footnote."

"You're a *primary source.* Do you know what that means at the academy? People argue about you. There's a second-year who thinks you should have sealed the door and another one who thinks you waited too long, and they both cite your tribunal record, which—" she grinned, sharp as her grandmother must have been, "—which you filed yourself. Under your real name. In the public index. Where anyone can read that you once got court-martialed-adjacent for refusing to falsify a chart."

"It was a bad chart," Tamsin said. "It would have put a colony ship nine degrees off its corridor. Someone had to say so."

"And it cost you everything, and then you spent nine years being nobody, and then you spent three years becoming the most footnoted technician in the Outer Reaches." Pip shook her head slowly. "Juno says trauma builds character. Brakkis says character is just scar tissue with good posture. I think you just kept saying the true thing until the truth got big enough to hold you."

She touched the reader at last, waking Varrow's face in the display—the recovered image from the Meridian Vale's boarding manifest, young and resolute and thirty years dead and none of those things anymore, if you believed the far-end pulses, which Tamsin did, carefully, the way you believe in weather.

"She looks tired," Pip said.

"She was tired. She'd been carrying the protocol alone for longer than anyone knew."

"No—I mean in this picture. Before everything. She already looks like someone who knows what it's going to cost." Pip looked up at him. "Did you ever decide what she was? Love or lure?"

Tamsin considered the question honestly, because Pip was the only person left who asked him honest questions without cushioning them. "I decided she was both," he said, "and that the difference didn't matter as much as I thought it did. A lure wants to take something from you. Love asks. She asked. The thing beyond the door took. I learned to tell the asking from the taking, and that's the whole protocol, really. That's what we wrote into it. Ask, don't take. Remember, don't reach."

Pip nodded slowly, filing it somewhere behind her eyes where cadets filed things they'd need later in dark rooms. Then she pulled a data chip from her uniform pocket and set it on the console between them.

"The academy archive wants your maintenance journals. All nine years. The quiet ones. The coolant flushes and the calibrations and the meals for one." She met his eyes. "There's a professor who says the heroism isn't the door. The heroism is the man who stayed sane beside it. They want the boring parts, Tamsin. The boring parts are the proof."

He looked at the chip for a long moment. Nine years of days, compressed onto something smaller than his thumbnail. Every meal for one. Every hour he'd insisted he chose this and almost believed it.

"They'll find a lot of complaining," he said finally. "About the coffee synthesizer."

"Good," said Pip. "Complaining is human. The archive's full enough of martyrs."

* * *

Odile Ferro came back twice a year, on no schedule she'd admit to, always with cargo that was transparently an excuse. This time it was a replacement airlock gasket Kestrel didn't need and a case of real tea from the Core systems that everyone knew she'd overpaid for, and she delivered both with the scowl of a woman doing charity against her will.

They drank the tea in the galley while Brakkis and Juno argued companionably in the background about whether the station's rebuilt power grid qualified as elegant or merely lucky. Through the viewport, Long Answer hung at dock, her hull patched with mismatched plate, her name repainted in fresh white letters by a hand—Pip's, clearly—that had added a small painted hymn-note beneath the *R*, because sentiment spreads through crews like rust through seawater.

"You could come with us," Odile said, not looking at him. "Next run. We're going out past the Reach. Long haul. Room for a passenger with nothing but time."

"I have a station."

"You have a station and a garden and a ghost who knocks twice a day. I know. I'm not recruiting, Lockridge, I'm offering. There's a difference." She turned her cup in her hands. "You ever notice you only refuse offers? You never even consider them."

"I considered yours."

"That's new," she admitted. "What changed?"

He thought about it while the tea cooled. Outside the viewport, the beacon array caught the light of the system's dim star and threw it back in slow pulses, patient as breathing.

"I used to think staying was what cowardice looked like when it got comfortable," he said. "Hiding with good housekeeping. But I signed the near end of the protocol. My biometrics are bound into it. If I leave—if the signature goes dormant—the archive degrades. Not catastrophically. Slowly. The way memory degrades. And she's at the other end of it, holding her half, and the whole design only works because both ends are *kept*." He looked at Odile. "So I stay. But it's not hiding anymore. It's a post. There's a difference too."

Odile studied him for a long moment with the expression she usually saved for hull inspections—looking for the crack, finding instead the weld.

"All right," she said. "Post. I can respect a post." She drained her tea and stood. "But I'm telling you the same thing I told Rehn, so listen: a post is only honorable if someone relieves you sometimes. We'll be back on the spring rotation. You'll drink tea and complain about the coffee synthesizer and pretend you don't count the days between our visits."

"I don't count the days."

"You keep a log," she said, pausing in the doorway. "You've logged every visit since the first one. Timestamped. I've read the archive, Lockridge. Everyone has. It's a *citation*."

She left him there with his ears burning and his tea going cold, and the worst part was that she was right, and the better part was that she'd be back.

* * *

Callas Rehn's message arrived on the winter solstice of the station's calendar—a calendar Tamsin had invented himself, in year two, because a man alone needs solstices or he forgets time is moving.

The message was short. Rehn had retired—not resigned, retired, with a pension and a hearing record and a reputation permanently split down the middle, half the Authority calling him the man who burned his career to stop a kill-pulse, the other half calling him the man who'd spent thirty years carrying orders signed by a dead woman. Both halves were true. He seemed, in his letter, to have made peace with the arithmetic.

*I visited the threshold coordinates last month,* the letter read. *Civilian charter, no warrant, no colors. Just an old man and a window. The structure is still there. Quieter now. The instruments show almost nothing—which my charter captain found disappointing and I found correct. An archive should be quiet.*

*I have one request, and I make it as a private citizen with no authority whatsoever, which is a condition I recommend highly. When you next receive the far-end pulse, log the exact interval. I have been keeping the same records, from my side, for three years. Mine match yours to within four milliseconds. I want you to understand what that means: whatever Ede Varrow is now, she is consistent. She is not fading, not fragmenting, not learning grief the way the entity learned it. She is keeping her half the way you keep yours. Deliberately. Daily. I checked the mathematics of it six times because I did not want to believe it, and it held six times.*

*She is at peace, Lockridge. Or she is performing peace with a precision no performance could sustain. Either way, the protocol holds. Either way, you did not fail her.*

*One more thing. The Authority has approved a memorial designation for the Meridian Vale and her crew—all of them, including the flight that officially never happened. They asked me to draft the inscription. I wrote: SHE ASKED, AND THE ASKING WAS HEARD. I stole that from your archive. Consider it cited.*

*Rehn.*

Tamsin read it twice, then walked, without deciding to, up to the beacon chamber.

The chamber was small and warm and full of the hum he had lived inside for twelve years now—the sound he'd once mistaken for purpose, then for prison, then for penance, and which was now simply the sound of the house he'd chosen. He pulled up the far-end log. The last pulse had come ninety-one minutes ago. Interval: exact. Consistent. Kept.

"Hello, Ede," he said, to the hum, to the pulse, to the woman at the other end of a closed door who had asked to be remembered and had gotten, instead, a colleague.

The beacon pulsed. Ninety-one minutes early, off-schedule for the first time in three years—one extra beat, soft, outside the protocol's bounds but inside its spirit, the way a hand squeezes yours a moment longer than the goodbye requires.

He laughed, alone in the chamber, and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, and logged it. Timestamped. Let the second-years argue about that one too.

* * *

Spring came to a station that had no seasons except the ones its keeper invented, and with it came Long Answer, right on schedule, and with her came the ritual they'd all stopped pretending wasn't a ritual: the whole crew at the table, the good tea, the coffee synthesizer complained about, the garden visited and praised, the archive queried and argued over. Pip, home between terms, taught Juno the second verse of the hymn. Brakkis installed a condenser coil in the synthesizer and fixed, at last, the coffee, and Tamsin felt a genuine flicker of loss at the death of his oldest grievance, and everyone laughed at his face when he tasted it.

In the evening, when the station dimmed to sleep-cycle, they went together to the hydroponics bay, all five of them, and stood among the crooked tomatoes and the climbing beans while the night-blooming vine opened its white flowers in the dark, one by one, unhurried, certain.

Above them, faint through the deck, the beacon sang. Below the horizon of the world, at the edge of honestly dead space, something that had been a woman and was now a keeping answered on schedule: *still here, still here.*

And Tamsin Amberdale—who had spent nine years being no one, and three years becoming a citation, and one long life learning the difference between hiding and holding—stood in the garden with his family and listened, and found that the silence between the pulses was not empty at all.

It was an archive. And it was full.
