## 6. Interception

There is a particular kind of paperwork that exists solely to justify other paperwork, and the boarding authorization for the Svalbard-Adjunct seed vault was three layers deep in it. I signed all of it myself, which my adjutant found either admirable or alarming — Ensign Voss has never learned to hide her face, which will serve her poorly in this service and her well after she leaves it.

*Inspection: routine agricultural compliance audit, Concord Charter Section 14-C.* It was a beautiful lie. I have written many beautiful lies in eleven years as an inquisitor, but this one had a certain elegance, because buried inside the compliance codes nobody has read since the armistice was a clause granting me authority to open any sealed container on any registered facility. Any container. Ten thousand canisters of seeds, if the manifest was to be believed.

The manifest was not to be believed. That was rather the point.

"Approach vector confirmed," Voss said. "The vault isn't answering our hail."

"It's answering," I said. "It's declining. There's a difference, and the difference is a person."

The Adjunct hung below us like a fossil — a wedge of reinforced ice and old concrete driven into the glacier a century ago, when both factions still pretended they might need to feed each other someday. One beacon light pulsed on its spine, patient as a lighthouse that had forgotten ships existed. I had read the file on it twice on the flight out from the *Vigilant*. Population: one. Function: seed preservation. Threat assessment: none.

I do not believe in threat assessments. I believe in anomalies, and the anomaly was sitting in orbit above the vault wearing the hull of a ship that had never flown.

"The *Halcyon* is holding position," Voss reported. "No emissions. No crew signs."

"A courier vessel decommissioned before launch, crewed by a dead woman, delivering itself to a glacier." I unbuckled. "Someone went to considerable trouble to make this look impossible. I find that more interesting than anything actually impossible would be."

"And the vault keeper?"

"Tamsin Villalobos." I let the name sit in my mouth for a moment. "Nine years alone. Grew up in Concord administrative territory, mother listed dead twenty years, no military record, no political record, no debts, no vices on file. Either the dullest human being in the system or the best-hidden one."

"You think she's Meridian?"

"I think," I said, stepping into the drop shuttle, "that nobody spends nine years talking to potatoes without something to protect."

* * *

The vault interior smelled of earth and cold iron, a smell so aggressively innocent that it circled back around to suspicious. We came down through the maintenance shaft with magnetic boots and drawn sidearms, six of us in grey armor, and were met at the bottom by a woman in a soil-stained jumpsuit holding — I am not inventing this detail; Voss logged it — a trowel.

She looked at the guns. She looked at me. She did not look afraid, which was the first thing wrong with her.

"Compliance audit," I said. "Section 14-C. You'll want to see the authorization."

"I'd love to," Tamsin Villalobos said. "It's been four years since anyone official visited. The last inspector took a cutting of my wheat strain and never sent results." She set the trowel down with the deliberate care of someone disarming herself voluntarily, which is either honesty or theater, and offered her hand. Her grip was calloused and unhurried. "Tamsin Villalobos. Keeper. You're standing in my nursery, so watch your boots."

We watched our boots.

She gave us the tour herself, unasked, walking us through the germination bays and the hydroponic rows with the flat competence of long habit. Potatoes, barley, a dwarf wheat she was clearly proud of, legumes cycling nitrogen through beds of engineered bacteria. She talked about soil pH the way officers talk about fleet formations. Somewhere in the middle of it I realized she had not once asked why an inspection required armed soldiers, and when I noticed that, I understood I was not touring a vault. I was being managed.

"Your orbital visitor," I said, while she demonstrated a moisture sensor. "The *Halcyon*."

"What about it?"

"You knew it was there."

"Of course. It's been up there two days. Hard to miss." She adjusted the sensor without looking at it. "Derelict, by the emissions profile. Or very good at pretending."

"Does the name Villalobos mean anything to you? E. Villalobos, listed as crew?"

Something moved behind her eyes — not a flinch, nothing so legible. A recalculation. "My mother's name was Elena Villalobos. She died twenty years ago." A pause exactly long enough to seem honest. "Is she on your ship?"

"That's what I intend to find out. Which brings me to the cargo manifests. Yours."

"My what?"

"The seed inventory. Every canister, every accession number. Section 14-C gives me authority to verify contents against registry."

"Sure," she said, and shrugged, and led us toward the archive stacks, and I want it on record that at that moment I believed her. That is the part that still bothers me. I have interrogated smugglers, defectors, and one admiral, and I know what lying looks like from the inside. She wasn't doing it yet. She was simply letting me walk where she wanted me to walk, and the distinction only became visible later, which is how the best lies work.

* * *

The archive was ten thousand identical canisters in floor-to-ceiling racks, each one numbered, each one containing seeds suspended in desiccated silence. Cold enough to make armor joints ache. My team fanned out with scanners, and I stood in the aisle watching Tamsin Villalobos watch them, arms crossed, breath fogging, expression arranged into polite boredom.

"Ten thousand containers," I said. "How often do you audit them yourself?"

"Every quarter. Takes six days."

"Ever find discrepancies?"

"Once. A vole got in through a conduit in year three. Chewed into a barley canister." She said it lightly. "I executed him personally. Vault justice is swift."

Voss snorted and then pretended she hadn't. Even my own people were being charmed. I moved down the rack line, running my glove along the canisters, feeling the small industrial poetry of them — identical, interchangeable, anonymous. If you wanted to hide one object among these, you wouldn't hide it. You'd make it boring. You'd give it a number between two other numbers and let ten thousand twins do the work.

"Your beacon," I said. "It pulses nightly. On whose authority?"

"Mine. It's a locator. Standard for remote facilities."

"Its frequency band is restricted."

"Is it?" Genuine-sounding surprise. "Nobody told me. It came with the vault. I log it every night because the manual says to log it every night. Would you like to see the logs?" And she produced them — nine years of nightly entries in a neat hand, frequencies and durations, before I'd even finished asking. Prepared. Of course she was prepared. But the logs themselves were clean, and clean things are their own kind of evidence.

Except. I turned a page. The maintenance annotations in the margins — calibration notes, component replacements — were in a second hand. A different script entirely, cramped and precise, and I knew that script. I had graded it, once, in a signals classroom on the *Meridian's Bane*, seventeen years ago, from a cadet who could hear a cipher in rain on a hull.

Ilan Reyes-Vashti. Deserted three years ago. Presumed gone to the Meridian, or dead, or worse.

His handwriting was in the margins of a seed vault's beacon logs, dated within the last decade.

I closed the ledger carefully, the way you close a door on a room you're not ready to search. "Thorough records," I said.

"I'm a thorough person."

"So it appears." I handed it back and watched her take it, watched her hands — steady, no tremor, no tell. "One more thing, Keeper. Your mother. Elena Villalobos. Tell me about her death."

"Convoy raid, the year I was twelve. Meridian interceptors." Flat. Rehearsed-flat, maybe, but grief rehearsed over twenty years has earned the right to sound smooth. "Why? Is she really on that ship?"

"We're going to board it and find out."

"Good," she said, and there was something under the word, some live wire, and then it was gone, buried under the boredom again. "I'd like to bury her properly, if it's her. Nine years is a long time to tend a grave that doesn't exist."

And there it was — the sentence that should have ended the interview in her favor, delivered flawlessly, and I stood there in the cold knowing it was flawless, knowing that was the problem, and unable to prove a single thing.

* * *

We spent five hours scanning canisters. The scanner reads composition, mass, seal integrity. It does not read meaning. Ten thousand canisters of seeds are also, technically, ten thousand canisters of data storage substrate if you know what you're looking at, and whoever built the *Halcyon*'s archive core knew exactly what they were looking at, because we found nothing. Nothing anomalous, nothing extra, nothing missing. A perfect inventory.

Perfect inventories are written by people who expect auditors.

"She's clear," Voss said finally, in the shuttle bay, keeping her voice low. "Manifest matches, logs match, she cooperated with everything."

"Everything we asked."

"That's what cooperating is, ma'am."

"No, Ensign. Cooperating is answering questions. What she did was answer the questions I was going to ask, before I asked them, and leave me no time to ask better ones." I pulled off my gloves. My fingers ached with the cold, or with something else. "Did you notice she never once asked what we thought was on the *Halcyon*? Not directly. Not once in five hours."

Voss frowned. "Maybe she doesn't care."

"Everyone cares. Curiosity is the one universal. She rationed hers out like water in a drought, and every drop of it went where she wanted it to go." I looked back through the bay door at the lit corridor, where Tamsin Villalobos had returned to her plants, trowel in hand, a woman made entirely of innocence and topsoil. "She lied to me today, Ensign. At least twice. Possibly continuously."

"How do you know, if there's no evidence?"

"Because the truth doesn't require that much architecture." I stepped into the shuttle. "Log the inspection as passed. No findings. Full cooperation."

Voss stared. "Ma'am —"

"If I report suspicion, command sends a garrison, and a garrison shoots first and reads manifests never, and whatever is in that vault gets destroyed in the crossfire. I didn't come eleven hundred light-years to burn the library." I settled into the harness. "Besides. She thinks she won today. People who think they've won get comfortable. Comfortable people make mistakes."

"And if she doesn't?"

"Then I'll be back, and next time I won't need a pretense."

We lifted away from the glacier into the long violet dusk, and below us the beacon pulsed once, twice, steady as a heartbeat. I watched it until the curve of the world took it. Somewhere in those ten thousand identical canisters was a secret worth a ghost ship and a dead woman's name, and somewhere aboard the *Vigilant* was a file I shouldn't have reopened — Ilan Reyes-Vashti, deserter, last known transmission routed through a relay forty minutes from this ice.

He had taught me everything I knew about hearing ciphers in noise.

I wondered what he'd hear, if he could see his student now, logging a clean inspection over a dirty truth.

The beacon pulsed again, and I found I couldn't stop counting the interval.
