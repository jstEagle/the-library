## 15. Bargaining Chip

I have boarded forty-one vessels in my career as an inquisitor of the Concord, and I can tell you that every one of them falls into one of three categories: the guilty, the terrified, and the guilty-and-terrified. The Meridian convoy *Long Patience* — a name so sincere it had to be bureaucratic — was none of these. Its crew sat in their harnesses with the calm of people who had rehearsed being captured, which told me everything before my boots touched their deck.

The boarding was clean. Three frigates out of the dark on a trajectory no patrol should have known, mag-clamps, sealed hatches, done in eleven minutes. Someone at Command had been tipped off. Someone always is. Inquisitors are not encouraged to ask who tips whom; we are instruments, and instruments do not audit the hand that plays them.

But I audit anyway. It's why they find me tiresome.

* * *

They brought her to me in the convoy's mess hall because it was the only room with a table, and Command likes its negotiations to look like negotiations. Two chairs, one table, a viewport full of slow ice. Somewhere behind me, two of my own marines pretended not to listen.

Tamsin Villalobos looked worse than the intelligence files promised and better than I expected. The files said: nine years isolated, frost-scars on both hands, malnourished. They did not say that she would sit down before I offered the chair, or that she would look at me with the flat patience of someone who has already decided how much of this conversation she intends to survive.

"Inquisitor Kestrel," she said. "You interviewed me once. In the vault. You asked about my potato yields."

"I remember."

"You wrote in your report that I was 'cooperative but evasive.' Which is a very polite way of saying you thought I was lying about the potatoes."

"You were lying about the potatoes," I said. "Nobody grows that many potatoes alone for nine years without developing opinions about them. You had no opinions. That was the tell."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that I had spent more time thinking about her potatoes than either of us would ever admit aloud.

"The core," I said. "Where is it?"

"Safe."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have that's true," she said. "I'm finding those are rare, so I ration them."

I sat down across from her. Up close she smelled of engine coolant and old wool, and there was a scratch healing along her jaw from the skiff, from the tunnels, from whatever the last weeks had cost her. I had read the vault's fire logs. She had burned ten thousand years of seed stock to keep it out of our hands — no. Not burned. Blacked out. Buried under darkness and cold. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole woman sitting in front of me.

"Do you know what your mother was?" I asked.

"A courier."

"Do you know what the Concord calls a courier who carries a message capable of fracturing fleet cohesion during wartime?"

"A witness," Tamsin said. "You call her a witness. I've read your statutes, Inquisitor. Article Nine. You're not allowed to destroy heritage vessels, heritage archives, or designated neutral persons. My family were designated neutral persons for four generations. Your own law has been protecting me this entire time, and it's been driving your command insane."

I let the silence sit for a moment, because she was right, and because I wanted to see whether she'd flinch in the gap. She didn't. Nine years alone will teach you to be comfortable inside silence the way sailors are comfortable inside storms.

"Here is what I know," I said finally. "You have half a message. A genetic key opened the first half. The second requires a cipher phrase, given, not inherited. And I know whose cadence is woven through the error messages, because I trained him."

Her breath changed. It was small — a hitch of maybe a quarter-second — but I have spent twenty years listening to people breathe in interrogation rooms, and a quarter-second is a library.

"Ilan Reyes," I said. "Signals officer, third class, decorated twice, then deserter. Except he wasn't a deserter, was he. He was here. In your vault. Maintaining your beacon. Logging his name in maintenance records like a man signing love letters he expected no one to read."

"He didn't sign anything," Tamsin said quietly. "He just did the work. He believed the work mattered more than the credit."

"Yes," I said. "That sounds like him."

And it did. That was the problem. Ilan Reyes had been my student for two years aboard the *Vigilant* — a thin, serious young man who could hear a jammed frequency the way musicians hear a wrong note, and who asked questions in seminars that made senior officers leave the room early. When he vanished, I was assigned to write the desertion assessment. I wrote that he showed no prior indicators. What I meant was: he showed nothing but indicators, and I had filed each one under *idealism* because idealism is easier to ignore than treason.

"You want him back," Tamsin said. It wasn't a question.

"I want to offer you a trade," I said. "The core — intact, delivered to me, unopened by anyone else — and in exchange, Ilan walks free. Not reassigned. Not quietly disappeared. Free. Civilian status, full pardon, whatever world he wants."

* * *

She looked at me for a long time then, and I watched her do the arithmetic, because I knew she would. That was the trap I'd been handed and the trap I was setting, all in one motion, and I hated the elegance of it.

Because here is what Command does not understand about offers like this: they only work if they're real. A fake trade collapses under scrutiny. So they made it real. Ilan Reyes is alive, held in the Tharsis facility, and the release order exists, signed, waiting on my confirmation code. I checked. I checked twice, because checking is the only prayer I know.

And here is what I do not say aloud, to her or to Command: I want him free regardless. The trade is real, and it is also the only honest thing in the room, and it is also a lie, because everyone at this table knows the message will be edited whether or not the core changes hands.

"Concord will broadcast it their way," Tamsin said. Not asking. Watching me confirm it.

"They'll frame it," I said. "Omit certain fleet actions. Emphasize Meridian's role in the seeding program. The truth, curated."

"Curated." She tasted the word like something spoiled. "My mother spent thirty years keeping that message whole. Both halves. Both sides' guilt in the same breath. That's the entire point — that neither faction gets to be the villain, because neither faction gets to be innocent. If Concord edits it—"

"Then it becomes propaganda with footnotes. Yes."

"Then why are you offering me this?"

And there it was. The question I had been dreading, aimed at me with those flat grey eyes, and I discovered — with genuine professional embarrassment — that I intended to answer it honestly.

"Because I've spent three years chasing a ghost," I said, "and the ghost turned out to be a man who chose exile over reporting a frequency match between a beacon and a prayer-chant. Because I've read the star charts in the first half of that message — yes, we recovered partial telemetry from your skiff, don't look surprised, you were picked up by a Meridian patrol broadcasting on open channels — and I know what the charts show. One origin. One garden. Two sets of children who burned each other's fields for sixty years over a difference we invented."

I leaned forward.

"I am an instrument of the Concord, Ms. Villalobos. Instruments do not get to choose what music they play. But you are offering me, right now, the only chance I will ever have to choose the note. Take the trade. Let me bring him home. Then fight the edit with everything you have — and you have more than Command thinks, because I have seen you lie about potatoes and I have seen you burn a vault, and I do not believe for one second that a woman like you delivers a message she hasn't already figured out how to protect."

* * *

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside the viewport, a shard of comet ice tumbled past, catching the convoy's running lights, turning slowly like something deciding.

"You said the release order waits on your confirmation code," she said.

"Yes."

"So if I refuse — if I keep the core and deliver it whole somewhere you can't reach — Ilan stays in Tharsis."

"That's the shape of it."

"And if I accept, he goes free, and Concord curates the message, and sixty more years of war get built on a truth with the guilt surgically removed."

"Also the shape of it."

"My mother designed this so it couldn't be split," she said. "Two keys. Genetic and given. Inheritance and choice. Do you know why? Because she believed any message worth killing for has to be carried by someone who chooses it — not someone born into it. She built the lock so that the courier has to decide. Every time."

"And what are you deciding?"

"I'm deciding that you just told me the truth three times in a row," Tamsin said, "which for a Concord inquisitor must feel like undressing in public. And I'm deciding that I believe you want him free more than you want the core, and that Command knows it, and that they gave you this offer precisely because they knew I'd see it."

She stood up. The marines shifted; I waved them still.

"But here's what you didn't calculate, Inquisitor," she said. "You think the core is the message. It isn't. The core is the *keyhole*. The message needs a transmitter powerful enough to reach both fleets — and there's exactly one, and both sides are already converging on it. You haven't captured the message today. You've captured the person who knows how to use it. And I am the only living soul who carries both keys — one in my blood, and one—"

She stopped. Something crossed her face that I recognized from mirrors, from the moment before an officer signs an order they'll spend the rest of their life defending.

"—and one I can speak aloud anywhere, to anyone, at any time," she finished. "Kill me and it dies with me. Trade me and it travels with me. Edit the message and I'll tell the whole galaxy which parts you cut. There is no version of this where Concord owns the truth. There is only the version where you help me give it away."

* * *

I sat there and understood, with the particular clarity that comes too late and costs too much, that Command had sent me to make a trade and I had instead witnessed a coronation. The frightened caretaker from the seed vault was gone. Sitting across from me was a courier — the last of four generations of them — and couriers, I now realized, are not people who carry things. They are people who cannot be stopped from carrying them.

"I need an answer," I said. "Command expects the core within the day."

"Then tell them this." She leaned down, close enough that only I could hear, though I suspect she knew the marines' throat-mics would catch it anyway, and suspected further that she wanted them to. "Tell them the Villalobos courier accepts the exchange — Ilan Reyes for safe passage to the relay. Unedited message as the price of my cooperation. And tell them if the broadcast goes out curated, I will spend the rest of my life correcting the record, one fleet, one station, one drunk spacer in one port bar at a time. I have nine years of practice talking to things that don't listen. Your admirals will be a novelty."

"And if they refuse?"

"Then you'll have to decide," she said, "what kind of instrument you are."

She walked out past the marines without looking back, hands loose at her sides, frost-scarred and unafraid, and I remained at the table with my confirmation code burning in my pocket like a coal.

On the bulkhead screen, the tactical plot updated itself. New contacts, inbound from the deep dark, transponders dark, trajectories converging on a single point in the Kuiper belt.

Both fleets were coming to the relay.

And somewhere in the Tharsis facility, a man who heard wrong notes in jammed frequencies was about to learn that his student had finally chosen a side — just not the one anyone ordered.
