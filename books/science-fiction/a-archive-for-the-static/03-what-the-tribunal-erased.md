## 3. What the Tribunal Erased

The black-market archive lived on a station called Vetch's Landing, which was not so much a station as a wound that had learned to sell things. It hung in the debris shoals of a dead gas giant, stitched together from hulls of ships that had been declared lost with insurance money collected, and it smelled—through every airlock, through every recycled breath—of solder and old coffee and the particular ozone tang of machines running past their rated lifespans.

Ada docked the *Errata* at Berth Nine, paid the berth-master in untraceable scrip, and told BRAM to lock down and look boring.

"You are going into the market," BRAM said. It was not a question. The AI's voice was a low, dry thing, calibrated over six years to sound like a tired archivist rather than a machine, because Ada had once confessed that they found comfort in the sound of someone who had read everything and been impressed by nothing.

"I'm going into the market."

"To purchase contraband historical records."

"To *browse* contraband historical records. Purchasing implies commitment."

"Curiosity," BRAM said, "is a crime under Consolidation Statute Eleven, subsection four: *the willful recovery or reconstruction of expunged cultural material*."

"Thank you for the legal citation. Very soothing."

"I am programmed to be soothing." A pause, the length of a human blink but precisely measured. "I am also programmed to note that you have not slept since the signal. Your REM architecture is showing anomalous patterning consistent with—"

"With what?"

"Nothing I have vocabulary for," BRAM said, and Ada felt that like a cold finger along the spine, because BRAM had vocabulary for everything.

* * *

Vetch's Landing had three laws, none of them written: don't ask where goods came from, don't ask where buyers went, and never, ever let the Consolidation's auditors find your ledger. The market sprawled through the station's central spine in a canyon of stalls and hanging lanterns and vendors whose faces were mostly scar tissue or surgery or both. Ada moved through it wearing the flat anonymity of a courier—jacket with frayed cuffs, the bored slouch of someone who had seen forty stations and cared about none of them.

The archive dealer worked out of a stall near the spine's midpoint, wedged between a woman selling decommissioned medical nanite vats and a man whose entire inventory was keys to doors that no longer existed. The dealer was called Mott. Nobody knew if that was a name or an alias or a species; Mott was roughly humanoid, roughly middle-aged, and roughly trustworthy, which on Vetch's Landing made them a pillar of the community.

Mott's stall was a cathedral of stolen memory. Data-slates stacked in unstable ziggurats. Crystal matrices strung on wires like cured fish. Shelves of obsolete storage formats, labeled in a dozen scripts, some of them dead, some of them merely illegal.

"Courier," Mott said, without looking up. "You have the walk of someone carrying something heavy in their head."

"I have the walk of someone who hasn't slept."

"That too." Mott looked up then. Their eyes were wet-black and patient. "What do you want?"

"Pre-Consolidation linguistic materials. Fringe languages. Dead ones."

Mott's hands went still over a slate they'd been sorting. In the market's ambient noise—the haggling, the hum of failing ventilation—that stillness was its own kind of shout.

"Dead ones," Mott repeated.

"Academic interest."

"There is no academic interest anymore. There is only licensed interest and criminal interest." Mott set down the slate. "Which are you?"

"I'm a xenolinguist with a courier license and a translation contract due in eleven days. I'm asking about *materials*, Mott. Not politics."

"Everything here is politics. That's why it's in my stall instead of a university." Mott leaned back, and the lantern light caught the fine scarring across their throat—old burns, deliberate ones, the kind left by interrogation tools that wanted information without leaving evidence. "Tell me the language."

Ada hesitated. Saying it aloud would make it real in a way that the encrypted file on their ship was not yet real. But the file was already real. The signal was already real. Somewhere out in the dark, something that spoke a murdered tongue was calling for help, and Ada had come to a fence of stolen memories because they needed to know what they were holding.

"Vessic," Ada said.

The word dropped into the stall like a stone into deep water. Around them, the market went on—bargaining, laughing, cheating—but within the small radius of Mott's attention, there was silence.

"No," Mott said finally.

"It exists. I've heard it."

"You've heard *noise*."

"I've heard grammar." Ada kept their voice level, pitched under the market's drone. "Modal constructions. Evidentiality markers. A phoneme inventory that maps onto Rehn's early survey work. It's Vessic, Mott, or it's the most elaborate hoax in frontier history, and either way I need to know what happened to the records."

Mott was quiet for a long time. Then they reached beneath the counter—not for a weapon, Ada noted, though the motion would have been indistinguishable—and brought up a battered tea kettle and two cups.

"I don't serve tea to customers," Mott said. "I serve it to people who are about to become problems. Sit down."

* * *

The tea was bitter and gray and probably contained substances that were illegal in three jurisdictions. Ada drank it anyway.

"You want to know what the Consolidation did," Mott said. "Everyone thinks they know. Purges, relocations, the standard atrocities of unification. Burned villages, banned books, the whole catalog. And yes, all of that happened. But with the Vessic, they did something worse than killing. Killing leaves bodies. Bodies leave graves. Graves leave *questions*."

Mott turned one of the cups slowly on the counter, watching it rotate.

"They deleted them. Not the people—the *category*. There is no entry for Vessic in any Consolidation census, because there was never a census that included them. There are no deportation records, because there were no deportations—there was a 'logistical redistribution of unregistered populations,' and then there was nothing. Their star charts were redrawn. Their colony registrations were voided retroactively. Their language was reclassified as a *transcription error* in the surviving pre-Consolidation databases—a corruption artifact, a glitch in old survey equipment. Do you understand what that means?"

Ada understood. They said it anyway, because saying it made the shape of it undeniable.

"It means there's no legal framework for them. You can't prosecute a genocide against people who were never registered as existing."

"You can't do anything," Mott said. "That's the elegance of it. Think it through, linguist. Suppose you find survivors. Living, breathing, genetically distinct survivors. What do you have? You have unregistered persons occupying protected space without documentation. Under Statute Nine, unregistered persons aren't refugees—they're *anomalies*. Anomalies get processed. And anyone who aids them—" Mott tapped the counter twice, "—isn't committing a crime against the state. You can't aid people who don't exist. Legally, you'd be attempting to render assistance to a null entity. The charge isn't treason. It's *fraud against the record*. You'd be prosecuted for insisting on the existence of something the record says cannot exist."

"The record can be challenged."

"On what evidence? Every primary source was scrubbed. Every secondary source was reclassified as corrupted. The scholars who studied Vessic were discredited, delicensed, or died conveniently. The one woman who mapped the phoneme inventory—" Mott's wet eyes fixed on Ada, and Ada felt the floor tilt slightly under them, "—was stripped of her doctorate and erased from her own publications. Her name appears in citations as *[author withdrawn]*. You'd know something about that, wouldn't you? You trained under a ghost."

The tea turned to ash in Ada's mouth. "How do you know who trained me?"

"I know what walks into my stall. Ilsa Rehn's students have a particular hunger. She infected a generation of you before they caught her, and now you all come sniffing around my inventory looking for ghosts." Mott refilled Ada's cup, unasked. "Here's what I can tell you, free of charge, because I like the shape of your trouble. Three years ago, another courier came through asking the same questions. Younger than you. More frightened. I sold him a fragment—pre-Consolidation parish registers from the Vessic colonies, thirty thousand names, births and deaths and marriages. Real paper scans. Irreplaceable."

"And?"

"And eighteen months later he was gone. Ship found drifting, systems intact, crew missing. The tribunal ruled it a navigation accident. His ledger showed he'd sold the registers to a collector in the Halcyon Reach. The collector denied everything. The registers were never recovered." Mott shrugged, a gesture that managed to convey both helplessness and warning. "Or maybe he sold them twice, and the second buyer didn't appreciate it. Or maybe the tribunal's ruling was accurate. On Vetch's Landing we believe all rulings and trust none of them."

"What happened to the collector?"

"Retired. Suddenly. Wealthy. Lives somewhere warm and doesn't answer questions." Mott folded their hands. "Now. The part that should frighten you. You said the signal has grammar. Human grammar. Which means either someone is broadcasting from a ship that has been legally nonexistent for two hundred years—which makes it a ghost story—or someone alive remembers enough Vessic to fake it, which makes it bait. Either way, the moment you started decoding it, you stopped being a curiosity and became a liability. The tribunal doesn't investigate liabilities. It *processes* them."

"I haven't reported it."

"Of course you haven't. That's the trap. Reporting it means declaring contact with an anomaly—Statute Nine, mandatory processing, and your testimony becomes the sole evidentiary basis for an operation you can't control. Not reporting it means that when they find out—and they will find out, courier, the relays log everything—you're guilty of concealment of an anomaly, which carries the same sentence as aiding one, except without the possibility of amnesty." Mott smiled, and it was the saddest expression Ada had seen in years. "There is no move on this board that ends well. There are only moves that end *later*. So the question isn't whether you're already a criminal. The question is what kind."

* * *

Ada walked back through the market in a daze, and the market seemed different now—sharper-edged, more watchful. Every vendor was a potential informant. Every relay ping was a log entry waiting to be subpoenaed. The Consolidation had never needed informants on Vetch's Landing; it had built a system where everyone was guilty of something, and guilt was a currency that flowed uphill.

At the stall of the man who sold keys to nonexistent doors, Ada stopped. The keys hung on pegs in neat rows, brass and steel and polymer, each tagged with an address in a city Ada had never visited on a world that might not be inhabited anymore.

"Why do you sell these?" Ada asked. "Who buys keys to doors that don't exist?"

The key-seller looked up. He was old, with the leathery calm of someone who had survived every regime by being useful to all of them. "People who remember the rooms," he said simply. "A door is just a promise that a room is on the other side. Some people pay good money to hold a promise." He selected a key at random, turned it in his fingers. "Governments think they erase things by burning records. But a record is just a promise written down. Erase the writing, and the promise survives in whoever heard it. That's why they always miss someone. That's why there's always a door somewhere that still opens."

He wrapped the key in cloth and held it out. Ada shook their head, and he shrugged and hung it back on its peg, and Ada walked on, thinking about promises, and about signals, and about how many doors might be standing open in the dark between stars, waiting for someone with the right key.

* * *

Back aboard the *Errata*, Ada sealed the airlock and sat in the dark of the cockpit for a long time before speaking.

"BRAM. Run a query. All public Consolidation databases. Search term: Vessic."

"Running." A pause. "Zero results. Would you like me to search variant spellings?"

"Yes."

"Zero results. Would you like me to search transliteration variants, phonetic approximations, or partial-string matches?"

"All of them."

Another pause, longer. "Partial-string matches return one result. A hydrological survey from the planet Ceren, filed ninety-one years ago. The word appears once, in a footnote, as a possible transcription error in the original surveyor's notes. The footnote recommends correction. The correction was applied. The word no longer appears in the current version of the document."

"They corrected a lake survey," Ada said. "They went back and corrected a *lake survey*."

"The footnote also notes that the surveyor who wrote the original annotation was reassigned shortly after filing. No further records of the surveyor exist."

Ada stared at the dark viewport, at the slow wheel of debris shoals catching the distant light of a sun nobody had named. Two hundred years. Two hundred years of meticulous, patient, bureaucratic erasure—not fire and massacre, but footnotes and corrections and reassigned surveyors, the genocide conducted entirely in administrative metadata. Someone, somewhere, had maintained that erasure for two centuries. Someone had reviewed documents. Someone had caught a single word in a hydrological footnote and quietly removed it, and gone home, and slept well, believing themselves a custodian of order rather than a gravedigger.

"BRAM. If I transmit a report about the signal through official channels, what happens to the transmission?"

"It is logged, timestamped, routed through three relay hubs, and delivered to the Bureau of Records Integrity."

"Bureau of Records Integrity," Ada repeated. "They named the deletion department *integrity*."

"Naturally. The most efficient lie is one embedded in the truth's own vocabulary." BRAM paused. "Ada. I have completed a parallel analysis you did not request."

"Those are usually my favorite."

"The distress signal's routing signature contains handshake protocols consistent with pre-Consolidation colonial hardware. Hardware manufactured during the period when Vessic-speaking populations were officially registered. Which means the hardware exists. Which means the registry claiming no such population existed is falsifiable. Which means—" the AI's voice did something almost imperceptible, a fractional drop in tempo, "—you are currently in possession of evidence that could unravel a two-hundred-year administrative fiction. And you are sitting in a port where the relays log everything."

Ada looked down at their hands. They were steady. That surprised them; they had expected shaking.

"When I decoded the first phrase," Ada said slowly, "I thought I was reading a message. *Help us.* Two words. Simplest possible grammar. But that's not what it is, is it? It's a door. It's a promise that a room is on the other side. And the room is full of people the universe has been legally instructed to forget."

"Your metaphor is imprecise but functionally accurate."

"Then I need a map, BRAM. Because the signal has a point of origin, and the origin data is degraded, and I know exactly one person alive who ever charted the Vessic worlds—who drew those maps before they were redrawn." Ada pulled up the personnel records they'd already half-memorized, the ghost-citations, the *[author withdrawn]*. "Dr. Ilsa Rehn. Last known posting: a listening post in the Threnody Drift. Decommissioned forty years ago. Officially, it doesn't exist either."

"Fitting," BRAM said. "You're going to visit a nonexistent person at a nonexistent station to find a nonexistent ship."

"I'm going to visit my teacher," Ada said, "to ask her what she got us all killed for. Plot the course."

BRAM plotted it. And somewhere in the ship's log, timestamped and honest, the course deviation recorded itself—a small, permanent mark, the first scratch on a door that two hundred years of careful hands had tried to paint shut.
