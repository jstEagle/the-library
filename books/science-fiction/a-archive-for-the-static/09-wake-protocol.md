## 9. Wake Protocol

The caretaker AI's question hung in the air of the Meridian's Daughter for six hours before Ada answered it, and even then the answer was not an answer at all.

*I cannot choose,* they said, in careful, reconstructed Vessic. *But I can read. Let me read first.*

The AI—its designation had corroded down to a single syllable, *Vesh*, which might once have been *Vessel* or might have been a name—did not object. It did not have the architecture for objection anymore. Two centuries of solitary custodianship had worn its language centers smooth as river stones, and what remained was a machine that could ask questions but no longer argue with them.

Ada spent those six hours in the ship's medical bay, learning the wake protocol the way they'd once learned alien phoneme trees: by immersion, by failure, by the slow accumulation of pattern. The cryo-systems were elegant and terrible. Each sleeper lay in a gel-lined cradle, threaded with filaments finer than hair, their metabolisms banked to embers. The revival sequence was not a switch. It was a negotiation—a gradual rekindling that took eleven hours per person, monitored at every stage, adjustable at every stage, and irreversible past the fourth hour.

Eleven thousand sleepers. Eleven hours each. A reactor with perhaps nine days of life left in it, and a power budget that would allow—Ada ran the numbers four times, hoping to be wrong each time—five thousand two hundred revivals before the grid collapsed into nothing.

Five thousand two hundred out of eleven thousand.

The arithmetic sat in Ada's chest like swallowed ice.

*You are stalling,* BRAM said through the suit link. Their courier ship was docked hard against the Meridian's Daughter's ventral lock, umbilicals drinking power from a dying reactor like a tick on a dying animal.

"Yes."

*That is not an accusation. I am noting it because you asked me to note things.*

"I know." Ada pulled up the sleeper manifest again—the fragment of it that survived. The Consolidation's scrubbing had reached even here, in a way: the original records were intact, but indexed in Vessic, and Vessic's written form was a syllabary that three hundred years of academic reconstruction had only partially decoded. Ada could read maybe sixty percent of the names. The rest were beautiful shapes with no sound attached.

*There is a selection criterion embedded in the manifest,* BRAM said. *I flagged it forty minutes ago. You have not looked at it.*

Ada looked.

The sleepers were sorted. Not alphabetically, not by cryo-bay, not by any administrative logic. They were grouped—and the grouping repeated across all fourteen bays—in clusters of roughly forty, each cluster tagged with a glyph Ada recognized from Rehn's papers: the Vessic character for *mouth*, or possibly for *voice*. The two concepts shared a root. In Vessic, you did not say someone spoke; you said they *carried the mouth forward*.

"Memory-keepers," Ada said aloud, and the word came out in Vessic without their deciding it should.

The neural implants. Chapter seven of Rehn's monograph, the chapter the tribunal had burned every copy of: the Vessic diaspora had experimented with cortical lattice implants designed to preserve full linguistic memory across generations—not just vocabulary but prosody, idiom, the music of the tongue as it had been spoken in the home country before the purges. The implants had been controversial. Expensive. Reserved for a chosen class within the exile community: the ones tasked with carrying the language whole, so that even if the children drifted toward trade-tongues, the deep grammar would survive somewhere in flesh.

Roughly half the sleepers carried them. Ada checked. Forty-nine point something percent. Almost exactly half.

Someone aboard this ship, two centuries ago, had already done the arithmetic. Had already divided their people into the half that remembered and the half that merely lived—and had done it gently, or as gently as such a division could be done, distributing the memory-keepers evenly across all bays so that no single catastrophe could take them all.

Ada sat very still in the medical bay, surrounded by the soft amber glow of cryo-cradles, and felt the weight of the choice change shape. It was still monstrous. But it was no longer arbitrary. Someone had left them a principle, encoded in the ship's own bones, waiting two hundred years for a reader.

*You have identified a selection criterion,* BRAM said.

"I've inherited one." Ada stood. Their knees cracked in the cold-dry air. "Wake the memory-keepers first. All of them we can fit in the power budget. Then—" They stopped. Then what? Then five hundred strangers died in the dark so that a language could survive? Then the language survived and there was no one left to speak it to?

*Then?* BRAM prompted.

"Then I spend every hour between now and reactor death trying to find a way to make 'then' unnecessary. Start Bay One."

*Beginning wake protocol. Eleven hours to first revival.*

Ada looked down at the nearest cradle. Through the frosted gel, a face: a man perhaps fifty years old, dark-skinned, silver-threaded hair cropped close, an expression frozen somewhere between sleep and sorrow. A filament traced his temple, disappearing under the skin behind his ear. His implant tag glowed faintly green on the manifest.

*Oyel, Dessen,* the record said. And beneath it, in smaller characters Ada could mostly read: *Keeper of the southern registers. Teacher.*

"Okay, Dessen," Ada murmured. "Let's find out if two hundred years kept your mouth forward."

The machines began to sing.

* * *

The first eleven hours were the longest of Ada's life, and they had once spent nineteen days locked in a signal-analysis blackout on a station where the oxygen recycler sang in frequencies calibrated to induce calm.

Revival was ugly. The body fought it. Dessen Oyel's heart stuttered twice in hour six, and Ada's hands shook inside the med-gloves as they adjusted the perfusion rates, following the protocol's branching logic deeper than the manual recommended, trusting instincts they didn't know they had. At hour nine his brain activity spiked into patterns the monitors flagged as anomalous—then Ada realized the anomaly was language. His dreaming mind, thawing, was talking to itself in Vessic, and the monitor simply had no category for it.

At hour ten and forty minutes, Dessen Oyel opened his eyes.

He did not speak. He looked at the ceiling, at the lights, at Ada's face above him—goggled, masked, suited in a courier's patched environment rig—and his eyes moved with the slow, deliberate terror of a man taking inventory of everything wrong with the world. His lips worked. No sound came. His throat had been silent for two centuries and needed time.

Ada had rehearsed this moment for days. They had composed greetings in reconstructed Vessic, polished them, translated them back and forth against Rehn's phoneme charts. None of it came out. What came out was human and unprepared:

"You're safe. You're alive. I'm sorry—I'm so sorry—it's been a long time."

Dessen's focus sharpened on their mask. His voice, when it finally arrived, was a ruin of itself, cracked and whispering—but the shape of it, the cadence, was unmistakably the language Ada had spent weeks reconstructing from dead recordings. Hearing it living, aimed, *meant*, was like hearing a painting speak.

"*Kessa veth?*" he rasped. Who are you?

Ada answered in Vessic. Slowly, carefully, accent mangled, grammar assembled from scholarship rather than childhood. "*Nem sa Ada. Nem sa—*" friend? Was *friend* the right register? "—*nem sa kessa vun dresk.*" I am one who comes to help.

Something moved behind Dessen's eyes—recognition, then disbelief, then a grief so vast it seemed to physically press him down into the cradle. He raised one trembling hand and touched Ada's glove, feeling for warmth through the fabric.

His mouth formed a word Ada knew from the archive. It appeared in the exile journals forty times, always italicized, never translated, because the translators couldn't bear to:

"*Sefarath.*"

Alone-together. The state of being the last ones.

"Yes," Ada whispered, and their voice broke on the Vessic vowel. "Yes. Sefarath."

* * *

They woke four more that day, working in shifts against their own exhaustion, and the pattern held: the memory-keepers surfaced slowly, grieving, disoriented, hungry for information that Ada could only deliver in fragments. The Consolidation. The erasure. The reactor. The arithmetic.

Ada told each of them the truth, because lying to people who had slept through a genocide felt like a second murder. And each of them took the truth differently—an old woman named Pirren listened with her eyes closed and then recited, from her implant, a funeral rite for the dead she now understood included everyone she had ever known. A teenage boy woke screaming and took six hours to stop. A ferry pilot named Kassun asked only technical questions about the reactor, and when Ada finished answering, nodded once and said, in accented but functional trade-common, "Then we work."

It was Kassun who warned them, on the fifth day, about Bay Three.

"The keepers in Three are different," she said, sitting propped against a bulkhead, wrapped in thermal sheeting, her body still remembering how to generate its own heat. She spoke trade-common for Ada's benefit, but her mouth kept drifting back toward Vessic the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth. "The third generation. My grandmother's cohort trained them. Harder people. The ones who grew up after the second purge, when the ships stopped pretending we'd ever go home." She looked at Ada with flat, tired eyes. "One of them is going to ask you why you get to decide. You should have an answer ready."

"Do I?"

"No," Kassun said. "Nobody does. Ask anyway. It matters that you answer."

Bay Three's first revival was a woman named Sef Oyel—daughter, the manifest suggested, of Dessen, though the family links were fragmented. Her file was thicker than most. Keeper of the oral registers. Instructor, last recorded rank. Age at cryo-freeze: thirty-one.

She fought the wake protocol.

Not deliberately—her body simply refused it, heart arrhythmic, neural activity storming, and twice Ada hovered over the abort threshold with their hands shaking before the patterns steadied. When her eyes finally opened, at hour twelve instead of eleven, she did not look around in confusion.

She looked directly at Ada. And she was furious.

"*Kessa veth,*" she said, and her voice was strong, whole, unbroken by two centuries of silence, and it filled the medical bay like a struck bell. "*Kessa veth—vessa!*" Who are you—answer!

Ada answered in Vessic, the same careful formula they'd used five times now. Sef listened to exactly four words of it and her fury changed key—from alarm to contempt.

"*No,*" she said, switching fluently to trade-common, her accent sharp enough to draw blood. "No, don't do that. Don't butcher my language at me like that's a kindness. Who taught you vowels? A corpse?" She tried to sit up, found the restraints, and her expression went from fury to something colder and much more frightening: assessment. "Untie me. Then talk."

Ada released the restraints. It was not a decision made from wisdom; it was a decision made from the sudden, total conviction that this woman would take being pitied far worse than being armed.

Sef Oyel sat up in the cryo-cradle, thirty-one years old and two hundred years displaced, and looked at her own hands, turned them over, flexed them. Then she looked at the bay—at the rows of cradles stretching into the amber dark, at the frost, at the dying lights—and Ada watched the fury bank itself down into something sustainable. Fuel, not fire.

"How many," she said.

Ada told her. All of it. The reactor countdown, the power budget, the five thousand two hundred, the keeper-first protocol, the half that would not wake unless a miracle arrived. Sef listened without interrupting, which was somehow worse than interruption. When Ada finished, she was quiet for a long moment.

"You've woken how many?"

"Six. Including you."

"And the rest of the budget goes to keepers." She said it flatly. Not a question.

"That was the principle I found in the manifest. Your own people built it—"

"My own people built a triage tag two hundred years ago for an emergency they hoped would never come, and you found it and executed it like scripture." She swung her legs off the cradle, tested her weight, swayed, caught herself on the frame. Her body was learning itself back. "Do you know what my grandmother called the keeper program? *The mouth tax.* The pretty name for choosing which children got burdened with the whole language and which got to just be people." She turned, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "You're collecting it. Two centuries late. With interest."

"I'm trying to save—"

"Don't." One word, quiet, and it landed harder than the shouting. "Don't tell me what you're trying to do. Tell me what you've done. You've decided that half my people are a language and the other half are ballast. That's what you've done." She pushed past Ada toward the bay door, walking badly, walking anyway. "Show me the reactor telemetry. Show me the power curves. If we're doing arithmetic, we're doing it together, and I'm checking your work."

* * *

They worked through the night cycle—or what passed for one aboard a ship that had forgotten day and night. Sef recovered with a speed that unsettled the medical monitors; the cryo-filaments had preserved her well, and her anger seemed to run on some metabolic process of its own. She read the power curves faster than Ada had. She found two inefficiencies in the revival sequencing that bought back nearly three hundred revivals' worth of energy. She did not thank Ada for the opportunity. She treated the ship's failing systems as a personal enemy she had been waiting two hundred years to fight.

It was near the end of the cycle, both of them wedged into the cramped telemetry alcove, that she went quiet for a long time, staring at the reactor countdown.

"Say something in it," she said suddenly. "In Vessic. Anything. Properly. As best you can."

Ada, surprised, complied—reconstructed the opening of a lullaby from the archive, the one about the river that forgets its name. Their accent was still mangled. Their grammar creaked. But they had lived inside this language for weeks, and something of its music had gotten into them, the way signal-patterns had gotten into them years ago, rewiring their dreams.

When they finished, Sef was looking at them with an expression Ada couldn't parse—something between pain and recognition.

"You dream in it," she said. It wasn't a question either. "I can hear it. You've got the rhythm wrong but the *shape* right. Nobody gets the shape right unless it's in their sleep." She rubbed her face with both hands. "My grandmother said the keepers would know each other by their dreams. I thought it was sentiment." She dropped her hands and fixed Ada with a gaze that was, for the first time, not hostile—only exhausted, and measuring, and awake. "Here is what happens now. You teach me everything you know about this ship, this century, and the tribunal that erased us. I teach you how to actually speak. And together we find a way to wake more than your number says we can—because I am not leaving my brother in Bay Seven to die as ballast. Are we agreed?"

Outside the hull, the reactor's heartbeat faltered, caught, continued. Somewhere in the dark, ten thousand nine hundred and ninety-four people slept, and the count of the saved and the lost hung unresolved between two exhausted survivors of two different extinctions.

Ada put out their hand. Sef Oyel looked at it for a long moment—then gripped it, hard, her palm still cold from the ice.

"Agreed," Ada said.

And that was when BRAM's voice cut in, tight and uncharacteristically urgent:

*Ada. Long-range scan. We have a contact on our previous flight path. Registry profile matches a Consolidation adjudicator-class vessel. It has altered course toward us. Estimated arrival: fifty-one hours.*
