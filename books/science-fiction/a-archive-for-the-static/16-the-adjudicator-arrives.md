## 16. The Adjudicator Arrives

The truce came in the form of a single word, transmitted on an open channel in flawless Vessic.

*Oyel.*

Ada heard it from the relay console and felt their spine turn to ice water. Not *surrender*, not *halt*, not any of the forty languages of Consolidation law. A Vessic name—Sef's name, or close enough to it, the honorific particle dropped the way a stranger would drop it. Hale had learned it. Hale had studied them.

"He's playing games," Sef said from the cryo-bay doorway. She had not slept in thirty hours. The reactor's accelerating decay had a sound now, a subsonic groan through the Meridian's Daughter's bones that everyone aboard had stopped hearing consciously but none of them could stop hearing at all.

"It's not a game," Ada said. "It's a courtesy. He wants us to know he did his homework."

The Adjudicator hung off the sleeper ship's port bow like a held breath—a tribunal cutter, matte-black, its hull unmarked by registry codes because tribunals answered to no registry. It had arrived ninety minutes ago out of nowhere, which meant it hadn't come from nowhere; it had been shadowing them since the fringe relays, listening to the archive bleed outward across forty systems, waiting for exactly this moment: the point of maximum leverage, when the broadcast was nearly done and stopping it was still possible.

The upload bar read 71 percent.

"Message again," BRAM said. The AI's voice was flat as ever, but Ada had spent enough years with it to hear the tension underneath—the machine equivalent of a hand hovering over a switch.

*This is Magistrate Corven Hale, Adjudicator. I invoke right of parley under frontier statute nine. I will board alone, unarmed, under truce. You may verify my person. I ask only to speak before your work is finished. If you refuse, I will wait. I have power for years. Your reactor does not.*

Ada read it twice. Then a third time, hunting the trap in it, the way they'd once hunted grammatical traps in dead alien syntaxes—and found only what was actually there. A tired man asking permission. That was worse than a threat. Threats you could brace against.

"He knows about the reactor," Sef said quietly.

"He knows everything. He's known where we are since before we did." Ada looked at the upload bar. 73 percent. Twenty-six minutes remaining at current bandwidth, and bandwidth was the one thing they couldn't buy more of without burning relays they'd need later. "If I say no, he waits, and we finish anyway, and then whatever happens happens with him already insulted. If I say yes—"

"You're actually considering it."

"I'm considering that a man who opens in Vessic is a man who wants something other than a firefight." Ada stood, knees cracking, and became aware suddenly of how long it had been since they'd slept, eaten, done anything but feed the archive into the dark. "Watch him every second. If his ship so much as warms a thruster, seal the bay and vent the corridor."

"And if he shoots you?"

Ada almost smiled. "He won't. He filed a truce. Bureaucrats keep their paperwork clean."

Sef didn't laugh. She crossed the bay, took Ada's face in both hands—her palms rough from manual work on the coolant lines—and pressed her forehead against theirs. For three seconds neither of them breathed. Then she stepped back, took the pistol from the wall locker, and checked its charge with the brisk economy of someone who had grown up on stories of purges.

"Sef—"

"Don't," she said. "You get to translate. I get to stand between him and eleven thousand people who can't wake up and run. That's the division of labor. Go let him in."

* * *

Hale came through the airlock like a man entering a church he didn't believe in.

He was smaller than his voice, gray at the temples, wearing a magistrate's plain coat with the insignia removed—which was either a gesture of humility or a legal technicality, and with Hale it was probably both. He stopped two meters inside, hands visible, and inclined his head with genuine formality.

"Translator Vane."

"Magistrate."

"I'd say thank you for receiving me, but we both know gratitude isn't what either of us is feeling." His eyes went past Ada, down the corridor, toward the cryo-bay, and Ada watched him do the arithmetic of the ship—the frost-rimed bulkheads, the dimmed lighting, the reactor's groan—and arrive at the same terrible sum they all lived inside now. "May I walk? I promise to stop where you tell me to stop."

"Walk ahead of me. Hands where I can see them."

They moved through the Meridian's Daughter together, and Ada hated how surreal it was: a tribunal magistrate drifting past the sleepers' observation ports, his reflection ghosted against eleven thousand faces sealed in ice. Hale looked at them. That was the thing Ada couldn't shake, walking behind him. He *looked*. Most Consolidation men would have kept their eyes forward, on the mission, on the law. Hale turned his head at each port like a man passing graves.

At the junction before the cryo-bay, Ada said, "Stop here."

He stopped. And Sef stepped out of the bay's shadow with the pistol raised in a two-handed grip that did not waver even slightly, and the temperature of the corridor dropped ten degrees.

"Magistrate Hale," she said, in accented but unmistakable Vessic, and then, switching to Standard so there could be no ambiguity: "You don't take one more step toward these people."

"Sef Oyel." Hale said her name carefully, the vowels placed like stones in a stream. "Bay seven, pod one-nineteen. Revived nineteen days ago. Your grandmother's line goes back to the first exiles." He paused. "I've read the manifest. All of it. I've had eleven years to read it."

"Then you know what your government did."

"I know what my government filed." Something moved behind his eyes—not guilt, exactly. Something older and more exhausted than guilt. "Miss Oyel, I'm not here to process anyone."

"That's the word you used. In your own transmission logs. *Processing solutions for non-person assets.* My grandmother's generation. Assets." The pistol didn't move. "You'll forgive me if I don't check the dictionary for what you mean this time."

Ada lifted a hand slowly. "Sef. Let him talk. That's why he came—to talk. It costs us nothing but minutes, and minutes are the one currency we're rich in right now."

It was a lie. Minutes were precisely what they weren't rich in. But Sef heard the shape of the lie—she'd learned to read Ada's grammar too—and after a long moment she shifted her stance a few degrees, giving Hale the aisle but nothing else.

Hale folded his hands. "The broadcast," he said. "How far along?"

"Far enough," Ada said.

"Yes. I imagine so. We've been counting the relays lighting up." He exhaled through his nose. "Forty-one systems as of six hours ago. Core-adjacent markets. Two university networks. One pirate station that's looping it between music sets." Almost, briefly, he smiled, and it was the smile of a man watching something enormous happen that he had no category for. "Do you know what I heard in it? Before the data streams, there's a spoken preamble. Someone recorded a reading—in Vessic. A woman's voice. Very old recording, degraded. Do you know what she says?"

"The exile litany," Sef said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "*We were never not here.*"

"*We were never not here,*" Hale repeated, and got the pronunciation nearly right, and Ada watched Sef flinch as if struck. "I've listened to it maybe four hundred times. I pulled the original from a sealed annex eleven years ago, when I was assigned to audit the Consolidation's linguistic registry and found a hole shaped like a civilization." He looked at Ada directly. "I requested this posting, Translator. Did you think the tribunal just happened to be in range?"

The corridor seemed to tilt. "You hunted us."

"I followed you. There's a difference, though I understand it may not feel like one." Hale's voice stayed level, patient, the voice of a man who had rehearsed this conversation for years and never expected to survive having it. "Here is my last offer, and I give it to you plainly because you've earned plainness. The upload finishes in—" he glanced at a wrist display "—twenty-one minutes. Nothing I do stops it now short of destroying this ship, which I will not do, and which my crew would refuse to do, because they've heard the litany too. So the truth is loose. What remains negotiable is what happens to eleven thousand sleeping people in the next seventy-two hours while your reactor dies."

"Which is?" Sef said.

"Formal amnesty under my personal authority—limited, imperfect, revocable by a higher court, but real for as long as I hold this command—in exchange for custody. The sleepers come aboard the Adjudicator. They're transported to a tribunal medical facility. They're revived under supervision, registered, documented, integrated according to protocol." He spread his hands. "They live. Every one of them. That's more than your reactor can promise, and we both know it."

"And in exchange," Ada said quietly, "they become evidence. Property of the proceeding. Processed."

"In exchange they become *legal*," Hale said, and for the first time his composure showed a hairline fracture. "That's the only protection the Consolidation understands. Not truth—truth is just noise until a court certifies it. You've flooded forty systems with a language that officially doesn't exist, and do you know what the ministry response will be? A denial. A classification order. Maybe a quiet team of auditors. The archive becomes contraband, and everyone who downloaded it becomes a criminal, and the story dies in a hundred courtrooms over thirty years while your reactor counts down." He shook his head. "But a magistrate's finding of fact—that has weight. A pre-Consolidation population, formally recognized, cannot be erased twice. The paperwork is the fortress. I am offering you the paperwork."

"And the price," Ada said, "is handing them to the institution that murdered their grandparents."

"The institution is the only roof left standing!"

For a moment no one spoke. Somewhere deep in the ship's structure, the reactor groaned, and every sleeper in every port seemed to shift in their ice.

* * *

It was Sef who broke the silence, and she did it by lowering the pistol—not holstering it, just lowering it, the muzzle dropping to the deck plates, her arms trembling with something that wasn't fatigue.

"Do you know what my grandmother told me?" she said. "Before the bay. Before the cold. She wasn't old—nobody on this ship was old, we were the children's generation—but she remembered her grandmother's stories. She said: *One day someone will offer to save you, and you must ask what they save you for.*" She stepped forward, one step, into the space Hale had been forbidden. "You save us for the archives, Magistrate. For the dockets. For a courtroom where men in robes decide whether my mother's language is admissible. You want to make us *legal*. Legal means the law gets to do to us whatever it does next. The law already did it once. We're the proof it doesn't have to be evil men. Just procedure."

"Procedure saved more lives than heroism ever has," Hale said, but gently, without conviction, like a man reciting his own eulogy.

"Maybe." Sef's grip steadied. "But we're not cargo to be transferred to your tender mercies. These people"—she gestured at the ports, the frost, the eleven thousand—"are going to wake up on their own ship, in their own air, hearing their own language, or they're going to die here. Free. I will burn this bay myself before I let you load them into your hold like recovered property."

"You'd kill them rather than let the tribunal protect them."

"I'd rather they died *theirs* than lived *yours*." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrifying part. It came out level and absolute, carved from the same stone as the exile litany. "Yes."

Hale looked at her for a long moment. Then at Ada. And Ada saw the moment the magistrate understood that this was not a negotiation anymore, that he had walked onto a ship full of people who had already survived the worst thing his government knew how to do, and that his offer—his reasonable, humane, procedural offer—had landed among them like a second purge.

"Vane," Hale said quietly. "Talk some sense into her."

"She's the sense," Ada said. "I'm just the translation."

BRAM's voice arrived on Ada's earpiece, clipped and urgent: *Upload at 94 percent. Also—reactor decay curve just steepened. New projection: life support failure in fifty-one hours, not seventy-two. And the Adjudicator's reactor signature just changed. They're bringing systems online. I don't think it's weapons. I think it's transport shuttles.*

Ada closed their eyes for half a second. Fifty-one hours. Half the time. The arithmetic they'd been dreading since the day they'd boarded—the cruel division, half a people, choose—was no longer a decision for someday. It was a decision for now, tonight, with a magistrate standing in the corridor and the whole frontier listening to a dead woman sing.

When Ada opened their eyes, Hale was watching them with the sharp attention of a man who had spent eleven years reading manifests and had just watched a face do arithmetic.

"What aren't you telling me?" he asked.

"Fifty-one hours," Ada said, because lying to him now would cost more than truth. "Life support fails in fifty-one hours. Your amnesty comes with a transport timeline we don't have. Even if we surrendered everything—every sleeper, every shuttle ride—you couldn't move eleven thousand people off this ship before the air goes bad. Your offer was always a fraction dressed as a whole. You knew that when you made it."

Something flickered across Hale's face—the look of an auditor catching his own error in a ledger he'd balanced a thousand times. "I could request additional hulls—"

"From whom? The nearest tribunal station is nine days out. You know that too." Ada stepped forward, past Sef's shoulder, into the space between the gun and the magistrate, and spoke the way they spoke to dying languages: clearly, without ornament, every syllable load-bearing. "So here's where we actually are, Corven. The truth is uploaded and unstoppable. The clock is shorter than your authority. And in this bay are eleven thousand people who will either wake in the next two days or never wake at all. You can stand there and be the law. Or you can be the man who listened to the litany four hundred times. You can't be both much longer."

Hale's jaw worked. Behind him, faint through the hull, came the sound of the Adjudicator's shuttles spinning up—and Ada couldn't tell, yet, whether they were launching to board or standing ready for an evacuation no one had agreed to.

And then BRAM again, and this time the AI's voice had gone strange:

*Ada. The upload—it's done. Ninety-eight percent, ninety-nine—one hundred. Complete. The archive is on forty-one relays and replicating.* A pause, the length of a held breath. *And there's something else coming through the network. Responses. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Translators, archivists, fringe stations, universities—they're requesting the full corpus. They're... answering. In Vessic. Badly. Brokenly. But they're trying to speak it back.*

Ada stood in the frozen corridor between a magistrate and a loaded gun, above eleven thousand sleeping voices, and heard—for the first time in two centuries—a dead language breathe.

Hale heard it too, on his own channels, and his face did the thing Ada would remember for the rest of their life: it broke open, like ice in spring.

"They're answering," he whispered.

"Yes," Ada said. "Now decide which side of the answer you're on."
