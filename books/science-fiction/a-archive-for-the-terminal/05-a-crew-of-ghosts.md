## 5. A Crew of Ghosts

The trouble with being invisible for five years, Ines reflected as she crouched in the ventilation crawl above Dez's workshop, was that you forgot how to knock.

She could have knocked. Dez's door still had a bell—a chipped brass thing shaped like a fish, salvaged from some drowned Earth port and wired to play three notes when pressed. She'd bought him that bell, back when buying things was something she did for people instead of avoiding them. But the customs officer on the docking ring had scanned her twice today, and the second scan had lingered like a hand on the back of her neck, so she'd come in through the ceiling instead, because paranoia was cheaper than funerals.

Below her, through a grate furred with dust, Dez sat hunched over a forgery bench, his magnifier rig throwing four small suns onto his work surface. He was aging a shipping manifest with a tea bag. Actual tea. He swore digital aging left fingerprints no human eye caught but every forensic algorithm itched at, and so he stained paper the way monks illuminated manuscripts, humming to himself, tongue between his teeth.

Ines dropped through the access panel, landed on a stack of blank identity chits with a sound like a sack of laundry, and Dez screamed in a register she'd have paid good credits to never hear again.

"Saints *above*—" He clutched his chest, magnifier rig swinging wildly. "I'm forging documents for a living, Ines. My whole nervous system is calibrated to detect people sneaking up on me. You just broke my only marketable skill."

"You'd have heard the bell."

"The bell is decorative! The bell says *I am a charming eccentric craftsman*, not *please announce your visits*." He pulled off the rig, revealing eyes ringed red from twelve hours under the lamps. Then he actually looked at her, and the humor drained out of his face the way heat leaves a hull. "You came through my ceiling."

"I know."

"You haven't come through my ceiling since the Meridian job. The last time you came through my ceiling, we both spent nine days in a cargo container pretending to be agricultural equipment." He stood slowly. "Who's looking for you?"

And there it was—the thing about Dez. He didn't ask *what happened*. He asked *who*, because what happened was always survivable; it was the *who* that killed you.

"A compliance director named Voss," Ines said. "And whatever enforcement arm Halcyon Consolidated keeps on retainer. And possibly everyone whose names are inside this." She set the drive on his bench, next to the tea-stained manifest.

Dez looked at the drive the way other men looked at unexploded ordnance. "That's not product."

"No."

"That's *people*."

"Three thousand of them." Ines told him about Relay Kestrel. About the waveform. About the children dreaming in forty beds across Halcyon Station, all dreaming the same drowning starlight. She watched him go pale in the lamplight, watched his hands—those precise, steady hands—begin to shake around the teabag he was still holding, dripping amber onto a forged customs seal worth two months' rent.

"I ran manifests during the Resettlement," Dez said quietly. "Nothing big. Passenger lists, mostly. I made them clean. I made them look *voluntary*." He set the teabag down with exaggerated care, like it might detonate. "You're telling me I forged the paperwork for a massacre."

"I'm telling you we can forge the paperwork for the opposite."

He laughed—one short, cracked note. "Ines Delacroix, asking me to commit treason against the largest corporation in the system. Do you know what they do to forgers? They don't even jail us. We're *evidence*. They keep us in a room until every document we've ever touched has been catalogued, and then—" He stopped. Looked at the drive again. Somewhere below them, faintly, a child on the station's lower rings was crying in her sleep; the walls carried sound strangely here, and Ines thought: *there goes number forty-one.*

"Okonkwo first or Sefa?" Dez said.

"Doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously. Okonkwo will say no. Sefa will say yes before you finish the sentence, and then Okonkwo will say yes specifically because Sefa said yes and he doesn't trust anyone else to fly her home alive." Dez was already pulling on his coat. "We do it in that order. Also, I'm bringing the bell."

"Why?"

"Because if we're going to die, I want to die in a household that answers its door like civilized people."

* * *

Okonkwo lived in a converted tugboat moored to the station's underside, where gravity was a rumor and the view was nothing but the turning dark. He answered the door holding a mug of something that smelled like engine coolant had married coffee, took one look at Ines, one at Dez, and one at the drive in her hands, and said, "No."

"You don't know what we're—"

"Ines. I retired." He said it the way other men said *I'm dying*. "Eleven thousand hours in the black. I flew the evacuation corridors myself, did you know that? Five years ago. Ferry runs out to the transfer barges. Families with their luggage. Kids waving at me through the viewport." His jaw worked. "I waved back. So whatever's in that box, whatever you're planning—I flew those people out already. It's done. It's been done."

"They never left the system," Ines said. "They're in the box."

The silence lasted long enough for the tugboat's old hull to tick twice as it adjusted to station spin.

"Say that again," Okonkwo said, "but slower, and standing where I can sit down first."

She told him everything, and he listened the way he'd once listened to engine diagnostics—completely, without blinking, building the failure model in his head. When she finished, he went to a locker she'd never seen open, and came back with a flight case covered in stickers from ports that no longer existed.

"Kestrel relay is eleven hours through dead space if you fly it angry," he said. "Fourteen if you fly it smart. No rescue coverage. No beacon traffic. If anything goes wrong out there, we are a story nobody tells." He opened the case: a pilot's certification, laminated, expired six years ago. He looked at it for a long moment. "I kept this so I'd remember who I used to be. Turns out I was saving it for a reason."

"Okonkwo," Ines started, "you don't have to—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't do the thing where you give me an exit. I've had five years of exits. They're very comfortable and they smell like coolant coffee." He finally smiled, and it transformed his whole face into the man who'd taught her to fly, a decade ago, back when she'd still let people teach her things. "Besides. Somebody has to keep these two idiots alive, and it clearly isn't going to be either of them."

"He's right," Dez said. "It isn't."

* * *

Sefa found *them*, which should have surprised no one. They were loading the drive into Okonkwo's tug when she stepped out from behind a stack of cargo pallets with a pry bar in one hand and an expression that promised to use it.

"Word travels," Ines said.

"Word *screams*. Half the memory-runners on the station know you're moving something hot, and the other half are enforcer informants, so functionally everyone knows." Sefa tossed the pry bar aside and crossed her arms. She was smaller than Ines remembered, harder too, the way people got when grief stopped being weather and became climate. "You're going to Kestrel."

"We're considering—"

"Don't insult me, Ines. I've known you eight years. You've never considered anything in your life; you decide and then you lie badly about having decided." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pushed through it. "My sister Amara was on the Resettlement lists. Third transport. I got a message from her, after. One message. *It's warm here, little brother, don't worry.* That's it. Five years ago." She looked at the drive. "Is she in there?"

Ines had checked. She'd spent four hours in a rented cubicle with the archive's index open, searching name by name, and she would carry the shape of that hour forever. "Yes," she said gently. "Sefa. She's in there. All of her."

Sefa made a sound that wasn't quite a sob and wasn't quite a laugh, and then she walked past all of them and up the tug's boarding ramp like she owned it.

"Where are you going?" Okonkwo called.

"You're flying to Kestrel. I'm coming. This is not a committee." She paused at the airlock and turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "Five years I thought she was somewhere far away and happy. Five years I *resented* her a little, for leaving without saying goodbye properly. And the whole time she was—" She shook her head. "I'm coming. And Ines? Next time you assemble a crew to fight a corporation, maybe lead with that part. Some of us would've signed on faster."

* * *

They lifted from Halcyon Station at 0300 station time, riding a garbage barge's transponder signature that Dez had forged so beautifully the barge itself filed no complaint. Ines sat in the cockpit's jump seat and watched the station shrink behind them—all those sleeping children, dreaming their shared dream, and somewhere among them an enforcer team pulling files with her name threaded through them like a fuse.

Okonkwo flew. Dez navigated and complained about the navigation. Sefa sat in the cargo hold with the drive cradled in her lap, talking to it in a low voice, telling her sister things—station gossip, mostly, the price of protein, the new dock foreman's terrible taste in music—and Ines understood that she was doing what mourners do when the grave turns out to be a door.

"So," Dez said from the nav console, not looking up. "Are we going to acknowledge what this is?"

"A smuggling run," Ines said.

"With three thousand plaintiffs aboard."

"A rescue mission, then."

"With no rescue possible, per the pilot himself." Dez gestured at Okonkwo, who raised his coffee mug in solemn confirmation. "This is a family, Ines. A small, armed, deeply illegal family. We're allowed to say it."

Ines looked at the back of Okonkwo's head, gray-shot now, steady as bedrock. At Sefa's voice drifting up from the hold, mid-anecdote, wet-eyed and laughing. At the drive glowing softly in its case, warmer than any drive had a right to be.

"Family," she said, testing the word like a forged seal, checking it for fingerprints.

It passed inspection.

Ahead of them, dead space opened like a held breath, and at the far edge of it, cold and patient, Relay Kestrel waited with three thousand voices—and eleven hours later, when Okonkwo cut the engines and the derelict relay drifted into the viewport, every light on its ring flickered on at once, though it had no power left to give.

"Oh," Dez whispered. "That's not ominous at all."
