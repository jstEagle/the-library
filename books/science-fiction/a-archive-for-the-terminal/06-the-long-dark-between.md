## 6. The Long Dark Between

There was a saying among the old hauler pilots, back before Okonkwo retired, that dead space wasn't empty. It was just polite. It didn't bother you with weather or traffic or customs officers with too much time on their hands. It simply waited to see whether you'd brought enough of everything.

He had brought enough of everything. He'd checked three times. He checked again anyway, because the *Magpie's Rest* was thirty-one years old, because her life-support scrubbers were older than that, and because the four people currently trusting him with their lungs deserved a pilot who counted twice.

"Status, Captain?" Dez called from behind him, where she'd wedged herself into the jump seat with a tablet and what she claimed was work. She was playing solitaire. He could see the cards.

"Eleven hours to Kestrel," he said. "Scrubbers nominal. Water recycling at ninety-four percent. Nobody touch anything."

"You said nobody touch anything last time and then you touched something."

"I am the captain. I'm exempt."

Ines sat in the second chair, the drive case strapped across her lap like a sleeping child. She hadn't let go of it since they'd cleared Halcyon's traffic control under a forged manifest that read, in Dez's best bureaucratic handwriting, AGRICULTURAL SEED STOCK — NONPERISHABLE. Sefa was in the back, running passive scans on a loop, watching for the thing they all knew was coming.

The enforcers had picked them up two hours out from the station. Two ships, holding position in the *Rest*'s wake like patient dogs. Not closing. Not hailing. Just there, close enough that their engine signatures showed on every sweep, far enough that no one could call it an attack.

"They're waiting for us to cross into dead space," Ines said quietly.

"Of course they are." Okonkwo adjusted his grip on the yoke out of habit more than need; the autopilot flew better than he did these days, but a captain who didn't hold the stick felt like a man who'd stopped being introduced at parties. "Out here, if something goes wrong, there's no rescue. No witnesses. They don't have to shoot us down. They just have to wait for the universe to do it for them."

"That's cheerful," said Dez.

"I'm seventy years old, girl. Cheerful is for people with time to spare."

* * *

Hour six. The enforcers still held formation, a half-light-second back, silent as debt collectors. Sefa came forward with tea — real tea, hoarded from her own stores, which told Okonkwo how seriously she took this run. You didn't waste good tea on a job you expected to survive easily.

"My sister hated flying," Sefa said, handing cups around. "Amara. She got motion-sick on the orbital trams. Used to say she'd never leave Halcyon." Her voice did something careful in the middle of the sentence, the way voices do when they've practiced a sentence many times. "She left anyway. Five years ago. On a ship with corporate markings."

Nobody had a good answer to that, so nobody tried. Okonkwo drank his tea. Ines put her free hand over the drive case, briefly, like touching a shoulder.

It was at hour seven that the universe stopped being polite.

The strike came without sound, because space is stingy with its drama. One moment the *Rest* hummed along; the next, a warning board lit up amber across its width, and Okonkwo felt the deck shudder through his boots — a hard, ugly knock, like God rapping on the hull asking if anyone was home.

"Micrometeorite," he said, already unbuckling. "Aft section. Sefa, seal the bulkhead behind me. Dez, get on the damage console. Ines—"

"I'm coming with you."

"You're staying with the drive."

"It can come too."

He almost argued. Then he looked at her face — five years of invisibility burning off her all at once — and decided some arguments weren't worth the oxygen. Which was about to become a literal concern.

The aft compartment told the story plainly. A grain-of-sand intruder moving at eleven kilometers per second had punched through the outer plating, through an interior panel, and into the secondary scrubber assembly, where it had died messily and taken the CO₂ exchange manifold with it. Frost bloomed along the ruptured lines. Somewhere behind the panels, the backup unit clicked and whirred and did not start.

"How bad?" Ines asked.

Okonkwo knelt by the wreckage and did the arithmetic he'd hoped never to do again. "Primary scrubber's gone. Backup won't cycle — the strike cooked its controller. I can hand-splice the primary, maybe. Eight hours' work, if the parts are there." He patted the panel beside him. "We have air for four people for about twenty hours. If we're very quiet."

"And if you splice it?"

"Then we breathe indefinitely and I take up smoking again out of spite."

Dez's voice came over the intercom, tight: "The enforcer ships just moved. Closing to intercept range. They know."

Of course they knew. A distress flare, a drifting ship, a slow suffocation — the corporation wouldn't even need to fire a shot. They could write the incident report from a warm office. *Vessel lost to debris impact in unmonitored space. No survivors recovered. Condolences issued.*

"They're hailing us," Dez added. "Audio only. Want me to—"

"No," said Ines and Okonkwo, together.

* * *

They gathered in the galley, because it was the smallest room with seats, and small rooms make honest math easier. Okonkwo laid it out the way he'd once laid it out for a panicking junior crew during the Meridian incident, back when he still believed problems came with solutions attached.

"Option one: I stay back there and splice. Takes eight hours. We burn air faster with me working — exertion, you understand — so call it sixteen hours of margin instead of twenty. If I succeed, we live. If I fail, I've spent our cushion for nothing."

"Option two?" Sefa asked.

"Somebody sleeps. Cold-sleep isn't real, whatever the entertainment feeds tell you, but metabolic suppression is. Med bay has two stasis wraps, old ones, from my mining days. Put a person under, their body burns a fifth the oxygen. Two people under, we stretch twenty hours to nearly forty. That gives me the time to work properly."

"Two people under means two people breathing for four," Dez said. "That's you and—"

"It's me and whoever I choose," Okonkwo said, "and I've already chosen. Me and Ines."

Ines looked up sharply. "No."

"Yes."

"I'm the one they want. If the enforcers board, I'm the one who needs to be awake to—"

"To what? Negotiate?" He kept his voice gentle, which took effort, because gentleness always did. "Child, I've been flying longer than you've been alive. If they board this ship, nothing you say will matter, and everything you carry will be in that case either way. But if that splice fails because I'm unconscious, everyone dies, and I will not spend my last hours asleep while other people do my work." He tapped the table. "Besides. I'm seventy. My body uses less air out of sheer laziness. It's efficient."

"That's not how biology works," Dez said.

"It's how *my* biology works."

Ines was shaking her head, and Okonkwo watched her do it and understood, finally, what the whole voyage had been about — not the drive, not the relay, not even justice. She had spent five years making sure no one else paid for her mistakes. And here he was, volunteering to pay.

"Sefa," she said. "You should take my wrap. Your sister—"

"My sister would slap me," Sefa said flatly. "She'd wait until I woke up, thank you kindly, and then she'd slap me. I'm staying awake. I'm the youngest. I use the least air already — ask Dez, she snores like a cargo loader."

"I do not snore," said Dez. "I breathe *expressively*."

And then Ines did the thing Okonkwo would think about for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be. She unlatched the drive case, set it on the galley table between them, and rested both hands on it.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm setting terms. If the splice fails — if we're truly out of margin — we open this case first. All of them. Three thousand minds, and the archive runs cooler than any of us. We give the air to the dead before we let anyone suffocate. They waited five years. They can wait a little longer, and they deserve the room more than we do."

Silence in the little galley. Dez looked at the case. Sefa looked at Ines.

"That's grim," Dez said at last, "but it's also weirdly beautiful, and I hate that I think so."

"The dead aren't cargo," Ines said. "I keep telling myself that until it stops sounding like a speech."

Okonkwo nodded slowly. "Terms accepted. Now everybody stop talking — talking uses air — and let an old man go fix your lungs."

* * *

Eight hours is a long time to lie inside a stasis wrap. Ines learned this the way she seemed to learn everything: completely, silently, and at cost.

From the cockpit, Okonkwo could hear the med bay monitor ticking softly, tracking her vitals as they slowed — heart rate dropping, breath thinning to almost nothing. Sefa sat with her until she went under, holding her hand, and reported afterward that Ines's last words before sleep were, "Tell Tomas I'm sorry about the tea," which made no sense to anyone except possibly Ines.

Okonkwo worked. His hands remembered the splice even when the rest of him complained — knees first, then back, then a shoulder that had been predicting rain since the Meridian incident. The manifold was worse than he'd hoped; the strike had sheared two junctions clean off, and he ended up fabricating a replacement collar from a coolant fitting and, when that proved stubborn, a piece of the galley's water heater that Dez sacrificed with genuine mourning.

At hour five, the enforcer ships closed to ten kilometers and held there, close enough now that he could see their running lights through the aft viewport. Watching. Waiting for the *Magpie's Rest* to become a tomb they wouldn't even have to explain.

At hour seven, sweating, hands bleeding in two places, Okonkwo sealed the final coupling and keyed the restart.

The scrubber cycled. Once. Twice. Found its rhythm.

"Air's flowing," he announced over the intercom, and heard Dez whoop, and heard Sefa laugh, and heard nothing at all from the med bay except the soft tick of a sleeping woman's heart doing its slow, patient work.

He allowed himself exactly one minute of sitting on the deck floor, bleeding and triumphant, before hauling himself back to the cockpit.

Kestrel hung ahead of them now, visible on the scope at last — a splinter of metal at the edge of the dark, dark as a closed eye. Behind them, the enforcer ships hesitated. Recalculating. Their quarry wasn't dying on schedule.

Then one of them broke formation and began to burn hard for the relay itself.

Okonkwo's stomach dropped through thirty years of retirement. They weren't trying to kill the messenger anymore.

They were going to kill the message.

"Wake her up," he said. "Wake her up now. Whatever's left of our margin — spend it. We're landing in eleven minutes, and I don't think we're going to be alone."
