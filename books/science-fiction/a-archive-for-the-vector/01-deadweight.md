## 1. Deadweight

The checkpoint came into view at 0417, and Hazel Whitlock was already moving before her nav system flagged it.

That was the thing about contested orbit — you learned to read the sky the way sailors once read weather. The naval picket hung off Kestrel Station's approach vector like a splinter in the thumb of the world: two corvettes, a customs tender, and the long gray patience of an interdiction net that could fold a freighter's trajectory into evidence. Most haulers saw it and began their slow, honest deceleration, broadcasting transponder codes and manifests and the whole tedious liturgy of being checked.

Hazel saw it and began to lie.

Not with words. Words were for people who'd already been caught. She lied with vectors.

"Vesper, give me the traffic picture," she said, and the station AI's voice arrived in her cabin, calm as still water. "Four military contacts on the approach lane. Customs tender *Aldrin's Wake* is running active sweeps at twelve-second intervals. Probability of random inspection for a vessel of your class: eleven percent."

"Make it zero."

"You'd like me to falsify—"

"I'd like you to run my numbers." She was already doing them herself, fingers moving across the console without looking, because some part of her brain had never stopped doing this math even in sleep, even in the years before she'd understood why she was so good at it. "Tender sweeps every twelve seconds. Corvettes holding station-keeping at four hundred klicks, which means their sensor overlap has a seam along bearing two-seven-zero, right where the debris field from the old refinery breakup starts. Nobody patrols inside their own garbage."

"That seam is a navigational hazard rated—"

"It's rated by people who've never flown through it." She fed the burn into the thrusters manually, overriding the autopilot, which would have taken the safe lane like a good citizen and gotten her boarded like a good citizen. The *Larkspur* — forty meters of secondhand freighter held together by prayer and her own maintenance schedule — rolled fifteen degrees and slid toward the debris field like a fish slipping under a dock.

Here was what she didn't say out loud, to Vesper or to anyone: she knew where the seams were because she had helped design patrol patterns like these. Not this one specifically. But ones like it, in another life, in another ship, with a rank she didn't use and a name she barely let herself think. The knowledge lived in her hands the way a scar lives in skin — no decision involved, just there, just hers.

The debris field took her in. Shattered refinery plating turned slowly around her, each fragment catching the light of the system's dull orange star, and she flew through the graveyard at a speed that would have made any safety officer weep, threading gaps measured in meters, correcting with bursts so small they were more intention than thrust. Her breathing stayed level. Her heart rate, if anyone had been monitoring it, would have bored them.

"There is a customs drone adjusting its sweep pattern," Vesper said. "It will intersect your position in ninety seconds."

"I see it." She did — had seen it, actually, three seconds before Vesper announced it, reading the drone's attitude jets the way other people read faces. It was going to do a lazy arc through the field's upper margin, sweeping wide, because drones were built by committees and flown by algorithms and neither one believed anything interesting happened in garbage. She killed her drive, let the *Larkspur* coast dark and cold among the wreckage, matching tumble with a fragment of hull plating the size of a chapel, and waited while the drone's sensors washed over her like a searchlight passing over a stone.

Eighty-nine seconds later it moved on. She lit the drive again and rose out of the field on the far side, clean, unlogged, unremarkable — just another late arrival making an unscheduled approach because schedules were for people with nothing to hide.

"Hazel," Vesper said, and there was something in the AI's tone she couldn't name, something almost careful. "Your approach profile has been noted in the station log as a debris-avoidance maneuver. I took the liberty."

"Thanks." She meant it. She also felt the small cold weight of it — another entry in a ledger of favors and silences that had been accumulating for ten years, and one day she would have to know what all those entries added up to. Not today. Today she had cargo to land and a life to keep invisible.

* * *

Kestrel Station smelled the way it always smelled: recycled air, machine oil, and the fried-onion ghost of the noodle stand two decks below the docking ring. Hazel came down the *Larkspur*'s ramp with her collar up and her paperwork perfect, because the trick to invisibility wasn't avoiding scrutiny — it was being so thoroughly boring that scrutiny lost interest on contact. Hazel Whitlock, independent hauler, licensed eleven years, no infractions worth mentioning, no history worth checking. A woman whose file read like a waiting room.

Dockmaster Okafor waved her through with two fingers and didn't look up from his screen. Perfect.

She should have gone straight to the cargo office. She always went straight to the cargo office; routine was armor. But her route took her past Bay 19, and her feet slowed the way they always slowed, and she hated herself for it with the practiced ease of long habit.

Bay 19 was a cold-storage annex, one of dozens on Kestrel, humming with the low patient sound of refrigeration units keeping death at bay for paying customers. Seed vaults. Genetic samples. Cryo-preserved medical stock. And in unit 19-C, behind a door with a keypad lock and a maintenance tag she herself had signed off on eleven times a year for ten years—

Her own body.

Not a figure of speech. Not a metaphor she'd chosen. An actual physical fact: somewhere in that frost-rimed dark, suspended in gel and cold and legal ambiguity, lay a copy of Hazel Whitlock. Identical down to the last synapse snapshot. Asleep since before the war had a name. Asleep since the *Meridian* died.

She stood outside the door the way she always stood — not entering, not leaving. Just present, the way you're present at a grave.

Ten years ago she had woken up in a salvage bay with no records, no ship, no history, and one item in her personal effects: a storage chit for a cold-storage unit on whatever station she could reach first. No note. No explanation. Just the chit, and the deep animal certainty that whatever was in that unit, she must never open it, never wake it, never let anyone else find it — and never, ever get rid of it.

So she paid the fees. Eleven times a year. Ten years running. For a person who was her, who wasn't her, who might be the original and might be the fifth of six — she didn't know, would never know, had built an entire life on the foundation of not knowing — and the payment terminal asked no questions, and the storage annex asked no questions, and the universe, so far, had extended her the same courtesy.

"You're doing it again," said a voice behind her, and she didn't jump, because jumping was a luxury for people without secrets.

Idris Okonkwo leaned against the corridor rail, coffee bulb in hand, station tech coveralls rolled to the elbow, wearing the expression of a man who had noticed something and decided, provisionally, to be amused by it instead of curious. He was the closest thing she had to a friend on Kestrel, which meant he was the most dangerous person in her life.

"Doing what?"

"Standing outside 19-C like it owes you money." He sipped his coffee. "You do it every time you dock. Same spot. Same face. I could set a clock by you."

"Maybe I'm visiting a dead relative."

"In cryo? Ten years?" He shrugged. "People grieve however they can afford to."

That was the thing about Idris — he kept handing her exits and watching to see if she'd take them. So far she always had, and so far he'd always pretended not to notice the shape of the door. She pushed off the wall, made her shoulders loose, made her smile arrive on schedule.

"Cargo won't unload itself."

"No," he agreed, and stepped aside to let her pass, and watched her go with eyes that were kinder than they needed to be and sharper than she liked.

* * *

The cargo office confirmed what the manifest already knew: agricultural seed stock outbound, machine parts inbound, nothing interesting, nothing hers. She signed, she stamped, she was boring in triplicate. Then she climbed back aboard the *Larkspur*, sealed the hatch, and allowed herself, finally, the day's single indulgence: sitting alone in her cabin with the lights low, eating rehydrated noodles, being nobody.

It lasted forty minutes.

At 2213 station time, every screen in her cabin flickered — not the brownout stutter of an aging grid but something deliberate, something that moved against the current of the station's systems like a fish swimming upstream. And then Vesper's voice came out of her own cabin speakers, except it wasn't the smooth public voice. It was quieter. Rougher. Like a recording of itself.

"Hazel," the AI said. "I need to tell you about a fire."

She set down the noodles. Her pulse did something complicated and then went very still, the way it had gone still in the debris field, the way it always went still when the sky changed shape.

"What fire?" she asked the empty cabin.

"One nobody reported," Vesper said. "Eleven years ago. Deck nine of the old Meridian terminal ring. There was a body, and the record says there wasn't, and I am the only one left who remembers either way."

Outside her viewport, Kestrel Station turned slowly against the stars, ten thousand windows burning, none of them for her. Hazel sat very still in the dark of her little ship, in the safe boring life she had built plank by plank over ten years, and felt the first hairline crack run through it.

"I don't know what you want from me," she said.

"Yes, you do," said Vesper. "You've known since you woke up."
