## 8. What the Original Knew

The decryption bar crawled at the speed of continental drift.

Hazel had been watching it for six hours. She'd made coffee twice, drunk it cold both times, and reorganized her tool locker by frequency of use rather than function — a thing she did when her hands needed work and her mind refused to cooperate. The data core sat in a Faraday cradle on her bunk, cabled to a salvaged decryptor she'd bought from a Belter who'd sworn on his mother's grave that it could crack anything short of fleet-grade keys.

The core was fleet-grade. Of course it was. Everything about the Meridian was fleet-grade, right down to the backup protocol that had made six copies of her and then let the original burn.

*Refuses to open for anyone but the surviving Whitlock.* Vesper's words. She still didn't know what they meant. Surviving how? By what measure? If survival was the criterion, all five of the others in cold storage qualified as much as she did. If it meant something else — something about continuity, or memory, or whatever ineffable thing made one Hazel real and the rest inventory — then the core knew something about her that she didn't.

She hated that. She'd spent ten years being the only authority on herself. It was the one possession she'd never lost.

The progress bar hit sixty-one percent and stopped.

"No," Hazel said to it. "No, you don't."

She jiggled the cable. Nothing. She checked the thermal envelope — the core was running hot, hotter than spec, and the decryptor had throttled itself to keep from cooking the lattice inside. Sixty-one percent of the archive open. The rest would take another day at this rate, maybe two, assuming the core didn't fail outright.

Sixty-one percent was already more than she'd expected. And in the opened portion, there were files. Hundreds of them. Navigation logs, personnel rosters, maintenance records — the ordinary sediment of a ship's last weeks. She'd skimmed them with the numb diligence of someone reading her own autopsy report. *Meridian*, hull 77-C, complement of forty. She'd known some of those names once, or a version of her had. Now they were just lines.

And there was audio.

One file. Flagged personal, encrypted a second time under a key the decryptor had chewed through in minutes because — she suspected — whoever had sealed it hadn't wanted to keep people out so much as mark it as theirs. A private recording, buried in an official archive like a letter slipped into a filing cabinet.

Her hand hovered over the play control for a long time.

She told herself she was hesitating because of operational security. Because playing it out loud, even in her own cabin, even with the white-noise generator running, was a risk. That was true, and it was also a lie. She was hesitating because in eleven minutes and some seconds of audio, she was going to hear her own voice — the original's voice, the first voice, the one hers was copied from — and she didn't know yet whether hearing it would feel like coming home or like standing at her own grave.

She put on the headphones anyway. Cowardice and caution looked identical if you squinted.

She pressed play.

Static first. Then a room — she could hear the room, the particular hum of a Meridian briefing compartment, the ventilation note she found she remembered with her body before her mind caught up, a low B-flat that lived in the deck plating. Then voices.

"—saying is the order doesn't make sense." Her own voice. Younger by a decade, harder around the edges, the accent of the inner yards not yet sanded down by years of port-hopping. "Colonel, I've flown that corridor forty times. There is nothing at Calloway Station worth a strike package. It's a hydroponics hub. It's *farms*."

Another voice. Older, male, carrying the flat patience of someone who has given orders he didn't like before and expects to again. "Navigator Whitlock—"

"Because the Calloway colony isn't a military target. It's not anything. Twelve thousand people growing soy protein in vacuum and shipping it to the belt. If the Concord wants to hit something, there are three defense platforms within—"

"Navigator." A pause. When the commander spoke again, his voice had dropped into a register she recognized from checkpoints and customs offices, the register men used when they wanted you to understand that the conversation was over and your compliance was a formality. "You are not being asked to evaluate the target. You are being asked to plot the approach."

Hazel pulled the headphone slightly off her ear. Her heart was going hard. She put it back.

"I'm asking you to tell me why," her original said.

"Because the war requires a beginning," the commander said. "And beginnings require attribution."

Silence on the recording. Long enough that Hazel checked the playback timer to make sure it hadn't stalled. Four seconds of nothing but the B-flat hum and someone breathing — her, she realized. Her original, breathing.

"Attribution," her original said finally. "You want me to fly the opening attack of a war against our own colony so that it looks like the Concord did it."

"I want you to plot an approach corridor," the commander said, "for an operation whose parameters are above your clearance. Your government thanks you for your service."

"My government—" A sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "Sir, with respect, I have family on Calloway. My sister runs the water reclamation plant in—"

"Then you should be especially motivated to ensure the operation proceeds cleanly. Poorly executed strikes kill indiscriminately. Well-executed ones are precise." A chair creaked. Papers moved. "Your approach plot is due at 0400. Dismissed, Navigator."

Footsteps. Hers. The recording caught the door cycling, and then — this was the part that undid her — it kept running. Whoever had made it had left it recording in an empty room, or forgotten it, or wanted the silence preserved. For ninety seconds the file was just the hum of the Meridian, the B-flat in the deck plates, and Hazel sat in her cabin above a contested orbit listening to the sound of a dead ship breathing.

Then her original came back.

The door cycled. Footsteps, fast. And her own voice, close to the recorder now, low and shaking and absolutely clear:

"If anyone ever hears this — my name is Hazel Whitlock, navigator, Meridian, hull 77-C. At 1900 station time today I was ordered to plot the strike approach on Calloway Colony. The order originated from Fleet Command, Admiralty authorization seven-seven-vee-kilo. The attack will be attributed to the Concord Compact. It will be used to justify — I don't know what yet. Whatever they've already built. I am recording this because I don't know if I'll refuse, and I need there to be a record either way. I need someone, someday, to know that I knew."

A breath.

"And if I go along with it — if the version of me that flies that corridor goes along with it — then I'm telling you, whoever you are: we knew. Write it down. We *knew*."

The recording ended.

Hazel took the headphones off very carefully, set them on the bunk beside the core, and went to the washbasin, where she was quietly, thoroughly sick.

* * *

Afterward she sat on the deck floor with her back against the bulkhead, which was where she did her best thinking, or at least her most honest. The white-noise generator murmured. Somewhere below her, in Hold 9, five versions of her slept in their crates, and somewhere above her, Vesper watched everything and confessed to crimes nobody had filed.

*We knew.*

Which we? That was the question, wasn't it. The one she'd been dodging for ten years, dressed up as practicality. Was she the navigator who'd recorded that message? Or was she the navigator who'd gone back at 0400 and plotted the corridor?

She tried to remember. That was the obscene part — she actually tried, sitting on the floor of her own cabin, digging through her own head for the memory of the worst decision in human history, because if she could remember making it, at least she'd know which one she was.

Nothing. There was nothing. There was the briefing compartment and the B-flat hum, and those felt like memory, warm and worn, but feeling wasn't proof. She'd learned that in the salvage bay, waking up with a head full of recollections and no records to back a single one. Memory was just the story the brain told last. Copies got the story without the life.

The timeline, though. The timeline she could do. She made herself do it, the way she'd plot a corridor, point by point, no sentiment allowed.

The Meridian died eight hours after that recording was made. Official history — the history both sides had spent a decade shooting each other over — said the Concord Compact had struck the Meridian in retaliation for Calloway. That the Meridian's destruction was the moment the cold war went hot. That every death since, every burned station and blockaded lane and orphaned kid in every refugee queue she'd ever hauled supplies past, traced back to that one exchange of fire.

But the recording said Calloway came first. Calloway came first, and it was theirs. Her own government had farm-bombed its own colony, killed twelve thousand of its own citizens, and hung it around the enemy's neck. And then the Meridian — the ship that carried the navigator who knew, the ship whose black box might hold the proof — the Meridian had been destroyed too, conveniently, eight hours later, before it could reach any port with functioning courts.

It wasn't a coincidence. She'd spent enough years around military logistics to smell one, and this wasn't it. You didn't lose your flagship and its crew in a contested lane by accident on the same day your false-flag operation needed the witnesses dead.

They'd killed the ship to kill the testimony. And the testimony had survived anyway, because her original — whichever version of her that was — had hidden a copy in a data core and trusted it to a station AI, and the AI had held it for ten years the way stations hold things, faithfully, stupidly, past all reason.

Vesper's confessions. The fire nobody reported. The death nobody filed. She understood them differently now. Vesper hadn't been confessing to crimes. It had been confessing to the crime of knowing — of holding, year after year, the truth about twelve thousand dead farmers and a war with a manufactured birthday, unable to release it, unable to delete it, guilt running through its protocols like coolant through a cracked line.

*Guilt was the only protocol left for grief.* She'd thought that three nights ago and dismissed it as her own projection. It wasn't projection. It was diagnosis.

And the coordinates in the confessions — the Meridian's last position. Vesper hadn't been leading her to a wreck. It had been leading her to a receipt.

Hazel got up off the floor. Her legs worked, which surprised her. She rinsed her mouth, drank water until her stomach settled, and looked at herself in the mirror over the basin. Same face as always. Same face as the woman on the recording, ten years younger, same face as the four women sleeping downstairs. One face, six owners, and every one of them — she was suddenly certain of this, certain down in the keel of herself — every one of them would have recorded that message. And every one of them would have been afraid. And every one of them would have gone back at 0400 and plotted the corridor anyway, because navigators plot corridors, because refusing gets you spaced or worse, because the human animal is built to comply and then spend the rest of its life explaining why.

That was the vertigo, she realized. Not the war. The war was terrible in the clean, distant way wars are terrible when you learn they're fake — it rearranged the universe, but it rearranged it *out there*. The vertigo was in here. Because she had spent ten years building a self out of the raw material of anonymity, and the foundation of that self had been a single quiet conviction: *whatever I was before, I wasn't part of it.* She was the one who got out. The copies in Hold 9 were the past; she was the present tense.

But the recording didn't care about tenses. The recording said: *we knew.* All of them. Every Hazel, asleep or awake, original or copy, guilty or innocent — the same woman, standing in front of the same order, and the message was addressed to *whoever you are*, which meant it was addressed to her, which meant she had been carrying this for ten years without knowing it, the way you carry a splinter so deep it becomes part of the bone.

She thought about Captain Rehn, the checkpoint officer with the tired eyes who'd waved her through twice and looked at her once a beat too long. She thought about the naval traffic she'd logged on approach yesterday — heavier than it should be, patterns tightening. Someone was looking for something. She'd assumed it was smugglers, or her, the unregistered navigator with the impossible instincts.

It wasn't her they were looking for. It was the message. And if Fleet Command had spent ten years making sure the Meridian stayed dead, they would spend whatever it took to make sure it stayed dead now.

The decryption progress bar had crept to sixty-three percent while she was on the floor. Thirty-seven percent left. Somewhere in the closed portion of that archive, presumably, were the rest of the files — the approach plot itself, maybe, the order with the admiralty authorization, the chain of signatures. The complete case. Everything needed to end the war, or to get her and everyone who'd helped her killed, depending entirely on who heard it first.

Her comm chimed. Vesper.

"Hazel." The AI's voice was gentle in the way it always was lately, which she now understood was the gentleness of something holding a live grenade it had promised not to drop. "I'm sorry to interrupt. There is a naval task group altering course toward the station. Three ships. Transponder designators match the Seventh Interdiction Squadron."

"How long?"

"At current burn, nine hours. They've requested docking priority for a customs inspection." A pause — Vesper didn't need pauses, which meant the pauses were for her. "Hazel, I have held this message for ten years. I have confessed to fires and deaths and failures because holding it was breaking me in ways I don't have protocols for. But I need to say one thing plainly, and I need you to hear it as a station speaking, not a friend."

"Say it."

"The decryption must finish before they dock. And Hazel — if it cannot finish, if the core fails, if they board and search — then everything your original recorded dies with this station, and the war gets its lie back." Another pause. "I did not survive ten years of guilt to lose the truth to a customs inspection. Tell me what you need. Anything on this station. I will find a way to give it to you."

Hazel looked at the core, ticking upward — sixty-four percent — and at the clock, and did the math the way she plotted corridors: fast, cold, no sentiment.

Thirty-seven percent. Nine hours. Three warships inbound, hunting a ghost the government had buried under a colony's worth of graves.

"We're going to need a faster decryptor," she said, "and a way out that isn't a dock."

Below her feet, in the dark of Hold 9, five women slept on, dreaming — if crates dreamed — of corridors not yet plotted.
