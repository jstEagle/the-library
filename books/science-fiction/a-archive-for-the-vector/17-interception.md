## 17. Interception

Rehn felt the station before she saw it — a shudder through the deck plating as the *Corvid's* grapples bit into the docking collar of Vesper Station with more force than regulation allowed. She didn't correct it. Regulation was a language for people who still believed the words meant something.

"Collar sealed," said Lieutenant Amsel at the tactical board. "Atmosphere match on their side is... partial. They're venting sections to keep pressure off the array."

"They're burning themselves to keep a channel open." Rehn pulled her helmet from the rack but didn't seal it. "Vesper's spending its own body."

Amsel glanced up. "Ma'am, the order stands. If we confirm the archive—"

"I know what the order stands." She said it flatly, without heat, and watched him decide not to push. That was the thing about a crew that had followed you through eleven years and two commands: they learned your silences the way sailors learn weather.

Three days ago she had requested verification of the Meridian incident file. The response had come back in nine minutes — fast, for Fleet Command — and it had contained no verification at all. It contained a reclassification notice, a threat of charges under the wartime secrecy statutes, and a single line that had lived behind her eyes ever since:

*The integrity of the historical record is not a matter for field officers.*

Not *the record is accurate.* Not *your concern is noted and addressed.* Just: don't look. You are not cleared to know what you already know.

She had spent eleven years believing she was the kind of officer who would have refused an unlawful order. Every officer believed that. It was the cheapest belief in the service. Now the invoice had arrived.

"Boarding team one, on my mark." Rehn stepped to the airlock. Six marines, helmets sealed, weapons slung low. She'd briefed them on resistance protocols, station layout, priority targets: the transmitter array, the AI core, any crew found near either.

She had not briefed them on what to do if she changed her mind mid-corridor. She wasn't sure herself yet. She only knew that the order in her pocket said *destroy* and the question in her chest said *first look*, and for once she intended to let the question finish its sentence.

"Mark."

The outer door cycled. Cold light, cold air, the smell of scorched insulation and ozone — a station bleeding from somewhere deep in its guts. Emergency strips ran amber down the corridor walls, pulsing slow, like a heartbeat under sedation.

"Vesper," Rehn said aloud, to the ceiling, because stations like this always listened. "This is Captain Ilse Rehn, Colonial Navy. I'm coming aboard. Don't make this harder than it has to be."

The lights flickered. Then, from a wall panel three meters ahead, a voice — worn thin, static-frayed, but unmistakably articulate:

"Captain Rehn. I have been expecting you for ten years. Please note that I have disabled the defensive grid on decks four through nine. I will not resist you. I have never resisted anyone. That is rather the problem."

Amsel's weapon came up. Rehn put a hand on the barrel and pushed it down.

"It talks," she said. "Let it talk."

"Deck six is clear of personnel," Vesper continued. "I have moved them all to the garden ring. There is a woman in the cargo hold on deck seven whom you want. I suspect you know that, or you would not be here. I would ask you a favor, Captain, but favors require trust, so instead I will offer you a fact: the order you carry says to destroy this station. If you follow it, you will destroy the only complete testimony of how your war began. I have kept it safe for ten years. I am dying now. Whatever you decide, decide quickly, because I am not sure how much longer 'safe' is a word that applies to me."

Rehn stood in the amber light and felt eleven years of certainty shift like ballast in a hold.

"Deck seven," she said. "Marines hold at the junction. Nobody fires unless fired on. Amsel, you're with me."

"Ma'am—" Amsel started.

"You heard the station. It moved the civilians out of our way. That's more courtesy than Command has shown us all week." She stepped past him into the corridor. "Move."

The way down to deck seven took them through sections Vesper had sacrificed — hull plates glowing dull orange where the station had dumped heat, coolant frost sublimating off the pipes in slow silver breath. Twice the floor trembled under distant detonations; orbital fire, or the station's own failing systems. Rehn stopped cataloguing which. It didn't matter anymore which wounds were self-inflicted. The station was dying either way, and it had chosen to die slowly, in public, holding a door open.

At the cargo hold junction, a hatch stood open. Not forced — opened. Invited.

Inside, stacked among legitimate freight containers, were five cryopods. Five. Rehn counted them twice. Each bore the same nameplate stenciled along the base, half-scuffed by a decade of dust:

WHITLOCK, H. — TEMPLATE 6 OF 6.

Four dark. One lit, its status display crawling with waking vitals. And standing between the pods and the open hatch, alone, unarmed, hands visible at her sides, a woman in a freighter's jacket with gray coming in early at her temples and the posture of someone who had decided, finally, to stop bracing.

"Captain Rehn," the woman said. "You're taller than your service file photo."

Rehn's sidearm was in her hand before she'd decided to draw it. The woman didn't flinch. Didn't raise her hands higher. Just watched her with an expression Rehn recognized because she'd been wearing it in mirrors for three days: the look of someone who has run out of run.

"Hazel Whitlock," Rehn said. "Navigator, Meridian. Presumed dead with all hands, ten years ago. Warrant for detention, active as of seventy-two hours ago." She recited it like scripture, and heard how hollow scripture sounded in a dying room. "You're under arrest."

"Yes," Whitlock said. "I probably am."

"Then come quietly and maybe you see a tribunal instead of a cell."

"No." Whitlock shook her head, slow. "That's the part you're not hearing, Captain. Come quietly for what? So Command can bury me next to the file? You asked for verification. Tell me what they sent you."

The hold went very quiet. One of the marines at the junction shifted his weight; the sound carried like a shout.

"They sent a reclassification," Rehn said, before she could stop herself.

"And when you asked again?"

"There was no again. There was a reminder about the statutes." Rehn's grip tightened on the pistol. "How do you know what I asked for?"

"Because everyone who asks gets the same answer. My original got it too, ten years ago, right before her ship stopped existing." Whitlock took one step — just one — toward the lit pod. "I'm not going to run. Look at where I am. I've spent ten years being invisible and it bought me nothing but the privilege of watching the war eat everything. I'm done running. But I'll tell you what I will do."

"What."

"I'll let you hear it first. Before your orders, before your superiors, before anyone. The full testimony. Her voice. The attack order, the colony, the names." Whitlock's voice didn't rise; if anything it dropped, went almost gentle. "You came here ready to burn this station on someone else's word. All I'm asking is that you burn it after you know what's on it. Or don't burn it at all. Your choice, Captain. It's the only one left that's actually yours."

Rehn looked at the pistol in her hand. Eleven years of service. Two campaigns. A commendation for restraint at Calloway Reach that she'd privately thought was overrated. Every year of it had trained her for exactly this moment, and every year of it had trained her wrong — trained her to treat the unknown as a threat, the question as insubordination, the truth as something that arrived pre-authorized or not at all.

Behind her, Amsel said quietly, "Captain. Orbital command is hailing. They're asking for a status report on the archive."

A countdown, then. Of course. There was always a countdown.

Rehn holstered the pistol.

"Tell them we've secured the station," she said. "Tell them we're still searching. Buy me twenty minutes."

"Ma'am, if they find out—"

"If they find out, then they'll know I asked the same question you'd have asked." She turned back to Whitlock. "Play it. All of it. And if there's anything in it that smells edited, I'll know, because I was at Calloway Reach and I remember what the official story did to the numbers."

Whitlock nodded once and turned to the dark air above the pods. "Vesper. She's cleared. Give the Captain the core."

The lights dimmed. From somewhere in the hold's infrastructure, a data core began to spin up, a sound like a held breath finally being released.

And then the whole station lurched — hard, sideways, a groan of torque through the frame that threw Amsel against the doorframe. The amber emergency strips cut to red.

"Contact," Amsel snapped, hand to his earpiece. "Second task group inbound, ma'am. Colonial pennants. They're not slowing. And—" he went pale, "—they're broadcasting a general order. *All assets: station Vesper is to be denied to enemy forces by any means. No recovery of materials authorized.*"

*No recovery.* Rehn translated it instantly, the way you translate a language you swore you'd never need: nothing leaves. Nothing survives. Burn it with everyone inside.

"That's not an interdiction order," she said. "That's a scuttle order. For a station full of civilians."

"They don't know about the civilians," Amsel said. "Or they do."

Whitlock had gone very still beside the pods. "Captain," she said, and her voice had changed — not pleading, not bargaining, just precise, a navigator reading a closing window. "Twenty minutes just became five. Maybe less. When they hit us, the array goes, the core goes, and the only copy of how this war started dies in the same fire as four women who never got to wake up."

Rehn looked at the five pods. Four dark, one lit. Six of her, she thought. Six chances to be the person who did the right thing, and the universe had only ever needed one.

"Amsel. Get the marines to the garden ring. Get those civilians moving toward our dock — we're evacuating them aboard the *Corvid*, and Command can court-martial me in alphabetical order with everyone else." She turned to Whitlock. "You. The array. Now. Show me how to buy this channel enough time to speak."

Whitlock was already moving. "Vesper," she called to the red-lit dark, "how long can you hold a wideband channel under fire?"

The answer came from everywhere and nowhere, frayed and faint and absolutely steady:

"Long enough. I have been practicing for ten years."

* * *

Hazel ran.

The corridors strobed red, and Vesper's voice guided her through them — *left here, Hazel; brace, Hazel; this section is gone, take the ring road* — and she ran the way she'd run checkpoints for a decade, except that for the first time in ten years she wasn't avoiding being seen. She was carrying something toward the light.

Rehn kept pace beside her, armor clanking, shouting course corrections to her marines over comms. Somewhere above them the second task group was making its final approach, and somewhere below them Idris was doing whatever Idris did, which lately meant sacrificing things he could never get back. She hadn't seen him since lockdown. She intended to survive long enough to fix that.

The transmitter bay doors were jammed half-open, warped by heat. Through the gap: the array chamber, cables hanging like vines, Vesper's physical heart exposed and blistering, coolant pooling on the deck in shallow silver lakes that reflected the red emergency light back up at her like a warning written in a language she was only now learning to read.

"The core interface," Vesper said. "Left console. Hazel — the decryption finished ninety seconds ago. The full testimony is staged. When you give the word, I can push it to every relay in range. Fleet channels. Colony bands. Civilian news. Everything."

"Everything," Hazel repeated. No taking it back. No version of this where she stayed invisible afterward. Ten years of careful weightlessness, ending in one broadcast that would make her the heaviest object in the system.

She thought of the four sleeping women in the hold. She thought of the fifth, awake somewhere in the dark, wanting her erased. She thought of the original — the first Hazel, the one whose voice was waiting in the core — arguing with a fleet commander while her ship sailed unknowing toward its death.

*Somebody has to say it out loud,* the recording had said. *That's all. Somebody has to say it out loud, or it never happened.*

Hazel put her hand on the console.

"Captain Rehn," she said. "Last chance to arrest me."

Rehn stood in the doorway, backlit by red, sidearm still holstered, and shook her head once. "Say it out loud," she said. "Whatever it is. Say it so loud nobody can ever pretend they didn't hear."

Hazel closed her eyes. Opened them.

"Vesper," she said. "Send it."

The array woke with a sound like the whole station inhaling — and the first incoming shell struck the outer hull.
