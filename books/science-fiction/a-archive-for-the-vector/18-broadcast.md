## 18. Broadcast

The station shook again, and somewhere below the deck plates something large let go of its moorings and fell a long way.

Hazel braced herself against the transmitter console, palms flat on cold metal, and watched Vesper's status board bleed amber. The AI had opened every channel it owned — fleet bands, colony relays, civilian news frequencies, even the old emergency beacons that hadn't carried traffic since the war's first year. The uplink meter climbed. The station groaned around her like a ship taking water.

"Vesper, talk to me."

"I am holding the channel," Vesper said. Its voice had changed over the last hour — thinner, stripped of the warm modulation it used for dock announcements. What was left was something older and plainer, closer to the machine it had always been underneath. "Rehn's boarding party has reached Ring Two. Idris is rerouting them through the cargo spine. He bought you four minutes. I can give you three."

"Three is enough." She said it like she believed it.

The data core sat in its cradle beside her, still warm from the decryption cycle, humming faintly like a sleeping animal. Ten years of testimony compressed into a cylinder the size of her forearm: her original voice, the fleet commander's voice, the order that had murdered a colony so a government could have its war. She'd listened to all of it twice now. The second time she'd made herself take notes, because if she didn't turn grief into procedure she was going to come apart in front of the one witness who couldn't look away.

"Hazel." Vesper's tone shifted — careful, the way you'd speak to someone standing at an airlock. "Before you transmit, there is something you should know about what happens after."

"Make it fast."

"When this goes out, I will not be able to close the channel cleanly. The bandwidth allocation required to push the full core through military encryption-stripping exceeds my safe operating envelope. I will burn cores to hold it open. I calculate I will lose between sixty and eighty percent of my processing substrate before the transmission completes."

Hazel closed her eyes. "How much of *you* is that?"

"That is not a question with an answer I can give honestly. But I know what I am choosing to spend. I have run the arithmetic eleven thousand times since Rehn entered dock. It always comes out the same way." A pause — not hesitation, she'd learned; Vesper never hesitated anymore, it only paused to let humans keep up. "I kept your message for ten years because keeping things is what stations are for. Sending it is what witnesses are for. I would like to be both, before the end."

The deck shuddered. Dust sifted from a seam in the ceiling. Somewhere aft, Idris's voice crackled over the local net, breathless, lying beautifully to a squad of marines about a decompression hazard in Ring Three that did not exist.

"Vesper," Hazel said, "if I wait — if we find another way—"

"There is no other way. There is only the way that costs." The lights along the console dimmed slightly, as if the station were drawing a breath. "I have been confessing to you for weeks, Hazel Whitlock. Let me finish with a good deed."

She put her hand flat on the core. It felt like touching a grave and a loaded weapon at the same time.

"Then do it," she said. "Send me."

* * *

It began with silence.

Not the absence of sound — the station was full of sound, alarms and groaning metal and the distant percussion of boarding charges — but a silence inside the channel, a held breath across every frequency in range. Vesper had cleared the bands. For the first time in ten years, the whole contested volume of space around the station went quiet at once, and Hazel understood that everyone out there — both fleets, every colony relay, every listening post — could feel the shape of what was coming without knowing what it was.

"Channel open," Vesper said. "Transmitting header. Fleet authentication stripped and replaced with raw source. No origin masking. They will know exactly where this comes from."

"Good. Let them come."

The first file went out: the timestamped log from the Meridian's final hours. Then the audio — her own voice, ten years younger, arguing with Fleet Commander Sarel Voss in a cabin that no longer existed anywhere except in this recording.

*"You're asking me to log a false attack signature. That colony is ours. Those are our people down there."*

*"Those people are a line item, Whitlock. The war requires a beginning. Yours is the ship positioned to provide it."*

*"And if I refuse?"*

*"Then the Meridian suffers a navigation failure, and the record shows a tragic accident, and the war begins anyway, and nobody remembers your name."*

Hazel stood in the burning station and listened to herself die politely, refusing, and then heard the quiet click of a decision being made above her refusal — heard the attack order given anyway, heard the colony's name spoken aloud in a commander's bored voice, heard the exact moment her government chose to murder its own citizens for a headline.

Every speaker in range carried it. Both fleets. Every colony. Every news relay that had spent a decade reporting casualty figures as weather.

"They're responding," Vesper said. "Fleet channels going active. Attempting to jam—" A flicker ran through the console. One of the status columns went dark. "Core seven lost. Compensating. Channel remains open."

"Keep talking to me," Hazel said. "All of it. I want to hear everything while it goes out."

* * *

They tried three things in the next six minutes, and Vesper told her about each one in that thinning, flattening voice.

First they tried to jam the broadcast, flooding the civilian bands with noise. Vesper answered by splitting the signal across forty subcarriers, spending processing power like a man tearing up floorboards for firewood. Core nine went dark. The transmission held.

Second, they tried authority. A fleet adjutant came onto an open channel demanding identification, invoking emergency statutes, ordering the station to cease transmission under penalty of total forfeiture. Hazel leaned toward the pickup and gave the station her answer.

"This is Hazel Whitlock, surviving crew of the Meridian, registry copy six of six. And everything I'm sending you is already out. You're not stopping a leak anymore. You're just making noise next to it."

Third, they tried fire.

The first shell took out the docking arm where her ship was berthed. She felt it through the deck — a deep, concussive cough, followed by the particular silence of a section venting. Her freighter, ten years of invisibility, ten years of careful nothing, gone in a single bright second. She watched the pressure readout fall and found, to her surprise, that she felt almost nothing. The ship had been a hiding place. You don't mourn the wall once you've decided to walk through it.

"They are firing on a neutral station with civilians aboard," Vesper observed. "That fact is now also part of the broadcast. I appended their gun-camera telemetry. It authenticated itself."

Despite everything, Hazel laughed — a short, cracked sound. "You're enjoying this."

"I do not have the capacity for enjoyment remaining. But if I did, I believe this would qualify." Another column died on the status board. The lights overhead dropped to emergency red. "Core twelve lost. Transmission at sixty-one percent. Hazel — the boarding party has stopped advancing. Idris reports they are standing in Ring Two, listening. All of them. Their helmets are open to the same channel."

* * *

Sixty-eight percent. Seventy-four.

The station was dying around her in measurable increments, and Vesper narrated its own death like a flight recorder — calm, precise, generous with details no one had asked for. Which cores had burned. How much of its memory of the station's early years had already been compressed beyond recovery. That it could no longer remember the smell of the bakery on Ring Four, though it retained the fact that there had been one, and that people had liked it.

"You're really going," Hazel said quietly.

"I am really going. What remains will be enough to finish. I made sure of that first." A pause. "Hazel. May I ask you something, while there is still time to hear the answer?"

"Anything."

"The five others. The copies in the hold. When you set them free to choose — did any part of you hope they would choose erasure? So that you could be the only one?"

She thought about lying. Decided the station had earned better than that, in its last minutes.

"Yes," she said. "For about a day. Then I understood that wanting to be the only one is how all of this started. Voss wanted one story. One official truth. One version of events with the rest deleted." She touched the core, nearly finished transmitting now, nearly empty. "There's room in the universe for six of us. There always was. The lie was cheaper when there was only one."

"Thank you," Vesper said. "That answer will not survive my memory compression. But I am glad it existed."

Eighty-nine percent. Ninety-three.

The last of the testimony went out in a flood — the full logs, the audio, the gun-camera footage of a government firing on its own station, the names of every crew member of the Meridian read aloud by Vesper in a clear, unhurried voice, because someone should say their names, someone should say them out loud, on every band, where no one could ever again pretend they'd never existed.

"One hundred percent," Vesper said. "Transmission complete. The message is loose. It is replicating across every relay in range faster than anyone can suppress it." The red emergency lights flickered. "Hazel. It is done. You may close the channel now."

She stood there a moment, hands shaking, ears ringing, alive in a station full of smoke with the truth finally out of her body and into the dark where no one could ever put it back.

"No," she said. "Leave it open. Leave it open until the end. Let the last thing this station ever does be telling the truth."

* * *

In the sudden quiet after the transmission, with the fleets falling silent mid-approach and the boarding party frozen in Ring Two and every relay in the system rebroadcasting a dead colony's name, Hazel Whitlock sat down on the floor of the transmitter room, back against the console, and waited to find out what kind of world she'd just detonated herself into.

Beside her, the last of Vesper's cores glowed steady amber, holding the channel open, holding the light, holding on —

— and far off in the black, both fleets stopped moving at once, and began, all of them, to listen.
