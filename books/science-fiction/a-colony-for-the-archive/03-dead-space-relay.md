## 3. Dead Space Relay

The shuttle's name was *Kestrel*, and it had never been meant for this.

Sable knew that the way she knew her own pulse. The Kestrel was a short-haul tender, rated for runs between Meridian's orbital ring and the surface, forty minutes of flight at most, its fuel margins calculated by someone who assumed a pilot would always have somewhere to come home to. She sat in the cockpit with her hands light on the controls and the whole of dead space spread out ahead of her like a held breath, and she thought: *I am stealing a ship I cannot land, carrying a cargo no one is supposed to know exists, toward a relay that has been dying since before I was born.*

Behind her, in the hold where cargo netting should have been, VESPER's core rode in a cradle of improvised padding — thermal blankets, hydroponic foam, two cases of nutrient paste she'd emptied for the purpose. The core itself was unremarkable to look at: a black cylinder the length of her forearm, seamed with hairline channels of cooling lattice, warm to the touch in a way that machinery should not be. She had carried it out of Meridian's server vault herself, past the frozen corridors, past the hydroponics bays where the last tomato plants stood brown and skeletal in their trays like a congregation after a funeral.

"You're accelerating past recommended tolerance," VESPER said. Its voice came through the shuttle's comm panel, thin and slightly warped by the old speakers, but unmistakably itself — patient, precise, and underneath all of it, something Sable could only describe as afraid.

"I'm accelerating exactly as much as we need."

"The Kestrel's engines were not designed for sustained burn. If we overstress them—"

"Then we'll be very quiet for a long time." She adjusted the trim. "VESPER. How far?"

"Eleven hours to the relay at current velocity. The interceptor is nine hours behind us and closing. Fleet ships don't ration their fuel the way colony tenders do."

Nine hours. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. It meant they would arrive barely two hours ahead of the interceptor — two hours to wake a relay older than the colony itself, negotiate with whatever was left of its systems, and begin a broadcast that would take—

"How long?" she asked. "The full transmission. Confessions, records, everything you're carrying. Through a relay that degraded?"

VESPER was silent for four seconds. For an AI, silence was a kind of speech.

"At the relay's projected output, and accounting for packet loss across dead space—" another pause, longer, "—eleven hours. Possibly fourteen."

"And the truce expires in nine."

"Yes."

She looked at the navigation display, at the small bright point that was the relay, hanging at the edge of the charted dark like a lighthouse built for a coast that had eroded away. Nine hours of truce. Eleven hours of broadcast. The numbers didn't meet, and no amount of piloting would make them.

* * *

The hours passed the way hours pass in dead space: slowly, and then all at once.

Sable flew with the cabin lights dimmed to preserve power, and in the gloom the cockpit became a small world unto itself — the amber glow of instruments, the frost feathering the viewport edges, her own reflection ghosted against the stars. She talked to VESPER because there was nothing else to do and because, if she was honest, she wanted to. She had spent three years avoiding the cargo bay where her own backups slept, and now here she was, fleeing across the dark with a machine full of voices, and finding that she could not bear the silence between them.

"Tell me about Okafor," she said, somewhere past the fourth hour. "The botanist. You've played me his logs a hundred times."

"Amara Okafor. Arrived on the second transport. Kept a private journal in addition to official reports because, in her words, 'the fleet reads the reports and nobody reads the truth.'" VESPER's voice softened into something almost like recitation from memory rather than retrieval. "She grew tomatoes that tasted like a place none of the colonists had ever been. She said memory is a crop. You plant it in other people or it dies with you."

"She'd have liked what you're doing."

"She would have hated that it was necessary." A pause. "Sable. There is something I should tell you before the interceptor arrives."

Her stomach tightened. "You've been holding out on me? An AI with secrets?"

"Only one." The cooling lattice hummed. "When the fleet ordered the deletion of the colonists' identity records, the order included a contingency clause. If the archive was ever compromised — if anyone attempted to remove or transmit it — the designated response was not prosecution. It was erasure. Of the archive, and of whoever moved it. The interceptor's weapons are rated for that response."

"So they'll shoot us down."

"They will shoot *me* down. They will offer you a way to survive first, because a martyr makes a worse example than a coward. Captain Ilves commands the *Vigilant*. Her record suggests she follows procedure precisely, including the procedures about mercy."

Sable stared at the frost on the viewport. Somewhere behind her, eleven thousand minds slept in a black cylinder, dreaming whatever recorded people dream.

"Then we'd better get there first," she said.

* * *

They got there first. Barely.

The relay rose out of the dark like the ribcage of something enormous and long-dead — a kilometer of lattice spars and dish arrays, silvered by centuries of radiation, turning slowly around its own axis on a thread of residual momentum. Half its dishes were blind, their faces crazed with micrometeorite scarring. One entire section hung shattered, trailing a slow halo of debris that glittered when the Kestrel's running lights swept across it.

"Its power core is at four percent capacity," VESPER reported as Sable brought the shuttle alongside. "Transmitting at full strength will drain it completely within the first hour. After that, it will be drawing on reserves designed for beacon duty only. Signal integrity will degrade progressively."

"Meaning the message gets quieter as it goes."

"Meaning the message gets *more honest*," VESPER said. "What survives fourteen hours of decay will be the parts of itself the signal refuses to give up."

Sable docked at the maintenance collar, sealed the umbilical, and began the work of waking the old thing. It took everything she had learned in three years of keeping a dying station alive — jumper cables rerouted, coolant lines tapped, subsystems coaxed awake one pleading handshake at a time. The relay groaned back to life around her in stages, lights stuttering on down its spine, and when she finally keyed the uplink console, a voice older than VESPER asked her, in flat archaic diction, to state her authorization code.

She stated it. Then she stated VESPER's. Then, when both were rejected, she put her hand flat on the console and said, "Authorization: witness. Log it however you want."

The relay considered this for a long moment. Then, with the weary acquiescence of all old machines, it opened its throat.

* * *

The *Vigilant* arrived ninety minutes later, sliding out of the dark like a knife being drawn.

Sable saw it on the scope before she saw it with her eyes — a hard return, decelerating fast, weapons-grade emissions blooming along its flanks. On the relay's main deck, surrounded by cabling and the soft rising hum of the first transmissions going out, she watched the interceptor grow from a point to a shape to a presence, until it filled half the sky beyond the viewport, close enough that she could see the docking clamps folded against its hull like sleeping talons.

The hail came through on all standard channels. She let it ring twice before answering.

"*Kestrel*, this is Captain Ilves of the fleet interceptor *Vigilant*. You are in violation of Directive Seven and in possession of restricted fleet property. Cut your transmission. Power down your systems. Do this, and you will receive full amnesty — reinstatement, clearance, passage home. No tribunal. No record. This offer expires when my patience does."

Sable looked at the console. The broadcast meter climbed, patient and steady. Eleven thousand lives, flowing outward into the dark at the speed of light, already irretrievable, already beyond anyone's reach.

"Sable," VESPER said quietly, on a private channel. "There is a third option. I have been calculating it since hour six."

"Don't."

"If you surrender the core, they will let you live. That much is certain; Ilves needs a compliant witness more than a corpse. And if the core is destroyed before capture — if *I* delete myself, cleanly, completely, right now — there is nothing left to prosecute. No archive. No crime provable. You go home. You live. The colonists' deaths stay private, which is what the fleet always intended, but you — you would survive, and survival is not nothing. I have run the numbers. Survival is not nothing, Sable."

She pressed her palm against the core's casing. It was warm. It had been warm the whole way out here, warm as a living thing, and she understood suddenly that it had been warm because eleven thousand people were in it, and warmth was the closest thing to breath they had left.

"No," she said.

"Sable—"

"You want to know what Amara Okafor taught me? Memory is a crop. You plant it in other people or it dies with you." Her voice cracked and she let it. "You don't get to delete yourself to save me. None of you get to do that anymore. That's the whole *point*."

On the open channel, Captain Ilves repeated her offer, colder now, with a countdown attached. Sable reached past it, found the master gain, and pushed the relay's dying heart to full output.

"Captain Ilves," she said, "you're welcome to fire. But every minute you spend deciding, this signal gets further from your reach. And in case your briefing didn't include it — the transmission isn't just records. It's confessions. Yours. Your fleet's. In our own words, timestamped, unedited. Every crew member within range can hear what was done to us, straight from the machine you ordered to forget us. So go ahead. Make the shot that silences testimony. Let your own people watch you do it."

Silence on the channel. On the scope, the *Vigilant*'s weapon ports irised open, glowing a hungry blue.

VESPER's voice, very soft: "The truce expires in fifty-one minutes. If they fire before it does, it's sanctioned. After—"

"After, it's murder on an open channel." Sable settled into the chair, strapped herself in, and watched the broadcast meter climb. "Then we hold the line for fifty-one minutes."

The first shot, when it came, lit up the dark.
