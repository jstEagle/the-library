## 6. Board of Inquiry

### Mirabel

The ship docked at 0400 station time, which was a fiction, since station time was whatever the clocks said and the clocks had been lying since Tuesday. But the docking clamps were real. Mirabel felt them through the deck plates like a handshake—firm, official, and entirely unwelcome.

She had rehearsed this moment for eleven years without knowing it. In her head it always went one of two ways: either she wept with gratitude at the sight of another human face, or she stood tall and defiant like the heroines in the pre-collapse dramas she watched on rotation. What she actually did was spill coffee down her front.

"Of course," she told the empty airlock corridor, dabbing at her shirt with a rag that smelled of seed preservative. "Eleven years of solitude, and my first impression is *decaf casualty*."

The outer hatch cycled. Six figures came through in salvage-rated suits, helmets tucked under their arms, breath fogging in the cold she'd never quite been able to afford to fix. The one in front was broad and gray as a filing cabinet, with the particular posture of a man who had never once sat down without first checking whether sitting was authorized.

"Mirabel Whitlock?" he said. "Caretaker of record?"

"That's me. And you'd be?"

He produced a tablet the way other men produced sidearms. "Administrator Corven Ashe, Office of Insolvency Recovery, Circuit Court of the Jovian Trade Zone." He held it out. "This order declares the assets of the Meridian Colony Project forfeit to its creditors. Effective immediately."

Mirabel took the tablet. She read it twice, mostly to buy time while her heart did something complicated behind her ribs. Five hundred thousand people, summarized as *assets*. Somewhere in the legalese there was a line about "biological inventory pending disposition."

"Disposition," she said aloud.

"As in eventual," said Ashe, in the tone of a man correcting a typo.

Behind him, five others stood in a loose wedge. A big man with a welder's burn scarred across his jaw. Two technicians who kept glancing at the walls—the walls, which were covered in eleven years of Mirabel's handwritten labels, because the digital tags kept corrupting and paper, she had learned, does not crash. A nervous young man clutching a case of diagnostic tools like a teddy bear. A woman with close-cropped hair and a rank pin that didn't match anyone else's uniform cut.

And at the back, slightly apart, a younger woman—dark-skinned, sharp-eyed, wearing her jumpsuit like it fit badly in a way that had nothing to do with tailoring. She wasn't looking at Ashe. She was looking past all of them, down the corridor, toward the cryo-bay.

"You'll want to see documentation of the facility's condition," Mirabel said, handing back the tablet. "I keep daily logs. Four thousand and change. You're welcome to read every one, though I warn you the early entries are mostly me learning to fix a water reclaimer by shouting at it."

Ashe almost smiled. It was the smile of a man who had learned smiling from a manual. "Efficient. I appreciate efficiency, Ms. Whitlock. This will go faster if we're both professionals."

"Oh, I'm very professional," Mirabel said. "Would you like tea? I have actual tea. Grown it myself. It tastes like punishment, but it's *mine*, which makes it the last legally unambiguous thing on this station."

That got a real reaction—not from Ashe, but from the sharp-eyed woman at the back, whose mouth twitched before she killed it.

"We'll begin with the cryo-bay," Ashe said.

"Of course you will," said Mirabel, and led them into the cold.

### Tamsin

Tamsin Okafor had boarded forty-one derelicts in six years, and she had a private taxonomy for them. There were wrecks, which were honest—fire and vacuum told you exactly what happened. There were ghosts, abandoned mid-sentence, coffee cups still on consoles. And then there were places like this, which were worse than either: places that had never stopped being lived in, tended by someone who refused to let them die.

The corridor lights followed them in sequence, dimming behind and brightening ahead, and Tamsin realized the caretaker was doing it manually—or by some ancient routine—and that this woman had walked these halls alone so long the building itself knew her footsteps.

Then they reached the cryo-bay observation gallery, and Tamsin forgot her taxonomy entirely.

Five hundred thousand pods. Rows upon rows upon rows, receding into blue-lit dark like a frozen ocean. Each one held a person. Not bodies—she corrected herself, professionally—*persons*. Sleepers. A woman with silver braids. A boy who would be a man now if he'd ever woken, frozen at fourteen forever, or until someone pressed a button.

Her breath caught audibly. Beside her, the caretaker glanced over.

"First time?" Mirabel Whitlock asked quietly.

"Is it that obvious?"

"You're standing the way I stood, eleven years ago. Like the floor might notice you're not supposed to be here."

Tamsin should have moved on. Ashe was already at the master console, pulling tonnage manifests, his voice flat and procedural: *"Biological units, cryogenic status, core integrity percentages..."* Her job was to log structural assessments and stay invisible until the contract paid out.

Instead she found herself reading pod placards. Names. Birthdates. Destinations: *Proxima Centauri b, berth assignment, cultural retention program.* One pod's occupant had a faded child's drawing taped inside the glass—crayon suns, a stick-figure family, the word COME BACK in letters that wobbled with effort.

"Tamsin." Reyes, the scarred one, low. "Admin's asking for numbers."

"On it," she said, and made herself walk away, and hated how easily her legs obeyed.

### Mirabel

Ashe conducted his inspection like an autopsy performed on someone still breathing. He touched nothing without gloved hands. He pronounced the seed vault "adequately maintained" in a tone suggesting adequate was a personal disappointment to him. When he reached the cryo-bay control room, he ran a finger along the console edge, inspected it, and said, "Dust."

"I dust Tuesdays," Mirabel said. "Today is not Tuesday. Also, your court order doesn't specify a dust standard, so I'm going to need you to cite the regulation."

Something flickered across his face—annoyance, quickly filed away. "You've read the order."

"Twice. It's forty pages long and uses the word 'heretofore' nine times, which I believe is a war crime in some jurisdictions." She folded her arms. "Here's what I know, Administrator. You can't touch those pods without my authorization codes, and you can't get my authorization codes without waking me from a very deep sleep, because they're stored—" she tapped her temple— "somewhere even I can't find them on a bad day."

"They can be overridden."

"Everything can be overridden. That's why I also keep a physical failsafe, location undisclosed, wired to vent the bay's coolant into the seed vault if anyone forces the locks. You'd lose your biological inventory *and* the agricultural assets. Both line items. In one afternoon." She smiled warmly. "I call it the Widowmaker Clause. I named it after my cat. She was also stubborn and destroyed everything she loved."

The nervous technician made a sound that might have been a suppressed laugh. Ashe silenced him with a look.

"You'd destroy half a million people to stop a lawful seizure?" he asked.

"I'd destroy *nothing*," Mirabel said. "The coolant vents into a secondary loop. But you don't know that, and now you have to wonder, and wondering slows people down wonderfully." She let the smile drop. "Eleven years, Administrator. I have nothing but time and spite. You have a schedule. Let's see whose resource constraint binds first."

For a moment—one long, cold moment—she thought he might actually test her. Then he consulted his tablet and said, "We'll proceed with non-invasive assessment," in the voice of a man adding a name to a list he intended to revisit.

Good, Mirabel thought. Wonder away. Meanwhile, in her quarters, a handheld drive was quietly copying everything Dr. Reyes-Mbeki had ever hidden in this station's bones.

### Tamsin

By second shift, Tamsin had learned three things about the Meridian station. One: it was colder than any occupied facility had a right to be, and the caretaker apologized for it like it was a moral failing. Two: the caretaker talked constantly—to them, to herself, apparently to the sleepers—and the talking wasn't madness, it was ballast. Three: Administrator Ashe had ordered a full power-draw audit of the cryo-bay, and the preliminary numbers had made him go very quiet, which in Tamsin's experience was the most dangerous sound a superior could make.

She found herself, without quite deciding to, back at the observation gallery after hours. The caretaker was already there.

"He does that," Mirabel said, not turning around. "Stands where I stand. It's like watching someone try on my life."

"Does it fit?"

"Haven't decided yet." Mirabel offered her a thermos cup. "Tea?"

Tamsin took it. It tasted, as promised, like punishment—but a sincere punishment, grown with care, and somehow that made it drinkable. Below them, the blue ocean of pods breathed their slow machine-breath.

"My grandmother left on a colony ship," Tamsin heard herself say. Why, she couldn't have told you. Maybe the tea. "Not this one. A Martian orbital project, before the funding died. She used to say the difference between a colony and a graveyard is whether anybody answers when you knock."

Mirabel was quiet a moment. "Your grandmother sounds smarter than our entire legal system."

"She'd have liked you. She also talked to things that couldn't answer." Tamsin hesitated. "The audit numbers. The draw on the cryo-bay. If it keeps climbing—"

"It will. Something's pulling more than the pods need. I've been chasing it for two days." Mirabel's eyes stayed on the rows below. "And if it keeps climbing, I start choosing what stays powered. And I have spent eleven years making sure I never had to choose anything."

The words hung in the cold air between them, and Tamsin felt something shift in her chest—a hairline crack, the kind you ignore right up until the whole pane comes down.

"You could refuse the audit," she said. "Slow-walk us. I've seen crews stall inspections for months."

"I know. I'm stalling." Mirabel finally looked at her, and there was no humor in it at all. "But you should know that whatever your administrator finds in those power records, it's going to scare him. And men like Corven Ashe do one of two things when they're scared."

Tamsin waited.

"They run," Mirabel said, "or they take the thing that frightened them apart to see how it works."

Across the bay, a warning chime sounded—soft, almost polite. On the master console, the power draw ticked upward again, and this time it didn't come back down.
