## 11. Jurisdiction of the Dead

Tamsin Okafor had signed enough asset-seizure orders to know the smell of one being prepared. It was a particular smell: recycled air gone stale with printer toner and the faint ozone tang of a man rehearsing his own righteousness.

She found Administrator Corven Ashe in the *Meridian's* borrowed conference room—the colony ship had one, because everything about the Meridian was built for a future that assumed dignity—and he was dictating to his slate while pacing a groove into a floor designed for people who were supposed to be awake by now.

"Item forty-one," Ashe said. "One deceased sleeper, pod nine-oh-six. Cause of death: catastrophic coolant failure secondary to gravitational anomaly. Note for the record that the station's caretaker failed to maintain redundancy systems adequate to—"

"She maintained them for eleven years," Tamsin said.

Ashe didn't startle. That was the thing about Corven Ashe; nothing startled him, because everything was already filed. He turned with the unhurried gravity of a man who had never once been interrupted at the right moment.

"Lieutenant Okafor. You're not on this rotation."

"I'm always on this rotation. You gave me this rotation."

"I gave you logistics." He tapped the slate. "This is now an inquest."

"It's a death certificate wearing a suit."

He almost smiled. She'd seen him do it before—the flicker that meant *you're sharper than my paperwork requires, and I haven't decided if that's useful or dangerous.* "The woman is dead, Lieutenant. I didn't kill her. The vacuum did. My job—our job—is to make sure her death means something legally. Structure. Precedent. Without structure, she's just a statistic floating in a cold room."

"She's just a person," Tamsin said, "floating in a cold room."

"That too." He set the slate down, and for a moment—half a moment—he looked tired in a way that wasn't performance. "You've been down there. In the bay. You've seen them."

"Yes."

"Five hundred thousand people who have been dead-adjacent for eleven years. The law finally caught up to them, Tamsin. That's all liquidation is. The law arriving late, but arriving." He picked the slate back up. "Inquest convenes at 0800. You'll be present as records officer. Wear your commission."

She wore her commission. She'd worn it for fourteen years, through two bankrupt salvage firms and one war that had ended before anyone finished declaring it. But walking out of the conference room, past the porthole where the seed vault glittered like a jewelry box someone had dropped in the dark, she thought: *the law arriving late.*

Ada Voss had waited eleven years for the law. It had arrived four days after she died.

* * *

The inquest lasted ninety minutes and managed, in that time, to make a dead woman feel like a line item.

Ashe ran it beautifully—that was the horror of it. He had Mirabel Whitlock brought in under escort, which was absurd, since the woman hadn't left the station voluntarily in a decade and could be trusted about as far as her own front door, which was to say infinitely. He asked his questions in a soft, reasonable voice. He expressed condolences. He used the phrase *unfortunate but foreseeable* twice, and each time Tamsin watched Mirabel's hands tighten around each other in her lap like two people holding on during turbulence.

"The station's power architecture," Ashe said, "was it ever audited by a certified third party?"

"No," Mirabel said.

"So we cannot verify maintenance standards."

"You can read eleven years of my logs. Every fluctuation, every repair, every time I crawled into a coolant junction with a soldering iron and a prayer. I wrote them so someone would know what happened here. I just never thought 'someone' would be reading them looking for a reason."

"Logs are self-reported, Ms. Whitlock."

"They're the only honest thing left on this rock," Mirabel said, "including everyone in this room."

Ashe let that hang, then moved on without visible damage, which was its own kind of violence. By the end he had established, to his satisfaction, that the death resulted from systemic negligence, that systemic negligence constituted breach of custodial duty, and that breach of custodial duty triggered acceleration clauses in the creditor liens—which meant liquidation could proceed immediately rather than after the standard review period.

"Immediate," Tamsin repeated, when the room had emptied except for the two of them.

"Immediate," Ashe agreed. "Every day of delay costs the estate money. Every day the pods draw power, the debt deepens. You do the arithmetic."

She did the arithmetic. She'd been doing it for three days, actually, alone in her bunk with a legal pad like some kind of Luddite, because she didn't trust the ship's systems not to log her queries. Five hundred thousand sleepers. Remaining reserves. Power required per waking cycle, per recovery ward, per calorie of food grown under lights that hadn't been manufactured in thirty years.

The number did not work. The number did not come close to working. Waking even half of them would take more energy than the vault had seen in a decade, and there was no grid out here to beg from, only the long dark and the distant smudge of a sun that owed them nothing.

Which raised a question she'd been chewing since the funeral-that-wasn't—a memorial, really, just her and Mirabel and Jonah Voss standing in the cryo-bay's antechamber while frost breathed across the glass—if liquidation couldn't save anyone, what exactly was it liquidating?

She went to find out.

* * *

The creditors' liaison traveled aboard Ashe's own vessel, which should have told her something from the start. His name was Durell Hask, and he occupied the best cabin like a man renting it ironically, surrounded by display cases of things Tamsin suspected were worth more than her entire career.

"Lieutenant Okafor." He knew her name. Of course he did. "To what do I owe the pleasure?"

"A procedural question." She kept her hands loose, her face bland. Fourteen years of boarding parties had taught her that the most dangerous thing you could bring onto a strange ship was curiosity, and the second most dangerous was letting people see yours. "Asset manifest. The pods themselves. When we strike them from the registry as liabilities, what's their disposition? Scrap in place? Towage?"

Hask's smile was warm as a heat lamp. "Why, they're reclaimed for their materials value, naturally. Structural alloy, coolant stock, and—" he waved a hand, delicate, "—the containment cores. Fusion-grade. Remarkably well-preserved. Do you know how hard it is to source certified containment cores anymore? The colonies hoard them. Earth stopped making them. There are perhaps four hundred units of this quality in the entire system."

"There are five hundred thousand pods."

"And every one of them a small miracle of engineering." He said it fondly, the way a man might praise a vineyard. "The consortium is quite excited. A generation ship needs roughly six hundred cores to make transit viable. Your dead colonists are going to carry living ones to Proxima after all. There's a poetry in it, don't you think?"

Tamsin thought several things, none of which she said. What she said was: "The pods are occupied."

"Not for long. Extraction is non-invasive. Well." The heat-lamp smile dimmed by a degree. "Non-invasive relative to the alternative, which is leaving five hundred thousand corpses frozen in a derelict until the orbit decays. We're being humane, Lieutenant. Efficiently humane. It's the only kind that scales."

She thanked him. She smiled. She walked back down the corridor with her pulse ticking in her ears like a countdown, and she did not go to her bunk, and she did not go to the mess. She went to find Corven Ashe, because somewhere beneath the fourteen years and the commission and the careful arithmetic, she still believed that the man she'd followed through two bankruptcies and a war would look her in the eye and tell her she'd misunderstood.

* * *

She found him in the observation blister off the command deck, alone, watching the vault turn below them. He heard her come in and didn't turn around.

"Hask talked to you," he said.

"He talked *at* me. There's a difference." She planted herself between him and the window, which was rude, and which was the point. "Tell me he was lying. Tell me the consortium isn't going to crack open those pods for their cores and sell the sleepers as scrap weight."

Ashe looked at her then. And here was the thing she would turn over in her mind for years afterward, in bunks and cells and eventually, God help her, courtrooms: he didn't lie. He didn't perform outrage. He just looked tired—the real kind, the kind he'd flashed for half a moment in the conference room—and said:

"You did the numbers, didn't you. On waking them."

"You know I did. I gave them to you."

"And?"

"They don't work. Not for half. Not for a tenth. Not without a power source nobody's built yet." Her voice cracked on the last part, and she hated it. "That doesn't make this right, Corven. That makes it *worse*. If we can't save them, fine—but we don't get to melt them down for parts and call it salvage. They're people. They have names. One of them has a grandson sleeping in our guest berth right now."

"Jonah Voss has a claim with no standing and a lawyer with no license," Ashe said flatly. "Sentiment doesn't generate terawatts, Tamsin."

"Don't you dare lecture me about sentiment. I've watched you read every log entry. All eleven years. At night, alone, with a drink. Nobody does that for inventory."

Something moved behind his eyes—guilt, maybe, or its fossil record. "I read them because due diligence matters."

"You read them because you wanted it to be different. Because some part of you hoped the caretaker would turn out to be wrong and the sleepers would wake up and none of this—" he flinched, barely, "—none of *you* would have to be the man who did it."

"Tamsin." His voice dropped, and the warmth in it was worse than coldness, because it was real. "Sit down."

"I'm standing."

"Then stand and listen." He stepped closer. "The order comes from above me. The consortium holds the paper. The courts hold the consortium. If I refuse, they replace me with someone who won't lose sleep over it—I've met the replacement, Tamsin, and he asks questions like 'can we begin extraction before towage'—and if I resist actively, that's not refusal, that's mutiny against a lawful chain of authority, and you and I both end our careers in a cell orbiting Ceres while the pods get cracked anyway. Is that what you want? Is that the win?"

"The win," Tamsin said slowly, "is not being the person who did it."

"Somebody does it either way."

"Then let it be somebody else. Let history have his name. Don't give him ours."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Below the window, the vault turned, patient as a held breath. Somewhere in that glittering dark, four hundred ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine people slept on, minus one, minus Ada, whose absence had a shape now, a pod-shaped hole in the world.

"You're a good officer," Ashe said quietly. "The best I've served with. Which is why I'm telling you this once, as a friend, off the record: stand down. Log your objections if you need to—file them, I'll even countersign them, that's how much I respect you. But when extraction begins, you will be on the *Kestrel*, doing inventory of the seed racks, far from the bay. That's not a threat. It's the only mercy I have left to give."

"And if I refuse the mercy?"

He held her gaze, and she watched the friend recede behind the administrator like a light going down a long corridor.

"Then I am obligated to relieve you of duty pending inquiry," he said. "And the charge will be mutiny."

The word landed in the little blister of glass and steel and hung there between them, absurd and enormous. Mutiny. As if the system were a ship worth staying loyal to. As if loyalty were a thing you owed to paperwork instead of to the people the paperwork was supposed to protect.

"Corven," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, "when did you stop believing any of this was legal and start believing it was merely authorized?"

He didn't answer. That was the answer.

She turned and walked out, and her hands only started shaking in the corridor, where no one official could see.

* * *

She didn't go to her bunk. She went down.

Down through the *Meridian's* spine, past the hydroponics bays with their dead grow-lights, past the chapel that had been built and never consecrated because the congregation was still asleep, down to the cryo-bay antechamber where the frost breathed its slow breath against the glass.

Mirabel Whitlock was there. Of course she was. She seemed to exist in a state of quantum superposition between the seed racks and the pods, collapsing into whichever one needed her. She was holding a cloth and cleaning the nameplate on pod nine-oh-seven—next to Ada's empty one—with the particular tenderness of a person who had run out of tears a decade ago and now performed grief as maintenance.

"He's filing charges, isn't he," Mirabel said, without turning around.

"How did you—"

"You have the walk. I've seen it on inspectors. The walk of someone who just lost an argument with a superior and won an argument with herself." She set down the cloth. "Mutiny?"

"Pending inquiry. Which is bureaucrat for *soon*."

"Mm." Mirabel looked out at the pods, ranks of them receding into the dark like a field of frost-covered wheat. "Can I ask you something, Lieutenant? You've been here four days. You've seen the logs, the bay, the boy with his grandmother's face. Knowing what you know now—knowing what they intend to do with these cores—" she turned, and her eyes in the low light were very steady, "—what does the law say we owe the dead?"

Tamsin opened her mouth to give the commissioned answer. *Due process. Estate resolution. Respectful disposition of remains.*

What came out instead was: "Nothing. The law says we owe the dead nothing. That's why it keeps killing them."

Mirabel nodded slowly, as if Tamsin had passed some test she hadn't known she was taking. Then she reached into her coat pocket and produced a handheld drive, small and scuffed, the kind of object that had been obsolete for twenty years and therefore invisible to every audit in the system.

"ECHO ANEW," she said. "Ever heard of it?"

"No."

"Neither had I, until four days ago, and I've lived here eleven years." She pressed the drive into Tamsin's palm and closed the lieutenant's fingers around it, her hands cold as the vault and twice as steady. "It's in the founder's files. Redacted project, buried deep. I think—I *think*—it's the reason the gravity stuttered. I think it's the reason Ada died. And I think whoever built it left instructions that nobody ever delivered." A pause. "I need someone who knows how the other side thinks. Someone who can read a legal order and find the seams in it. Someone who's just decided the system isn't worth her signature."

Tamsin looked down at the drive in her hand. Small. Scuffed. Heavy as a verdict.

"If Ashe finds out," she said, "I'm done. Court-martial, forfeiture of pension, the whole liturgy."

"If Ashe finds out," Mirabel said, "we'll all be done, along with everyone in this room. So let's make sure he doesn't." And then, with the ghost of a smile—the first Tamsin had ever seen on her—"Besides. You already committed mutiny in your heart about an hour ago. The paperwork's just catching up."

Outside the glass, the pods breathed their frost-breath. Inside, a caretaker and a traitor stood among the seeds of everything that hadn't been planted yet, and Tamsin Okafor—who had spent fourteen years believing that loyalty was a thing you gave downward to the chain of command—felt it reverse direction all at once, flowing sideways, toward the woman beside her and the sleeping hundreds of thousands beyond the glass and a grandson on the deck above who deserved better than a headstone made of fusion cores.

"What's on this drive," she asked, "that's worth my career?"

Mirabel's smile widened by a millimeter.

"A machine," she said, "that's been trying to talk to us for eleven years. And I think it's finally learned our language."

Somewhere far overhead, past the hull, past the dark, the stellar grid hummed—and for the first time, Tamsin understood that the humming might be a question, waiting patiently for someone brave enough to answer.
