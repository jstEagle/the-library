## 13. The Claimant

Jonah Voss had spent the whole approach trying to remember his grandmother's face, and failing, which was its own kind of joke, because everyone on Mars said he wore it like a hand-me-down coat.

"You've got her chin," his aunt used to say, poking him at family dinners. "You've got her exact chin. And her way of looking at people like they owe you money."

He didn't remember the chin. He remembered a photograph, one photograph, of a woman in a launch-day scarf holding a paper cup of something celebratory, squinting against the lights of a ship that never went anywhere. Ada Voss. Sleeper 114,207. Frozen for thirty-one years in a vault above a world she'd never chosen to stay behind on.

And now, apparently, he was flying out there to claim her.

"Jonah." Priya's voice from the cockpit, dry as Martian regolith. "You're doing the thing where you stare at the bulkhead and rehearse speeches nobody asked you to give."

"I'm not rehearsing."

"Your lips are moving."

"They're practicing breathing."

The *Kindly Interest* — their freighter, named by his grandfather as a joke about compound interest that nobody else in the family ever found funny — shuddered through the last deceleration burn. Through the viewport, Svalbard-2 resolved: a white knuckle of a station gripping an ice field, seed-vault domes glinting, and docked alongside it, sleek and ugly, a salvage vessel with corporate lines and the name *Equitable Recovery* stenciled on its flank.

Priya whistled low. "We're not the first ones here."

"No," Jonah said. "We're just the ones with the paperwork."

That was the family specialty, actually. Thirty years of inheritance claims filed every single year from a cramped office in Olympus Town, by his grandfather, then his mother, then him after the accident. Claims nobody processed, addressed to courts that no longer existed in any meaningful sense, citing laws that had been amended twice since anyone bothered reading them. His mother called it gardening. You kept planting even when the season was wrong, because seasons turned.

His mother was dead now. The claims were his.

"Approach vector's clean," said Dev, youngest of them, nineteen, who had insisted on coming because somebody had to work the comms and because, in his words, "I want to see if they're real. The sleepers. I've seen the manifests my whole life and I don't believe a word of it."

"They're real," Jonah said.

"How do you know?"

Because his grandmother was in there. Because you didn't spend three generations filing paperwork about a ghost. He didn't say this. He said, "Because the power bills are real, and half a million people draw a lot of power."

* * *

Mirabel felt the second ship before she saw it — a tremor through the docking clamps, a new weight settling onto the station like a guest arriving uninvited to a house that already had too many guests.

She was in the seed racks, cataloguing barley cultivars, when the station chime announced an unscheduled docking. She stood very still among the wheat. Down the corridor, in the cryo-bay, Administrator Ashe's people were still running their inventory, clipboards and scanners, treating five hundred thousand human beings like pallets of machinery. Tamsin Okafor had been at the pod window again that morning. Mirabel had watched her through the security feed, standing too long, arms crossed, doing arithmetic with her face.

Now a second ship. Smaller. Unregistered until it wasn't — the transponder flickering up late, patched together, broadcasting a name that made Mirabel laugh out loud alone among the seeds.

*Kindly Interest.*

"Oh," she said to the barley. "Oh, you have got to be kidding me."

The manifest pinged her console before she'd finished laughing. Six passengers. Family names she recognized from the registry she'd decrypted two weeks ago — Voss, Okafor-adjacent names, names that appeared in the passenger manifest of the *Meridian* and then, generation later, in the annual inheritance filings that had sat unread in a dead court's queue for decades.

One name in particular. Voss, J.

Ada's file was open on her secondary screen. It had been open for days, since the pulse cracked the coolant line and took her. Sleeper 114,207. Age fifty-three at freezing. Grandson listed as next of kin: Voss, Jonah, b. Mars.

Mirabel smoothed her jacket, checked her reflection in the dark of a monitor, decided she looked like a woman who hadn't had a conversation with a new person in eleven years — accurate — and went to the airlock.

* * *

The airlock cycled and Jonah stepped onto a station that smelled like cold metal and, unbelievably, green things. Growing things. He'd read about the seed vault but reading wasn't smelling, and the smell hit him like a memory of a place he'd never been.

Six of them came through, blinking in the light. Behind them, the salvage crew's junior officer — a tall woman with careful eyes — stood watching from the corridor junction, making no move to stop them and no move to welcome them either.

And in front of him, extending a hand, was a small woman with grey-streaked hair and the posture of someone who had spent a decade talking to rooms full of people who couldn't answer.

"Mirabel Whitlock," she said. "Caretaker. You must be the descendants."

"We must be," Jonah said, shaking her hand. Her grip was stronger than expected. "Jonah Voss."

Her face did something complicated. "Voss," she repeated. "Ada Voss is your grandmother."

"She is. Was. Is." He winced. "Legally she's in a state we haven't invented grammar for yet."

Something in the woman's shoulders loosened, like she'd been holding a breath for eleven years and here, finally, was someone who understood the tense problem. "You have no idea how long I've waited for someone to say that," Mirabel said. "Everyone else says 'the assets.' Everyone else says 'the cargo.' You said 'she.'"

"My grandmother raised my father on stories about that ship," Jonah said. "Half of them were complaints about the food."

"The food would be worth complaining about," Mirabel said solemnly. "I've read the menus. There was a dish called Protein Medley that appears to have been a war crime."

Behind him, Dev snorted. Priya elbowed him. And just like that, Jonah thought, the ice cracked — not all the way, not enough to walk on, but cracked.

* * *

They gathered in what Mirabel called the commons and what the salvage crew probably called a conference room, and what was honestly just a room with chairs and a table and a window looking out onto the ice. Mirabel served tea grown from vault stock — actual tea, grown in trays under grow-lights, hoarded like treasure — and Jonah watched his delegation drink it like it was communion.

He laid the documents out on the table. Thirty years of filings. His grandfather's handwriting, his mother's, his own. Inheritance claims, counterclaims, a writ of kinship succession filed under a statute so old it referenced a parliament that had dissolved before Jonah was born.

"This is a counterclaim to the colony itself," he said. "Not to the salvage value. To the colony. The sleepers aren't property. They're creditors' nightmare and our family. Every person in that bay has living kin who never stopped filing. We're asking for recognition of the estate as held in trust for its own people."

Across the table, Administrator Corven Ashe listened with the expression of a man watching weather he intended to ignore.

It had taken two hours to get him into the room. He'd arrived flanked by two of his crew, sat down, folded his hands, and let Jonah talk the entire case without interrupting once, which Jonah knew from experience was worse than interruption.

When Jonah finished, Ashe unfolded his hands.

"Mr. Voss," he said. "Your filings are addressed to the Colonial Adjudication Office. That office was dissolved fourteen years ago. Your claims have no forum, therefore no standing, therefore no force. This station and its contents fall under a valid forfeiture order issued by a court that does exist. I'm executing that order. I'm sympathetic — genuinely — to your family's persistence. But sympathy isn't jurisdiction."

"The court that issued your order," Priya said, leaning forward, "is funded by the same consortium that holds the debt. Your adjudicator and your creditor share a board member. We checked."

Ashe didn't blink. "Then you should file a complaint."

"With whom?"

Silence. The gentle humor drained out of the room and left something colder behind.

Mirabel spoke up from the end of the table, mild as milk. "Administrator, correct me if I'm wrong, but your order covers the colony's *assets*. The seed stock, the hardware, the hull. Does it cover the sleepers?"

"It covers everything aboard."

"Everything aboard," Mirabel repeated. "So you're asserting that five hundred thousand living human beings are assets."

"Dormant human beings," Ashe said. "In suspended legal status. Their estates can be honored after recovery costs are met—"

"After," Jonah said. "You mean if."

Ashe looked at him then, really looked, and Jonah saw the moment the man registered whose face he was looking at. A flicker. Quick, but Jonah had grown up being told he had his grandmother's eyes, and he'd learned they could be a weapon.

"You have her face," Ashe said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone keeps telling me that," Jonah said. "I wouldn't know. I was four when she froze. But here's what I do know, Administrator. My grandmother boarded that ship believing in a future. Your backers boarded her believing in scrap metal. One of those beliefs is going to lose, and I'd think hard about which side of it you want to be standing on when it does."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Ashe stood, buttoned his jacket, and said, "This meeting is concluded. The forfeiture proceeds on schedule." At the door he paused, and without turning around added, "Mr. Voss. The dead don't inherit. The living do. Remember who's actually alive down here when the power runs out."

He left. The door sighed shut behind him.

Tamsin Okafor, who had been standing by the window the entire time saying nothing, looked at the documents on the table for a long moment. Then she picked up the top page — the original 2041 passenger registry, Jonah's grandfather's copy, soft at the edges from handling — read a few lines, and set it back down with a care that struck Jonah as almost ceremonial.

"Sleeper 114,201," she said. "Amara Okafor. My great-aunt." She met Jonah's eyes. "Nobody told me there were filings."

"There were thirty years of filings," Jonah said.

"Of course there were," Tamsin said, and something in her voice made Mirabel look up sharply from her tea. "There always are. Nobody processes them either." She straightened. "I'll be in the cryo-bay. If the Administrator asks, I'm verifying inventory."

She left. Priya exhaled slowly. Dev whispered, "Was that a defector? Did we just acquire a defector?"

"We acquired nothing," Jonah said, but he was smiling. "Alliances aren't acquisitions. They're more like—" he gestured vaguely at the tea, the documents, the window full of ice and stars, "—gardening."

Mirabel laughed, a real laugh, rusty at the hinges. "You sound like someone I used to know," she said. "Sit down, Mr. Voss. Both of you. All of you. We have a lot of work to do and I've had eleven years to learn where everything on this station is hidden, including the good tea."

* * *

But that night, alone in the caretaker's quarters with the station humming around her, Mirabel pulled up the cryo-bay telemetry and felt the warmth of the day drain away.

Power reserves: down another fraction of a percent. Faster than the baseline drain. Faster than ECHO ANEW's pulses could explain.

And on the salvage ship's comm band, encrypted traffic — heavy, frequent, scheduled. Cargo manifests being transmitted outward. She couldn't decrypt them. She didn't need to. She'd spent eleven years learning to read the shape of data rather than its content, and the shape of this traffic was the shape of a shopping list.

She thought of Ada Voss's grandson asleep in the guest bunk down the corridor, wearing his grandmother's chin, having crossed a system to plant a garden in frozen ground. She thought of Tamsin standing at the pod window. She thought of the coolant line, and the crack, and the certificate she still hadn't finished writing.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would show them the founder's files. All of them. The machine, the pulses, the testament — everything.

Because whatever Ashe's encrypted manifests were ordering, it was clear now that time had stopped being on anyone's side.

Down in the dark of the cryo-bay, five hundred thousand people slept on, and somewhere beneath the ice, the old machine pulsed once — patient, insistent — like a knock at a door that everyone had finally gathered on the right side of.
