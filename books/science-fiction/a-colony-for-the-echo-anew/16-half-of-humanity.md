## 16. Half of Humanity

### MIRABEL

The number arrived the way bad news always does on Svalbard-2: quietly, in a readout, while she was doing something mundane.

Mirabel had been re-taping a thermal wrap around coolant junction nine when Tamsin's voice came over the intercom, flat and careful in the way people get when they've checked their math three times and prayed it would change.

"Mirabel? You should come see this."

She came. The engineering alcove off the cryo-bay had become, over the past two days, something like a war room, if a war room could be furnished with seed racks, salvaged chairs, and a coffee maker that Jonah Voss had coaxed back to life and now guarded jealously. Tamsin stood before the power schematic, her tablet propped against a conduit, and she didn't turn around when Mirabel entered.

"Just say it," Mirabel said.

"With ECHO ANEW's grid draw stabilized, and assuming the stellar feed holds at current levels—" Tamsin's jaw worked. "We can bring up half of them. Four hundred and ninety thousand, give or take. Not one more. The other half stays frozen until the grid can carry them, which is years, or decades, or never, depending on who you ask."

"Half," Mirabel repeated. The word felt like a seed pod cracking open to reveal nothing inside.

"Half." Tamsin finally turned. Her face had the particular grayness of someone who had spent a career making numbers behave and had just watched one bite her. "I've run it every way I can. There's no cleverness left in it. No efficiency trick. It's a knife cut, clean down the middle, and somebody has to decide which side of the blade each person falls on."

Mirabel looked past her, through the observation glass, at the long dim rows of pods stretching away into the dark—half a million small lit windows, each one a person who had signed up for Proxima and gotten, instead, eleven years of her voice telling them the weather in a place they'd never see.

"I know how this goes," she said quietly. "I've read the histories. Lifeboat arithmetic. It always starts with *half*."

"It never ends there," Tamsin agreed. "That's the problem."

* * *

They called the meeting for 0800 station time, which meant nothing to anyone anymore except that it was after coffee, which meant everything.

Mirabel set it up in the seed vault itself, in the long central aisle where the racks rose on both sides like wheat-colored canyon walls. She'd thought about using the command center—it had better screens—but something in her resisted the idea of deciding human lives under fluorescent panels and asset manifests. Here, at least, they'd be surrounded by growing things. By the argument that life insists on itself.

Ashe arrived first, immaculate as ever, his court order folded in his breast pocket like a talisman he no longer quite believed in. He surveyed the seed racks with the expression of a man auditing a cathedral.

"You realize," he said, "that whatever we decide here has no legal force whatsoever."

"Yes," Mirabel said pleasantly. "Like most important things."

Jonah came in carrying his grandmother's death certificate, folded soft as cloth from handling. He'd taken to keeping it on him; Mirabel understood. Some papers you carry so you don't have to carry the person, and then you find out you're carrying the person anyway. He sat down across from Ashe and said nothing, which from Jonah was louder than most men's shouting.

Tamsin came last. She paused in the doorway, and Mirabel saw her look at the room—the caretaker, the administrator, the grandson—and something in her posture shifted, a decision being made in the body before the mind signed off on it.

"All right," Mirabel said. "You've all seen the numbers. Half. We need to choose the half. Ashe—you've argued your case already, but say it again, for the record. We're recording."

"We are?" Ashe asked.

"The vault records everything," Mirabel said. "It's kind of our whole thing."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth—almost humor, quickly suppressed. He straightened. "Skilled adults," he said. "Engineers, physicians, agronomists, systems specialists. If we can only wake half, we wake the half that can *build*. Wake the children and you have half a million dependents and no infrastructure to raise them. Wake the elders and you have wisdom without hands. Wake the working adults and within five years you have a functioning settlement that can eventually afford to wake everyone else. It isn't cruel. It's sequencing."

"It's a résumé review," Jonah said.

"It's triage," Ashe shot back. "And triage is only monstrous to people who've never had to do it."

"I've done it," Jonah said quietly. "My grandmother did it, on Mars, during the water riots. She used to say the worst part wasn't choosing. It was how reasonable you sounded while you were doing it." He unfolded the certificate, though he didn't need to look at it. "Ada Voss. She was thirty-four when she boarded the Meridian. A hydroponics specialist—which, by your criteria, Administrator, makes her wakeable. Valuable. Useful." His voice caught and steadied. "She died because the power wasn't there. So don't tell me about triage. Tell me why the adults get the power and the kids get the dark, and tell me in words my mother could hear."

The seed racks hummed their faint green hum. Nobody answered him immediately, and Mirabel let the silence do its work. Silence, she had learned in eleven years alone, was not empty. It was full of everything people weren't ready to say.

* * *

### JONAH

Jonah had rehearsed his argument for six hours and abandoned it in the first minute, because rehearsed arguments were what Ashe did, and Jonah had decided somewhere between Mars and here that he would rather lose honestly than win like that.

But he hadn't expected Tamsin Okafor to speak next.

He'd pegged her early—correctly, he'd thought—as the loyal one. The junior officer who lingered at windows but followed orders. When she stood, he braced for the second verse of the skilled-adults hymn.

"Elders," she said.

Even Ashe blinked. "Tamsin—"

"Let me finish." Her hands were steady; her voice wasn't, entirely. "I ran the numbers again last night. Not the power numbers—the *after* numbers. Say we wake four hundred and ninety thousand adults. Skilled ones. They wake into a frozen vault on a dead colony ship orbiting a rock at the top of the world. No settlement. No institutions. No memory of why any of this mattered. Skills without context are just tools in a drawer." She turned to Jonah. "Your grandmother's generation—the defectors' parents, the founders, the people who chose to board the Meridian—they carry the story. Why Proxima. What was promised. What was lost. Culture isn't decoration. It's the operating system. Wake the elders, and the adults who wake later wake into something. Wake only technicians, and you get a very capable population with amnesia."

"That's—" Ashe began.

"And before you say it," Tamsin went on, "yes, I know the counterargument. Elders die sooner. Every elder we wake instead of an adult is years of expertise we don't get. I've weighted it. I've modeled it. I stayed up until my eyes hurt doing the arithmetic." She looked down at her own hands. "And then I walked through the cryo-bay, and I looked at pod forty-one thousand and change, and there's a woman in there, sixty-eight years old, who taught history in Uppsala, and I realized I was weighing her against a man in pod eighty-two thousand who's good with fusion reactors, and both of those sentences are insane. Both of them. We're pricing human beings by the kilowatt-hour and calling it ethics."

The gentle humor Jonah had been clinging to all morning—somebody had to make jokes or the room would tip over—drained out of him. Because she was right. That was the horror of it. Every option was insane, and they were going to pick one anyway, and then live inside it.

"So what are you proposing?" Ashe asked. "Sentiment as policy?"

"I'm proposing we admit what we're doing," Tamsin said. "Out loud. In front of the record. That we are choosing who lives first, and no framework makes it clean."

* * *

### MIRABEL

Mirabel had known, since Tamsin showed her the numbers, that this moment would come. She had hoped to delay it. Hope, she'd found, was mostly a scheduling strategy.

"There's a third option," she said.

All three of them turned.

"The children," Mirabel said. "Everyone under sixteen. All of them."

Ashe made a sound—not quite a laugh, not quite anything else. "Mirabel. Be serious. Two hundred and some thousand children, awake, with—what—a few hundred adults to care for them? Us? Four adults and a coffee machine?"

"Ships arrive monthly now," Mirabel said. "Volunteers. Jonah's people. And more will come once word spreads, and word will spread, because—" she gestured at the racks around them, at the whole improbable green aisle, "—look where we're standing. Nothing here survives alone. That's the entire principle of a seed vault. You don't store one seed. You store thousands, because you don't know which will take, so you give them all a chance and you tend whatever comes up."

"They'll be frightened," Ashe said. "Confused. They went to sleep expecting Proxima."

"They went to sleep expecting a future," Mirabel said. "So did all of us." She heard her own voice go quiet and didn't fight it. "Administrator, I've spent eleven years talking to these sleepers. I know their files. Do you know what percentage of the adult manifest listed 'children waiting at destination' as their reason for boarding? Sixty-one percent. They got on that ship *for* the kids. If we wake the adults first, we wake them into a promise we still can't keep. If we wake the children first—" she stopped, started again. "If we wake the children first, then whatever we build next gets built *around* them. Everything we do afterward has witnesses. Small, sharp-eyed, merciless witnesses who will ask us why, forever, and we will have to answer."

"That's not a plan," Ashe said. "That's a guilt engine."

"It's a conscience with a birthday," Jonah said, and even in the middle of it, Mirabel saw him almost smile at his own terrible joke, and she loved him a little for it.

Ashe stood. He'd been sitting too long; Mirabel recognized the movement of a man whose arguments worked better vertical. "You're all letting grief vote," he said. "Grief for Ada. Grief for your eleven years. Grief is not a selection criterion. I have a court order. I have creditors. I have—"

"You have backers," Tamsin said.

The room changed temperature.

Mirabel watched it happen: the way Ashe stopped mid-breath, the way Tamsin's hand moved to her tablet, the way Jonah leaned forward like a man watching a fuse burn toward something he couldn't yet name.

"What did you say?" Ashe asked, very softly.

"I said you have backers," Tamsin repeated. "And I've been reviewing the transfer schedules you filed with the salvage manifest, and Administrator, the priority listings don't match your stated criteria. The pods flagged for earliest extraction aren't the skilled adults. They're not anyone who fits any ethical framework we've discussed tonight. They're flagged by *core output rating*." She lifted the tablet. On it, glowing, was a list. "You're not choosing who wakes. Someone chose who gets *harvested*, and you signed the schedule."

For a long moment, Ashe looked at her the way a man looks at a bridge as it starts to sway—calculating whether it will hold, whether he can cross, whether it's already too late.

"Tamsin," he said at last, and his voice had lost its polish entirely, "you should stop talking."

"No," she said. "I really shouldn't. I did the arithmetic, Corven. All of it. Including yours."

Mirabel's heart was pounding so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Around them the seed racks hummed, indifferent and patient, ten thousand varieties of tomorrow waiting in the dark for someone to decide the terms of the light.

And then, before anyone could speak again, the lights dipped—once, twice—and every console in the vault chimed at once with a signal Mirabel knew in her bones, a pulse-pattern she'd been hearing in her dreams for weeks.

ECHO ANEW was asking again.

And this time, it wasn't asking permission.
