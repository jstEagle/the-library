## 18. The Vote of the Living

The station's temperature had dropped to nine degrees Celsius by the time I finished counting heads.

I counted them twice, because the first time felt like a dream and the second time felt like a mistake, and I needed it to be neither. Forty-one people. Six salvagers under Corven Ashe. Nine descendants who'd come on a retrofitted freighter with paint peeling off its hull like old sunburn. Twenty-six of us from the vault side—me, Jonah Voss standing at my shoulder with his grandmother's jaw set in his face, Tamsin Okafor who had crossed a line two days ago and not yet decided whether to build a bridge back or burn it.

Forty-one souls in a seed vault at the top of the world, deciding what half of humanity was worth.

"Everyone can hear me?" I asked, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted. The comm system crackled its confirmation into every corridor, every compartment, every corner where we'd all retreated to escape the cold that was eating the station one degree at a time.

I stood in the central germination hall, surrounded by ten thousand seed racks frosted silver, my breath hanging in front of me like a small ghost that refused to leave. Behind me, someone had dragged the station's single display screen out of the archive office and propped it against a rack of heritage wheat. On it, in numbers that kept ticking downward as the coolant bled, were the only figures that mattered:

**Power remaining: sufficient to revive 247,000 sleepers. Total sleepers: 493,881.**

Half. A little less than half, if you were honest about conversion losses. A little more, if you were honest about hope.

* * *

Let me tell you how we got here, because it happened fast, the way disasters and miracles both do—no warning, just suddenly everything is different.

Ashe had proposed his allocation first, of course. He'd stood in this same hall with his court order held up like a lantern and argued for the skilled adults: engineers, physicians, agronomists, the people who could *rebuild*, he said, as though humanity were a construction project and not a family. His voice was reasonable. That was always the worst thing about Ashe. He could make abandonment sound like prudence.

Jonah had answered him with the elders. "You want hands," Jonah said. "I'm telling you the hands are useless without the memory in them. My grandmother sang songs in three languages that exist nowhere else on Earth or off it. You wake your engineers and they'll build you a colony with no songs in it."

And then Tamsin—who had been quiet through all of this in the way of someone doing arithmetic they don't want to finish—Tamsin had said, "The children," and the word fell into the room like an icepick.

"The children can't work," Ashe said.

"No," Tamsin agreed. "They can't. They'll need feeding and teaching and protecting for fifteen years before they're worth anything to anyone. They're the most expensive possible choice." She looked around the hall. "They're also the only choice that isn't triage. Everyone else we're choosing between versions of the past. The children are the only future in those pods."

Silence, then. The bad kind, the kind where everyone is thinking the same terrible thought: *she's right, and it changes nothing.*

Because here was the arithmetic none of us could argue with. Whoever we chose, half the sleepers stayed frozen. And whoever made the choice would have to live with it—and so would whoever heard about it, which, given that I'd spent eleven years broadcasting logs to a system that mostly didn't listen but occasionally did, meant eventually everybody.

It was Jonah who said, bitterly, "So who decides? Him?" jerking his chin at Ashe. "A court order from creditors? Her?" nodding at me. "The caretaker? You?" pointing at Tamsin. "The junior officer having second thoughts?"

And I don't know where it came from. I've thought about it since, tried to trace the thought back to its root, and I can't find one. It was just there, fully grown, the way some ideas arrive—like they'd been waiting in me for eleven years, patient as seeds in cold storage.

"Everyone decides," I said.

* * *

"You cannot be serious," Ashe said.

"One person, one vote," I said. "Every soul on this station. Salvagers included. Descendants included. Me included. No rank. No ownership stakes. No seniority. You get exactly as much say as the youngest deckhand on your crew."

"That's not how law works."

"I know," I said, and something in me went very calm and very cold, colder than the vault, a cold I recognized because I'd lived inside it for four thousand days. "That's rather the point."

He stared at me. I stared back. Around us, forty-odd people in parkas and thermal wraps and, in one case, a descendant named Priya wearing a coat that had clearly been sewn from ship insulation blankets, began—slowly, quietly, without any signal—to move closer. Not threateningly. Just closer. The way water moves toward a drain.

"It's insane," Ashe said again, but softer, and I heard the crack in it.

"Probably," I admitted. "But Administrator, look around. Your court order assumes there's a functioning legal order to enforce it. There's a gravity machine older than your career asking permission to reroute stellar power. There's a coolant line bleeding out in Bay Three. There are half a million people asleep who never consented to be collateral in anyone's dispute. The law abandoned this place eleven years ago. What's left up here—" I spread my arms, taking in the frost, the racks, the frozen dark beyond the windows, "—is just people. So let the people decide."

* * *

We took six hours to prepare, because even improvised justice deserves a little care.

Tamsin built the tally system—of course she did; she'd been running numbers since she boarded, and her hands moved through the station's systems like she'd always lived here. Every person got a token. We didn't have ballots, so we used what the vault offered: seed packets. Three varieties. Wheat for the adults, maize for the elders, amaranth for the children. Amaranth, I should note, was chosen partly for symbolism and partly because it was the packet closest to hand when Priya suggested it, and I have learned over eleven years that most profound decisions are made for reasons that would embarrass their authors.

Each person would drop one packet into one of three storage drums. Sealed. Anonymous. Tamsin insisted on anonymity, and when Jonah bristled—"People should stand behind their choices"—she looked at him with a tiredness that went deeper than the cold and said, "Jonah. Some of Ashe's crew are going to vote against him. Do you want them doing that with his eyes on their backs?"

Jonah shut up. So did the rest of us.

We fed everyone first. This mattered more than I expected it to. There's a thing that happens when strangers share food in a freezing room—the shoulders come down a centimeter, maybe two, and conversation becomes possible across the lines. I opened the galley stores I'd rationed for a decade and made the only thing I know how to make in quantity: a barley-and-lentil stew seasoned with freeze-dried herbs from Rack 44, the culinary heritage collection, basil and thyme bred before the Launch Failure by people who believed someone would someday taste them.

Ashe ate standing apart, cup in both hands, staring at the cryo-bay readout. But he ate. I watched his junior officers eat together, laughing once—a short, startled laugh, like they'd forgotten the machinery of it—and I understood that whatever happened with the vote, something had already shifted on his ship. Loyalty is a temperature too, and theirs was dropping.

* * *

Before the vote, I asked to speak. Just a minute. Nobody objected, possibly because nobody had the energy.

"I've recorded four thousand two hundred and sixty-one daily logs," I said. "For eleven years I've talked to five hundred thousand people who couldn't answer. I told myself it was duty. Honestly, some days it was just loneliness with a schedule." A ripple of something—not quite laughter, but adjacent to it—moved through the hall. "So believe me when I tell you I understand the temptation to decide *for* the sleepers. To be their voice. It's seductive. It makes you feel important instead of helpless.

"But they aren't voiceless. They wrote down what they wanted, before they froze. It's in the manifest files, in their own words, hundreds of thousands of statements of intent. And do you know what the most common sentence is? Not 'build me a colony.' Not 'avenge me.' The most common sentence, across half a million files, is some version of: *'Tell my grandchildren I loved them.'*

"We are not their grandchildren. But the children in those pods will be somebody's. And if we wake the elders, we preserve the past. If we wake the skilled, we preserve the means. If we wake the children—" my voice caught, and I let it, because pretending otherwise would have been a lie, "—we preserve the *telling*. Someone has to survive long enough to deliver the message."

I stepped back. Tamsin uncapped the first drum.

* * *

The voting itself took forty minutes and was, in its way, the quietest hour of my life.

People came forward one at a time. No speeches—that was the rule, and remarkably, everyone kept it, even Jonah, whose whole personality is a speech looking for a venue. Each person stood before the three drums, held their seed packet a moment, and chose.

I watched faces. I couldn't help it. The salvager called Deng, a big quiet man who'd spent the standoff repairing our failing air scrubbers without being asked—he hesitated longest of anyone, wheat packet in one hand, amaranth in the other, and finally dropped the amaranth and walked away fast, not looking at his commander.

Priya voted children and pressed her palm flat to the drum afterward, briefly, like a blessing.

One of the descendants, an old man named Ekwueme who had crossed the system at seventy-three years old to claim a grandmother he'd never met, voted elders. "Forgive me," he murmured, to no one, to everyone. "But someone must remember how to grieve properly."

Ashe voted last. Of course he did. He stood before the drums for a long time, and I saw something pass over his face that I hadn't seen in him before—not calculation, not menace, but genuine grief. Whatever else Corven Ashe was, he had once been a man who believed in the order he served, and watching that belief die visibly in public is its own kind of execution.

He dropped the wheat packet in the adult drum. Then, after a pause that lasted three full seconds—which in a silent room is a geological era—he dropped a second packet, an amaranth, into the children's drum.

Tamsin saw it. I saw her see it. Neither of us said anything. Some votes are confessions.

* * *

The count, when Tamsin finished, she announced in a voice that shook exactly once:

"Amaranth. The children. Eighteen votes."

Pause. The frost ticked on the racks. Somewhere in Bay Two, a pump cycled.

"Wheat. Skilled adults. Thirteen votes."

Another pause.

"Maize. The elders. Ten votes."

Eighteen of forty-one. Not a mandate. Not a landslide. A plurality, purchased one anonymous seed packet at a time by people who would all have to live inside the consequences. Imperfect. Improvised. Real.

"Ashe's crew," Tamsin continued, because she is thorough to the marrow, "voted five to one for the children. Administrator, your own people—"

"I heard the count, Okafor." His voice was flat and gray. He looked around the hall—at Deng, who wouldn't meet his eyes; at the young comms officer whose name I still didn't know, who was crying silently and furiously; at the whole shivering assembly of a justice that had no statute behind it and didn't need one. "Five to one," he repeated softly. "Eleven years I've done this work. Do you know how many asset recoveries I've run? Thirty. Thirty clean ones. Nobody ever put it to a *vote* before."

"Maybe that's why they were clean," Tamsin said.

He laughed then—one short, broken note, nothing like humor. "You have no idea what's coming when the grid answers you. When the pulses start channeling power and my backers see their cores lighting up for a bunch of eight-year-olds—" He stopped himself. Looked at me. Something moved behind his eyes, some final door either opening or closing; I genuinely could not tell which. "Get your authorization ready, Whitlock. Because when ECHO ANEW wakes up, you're going to need every friend in this room. And I promise you—" his gaze swept the seed racks, the frost, the sleeping dark beyond the hull, "—you're going to need friends you haven't met yet."

"What does that mean?" Jonah demanded.

Ashe didn't answer. He pulled his collar up, turned, and walked alone toward the cryo-bay, past the pods, past the window where Ada's empty berth still bore the small paper star I'd taped there after writing her death certificate—a star one of the children's descendants had folded and left for her, though none of us knew which one.

Tamsin finished sealing the drums. Then she unpinned her commission badge, set it on top of the children's drum like a paperweight on a treaty, and said, to me, quietly:

"So. How do we introduce half a million kids to a solar system that forgot their grandparents existed?"

Outside the viewport, far off and getting nearer, the stellar grid flickered—once, twice—like something enormous turning over in its sleep, waiting to be told yes.

I picked up the microphone.

"This is Mirabel Whitlock, Svalbard-2, logging day four thousand two hundred sixty-two," I said. "Today we voted. Today the children won. And now I'm going to do the thing this whole station was built for, and pray I've understood the question correctly."

My finger hovered over the authorization key—the same key Dr. Reyes-Mbeki had sealed in a dead woman's testament, waiting decades for a hand brave enough or foolish enough to press it.

Somewhere beneath the ice, ECHO ANEW pulsed. Once. Like a heartbeat asking a question.

I pressed send.
