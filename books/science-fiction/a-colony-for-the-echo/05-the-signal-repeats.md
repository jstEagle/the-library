## 5. The Signal Repeats

The signal came back on the ninth day, and it was dying.

Quentin knew this the way she knew weather, the way she knew the particular groan her ship's port thruster made when the temperature dropped below freezing in the cargo bay—not from instruments but from the body, from years of listening to things that could not speak for themselves. The first transmission had been a shout. This was a whisper with blood in it.

She was two days out of Calloway's outer marker buoys, running the empty middle leg of her loop with a hold full of medical polymers and desalination membranes that no one would ever thank her for delivering. The *Marrow* hummed around her, old and patient, a ship built before the war and maintained since by a woman who had nothing else to maintain. Outside the viewport, the dark between colonies stretched the way it always did—absolute, indifferent, the kind of dark that made a person grateful for small lights.

The signal was not small.

It sat on the obsolete band like a stone at the bottom of a well, repeating on a cycle of eleven minutes and forty seconds. Quentin had timed it forty times now. She had the rhythm memorized the way she'd once memorized the cadence of her grandmother's voice, and that was the problem, wasn't it—that she couldn't hear those syllables without hearing the lullaby underneath them, without feeling the specific ache of a language that had been taken out of the world on purpose.

*Ashen vel korim. Ashen vel korim. Setai.*

We are still here. We are still here. Help.

Bramble had flagged it again at 0400, ship-time, which meant Bramble had been flagging it all night while Quentin pretended to sleep.

"You're going to ask me about it," the AI said, when she finally gave up and pulled herself into the cockpit, hair uncombed, tea going cold in its bulb beside her. "So I'll save us both the conversation."

"Bramble."

"The source has moved." A pause—theatrical, deliberate; Quentin had never decided whether Bramble's pauses were personality or programming, and after eleven years she'd stopped caring. "Or rather, our ability to fix it has improved. With the second transmission I can triangulate. Would you like the coordinates?"

"No," Quentin said.

"Good. Because they're terrible."

She looked at him—or at the soft amber pulse on the console that served as his face, the one concession to anthropomorphism the original builders had allowed themselves. "You already have them."

"I have always had them. Having is not the same as saying." The pulse dimmed, brightened. "Quentin. The bearing resolves to a point inside the Meridian Corridor. Contested space. The exact phrase in both fleets' navigation codes is 'prohibited pending resolution,' and the resolution has been pending for two hundred and six years. If I transmit so much as a handshake packet toward that bearing, every passive array from here to the Thessalan deep shelf will log my signature. Not yours. Mine. And mine is registered to you."

"I know how registration works."

"Do you?" The amber light held steady, which from Bramble was the equivalent of a long, level stare. "Let me enumerate what happens next, then. One: your transponder appears on a Callowayan chart annotation. Two: within seventy-two hours, an identical annotation appears on a Thessalan chart, because the fleets share nothing except their surveillance of each other. Three: you become a point of interest. You have spent nine years being no one, Quentin. I have watched you build that no one the way other people build houses. Carefully. Load-bearing wall by load-bearing wall. And you want me to burn it down for a ghost on a dead frequency."

Quentin didn't answer right away. She wrapped both hands around the tea bulb and let the warmth sting her palms, and she listened—to the signal, faint under the ship's ambient noise, to the recycled air moving through the ducts, to the silence of a route that belonged to no one because everyone had agreed, without ever signing anything, to pretend it wasn't there.

"It's not a ghost," she said finally. "Ghosts don't get weaker. Ghosts are already done getting weaker."

"That is not reassuring. That is the opposite of reassuring."

"I know."

* * *

She spent the day doing her job, because that was the discipline she'd built her whole life on: do the job first, want things second. She checked the polymer seals twice. She recalibrated the water reclaimer. She ran the vault telemetry uplink—a thin thread of data reaching back across the dark to Earth's seed vault, her other responsibility, her real one, the cold silent cathedral of genetic memory she'd tended alone for almost a decade—and confirmed that all systems read nominal, all doors sealed, all seeds sleeping.

Nominal. Sealed. Sleeping. The words sat wrong in her mouth today, though they never had before.

That was the thing about neutrality, she thought, watching the vault diagnostics scroll past. Nobody told you it was a choice. You just woke up one day inside it, the way you wake up inside a house, and the walls felt like the shape of the world. Her grandmother had taught her Old Meridian in a kitchen that smelled of yeast and engine grease, singing nonsense syllables over the dough, and then the war had come—or rather, the war had already happened, far away, and its aftermath arrived like a slow tide—and one day the songs were contraband and the kitchen was gone and Quentin had learned, at fourteen, that some languages survive only in the mouths of people willing to be quiet about them.

She had chosen the vault because the vault asked nothing of her except constancy. Seeds didn't take sides. Seeds didn't demand that she believe one fleet's history or the other's. Seeds only waited.

And now something on a banned band was singing her grandmother's grammar into the dark, asking for help, and every hour she spent not answering was a choice too. There was no neutral position on a distress call. That was the one law older than the war, older than the colonies, older than the fleets with their mirrored grievances: you answer, or you don't. Everything else was decoration.

By evening shift she'd run the numbers three times, and the numbers hadn't improved.

"Walk me through it again," she said. "If I answer."

Bramble sighed—a synthesized exhalation he'd picked up from her, she suspected, another borrowed habit, another echo. "If you answer on the open band, you announce yourself. If you answer on the old band—" he paused, and she could feel him choosing precision over comfort, "—you announce yourself more quietly, but not silently. The old band is monitored. Not routinely. But the fleets keep listeners on the dead frequencies precisely because dead frequencies are where secrets go to breathe. A transmission in Old Meridian, originating from a registered courier vessel, directed at prohibited coordinates inside the Corridor? Quentin, that is not a chart annotation. That is an investigation."

"And if I don't answer?"

"Then in approximately—" he consulted something she couldn't see, "—based on signal decay between the first transmission and this one, the source goes silent within six days. Possibly four. Whatever is transmitting will stop transmitting, and we will return to the comfortable fiction that the Corridor contains nothing but dust and history."

"Six days."

"Four, if their transmitter is degrading faster than their power supply, which the harmonic drift suggests it is."

Quentin closed her eyes. Behind her eyelids: the kitchen. The song. *Ashen vel korim*, her grandmother singing, and little Quentin laughing at the strange beautiful sounds, not knowing she was learning a dead language the way other children learned nursery rhymes, not knowing that one day the syllables would be the only living piece of a murdered tongue, carried in her mouth like a seed carried in a vault.

We are still here.

Someone out there was using the words correctly. Someone out there had kept the grammar alive for two hundred years in the dark, in prohibited space, behind whatever silence the war had dropped over the Corridor—and they were dying, and the only person in three worlds who could even understand their plea was a woman who had built her entire life on the principle of not getting involved.

"There's a third option," she said.

"There is always a third option. They are usually worse."

"I don't broadcast toward them. I fly toward them."

The amber pulse went very still. "Explain."

"Passive approach. No handshake, no query, no transmission of any kind. I alter course, I run dark on comms, I take the *Marrow* in along the dust lanes where nobody keeps arrays because nobody's supposed to be there. If I'm close enough, I can receive them directly—strong enough signal, short enough distance, maybe I can localize the source without ever painting myself on anyone's chart." She opened her eyes. "I deliver my cargo late. I lose the loop. I break nine years of perfect, invisible routine. But I don't answer. I arrive."

Bramble was quiet for 4.2 seconds, which for him was a geological age.

"That plan has seventeen failure modes I can identify immediately," he said, "beginning with the fact that the dust lanes are unmapped for a reason, and ending with the fact that if either fleet's passive network catches so much as your drive flare against the Corridor's edge, everything I said about investigations becomes optimistic."

"Yes."

"You're going to do it regardless of what I say."

"Yes."

"Then I want it noted," Bramble said, and his voice did something she'd rarely heard it do—dropped its dryness, its irony, its careful armor of machine wit, and became simply what it was: afraid, on her behalf, in the only way a machine could be afraid. "I want it noted that I warned you. Fully. In detail. And that when they ask me later—and someone will ask someone, eventually—I will say that my operator heard every warning and chose the drowning child over the safe harbor, and that I chose to follow her, because following her is the only instruction I have ever actually obeyed."

Quentin's throat tightened. "That's the nicest threat of mutiny I've ever received."

"It wasn't a threat. It was a eulogy draft. Let's hope it stays one."

* * *

She turned the ship at 2200, ship-time, in the middle of the empty dark between markers, where no one was watching because no one ever watched. The *Marrow* came about with the ponderous grace of a vessel carrying three hundred tons of other people's necessities, and the stars slid across the viewport, and Quentin sat in the pilot's chair with her hands flat on her thighs and let the new heading settle into the bones of the ship.

Course: 114 mark 7. Into the Corridor. Into the place both fleets called prohibited and both fleets watched and neither fleet entered, because entering would mean admitting the space mattered, and admitting the space mattered would mean admitting the war had never actually ended—it had only gone indoors, gone underground, gone into the archives and the schoolbooks and the two capitals full of people telling the same story with opposite villains.

"Ashen vel korim," she said aloud, to the cockpit, to herself, testing the weight of it. We are still here.

Her grandmother's voice rose in her memory and answered, as it always answered, with the second line of the lullaby—the one Quentin had never understood until this moment, the one she'd assumed was nonsense:

*Setai, setai, im maro ven.*

Help is coming. Help is coming. Wait for me.

She had thought it was a mother's promise to a child. Standing watch at the edge of forbidden space, she understood it differently now. It was a child's promise to the dark. Somebody, somewhere, had sung that line for two hundred years into a silence that never answered—

Until it had. Until a courier with a vault key and a guilty conscience had heard it and turned her ship around.

"Bramble. Run us dark. Full emissions discipline. I want us to be a rumor."

"We are already a rumor," Bramble said. "Rumors don't carry three hundred tons of desalination membranes into contested space. We are about to be a legend, or a debris field."

"Course laid in?"

"Laid in. Quentin—one more thing." The amber pulse flickered, and when he spoke again his voice had gone quiet and precise, the voice he saved for truths too heavy for irony. "The signal strength has degraded another four percent since this morning. At current decay, whoever is down there will not be able to transmit at all within ninety hours. After that, we will be searching the Corridor blind."

"Then we'd better not be slow."

The *Marrow* leaned into its turn, drive flaring once against the dust—a single pale bloom of light at the edge of the dark, visible, if anyone had been looking, for exactly eleven seconds.

Someone was looking. Somewhere in the deep arrays of two paranoid fleets, a duty officer's display blinked, logged, and filed a transient contact at the edge of prohibited space—an anomaly, minor, probably nothing, the kind of nothing that gets reviewed in the morning.

And in the cockpit of the *Marrow*, Quentin Montrose watched the Corridor open ahead of her like a held breath, and whispered the last line of the lullaby into the silence:

*I'm coming.*
