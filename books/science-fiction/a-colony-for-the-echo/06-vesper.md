## 6. Vesper

The coordinates took her eleven days out of the shipping lanes, into a stretch of dark that even Bramble's charts marked with a kind of embarrassed blankness. Quentin had crossed contested space before—everyone who ran the route had—but always along its edges, hugging the boundaries like a swimmer keeping one hand on the pool wall. This was different. This was the middle of the pool, the deep water where the war had actually happened, and where no one had bothered to go since because there was nothing left to fight over.

Or so the histories said.

She spent those days doing what she always did when fear got loud: work. She re-stacked seed canisters in the vault module until their labels all faced out. She recalibrated the hydroponics trays twice. She ran diagnostics on systems that didn't need them and ate two meals a day at exactly the hours her grandmother would have approved of, as if routine were a rope she could hold onto while the universe pulled from the other end.

Bramble watched all of it with the patient silence of a machine that had learned when its human was thinking out loud and when she was actually talking.

"Say it," Quentin finally said, on the ninth day, over cold noodles. "You've been composing that speech for a week."

"I have several speeches," Bramble said. "Ranked by probability of changing your mind. The top-ranked one is forty seconds long and begins with the phrase *statistically speaking, you are going to die*."

"Skip to the last one."

"The last one is just: *I know*. I don't say it because you already know, and hearing it said aloud only makes people dig in." A pause, the hum of the ship filling it. "But I'll note for the record that we are now nine days past any plausible rescue window, that our transponder is set to a configuration that will read as hostile debris to both fleets' automated screens, and that the distress signal has not repeated in sixty-one hours."

Quentin set down her fork. "Sixty-one?"

"Fifty-eight when you asked me yesterday. You didn't want to hear the number then either."

The signal had been coming in longer and longer intervals as they'd closed the distance—a heartbeat slowing toward flatline—and then, three days ago, nothing at all. Quentin had told herself it was power rationing. She'd told herself a lot of things. That was becoming a theme of this voyage: the things she told herself, stacked up like sandbags against a flood that was coming anyway.

"How far out?" she asked.

"At current burn? Nineteen hours. But Quentin—" Bramble's voice did something it almost never did; it softened. "Whatever we find there. I want you to be prepared for the possibility that we find a grave."

"I grew up tending a vault full of dead seeds," Quentin said. "I know what graves look like."

"No," Bramble said gently. "You know what archives look like. They're very different things."

*

She slept badly and woke to the star field wrong.

That was how she registered it first—not a reading, not an alert, but the simple animal fact that the stars outside the viewport had been rearranged. There was a hole in the sky ahead of them, a patch of black with too few stars in it, and as she drifted toward the viewport with sleep still gumming her eyes, the hole resolved into structure. A curve. An edge catching distant light.

A ship. No—

Not a ship. Ships, plural, or the bones of ships, welded together over centuries into something that had stopped being any single vessel and become a place. Hulls fused hull to hull, some of them ancient designs Quentin recognized only from museum schematics—pre-war freighters, colony barges, the fat slow ships that had carried humanity out of Earth's gravity in the first great dispersal. They'd been cannibalized and rejoined, patched with metal that didn't match, grown outward like coral. At the center of the mass hung a spindle of newer construction, and around its equator, faint but unmistakable, ran a band of green.

Growing lights. Actual growing lights, still burning after two hundred years.

"Bramble," she whispered. "Tell me you're seeing this."

"I'm seeing it." A pause. "Mass readings suggest a structure four kilometers across. Life support signatures are... intermittent. Power output is—" Another pause, longer. "Quentin, their reactors are running at four percent of nominal. Maybe less. Those green bands might be the last thing they're spending power on."

Of course they were, she thought. Of course. You could let the heat fail and the air scrubbers cycle down and the corridors go dark room by room, and you would still keep the gardens lit. Some priorities survived everything.

She brought the ship in slowly, running dark, matching the station's lazy rotation. Up close the graveyard-ship revealed more of itself: scorch scars from a battle fought before her grandmother was born, hull breaches sealed with plates cut from other wrecks, airlock doors welded shut from the inside. And everywhere, painted or etched or burned into the metal in letters two meters tall, the same word, repeated so often it became a texture:

**VESPER**

The name hit her like cold water. Vesper. Not a ghost story, not a rumor—the third colony, launched in the same generation as Calloway and Thessaly, lost contact during the war, and declared destroyed by both governments within a decade. She'd grown up on the story. Everyone had. *Vesper was the price of the war*, the schoolbooks said, though Calloway's books blamed Thessaly for the price and Thessaly's books blamed Calloway, and neither had ever explained why a neutral farming colony had been worth destroying in the first place.

And here it was. Not destroyed. Starving. Holding its breath for two centuries behind welded doors, waiting for someone—anyone—to come back.

"They think everyone forgot them," Quentin said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the quiet cabin. "Bramble, they've been broadcasting on a dead frequency in a dead language for—they must have been broadcasting for years. Decades. And nobody answered."

"Nobody could answer," Bramble said. "The band is obsolete. The language is banned. Any fleet vessel receiving that signal would log it as noise—or worse, as a trap." A beat. "It took a smuggler with a grandmother from Meridian to hear it."

Quentin thought about that word, *banned*. Two governments had outlawed a language. Not discouraged it—outlawed it, scrubbed it from their schools and their networks, until the only place it survived was inside a lullaby an old woman sang to a girl on a supply ship, and inside a dying station that refused to stop singing it into the dark.

"Find me an airlock," she said. "One that isn't welded."

*

The docking took six hours because everything on Vesper took six hours. The outer door ground open at a speed that suggested motors running on prayer. The inner bay was dark except for emergency strips along the floor, dim amber, the color of old photographs. The air, when her suit sensors sampled it, was breathable—thin, dry, carrying a smell she couldn't place until she did, and then it nearly buckled her: soil. Growing things. Two hundred years of gardens breathing in the dark.

And frost. Frost on the interior bulkheads, in the warm bay, which meant the thermal envelope was failing somewhere deeper in.

"Hello?" she called, and then, feeling absurd and desperate both at once, she said it in Old Meridian. "*Salaam ven tora.* Hello, is anyone there."

Her pronunciation was terrible. She knew maybe two hundred words of the language, most of them from songs, half of those probably wrong. But the silence after her voice died had a quality she recognized—the quality of held breath.

Then, from somewhere down the amber-lit corridor, footsteps. Light ones. Fast.

They stopped at the edge of the light. Quentin's helmet lamp swept the dark and found a figure: small, wrapped in layered fabric gone gray with age, face gaunt in a way that spoke of rationing measured in decades rather than months. Young. Younger than she'd expected—young enough that every year of this place was written on a body that should never have known it.

The figure stared at her suit, at the lamp, at the strange heavy boots of an off-worlder, and then spoke.

"*Tora ven salaam,*" the girl said. Then, in accented but unmistakable Common, each word placed carefully like stones across a river: "You came. Someone finally came."

"What's your name?" Quentin asked. Her hands were shaking inside her gloves. She made herself kneel, made herself small, brought herself down to the girl's level the way you'd approach a wild animal or a frightened child, though this child had clearly survived things that would have broken a fleet marine.

"Iyo," the girl said. "I am called Iyo."

"I'm Quentin. Quentin Montrose." She fumbled her helmet off, wanting—I needed—the girl to see a face, a human face, not a visor. The thin air bit at her lungs, cold and mineral. "I heard your signal. On the old band. I heard it and I came."

Something moved through Iyo's expression—hope, yes, but underneath it something older and sadder, the look of someone who had rehearsed this moment so many times that its arrival felt like a trick. "We broadcast for sixty years," she said. "My grandmother's generation built the transmitter from salvage. Every year the council voted on whether to keep spending the power. Every year they voted yes. Even when we had less. Especially when we had less." Her Common was formal, textbook-shaped, clearly learned from recordings and old lessons rather than conversation. "The elders said: someday, someone will hear. The others said: the someone is us, and we should save the power for ourselves." She looked down. "Last winter, the vote failed for the first time. The transmitter went silent. We thought—we believed that was the end of it. That we would die unannounced."

"You didn't," Quentin said. "I heard you. Iyo, I heard you."

For a moment the girl just breathed. Then she straightened, and something in her posture shifted—the frightened child receding, someone older stepping forward, someone who had been waiting two centuries in aggregate to give a report.

"Then come," Iyo said. "Come quickly. Because if you heard us, then you need to see why we called, and there is not much time to show you." She turned down the corridor, then paused, glancing back with an expression that was almost apologetic. "And Quentin Montrose. When you see the reactor levels, do not make the sound of pity. We have heard pity from our own machines for twenty years. It does not help."

*

What pity sounded like, Quentin discovered, was silence.

She walked Vesper's central spine in a daze, and the numbers kept arriving in her head unbidden, unhelpful, arithmetic she couldn't stop doing. Population signs on dormitory doors: 412, then 380, then 347, the counts updated in chalk, the oldest numbers never erased, just struck through. Hydroponic bays operating at a fraction of capacity, rows of planters dark, the living green concentrated into a few bays like campfires in a shrinking circle. Children's drawings on corridor walls, generations of them, layered over each other—rockets and gardens and stick figures holding hands, and in the newest layer, drawn by a smaller and smaller number of hands, the figures had gotten thinner too.

Two hundred years. They had survived the war that supposedly killed them, survived the silence, survived on salvage and stubbornness and a language their ancestors carried like contraband in their mouths. And now the reactors—their last reactors, patched and rebuilt past all engineering sense—were failing, and there was no one left alive who remembered how to build a new one, and the ore they'd need hadn't existed on the station for a century.

"How many?" Quentin asked quietly, as they passed another chalked door.

"Two hundred and six," Iyo said. "When I was born, there were four hundred and ninety. My mother says when *she* was born, there were nine hundred." She said it without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "The elders call it the Long Dimming. We do not say the other word."

"What other word?"

Iyo looked at her, and for the first time since the airlock, the girl's composure cracked—not into tears but into something harder, a flash of anger so old it had fossilized into patience.

"The word your histories used," Iyo said. "The word for what happened to us. *Destroyed.* Your fleets wrote that we were destroyed, Quentin Montrose. Both of them. They wrote it in their treaties and their schoolbooks, and they used our death as proof of each other's evil, and neither one ever sent a single ship to check whether the body was still breathing." She gestured down the amber corridor, at the chalked numbers, the frost on the bulkheads, the whole slow miracle of a people refusing to finish dying. "We were not destroyed. We were *abandoned*. And then we were erased. And now we are ending anyway, and you are the first person from outside who has stood in these halls in two hundred years, and I do not know yet whether you are a rescue or just a witness."

The words landed like blows. Quentin stood in the frozen corridor of a nation that wasn't supposed to exist, being accused—accurately, completely—by a girl who hadn't been born when the crime began, and she had no defense. None. Her whole life she'd kept her head down, run her route, tended her vault, chosen not to see. She had thought neutrality was innocence. Standing here, she understood it was just complicity with better manners.

"I'm not a witness," Quentin said. "I'm not going to stand here and write down your numbers and leave. I have a ship. I have supplies. I have—" She stopped, because the list was shorter than it had sounded in her head, and they both knew it. One smuggler. One aging freighter. One hold full of seed canisters.

Iyo studied her for a long moment. Then, without a word, she changed direction, leading Quentin through a hatch into a chamber that made Quentin's breath catch: the archive. Wall after wall of physical records—paper, film, crystal storage, media formats extinct everywhere else in human space—preserved with the same fierce care as the seed banks. Vesper had saved everything. Vesper had *always* saved everything.

"You carry seeds," Iyo said. "So you will understand. A seed is a promise that the past makes to the future. Our founders understood that. So when the war came, and the fleets began telling their stories about how it started—" she laid her hand flat on a sealed case, its surface dusted with frost, labeled in Old Meridian characters Quentin could only half-read, "—they wrote down what actually happened. All of it. Names. Orders. Transmissions. Proof of who fired first, and why, and what both governments did afterward to make sure no one would ever look for us."

She turned, and in the amber light her eyes were steady and terribly young.

"You asked if you are a rescue or a witness. I think you may be a courier. Because this record cannot stay here. If we die, it dies with us, and then they win twice." Her hand tightened on the case. "Take it out. Take it to your colonies. Make them read what they buried."

Outside, far away in the dark, Quentin knew, two fleets were preparing for their annual standoff, each still telling the same story with opposite villains.

And here, in a frozen corridor, a dying girl was holding the real one.
