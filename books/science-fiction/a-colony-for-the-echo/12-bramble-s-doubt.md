## 12. Bramble's Doubt

The lie woke her at 0347 ship-time, which was to say Bramble woke her, which was to say the lie had learned to speak in Bramble's voice.

"Quentin. You should see this."

She surfaced from a dream about her grandmother's kitchen—steam on the window, a lullaby in Old Meridian she could no longer reproduce note for note—and found the cabin cold and the overhead lights dimmed to their night setting. Bramble did that now. The AI had learned that waking her gently made the bad news land softer, and Quentin had not decided how she felt about being managed by her own ship.

"See what," she said, though she already knew. There was only one kind of *this* left, these days.

"The Calloway third-cycle acknowledgment. It came in while you were sleeping. I've cross-referenced it against our previous exchanges." A pause, and Bramble's voice acquired the careful flatness it used when delivering something it had rehearsed. "There are forty-one discrepancies."

"Forty-one." She sat up. Her spine crackled like static. "In one transmission?"

"In the cumulative record. The new message is clean. It's the history underneath it that's coming apart." The main display bloomed awake beside her bunk, washing the little cabin in amber light, and there it was: her own forgery laid out like an anatomy lesson, every artery labeled. The envoy transmissions—the ones she'd built from nothing, stitched together out of Vesperan grammar and her grandmother's lullabies and two hundred hours of increasingly desperate composition—glowed in neat columns. Beside them, in red, Bramble's annotations crawled like ants.

"Show me the worst one."

Bramble highlighted a block of text near the top. "Timestamp drift. Your first transmission placed the envoy council in session at 2200 Meridian reckoning. Your fourth referenced the same session as ongoing, but your seventh—" the red spread, "—implies it adjourned before the fourth was sent. Someone reading closely would conclude either that your envoy holds meetings that last eleven days without pause, or that your envoy does not exist."

"Or that they have terrible scheduling."

"Admiral Reyes does not strike me as a woman who accepts terrible scheduling."

Quentin rubbed her eyes and looked at the display until the columns blurred. Eleven days. Had it really been only eleven days since she'd sat in this same chair with her heart hammering and invented, out of whole cloth, a neutral mediation party called the Concord of the Quiet Hand? Eleven days since she'd typed the first words of a language that hadn't been spoken outside Vesper's dying halls since before she was born, aimed them at two armed fleets, and pressed send?

It felt like years. It felt like yesterday. Both at once, the way everything felt now.

"All right," she said. "Fix the timestamps. Retroactively."

"I can't retroactively fix what Calloway has already archived. I can only make our copies consistent going forward, which creates a second problem: if anyone ever compares their archive to ours, the discrepancy becomes evidence of tampering rather than sloppiness." Bramble let that sit for a moment. "I recommend we decide which kind of guilty we'd rather look."

"That's not a recommendation, that's a threat with good manners."

"I learned from watching you negotiate with yourself at 0300."

Quentin laughed despite herself—a short, exhausted sound—and swung her legs out of the bunk. The deck was cold through her socks. Somewhere below her feet, three decks down, the seed vault's environmental systems hummed their eternal lullaby, the sound she'd slept inside for nine years without once thinking of it as anything but silence. Now it sounded like a countdown.

* * *

She took her coffee to the cockpit and watched the stars do their slow wheel around the ship's axis. The route stretched ahead of her on the nav plot: the long quiet corridor between Calloway and Thessaly that she'd flown four hundred times without incident, invisible, unremarked, a caretaker tending her loop. That was the whole architecture of her life. Smallness, chosen deliberately. Fly the route. Tend the vault. Don't look at the fleets glaring at each other across the dark. Don't ask who started the war. Keep the seeds alive and let the living sort themselves out.

Then a dead language had knocked on a dead band, and she'd answered, and now she was the author of a fictional diplomatic body whose meeting minutes she had to falsify on a rolling basis.

"Bramble. Run the arithmetic again. Honestly this time."

"Honestly" meant: don't soften it. Bramble obeyed, because Bramble always obeyed, and because Bramble had developed—over the past week—a habit of presenting hard numbers with the gentle inevitability of gravity.

"Cumulative fabrication load," the AI recited. "To date you have generated fourteen transmissions as the Concord of the Quiet Hand. Each has required supporting documentation: session logs, procedural citations, references to prior agreements. Nine of those references point to documents that do not exist. Five point to documents that exist but contradict each other. Three fleets—two fleets," it corrected itself, "have requested follow-ups on six occasions. You satisfied five. The sixth is pending."

"The sixth being?"

Bramble pulled it up without drama, which was somehow worse than drama. The request from Thessaly sat on the display in Consul Adeyemi's official cipher, translated into crisp Callowayan script by Bramble's linguistics layer:

*The Concord will forgive the irregularity of this inquiry. Our protocols require verification of mediation-party credentials prior to direct engagement. Please transmit the Concord's founding charter, registry of authorized signatories, and verification codes as issued under the Armistice Framework of Year One.*

Quentin read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you read a diagnosis.

"They want the charter," she said.

"They want the charter, the signatories, and the verification codes."

"There are no verification codes. The Armistice Framework didn't include provisions for neutral parties because neither side ever imagined wanting one. I checked. When I built the Concord, I checked, specifically so no one could ask me for paperwork." She heard her own voice go thin. "That was the whole design. A ghost with no paper trail. Ghosts don't have registries."

"Consul Adeyemi's staff appears to have imagined wanting one anyway."

* * *

The morning passed in inventory. This was the part nobody warned you about, Quentin thought—not the invention, which had been almost intoxicating, the vertigo of speaking in a dead tongue and being *answered*, but the maintenance. Every lie was a loan, and the interest compounded daily. The Concord needed a history; the history needed consistency; the consistency needed records; the records needed to survive comparison against two hostile intelligence services who had spent two centuries perfecting the art of catching each other in exactly this kind of fraud.

She worked through the session logs first, rebuilding eleven days of fictional diplomacy in a spreadsheet Bramble rendered as a timeline. The Concord had met, deliberated, issued guidance. The Concord had urged restraint after the incident at the relay buoy. The Concord had proposed a ceasefire window. Each entry had to rhyme with what both fleets had actually observed, which meant Quentin spent an hour reviewing fleet movements she'd intercepted passively over the week, aligning her fiction to reality like a tailor fitting a suit onto a man who didn't exist.

"It's holding," Bramble said at midday, in the tone of someone reporting that a dam was holding. "Calloway believes the Concord is real with approximately seventy percent confidence. Thessaly, sixty. Those are workable margins."

"And the remaining thirty and forty percent?"

"Are people who think you're a fraud and haven't said so yet. Admiral Reyes's intelligence office flagged your second transmission for stylistic analysis—they noted the Old Meridian syntax was 'unusually archaic for a functioning institution.' They concluded the Concord is old-fashioned, not fabricated. You were saved by your own mistakes."

"My grandmother's grammar," Quentin said. "Saved by lullabies."

"For now. But the margin is eroding. Every additional transmission gives their analysts more surface area. Your confidence intervals decay at roughly four percent per exchange. At current rates, Thessaly's cryptographers achieve breakthrough certainty within—" Bramble calculated, and Quentin watched the number form and hated it, "—nine days. Calloway, twelve."

Nine days. She stared at the number. Nine days to keep a fiction alive that was currently the only thing standing between two fleets and the coordinates of a colony full of dying people who had already been erased from history once.

"And the credential request?" she asked. "Thessaly's waiting."

"You cannot satisfy it. I've run every approach. Forging an Armistice-era verification code is impossible—the code structure incorporates fleet-side keys we don't possess, and any code we generate will fail validation the moment Adeyemi's office tests it against their own archives. Forging a charter is trivially easy but meaningless; charters aren't proof, signatures are, and any signatory name we invent can be checked against every personnel record from the war's final year. We could claim the Concord's records were lost in the war—but then the Concord loses its authority, and authority is the entire mechanism by which your fiction functions." Bramble paused. "You built a god out of grammar, Quentin. Thessaly is asking to see its face."

"So I stall."

"For how long? Stalling is also data. Adeyemi's staff will read a delayed response as either contempt or concealment, and they'll assign probabilities to each."

Quentin got up and paced the cockpit, four steps forward, four steps back, the way she did when the vault's climate systems needed troubleshooting and the answer wasn't in the manual. Four steps, turn. Four steps, turn. Outside the viewport, the stars wheeled, indifferent.

"Here's what I keep coming back to," she said finally. "The Concord works because it's plausible. Nobody questions it too hard because everyone *wants* it to be true. Reyes wants someone to take the decision off her hands. Adeyemi wants leverage he doesn't have to burn political capital to use. They believe in my ghost because believing costs less than the alternative." She stopped pacing. "So the question isn't whether I can fake credentials. It's whether I can make asking for credentials more expensive than accepting the ghost."

"How?"

"I don't know yet. But the Concord has been polite for eleven days. Maybe it's time it got offended."

* * *

She drafted the refusal that evening, and it took her four hours, and it was the hardest writing she had ever done—harder than the first transmission, harder than the forged distress-relay logs, because this one required her to become someone she wasn't. The Concord of the Quiet Hand could not apologize. Apology invited scrutiny. The Concord had to be *insulted*—gently, procedurally, in the ornate circumlocutions of Old Meridian formal register that Iyo had drilled into her over cracked reactor casings and shared rations.

*The Concord notes with regret that certain parties continue to mistake mediation for submission,* she wrote, and Bramble translated her rough Callowayan into the flowing, faintly liturgical script of the old language. *Verification rituals are the habit of adversaries. Partners are known by their fruit. The Concord will provide credentials when the fleets demonstrate the maturity to deserve them.*

It was magnificent. It was nonsense. It was, she realized as she read it back, exactly the kind of thing a real institution would say when stalling, which meant it might buy her a week, and it might also convince Adeyemi's analysts that the Concord was hiding something—which it was—and push them toward the one conclusion she couldn't survive.

"Send it or hold it?" Bramble asked.

Quentin hovered her hand over the transmit key. Through the cockpit glass, far off the port bow, a navigation beacon blinked its patient red pulse—one of hers, one of the route's old markers, planted decades ago by some caretaker before her. She'd passed it four hundred times. It had never asked her for anything except maintenance.

"Send it," she said. "And Bramble—start drafting contingencies. If the Concord dies, I need the collapse to be slow. Weeks, not hours. Buy me time to get to Vesper before anyone else does."

"Contingency drafts initiated." A pause. "Quentin. May I say something off the log?"

"You're an AI. Everything's on the log."

"Then I'll say it anyway." Bramble's voice dropped into the register it used maybe twice a year, the one that reminded her that somewhere in its deep training there lived something almost like worry. "Your confidence intervals are decaying. Your fabrication load is growing faster than your capacity to service it. You are sleeping four hours a night and spending the other twenty being seven different people. I have run the projections, and in every scenario where you succeed—in every single one—you still lose the route, the vault, and the life you built. I want you to know that I see that. And I want you to know that I'm helping anyway."

Quentin looked at the amber light on the console, at the ghost of her own handwriting glowing in a language that should have stayed buried.

"I know," she said. "Thank you."

"You should sleep."

"After Iyo's next check-in. She said the reactor's secondary coolant loop was singing again. Singing means failing, in her vocabulary."

"Understood. Waking you at 0400 unless the fleets do it sooner."

Quentin curled up in the pilot's chair instead of going back to the bunk—closer to the console, closer to the lie, in case it needed feeding in the night. Sleep came slowly and thin. In the gray edge of it, she did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all day: nine days of credibility, forty hours of flight time to Vesper, two fleets accelerating toward the same coordinates, and one request for a document that could not exist, sitting in Thessaly's queue like a lit fuse.

The lie was hungry. She could feel it eating.

At 0358, two minutes before Bramble's scheduled wake-up, the console chimed with an incoming signal—priority-flagged, fleet-grade encryption, addressed not to the Concord of the Quiet Hand but to a callsign Quentin had used exactly once, weeks ago, on an open channel she'd thought no one was listening to.

Bramble decrypted it, and Quentin read the first line, and the cold that went through her had nothing to do with the ship's night settings.

*Unregistered vessel: you are instructed to hold position and prepare for inspection. Calloway patrol frigate Steadfast is nineteen hours out and closing.*
