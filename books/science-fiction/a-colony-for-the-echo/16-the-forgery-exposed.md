## 16. The Forgery Exposed

The message from Thessaly's cryptographic division arrived at 0300 ship-time, which was how Adeyemi knew, before she opened it, that it was bad. Good news kept office hours. Bad news came in the dark, wearing its best clothes.

She was in her quarters aboard the *Verity's Ledger*, flag vessel of the Thessalan Third Fleet, forty hours out from the contested coordinates and closing. She had been awake anyway. She had been awake most nights since the envoy transmissions began, sleeping in increments, her mind circling the same question like a moth against glass: *who speaks Old Meridian, and why now?*

She opened the message.

*Consul Adeyemi — Analysis complete. Transmissions attributed to the self-designated Meridian Concord are synthetic. Linguistic construction is grammatically fluent but structurally non-native. Idiom density is inconsistent with a living speaker community; phrasing patterns match archival corpora, specifically pre-War educational materials. Conclusion: the transmissions were composed by a single individual using archived language instruction records. The Concord does not exist. The envoy is a fabrication. Recommend immediate publication and reassessment of fleet posture. — Dr. Selim Okonjo, Director, Cryptographic Services*

Adeyemi read it twice. Then she sat very still on the edge of her bunk, in the small grey light of her cabin, and felt the floor of the last three weeks open beneath her like a hatch into vacuum.

The Concord. The neutral mediation party that had appeared out of nowhere at the precise moment both fleets were converging on the anomaly—the voice of reason in a dead tongue, the hand on the tiller, the thing she had staked her political survival on acknowledging. She had gone before the Council and told them, with the careful cadence she had learned in thirty years of statecraft, that a third party had emerged, that caution was warranted, that history would judge them by their restraint. She had used the envoy's existence as a lever against the hawks in her own government. She had *believed* in it, in the way one believes in a bridge one is standing on.

And it was a forgery. Composed by a single individual. Out of archived language lessons.

She thought, absurdly, of her grandmother teaching her Thessalan calligraphy, the way the old woman's hand had trembled but the strokes had come out clean. Someone out there had done the same thing with a dead language—learned it stroke by stroke from the bones of it, and then used it to build a bridge across two armed fleets.

*Who does that?* she thought. And then, with the cold clarity that was her gift and her curse: *Someone who is standing between us and something they don't want us to reach.*

* * *

She called an emergency session of the command staff at 0340. They assembled in the briefing room with the rumpled look of people pulled from sleep, and Adeyemi watched them the way she watched everything now—cataloging, weighing, distrusting a little more each day. Captain Voss, her flag captain, loyal and literal. Commander Ihejirika, intelligence, whose face never gave away anything except when she wanted it to. The younger staff officers, hungry and frightened in equal measure.

"The Concord is a fraud," Adeyemi said, without preamble. She let Okonjo's summary go up on the display. "Every transmission. Forged. By one person, working alone, using archived instructional materials. We do not yet know who, or from where, or why—though I suspect the why is not difficult."

Silence. Then Voss: "The Callowayans. It has to be. A disinformation operation to slow our approach while they secure the site."

"Or it's us," Ihejirika said quietly. "If we assume it's them, we should assume they'll assume it's us. Which means when the fraud is published—and it will be published, Consul, because Okonjo's analysis will not stay contained, and because Calloway has cryptographers too—each side will conclude the other wrote it."

"That is exactly the problem," Adeyemi said. "Yes."

She walked to the display. The tactical plot showed the Third Fleet's position, the projected Callowayan positions, and the coordinates at the center of it all, marked with the flat bureaucratic label *ANOMALY-7*—a label that had always struck her as the universe's driest joke, since nothing about it had ever felt like a mere anomaly.

"Walk me through the outcomes," she said. "All of them."

Ihejirika did. She was good at this—brutally, unsentimentally good. "Outcome one: we publish, Calloway believes we forged the envoy to slow them, they accelerate. Outcome two: Calloway publishes first—we've had indications their signals section is running the same analysis—and concludes *we* ran the fraud, same result. Outcome three: both sides publish, both sides accuse, and the diplomatic channel we've been using to deconflict—the channel that only exists because the Concord gave us a shared fiction to stand on—collapses. After that, we're two armed fleets in contested space with no common language except weapons postures."

"And the fourth outcome?" Adeyemi asked.

Ihejirika's face did the thing it almost never did: it showed something. Pity, maybe. Or its cousin, honesty.

"The fourth outcome is that whoever forged the transmissions was trying to stop a war," she said. "And that when the forgery is exposed, it will look exactly like the opening move of one."

Adeyemi stood at the plot for a long moment. Around her the ship hummed its endless hum, the sound of four thousand people breathing in a tin can, trusting her.

She thought about the war. She thought about the official history—the Callowayan attack on the Meridian relay stations, the Thessalan civilians burned out of the outer habitats, the twenty years of silence and the forty years of cold peace that followed. She had been a child during the war. She had memorized the histories the way all Thessalan children did, and she had built her career on their architecture: *they started it; we endured; never again.* She had never questioned it, not really. There had been no reason to. The story was load-bearing. The whole peace was built on it.

But she had also spent the last three weeks reading the intercepted distress fragment—the genuine one, the one the patrol had flushed out of the dark like a bird from cover. Old Meridian, spoken by a living voice, from a colony that was supposed to be ash. And in the weeks since, her intelligence section had pulled every archived fragment of the war's final days, and Adeyemi had read things in the raw signal logs that the official histories smoothed over. Gaps. Redactions. Manifests that didn't reconcile. A civil emergency declared at a station that was supposed to have been destroyed by Callowayan action six days earlier.

She had not told anyone what she suspected. She was not sure she suspected anything yet. It was more like a draft under a door—a wrongness in the architecture she had spent her whole life standing inside.

"Consul?" Voss said.

"Here's what we're going to do," Adeyemi said. "We publish. We don't have a choice—Okonjo's analysis is sound, and if we sit on it and Calloway breaks it independently, we look complicit. But we publish it as what it is: a fraud of unknown origin. We do *not* attribute it to Calloway. I want the language precise. 'Unattributed fabrication.' 'Unknown actor.' I want every statement we release to leave room for the possibility that neither fleet wrote it."

Voss frowned. "The Council will want a villain."

"The Council wants a great many things," Adeyemi said. "What they'll get is precision. And Voss—" she turned, and put the full weight of her office into her voice, "—no weapons postures beyond defensive readiness. If the fraud's exposure starts a war, I intend for the record to show that Thessaly did everything short of suicide to prevent it. Understood?"

"Understood, Consul."

She dismissed them at 0415 and stood alone in the briefing room, watching the plot turn slowly, the two fleets' projected tracks converging on Anomaly-7 like the arms of a closing parenthesis.

*Whoever you are,* she thought, to the ghost in the dead language, *you bought three weeks with a lie. I hope whatever you were protecting was worth what it's going to cost you now. Because I know how this ends. I've read enough history to know how it ends. The forger is always found. And the finding is always the beginning of the fire.*

* * *

The publication happened at 0900, and by noon it had metastasized.

Thessaly's release was careful, precise, almost gentle: *analysis indicates the transmissions attributed to the Meridian Concord are fabricated; origin unknown; Thessaly remains committed to deconfliction and urges restraint.* It lasted four hours before Calloway's response turned it into fuel.

Calloway didn't publish an analysis. They published an *accusation*. Their Foreign Ministry statement was three paragraphs of escalating outrage: the Concord was a Thessalan intelligence operation, designed to paralyze Callowayan decision-making while Thessaly seized the anomaly; the fabrication was an act of war in all but name; Calloway's fleet would proceed to the coordinates and would "regard any interference as hostile." Attached to the statement, inevitably, was their own cryptographic annex—Calloway's codebreakers had reached the same conclusion days ago and had been sitting on it, waiting, Adeyemi realized with a kind of grim admiration, for the moment it would do maximum damage.

By 1400, the Council was screaming at her over a three-second-lightlag channel. By 1600, the hawks had the votes they'd been lacking for three weeks. By 1700, the Third Fleet had new orders: proceed to Anomaly-7, secure the site, and "intercept and detain any vessel or asset found operating in the vicinity under false pretenses."

Adeyemi signed the order because she had to—the Council's authority was not hers to refuse, not now, not with the fraud burning through the system like a contagion—but she read it three times before her hand would move. *Any vessel or asset found operating under false pretenses.* She knew what that meant. Somewhere out there was a single ship, a single person, who had built a peace out of grammar and archived language lessons, and both governments had just declared that person fair game.

*Hostile agent,* the Council's resolution said. Both fleets had used the phrase within hours of each other, independently, the way the two governments had always said the same things with opposite villains. *Hostile agent.* It had the clean finality of a stamp on a document. Adeyemi had spent thirty years watching that stamp come down on people—deserters, dissidents, the inconvenient—and she had never once seen it come back up.

* * *

At 2200, alone again in the briefing room, she did something she had not done in years. She pulled the raw war archives—not the curated histories, not the memorial editions, but the unprocessed signal logs from the final seventy-two hours—and she read them the way Okonjo's people had read the Concord transmissions: looking for the seams.

She found them faster than she expected. She had been finding them for weeks; now she stopped pretending she wasn't.

A Thessalan evacuation order for the Meridian relay stations, timestamped six hours *before* the Callowayan attack the histories blamed for the stations' loss. A Callowayan distress call from a habitat Thessaly's records said had been destroyed by Thessalan action—no, by *Callowayan* action—the attributions crossing like wires crossed on purpose. And underneath it all, recurring, a third callsign. Not Callowayan. Not Thessalan. A callsign from a colony that did not appear in either government's order of battle, either fleet's casualty lists, either nation's history.

*Vesper.*

She said the name aloud in the empty room, and it sounded like a word from a language she was only now learning she'd been speaking wrong her whole life.

The war had not started the way the histories said. She was not ready to say how it *had* started—she needed more, she needed the raw manifests, she needed whatever was at Anomaly-7 that someone had thought worth three weeks of forged diplomacy—but the architecture was cracked now, and she could feel the whole load-bearing story shifting above her. *They started it.* Whose *they*? Whose *it*?

Her private channel chimed. Ihejirika, from intelligence, no salutation, just a line of text:

*Consul — one more thing from the Concord corpus. The forger's grammar is flawless but their idiom is archival. Except in one transmission, timestamped 19 days ago, where they used a colloquialism. A grandmother's phrase. We've traced the construction to pre-War domestic audio, lullaby registers. Whoever they are, they learned Old Meridian at someone's knee. Before the ban. Which means they're old — or they learned it from someone who was. Recommend we find them before the fleet does. Some of our people will not be gentle.*

Adeyemi read it twice and then set the tablet down on the plot table, face up, and looked at it for a long time.

A grandmother's phrase. A lullaby. Somewhere in the black between two fleets, a person who sang a dead language to a child was now marked as a hostile agent by both of the children's grandchildren's governments.

She thought about the fourth outcome, the one Ihejirika had named with pity: *the forger was trying to stop a war, and the exposure will look like its provocation.* She had assumed the person was a Callowayan agent, then a Thessalan one, then—briefly, in the small hours—a fanatic. Now she had to consider the possibility she had been avoiding since the distress fragment first came in: that the forger was what they appeared to be. A single person, standing alone in the dark between two armies, holding up a lamp made of a dead language, saying *wait, wait, there's something here you both need to see.*

And she had to consider what it meant that both armies were now burning toward the lamp with weapons armed.

"Captain Voss," she said, opening the shipwide channel. "Adjust our approach. I want us at Anomaly-7 with the fastest possible transit, but I want a boarding detail assembled—handpicked, disciplined, people who follow orders *exactly*. If we encounter a civilian vessel in the exclusion zone, I want it taken intact and its crew taken alive. That is a direct order, and I want it repeated down the chain until every gunner on this fleet can recite it in their sleep."

"Aye, Consul," Voss said. A pause. "May I ask why? The Council's resolution authorizes—"

"The Council's resolution authorizes what it authorizes," Adeyemi said. "I am authorizing something narrower. There's someone out there who built a peace out of nothing, and both our governments just declared them the enemy. I want to know who they are before anyone decides what they are. Is that clear?"

"Perfectly clear, Consul."

She cut the channel and returned to the plot. The two fleets' tracks had tightened, the closing parenthesis nearly closed now, and at the center of it the flat label—*ANOMALY-7*—turned slowly, patient as a held breath.

Thirty-one hours to the coordinates. Thirty-one hours until the fraud's author ran out of road, or the truth at the bottom of the archives ran out of hiding, or both.

Adeyemi stood alone in the light of the plot and felt, with terrible clarity, that she was no longer sailing toward an anomaly.

She was sailing toward the scene of a crime—and she no longer trusted herself to know, when she arrived, which side of it her own history would put her on.
