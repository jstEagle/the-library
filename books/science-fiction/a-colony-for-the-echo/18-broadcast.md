## 18. Broadcast

**QUENTIN**

The fleets came out of the dark like weather.

Bramble put them on the display without being asked—two blooms of drive flare, one bearing Calloway's blue-white signature, the other Thessaly's amber, both decelerating hard toward Vesper's coordinates. Forty hours had become thirty. Then twenty-five. The universe, it turned out, had no respect for arithmetic done by a woman running on tea and terror.

"They'll be in weapons range in nineteen hours," Bramble said. "Assuming nobody accelerates."

"Assuming," Quentin said.

She sat in the *Long Route*'s cramped comm bay with Iyo beside her, and between them, floating in the holo like a small green sun, was everything: the vault's oldest seed manifests, Vesper's founding records, the genetic markers that named defectors from both fleets among the first colonists. Two hundred years of paperwork proving that the war—the War, capital W, the thing both governments built their identities on—had been a family argument that got out of hand, then buried under two centuries of mutually convenient lies.

"Raw data," Quentin said. "No narration."

"You've said this eleven times," Bramble said.

"I'm checking."

"You checked it eleven times."

Iyo reached over and touched the edge of the holo, her fingers passing through the light. She'd been quiet for an hour, which for Iyo was unprecedented. When she finally spoke, it was in Old Meridian, soft, almost to herself: *"Síla ven orath."*

Truth travels far.

"That's the idea," Quentin said.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking the way you notice rain on a window—as something happening slightly outside yourself. Nineteen hours until two armadas arrived at a colony of dying reactors and four hundred survivors, each fleet intending to claim Vesper as evidence, as leverage, as a verdict in a trial that had never actually been held. Her forged envoy was dead. Adeyemi's cryptographers had gutted it and published the corpse. Both governments had named her a hostile agent. There was no fiction left to hide behind, no invented authority to buy time with.

There was only this: a hold full of archive drives and a voice on every open channel.

"It won't work," she said, not because she believed it but because someone had to say it aloud so it could be survived. "They'll jam us. They'll call it more forgery. They'll—"

"They called your last lie a forgery," Iyo said, "and they believed it for six weeks. People believe what they need." She looked up, and there was something old in her young face, something that had grown in the dark behind failing reactors where children learned their grandparents' griefs before they learned to read. "My people believed we were forgotten. It kept us alive. Belief is a reactor too. You just have to give it fuel."

Quentin looked at the green sun of data. "This is fuel?"

"This is the whole star."

* * *

**IYO**

Iyo had imagined this moment for three days, ever since Quentin had laid out the plan in her terrible, halting Old Meridian—the grammar wrong in six places, the intent unmistakable anyway. She had imagined standing at a console, speaking formally, the way the archive-readers spoke during the Remembrance cycles, when Vesper recited its dead into the reactor hall and the names echoed off the cooling fins.

What she had not imagined was how ordinary the console would look. A screen. A switch. A microphone older than her mother.

"Bramble says the channel architecture is open," Quentin said. "Every fleet keeps emergency bands clear by treaty. Nobody's used them for anything but distress since the war ended." A thin smile. "Nobody's used them for truth, either."

"The treaty bands," Iyo said. "You're sure they can't refuse delivery?"

"Refusing an open-band broadcast means admitting you're afraid of what's in it. In front of your own crew." Quentin shrugged. "Reyes might do it. Adeyemi might. But their crews will know. Silence is loud when everyone's watching for it."

Iyo thought about that. On Vesper, silence had been the water they swam in—two centuries of it, the long quiet after the fire, the quiet her people had mistaken for the whole ocean. She had grown up believing the silence was death. Now she understood it could also be a choice, made by powerful people, in rooms she would never see, for reasons written in ledgers instead of lullabies.

"And if they don't listen?" she asked. "If they hear it and choose the lie anyway? Lies are comfortable. My grandmother said comfort is the strongest material in the universe. Stronger than steel. That's why ships fail—they're built from comfort."

Quentin was quiet for a moment. "Then we'll have said it anyway. Out loud. On the record. Every crew member on every ship will have heard the same numbers at the same time, and after that, the lie has to be maintained *in public*, in front of people who know better." She tapped the console. "Lies are cheap alone. They're ruinously expensive in company."

That, Iyo decided, was the most frightening sentence anyone had ever said to her. Because it meant the truth wasn't a shield. It was a seed. And seeds did not protect anyone. Seeds only promised that something might grow, later, in soil you would not live to stand in.

She thought of the vault under Earth's crust, the one Quentin had tended for years—the shelves of sleeping possibility, the patience of it. *A caretaker*, Quentin had called herself. Not a savior. Someone who kept things alive until the season changed.

"Say the words first," Iyo said. "In my language. Before you send anything. I want to hear how it sounds."

Quentin looked at her. "It's just a preamble. There's no authority attached. No name. We agreed—no narration, no—"

"Not the preamble." Iyo shook her head. "The other part. The part you wrote last night when you thought I was asleep."

Quentin's ears went red. "That's not—I didn't write it *for* the broadcast—"

"Then say it here. Just here."

A long pause. Outside the viewport, Vesper hung against the stars, its reactor glow flickering like a candle in wind, and Iyo watched the woman who had crossed a war's graveyard to reach her gather herself the way you gather yourself before cold water.

"In Old Meridian," Quentin said quietly, "*ven síla orath i merath.* What is planted in truth does not fear the frost."

It was grammatically perfect. Iyo had checked every case marker while pretending to sleep. Somewhere in the last weeks, between reactor repairs and vocabulary lessons, her language had stopped being a dead tongue in Quentin's mouth and started being a living one.

"That one," Iyo said, "you send."

* * *

**QUENTIN**

She sent everything at once, because hesitation was a door and doors could be closed.

Bramble seized the treaty bands—all forty-one of them, emergency, navigation, diplomatic—and poured the archives through like water through a burst dam. Seed manifests. Reactor logs. Founding charters with signatures in three scripts. Genetic registries showing Vesper's first generation carrying markers from Calloway's founding families *and* Thessaly's, side by side, in the same blood. The falsified after-action reports, both versions, laid end to end so the seams showed. No commentary. No attribution. No *this proves* or *they knew*. Just the documents themselves, naked, in Old Meridian, Callowayan, and Thessalan, translated by Bramble with Iyo checking every line.

The transmission would take nine minutes to complete. Quentin spent all nine minutes watching the signal-strength readout and breathing like a woman underwater.

On the tactical display, the two fleets kept coming.

"They see it," Bramble reported. "Calloway's lead ship has gone active-sensor. Thessaly's frigate is... holding course. Both fleets' internal comms traffic just tripled."

"Jamming?"

"None. Yet." A pause that Bramble filled with something almost human. "Reyes's flagship just went silent on all command channels. That's either very good or very bad."

"Both," Quentin said. "It's always both now."

Iyo stood at the viewport with her hands pressed flat against the glass, watching her world turn slowly below them, and Quentin came to stand beside her. Below, Vesper's southern hemisphere was a smear of ash-forest and dead cities; the north still glowed faintly where the surviving enclave clung to its geothermal vents. Four hundred people. Four hundred people who had been a rumor, then a distress call, then a legal question, and who were about to become, in nineteen hours, whatever two armed governments decided they were.

Unless the ground shifted under everyone's feet first.

"Look," Iyo whispered.

Below, lights were coming on across the northern enclave—not emergency lighting, which flickered amber, but the white work-lamps of the surface arrays, the ones Iyo's people hadn't had power to run in a generation. Blinking. Deliberately. In patterns.

"What are they doing?" Quentin asked.

Iyo laughed—a wet, cracked sound. "They're reading your broadcast down there. The whole enclave stopped to listen. Those are lamp signals. Old Meridian signal-code." Her hand found Quentin's on the glass. "They're saying *we hear you. We are here. We are not evidence.*"

Quentin's throat closed. She keyed the comm bay's local transmitter—the short-range one, useless against fleets, perfect for a colony below—and sent back the only reply she trusted, in a language she'd learned from lullabies and a dying girl:

*"Ven síla orath i merath."*

Planted in truth does not fear the frost.

* * *

**IYO**

The fleets answered within the hour, and they answered exactly as her grandmother's stories said powerful people answer: with noise.

Calloway transmitted first—an official demand, stiff with protocol, ordering "all unidentified vessels in contested space" to cease broadcasting "unverified materials" pending "proper adjudication." Proper adjudication. Iyo turned the phrase over. Two hundred years ago, her founders had fled from proper adjudication; it had been the gavel coming down on half of humanity while the other half applauded.

Thessaly's message was smoother. Consul Adeyemi herself, voice like warm oil: acknowledging receipt of "historical materials," noting their "apparent provenance concerns," inviting "the custodian of these documents"—and here the pause, the trap—"to surrender them for authentication aboard the *Meridian's Daughter*."

They wanted the originals. Of course they did. Put the truth in a room with two lawyers and it comes out as a weapon for whichever side pays better.

"They're stalling," Quentin said. "Nineteen hours was always optimistic. They're trying to keep us talking until they're in range, and then talking stops mattering."

"Then we stop talking," Iyo said.

Quentin looked at her.

"My grandmother told me a story," Iyo said. "From the last days. When the warships came to Vesper the first time—the ones that burned us—one of the archive-readers climbed the main array tower and broadcast our founding charter on every band, over and over, while the missiles fell. Not to save anyone. To make sure that whoever came after, scavengers or historians or God, would find the words before they found the ashes." She met Quentin's eyes. "They called her the Echo. Vesper's whole culture grew around her. *A colony for the echo.* That's what we are. We are what's left after someone refused to stop saying it."

The comm bay was very quiet. Even Bramble said nothing.

"So we don't argue with them," Iyo continued, and her voice steadied as she went, the way it always did when she stepped into the current of the old language. "We don't authenticate. We don't surrender. We repeat. Every band, every hour, in every language, until their crews can recite it in their sleep. You cannot jam a thing that has already been heard. You can only admit you're afraid of it."

Quentin stared at her for a long moment. Then she started laughing—low, disbelieving, edged with something close to tears.

"Adeyemi asked for the custodian," she said. "Fine. Let me show her what custody looks like."

* * *

**QUENTIN**

The second broadcast went out at 0400, ship-time, and it was simpler than the first: the same archives, compressed tighter, followed by a single line of text repeated in all three languages:

*These documents belong to no government. They belong to the dead who wrote them and the living who survived them. Authentication is not required for memory.*

By 0600, things began to happen that Quentin had no framework for.

A Callowayan corvette, the *Kestrel*, broke formation—drifted, officially, a thruster calibration—but its position put it squarely inside the broadcast's clearest reception, and it stayed there. Its captain opened a private channel to the *Long Route*. No demands. Just a single request: *"Send the founding charter again. Slower."*

Thessaly's fast frigate answered differently: it began transmitting counter-programming, official histories, the war as both governments had taught it, layered over the bands like paint over graffiti. But Bramble tracked the listeners, and the listeners were not obeying the frigate. Fleet-wide comm logs—intercepted, parsed—showed Thessalan crew channels lighting up with the same word, over and over, in a dozen dialects of disbelief: the genetic registry numbers. Everyone was checking the genetic registry numbers. Everyone had family trees. Everyone was doing the arithmetic.

And on Vesper below, the lamp-signals had spread. From the northern enclave to the southern ruins, from the ruins to the orbital mirrors, the old code climbing the dark like ivy: *we hear you, we are here, we are not evidence.*

"They're going to arrive angry," Bramble observed. "Both flagships have resumed acceleration. Weapons-grade posturing on all channels. Reyes has declared the corridor 'under Calloway security jurisdiction.' Adeyemi has declared it 'a Thessaly survey zone.' They are about to discover they declared the same piece of sky."

"How long?" Quentin asked.

"Eleven hours. Maybe less."

Iyo stood at the viewport, backlit by her burning-bright world, and Quentin realized the girl was reciting something under her breath—the Remembrance litany, the names, all four hundred thousand of them, the prayer her people said when they expected to die.

"No," Quentin said, and took her hand. "Not this time. This time they arrive and find witnesses. Thousands of them. Their own crews, carrying the same numbers, standing on the same decks, looking at the same colony." She squeezed. "Corruption can't aim its guns at people who already know."

Iyo's recitation slowed. Stopped. "And if it can?"

"Then we're the echo," Quentin said. "And echoes outlast the voices that try to silence them."

On the display, the two blooms of drive flare burned closer, closer, twin suns dragging their shadows behind them—and then, without warning, every band went silent at once. Total. Absolute. The kind of silence that isn't empty but *held*, the way a room goes quiet before a verdict.

Bramble broke it, voice stripped of all its dryness:

"Both flagships just cut their drives. They're hanging in high orbit, weapons hot, facing each other across Vesper—and Reyes is hailing Adeyemi directly. Open channel. Unencrypted." A pause. "Quentin. They're about to talk. In front of everyone."

Quentin looked at Iyo. Iyo looked at the sky.

"Then everybody listens," she said.
