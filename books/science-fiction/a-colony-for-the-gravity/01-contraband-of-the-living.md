## 1. Contraband of the Living

The trick to carrying other people's pasts, Silas had learned by her ninth year, was never to want them.

She held that rule the way other spacers held superstitions — close, unexamined, load-bearing. The Kestrel's cargo bay hummed around her, forty memory-vaults racked in cryo-stasis like sleeping children, each one a stolen life sealed in lattice-glass. A magistrate's first betrayal. A widow's last good summer. An heir's confession, recorded on his deathbed and worth more dead than he'd ever been alive.

Silas Dravenmoor carried them all and read none of them. That was the guild's oldest law and her only inheritance: *you are the vessel, not the wine.*

"Approach vector confirmed," said Wren from the cockpit — not a person but the ship's ghost, an intelligence old enough to predate the Sundering Wars and too obsolete for anyone to bother outlawing. "Customs buoy Threnody-9 is pinging us. Tribunal signature."

Silas didn't look up from the manifest. "Let it ping."

"It's asking for a hull sweep."

"It can ask." She tapped the manifest closed. "We're a grain freighter out of Meridian Reach with a boring history and a boring future. Let it be bored."

The buoy's ping faded, sulking. That was the tribunal these days — all appetite, no teeth yet, but growing them fast. Ten years ago contested orbit had been a bazaar where anything with a serial number could be bought twice. Now the customs cutters ran paired patrols, and the memory-guilds were bleeding couriers every season. Silas had buried three of them. Not literally; the tribunal did literal burials. She'd just crossed their names off her mental ledger and kept flying, because grief was a luxury priced in the same currency as hesitation.

The handoff was at Relay Station Vesper, a rusted ring spinning in the shadow of a dead moon, its docks leased to a fish-cannery front so ancient nobody remembered there were no fish within forty light-years. Her buyer would be waiting in berth fourteen: a soft-spoken archivist named Oduya who paid in untraceable credit and never asked what he was buying, which made him either the best client she'd ever had or the most dangerous. With Oduya it was genuinely hard to tell.

"Wren, run the approach quiet."

"Running quiet. Also running a diagnostic you didn't ask for. Your port thruster coupling is at sixty-two percent."

"Noted and ignored."

"You ignore everything I say."

"That's why we work."

* * *

Vesper smelled the way all relay stations smelled: recycled air, ozone, and the sweet chemical rot of cheap nutrient paste. Silas moved through berth fourteen's access corridor with the easy gait of someone who belonged everywhere, duffel over one shoulder, forty lives inside it weighing less than laundry.

Oduya was already there, seated on a supply crate, reading something on a slate. He looked up, and for once he wasn't smiling.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm early. You're paranoid."

"The two overlap." He stood, and his eyes went past her shoulder to the corridor mouth, then back. "There's a problem, Dravenmoor."

"There's always a problem. It's usually money."

"This one's language." He turned the slate toward her.

On it played a signal capture — raw, unfiltered, the kind of intercept customs picked up and discarded by the thousands every day. Except this one had been flagged by three different listening posts and quietly buried by all three, which meant someone above the listening posts wanted it gone.

Silas watched the waveform crawl across the screen and felt something cold walk down her spine.

She knew languages the way she knew faces — smuggled enough minds, and you absorbed the shape of how people thought. Old trade-tongues, guild ciphers, the liturgical dialects of the Rim sects. But this —

"This is pre-Sundering," she said. "This is *Old Concordance.*"

"Extinct since the Wars," Oduya agreed. "Two hundred years. Every native speaker died or was erased, and the tribunal burned the archives after. Nobody living speaks it. Nobody living should be *transmitting* it."

"Where's it coming from?"

He hesitated. That hesitation told her more than the answer would have.

"Oduya."

"The outer belt," he said finally. "A derelict. Registry says it's called the *Halcyon*. Cryo-ark class. It's been drifting out there since before either of us was born, and as far as anyone official is concerned, it doesn't exist."

"And as far as anyone unofficial?"

"As far as anyone unofficial—" he pocketed the slate, "—the signal repeats on a nine-hour cycle. Same transmission, same timestamp drift, like a clock counting down. And yesterday, a tribunal survey cutter altered course toward it. Then another. They're not going to salvage it, Silas. Salvage crews don't move in pairs."

The station's ambient hum filled the silence between them. Somewhere below decks, the cannery machinery that processed nothing clanked through its empty shifts.

"What's in the ark?" she asked.

"If I knew, I'd be selling it." He extended his hand. "Which is why I'm taking my memories and getting off this rock before the survey cutters start asking questions about everyone who's asked questions. Are we doing business?"

Forty lives in a duffel. A buyer with shaking hands. A dead language singing from a ship that shouldn't exist, and the tribunal already circling it like gulls over a drowned thing.

Ten years of never opening the cargo. Ten years of being the vessel, not the wine.

Silas looked at the duffel, then at Oduya's outstretched hand, and heard herself say: "Rain check."

His eyebrows rose. "You've never taken a rain check."

"First time for everything." She shouldered the duffel. "Something's wrong with this picture, and I don't sell to wrong pictures."

"Dravenmoor—"

"Keep your credits, Oduya. Buy yourself a conscience. They're about to appreciate in value."

* * *

The Kestrel broke orbit under a forged flight plan bound for nowhere in particular, and Silas sat in the cargo bay with the racks humming around her and the signal capture playing on a loop against the bulkhead.

Old Concordance. She'd heard fragments of it once, years ago, in a smuggled memory she'd almost opened and hadn't — a lullaby thread woven through some magistrate's childhood, nonsense syllables the decryption software couldn't parse. At the time she'd filed it as corruption. Now the syllables came back to her whole, and they matched the waveform like a key matching a lock.

Someone down there — out there — was singing a lullaby into the dark, on a nine-hour loop, to an audience of nobody.

Or to an audience of somebody frozen.

"Wren," she said. "How long to the outer belt coordinates?"

"Eleven hours at cruising burn. Twelve if I baby the port coupling."

"Baby it."

"You're deviating from the handoff route. The guild will notice."

"The guild notices everything. That's their whole personality." She pulled one vault from the rack — the magistrate's childhood, the one with the lullaby threaded through it — and set it on the workbench, lattice-glass catching the bay lights like a chip of frozen dawn.

Her rule sat on her shoulder like Maren used to, back when Maren was just the woman who'd found her scavenging dock-rats out of the Meridian slums and taught her that a courier who reads her cargo is a courier who dies curious. *You are the vessel, not the wine.*

But the vessel had ears. And for ten years she'd heard nothing worth breaking the rule for.

Silas keyed the vault open.

The memory bloomed around her — a kitchen, wood-smoke, a woman's hands kneading bread, and beneath it all, faint as breath, the lullaby in Old Concordance, sung by a voice that had been dead for two centuries. The child hearing it had grown into the magistrate whose betrayal she'd carried across half the system without ever once wondering where the song came from.

Now she wondered. Now she couldn't stop.

The lullaby ended. In the memory's silence, another sound surfaced underneath — words, spoken plainly, in the same extinct tongue, followed by a translation stitched into the memory's metadata like an afterthought:

*If any hear this: we are not what they said we were. We are still here. We remember being innocent.*

Silas closed the vault with hands that weren't quite steady, and stared at the forty sleeping lives racked around her, and understood with sudden terrible clarity that somewhere in all the years of cargo she'd carried without reading, she had already delivered this message once before.

She just hadn't known she was the messenger.

"Wren," she said. "Change of destination. Log it as a grain run."

"To the derelict singing dead languages?"

"To the derelict singing dead languages."

"Your funeral," said Wren, and turned the ship.
