## 4. The Man Who Split Her

The man who split her lived at the bottom of a gravity well, in a city called Meridian Deep, where the light came down through nine kilometers of atmosphere and arrived already tired.

Kestrel had spent eleven days finding him. Eleven days of pulling threads through the Concord's civil registries, through the pension rolls of the Meridian Line's decommissioned shipyards, through a single line in a twenty-year-old medical census that read, in a clerk's indifferent hand: *A. Senn — neuroengineering (lapsed license) — residence: unspecified.* Unspecified meant hidden. Hidden meant he was still afraid of something, and fear left tracks like anything else that moved.

She found him in the flooded district, where the old tidal generators had been abandoned and the streets ran knee-deep with warm green water that smelled of algae and rust. The houses there were built on stilts from an earlier century, their lower floors surrendered to the sea, their upper windows hung with drying nets and strings of paper lanterns gone soft with damp. It was the kind of place people went to be forgotten, and it worked, mostly. Kestrel stood on a walkway of warped planks and watched a boy pole a flat skiff between the houses, delivering canisters of reclaimed water, and understood why Senn had chosen it. No one looked at anyone here. Looking was a debt you didn't want to owe.

His house was the one with the antenna. That was what gave him away, in the end—twenty years of hiding, undone by a man's inability to stop listening to the sky. The dish was old Concord survey surplus, its paint blistered, aimed not at the shipping lanes or the relay buoys but up and out, toward the long dark where a certain ship had been drifting for two decades. He couldn't help himself. She almost pitied him for it.

She knocked at dusk.

The door opened on a chain, and through the gap she saw one eye, brown and bloodshot and very old behind its wariness. "Wrong house," said a voice like gravel settling.

"Dr. Aurel Senn," Kestrel said. "Formerly of the Halcyon Project, neurointegration division. You filed a patent in the year before launch for a cortical mapping lattice that the Concord classified as theoretical. It wasn't theoretical. I've seen it working."

The eye didn't blink. Water lapped against the stilts below them, patient as a clock.

"I don't know what you've seen," the voice said.

"You know exactly what I've seen." She held her hand up into the gap in the door, palm out, where the port scanner sat embedded beneath the skin of her wrist—a standard-issue investigator's implant, but she'd angled it so the low light caught the fine scarring around it. Scarring from a surgical install. Scarring done by someone very good, somewhere off the books. "Because some of it is mine."

* * *

He let her in because she asked him about the lattice, and men who have buried their life's work will dig it up for anyone who says the right words. That was her read, anyway, standing in his kitchen while he boiled water over a spirit stove and refused to meet her eyes. The room was small and clean in the way of rooms kept by people who have nothing else to govern. Books everywhere, real paper ones, swollen with the humidity. A cot behind a curtain. And on the wall above the stove, framed behind cracked glass, a photograph: a hundred and forty people in grey coveralls standing on the gantry beside the Halcyon's hull, squinting into a sun that no longer existed for any of them.

Kestrel found herself in the photograph. Third row, near the left. A woman with her face, laughing at something outside the frame.

"That's not me," she said quietly.

"No," Senn said. He poured the water over leaves in a chipped pot. "That's Ilsa Vane. She took your name when she boarded. Legally, in every registry that mattered, she *was* Kestrel Blackwood. You—" he set the pot down with exaggerated care, as if his hands wanted to shake and he was forbidding them, "—you were never on the manifest at all. Not until I put you there."

The kettle's heat ticked in the silence. Somewhere out over the water, a buoy moaned.

"Tell me," Kestrel said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. Level, and cold, and steady as a surgeon's lamp. "Tell me what I am."

Senn lowered himself onto a stool across from her, and twenty years fell off him and then settled back twice as heavy. Up close she could see the tremor in his fingers, the way his eyes kept going to the photograph and away again, a man unable to stop looking at the scene of his own crime.

"The Halcyon was sabotaged eleven days before launch," he said. "You know that part, or you think you do. The official record says a coolant cascade. It wasn't. Someone seeded the cryo bays with a catalytic agent—a slow poison, keyed to activate under freeze conditions. Two thousand sleepers, and the agent would have killed every one of them inside the first year of transit, quietly, in their dreams, while the ship flew on empty to nowhere. No explosion. No evidence. Just a hull full of corpses arriving at a colony that would never know why."

"Who?"

"I don't know." He said it fast, and she heard the truth in it, and heard also the thing underneath the truth—the shape of what he did know, held back like a card against his chest. "I knew something was wrong. I was running pre-freeze neural baselines, and the readings were contaminated. Trace compounds in the cerebrospinal fluid of every sleeper. I pulled samples, I analyzed them, and then—" his mouth twisted, "—then three of my lab staff stopped coming to work, and my quarters were searched, and I understood that whatever I did next, I had perhaps days. So I did the only thing I could think of."

"Which was?"

"I stole the ship." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd heard in years, dry and cracked and utterly without humor. "Not the hull. The hull they were watching. I stole the *people*. Or pieces of them. My lattice could map a mind—all of it, the whole architecture, every fold of memory and habit and grief. It was designed for backup. For continuity. I pointed it at two thousand sleeping passengers and I copied everything I could reach before they sealed the bays."

Kestrel felt the floor tilt, though the room was still. "Copied them where?"

"Into bodies." He met her eyes at last, and she made herself hold the look. "We had a fabrication annex. Medical frames, growth-vat substrate—I won't bore you with the process. It takes fourteen months to grow a body to adult viability. It takes six hours to write a mind into it. I had neither, so I did it after. After the launch was cancelled. After the Halcyon was towed out to the drift yards and 'decommissioned,' which is the Concord's word for *abandoned where no one will look*. I had the annex for another eight months before they shut it down. In those eight months, I grew forty-one bodies and wrote forty-one minds."

The number landed in the room like a dropped tool. Forty-one. Kestrel thought of all the impossible accidents she had closed—the station fire at Ceres Gate that one woman survived; the airlock failure on Thessaly that killed everyone except a technician whose records began eighteen months too recently; the ferry that burned, the sole survivor with no family, no history, no past. Dozens of survivors. Dozens of people who shouldn't exist, scattered along the Meridian Line like seeds from a split pod.

"They're all out there," she said. "All forty-one."

"Thirty-seven." Senn's voice dropped. "Four died early. Accidents." His eyes flicked to hers, and she saw he'd done the same arithmetic she had. "The rest are alive. Working. Living. Most of them don't know. I wrote the memories in whole, but I couldn't give them provenance—biometric registration trails, birth records, the connective tissue of a legal identity. So they woke in halfway houses with complete pasts and no paperwork, and they did what anyone does. They improvised. They survived." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Some of them became investigators. Isn't that a joke? People with stolen memories, drawn to other people's mysteries."

"Why me?" The question tore out of her before she could dress it in patience. "Of all of them—why copy *her*? Why give me her face, her name, her—"

"Because she was dying." For the first time his voice broke open, and what was inside it was not guilt but grief, raw and twenty years old. "Ilsa Vane. She wasn't asleep yet when the contamination showed—she was a late boarding, a systems tech, she came to my lab for her baseline scan personally. I told her what I'd found. And three days later she was in a med bay with a neurotoxin in her blood that no one could source, and we both knew what it meant: they'd noticed her noticing. She had maybe a week. So she looked at me and said, *'Then take it. Take all of it. If they're going to kill me for knowing, make sure the knowing survives.'*" He stared at the photograph on the wall. "She chose it. Whatever else I am, whatever you decide I deserve—she chose it. I copied her whole. Every memory. Every scar. Everything she ever was, written into a body I grew in a vat in the dark, and then I gave that body her legal name and turned it loose on the Concord that murdered her, because a ghost with a grudge and a badge is the only kind of justice I could build."

Kestrel stood up. Her legs felt borrowed, which was, she thought distantly, the most accurate thing she had ever felt.

"So I'm her," she said. "I'm Ilsa Vane."

"No." Senn rose too, and something in his spine straightened, the engineer reasserting itself over the penitent. "You are not her. That's the part nobody understands—not the Concord, not the philosophers, not the priests. A copy is not the person. It's a new person carrying the same cargo. You have her memories up to the night she died. Everything since—" he gestured at her, at the walkway outside, at the green water and the tired light, "—everything since is yours alone. Twenty years of a life she never lived. You read accidents like confessions because *she* taught you how, but the loneliness, the restlessness, the way you keep moving—that's not her. I've watched the manifests. Ilsa Vane was a homebody. She loved one city her whole life." His voice gentled into something almost apologetic. "You've never stayed anywhere longer than a season. That's not her ghost. That's you."

Kestrel turned away from him, to the window, to the water. Below, the boy with the skiff was making his last rounds, lanterns waking yellow in the stilted windows one by one. She tried to feel the vertigo properly, the way the archivist must have felt it before he vanished—but what rose in her instead was stranger and quieter. Relief. Terrible relief. *You are not her. You are the one who walked away.*

"The Concord is moving on the Halcyon," she said to the glass. "Salvage claim. Which means seizure, which means two thousand sleepers in Concord hands—and if even half of what you've told me is true, the man who poisoned them is probably the one filing the paperwork."

Behind her, silence. Then, softly: "Deputy Director Corvane."

She turned. Senn had gone pale, paler than the lamplight could account for.

"You know the name."

"I know that Corvane chaired the launch review board," Senn said. "I know that he signed the decommission order that sent the Halcyon to the drift yards. And I know that three weeks ago, someone tried to buy my research archive through a shell company, and when I refused, the offer came back with a different signature." He swallowed. "His."

Outside, the buoy moaned again, and the tide turned, and far overhead—past the tired light, past the wet dark—at the end of a twenty-year fall, a ship full of sleeping witnesses drifted on toward whoever reached her first.

Kestrel picked up her coat.

"Then we'd better get there before he does," she said.
