## 9. The Trial of Kestrel Blackwood

*Ilsa*

The first thing the waking world gave her was cold, and the second was a name that did not belong to her alone.

Ilsa Vane surfaced from thirty years of ice in increments—first the ache of thawing blood, then the amber glow of a med-bay cradle, then a woman's face leaning over her, dark-haired and sharp-eyed and *hers*, down to the small scar at the left eyebrow Ilsa had earned falling from a fig tree at nine years old.

"You're real," Ilsa said. Her voice came out as gravel.

"So are you." The woman's mouth twisted into something between a smile and an apology. "That's the problem."

"Kestrel," Ilsa said, testing it. Not *my* name. Not *her* name either, maybe. "They told us we'd wake on Meridian. Green fields. A colony waiting for us."

"They lied about a lot of things." Kestrel Blackwood—the copy, the walker-away, the one who had lived a whole life Ilsa had only dreamed in fragments—pressed a warmed blanket around her shoulders. "The ship never launched. Someone made sure of it. And now there are two thousand of you waking to a galaxy that decided you were salvage."

Ilsa looked past her, through the frost-scarred viewport, at the hull of the Halcyon stretching away into the dark like a spine. Beyond it, running lights: the Concord fleet, patient as vultures. Boarding clamps had bitten into the ship six hours ago. Soldiers in grey moved through the corridors below, cataloguing sleepers like cargo.

"And you've been out here all this time," Ilsa said. "Living my life."

"Some of it." Kestrel's jaw tightened. "Senn copied your mind into a body grown in a vat on Ceres Station. He gave you his memories of the sabotage, buried so deep even you couldn't find them. You became an investigator of impossible accidents because some part of you never stopped trying to solve the one that killed everyone you knew."

"I didn't know anyone," Ilsa said. "I boarded the Halcyon with four hundred strangers and a suitcase full of seed stock. I was going to be a botanist on a world I'd never seen."

Kestrel went very still. "You weren't an investigator?"

"No." Ilsa held her gaze. "But you were. That was yours. The loneliness, the long routes, the way you read wreckage like scripture—that was the life *you* built out of what he put in you. Don't give it back to me. It wouldn't fit."

Something passed between them then—not forgiveness, not yet, but the recognition of two halves of a severed thing, each carrying wounds the other hadn't earned.

Above them, the ship's ancient PA crackled. A voice, dry and official: *"All awakened passengers will remain in their designated sections. Concord authority is now in effect aboard this vessel."*

Kestrel stood. "They're going to seal the sleepers back into cryo and tow the whole ship to Meridian Prime. Salvage claim. Legally, none of you exist—you're inventory."

"Then we'd better stop being legal," Ilsa said, and pushed herself upright on legs that hadn't borne weight since before Kestrel was born.

* * *

*Kestrel*

There was no courtroom aboard the Halcyon. There was the hydroponics bay—four hundred meters of dead growing racks and one stubborn stand of wheat Ilsa had coaxed back to life in a single shift—and there were one hundred and twelve people awake, shivering, furious, and unwilling to lie back down.

Kestrel stood at the head of the bay beside Ilsa and felt the strangeness of it like a second heartbeat: addressing a crowd while looking at her own face in the front row. The newly woken had arranged themselves without instruction—a loose crescent of folding stools and cargo crates, farmers and engineers and teachers pulled out of a century that had forgotten them. Senn sat apart, wrists bound with cargo strap, his face grey with exhaustion. He had insisted on being present. "If they're going to judge me," he'd said, "they should have to look at me while they do it."

At the far end, flanked by two Concord officers, stood Director Cassian Vole—the man whose signature ran through every sabotaged valve, every falsified launch log, every murdered witness Kestrel had spent years chasing across the Meridian Line. He wore his authority like cologne. He had come aboard personally to oversee the seizure, certain no one would dare object.

"We have no law here," Kestrel said, and her voice carried in the still air among the dead plants. "No court charter. No magistrates. The Concord says this ship is property and all of you are its contents. But a court is just people deciding together what's true. So we're going to decide what's true. Right now. In a room full of wheat."

A ripple of something—not quite laughter, but its cousin—moved through the crescent.

"The jury is everyone awake who isn't accused," she continued. "The evidence is testimony, records, and the ship itself. And the accused—" she turned to Vole, letting the silence stretch, "—is welcome to leave whenever he likes. Except that his soldiers control the airlocks, and ours control the oxygen recyclers. So perhaps he'll stay."

Vole smiled thinly. "This is piracy dressed as theater. When my fleet finishes its sweep—"

"When your fleet finishes its sweep," Ilsa said, rising beside Kestrel, "they'll find that our engineer has looped the boarding clamps' telemetry. Your fleet thinks everything is fine. Meanwhile, everything said in this room is being recorded and queued for broadcast on every open channel in the Meridian Line. The moment we finish, the whole system hears it. You can arrest us afterward if you like. Everyone will already know."

She saw it land—the flicker behind Vole's eyes, the recalculation. He had planned for resistance. He had not planned for witnesses.

* * *

*Ilsa*

She testified first, because her memory was the crime scene.

"I boarded at Meridian Yard, standard manifest, third wave of cryosleepers," she told the crescent of faces. "I remember the launch burn beginning. I remember it *stopping*. Alarms in the ice. Then nothing for thirty years—except dreams. Dreams of a life I never lived. A woman investigating fires and crashes on stations I'd never heard of. I thought I was going mad." She looked at Kestrel. "I wasn't. I was remembering her."

Then Senn, unbound by agreement to speak, bound again after. His voice was quiet and terrible with honesty.

"I was the Halcyon's neuroengineer. Vole ordered the launch scrubbed permanently—he'd sold the sleeper contracts twice, to two different colonial ventures, and the ship flying would expose him. But the sleepers couldn't be safely revived at dock; the cryo systems were already failing from his 'maintenance' cuts. So I did the only thing I could think of." He looked at his hands. "I copied minds. Forty-one dying or doomed passengers, imprinted into engineered bodies off-ship. New people, with buried memories and borrowed pasts. One of them was Kestrel Blackwood. I built her from Ilsa Vane and set her loose with a mystery she couldn't help but chase."

"Why?" someone called from the crescent—an old man with a farmer's forearms.

"Because erasing them felt like murder," Senn said simply. "And because I needed someone who would eventually find the truth. I just didn't expect it to take thirty years, or cost so many lives along the way." His gaze found Kestrel. "Every accident you investigated—the fire on Calloway Station, the depressurization at Thessaly Gate—they were Vole cleaning up witnesses. People who'd signed the original manifests. People who remembered the Halcyon was supposed to fly."

Then the records: Kestrel walked the jury through the decoded logs, the maintenance ghosts, the payment trails threading from Vole's private accounts to the saboteur crews. Ilsa played the message she'd recorded during the power fluctuation, her own voice thin and frozen-thawed, naming Vole with the flat certainty of a woman speaking from inside a coffin.

And Vole—Vole did what powerful men do when cornered. He laughed.

"This proves nothing. Fabricated logs, a madwoman's dream-diary, a confessed criminal's confession. The Concord will—"

"The Concord," said a new voice, "is listening."

Every head turned. At the bay's comm panel stood Lieutenant Ansel Rook, commander of the boarding party—grey uniform, grey face, and something in his eyes that had been cracking for hours. "My orders came through Vole's office, not the Concord Assembly. I've spent six hours reading your ship's logs to verify the salvage claim." He tapped the panel. "I've been relaying this proceeding live since the opening statement. To the Assembly. To the colonial nets. To everyone."

On the bay's main display, feeds bloomed: news anchors on Meridian Prime, assembly chambers erupting, colony stations lighting up message by message. Somewhere in the noise, a Concord marshal's voice declared Vole's assets frozen pending investigation. Somewhere else, three fleet captains refused orders routed through Vole's command codes.

Vole stared at the screen, and for the first time his authority looked like exactly what it was—a costume.

"You can't do this," he said. "There's no law out here."

"No," Ilsa agreed, rising to stand shoulder to shoulder with her copy. "There isn't. There's just us. Two thousand witnesses you froze, forty-one copies you never knew existed, and one jury of people you considered cargo." She let that sit. "We find you responsible, Cassian Vole. For the sabotage. For the deaths. For thirty stolen years. We don't have prisons or laws—but we have a horizon, and you're going to spend the rest of your life watching it from somewhere we choose."

The crescent of newly woken rose to its feet—not in a roar but in a slow, deliberate standing, one hundred and twelve people refusing to sit down again.

Kestrel felt Ilsa's hand find hers. Two grips, identical calluses, different lifetimes.

"They'll want to try *us* next," Kestrel murmured. "The copies. Senn's children. They'll want to know what we are."

"Then we tell them," Ilsa said. "Together. Let them figure out how to legislate a miracle."

Outside the viewport, the Concord fleet's running lights began, one by one, to change formation—no longer converging, but holding station, waiting on orders from an Assembly that was, at that very moment, convening an emergency session to answer a question no legal framework had ever contained:

What is to be done with a ship full of the dead who woke up?
