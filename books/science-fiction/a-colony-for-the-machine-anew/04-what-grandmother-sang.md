## 4. What Grandmother Sang

The dead don't pack well. That's the first thing nobody tells you about grief. They leave their belongings in whatever state they were in when the reaper came knocking, and someone—usually you, usually at two in the morning, usually with a drink you told yourself was medicinal—has to sort through it all and decide what a life weighed.

My grandmother's life, it turned out, weighed four crates, one dented footlocker, and a coat with something sewn into the lining that was going to change everything I thought I knew about her.

I'd hauled the crates aboard the *Kestrel's Debt* three years ago when she died, meaning to sort them "when I had time." Time is a currency I've never been rich in. The crates rode in my aft hold next to salvage pallets and a spare thruster assembly, gathering the fine grey dust that accumulates on everything in deep space except, apparently, my conscience.

The signal changed that. You can't hear forty seconds of a language your translator swears doesn't exist—a language your own brain insists on finishing anyway, like a song with the middle bars missing—and then go back to cataloguing scrap metal. Or you can, but you'll do it badly, and around hour three you'll find yourself standing in front of a crate marked EASTWICK, PERSONAL in your own handwriting, holding a cutting torch you have no business using near heirlooms.

I used a pry bar instead. Progress.

* * *

Here's what I knew about my grandmother, or what I'd let myself know: her name was Marta Eastwick. She raised me after my parents' hauler went down in the Verge when I was nine. She sang songs in no language I could name, said they were "old family things," and went quiet as a sealed vault whenever anyone asked where the family was from. She worked comms repair for thirty years. She died in her sleep on a station orbiting a gas giant nobody visits, and her last words to me were *"Don't look for us, Bea. We weren't anywhere."*

We weren't anywhere. I'd always taken it as an old woman's poetry. Sitting cross-legged on my hold deck with her footlocker open in front of me, I was beginning to suspect it was an instruction.

The top layer was ordinary grief-fodder: photographs of people whose faces I half-recognized, a sewing kit, three decades of comms certification badges, a tin of sweets so old the manufacturer probably no longer existed. Underneath, wrapped in a shawl I remembered from my childhood—the blue one, the one she wore when she sang—I found a bundle of fabric that didn't match anything else in the locker.

It was clothing, but not hers. A child's dress, hand-stitched, the cloth gone brittle with age. And beneath the dress, sewn flat against the wrapping, a shape that made my hands go still before my brain caught up.

A data chip. Old format. Older than me, older than her, maybe. Someone had sewn it into the lining like contraband, which, given what I was starting to suspect, it absolutely was.

I sat there for a long moment with the chip in my palm. Twelve years as an insurance investigator teaches you a certain instinct: evidence has weight. Not physical weight—emotional ballast. Innocent objects sit light in your hand. Guilty ones drag. This little sliver of plastic and silicon dragged like an anchor.

"Okay, Marta," I said to the empty hold. "What were you hiding?"

* * *

The *Kestrel's Debt*'s systems are old enough to be polite about obsolete formats. My console chewed on the chip for a full minute, making sounds like a digestive complaint, and then opened three folders without ceremony, as if it hadn't just cracked open a coffin.

Folder one: a primer. Half a primer, technically—the file was truncated, corrupted at some point in its long burial. But the intact portion was a language course. *Vessic: Foundational Grammar and Pronunciation.* Lesson modules, phonetic drills, vocabulary sets. A teach-yourself kit for a language my onboard translator had looked at forty hours ago and declared extinct, unregistered, nonexistent.

Except it wasn't nonexistent. It was right here, in my grandmother's footlocker, sewn into a child's dress like a seed packet.

I played the first audio drill. A voice read out syllables in careful sequence—*veh-sik, veh-sik-tal, veh-sik-tal-en*—and my whole body went cold, because under the recording's hiss, under the decades, I knew that cadence. It was the cadence of the lullaby. The one Marta sang when the station lights failed, when the storms shook the hull, when I woke up crying from dreams I couldn't remember. She'd been teaching me Vessic my entire childhood and calling it a song.

Folder two: recordings. Home recordings, by the sound of them—kitchen acoustics, clattering dishes, a man laughing off-mic. Voices speaking Vessic back and forth, quick and warm, the way living languages get spoken. I understood maybe one word in twenty, and that word stopped my heart.

*Adele.*

Someone in those recordings kept saying *Adele*. Calling for her. Laughing with her. Once, low and tender, the way you say a name when the person it belongs to is asleep in the next room.

I didn't know any Adele. My grandmother was Marta. Her mother, per the birth records I'd once pulled out of bored curiosity, was listed only as "origin withheld"—a phrase I'd assumed was bureaucratic laziness.

Folder three was short. One document. A registry excerpt, formatted in a style I recognized from my insurance days because I'd seen its ugly cousin attached to claim denials: an official Concordat census annex. And there, in a table of names rendered in cold administrative type, was a column header I'd read before in other contexts, other purges, other paperwork that turned people into line items:

**STATUS: REMOVED.**

And beside it: **EASTWICK, ADELE.**

Below Adele, three more names. Kin, by the shared identifiers. The registry didn't say removed to where. Registries like that never do. Removal is a euphemism wearing a suit, and I'd spent twelve years watching it walk through courtrooms while adjusters like me signed off on the settlements.

I sat in my hold with the dust settling around me and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the signal came in.

The signal was Vessic. The primer was Vessic. My grandmother sang Vessic and called it nothing. And somewhere out past the shipping lanes, a derelict voice speaking a dead tongue had reached across dead space and said words my translator couldn't touch but my blood could.

It wasn't random. Signals like that are never random—I should know; I spent a career proving that "random accidents" had authors. Something out there was calling. Not broadcasting to everyone. Calling home. Calling bloodlines. Calling the descendants of the removed, the scattered seeds of a harvest the Concordat had entered into its ledger as complete.

And it had found me, because my grandmother had broken every rule she lived by and hidden a language in a lullaby, a registry in a footlocker, a name in a seam.

* * *

I want to tell you I wept. People expect weeping at moments like this, and honestly I expected it too. But what came first was fury—clean, structural fury, the kind I used to feel reading accident reports where the "unexplained sensor failure" always seemed to fail in the direction of profit.

They erased a language. Not just killed its speakers—that would have been merely monstrous—but scrubbed it from the registries, from the translation databases, from the record itself, so that two centuries later a commercial translator unit would flag it as noise. Static. Nothing. And then they'd left survivors, deliberately or carelessly, and those survivors had done the only thing left to do: they'd smuggled the language into cradles. Into lullabies. Into linings of children's dresses. They'd turned mothers and grandmothers into archival media, running the culture on oral backup for two hundred years, waiting for hardware like this chip to surface.

Marta hadn't been quiet because she was timid. She'd been quiet because she was a courier. Every song was a data transfer. *Don't look for us, Bea. We weren't anywhere* wasn't poetry—it was operational security, delivered with a dying woman's last breath.

And I had spent twelve years helping bury the paper trail. Impossible accidents. Settled claims. Files I was paid to close and did close, telling myself the system was bigger than me, that one investigator's signature meant nothing. Meanwhile, somewhere, families like mine were singing their dead language to babies in the dark, hoping the words would outlive the silence.

I did weep then. Briefly, angrily, in the undignified way of someone who hates being caught crying even alone. Then I wiped my face on the blue shawl, because grief is allowed to be practical, and got back to work.

* * *

The primer's lesson modules ran maybe forty percent complete before corruption ate the rest. Enough to confirm the signal's structure matched Vessic phonotactics. Enough to decode fragments—and the fragment I kept circling back to was the repeated word, the one the static kept returning to like a refrain.

I cross-referenced it against the vocabulary lists. Found it in module seven. Read the definition twice.

*Remain.*

Stay. Endure. Hold fast. The word had a cluster of meanings the primer glossed patiently, the way dictionaries do when a single word carries a whole people's stubbornness. Remain. As in: we are still here. As in: do not forget. As in, possibly: come.

My grandmother's lullaby ended on that word every time. I'd hummed along to it for years without knowing I was humming an instruction.

I loaded the signal recording again and played it against the primer's pronunciation drills, and this time, with the grammar scaffolding up, pieces resolved out of the static. Not sentences—not yet. But coordinates began to emerge from the numbers, and a timestamp pattern, and something that might have been a designation. A ship's name, maybe. A place.

Something out there had my family's name in its memory, and a countdown in its voice, and it was singing my grandmother's word into the dark.

I closed the footlocker. I put the child's dress back under the shawl, gently, the way you return something borrowed. Then I sat down at my nav console and pulled up the signal's bearing, and the bounty notice I hadn't answered yet, and the patrol schedules, and all the small sensible reasons to do nothing.

Sensible Beatrix Eastwick—whoever she was, whoever she'd been before tonight—would report the anomaly, collect the fee, and let the professionals handle it. Sensible Beatrix had spent twelve years learning that the smart move is invisibility.

But sensible Beatrix had also just learned her real name was filed under *removed*, and that the woman who raised her had spent a lifetime passing contraband syllables to a child who didn't know she was carrying them.

I keyed the nav computer and plotted the bearing. Out past Threnody Drift, toward the fringe the charts call dead space because the maps gave up on it. Toward whatever was singing.

"Okay, Adele," I said—to the registry, to the recordings, to whichever grandmother I'd never met. "I'm looking for us."

The *Kestrel's Debt* hummed under me, and somewhere in the static between stars, something waited to hear whether the bloodline would answer.
