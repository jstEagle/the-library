## 12. The Relay at the Edge

The relay station hung at the edge of dead space like a gallows nobody had bothered to take down.

I'd been staring at it for six hours on approach, and that was the only honest description I could manage. It was old—older than the Concordat, older than the Purges, maybe older than the insurance industry, which in my experience was the only institution with a genuine claim to immortality. A spindle of dark metal and dead lights, a kilometer of antenna struts fanned out like the ribs of something that had once breathed. It turned slowly on its axis, the way derelicts do when nothing is left to argue with their momentum.

"Tell me you're seeing what I'm seeing," I said to ASH.

The ship-mind's voice came through my headset in Vessic first, then in trade-tongue, the way it always did now—as if it couldn't quite believe I'd learned enough to deserve the original. "I see it. Relay Nine-Adar. Last confirmed transmission: one hundred and twelve years ago."

"One hundred and twelve." I chewed on that. "And nobody noticed?"

"Nobody who was permitted to notice," ASH said, which was its way of saying *the Concordat*, which was its way of saying everything and nothing.

The Kestrel's Debt groaned around me as I brought her in on final burn. Twelve years I'd flown that ship. She'd hauled scrap, corpses, contraband I pretended not to inventory, and one memorable shipment of frozen embryos that taught me more about maritime law than any certification course ever had. She was ugly, underpowered, and held together by welds I'd done myself at three in the morning with a grudge in one hand and a torch in the other.

She was also, as of this moment, about to become an organ donor.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. First I had to see the body.

* * *

Docking with a station that has no power is a lesson in humility. No magnetic clamps, no guidance beams, no friendly voice on the comm telling you your approach vector makes you look like an amateur. Just thrusters, nerve, and a docking collar you have to align by eye while your own heartbeat tries to syncopate your hands.

I got us sealed on the fourth attempt. I chose not to count the first three.

The collar's seal hissed and held, and for a moment there was just the sound of my own breathing and the tick of cooling metal—the sound every derelict makes, the sound I'd heard on two hundred salvage jobs, the sound that says *nobody home*. I'd built a career on walking into that silence and reading it like a ledger. Cause of death: micrometeorite. Cause of death: reactor fault. Cause of death: crew error, per the report, though the scorch patterns told me otherwise, and I took the settlement money and kept my mouth shut, because that was the job, because that was the price of staying invisible, because—

Because I had a lot of practice not finishing those sentences. Less every day, lately.

I cycled through into the relay's spine and popped my helmet lamp.

Dust in null gravity doesn't settle; it hangs, a grey fog of skin cells and metal particulate that two centuries of vacuum never bothered to sort out. My lamp carved a cone through it, and the cone landed on the main trunk line running down the station's axis—and I stopped.

Not because it was broken. Broken I knew. Broken had a grammar: stress fractures, heat scoring, the clean amnesia of component failure. I'd written up broken a thousand times.

This wasn't broken. This was cut.

Someone had taken a plasma torch to the primary data conduit—not at a joint, not where a failure would be expected, but mid-span, in a place no engineer would ever route maintenance. And they hadn't just cut it. They'd cut it four times, at intervals, and then they'd pulled the severed sections *out* and capped the ends with fresh welds. Neat welds. Professional welds. Welds done by someone standing exactly where I was standing, doing exactly what I was doing, except with orders instead of grief.

"Well," I said to the dust. "Hello, murder weapon."

ASH's voice came soft in my ear. "Beatrix?"

"It's not decayed." I ran my glove along the welded cap. Under the dust, the metal was still bright. "It was killed. Deliberately. Somebody came here with a torch and a work order and made sure this relay would never pass another word."

"How long ago?"

I checked the oxidation bloom on the weld seams, the micro-pitting, the way the dust had stratified over the cuts. Old salvage habit. "Sixty years. Maybe seventy. Long enough that everyone who ordered it is dead or promoted."

"That is often the same thing," ASH said.

* * *

I spent nine hours going deck by deck, and the story got worse the deeper I went.

The power plant hadn't failed—it had been shut down from the control room, cleanly, by someone with valid credentials. The backup batteries hadn't drained—they'd been disconnected, terminals wrapped in insulating film like presents being saved for later. The long-range antenna array, the beautiful kilometer-wide fan of it, hadn't degraded. Three of its feed lines had been severed and the rest detuned, a sabotage so thorough it was almost tender, the way you'd disable a dangerous animal you didn't want to watch suffer.

Every system on this station had been murdered individually. By hand. With paperwork.

I found the work order eventually, in a maintenance terminal still holding its last charge like a dying man's last sentence. Concordat Directorate of Communications Infrastructure. Authorization code, supervisor sign-off, a completion certificate filed and stamped. *Station decommissioned due to structural obsolescence.* Sixty-eight years ago.

Structural obsolescence. I'd seen that phrase before. I'd *written* that phrase before—or variants of it—in claim reports where the truth was too expensive to print. There's a whole vocabulary the powerful use to describe the things they break: obsolete, redundant, nonviable, regrettable. Words that turn violence into housekeeping.

"You understand what this means," I said. I was sitting on the floor of the control room, back against a console, because my legs had opinions and I'd decided to ignore them.

"They did not let it die," ASH said. "They ensured it."

"They killed the relay so the testimony could never leave. Sixty-eight years ago. Which means—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Which means they've known. All this time. They knew the archive existed, they knew where it would try to go, and they cut its throat at the door and then went home and had dinner."

"And yet," ASH said, "the Vigilant Remembrance continued to run. Two hundred years. Waiting for a custodian."

"Yeah." I looked at my hands inside the gloves. "About that."

* * *

Here's the arithmetic, laid out flat, because I'm an investigator and lying to myself is the one fraud I've never managed to certify.

The Vigilant Remembrance had nineteen days of reactor life when I found her. We were eleven days into that now. Eight left. The nearest functioning relay—the next one in the chain, the one that could actually push the archive into settled space—was twenty-three days away at best burn. This station, Relay Nine-Adar, was the only other option, and it was a corpse with its tendons cut.

Unless somebody performed a resurrection.

I floated in the control room and inventoried what a resurrection would require. New power source: the station's batteries were disconnected but intact—I could reterminate them, given parts. Conduit repair: the cut sections were gone, so I'd need to bridge roughly forty meters of missing data line. Antenna retuning: doable from the console if the console lived, which meant feeding it stable power, which meant—

Which meant the Kestrel's Debt.

Her fuel cell stack could run this station's systems for the duration of a broadcast. Her power conduits could bridge the gaps in the trunk line—she carried spares, she always carried spares, she was a hoarder of spare parts the way I was a hoarder of unresolved guilt. Her antenna coupler, her regulator array, her backup comms board—all of it, donated.

And without her? Without her I was a person in a suit, tethered to a dead station, at the edge of dead space, with no ride home. The Vigilant Remembrance had maybe eight days of reactor life and no shuttle worth the name. Even if the broadcast worked—even if the archive flew—there was no version of this where I climbed back aboard my ship afterward and sailed away whistling.

I sat with that for a long time. Long enough that the station's slow rotation carried the distant stars across the whole sweep of the observation port, a slow procession of witnesses.

Twelve years ago, an adjuster named Beatrix Eastwick had stood in the wreckage of a mining platform and read a report that blamed a gas leak on crew negligence, and had known—it had known, the way you know a lullaby—that the leak was deliberate, that the crew had organized, that the corporation had solved its labor problem with a spark, and had signed the report anyway because signing was the job and the job was invisibility and invisibility was survival. Grandmother Adele had survived the Purges by disappearing. She'd taught my mother to disappear, who taught me. Vanish, blend, don't be legible, don't be findable, don't be *targetable*. The family art. The inheritance I'd actually received, before I knew there was another one waiting in a dead language.

And here was the bill for all that safety, itemized: a plasma-cut relay, sixty-eight years of silence, two thousand voices held in a dying ship because the people who might have carried them had learned my family's trick too well.

Invisibility wasn't neutral. Invisibility was the Concordat's favorite tool, and every one of us who practiced it sharpened the blade for them.

"ASH," I said. "How much of the Kestrel can this station use?"

A pause. When ASH paused, I'd learned, it wasn't computing. It was being kind. "All of her," it said finally. "If you give her all."

"Yeah," I said. "That's what I thought."

* * *

I won't pretend the decision was clean. It wasn't one of those moments the entertainment dramas like, where the hero stares into the middle distance and the music swells and everything resolves. It was me, alone in a dead control room, eating a ration bar I couldn't taste, making lists.

List one: everything the Kestrel had that the station needed.

List two: everything I would lose.

List two was short. List two was *everything*.

I thought about the bounty. Report the signal, collect the finder's fee, buy a new ship, stay small, stay safe. I'd priced that option honestly back in chapter one of this disaster, and I'd priced it again at Threnody Drift with Ovid Rask counting credits across the table, and I'd priced it a third time somewhere in the black between the Drift and the Remembrance, at 0300 ship-time, staring at the ceiling of my bunk. The number never changed. The number was always *everything I am versus everything they were*.

Two thousand testimonies. Ordinary voices. People who'd planted gardens on a colony ship and argued about ration portions and sung songs to children who would grow up hunted for the crime of remembering. My grandmother among them—young, recorded, alive in the memory core, singing a lullaby I knew by heart before I knew what it meant.

You don't get to inherit a song and keep the silence that came with it. That's not how inheritance works. That's how theft works, and I'd been on the wrong side of that transaction my entire career.

I finished the ration bar. I wiped my hands. I opened the tool locker on the Kestrel's Debt and started pulling her apart.

* * *

Three days. That's what it took, and I'll spare you most of it, because most of it was the least glamorous labor in the universe: hauling conduit by hand through zero-g corridors, welding bridges over the murdered sections of trunk line, reterminating battery banks while my fingers cramped inside gloves rated for work half this fine. I slept four hours a night in the Kestrel's cockpit because it was the only warm place left, and each morning I took something else off her to keep the station alive.

The fuel cell stack went first. Then the regulator array. Then forty meters of power conduit, stripped out of her walls so methodically that by day two she looked gutted, hollowed, a fish skeleton in a vacuum dock. I talked to her the whole time. I'm not embarrassed. Well. I'm somewhat embarrassed. But anyone who's kept a ship alive for twelve years talks to her, and anyone who says they don't is lying or rich.

"I know," I told her, wrestling her comms board free of its mounts. "I know, girl. Worst salvage job of your career, and you're the cargo."

She answered with the tick and groan of expanding metal, which I chose to interpret as forgiveness.

On the third day, I fed the last splice into the trunk line, sealed the junction, and stood back in the control room while ASH walked the station's systems awake one by one through my suit link. Batteries: live. Console: live. Trunk line: continuous, end to end, green across the board. And then the antenna array, the great ribcage of it outside the port, began to drink current for the first time in sixty-eight years, and the dust in the control room stirred as old servos woke in the walls, and the station hummed—a low, vast note I felt through my boots more than heard.

Relay Nine-Adar, breathing again.

"Alignment will take time," ASH said. "The array was detuned deliberately. I must re-phase each element. Hours, perhaps a day."

"We have eight minus three," I said. "Five days, give or take the Remembrance's optimism about her own reactor."

"Then we are sufficient," ASH said, and there was something in its voice I hadn't heard before. Not hope exactly. Hope's older, tireder cousin. "Beatrix. Why did you do this? You had alternatives. Costly ones, but survivable."

I watched the antenna turn against the stars, catching light along its spars like a blade being drawn slowly from a sheath.

"Because they cut this thing's throat sixty-eight years ago and filed the paperwork," I said, "and everybody who signed that paperwork went home to dinner, and nobody ever got a bill. I spent twelve years writing reports that helped them skip out on the check." I tapped the console, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "This is the bill. I'm the delivery service."

ASH was quiet a moment. "Your grandmother would have said something in Vessic here. Something about debts and doors."

"Teach it to me later," I said. "Right now, show me the alignment diagnostics. If I'm going to die at the edge of nowhere, I intend to die having balanced my books."

We worked. The station hummed. And that was when the long-range passive array—awake barely an hour, listening out of pure professional habit—pinged me with a contact warning.

One signature. Far out, deep in the fringe, burning hard toward us.

Running the registry profile in my head took two seconds. Hull class, drive signature, fleet transponder pattern burned into my memory at Threnody Drift when Ovid Rask slid the intel across the table and said *they're hunting the source, Eastwick, and they don't come in peace*.

The Concordat salvage fleet. Lead ship: CSV Meridian's Bane. Captain: Ilsa Vane.

Four days out, at their burn. One day behind my schedule. Give or take.

I looked at the humming antenna, at the gutted ghost of my ship in the vacuum dock, at the five-day countdown ticking in the corner of my display, and I did the only reasonable thing.

I started working faster.
