## 15. Nineteen Minutes

The reactor gave us nineteen minutes. ASH told me the number the way a doctor tells a family there's nothing more to be done—gently, precisely, in Vessic first and then in trade-tongue, as if the dead language might soften the arithmetic.

*Nineteen minutes,* it said. *Perhaps twenty-two. The containment lattice is failing asymmetrically.*

"Asymmetrically," I repeated. My hands were shaking, and I watched them shake with a kind of detached professional interest, the way I used to watch my own signature move across falsified claim settlements. "That's a very polite word for *exploding*."

*All words are polite words for exploding,* ASH said, *if you wait long enough.*

Outside the relay station's cracked viewport, the Vigilant Remembrance hung against the black like a wound that had learned to hold its shape—two centuries of dying, and still refusing to. And beyond her, small and cold and closing, the running lights of the Concordat salvage fleet. Three ships. The lead one—Ilsa Vane's *Charitable Account*, because of course the Concordat named their hunters after virtues and accounting practices—had gone to weapons-hot forty seconds ago.

I knew because my console told me so. I also knew because some animal part of me, the part twelve years of insurance work had never managed to kill, could feel targeting radar the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder.

"Nineteen minutes," I said again. "And they'll fire before ten."

*Then you have perhaps nine minutes to make your case,* ASH said. *I have reviewed your case files, Beatrix Eastwick. You were very good at making cases.*

"I was very good at burying them."

*Yes,* said the ship-mind, with two hundred years of patience and not one gram of comfort. *That is why they will believe you when you stop.*

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about the last minutes of your life: they're mostly logistics.

I'd spent the last six hours splicing cable by hand, jury-rigging the murdered relay back to life with parts stripped from the Kestrel's Debt—my ship, my home for eleven years, my coffin-to-be if this went wrong, cannibalized into an antenna array because justice apparently runs on salvaged copper. My fingers were burned in three places. There was coolant residue in my hair that smelled like burnt sugar. Somewhere on the deck plating was the wrench I'd thrown in frustration at hour four, and I remember thinking, with terrible clarity: *if I die here, someone will find that wrench, and they will know exactly who I was.*

A person leaves evidence. That was the first thing they taught me at Meridian Mutual. Every accident is a confession; every scene is a signed statement. You just have to know how to read it.

So I read mine. One exhausted investigator. One sabotaged relay, repaired. One derelict colony ship rigged to broadcast—or trying to be. One weapons-hot Concordat captain with orders to recover or destroy the anomaly. And nineteen minutes on the clock.

The evidence said: pirate. That's what Vane's marines would see when they boarded, if they boarded. A scavenger stripping a dead station. Easy report, easy file, easy erasure—the Concordat had been writing that same sentence for two hundred years, in different hands.

Unless the whole crew heard something else first.

"ASH," I said. "The transmission plan. Walk me through it again."

*The archive requires the relay's main antenna, which you have restored to sixty-one percent function. Full broadcast time: fourteen minutes. We do not have fourteen minutes.*

"So we don't send everything."

*You refused to choose which testimonies to jettison. I admired that refusal. I am now asking you to violate it.*

"No." I said it fast, before the arithmetic could get its hooks in me. "We're not cutting the archive down to fit the window. We're changing the window."

*Explain.*

I looked at the fleet lights. At the guns I couldn't see but could feel. And I thought about Ilsa Vane—not the captain, the person inside the uniform, the one whose executive officer I'd heard on the open channel urging her to follow orders, the one who'd stalled a boarding action for three hours demanding proof of authorization she almost certainly already had. I'd spent twelve years studying how institutions eat people. I knew the shape of the moment a person decides whether to be eaten or to bite.

"You've been keeping those testimonies running for two hundred years," I said. "Two thousand voices. You know them all."

*I do.*

"If you could make one of them speak—just one—to a room full of people who were raised to believe none of them ever existed. Which one?"

ASH was quiet for 4.2 seconds. For a machine mind, that's a speech.

*There is a testimony recorded by a woman named Sefa Ilves-Vane,* it said. *Recorded eleven days before the Purge fleets arrived. She was a schoolteacher. She describes teaching her students the Vessic alphabet in secret, chalk on slate, under the sound of singing so the monitors would hear only music. She ends the recording by saying—"*

"Don't tell me," I said. "Play it."

*To whom?*

"To everyone." I was already moving, hands finding the broadcast controls, the ones I'd rebuilt from my own ship's guts. "Not encrypted. Not tight-beam. Open channel, wide-spectrum, plain as a town crier. If Vane's got her fleet channel open—and she does, because discipline is a habit—you put Sefa Ilves-Vane's voice on it, and every man and woman on those three ships hears a dead woman teach children to spell."

*They may fire anyway.*

"They might. But it's harder to shoot a grandmother." I paused. "It's harder to shoot a schoolteacher. Believe me. I've read the reports of people who tried."

*Beatrix.* ASH's voice did something I hadn't heard it do before—it dropped, the way a human voice drops when it's afraid of being overheard. *Once transmitted openly, the testimony cannot be recalled. The Concordat will know the archive exists, know its content, know you sent it. Whatever happens in the next nineteen minutes, you will spend the rest of your life as a visible woman.*

"Not a woman," I said. "But yes. Visible." I laughed, and it came out wrong, too high, the laugh of someone standing on a ledge admiring the view. "You know, I spent eleven years making myself unfindable. Changed my registry name twice. Paid a fixer in Threnody Drift to scrub my biometrics from three databases. I told myself it was tradecraft. It was fear wearing a work uniform." I set my palm flat on the console, over the cables I'd spliced from the Kestrel's Debt, from the ship that had been my invisibility made metal. "My grandmother hid so well that even her own blood forgot her. Two centuries of hiding, and the Concordat calls it peace. So no, ASH. I'm done being a rumor. Put her on the line."

*Sefa Ilves-Vane,* ASH said, *is on the line.*

* * *

The open channel hissed for a moment—that dead-air breath before any broadcast, the universe clearing its throat—and then a woman began to speak Vessic.

I understood maybe one word in five. My primer was half-finished; my ear was worse. But I didn't need the grammar. I needed the cadence, and the cadence was unmistakable, because I'd heard it every night of my childhood through a bulkhead thin as paper: the rising lilt of a teacher asking a question, the patient fall of a teacher waiting for the answer. Chalk on slate. Singing to cover the lesson.

Then ASH laid the translation beneath it, subvocalized into my ear and, I realized with a jolt, riding the carrier wave into every receiver within light-seconds:

*"My name is Sefa Ilves-Vane. Today is the eleventh of Thawmonth, in the year the Concordat calls 2147 and we call the last year. I am recording this in the school cellar because tomorrow they install the new monitoring array, and after that, singing will have to be enough.*

*"Today I taught them the letter that begins 'remain.' Twenty-three children. The youngest is four. She drew the letter wrong and then drew it right, and when she got it right she looked at me the way you look at a door opening.*

*"If you are hearing this, then someone kept the door open. Whoever you are: we were here. We taught our children to spell in cellars. We sang loud so the monitors would hear music and miss the words. We were not ghosts. We were a people, mid-sentence.*

*"Do not let the sentence end with us."*

Silence on the channel. Real silence—the kind that has weight, that presses on the eardrum. Even the reactor's death-rattle seemed to hold its breath.

Then, on the fleet frequency, still open, still disciplined, someone aboard the Charitable Account said, very quietly, in a voice that forgot to cut the transmit key:

"That's... my family name."

* * *

I want to tell you I felt triumphant. What I actually felt was cold, and tired, and aware of the wrench on the deck plating, and counting.

Fourteen minutes since ASH gave me the number. Five left, maybe six, and the containment lattice was failing asymmetrically, which meant unpredictably, which meant the countdown was less a countdown than a promise the reactor intended to break early.

"Vane's channel," I said. "Is she transmitting?"

*She has opened a point-to-point link to this station. Audio only. She has not fired.*

"She's asking permission."

*She is asking for authorization to stand down,* ASH corrected. *There is a difference. Her commodore has denied it twice.*

"Put her through."

The channel clicked. And there she was—Ilsa Vane, compressed to a voice, and the voice was nothing like I'd expected. I'd built her out of case files: Concordat command cadence, clipped, certain, the accent of people who sign things. Instead she sounded like someone talking with a hand pressed hard over a wound.

"Station, this is Captain Vane, Concordat Salvage Command. Identify yourself."

"Beatrix Eastwick," I said. And saying it—my real name, the buried one, the one I'd paid good money to erase—felt like pulling a splinter out of my own spine. "Formerly of Meridian Mutual Investigations. Currently of nowhere, which is where you people put my grandmother."

"Eastwick." A pause. On the open fleet channel behind her, I could hear the crew noise change—could hear Sefa Ilves-Vane's recording being replayed, someone requesting it again, someone else saying *shut up, I'm listening.* "That name is on a manifest I read tonight. Passenger list, colony ship Vigilant Remembrance. Adele Eastwick. Custodian-class."

"My grandmother."

"Your grandmother's name is in my orders, Eastwick. As a deletion."

"I know," I said. "I spent twelve years deleting things for the Concordat's insurers. Impossible accidents. Fires that started themselves. Ships that sank with their paperwork conveniently aboard. I was good at it. That's why I'm telling you this, Captain, and not some idealist: I know exactly what your commodore asked you to recover or destroy. It's not a ship. It's a receipt. Two thousand receipts, in the voices of the people he erased, and you are the last pair of hands they will ever pass through."

"You're asking me to mutiny."

"I'm asking you to investigate." I leaned toward the pickup, and my burned fingers left a smear on the housing, and I thought: *evidence.* "You demanded proof of authorization for three hours. Fine. Here's mine. ASH—give her the manifest segment. Adele Eastwick, custodian registration, bloodline succession, timestamped two hundred years ago. Give her the Purge order too. The real one. Let her run it against her own academy records."

*Transmitting,* ASH said.

And then, because nineteen minutes was really more like four, and because I have never in my life known when to stop talking, I said the rest of it fast:

"Captain Vane, I'm not asking you to save me. In about three minutes I'm going to be a debris field no matter what you do. I'm asking you to decide what your report says. Option one: investigator destroyed resisting lawful salvage, anomaly neutralized, file closed, and you spend the next thirty years knowing what you filed under *closed*. Option two: you write down what you just heard. Schoolteacher. Cellar. Twenty-three children learning to spell the word *remain*. You write it in your own hand, and you sign it, and you let whatever comes of that come." I swallowed. My mouth tasted like coolant and copper. "I did option one for twelve years. I can tell you exactly what it costs. It costs everything except your life, and eventually it takes that too, from the inside."

Static. Long static. Behind it, faintly, the fleet channel: someone playing the schoolteacher's voice a third time, softer now, like a lullaby—which is, I suppose, what it had always been.

"Eastwick." Vane's voice had changed. Not warmer. Decided. There's a difference, and I've heard both, and only one of them ever mattered. "How long does your full archive need to broadcast?"

"Fourteen minutes at full power. I have maybe three."

Another pause. When she spoke again, she wasn't speaking to me.

"Helm, bring us alongside that station. All ships: secure weapons. You heard me. *Secure.* Comms, open a channel to Fleet Command—no, not the commodore's channel. The broad legal channel. Everything we receive from this station goes out on the record, unedited, logged under maritime salvage statute seven. If the Concordat wants to destroy testimony, it can do it in front of witnesses." A breath. "Eastwick. Your three minutes just became however long my ship can buy you. Don't waste them on speeches. Send the dead."

* * *

I didn't waste them on speeches.

I spent them the way you spend anything precious: badly and completely, hands moving, calling up the archive queue while ASH and I argued about bandwidth allocation like an old married couple, while the Charitable Account swung her bulk between the relay and the reactor blast vector like a mother turning her back to the wind, while somewhere below my feet the Vigilant Remembrance's heart finally, finally began to give out.

*Queue loaded,* ASH said. *Two thousand one hundred and six testimonies. Fourteen minutes required. Available time: uncertain.*

"How uncertain?"

*The reactor says ninety seconds. The reactor has been optimistic all day.*

"Then start the clock and start the broadcast, and if we run out—" I stopped. Looked at the viewport, at the old ship that had kept faith alone in the dark for two hundred years, longer than any nation, longer than any lie. "ASH. When it goes. Say goodbye in Vessic. I want to hear it in the language they tried to kill."

*Beatrix Eastwick,* the ship-mind said, *heir of Adele, keeper of the door—it has been my honor to remain with you.*

The relay fired.

The first testimony went out across settled space, plain and open and undeniable—a dead woman's voice, saying *we were here*—and behind it, gathering like weather, came the rest.

Behind *that*, silent and enormous, the reactor began to sing.
