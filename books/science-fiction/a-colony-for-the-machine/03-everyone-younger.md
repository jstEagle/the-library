## 3. Everyone Younger

The coordinates the dying signal had given her were wrong. Casper knew this the way a sailor knows a storm by the taste of the air — not through instruments, but through the body's older instruments, the ones that predated language. The Vespera should not have been where the signal said it was. The closed zone had no navigational logic to it; the war had seen to that. Buoy chains had been scuttled, beacon frequencies reassigned, and the charts that remained were the kind of charts that lie politely, the way official things lie, with stamps and serial numbers and the smell of authority.

So she flew the last six hours by eye and by feel, the *Magpie's Lament* crawling through a debris field that no registry had ever admitted to. Hull plating drifted past her viewport in slow rotations, each piece catching the light of a distant, unnamed star. Some of it was old — decades old, by the pitting and the micrometeorite scarring — and some of it was not old at all. She passed a section of bulkhead with a stenciled serial number still legible, and her eye snagged on it the way a finger snags on a splinter, and she could not have said why. She logged it without logging it, the way she did everything she didn't want to remember.

Forty-one backups slept behind her in the hold, drawing their trickle of power, dreaming whatever backups dreamed. She had not opened that door in three years, four months. She knew the count the way she knew her own heartbeat.

The Vespera appeared out of the dark the way drowned things appear out of dark water — all at once, and too close, and with the terrible intimacy of something that has been waiting for you specifically.

She saw it first as an absence: a hole in the starfield, a patch of black that swallowed the light behind it. Then her running lights swept across it and the hole became a ship — a deep-salvage hull, heavy-shouldered, built for hauling things that did not want to be hauled. Eleven years of dust lay on it in a fine grey fur, thick enough to blur the registry markings, and the dust was unbroken. No thruster wash had touched it. No maintenance arm. Nothing had moved on the outside of that ship in a very long time.

But the transponder answered her hail.

That was the first wrong thing. The transponder answered with a carrier tone, clean and strong, from a ship that should have been a cold hulk — and the identification string it broadcast was eleven years out of date, still carrying the old route codes, still announcing to an empty sector that the Vespera was inbound to Meridian Station with a full salvage manifest, all systems nominal, expected arrival in forty days.

Forty days. Forty days from eleven years ago.

Casper sat with her hands hovering over the console for a full minute before she touched anything. The habit of caution, worn smooth as a river stone. She ran a thermal scan. The ship was warm. Not engine-warm — life-warm. Nine heat signatures, distributed through the crew deck, steady as sleeping breath.

Nine. The Vespera had carried nine.

She should have reported it. That was the protocol, and protocol existed for reasons, and the reasons were all good ones, and she had followed them for eleven years without exception. A derelict with live signatures in the closed zone was an Authority matter, full stop. You flagged it, you backed off to the minimum safe distance, and you let the people with gunships and jurisdiction do whatever it was they did with things found in the closed zone.

She thought about the signal. *Please. Anyone. We can't—* and the cut, mid-word, mid-breath.

She thought about the dust on the hull, and the warm bodies underneath it.

She thought, though she tried not to, about the hold behind her, and the rule she lived by, and how rules were just fears that had learned to stand up straight.

"Magpie's Lament to Vespera," she said, and her voice came out rougher than she intended. "I'm answering your ping. I'm coming alongside. If anyone's alive over there, I'd take it kindly if you didn't shoot me."

Nothing answered. But as her docking clamps found the old salvage frame and the airlock cycled, she heard — faint, through the hull, through the dust, through eleven years — a sound that might have been a bell, or might have been a voice, or might have been nothing at all, the way most sounds in the deep dark were nothing at all until they weren't.

* * *

The air inside the Vespera was wrong in a way she couldn't name at first. It took her three steps down the access corridor to understand it: it was warm air, lived-in air, air that had been breathed and rebreathed by scrubbers until it carried that faint human sourness every crewed ship had — and it was also stale in the way air goes stale when it hasn't been refreshed in years. Both at once. Air that had been breathed for a long time by people who had not opened a single vent.

Her lamp swept the corridor. Everything was neat. Too neat. Tools racked in exact order. A mug on a hook, washed and dried and hanging precisely at the angle of a hook that had been adjusted to hold it. A child's drawing taped to a bulkhead — no. Not a child's drawing. She looked closer. It was a technical schematic of the relay array, drawn in careful, competent line-work, the kind of thing a ship's engineer might sketch from memory. But it was drawn in crayon.

She found the crew in the mess hall, because that was where crews gathered when there was nothing left to do, and there was clearly nothing left to do on the Vespera, and there had been nothing left to do for a very long time.

Nine of them. Alive. Awake. Watching the door when she came through it, all nine faces turning toward her lamp with the synchronized hunger of people who had been waiting — and there was the second wrong thing, the one that stopped her in the doorway with her hand frozen on her sidearm's safety, though she hadn't decided to move it there.

They were old. And they were young. And both of those things were true in a way that made no sense, and her mind kept trying to resolve them the way an eye resolves an optical illusion, flipping and flipping and failing to settle.

The woman nearest her — grey at the temples, lines carved deep around her mouth, a captain's bearing worn into the spine — had hands folded on the table in front of her, and the hands were a child's hands. Small, smooth, the knuckles still soft, the fingernails like little polished shells. The wrists above them were an old woman's wrists, corded and spotted. The mismatch ran all the way through them. A heavyset man in the corner had a young man's thick dark hair and a face gone soft and slack with age, jowls and all. A tall figure by the viewport had the posture of someone in their sixties and the skin of someone in their twenties, taut and unlined, stretched over a structure that had settled.

No. She looked again. It was worse than that, and better than that, and she couldn't decide which. It wasn't that they were mixed. It was that they were *reversed* — as if someone had taken each of them and run them backward through their own lives, unevenly, the way a recording degrades. The captain's face had the deep lines of sixty years but the smooth, unweathered texture of youth underneath them, like a mask of age laid over new skin. The youngest-looking among them — a slight man with a boy's fine features — moved with the deliberate economy of the very old.

They were all looking at her, and their eyes were the oldest things in the room. Eyes do not age backward. Whatever had happened to their bodies, it had not touched the thing behind the eyes, and nine pairs of ancient, patient, exhausted eyes watched her from faces that her mind refused to reconcile.

"You're real," the captain said. Her voice was a ruin — cracked, thinned, the voice of a woman who had talked herself hoarse years ago and never fully recovered. "You're an actual ship. An actual person."

"Casper Oyelaran," Casper said. "Freight. I picked up your distress ping. Or — a ping. From this sector." She kept her hand where it was. "You're the Vespera."

"We're the Vespera." The captain said it like a woman confirming her own name after a long illness. "Anaya Okonkwo-Reiss. Captain. This is my crew." She gestured, and the child's hand swept across the room, and Casper saw that the gesture was practiced — that the captain had introduced her crew this way many times, to no one, in an empty room, keeping the ritual alive the way you keep a fire alive when the wood is gone.

The crew said their names. Some of them were barely audible. The engineer — a huge man reduced to a huge man's skeleton wearing a young man's face — wept silently through his own introduction, and no one remarked on it, which told Casper they had all wept through it, many times.

Then the slight man with the boy's features stepped forward, and something in the way the others parted for him told Casper this was the one who mattered.

"Iyo Sefu," he said. "Navigator." And then he did the thing she would think about for the rest of her life, the thing that turned the whole encounter from a horror into something else: he looked at her flight jacket, at the patch on her shoulder, and his ancient eyes went sharp and quick, and he said, quietly, urgently, before she had even finished deciding whether to be afraid:

"That's a Kestrel-class long-hauler. Pre-war. You didn't buy that from the Authority — they scrapped the class. You bought it gray-market, which means you don't love the Authority, which means—" He stopped himself. Drew a breath. The boy's chest rose and fell under the old man's careful breathing. "Please. Before you say anything. Before you do anything. Don't report us. Not to the Colonial Authority. Not to anyone with an Authority channel. Please."

The room went very still. Nine ancient-young faces, all watching her. The engineer's silent tears kept falling.

"The Authority doesn't come out here," Casper said slowly. "The closed zone is closed. That's the whole point of it."

"It comes out here for us," Iyo said. "It will come out here for what we found. And when it comes, it won't be a rescue. Captain Oyelaran — may I call you that? — I was a navigator for thirty-one years before—" he made a small, terrible gesture at himself, at all of them, "—before this. I know ship classes the way you know your own hands. And I know what the Authority does with things it wants forgotten, because I've spent eleven years in a dead ship with eight people who watched it happen, and I have had a very long time to think about what forgetting costs, and who pays it."

He came closer. Up close, the wrongness of him was almost unbearable — the fine boy's skin, the faint down on his cheeks, and the eyes above it that had navigated thirty years of dark and had seen something in this particular dark that had aged his soul while his body ran the other way. His voice dropped to almost nothing.

"You heard the ping. You came anyway. Into the closed zone, alone, against every protocol there is. So either you're a fool, or you're someone who answers when the dead call." He held her gaze. "I'm betting my crew's lives that you're the second one. Don't make me wrong."

Casper stood in the doorway of the mess hall of a ship that had been dead eleven years and full of the living the whole time, and she felt the strange vertigo of a decision arriving before she'd noticed herself making it.

Behind her, through the umbilical, her own ship hummed quietly, and somewhere in its hold, forty-one pods drew their trickle of power, and she thought — with a tenderness that startled her, that came out of nowhere, that had no business arriving in this place — *they've been waiting too. All of them. Everyone out here is waiting for someone to come back.*

"I'm not reporting you," she heard herself say.

* * *

They fed her tea that tasted of recycled water and eleven-year-old leaves, and she drank it because refusing would have been worse. The mess hall was warm in a way that spoke of rationed heat, carefully hoarded. She began, without quite deciding to, to see the shape of their survival: the dust on the hull was real, but the scrubbers had been hand-maintained, cannibalized, rebuilt. They had not been adrift. They had been *buried alive and tending their own grave*, and they had done it well enough to live.

"How," she said. Not *why are you young* — she wasn't ready for that, and she could see they weren't ready to tell it. Just: how.

"Later," Captain Okonkwo-Reiss said. Her child's hands wrapped around an adult-sized mug. "There's an order to it. There has to be, or none of it holds together."

But Iyo Sefu walked her back toward the airlock, alone, and in the corridor with the crayon schematic he stopped, and put his small hand flat against the bulkhead, and said:

"Before you go back to your ship — before you decide how much of us to believe — I want you to look at one thing. Not the thing itself. I'm not ready. We're not ready. But I want you to see the shape of it, so you know we're not mad."

He led her one deck down, to a sensor station that had been kept running on scavenged power, its screens dim but alive. On the main display, held in memory for eleven years like a pressed flower, was a scan.

A hull. A big one. Colony-ship scale, the kind of hull that only got built in the first century, before the corporations standardized everything into interchangeable boxes. It was broken — broken the way things are broken by other things, not by accidents. The scan rendered the damage in cold topographic lines, and even in the cold lines Casper could read it: scoring, penetration, sequential failure along a deliberate axis. She had never seen a weapons-damaged hull. No one her age had. But the shape of it was legible anyway, the way a scream is legible in any language.

"What am I looking at?" she asked, though some part of her, the part that had snagged on the serial number in the debris field, already knew.

"Debris," Iyo said. "From a colony ship called the Kestrel. Lost with all hands, sixty-two years ago. That's what the record says." His voice was very quiet. "The record is wrong, Captain Oyelaran. The record was *made* wrong. And we found the proof of it, and the proof is why we are what we are now, and I need you to help us carry it home."

The screens glowed. The nine of them waited somewhere above, in their reversed bodies, with their unaged eyes. Behind her, through the umbilical, her own hold hummed with its forty-one sleepers, and Casper Oyelaran stood between two cargoes of the dead — the ones she carried and the ones he'd found — and understood, with a cold settling certainty, that her route had just changed forever.

"How long do we have," she said, "before someone comes looking for me?"
