## 5. Records That Agree With Each Other

The archives lived in the oldest part of Casper's ship, a closet-sized compartment behind the galley where the hull plating still bore the manufacturer's stamp from forty years before she was born. She had inherited the collection the way you inherit a scar — without asking for it, without quite understanding it. Three physical drives, sealed against radiation and tampering, holding every colonial record she'd been able to buy, beg, or salvage across eleven years of running the Loop: shipping manifests, census fragments, colony charters, court rulings, weather telemetry from worlds that no longer answered their radios.

She had never needed all of it at once. She had never needed any of it, really. The archives were ballast, something heavy to keep her honest.

Now she spread them across the galley table like a hand of cards she was afraid to read.

Iyo Sefu sat across from her, his hands folded on the tabletop — an adult's deliberate gesture performed by hands that were not adult's, knuckles smooth as river stones, fingers too short for the wrists they belonged to. He had refused the copilot's chair because his feet didn't reach the floor rests properly, and he'd made the refusal sound like courtesy. That was the worst of it, Casper thought. Not the wrongness of their bodies. The dignity with which they carried the wrongness.

"Start anywhere," Iyo said. "That's the point of a seamlessness like this. It doesn't matter where you enter. You'll come out the same door."

Casper entered with the Kestrel.

She typed the name slowly, the way you approach a dog that might bite. *Kestrel.* Colony transport, registered Meridian Loop, class: long-haul generation vessel. She knew that much already; everyone on the Loop knew that much, the way everyone knows the shape of a word they've heard but never said aloud.

The first archive returned its answer in under a second. *Vessel Kestrel: lost with all hands, transit between Calloway Deep and Meridian Prime. Cause: drive cascade failure. No survivors. No wreckage recovered. Memorial services held at both ports.*

"Drive cascade," Casper read aloud. "That's a real thing. That happens."

"It happens," Iyo agreed quietly. "Once every few decades, somewhere in the settled volume. A drive tears itself apart and takes the ship with it. It's a clean story. Everyone believes it because everyone has heard of it happening to someone else."

She pulled the second drive. Independent port records, Calloway Deep customs logs — the kind of granular, boring data no one bothered to forge because no one bothered to read it. *Kestrel, departure confirmed, standard manifest, 4,112 souls.* The number landed in her chest like a dropped tool. Four thousand people. Families. Children old enough to walk and young enough to be carried.

She checked the arrival logs at Meridian Prime. Nothing. Of course nothing. Lost with all hands.

But then she did the thing she had trained herself never to do, the thing her whole careful life was built around avoiding: she started pulling threads.

* * *

The galley filled with light. She ran the drives through the ship's display system until three holographic columns of text hung in the air around the table, and she moved through them physically, reaching into one record and then another, cross-referencing by hand because she didn't trust the search functions anymore — didn't trust anything that happened too smoothly, too completely, inside a machine she hadn't opened in years.

"No wreckage," she murmured. "A drive cascade leaves debris. Miles of it. Calloway Deep logged a recovery operation?"

Iyo shook his head. "There was no recovery operation."

"There's always a recovery operation." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "Even when everyone's dead. Especially then. You recover the black box. You recover the drive cores so the insurance can rule out sabotage. There are *laws*—"

"There were laws."

She found the insurance filing. Or rather, she found the place where the insurance filing should have been. The Meridian Mutual registry had a gap — a single entry number skipped in an otherwise unbroken sequence, the way a tooth is missing from a comb. Entry 88,441: absent. Entries 88,440 and 88,442 sat on either side of it, mundane and intact, a cargo dispute and a dockworker's injury claim, and between them yawned a hole exactly the size of the most expensive loss in Loop history.

"They didn't even file," she whispered. "Four thousand lives and the insurers just... let it go."

"Would you argue with someone who could do everything else they'd done?" Iyo asked, and there was no accusation in it, only a tired kind of instruction, a teacher who had given this lesson before and would give it again.

She kept going because stopping felt like drowning.

The memorial services — those existed, she found them, small notices in the port bulletins, *in loving memory of the souls of the Kestrel*, dated eleven days after the scheduled arrival. But when she pulled the passenger lists attached to the memorials, the names didn't match the departure manifest. Not entirely. Not in structure. The departure list had family groupings — Okafor, four souls; Lindqvist, two souls; the naming conventions of people traveling together. The memorial list was alphabetical. Flat. Anonymous. As if someone had been handed a number instead of a census and had generated names to fill it.

Her skin tightened along her forearms. "These aren't the same people."

"You noticed that faster than I did," Iyo said. "It took us six months. We kept telling ourselves we were misreading the formats."

"I'm not misreading anything." She stood up, paced the narrow galley, pressed her palm flat against the cool bulkhead the way she did when the ship hummed too loud at night. "The manifest says four thousand one hundred twelve. The memorial lists nine hundred and change. Where are the other three thousand names? You don't forget three thousand people. You don't *abbreviate* them."

"No," Iyo said. "You edit them."

* * *

She worked through the sleep cycle without sleeping. The ship's lights dimmed to night-mode amber and she overrode them, twice, three times, the displays painting her face blue-white while the corridor outside stayed dark and the hold — she made herself not think about the hold — stayed silent beneath her feet.

Somewhere past what should have been midnight, the Vespera's captain came forward. Ade Yoon-ji — a woman of perhaps fifty years' experience compressed into a face of perhaps twenty-five, gray threading a head of hair that belonged above a younger brow. She moved like someone relearning her own skeleton, each step slightly deliberated, and she stood in the galley doorway watching Casper work without speaking for a long time.

"You're going to make yourself sick," Ade said finally.

"I've been sick for eleven years. This is the first thing that's ever explained it."

Ade pulled a second chair over with hands that couldn't have weighed more than a child's and sat down heavily, which is to say, carefully, because her new body bruised. "What do you need?"

"A second set of eyes that isn't mine. And someone to tell me when I stop making sense."

"All right." Ade leaned toward the displays. "Show me the silence."

"The silence?"

"The colonies stopped talking to each other. Everyone on this route knows it — the dead frequencies, the closed zones, the ships that run dark because answering a hail means opening a channel someone might be listening to. When did it start? Exactly?"

Casper stared at her. Then she turned back to the displays, and her hands, which had been steady all night, were suddenly not.

She built the timeline in a vertical column, year by year, colony by colony. Calloway Deep's last public broadcast. Meridian Prime's reduction of open-band traffic. The closure of the deep-space relay corridor. The Colonial Authority's expansion of inspection jurisdiction — *temporary measures*, the legislation had said, *pending resolution of inter-colonial security concerns*. Each entry had its own date, its own justification, its own local logic. Separately, each one looked like drift, like entropy, like the ordinary cooling of a civilization's enthusiasm for talking to itself.

Aligned in a column, they looked like a door closing.

"The Kestrel is lost in—" she tapped the display, "—this year. Standard year 2411, third quarter. Calloway Deep goes quiet on public bands within eight months. Meridian Prime restricts civilian transmission the following year. The relay corridor closes eighteen months after that. The Authority's emergency powers pass before the memorial notices have finished running in the port bulletins." She stepped back. The column glowed in the dark galley like a spine. "It's not a pattern. Patterns repeat. This is a *sequence*. One thing, then the next thing, then the next. Someone knocked over the first domino and called the noise afterward 'the war.'"

"You said no one will name it," Ade said. "The war."

"Because there wasn't one." The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she couldn't take them back, and she found she didn't want to. "Look at it. Look at the timing. There's no enemy declared, no territory disputed, no treaty signed, no reparations paid. Wars end. This just... became furniture. Everyone agreed to be afraid of the same empty room and nobody remembers deciding." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "My mother used to say the Loop went quiet after the war. My mother *believed* in the war. There are songs about the war."

"There are songs about lots of things that didn't happen," Ade said gently. "That's what songs are partly for."

* * *

Near dawn — ship's dawn, an arbitrary convention she maintained because otherwise the days dissolved — Casper ran the last check. She had saved it deliberately, the way you save the deepest cut, knowing what it will show.

Weapons.

If the Kestrel had died of a drive cascade, there would be nothing to find about weapons anywhere in any record. If it had died of anything else — piracy, mutiny, collision — weapons would appear somewhere, in some report, in some precautionary regulation written in the grief afterward. Every disaster breeds a regulation. That was how you knew disasters were real: they left rules behind like shed skin.

She searched all three drives. All colonies. All years, forward and back from 2411.

Nothing.

No new ordinance on convoy travel. No insurance rider for hostile action in the outer corridor. No port security bulletin, no advisory to civilian traffic, no *be vigilant, report suspicious vessels*. The Loop's entire regulatory history scrolled past her like a river with a missing bend — smooth, continuous, flowing as though nothing had ever interrupted it.

Four thousand one hundred twelve people had died — or not died, or been killed, or been erased — and not one rule had changed.

"That's impossible," she said aloud, to the empty galley, to the humming ship, to whatever was listening. "Grief writes law. That's all grief does, eventually. Somebody always makes a rule so it won't happen again. Somebody always—"

"Somebody did make a rule," Iyo said from the doorway. She hadn't heard him come; his bare feet made no sound on the deck plates, another wrongness she was still cataloguing. He crossed to the display and touched the timeline column, the closing sequence of silences and closures and emergency powers. "Here. This is the rule. *Stop talking. Stop looking. Stop remembering out loud.* The regulation after the Kestrel wasn't about drives or convoys. It was about *you*." His child's hand hovered over the glowing dates. "They didn't write a rule so it wouldn't happen again. They wrote a rule so no one would find out it had."

Casper sat down. Her legs simply declined to hold her, and the chair caught her, and for a while she just breathed, the recycled air tasting of metal and coffee gone cold, the three drives ticking softly in their cradles like things asleep.

She thought about her own memories then — really thought about them, which she hadn't permitted herself to do in years. The disaster she half-remembered. The edges of it, smooth where edges should be ragged. The way certain years of her life felt like rooms she'd walked through with her eyes closed. She had always assumed grief had done that, grief and time and the long anesthesia of routine. Grief did do that. People lost whole seasons to sorrow; it was normal; it was human.

But now she stood inside the vertigo of a different possibility, and the possibility had teeth.

*How much of me is original?*

The question arrived fully formed and refused to leave. If a committee somewhere could delete four thousand people from the historical record — could generate false names for a memorial, could skip an insurance entry, could close a corridor and rename fear as law — then what was sacred about the contents of one freight pilot's skull? What had they taken from her, and when, and had they left a receipt?

"Casper." Iyo's voice, careful. "You've gone somewhere far away."

"I'm checking whether I came back with all of myself." She rubbed her face with both hands. "Eleven years I've run this route. Eleven years I've told myself the silence was natural, the way weather is natural. And it was *installed*, Iyo. It was installed the same season the Kestrel disappeared, and I've been flying my whole adult life through the hole they left where the truth used to be." She looked up at him — at the navigator's experience sitting behind those too-young eyes — and asked the question that mattered more than any archive:

"What did your crew actually see out there? At the relay. Because I don't think I can decide what to do next until I know what's buried under it."

Iyo exchanged a glance with Captain Ade, some whole conversation passing between them in the space of a breath. Then he pulled out the chair opposite hers and sat, folding his impossible hands on the table, and when he spoke, his voice had the flat steadiness of a man about to hand over something heavy.

"We saw the Kestrel," he said. "Or what was left of it. And Casper — the hull was scarred by weapons fire. Dozens of impacts. We brought back scans, and samples, and one piece of the hull itself, and every hour we sit here, the people who wrote your seamless records are deciding how much longer we're allowed to exist."

* * *

Outside the viewport, the Loop stretched away in darkness — the dead frequencies, the closed corridors, the silent colonies hanging like held breath. Somewhere in that darkness was a relay station built over a grave, and somewhere behind her, below the deck plates, forty-one pods slept with their cargo of unfinished goodbyes.

Casper looked at the timeline glowing in the air, at the dominoes falling in perfect order, and understood that she had already decided. She just hadn't admitted it yet.

"Nineteen days," she said. "Until the jump window closes."

Iyo nodded slowly. "Halcyon Reach."

"I need to think about what carrying you means." She stood, and her voice was quieter than she wanted it to be, and more honest. "Because opening my hold to your crew means opening it to everything. And there are things in that hold that I have spent eleven years making sure no one — including me — ever looks at."

She turned off the displays. The timeline vanished, but the sequence stayed burned behind her eyes, orderly and terrible, a door closing in slow motion across eleven years of her life.

The silence between the colonies had a birthday. She had just found it.
