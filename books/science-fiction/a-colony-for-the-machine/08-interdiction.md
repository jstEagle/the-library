## 8. Interdiction

The hail came on the fourth day out from the Vespera's drift, while Casper was eating reconstituted noodles over the nav console and pretending they were a meal.

*Freighter Oyelaran, this is Colonial Authority cutter Sternwatch. You are directed to heave to for routine inspection. Acknowledge.*

She knew the voice type before she knew the words — flat, patient, the tone of someone who had never once been told no and found it interesting. Authority cutters didn't patrol this stretch of the Loop. They patrolled it when they were looking for something.

"Acknowledged," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Oyelaran complying. Reducing thrust."

She set the noodles down without tasting them. In the galley doorway, Iyo Sefu stood with a child's hands wrapped around a mug too big for him, his middle-aged eyes watching her face for the answer before she gave it.

"How long?" he asked.

"Six hours to intercept. Maybe less if they burn hard."

He set the mug down. It was very quiet in the galley, the kind of quiet that has machinery inside it — the recycler's hum, the coolant whispering through the walls, nine people breathing somewhere aft of her bulkheads who did not officially exist.

"They'll scan," Iyo said. Not a question.

"They'll scan."

"And your hold?"

Casper looked at him for a long moment. Forty-one memory pods slept back there behind a sealed hatch she had not opened in six years, each one holding a dead person's last saved self, each one humming its small refrigerated hum. And now, crouched among them in the dark between the racks, nine living bodies doing their best to be furniture.

"My hold," she said, "is full of cargo that doesn't show up right on sensors. That's either our problem or our miracle."

* * *

They had three hours of warning after all — the cutter slowed to make a show of procedure, because Authority inspections were theater as much as force, and theater needed an audience. Casper spent those hours turning her ship into a lie.

The lie began with paperwork. She pulled up her manifest — legitimate, boring, the product of years of careful dullness — and added a line: *cryogenic storage units, agricultural stock, forty-one units, non-perishable.* Seed vaults. Nobody inspected seed vaults twice; the colonies were always hungry and never grateful about it. She forged the provenance codes with the practiced ease of a woman who had been small-time criminal in tiny ways for so long she'd stopped noticing the shape of it.

The second part of the lie was physical. The crew went into the hold.

She walked them through it herself, one at a time, because the engineer — Vashti, sixty-one years old in a body of perhaps nineteen, hands still remembering controls now mounted too high — kept trying to help and knocking things over. The hold was cold. That was the first thing. The pods breathed their frost-breath along the deck plates, and the overhead lights ran dim by design, amber strips that made everything look like the inside of a closed eye.

"Between the racks," Casper said. "There are gaps. Blankets are in the crate marked seven. You don't move unless you hear my voice say the word *orchard*. Nothing else. Not a knock, not a cough, not a sneeze. If you have to vomit, you vomit quietly and you aim away from the pods."

The captain — Okonkwo-Reyes, a woman whose gray authority lived entirely in her voice now, since her face had gone round and young as a schoolgirl's — stood at the hatch with her arms crossed over her chest like a general reviewing troops who happened to be children wearing her friends.

"You've done this before," she said.

"I've hidden cargo before." Casper met her eyes. "I've never hidden people. So do me the courtesy of being good cargo."

Something moved across the captain's young face — offense, maybe, or the ghost of a laugh. "You know what we saw out there, pilot. At the relay. We spent eleven years getting home. Six hours in a cold room is nothing."

It wasn't nothing. Casper could see it wasn't nothing. But she let them believe their own bravery, because that was what you did for passengers, and shut the hatch.

Then she stood alone in the corridor outside the hold, hand flat against the metal, listening to the faint chorus of it: forty-one pods cycling their coolant, and underneath, almost lost beneath the machines, the sound of nine human hearts trying to slow down.

* * *

The Sternwatch came alongside at 0400 ship-time, docking clamps biting into her hull with a shudder she felt in her teeth. Casper waited in the airlock bay with her manifest displayed on a slate and her face arranged into the expression she'd perfected over six years of Loop runs: tired, cooperative, slightly bored. The expression of a woman with nothing to hide and less to say.

The inspector came through the lock alone. That was standard — the cutter stayed manned, the inspector boarded, the threat lived in the fact that the cutter could simply refuse to leave. He was younger than she expected. Mid-thirties, close-cropped hair, gloves that flexed with sensor filaments woven through the fingertips. His name tag read OKAFOR, and under it, the corporate liaison mark that made her stomach tighten: Meridian Combine. Not the Authority proper. The Authority wore one flag; the Combine wore another; lately, from what she'd seen, the flags shared a laundry.

"Pilot Oyelaran." He glanced at the slate without taking it. "Route?"

"Meridian Loop, standard circuit. Callao Station to Verge Landing, then out to the agri-colonies."

"Your transponder shows a deviation."

"Debris field off the Verge approach. Old war salvage. I went around." She said it easily, and only afterward realized how much truth she'd folded into the lie — there *was* old war salvage out there, more than anyone official would admit, and she had gone around it, though not for the reason she was giving.

Inspector Okafor looked at her. It was a professional look, unhurried, the look of a man reading a document rather than a face. "War salvage," he repeated. "There hasn't been a war."

"No, sir."

"Then what would you call it?"

"Insurance fraud, mostly. People scuttling hulls and claiming losses." She shrugged. "Out here we call it weather."

For a moment — half a breath, no more — something flickered behind his eyes. Not suspicion. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man who had heard a joke he wasn't permitted to laugh at. Then it was gone, filed away wherever inspectors file things, and he turned to the interior scan readout on his wrist.

"Cargo hold," he said. "Forty-one cryo units. Agricultural stock."

"Seed vaults. Non-perishable. Provenance codes are on the manifest."

"Mm." He was already walking.

* * *

The walk down the spine of her ship took four minutes and lasted approximately a decade. Casper followed two steps behind him, the way cooperative pilots followed inspectors, and catalogued every sound the ship made as though hearing it for the first time. The recycler. The coolant. Her own pulse, loud and traitorous in her ears.

The hold hatch irised open onto amber light and cold air.

Okafor stepped through and stopped, and Casper watched him take it in: the racks, the ranks of pods, frost feathered across their viewports, each one glowing its soft standby green. From the outside they looked like what she claimed they were — industrial cryo units, anonymous, interchangeable. Inside each one slept something the Combine had outlawed in every colony it touched: a copy of a person, held past death, waiting for a law to change or a courage to arrive.

"Forty-one units," Okafor said.

"That's right."

"Running warm for seed stock." He laid one gloved hand against the nearest pod's casing, and the filament weave in the glove pulsed faintly. "These are drawing power like they're maintaining neural substrate."

"Old units." Casper heard her own voice continue, smooth and unbothered, from somewhere just outside herself. "Pre-embargo manufacture. They run inefficient. Half my fuel budget goes to keeping them cold enough that the stock doesn't sprout early." She gestured down the rack line. "I can show you the maintenance logs. Twelve years of them. My father bought these secondhand and I've babied them ever since."

It was a good lie. It was a *great* lie, and the reason it was great was that most of it was true — the pods were pre-embargo, they did draw obscene power, her father had bought the ship, and she had babied its secret cargo through six years of silent colonies. Lies built on true bones held weight. She'd never known that about herself before. She felt it now, the strange structural pleasure of it, like finding a load-bearing wall where you'd expected cardboard.

Okafor moved down the aisle between the racks. Behind the third rack on the left, wedged into the gap with a blanket over her shoulders, was Vashti the engineer. Behind the fifth, Okonkwo-Reyes. Behind the seventh, Iyo Sefu, whose heartbeat Casper imagined she could hear through the pod casings, quick and child-sized, like a bird in a box.

The inspector paused at each pod. Touched some. His glove-filaments drank data from the casings, and Casper prayed silently — to no god she believed in, to the dead in the pods instead, *you were quiet in life, be quiet now* — that dormant memory-backups read on a scanner exactly like what they were: cold, dark, full of static.

At the eleventh pod, he stopped.

Casper's mouth went dry. She knew that pod. Of course she knew that pod; she knew all forty-one the way a warden knows cells, but this one she knew the way you know a scar. Pod nineteen. The one that had flickered awake during transit, three days ago, and whispered a name through its speaker in a voice she had spent six years not thinking about.

The green standby light on pod nineteen was pulsing. Slowly. Rhythmically. Not the steady burn of dormancy — a pulse. Like breathing. Like a heart.

Okafor leaned closer. His breath fogged the viewport, and beneath the frost, for half a second, Casper saw the pod's interior display wake: a thread of waveform, rising and falling. A voiceprint, idling. A name field, half-lit.

"This unit's active," Okafor said.

"It cycles." The words came out of her level and unhurried, and she marveled at them even as she spoke them. "Number nineteen's always done that. Thermal regulator's shot — it wakes, tries to reboot, fails, goes back down. I keep meaning to cannibalize parts from another unit and I keep not doing it, because the moment I crack the casing I void the warranty on the whole rack." She stepped forward, casual, and rested her own hand on the pod beside his — claiming it, making it hers, ordinary. "You want to hear something funny? It talks in its sleep. Little chirps. Scared the hell out of me my first year. Now it's just the ship snoring."

Okafor straightened. He looked at her — that document-reading look again — and for a long moment the hold was so quiet Casper could hear the frost ticking as it grew on the rack frames.

"Seed stock doesn't talk in its sleep, Pilot Oyelaran."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "Faulty regulators do. You're welcome to pull the service record. Unit nineteen, complaint filed year one, response logged, repair deferred for cost. It's all there. I'll even give you a copy, if bureaucracy's short on bedtime reading."

And here was the thing she discovered about lying, standing in the cold with her brother's sleeping ghost between herself and the law: it was easier than she'd thought. Not because she was good at it, but because she had been rehearsing it for six years without knowing. Every time a stationmaster asked what was in the hold and she said *agricultural stock*; every time a fellow trader joked about her fuel bills and she blamed the old cryo units; every silence she'd kept about the disaster, the backups, the forty-one reasons she ran the Loop alone — all of it had been rehearsal. This was just the performance finally opening.

Okafor's glove hovered over pod nineteen's latch. One twist and the seal would break. The pod would wake fully, defensive protocols would fire, and whatever came out of that speaker next would not sound like thermal cycling. It would sound like a young man's voice saying a name — *Casper? Casper, is that—* — and then the whole architecture of her life would come down, and nine stowaways would come out of the racks with their impossible hands raised, and the cutter in orbit would radio home, and home would come for all of them.

His fingers touched the latch.

Somewhere behind the seventh rack, Casper heard — or imagined she heard — a held breath.

Then Okafor's wrist chimed. He glanced down at it, annoyed, and whatever the cutter's officer-of-the-watch wanted from him, it made his jaw tighten. "Hold position," he muttered — to her, to the pod, to the whole cold room — and stepped back, and answered on a private channel she caught only fragments of: *"...yes... no, manifest reads clean... because the order says sweep and clear, not strip and..."* A pause. His eyes came up and found hers, and there was that flicker again, deeper this time. *"Understood. Out."*

He lowered his wrist. For a moment he just looked at her, and Casper understood, with the clarity of a woman balancing on a wire, that he knew. Not everything — maybe not even much — but enough. Enough to see that pod nineteen pulsed like a heart and seed stock didn't breathe. Enough to know that the order pulling him off the inspection had come from above, from the Combine, from whoever had flagged the *Oyelaran*'s route weeks ago and wanted her searched and wanted her searched *quietly*, and that obeying it meant leaving whatever was in this hold unexamined for reasons that had nothing to do with agriculture.

"Your regulator's failing," Okafor said at last. His voice was carefully neutral. "Unit nineteen. You should get that looked at."

"As soon as I dock somewhere with parts."

"There aren't many places left with parts." He was moving toward the hatch now, brisk again, the inspection resuming its theater. "Verdict: manifest consistent, no contraband detected. You're free to proceed, Pilot." At the threshold he stopped, back to her, and said — softly, not turning around, words pitched for her alone beneath the hum of the pods:

"The Kestrel carried seed stock too. Did you know that? Agricultural colonists. That's what the manifest said, the day it launched." A beat. "Funny what ends up in holds, out here."

Then he was through the hatch and gone, boots ringing up the spine toward the airlock, and Casper stood alone in the amber cold with her heart slamming against her ribs.

* * *

She waited until the cutter's clamps released and its drive flare faded against the viewport before she said the word.

"Orchard."

The racks came alive. Blankets shifted; gaps exhaled; nine people unfolded themselves from between the dead with the stiffness of the long-frozen. Vashti emerged rubbing her too-young knees. Okonkwo-Reyes came out last, moving like a woman who had spent six hours learning to breathe through her skin. Iyo appeared at Casper's elbow, pale, his child's hand gripping the rack frame.

"He touched the latch," Iyo said. "On nineteen. I heard it click."

"He didn't turn it." Casper was looking at the pod. Its light had settled back to steady green, the waveform gone, the name field dark. Asleep again. Or pretending to be. "He got his orders instead. Someone told him to stop."

"Why would they stop?" Okonkwo-Reyes demanded. "Why board us at all if they were going to wave us through?"

"Because they weren't waving us through." Casper turned to face them, and heard her own voice drop into a register she didn't recognize — flat, certain, the voice of a woman who had lied well for the first time that day and found out what she was capable of. "That cutter wasn't inspecting us. It was confirming us. Checking we were on course, on schedule, where they expected us to be." She tapped the slate against her palm. "Which means somebody knows where we're going. Somebody's known since before we left the Vespera."

Iyo's eyes went wide. "The tribunal route—"

"Is written down somewhere I didn't write it." She looked back at pod nineteen, at the frost creeping across the glass, at the faint green glow like a nightlight in a child's room. "Get some rest. All of you. Eat something hot." She was already moving toward the nav core, already pulling up her logs, because there was one more thing she needed to check — one thing that had been nagging at her since the inspector's wrist chimed.

Her navigation history opened on the console. Course plotted, course flown, course recorded.

And there, in yesterday's entries, in her own handwriting of numbers: a correction she had never made.

A two-degree deviation, logged and confirmed, steering them gently off the Halcyon approach — signed with her authorization code, timestamped while she slept.

Someone aboard was editing her.
