## 9. The Name in the Pod

The whisper came again at 0300, ship's time, which was a fiction Casper maintained the way she maintained everything else aboard the *Long Answer* — through stubbornness and routine and the refusal to consult anything larger than herself.

She was awake anyway. She was always awake at 0300. There were hours her body kept like debts, and this was one of them: the hour when the Loop went quiet enough that she could hear the ship breathing — coolant ticking through the walls, the hold's climate system cycling with a sound like slow swallowing, the reactor humming its one long note beneath everything, so constant she'd stopped hearing it years ago except on nights like this, when it seemed less like machinery and more like a held breath.

The whisper came from the hold.

She knew that was impossible. She had built the impossible into the ship herself, pod by pod, seal by seal: forty-one memory backups in stasis, drawing trickle power, dreaming nothing. Backups didn't whisper. Backups weren't even asleep, really — they were paused, mid-thought, mid-person, stored at the temperature of almost-nothing. If you opened a pod you wouldn't find a person. You'd find a pattern, cold and patient, waiting for hardware that could run it.

And yet.

*"...Casper."*

Her name. Not spoken so much as surfaced, the way a drowned thing surfaces — slowly, rotating, arriving all at once after a long time of being just below.

She stood in the corridor outside the hold for four full minutes before she keyed the lock. She counted them. It was something her mother used to do — count before entering a room where someone might be crying, to give them time to stop. Casper had inherited the habit without inheriting the reason, or rather, she had inherited both and buried the reason so deep she sometimes mistook it for instinct.

The hold lights came up amber and low. Forty-one pods in their racks, frosted, dim, each one tagged with a hand-written label because she had never trusted the digital manifests — labels in her own cramped script, names and dates and nothing else. No epitaphs. She had never allowed herself epitaphs.

Thirty-nine of the pods were silent.

The fortieth flickered.

It sat third rack from the portside wall, and its status light — which should have been the steady green of suspended storage — pulsed instead, green, dark, green, dark, in a rhythm too irregular to be mechanical and too regular to be random. Frost crawled across its faceplate in patterns that looked, if she let herself look, like breath on glass.

She crossed the deck and put her palm flat against the pod's casing. Cold bit through her skin down to the bone, honest cold, machine cold. But under her hand, faint as a pulse in a wrist, she felt the power draw stuttering — the pod waking itself in fragments, running a process it should not have been able to run, some subroutine of the stored mind reaching up through layers of stasis like a swimmer toward light.

*"...Cas. Casper. You're doing the counting thing."*

She closed her eyes.

* * *

Tomas Oyelaran had been afraid of the dark until he was nine, and then he had been afraid of the water, and by fourteen he had consolidated his fears into a single elegant terror: being awake while everyone else slept. He said it made him feel like the last man on a continent. So he stayed up. He learned every creak of their hab-unit on Meridian Station, learned which pipes sang at which hour, learned to tell time by the freighters passing overhead — their drive wash shivering the ceiling panels in slow waves, like the station was a boat and he alone felt the swell.

"You count before you come in," he told her once, when she was eleven and thought she was sneaking. "Mama does it too. Four seconds. You think we don't notice?"

"I'm not crying," Casper said.

"No," Tomas agreed, "but you're about to ask me for something."

He was nineteen when the backups were made. That was standard, then — everyone on the inner colonies backed up annually, cheap insurance sold by companies whose names were later scrubbed from the record along with everything else. Tomas had gone in on his birthday, joking with the technician, letting Casper come along because she liked watching the machines. She remembered the booth: white light, the hum of the scanner, her brother making faces at her through the glass while the machine read him — every fold of his mind lifted gently out and pressed flat, like a flower kept between pages.

"Say something for posterity," the technician said.

Tomas leaned toward the pickup. "Testing," he said. "One, two. Hey, Cas. If you're hearing this, I'm either famous or dead, and knowing me, probably neither. Probably I'm just late for dinner."

The technician laughed. Casper did not, because even at eleven she understood that jokes about death were doors left open on hinges, and she hated doors that moved.

Three years later, the disaster came — the one she could not fully remember, the one that lived in her head as a shape with the middle burned out. She remembered the morning. She remembered the evening. Between them lay a gap the size of a colony, and every time she reached into it she came back with her hands empty and bleeding.

What she knew, in the way you know things you've decided never to examine: there had been fire, or something like fire. There had been a list of names, and the list had been shorter than it should have been, and no one would tell her why. There had been an official account, sealed, and a settlement, and a form she signed at seventeen without reading because reading it meant believing it.

And there had been the backups. Forty-one of them, pulled from the disaster site's local vault before the Authority could seize and — what was the word they used? *Retire.* Before the Authority could retire them. Forty-one people who died with copies still warm, still legally alive in the eyes of any system that hadn't been paid to look away.

She stole them. That was the plain truth under all her careful language. She bought a battered freighter with settlement money — blood money, though she never called it that — and she stole forty-one dead people and ran.

* * *

In the present, the pod whispered her name again, and this time she answered.

"Tomas."

The frost on the faceplate shifted. The status light pulsed faster — green, dark, green — like something knocking.

"I know," she said aloud, to the hold, to the pod, to the version of her brother pressed flat between pages of cold. "I know you're in there. I've always known. That was the whole point."

Her voice sounded strange in the hold. She almost never spoke here. The hold was the one place on the ship where she permitted silence to accumulate undisturbed, like dust on a shrine.

Here is what she had told herself for eleven years: *I am a courier. This is cargo. I keep it safe because it's right to keep it safe, because these are people and people deserve dignity, because someday there will be a world that can run them again and I will deliver them to it.*

Here is what was true: she had never once looked for that world. Not seriously. Not with the part of herself that finds things. She had traded along the Loop for eleven years, hauling legitimate freight over the top of an illegal hold, and in all that time she had never made a single inquiry, followed a single lead, spent a single credit on the question of whether the dead could be woken. Because the dead, woken, would not be the dead. And the dead, woken, would ask her questions.

They would ask her why she never visited their families. They would ask her what happened in the gap. They would ask her — Tomas would ask her first, because he had always been the one who noticed — *Cas, why do you still count before you come in? Who are you giving time to stop?*

The backups weren't cargo. She saw it now with terrible clarity, standing in the amber light with her hand on her brother's coffin-that-was-not-a-coffin. They were a refusal. Forty-one ways of not mourning. A hoard. She had taken the worst day of her life and frozen it, literally frozen it, and called the freezing mercy, and flown the freezer around the Loop for a decade the way a miser counts coins — touching nothing, spending nothing, letting nothing change hands, least of all grief.

And grief unspent doesn't stay grief. It ferments. It rots. It becomes the thing you are instead of the thing you carry.

She thought of the Vespera's crew, those impossible passengers sleeping two decks up — adults' memories in children's bodies, time running the wrong way through them. Iyo Sefu had said something the night before, sitting in her galley with his too-small hands wrapped around a mug he could barely lift, saying it lightly, the way he said everything: *"You know what time did to us, captain? It didn't hurt us. It just refused to let us finish. We're all mid-sentence now."*

Mid-sentence. She looked at the pod. Her brother was mid-sentence too — had been for eleven years, suspended at twenty-two forever, at *hey Cas, probably I'm just late for dinner*, at the last complete person she had ever loved without complication.

The pod's light steadied. The whispering stopped. Whatever fragment had surfaced sank back down, and the frost began its slow crawl again, and Casper stood alone in the hold of her own refusal with her hand going numb against the cold.

"I'm sorry," she said. It was not enough. It was the first true thing she'd said in the hold in eleven years, and it was not enough, and she suspected now that nothing would be, that *enough* was a currency she had refused to earn.

She thought about opening it. Just the faceplate. Just to see the diagnostics, the integrity readouts, the timestamp of the last full backup — his birthday, three years before the end, the version of him saved *before*. Before the gap. Before the fire. Before whatever she had survived and refused to remember. The man in the pod didn't know how he died. The man in the pod didn't know he died at all. If she woke him, she would have to teach him his own ending, watch it land in a face she knew better than her own.

If she woke him, she would finally have to mourn him — because a restored copy isn't a resurrection; it's a eulogy you perform in real time.

Her hand hovered over the manual release.

Then, from the intercom above the hold door, the ship's comm chimed — twice, urgent, the pattern she'd set for contact. She pulled back as if the panel had burned her.

"Casper?" Iyo's voice, tinny, young-sounding in a body that wasn't. "Sorry to wake you. There's traffic on the approach band — Authority transponder codes, two ships, holding position off our bow. They're hailing everyone on this lane. Routine inspection sweep, they say." A pause. "Captain, they're between us and Halcyon."

She looked at the pod one last time. The frost had covered the faceplate completely now, erasing the light behind it, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense that she was looking at a mirror.

"Noted," she said. "I'm coming up."

She killed the hold lights. In the dark, forty-one pods breathed their slow cold breath, and one of them — only one — seemed, in the last second before the door sealed, to whisper her name again.

She locked the hold. She did not count to four.

She would pay for that later.
