## 10. Testimony Under Way

I had spent eleven years learning the grammar of silence.

Out past the relay, in the zone where the charts simply stopped and the stars kept their own counsel, silence was not an absence. It was a material, like lead or ballast, and we had learned to live inside it the way miners live inside stone. So when Casper Oyelaran gave me her ship's small recorder — a battered handheld unit with a cracked display and a microphone that hissed like rain on a hot engine — I understood what she was really handing me. Not a device. A permission. For the first time since the Vespera had come home to bodies that betrayed us, someone was asking us to break the silence on purpose, and to keep the breaking.

We set up in her mess, because it was the only room aboard with a table long enough for all nine of us, though we never filled it. Nine chairs. Usually seven occupied. Captain Adeyemi could not always sit through a session; her body, eleven years old now in every way that mattered to her shirtsleeves, would simply exhaust itself mid-sentence, and she would fall asleep with her chin on her folded hands like a student at a lecture she loved. We learned to keep talking when she slept. She had ordered us to, in the first week, in that voice that came out of her small throat with all its old command still folded inside it, compressed the way a star's mass is compressed into something dense and unrecognizable.

"Record everything," she had said. "Even the parts that sound like madness. Especially those."

So we recorded everything.

* * *

The first testimony was mine, because I insisted. If they were going to sit in front of a hissing microphone and hand over the most humiliating weeks of their lives, they deserved to see me do it first.

"Iyo Sefu, navigator, Vespera, registry 447-Kestrel-line." The old registry naming convention. I had not said it aloud in years. "We were contracted by Meridian Consolidated for deep salvage survey beyond the relay. Eleven years, four months ago, by my reckoning — though my reckoning has become a joke I tell against myself."

Casper sat across from me with her arms crossed, not watching me but watching the recorder, as if the little machine might flinch. She had a way of listening with her whole body angled away from you, like a cat pretending not to care. She had not yet told me what slept in her hold. I knew there was something — she had opened that hold to stow us, and her face while she did it had been a face I recognized from mirrors, the face of someone performing a choreography of control over something that wanted to scream. But that was her business. Everyone aboard was carrying a sealed hold of one kind or another.

"We found debris at the relay site," I said. "That's the heart of it. Not junk. Not the ordinary confetti of the trade lanes. Hull plating, meters thick, in fields. A keel section. And serial numbers."

"Whose serial numbers?" Casper asked. She was supposed to be a silent witness. She was bad at it.

"Kestrel-line," I said. "The same line as this ship's registry. The colony ship Kestrel, lost with all hands, sixty-one years ago. Except it wasn't lost. It was *here*, and it had been torn open by something that leaves patterns, and patterns have authors."

The microphone hissed. Somewhere in the ship's bones, a coolant pump cycled, patient as breathing.

* * *

After me came Okonkwo, our engineer, and Okonkwo's testimony was the first one that made the picture stop being a picture and start being a wound.

Okonkwo had been fifty-three when we went out. He was nine now, with a nine-year-old's hands, and the tragedy of Okonkwo was that his hands remembered being fifty-three. He would reach for a coupling and his fingers would know exactly where to go and then simply not be able to grip it. He had taken to sitting on cushions to reach his own consoles. He recorded his testimony standing, because sitting made him feel like a child being questioned by a schoolmaster, and he began not with the debris but with the radiation.

"You want to know how they hid it," he said. "Everyone wants to know how you hide a massacre. You hide it with a power plant."

Casper leaned forward.

"The relay draws from a fusion tap, standard pattern. But the tap was oversized. Double what the relay needed, double what any relay on the Loop needed. I ran the numbers off our own logs before we — changed." He looked at his hands. "That much surplus generation isn't for running a relay. It's for running a *mask*. Broad-spectrum electromagnetic noise, continuous, seeded into the local medium. To any passive scan, that whole volume of space reads as a storm front. Permanent, natural, boring. You don't hide a wreck by moving it. You hide it by making the sky around it too loud to listen to."

"And no one noticed an oversized plant?" Casper asked.

"It was filed," Okonkwo said, and something old and bitter moved through his young voice. "Everything was filed. That's the genius of it. A massacre hidden in a mountain of paperwork is hidden better than one hidden in space. Who audits a power budget from sixty years ago? Who cross-references a colonial charter against a fusion rating? The record doesn't need to lie, Ms. Oyelaran. It just needs to be *tired*. Tired and long and nobody's job."

I watched Casper write that down in her small precise hand, though the recorder was catching every word, and I understood that she was not writing to remember it. She was writing to make it real, the way a pilot logs a course she doesn't trust her instruments to confirm.

* * *

Vashti Reyes went third, and Vashti's testimony broke something in the room that never quite knitted back.

Vashti had been our xenolinguist — the closest thing the Vespera had to a historian. She was seven now. She sat on two cushions and folded her small hands in her lap with the deliberate grace of a woman of sixty, because Vashti had been sixty-one, and the dissonance of her was the hardest to look at directly: a child's face conducting a lifetime's composure like an orchestra playing on after the hall has caught fire.

"I found the charter," she said. "Or the ghost of it. In the relay's own archive — the local one, the one nobody bothers to scrub because nobody can reach it. There was a colonial transport charter filed sixty-one years and some months back, under a cooperative registration. Not corporate. Cooperative. Nine hundred and forty souls, pooled savings, their own hull. The Kestrel wasn't a corporate colony ship. It was a *refusal*. It was nine hundred and forty people who looked at the relocation offers — and I found fragments of those too, the standard Consolidated indenture packages, and I want it on the record that the word *voluntary* appears in them eleven times — and said no. They built their own ship. They filed their own charter. They left."

"Left for where?" Casper asked.

"That's the question, isn't it." Vashti's small hands tightened. "The charter's destination field is redacted — not deleted, *redacted*, which means someone had to sit at a terminal and choose to black it out, which means the destination was embarrassing. My theory — and it is only a theory — is that they weren't going anywhere on the charts. They were going *out*. Off the Loop. Beyond the relay, into the quiet, to be nobody's tenants. And the corporations could survive a colony lost. They could not survive a colony *free*. Because a free colony is a precedent, and precedents are the only cargo that ships itself."

The microphone hissed. Casper had stopped writing. Her pen was still moving but it had stopped making letters; it was just drawing a line down the margin, over and over, a fence being built and rebuilt in ink.

"The debris was on the approach vector to the relay site," Vashti said. "Which means the relay wasn't built near the wreck. The wreck is *under* the relay. They built the burial ground on top of the grave, and then they built the mask to keep it quiet, and then they waited for everyone who remembered to die of ordinary time. Sixty-one years is two generations, Ms. Oyelaran. You don't need to rewrite history if you can just outlive the historians."

* * *

We recorded for nine days.

I will try to render what those days were like, though language keeps failing me, because the days had a texture I have no word for — the texture of a document assembling itself out of grief. Each testimony was a fragment, and the fragments did not agree in the small things — Adeyemi remembered the debris field's density differently than Okonkwo; our medic, Sunn, placed the discovery of the keel section a week later than I did — but they agreed in the large things, and it was the large things that mattered, and I learned in those nine days what an archivist must already know: that truth is not a single voice. Truth is corroboration. Truth is seven people who each saw one edge of the same knife and, without conferring, describe a knife.

Adeyemi gave the order that had been given to us. She reconstructed it from memory, the transmission from Meridian Consolidated relayed through the Authority, the night after we found the keel section: *Survey terminated. Return to Meridian. Do not approach the source. Do not photograph the source. Data seizure will follow on docking. Acknowledge.*

"And we acknowledged," Adeyemi said, in her child's voice, with her captain's shame. "We acknowledged and we turned around and we ran, because we had families and the Authority has guns, and I have spent eleven years telling myself that was survival. It was. But it was also the last edit. Every person who turns away is an editor. They don't need to burn the archive if they can make the witnesses carry it home and shelve it themselves."

Sunn testified to the bodies. I will not render that testimony here, in this telling; some documents should exist in full and be quoted never. I will say only that Sunn, who was four now and spoke with the flat precision she had learned in forty years of triage, established that the wounds were weapons wounds, that the patterns were patterns, and that she had catalogued enough of them to estimate the minimum count, and that the minimum count was not nine hundred and forty, because some of the Kestrel's people had almost certainly reached the surface of the moonlet below and died there, slower, and the relay's foundation piles had been driven through that layer, and Sunn had seen the core samples.

Casper left the room during that one. She came back ten minutes later, her face composed, her eyes rimmed red, and said, "Continue," and I loved her a little for the composure and a little more for the ten minutes.

* * *

On the seventh day, we recorded the anomaly itself.

I had resisted this. The physics of what happened to us was the least of it — a curiosity, a horror, but not a *wrong* that needed righting — and I feared it would swallow the testimony whole, the way a strange thing always swallows an important thing. Casper overruled me, in her way, which was to say nothing and simply keep the recorder running while I argued.

"Say it anyway," she said finally. "The tribunal will need to understand why your bodies are evidence. You're not just witnesses. You're *contaminated* — in the literal sense. You're matter that was at the site, and the site marked you."

So I said it.

"The relay's mask isn't only electromagnetic," I said. "There's a temporal shear at the core of the site — we measured it, before it measured us. Local time runs backward in a gradient around the keel section. Faint at the perimeter. Absolute at the center. We were in the gradient for six days. The universe, at that spot, is trying to *unhappen*. And here is the thing I need on the record, the thing I have thought about every day since: a gradient like that doesn't occur. It's engineered. Someone built a machine that makes time flow backward over a grave — and the only reason to build such a machine is that you are afraid of what time will reveal if you let it run forward. Decay reveals. Erosion reveals. Forward time is the great honest archivist. So they built a machine to make time lie."

"Or," Casper said slowly, "they built it to erase the site entirely, eventually. Wind the whole grave backward to before the ship."

"Maybe. It's slow. It's imperfect. And it's failing — it caught *us*, but it caught us incompletely; our bodies went back and our minds stayed. That's not a working machine, Ms. Oyelaran. That's a machine breaking down. And when it finishes breaking down—"

The site would speak. We all understood it without saying it. The mask was failing, the shear was failing, and somewhere under all that engineered quiet, the Kestrel was coming back into forward time, back into honest decay, back into the reach of any scanner that came looking. The burial ground was fighting its own burial. We were what it had produced instead of a clean erasure: nine impossible bodies, walking testimony, the past given flesh and a deadline.

Casper sat very still for a long moment. Then she reached over and stopped the recorder, which startled me — she had never once stopped it early.

"Play me the Vashti session again," she said. "The part about the charter. The redacted destination."

I queued it. Vashti's voice, small and sixty-one years deep: *—not deleted, redacted, which means someone had to sit at a terminal and choose—*

Casper was staring at the recorder's cracked display, at the waveform of a dead woman's testimony rendered as light.

"My brother," she said, and then stopped, as if the sentence were a door she had decided, after all, not to open. She stood. "Keep recording. I need to check something in my archives."

She left, and her footsteps went aft, toward the hold she never opened, and I sat alone in the mess with nine chairs and a hissing microphone and the growing, cold, exhilarating sense that the document we were building was not only about the Kestrel. Documents have a way of finding their relatives.

* * *

On the ninth day, I assembled the fragments into order, because that is what navigators do: we take scattered fixes and derive a position.

The picture, when it finally stood whole in my mind, was almost beautiful in its terrible economy. Nine hundred and forty people refused to be tenants. They built a ship and left for the quiet. The corporations destroyed them — with weapons, at leisure, far from eyes — and could not permit the precedent, so they erased the charter's destination, wrote the loss as accident, and then, in the year the Kestrel vanished, the colonies of the Loop began to stop talking to one another. I had noticed it before, the way you notice weather: the Meridian Loop's great silence, the severing no one would name, the trade lanes going quiet like a house going quiet after a death. I had always assumed it was war. Everyone assumed it was war; the not-naming was the war's true monument.

But sitting there with the testimony spread across the mess table like a navigator's plot, I saw it plainly for the first time: the silence began the year of the Kestrel. The severing was not a war's scar. It was a *burial practice*. You cut the colonies off from one another so that no one could compare records, so that every world held its grief privately, uncorroborated, and a private grief can be edited forever. The Loop's silence was not the aftermath of the crime. It was the crime's *architecture*.

And we — nine de-aged sailors and a freighter captain who kept dead people in her hold and never spoke of it — were carrying the only unedited copy in existence, in a cracked handheld recorder, toward a tribunal that might already be bought.

I finished the index at ship's midnight, marked each session by witness and subject, and sealed the recorder's core against casual reading. A document. That was the whole miracle of it. Not a gun, not a fleet, not a revelation from heaven — just words, fixed, ordered, corroborated, *kept*. Justice taking shape as paperwork. It seemed too small. It seemed, also, like the only thing that had ever actually worked.

I was carrying the recorder back to my bunk, admiring the neatness of the index, when Casper came forward from the nav station with an expression I had not seen on her before, and I have seen every expression a frightened person can wear.

"Iyo," she said. "Come look at my logs."

Her voice was very quiet. The ship hummed around us, patient, obedient — and for the first time since I had come aboard, it did not sound patient to me at all. It sounded like something listening.
