## 17. Nineteen Hours

The damage report came in at 0400, ship-time, and Casper read it three times before she let herself believe it.

*Coolant loop two: compromised. Thruster array port: seventy percent loss. Hull breach, cargo bay micro-fracture, sealed. Life support: nominal.* Nominal. She almost laughed at the word. The *Meridian Rose* had taken a Authority grapnel strike during the escape from the lighthouse station — a glancing blow, the pilot had been sloppy or merciful, she still hadn't decided which — and now her ship was dying by degrees in the dark between Halcyon Reach and everything else.

She floated in the cockpit with her knees drawn up, watching the jump window countdown tick on the nav display. **18:51:22**. Nineteen hours, give or take a minute. After that, the gravitational corridor to Halcyon would shear shut like a door slamming, and no civilian ship would reach the tribunal world for a full year.

A year. She turned the number over in her mind and felt it go cold. A year was enough time for nine witnesses with impossible bodies to be reassigned, relocated, retired. A year was enough time for Dara Okonjo-Vey to die in a holding cell of something officially called natural causes. A year was enough time for the record to close over them all like water over a stone.

She'd seen how fast the corporations could edit a world. She'd spent eleven days reading archives that agreed with each other too perfectly, learning the shape of an absence. She knew what a year could do.

* * *

The galley smelled of recycled air and reheated protein, and it was full of children who were not children.

Iyo Sefu sat at the table with his small hands wrapped around a mug he could barely lift, his navigator's eyes — old, patient, lined with forty years of star-charts — set in a face gone smooth as riverstone. Beside him, Captain Adeyemi was teaching the youngest of them, a de-aged comms officer named Petra, how to fold a ration wrapper into a bird. Her hands shook when she didn't mean them to. Sometimes she cried without warning, mid-sentence, and kept talking through it as if the tears were weather happening to someone else.

Casper stood in the doorway and counted them the way she counted everything now: nine. All nine. Still alive, still aboard, still hers to lose.

"You should sleep," Iyo said, without looking up. He always knew when she was standing there. Something about the way the deck hummed under her boots, maybe. Or something older than that.

"Later."

"You said that six hours ago." He set down the mug and looked at her, and the wrongness of it still caught in her throat — that ancient gaze coming out of that unlined face, grief wearing a child's skin. "Casper. Sit down. Tell me the number."

"The number hasn't changed."

"Tell me anyway."

She pulled herself into the seat across from him. "Eighteen hours and change. Then the window closes for a year."

"And the ship?"

"Coolant's the problem. We can make the burn to the window mouth, but if we jump with loop two leaking, we come out the other side either frozen or cooked. Fifty-fifty, maybe worse." She heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used for cargo manifests and bad news. "I've rerouted what I can. It'll hold if nothing else goes wrong."

"Nothing else will go wrong," Petra said brightly, from across the table, and then her face crumpled and she was crying again, silent, furious at herself, folding her paper bird with wet hands.

"It already has," Casper said quietly. "Go look at the nav core."

Iyo went. She watched him pull himself hand-over-hand down the corridor, a grown man's mind in a body that moved like it was still learning its own weight, and she hated the corporations a little more cleanly, the way you hate something when you're too tired for rage.

* * *

The compliance module sat in the nav core like a splinter working toward the heart.

They had found it twelve days ago — buried so deep in the secondhand systems that Casper had flown over it for years without knowing, a corporate ear pressed to every course she'd ever plotted. They had turned it into a weapon, fed it false data, used its uplink to lie to the Authority. But lying through a channel isn't the same as closing one, and closing it meant cutting certain power relays they couldn't afford to cut with the coolant loop bleeding.

So it still listened. And somewhere in the last nineteen hours — she couldn't pinpoint when; that was the worst part, the not-knowing — it had started transmitting again. Not their false route. Their real one.

She showed Iyo the signal log. His face did something complicated.

"It's broadcasting our position," she said. "Tight-beam, directional, aimed at the relay buoy network. Someone on the other end is collecting it. Which means someone knows exactly where we are, how damaged we are, and exactly how long we have left."

"They're waiting," Iyo said. "They don't need to catch us. They just need the window to close."

"Yes."

"They'll have a cutter at the mouth of the corridor. Maybe two. Waiting for us to arrive broken, on schedule, into their hands." He said it without drama, the way he reported everything, testimony-voice, facts laid out flat. "And if we turn away, we live, and everyone we're doing this for disappears for a year, and after a year—"

"After a year there won't be anyone left to testify." She finished it because someone had to. "Dara first. Then the crew. Then me. Neat, quiet, administrative."

The engine room was hot and smelled of scorched insulation. The Vespera's engineer — Okafor, sixty-one years of experience in a body of maybe ten years old — was wedged into the coolant manifold housing with a plasma torch, swearing with astonishing fluency for someone whose voice hadn't finished changing. He'd been at it for thirty hours. When Casper offered to relieve him he laughed at her, high and cracked.

"You'd weld your own sleeve to the pipe, captain. No offense." He backed out, face smeared with grease, eyes ancient and bloodshot. "I can get you eighty percent on loop two. Eighty percent gets you through the jump if the burn profile is gentle. Gentle means slow approach. Slow approach means we arrive at the window mouth late and tired, right where anyone waiting would want us."

"How long to fix it properly?"

"Properly?" He looked at her with those old eyes. "Properly is a dry dock and six weeks. You've got nineteen hours and a ship that's been shot. I can give you eighty percent and a prayer. Take it or leave it."

"I'll take both," Casper said, and Okafor grinned, gap-toothed, a child's grin on a veteran's face, and went back into the manifold.

* * *

She found the captain alone in the observation blister, which was where people went on this ship to be alone, because the ship was small and grief needed somewhere to put itself.

Adeyemi was looking at the stars. Up here, between colonies, the stars were the old stars, unedited, and Casper had never thought about that before this voyage — that even the sky could be a record, that even the sky could be rewritten if someone got powerful enough.

"My daughter turns fourteen next month," Adeyemi said, without turning around. "Biologically, I'm younger than she is now. Do you understand what that's like? To have carried her inside you, to have taught her to walk, and to look in a mirror and see—" Her breath caught. "I keep my memories. That's the mercy of it. That's the cruelty of it. I remember being her mother. My body doesn't remember anything at all."

"We'll make the window," Casper said. "Okafor says eighty percent."

"Okafor also told me once that the Vespera's drive couldn't possibly fail, and then we spent eleven years falling backward through time." Adeyemi laughed, and it broke in the middle. "I'm sorry. I'm not brave tonight. Tomorrow I'll be brave again. Tonight I'd just like someone to tell me the truth plainly."

Casper looked out at the stars with her. "We might die making this jump. The ship is hurt, someone knows where we are, and the people who rewrote history would prefer us erased. That's the truth, plainly."

"Thank you." Adeyemi wiped her face with a sleeve too big for her arm. "You know what the worst part is? I testified for eleven days. Every horror, every hull plate, every name. And tonight, floating here, what I feel isn't fear about the tribunal. It's fear that I'll forget. That whatever made us young will finish the job and take the memories too, and I'll stand in front of the judges as a child who doesn't know why she's crying." She turned to Casper then, and her eyes were dry for the moment, and very old. "Promise me something. If I start forgetting — promise me you'll tell me who I am. Even if it hurts. Especially if it hurts."

"I promise," Casper said, and heard, underneath the words, the echo of her own private arithmetic: forty-one pods in her hold, one of them whispering her brother's name, and herself, eleven years refusing to open the door. She knew exactly what it cost to keep a person alive as a memory. She was starting to learn what it cost to keep a memory alive as a person.

* * *

At 1100 ship-time, the countdown read **12:04:37**, and the compliance module transmitted again.

Casper felt it this time — or imagined she felt it, a faint stutter in the deck plates, a shiver through the ship's nervous system as the splinter spoke to its masters. She stood in the nav core with her palm flat against the housing, as if she could hold it shut by pressure alone.

Iyo found her there. "Options," he said. "Give me the real ones."

"One: we run. Burn hard for the outer colonies, scatter, survive, and the truth dies quiet in a lighthouse cellar. Two: we go to the window mouth as planned, slow and gentle, straight into whatever's waiting. Three—" She stopped.

"Three?"

"Three is something Iyo said once, back at the relay, that I haven't stopped thinking about. About the anomalous drive. About jumping early, off-corridor, exposure be damned." She looked at him. "How fatal are we talking? Honestly."

He was quiet a long moment. In the hum of the core, with the module ticking its betrayals beneath her hand, he looked very small and very old at once.

"Honestly? I don't know. Eleven years ago it made us children. The exposure gradient near the drive is worse inside a ship this size. It could age us forward past our true years. It could take the last weeks of memory — the testimony, the lighthouse, Dara's face. It could kill us." He met her eyes. "It could also get us to Halcyon in six hours, ahead of the window, ahead of the cutters, ahead of everything. The burial ground fought back once, Casper. It's the only weapon we have that they've never planned for."

"Six hours," she repeated. Against twelve hours of gentle burn into a trap. Against a year of silence and disappearing witnesses. Against nineteen hours until the door closed forever.

She thought of Tomas in his pod below her feet, his recorded voice, the version of him she'd hoarded until it rotted. She thought of Adeyemi asking her to promise. She thought of Dara burning twenty years of work in the dark and saying: *records only matter if they arrive.*

Twelve hours to walk into their hands. Six hours to gamble everything, including who they were when they arrived.

The countdown ticked. **11:58:02.**

"I need to talk to the crew," Casper said. "All of them. And then—" She paused, and the truth came out of her plain, because the hour demanded plain things. "Then I think we stop running, and we jump early, and we see who we are on the other side."

Iyo nodded slowly. "There's one more thing," he said. "If we do this — the exposure won't just take memories. It takes the recent ones first. The last few weeks. The testimony, the plan, the names." He held up one small hand before she could speak. "Which means before we jump, everything has to be hard-copied. Every recording, every scan, every authentication. Paper, crystal, anything that doesn't live in a head. Because the people who wake up on the other side might not know why they came."

Casper looked down through the deck, through the coolant lines and the cargo bay and the sealed micro-fractures, down to the hold where forty-one dead slept and one of them whispered.

"Then we'd better start writing," she said.
