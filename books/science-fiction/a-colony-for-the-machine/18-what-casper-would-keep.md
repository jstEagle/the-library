## 18. What Casper Would Keep

The ship smelled of solder and old coffee and the faint ozone tang of a drive that had no business existing. Casper stood in the engineering bay of the *Vespera*, her boots sticking slightly to a deck plate that someone had mopped with something that was not water, and watched Iyo Sefu lay out the mathematics of their ruin on a salvaged display screen.

He had grown again. That was the thing she kept forgetting and then re-remembering like a bruise: the de-aging had stalled somewhere in the last week, frozen them all at whatever age the anomaly had decided to leave them, and Iyo had settled into a body that looked perhaps nineteen — smooth-faced, narrow-shouldered — while his hands moved with the economy of a man who had been navigating for thirty years. Watching him was like watching a child's puppet operated by a master from inside.

"The drive doesn't make jumps," he said. "That's what nobody understood about it, including us, including whoever built it. It makes *negotiations*. It asks spacetime to reconsider its position. And when we were caught in its field at the relay—" he tapped the display, where a diagram bloomed: concentric rings around a point labeled in his cramped handwriting, *the relay / the Kestrel / the grave* "—it asked us to reconsider too. Our position in time. Some of us went backward."

"And if we use it now," Casper said, because she wanted him to say it plainly, wanted it said aloud so she could stand in front of it and measure herself against it, "it asks us to go forward."

"Forward past our true ages. The field is unstable since the raid — we burned capacitors getting out of Halcyon orbit, and the shielding is patched with prayer. The exposure won't be clean. It won't be a neat reversal." He looked up at her, and his eyes were the oldest thing in the room. "Some of us might not survive it. Those who do will lose recent memory. Weeks, maybe months. Everything since the exposure began, smeared or gone entirely. The brain records differently when time is being renegotiated around it. What's written in wet ink gets blotted."

Casper turned away from the display and looked instead at the drive itself, crouched behind its cage of scaffolding like an animal in a pen. It hummed at a frequency she felt in her back teeth. Nineteen hours until the window opened. Nineteen hours, and then it would close forever, and a year of darkness would fall across the route to Halcyon Reach — a year in which every witness could be disappeared, every document shredded, every lighthouse darkened, and the silence between colonies would settle back over everything like snow.

"You're telling me we can jump early," she said. "Before the Authority expects it. Before they've repositioned the cutter."

"I'm telling you we can jump early *if* we're willing to pay the fare." Iyo folded his too-young hands together. "And the fare is ourselves."

* * *

She carried the arithmetic back through the connecting tunnel between ships, past the seal she'd cut and re-welded twice, into the familiar dimness of her own hold, where forty-one pods slept in their racks like a choir holding one long note.

The crew was waiting for her there — what remained of the nine, huddled among her cargo as though her ghosts were the safest company available. Captain Adeyemi sat propped against a crate with a blanket around her shoulders; her body had settled somewhere around eleven years old, and she wore her rank like a coat several sizes too large, which in a way it was. The engineer, Ruth Okafor, was perhaps eight now, and had spent the voyage teaching Casper's autopilot to swear in three languages. They looked up when Casper came through the hatch, nine faces in various stages of childhood and adolescence, and the sight of them still stopped her breath sometimes — this classroom of survivors, this school photo of the damned.

Iyo had already told them. She could see it in the way they'd arranged themselves: not scattered but gathered, deliberate, a quorum. They'd been talking without her.

"We voted," Adeyemi said. Her voice hadn't aged backward with her body; it came out of that small face rough and smoke-cured, and the mismatch never stopped being unsettling. "While you were over there. We vote yes."

"On dying," Casper said.

"On arriving," Adeyemi corrected. "Dying is a possible side effect. Arriving is the intent. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point." She pulled the blanket tighter. "I've been a captain for twenty-two years, Ms. Oyelaran. I've buried four crew and lost two ships. If the price of putting the Kestrel on the record is that I don't live to read it — or that I live longer than I should and don't remember reading it — then the price is fair. I signed on for deep salvage. Nobody signs on for deep salvage expecting to come home whole."

"It's not just you," Casper said. "It's me. It's my ship, my body, my—"

"Your memories." Ruth Okafor spoke up from the floor, where she sat cross-legged with a diagnostic slate across her small knees. "Iyo says the wet ink. Recent months, gone or smeared. He says testimony has to be hard-copied first — printed, etched, physical — or it might not survive in anyone's head." She tapped the slate. "We've started. All nine of us are recording everything we can remember right now, tonight, before the jump. Every detail. The hull scars. The serial numbers. The relay's construction logs. Dara's authentication codes. We're writing ourselves onto metal."

Casper looked at the pods. Forty-one of them, ranked along the walls, their status lights breathing slow green. And one of them — third rack, second row, the one the inspector's gloved hand had lingered over — breathed a little faster than the rest.

Tomas.

* * *

She didn't tell them why she left the bay. She told them she needed to check the cargo restraints, which was even true, in the way that most lies are true adjacent. But her feet took her down the aisle between the racks, and her hand found the pod's edge before her mind gave permission, and she stood there in the green light with her palm flat against the cold curve of it, listening to the whisper-thin audio bleed that she had never, in eleven years, managed to fully seal out.

Her brother's voice. Not words, exactly — the backups dreamed in fragments, half-phrases surfacing like drowned things — but the timbre of him, the particular grain of a voice she had known before she knew language. *Cas. Cas, look.* The way he'd said her name when they were children, tugging her toward some marvel or other. The way he'd said it later, over comms, the last time, in the static and the screaming, a transmission she had never been able to scrub from her memory no matter how much she drank or flew or worked.

The disaster was eleven years gone and she still could not hold all of it in her head. There was a hole in that day, a shape like a person, and she had filled the hole with these pods — forty-one of the dead, bought and bartered for across six colonies, illegal as sin and twice as expensive — because as long as they slept in her hold, the disaster wasn't finished. As long as Tomas could be restored, someday, by some technology not yet invented, she didn't have to mourn him. Mourning was a door, and she had nailed it shut with cargo.

And now Iyo was telling her that the door was going to be blown open whether she liked it or not. That the jump would take the wet ink — the last months, the voyage, the Vespera, the whispering pod, the sound of his voice bleeding through the seal at 0300 when she couldn't sleep. That she could hard-copy the testimony, could etch the massacre onto metal and deliver it to the tribunal, could save everyone's memory except her own.

She would remember the Loop. She would remember the route, the routine, the discipline. She would remember the shape of her guilt without remembering what fed it. And Tomas's voice — the fragment-voice, the dreaming voice, the voice she'd spent eleven years pretending she didn't listen to — would be gone from her the way it had gone from the air, irrecoverable, and she would stand in this hold with forty-one sleeping strangers and not know why she wept.

"That's the fare," she said aloud, to the pod, to the dark. "You understand that? They want me to pay you."

The status light pulsed. Green, green, green. Somewhere inside, a dead man dreamed in incomplete sentences, and did not answer, and would never answer, and had never once answered in eleven years, and she had known that, had always known that, the way you know a wound is mortal while you bandage it anyway.

* * *

She found Iyo alone in the nav bay at what the ship insisted was 0400. He had the star charts up, the closed zone rendered in the Authority's official cartography — blank space, unmarked, a hole in the map shaped like a denial — and beside it, overlaid in his own annotations, the truth: debris fields, relay coordinates, the slow drift of the Kestrel's bones.

"If I do this," Casper said, "I lose him. Again. The version of losing that I actually chose, this time, with my eyes open."

Iyo didn't turn around immediately. When he did, his young face was careful. "You've said his name twice since I met you," he said. "Both times to the pod. Never to me, never to the crew. You think I haven't noticed?"

"He's my brother."

"I know. The manifest lists the pod as 'Oyelaran, T.' under a freight code. I read manifests. It's a disease." He gestured, and she came to stand beside him at the console. On the screen, the two maps of the same space stared at each other, the lie and the truth, and neither blinked. "Can I tell you what I learned at the relay? Not the physics. The other thing."

She waited.

"Time ran backward out there, but memory didn't. That's the cruelty of it. My body got younger and I remembered everything forward — I remembered burying crewmates whose bodies were sitting up in the medbay growing younger, getting healthier, coming back to me. I grieved people who then un-died. Do you understand what that does to a person?" His voice stayed level, but his hands, those old hands, trembled slightly on the console edge. "It taught me that memory isn't a record. It's a *relationship*. You don't keep someone by storing them. You keep them by continuing to be in relation to them — by carrying what they changed in you. The body is just the vessel the relationship travels in."

"Tomas is in a pod," Casper said. "That's not a relationship. That's a hostage situation."

"No," Iyo said gently. "A hostage situation is when you keep yourself in the pod. Which, if you'll forgive me, is what you've been doing for eleven years." He let the silence sit for a moment, then turned to face her fully. "Here's what I'm actually offering. Not immortality, not safety. A choice. You can stay as you are — intact, guarded, your brother's voice sealed in a canister, your mourning postponed indefinitely — and we take our chances with the window, with the cutter, with a year of darkness and disappearing witnesses. Or you spend the fare. You pay with the wet ink, and you arrive at Halcyon not knowing why your hands shake when you pass the third rack, second row. And you find out who you are on the other side of that."

"And if I die?"

"Then you die having delivered the truth, and the tribunal will have your hard-copy testimony, and the record will show that Casper Oyelaran of the Meridian Loop chose the fare." He almost smiled. "For a smuggler, you have a very legalistic imagination of your own death."

"For a navigator," she said, "you have a very theological imagination of a jump drive."

"My mother was a cantor," Iyo said. "Everything is liturgy if you run it often enough."

* * *

She went back to the hold one more time before dawn, or the ship's idea of dawn, when the lights warmed from white to amber and the recycled air took on the faint bread-smell of the galley cycle. The crew was asleep in the berths she'd rigged for them, nine bodies in child-sized bundles, and the ship was quiet in the way ships get quiet — not silent, never silent, but murmurous, a held breath.

She stood at the pod with a hard-copy stylus in her hand. She had recorded her own testimony that night, three hours of it, everything she knew about the disaster she'd survived and the disaster she'd discovered, every fact she could excavate, and she had saved the last minute of the recording for something she hadn't planned.

She played the pod's audio bleed one final time at full clarity, the seal opened for the first time in eleven years, and let the fragments surface. *Cas. Cas, look.* A laugh, half a laugh, cut off by whatever had cut off everything. Her name, spoken by a boy who became a man who became a casualty who became her cargo.

Then she spoke into the recorder, her voice low so as not to wake the children in the next compartment:

"This is Casper Oyelaran. For the record. The backup in pod seventeen is my brother, Tomas Oyelaran, and I am not restoring him. I am letting him stay dead. Not because I've stopped loving him — I don't think love stops, I think it changes address — but because keeping him was killing the part of me that remembers him freely. He deserves better than to be my refusal. And I deserve better than to be his warden." Her throat tightened. She let it. "If I survive the jump and don't remember saying this: someone read this back to me. Please. It matters that I chose it, even if I don't remember choosing."

She sealed the recording, etched it to metal alongside the others, and racked it with the testimony core — the mass of hard-copy evidence, Dara's authentication, the nine voices of the Vespera, all of it written in the only ink that couldn't be edited.

Then she put her palm against the pod one last time, feeling the pulse beneath the shell, and keyed the shutdown sequence. The status light went from green to amber to a soft, final gray. The whispering stopped. The hold had never been so quiet, and she stood in that quiet and found, to her astonishment, that it did not crush her. It was the quiet of a door finally opening, not nailing shut.

"Mourning completed," she said to no one, to everyone, to him. "You're dismissed, little brother. Go on ahead."

* * *

At 0600 ship-time she called the crew to the galley, all nine of them plus herself, ten faces around a table built for four, and laid it out the way Iyo had laid it out for her: the drive, the negotiation, the fare. Wet ink. Possible death. Certain loss.

They listened the way her crew had never listened to her — completely, without checking the clock — and when she finished, Captain Adeyemi stood, blanket and all, and said, "We voted yesterday. The vote stands. But we'll vote again, because you weren't in the room, and this is your ship, and the fare comes out of your pocket most of all." She looked around the table. "All in favor of jumping early, paying full price, and making the bastards read what we wrote — raise your hands."

Ten hands went up. Ruth Okafor raised hers with her slate still in it. Iyo raised both of his, and smiled with his nineteen-year-old face and his ninety-year-old eyes.

"One condition," Casper said, and her voice held, mostly. "If I come out the other side and I don't know why I cry when I walk past the empty rack — somebody tells me about him. Not the file. Him. The boy who said my name like it was a marvel. Somebody owes me that story."

Iyo reached across the table and closed his old hands over her young-seeming ones. "Somebody will," he said. "Now rig the drive. We jump in eighteen hours, and this time, the past doesn't get to keep us."

Outside the viewport, the closed zone wheeled slowly past, blank on every official map, full on every honest one — and in eighteen hours they would drive straight into the heart of the lie, and ask spacetime to reconsider, and pay whatever it charged.

Casper went forward to the helm and began, with steady hands, to plot a course no ship had ever flown.
