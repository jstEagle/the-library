## 1. Dead Weight

The ore was honest work. That was the whole reason Theo Eriksen hauled it.

Eleven thousand tonnes of unrefined nickel-iron out of the Halcyon drift, packed into the Long Answer's belly like ballast for a soul. It didn't ask questions. It didn't care where she'd been or who she'd been before. You put it in a hold, you burned the math, you delivered it to a smelter that paid late and complained about purity. The ore was the closest thing in the contested orbits to a friend that never lied.

"Course locked," Cass said from everywhere and nowhere, the ship's voice coming through the deck plates more than the speakers. "Burn window in four minutes. You could sleep."

"I could," Theo said, and didn't.

She sat in the pilot's cradle with her boots hooked on the console rail, watching the drift fall away behind them on the feed. Halcyon was a scar of rock and scaffolding, a mining claim that three different registries said belonged to three different owners, which in practice meant it belonged to whoever had guns on station that week. This week it was Concord. Last month it had been Voss. The miners didn't care. The miners just dug, and people like Theo just carried, and the fleets just circled each other in the dark like two dogs pretending they weren't looking at the same bone.

Fifteen years of that. Long enough that the war-that-never-happened had become a kind of weather. You dressed for it. You didn't talk about it.

"Your heart rate is elevated," Cass said.

"My heart rate is fine."

"It is sixty-eight. Your resting rate is fifty-four. You have been sitting still for two hours."

Theo tapped the burn solution with one finger, watching the numbers settle. "Maybe I'm in love."

"With the nickel-iron."

"It's very dense. Reliable. Never wakes up on the wrong side of the hold."

Cass made the sound it made instead of laughing—a soft harmonic in the ventilation, almost a chord. Theo had stopped trying to figure out whether the AI was joking years ago. It was easier to let it be a little bit alive than to ask what it actually was. Some questions you haul around the same way you haul ore: because somebody has to, and because putting them down feels worse than carrying them.

"Four minutes," Cass said. "Sleep."

"Watch the drift for debris."

"I always watch the drift for debris."

"I know." She closed her eyes anyway, not to sleep, just to not look at anything for a while.

That was the trick of the Long Answer's hold, and Theo had gotten very good at not looking at things.

* * *

The backup pods sat along the port side, six of them, bolted into the frame where a secondary fuel bladder used to be. They'd come with the ship. That was the story, anyway—came with the ship, previous owner's estate sale, no paperwork, no returns. It was even mostly true. What she'd left out, in the one conversation she'd ever had about them, was that she'd known exactly what she was buying, and that she'd paid too much, and that she'd have paid more.

Six pods, each the size of a coffin stood on end, frosted glass, amber standby lights pulsing slow as breathing. Inside each one: a person. Or a copy of one. The distinction mattered to lawyers and priests and nobody who'd ever actually stood in front of a pod.

They'd never woken. That was the part Theo didn't let herself think about on the good days. Whoever had printed them—backed them up, spun them into flesh, whatever verb you preferred, and every verb was a fight—had never brought them up. Six sleeping copies of crew who'd died somewhere, sometime, on a ship whose name wasn't in any registry Theo could find. She'd checked once, early, with a smuggler's data-rig and a bottle of cheap gin for courage. The serial numbers on the pods had been ground off. Not scratched—ground, professional, the kind of erasure that costs more than the thing erased.

She'd meant to sell them. That was the plan, in the beginning. Backup pods with live prints were worth a fortune to the right buyer and a firing squad to the wrong one, and Theo had always been good at telling the two apart. Fifteen years, and she'd never made the call. She told herself it was because the market was too hot, because Concord biometrics enforcement had gotten tighter, because the risk math didn't work.

The truth was dumber and quieter: she didn't like the idea of them waking up alone.

So they rode in the hold, year after year, port to port, dead weight in the most literal sense, and Theo hauled ore around contested space and did not look at them, and was very good at not looking, and told herself that not looking was a kind of mercy.

The burn hit at 0340 ship time, a long smooth push that pressed her back into the cradle. Eleven days to Kestrel Gate, if the customs queue was kind. It was never kind.

* * *

Day four, she did the cargo check.

It was ritual more than necessity. Cass tracked every gram aboard with an accuracy that made manual verification pointless, but Theo walked the hold anyway, hand on the tie-down rails, counting containers she already knew the number of. Forty-one ore pods, sealed and stable. Water recycler, spare coil, the galley stores Ryn-the-cat—no. Not that. There was no cat. There had never been a cat; that was a different life, a life before, and she'd stopped keeping things that could die.

She counted. She checked seals. She did not turn left at the end of the row, because turning left at the end of the row put the pods in your eyeline, and she was very good at not looking.

"You've walked this hold nine hundred and six times," Cass said over the intercom.

"You count?"

"I count everything. It's nine hundred and six since you repainted the deck, which is the only interval that matters, because before the repaint you walked a different hold."

"The paint's the same color."

"It isn't. You went from grey to grey-with-more-blue. It was the correct choice."

Theo smiled at a tie-down rail where nobody could see her do it. This was the arrangement, and it worked: she flew the ship, Cass flew the loneliness. Between them they'd made something that functioned.

She was halfway back to the ladder when the comm chimed with a handshake request. Transponder ping, automated, from a broker relay out of Ceres-side. Not Concord, not Voss—freelance, the gray layer where people like Theo did most of their living. She let it ring twice, then answered.

"Long Answer. This is Eriksen."

"Evening, Eriksen." A voice like gravel in a drum, the kind brokers used because it made small numbers sound big. "Vashti, Meridian Line brokerage—"

Something cold walked up Theo's spine and stood there.

"—we've got a cross-haul job, Kestrel Gate to the Verge, sealed crates, no questions. Standard rates plus twenty."

"Say the first part again."

"Standard rates plus—"

"Before that."

A pause, the sound of a man recalibrating. "Meridian Line. We're a small house. You've probably not heard of us."

"No," Theo said. "Probably not." The word *Meridian* sat in her ear like a splinter. She knew the name the way everyone in the contested orbits knew it—the colony ship, the fire, the fifteen thousand souls, the martyrdom that the Concord fleet had been building monuments out of since before Theo had been anyone at all. *The Meridian gave everything so the fleet could be born.* You heard it in recruitment halls. You heard it in school feeds, if you'd had school feeds. You heard it every time an admiral needed a reason.

"You'll take the job?" Vashti asked.

"Send the specs," Theo said, because refusing fast was its own kind of flag. "I'll review."

She killed the channel and stood in the hold with her hand still on the rail, and found that her eyes had drifted left without her permission, down the row, to the frosted glass and the amber lights and the six people who never woke.

She looked away. She was good at that.

* * *

Kestrel Gate rose out of the dark on day eleven like a fistful of lit knives—a Concord fortress-station wrapped around a captured asteroid, customs cradles jutting from its flanks, the fleet's flag painted on the hull in colors you could see from a klick out. Theo had cleared this gate two hundred and change times. She knew the dance: kill your drive at the marker, broadcast your manifest, open your hold on request, smile with your voice.

"Manifest is clean," Cass murmured as they slid into the customs cradle. "Ore, spares, personal effects. Nothing that scans."

"Nothing that scans," Theo agreed, and hated how easily the sentence came.

The inspector who came up the boarding tube was young, Concord navy blues pressed sharp enough to cut, a scanner slate under one arm and the particular tiredness of someone three shifts into a rotation. He walked the hold with his slate held out, reading the ore, reading the recycler, reading the air.

Theo stood by the ladder with her arms crossed and her face arranged into the expression she thought of as *competent and bored*. It was her best one. Fifteen years of practice.

"Forty-one pods, nickel-iron, Halcyon drift provenance." The inspector frowned at his slate. "Purity's high for Halcyon."

"Good week."

"Good week." He didn't write it down. He moved down the row, and Theo tracked him in her peripheral vision the way she tracked everything—loosely, lazily, with total attention—and he was walking toward the left end of the hold, toward the frame where the fuel bladder used to be, toward the six frosted coffins with their amber hearts.

"Structural void there," Theo said. "Old bladder mount. Never refitted."

The inspector paused. His slate flicked. "Bladder mount. Right." He glanced at the plating—Cass had long ago fabricated a scan-ghost for that section, a nice boring echo of empty metal—and moved on, and Theo let her breath out through her nose, slow, a quarter second at a time.

"Clean," the inspector said at last, at the tube, stamping the slate. "Welcome to Kestrel Gate, Eriksen."

"Always a pleasure."

It was, as a rule, never a pleasure. But the stamp took, the cradle released, and the Long Answer drifted free into the station's traffic web with eleven thousand tonnes of honest ore and six secrets that didn't scan, and Theo told herself the cold feeling in her stomach was just the usual customs hangover.

It wasn't. It was the feeling of being watched, and she'd had it for three days, and it wasn't Cass, and it wasn't the station, and she couldn't find its face.

* * *

She docked at a commercial spindle two arms away from the fortress section, paid her berthing fees in untraceable scrip, and took the shuttle across to the broker exchange because Vashti's specs had finally arrived and because sitting still had started to feel like sinking.

The exchange was the usual cathedral of gray market: recycled air, bad coffee, a hundred screens running a hundred ledgers for a hundred things that officially didn't exist. Theo found the Meridian Line booth in the back row, small and tidy, and a junior factor with damp palms who slid her a data chip across the counter like it was hot.

"Sealed crates, Kestrel Gate to the Verge," the factor said. "Contents listed as archival media. No questions, no scans, no transfers. You take the chip, you take the job."

Theo turned the chip over. Cheap casing. The kind of job that was either easy money or a coffin with a payment plan.

"Standard rates plus twenty for no-questions sealed freight," she said. "That's either stolen or dangerous."

"It's *archival*," the factor said, with the emphasis of a man repeating a word he'd been told to repeat.

"Yeah." She should have walked. Fifteen years of instinct said walk—no-questions freight was how small ships became rumors. But the cold watching feeling was still on her neck, and the word *Meridian* was still in her ear like a splinter working its way toward the bone, and she heard herself say, "One question. Free of charge, since I'm asking it about you and not the cargo. The name. Meridian Line. Why that name? It's a graveyard name. Nobody names a brokerage after the Meridian unless they want people to remember something."

The factor's palms got damper. He glanced at the booth behind him, at the curtain, at nothing.

"House tradition," he said. "Old founder thing. You want the job or not?"

Theo looked at the chip in her hand. She thought about the six amber lights in her hold, pulsing slow as breathing, and about the fact that she had spent fifteen years not looking at them, and about how a person could get so good at not looking that she'd stopped noticing she was doing it.

"I want the cargo manifest for the cross-haul," she said. "Unsealed. Before I commit my ship."

"That's not—"

"Then I don't want the job." She set the chip on the counter. "And here's the funny part. I'm going to read whatever's on this chip anyway, because you handed it to me, and because I'm curious about house traditions."

She walked out with the chip in her pocket and the factor too scared to stop her, and the cold feeling on her neck had a shape now, though she couldn't have said what it was. It felt like a name she used to know.

Back aboard, she slotted the chip at the galley table with a cup of coffee she wouldn't drink, and Cass dimmed the lights without being asked, and the manifest of the Meridian Line's cross-haul scrolled up the screen in tidy columns: crate numbers, masses, handling codes.

And there, in the provenance field of the first crate, where the origin registry was supposed to be, was a ship's registration number that Theo's eyes snagged on like a nail.

She knew that number. Not from memory—memory didn't work that way, not hers, not anymore. She knew it the way you know a face across a crowded room, the way you know a name called in a dream: some part of her, some part that had been filed away and locked, stood straight up and *paid attention*.

"Cass," she said. "Run that registration. Origin registry, deep archive, all flags."

A pause. Cass's pauses were never long. This one was three full seconds, which for Cass was a geological age.

"The number doesn't resolve," Cass said. "It's been struck from every open registry. But the format is Concord fleet construction, pre-Exodus class, and the strike order is dated—" another pause, longer, "—fifteen years ago. The same week."

"The same week as what?"

Cass didn't answer. And Theo Eriksen, who was very good at not looking at things, looked at the screen, and read the line above the registration number, the provenance field, the origin ship of the sealed crates she'd been offered to carry without questions.

The field said: *MERIDIAN, colony transport, lost with all hands.*

The coffee went cold. The amber lights pulsed in the hold below her feet, slow as breathing, six hearts that never woke. And somewhere in the station's traffic web, in the fortress section, in a customs office full of tired inspectors and clean stamps, something that had been watching her for three days watched her still.

Theo sat very still in the galley of the Long Answer and felt the first stone of an avalanche come loose somewhere far above her.

"Cass," she said quietly. "I think I just found a ship that doesn't exist."

"I know," said Cass, in a voice Theo had never heard it use before. "I was there."
