## 6. Stowaway

The Long Answer's hold smelled like cold iron and old grain, and Theo liked it that way. Smells meant the scrubbers were working. Scrubbers working meant she was alone, undisturbed, and rich enough in air to waste a little on sentiment.

She was doing her weekly walk-through—hull integrity, tie-downs, atmospheric seals, the whole litany she'd performed so many times it had worn a groove in her like water through stone—when she noticed the ration lockers had been opened.

Not broken into. Opened. The magnetic seal had been tripped with a code, which meant someone had watched her punch it in, or someone had gotten lucky, or someone had spent three days getting unlucky until the lock gave up its secrets out of sheer boredom.

Theo stood very still in the middle of her own ship and listened.

The drive was down for maintenance cycle, so the hold was quiet in the way deep space is quiet—not silent, but humming at the frequency of things holding themselves together. Air recyclers. Coolant loops. Her own blood, maybe, loud in her ears because her heart had picked up speed without asking permission.

She should have gone straight to the cockpit and sealed the inner hatch. That was procedure. That was what any sane hauler did with an intruder aboard: contain, contact port security, let professionals handle it. Port security on Ceres Verge would have the intruder processed before her next cargo load finished cooling.

Instead she found herself walking toward the aft section. Toward the backup pods.

She told herself it was because the pods were expensive equipment and whoever was aboard might damage them. She told herself a lot of things on the walk back there. She'd gotten good at telling herself things over fifteen years of hauling freight through contested orbits, where every day you survived was proof you were competent enough to keep surviving, and competence was easier to believe in than happiness.

The backup pods sat along the port bulkhead like a row of pale coffins, six of them, each one a meter and a half of frosted glass and medical-grade promise. Sleeping copies of crew who never woke. She'd won them in a card game eleven years ago from a man who wouldn't say where he got them, and she'd never once powered them up, never once sold them, never once looked directly at them if she could help it. They rode in her hold like passengers who'd paid their fare in a currency she didn't want to count.

And there, curled in the gap between pod four and pod five, small enough to fit in a space no adult would choose, was a girl.

Asleep. Or pretending well enough that it didn't matter. Dark hair cut short and unevenly, like she'd done it herself with a knife and a mirror. A Voss Combine flight jacket two sizes too big, the collar frayed, some kind of unit patch half-torn off the shoulder. Boots too big too. Everything about her was too big, like she was wearing someone else's life and hadn't grown into it yet.

Theo's first thought, absurdly, was: *she's using my thermal blanket.* The grey one folded under the girl's head. Theo's second thought was that she should be thinking about weapons, distance, escape routes. Her hand actually moved toward the utility knife on her belt, and then stopped, because the girl's face in sleep looked like every face Theo had ever seen in a dockside shelter queue: young, exhausted, and braced even unconscious for the moment someone would make her leave.

Fifteen years ago, someone had made Theo leave. She couldn't remember the details—that was the thing about her memory, the soft spots, the gaps she'd learned not to poke—but she remembered the feeling. Being somewhere you weren't supposed to be, wanting something you weren't supposed to want, waiting for the boot.

She crouched down, keeping a meter of distance, and said, "Hey."

Nothing.

"Hey. I know you're awake. Your breathing changed."

The girl's eyes opened. Grey eyes, sharp, doing fast arithmetic—distance to the hatch, odds of talking her way out, odds of fighting. Theo recognized the calculation because she ran it herself every time a Concord patrol boat hailed her.

"I didn't take anything," the girl said. Voice rougher than expected. "I was going to pay you back."

"With what?"

The girl's jaw set. "I don't know yet. Something."

"You've been eating my rations."

"Some of them." A pause. "I left markers. Little strips of paper. In the locker. I'm keeping count."

Theo blinked. Of all the possible answers, that one landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there. She thought of every dockside ledger she'd ever kept, every debt tracked to the decimal because debts were the only relationships that never lied to you.

"How long?"

"Nine days. You were on Ceres Verge for six of them. I almost left twice." The girl sat up slowly, hands visible, palms out—an old gesture, trained or learned hard. "I'm Ryn."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

Theo stood up. This was the part where she called it in. This was the part where she docked at Thessaly Ring in four days and handed over a Voss runaway to whatever authority paid the handling fee, and went back to being a woman who hauled ore and avoided looking at backup pods and kept her life as sealed and routine as a pressure hull.

"The Voss Combine runs notices," she heard herself say instead. "Runaways from fleet families. There's a bounty."

"I know how much it is." Ryn's chin came up. "It's not enough to matter to you. I checked your ship's registry. You run solo, low-margin ore contracts, no bounties claimed in nine years. You don't do that kind of work."

"You checked my registry."

"I check everything. It's how I stayed alive nine days on a ship with one crew member." Something flickered across the girl's face—pride, maybe, or fear wearing pride's jacket. "You also have a dead spot in your sensor logs, aft section, cycle 4471. Three hours missing. If you're going to hide something, you should hide it better."

The temperature of the conversation changed. Theo felt it in her shoulders first, the old tightening, the sense of being seen by someone who didn't know what they were looking at but was looking anyway.

"That sensor gap is a coolant recalibration," Theo said.

"It's not. I read the power draw. Recalibration doesn't spike like that." Ryn held her gaze, and there was something underneath the bravado now, something that wasn't about leverage at all. "I'm not going to tell anyone anything. Telling people things is how I ended up running."

Theo looked at her—at the too-big jacket with its half-torn patch, at the paper markers she'd left in a ration locker like a child paying rent, at the way she'd wedged herself between the backup pods like she'd known, somehow, that those pods were the one part of the ship nobody touched—and felt the first crack go through fifteen years of armor. Not a break. Just a crack. Hairline. The kind you don't notice until something leaks through.

"Get your stuff," Theo said.

Ryn went still. "What?"

"You heard me. Blanket, papers, whatever else is yours. You're not sleeping in my hold another night."

Something fell out of the girl's face—the braced look, the waiting-for-the-boot look—and for a second she just sat there, empty-handed in every way that counted.

Then Theo turned and walked toward the galley, and after a moment she heard the scramble behind her, the rustle of a thermal blanket being gathered with shaking hands.

* * *

The galley on the Long Answer was built for one person and had been slowly colonized by the habits of one person: one mug, one bowl, one chair used, two chairs stored. Theo pulled down the second bowl for the first time in years and hated herself a little for noticing how natural it felt.

She heated stew. Not the good kind—there was no good kind, only gradations of acceptable—but the kind with actual protein in it, the kind she saved for days when a contract closed clean. She put the bowl in front of Ryn without comment and sat across from her with her own.

Ryn looked at the stew. Then at Theo. Then at the stew again.

"This is the good kind," she said.

"It's stew."

"It's the good kind. I inventoried your food stores. That's batch twelve, you have four units left, and you eat batch fourteen on ordinary days because fourteen has more filler." She picked up the spoon and then stopped, holding it like it might be a trap. "Why are you feeding me this?"

Because you left paper markers in my ration locker. Because you slept between my ghosts like you knew they were mine. Because somebody once fed me, in a year I can't properly remember, and I've been trying to pay it forward ever since without knowing to whom.

"Because you're going to work it off," Theo said. "Air, water, food, berth. You'll run numbers, check seals, learn the tie-down rig. You'll earn your keep or you'll get spaced at the next port, and I haven't decided which."

That was a lie. She knew it was a lie while she said it, and judging by the look on Ryn's face—a quick, searching glance, the same fast arithmetic as before—the girl knew it too. But neither of them said so. Some lies are load-bearing.

Ryn ate. Fast at first, then slower, catching herself, remembering manners she'd clearly been taught somewhere better than where she'd ended up. Fleet family. Officer class, probably, from the jacket. Runaways from that world didn't flee comfort; they fled what comfort cost.

"My aunt commands a picket squadron," Ryn said suddenly, to the bowl. "Captain Ilsa Varga. That's whose jacket this is. My mother's sister. I took it because it scans as authorized in Voss corridors." She glanced up, defiant, like she was handing Theo a weapon and daring her to use it. "If you're going to sell me, sell me knowing what you've got."

Theo chewed. Swallowed. Considered the offer.

"I'm not going to sell you."

"You said—"

"I know what I said." She pushed her bowl back. "Here's how it works on my ship. I don't ask where you're running from. You don't ask why I ride alone. When we hit Thessaly Ring, we reassess. Until then you pull your weight and stay out of the aft section."

Ryn's eyes flicked toward the hold. Toward the pods. "What's in the aft section?"

"Nothing that concerns you."

"It's the backup pods, isn't it. Six of them, medical grade, pre-Fracture manufacture. Those are worth more than your ship. Why does a solo ore hauler have six illegal backup pods she never powers up?" The question came out fast, hungry, the hunger of someone who'd survived on information and knew no other diet. "I'm not asking to pry. I'm asking because if I know what matters to you, I know what not to break."

There it was again. The crack, widening by a millimeter. Theo stood, collected both bowls—both bowls, one motion, like she'd been doing it forever—and said, "Wash these. Then report to the cockpit. I'll show you the nav board."

Ryn's face did something complicated. "You're teaching me nav?"

"I'm showing you which buttons not to touch. Same thing."

But as she walked away, she heard the girl laugh—one short, surprised syllable, the sound of someone who hadn't expected to laugh for a long time and had forgotten how loud it was—and Theo kept her back straight and her face neutral until she reached the corridor, where she stopped, one hand against the bulkhead, and breathed.

Four days to Thessaly Ring. Four days to decide.

She already knew what she'd decided. She'd known since the paper markers. The question wasn't whether to space the stowaway. The question was what it meant, that the first person in fifteen years she'd let past her seal line was a sixteen-year-old thief who inventoried everything—including, apparently, her captain.

* * *

Second shift. Theo sat in the cockpit running burn calculations she didn't need to run, and listened to Ryn move through the ship.

The girl was quiet, but not silent—she narrated under her breath as she worked, a habit Theo recognized from dockside kids who'd grown up around machinery that punished inattention. Checking tie-downs. Testing the galley seal. And then, inevitably, drifting aft, because sixteen-year-olds and gravity both tend toward the thing you forbid.

Theo let her go. For ten minutes. Fifteen.

When she finally walked back, Ryn was standing in front of the backup pods, close but not touching, her breath fogging the frosted glass of pod three. Inside, faintly visible through the frost, a face. A woman's face, peaceful, mid-thirties, someone who had lain down to sleep and been promised a morning that never came.

"They're occupied," Ryn said quietly. She didn't turn around. "All six. I thought they were empty stock. Salvage. But they're occupied."

"They are."

"Who are they?"

Theo stood in the hold doorway with her arms crossed and felt the question land on top of all the other questions she carried—the manifest with her name on it, burned but not forgotten; the inspector's eyes on her at Kestrel Gate; the soft spots in her memory where fifteen years ago should have been.

"Crew," she said. "From a ship that doesn't exist anymore."

"What happened to their ship?"

"I don't know." And that, at least, was true. "They were asleep when I got them. They're still asleep. Someday I'll wake them up or bury them properly. Until then they ride with me."

Ryn turned around. In the dim hold light, with the pods glowing pale behind her, she looked older than sixteen and younger at the same time, and she studied Theo with those sharp grey eyes like she was reading a manifest of her own.

"You keep ghosts," she said. Not accusing. Almost gentle.

"I keep cargo."

"No." Ryn shook her head slowly. "Cargo you sell. Cargo you use. You don't feed them air for eleven years and never look at them unless you're keeping them for someone. Or for something." She hesitated, then said, quieter: "My mother used to say people who keep ghosts are waiting for permission to stop."

Theo didn't answer. Couldn't. The words went into her like a splinter, finding something true beneath the armor, and she stood there in the hum of her own ship, surrounded by sleeping strangers and one awake one, feeling the crack widen another millimeter.

"Back to work," she said finally, roughly. "Tie-downs, row three. And Ryn?"

"Yeah?"

"Stay out of my logs."

The girl smiled—small, tired, real. "No promises, Captain."

* * *

Later, alone in her bunk, Theo pulled up the ship's registry and looked at the crew field. One name. Hers. It had been one name for fifteen years, through every contract, every inspection, every cold transit. One name, because names were liabilities and solitude was armor and armor had kept her alive.

She stared at the field for a long time.

Then she added a second line. Provisional crew, probationary status, review at Thessaly Ring. She typed *Ryn* into it, no surname, because the girl hadn't offered one and Theo hadn't asked, and that felt right somehow—two incomplete records riding the same ship, each holding a piece the other was missing.

She closed the file before she could think better of it.

In the hold, six pods hummed their patient hum. Somewhere ahead, Thessaly Ring waited with its customs drones and its bounty boards and its easy exit. And somewhere behind her, in a data lot she'd burned and couldn't unburn, a name on a manifest that shouldn't exist kept whispering that nothing about her life was what she thought it was.

One problem at a time, she told herself.

She fell asleep listening to the sound of someone else breathing aboard her ship, and for the first time in fifteen years, it didn't feel like a threat.

It felt like a leak in the armor.

And she let it.
