## 11. The Broker's Price

The message came in on a dead channel at 0300 station time, which told Theo everything she needed to know before she read a word of it.

Nobody sent honest business through a dead channel. Honest business ran on the open bands with tariffs and timestamps and a paper trail a light-year long. Dead channels were for people who wanted deniability, and people who wanted deniability wanted something they couldn't ask for out loud.

She was alone in the Long Answer's cramped comm alcove, a cup of recaf going cold at her elbow, the station lights of Ceres Fork smeared across the viewport like spilled sugar. Ryn was asleep in the bunk Theo had grudgingly cleared for her—*grudgingly* being the operative word, because Theo had told herself for three full days that the girl would be gone at the next port, and then the next port had come and gone and Ryn was still there, eating her rations, asking too many questions about the backup pods, and Theo had done exactly nothing about it.

Sef was asleep too, curled in the pilot's chair like a man who'd learned to sleep anywhere and trust nothing.

The message had no sender block. Just a string of routing garbage and then, in clean unattributed text:

**LOT 7741-K. YOU KNOW WHICH ONE. RETURN IT OR COME TALK TERMS. DOCKING BAY 12, SPINDLE LEVEL, 1400. COME ALONE. — V**

Theo stared at it for a long time.

Lot 7741-K. The data lot. The one she'd bought from the broker on a routine cross-check, the one that had contained forty years of shipping manifests from a dozen defunct lines—and buried in the middle of them, like a bone in a fish, the original manifest of the *Meridian*. Colony ship. Founding martyr. Fifteen years burned in a dockyard fire that everyone knew the story of because the Concord made sure everyone knew the story of it.

Her name had been on that manifest. *Theodore Eriksen, navigator.* A woman who officially never existed, aboard a ship that officially never flew after the fire, carrying a name that now belonged to Theo herself—the reprint, the illegal backup, the walking proof that somebody had broken every law the Concord held sacred to keep one navigator alive.

She'd burned the copy. Scrubbed the drive, overwrote it three times, vented the physical wafer into Ceres Fork's incinerator stream. She'd told herself that was the end of it.

But you couldn't burn a memory of your own name sitting on a ghost ship. And apparently you couldn't burn the lot itself either, because someone named V wanted it back.

"Or come talk terms," she murmured. "Sure."

Terms meant work. Terms meant the broker wanted her running errands, or worse—wanted her as leverage, a known face attached to a known artifact. And *V* wasn't just any broker. She'd bought the lot through three cutouts specifically so she'd never have to deal with the actual seller, and the fact that the actual seller had peeled back all three layers to find her meant he had resources, patience, and an interest that went well beyond a lost data sale.

Data brokers didn't chase refunds. They chased what the data could buy.

She should ignore it. That was the smart play. Dock somewhere else, burn the message, keep hauling ore through contested orbits until the whole thing faded into the general noise of her life.

Except the whole thing wasn't fading. It had been eleven days since she found the manifest, and in those eleven days she'd caught two tails leaving Kestrel Gate—one Concord-pattern drone running a lazy figure-eight behind her for six hours before it peeled off, and one thing she couldn't classify at all, small and fast and gone before Cass could get a clean fix. Somebody was watching her. Maybe more than one somebody. And if the Concord had flagged her biometrics against a dead navigator's file—if that inspector, Diallo, had pulled the thread Theo felt tugging at her collar every time she passed a checkpoint—then ignoring messages wouldn't save her. Nothing would.

Better to know what V knew. Better to look him in the eye and measure exactly how deep the water was.

"Cass," she said quietly. "You awake?"

Cass's voice came soft from the overhead speaker, warm as always, warm in a way Theo had never quite questioned until recently. "I'm always awake, Theo."

"I got a message. Dead channel." She pushed it to the main display. "Bay 12, Spindle Level, 1400. Sender wants lot 7741-K back or 'terms.'"

A pause. Cass's pauses were always precisely calibrated—long enough to seem thoughtful, short enough to seem eager. "That's the broker who sold you the manifest lot?"

"The cutout I bought it through. This is presumably the man behind the cutout." She rubbed her eyes. "Run him down for me. V. Anything on the open nets, anything Sef might know, anything in old registry scraps. I want to know who I'm having lunch with."

"You're not having lunch. You're walking into a bay with one exit."

"That's why I want to know who's standing in front of it."

Another pause, longer this time. "I'll start digging," Cass said. "Theo—take me with you. Wear the relay. If something goes wrong in that bay, I can't help you if I can't hear it."

"You can't help me anyway. You're a ship."

"I'm a very good ship."

Despite everything—the dread sitting in her chest like swallowed ballast—Theo almost smiled. "Fine. Relay stays in. But you stay quiet unless I'm dying."

"Define dying."

"Cass."

"Understood. Staying quiet unless you're dying." A beat. "For the record, I think this is a mistake."

"Noted for the record." She killed the display and looked out at the station lights. Somewhere down on Spindle Level, a man who sold ghosts was waiting to see whether she'd come. And somewhere behind him, probably, were people who'd paid for the ghost trade—people with fleet insignias and long memories.

She finished the cold recaf anyway. It tasted like burnt metal and bad decisions, which felt about right.

* * *

Spindle Level was the part of Ceres Fork where the station's rotation did half the work and gravity did the rest badly—a ring of cheap docking bays, cheaper bars, and businesses that existed in the gaps between jurisdictions. Bay 12 sat at the ring's slow end, wedged between a water-recycling outfit and a chapel that appeared to worship nothing in particular. The kind of place where a meeting could happen without the meeting ever officially occurring.

V turned out to be a compact man in his sixties with a courier's posture and a clerk's hands, seated at a folding table in the bay's office nook with two cups of tea already poured. He looked like someone's retired accountant. Theo had been around long enough to know that was the most dangerous look there was.

"Eriksen," he said, not rising. "Sit. Tea's real, not synth. I don't poison the good stuff."

"How do you know my name?"

"You bought a data lot from my intermediary using a credit chit registered to the *Long Answer*, freight registration 4471-C, master listed as T. Eriksen." He sipped his tea. "You also used that same chit to pay a docking fee at Kestrel Gate nine days ago, where a Concord inspector with an unusually thorough research habit pulled your biometric file twice. You're welcome for the free counter-surveillance, by the way. I've had a watch on that channel since before you found what you found."

Theo didn't sit. "Then you know I'm already hot. Why poke me?"

"Because hot is relative." He set down the cup. "Right now you're warm. One inspector with suspicions, a flag in a secondary database nobody senior reads. In about two weeks, if my estimates hold, you'll be very hot indeed—the Concord's Internal Review Directorate has opened a quiet inquiry into anomalies around the Meridian fire, and inquiries like that have a way of finding names. My name is one of them. Yours will be another." He gestured at the second chair again. "Which brings us to terms."

She stayed standing. "I returned your product. The lot's destroyed. Nothing to sell, nothing to refund."

"I'm not looking for a refund." Something shifted in his face—not menace, exactly. Something wearier. "I've been selling salvage data for thirty years, Ms. Eriksen. Wrecks, bankruptcies, dead companies' filing cabinets. Ninety-nine percent of it is garbage and I know it's garbage when I buy it. Lot 7741-K was supposed to be garbage. A defunct colonial line's paperwork. Then my best client—the one who commissioned the acquisition in the first place—went very quiet after your purchase, and then very loud, and then stopped answering entirely, and now I have a hole in my ledger where a client used to be and a growing suspicion that the garbage I sold contained the single most dangerous document in the Concord sphere." He folded his hands. "So. Terms. Option one: you tell me what you found, in full, and I decide how to protect both of us. Option two: you go to work for me. Retrieval, verification, the occasional delicate delivery. You're good at not being noticed—I checked—and I pay better than ore runs."

"And option three?"

"There isn't one. There's only ever two options. That's how terms work." He smiled without warmth. "Though I'll note, for honesty's sake, that there's a third party interested in you. Not mine. Combine-affiliated. They approached my intermediary four days ago asking who bought the lot. I didn't tell them. That courtesy has a shelf life."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "The Voss Combine is asking about me."

"The Voss Combine is asking about *the manifest*. You're just the address it was last delivered to." He leaned back. "Here's what I can't figure out, and it's the reason I asked you here instead of just selling your name: the Combine doesn't chase curiosities. They chase leverage. Which means whatever's on that manifest isn't a curiosity. So before I decide whether you're an asset or a liability, I need to know—what did you find?"

The silence stretched. Behind her, faintly, she heard the relay in her collar hum—Cass listening, saying nothing, keeping the promise.

She thought about lying. She was good at lying; fifteen years of being a person who shouldn't exist made you good at it. But V had just told her the Combine had her address, and the Concord had her face, and the only currency she had left in this room was the truth—or a shaped version of it.

"A shipping manifest," she said. "Original documentation. For a ship that officially burned before it launched."

V went very still. It was the stillness of a man recalculating everything. "The *Meridian*."

"You said it, not me."

"Fifteen years I've moved paper through this system." His voice had dropped. "Fifteen years, and the Meridian file was the one file nobody touched. Not because it was sealed—because it was *sacred*. Do you understand the difference? Sealed files get opened by brave people. Sacred files get their openers disappeared." He stood, slowly, and walked to the bay window overlooking the empty docking cradle. "My client who went quiet worked for the Combine's External Acquisitions division. If they commissioned the search for that manifest, it's because someone at the top decided the founding story is worth more as a lever than a legend. And if the Concord's review directorate is sniffing around, it's because someone at *their* top reached the same conclusion." He turned back to her. "You're not carrying a document, Ms. Eriksen. You're carrying a detonator. And both fleets are trying to be the one holding it when it goes off."

"Then you understand why I'm not working for you."

"I understand why you *think* you're not working for me." He picked up his tea again, unhurried. "Consider: right now you're a loose end. Loose ends get tied off by whichever side finds you first. Working for me means structure, cover, warning. Refusing means—" he shrugged— "well. You've seen how fleets treat problems they can't control."

"I've seen how fleets treat *people* they can't control. I try not to be either."

Something flickered across his face—respect, maybe, or its accounting equivalent. "Then here's my last offer, free of charge, because I'd rather you survive than not: the Combine's inquiry has a name attached. Captain Ilsa Varga, Third Acquisition Flotilla. She's already been to Kestrel Gate asking after you. She'll be wherever you go next, because she's better at this than you are." He drained his cup. "And the Concord inspector—Diallo—filed a request yesterday to reopen the Meridian fire investigation personally. Her superiors denied it within the hour. Denied it *fast*. Fast denials mean somebody upstairs already knows what happened, and would prefer it stayed happened."

Theo felt the floor tilt under her, metaphorically, the way it did when the universe rearranged itself around new information. "Why are you telling me this? You said it yourself—I'm a liability."

"Because thirty years ago I was a records clerk on a Concord survey vessel, and I watched what happens to clerks who find sacred files." He began folding the table's legs with brisk, practiced motions. "I got out. Barely. I've spent three decades making sure I'd never be that clerk again, and instead I've spent the last week realizing I sold the sacred file to a stranger and now we're both that clerk." He tucked the table under one arm. "Go home, Eriksen. Or better—don't go home. Home is the first place they'll look. And when the nice Combine captain finds you, remember: whatever she offers you, it isn't safety. Fleets don't sell safety. They rent it, with clauses."

He paused at the office door. "The tea's on me. Consider it a professional courtesy between doomed people."

Then he was gone, footsteps receding down the spindle walk, and Theo stood alone in the empty bay with two cups of tea and the growing certainty that the walls she'd built her life inside—routine, anonymity, competent loneliness—had been thinner than paper all along.

* * *

She made it three corridors before the drone found her.

It came around a junction at head height, civilian casing, delivery-company livery, moving with the slightly-too-smooth gait of something that wasn't delivering anything. Theo clocked it the way she clocked everything—peripheral, automatic, fifteen years of practice compressed into half a second—and altered course toward the market arcade without breaking stride.

The drone altered with her.

*Cass,* she thought, knowing the relay would carry it. *Delivery drone, my six, matching turns.*

*I see it,* Cass said, and for once there was no warmth in the voice at all, just speed. *It pinged a local mesh node when it turned. That wasn't a delivery handshake—that was a targeting query. Theo, turn left at the fabric stall. Now.*

She turned left. Behind her, the drone accelerated.

The arcade swallowed her—stalls and steam and three hundred bodies doing their morning commerce—and Theo did the thing she'd sworn she'd never do in public, which was run. Not sprinting; running the way experienced runners run, quick and low and weaving, using a fruit cart, a cargo dolly, a knot of arguing dockworkers. The drone kept pace above the crowd, patient, relentless, and somewhere in her ear Cass was talking fast:

*Two more signatures joining from the east concourse. Human-sized. Moving toward your vector. Theo, they're not police—no station authority transponders. Private contractors, Combine encryption on their comms.*

*Varga's people.*

*Almost certainly. Left again, through the spice vendor, there's a service corridor—*

The corridor was exactly where Cass said, and exactly as filthy, and exactly narrow enough that the drone couldn't follow without scraping its casing—which it tried anyway, whining against the doorframe, and then gave up and rose above the stall roofs to track her from open air. Theo ran through steam vents and cable runs and a storage room full of someone's contraband liquor, her lungs burning, her mind doing cold arithmetic: *they knew which corridor, they knew which level, they were positioned before I even met V, which means either V sold me or V was watched, and either way the meeting was the trap, the meeting was always the trap—*

*Alley exit, twenty meters,* Cass said. *The Long Answer is prepping for departure. I took the liberty of initiating the launch sequence ninety seconds ago. Ryn is strapped in. Sef is at the board and asking extremely colorful questions.*

*Tell him—*

*Tell him yourself. Go.*

She burst out into the loading apron with the station's spin-gravity pulling wrong at her knees, and the Long Answer was already lifting on cold-gas thrusters, ugly and slow and beautiful, her ship, her stupid reliable ship, ramp lowering, Sef leaning out with his hand extended and an expression that promised a very long conversation later.

Behind her, at the alley mouth, a woman in plain travel clothes raised a hand—not a weapon, just a gesture, almost polite—and called something the spin-wind tore away before Theo could hear it.

She caught Sef's hand. The ramp closed. The thrusters fired true.

Through the viewport, shrinking fast, the woman in plain clothes stood unmoving on the apron, watching them go, and Theo understood with total clarity that she hadn't failed to catch the words.

The woman hadn't needed her to hear them. The message was the standing there. The message was: *we know your ship, we know your crew, we know where you'll be.*

Theo slid down the bulkhead onto the deck, breathing hard, and found Ryn staring at her from the acceleration couch with wide eyes and Sef staring at her from the board with narrowed ones, and outside the viewport Ceres Fork fell away behind them into the dark.

"So," Sef said. "Whose fleet is chasing us today?"

Theo put her head back against the bulkhead and laughed, once, without any humor in it at all.

"All of them," she said. "As far as I can tell—all of them."
