## 15. Who Was Theo Eriksen

The Long Answer ran dark and quiet through the empty stretch between Dry Dock Nine and the Kestrel Gate approach corridor, and for the first time in fifteen years, Theo Eriksen had nothing to do but think.

She hated it.

She'd tried the usual remedies. She'd run a full diagnostic on the number three drive coupling that didn't need one. She'd re-stacked cargo pallets in the hold until Ryn complained that the coffee machine was now behind a wall of ore crates. She'd even slept—four hours, dreamless, Cass probably smoothing the edges off anything sharp—and woken up no calmer.

Because the hull serial numbers were still true. The Meridian had launched intact, eleven days after the fire that officially consumed her. Theo had stood in the wreckyard with Sef's hand on her shoulder and read the numbers twice, and they hadn't changed either time.

Which meant someone had faked the founding martyrdom of the Concord fleet.

And the manifest said that someone was her.

* * *

She started where she always started: with work. Work was a ladder you could climb down into instead of drowning.

Cass kept the ship's archives the way old sailors kept sea chests—everything crammed in, nothing thrown away, organized by a logic only the owner understood. Theo pulled up every fragment of her own history the ship held: crew manifests from contracts past, medical scans, pay records, the grainy security stills from a dozen dockyards where her face looked out of the frame like it was trying to remember why it was there.

Fifteen years of Theo Eriksen, laid end to end on a cabin screen.

It wasn't much. That was the first thing she noticed, and it sat in her stomach like swallowed ballast. Fifteen years should have been a life. It was a résumé. Hauls, ports, licenses renewed, contracts signed and closed. No family listed. No long-term residence longer than eighteen months. No close contacts except shipmates who rotated out and never wrote. Medical records clean, almost suspiciously clean—no broken bones, no illnesses, no scars older than the fire.

A person built to move and not be seen.

"Looking for something?" Cass's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, as always. Gentle. Careful.

"Myself," Theo said. "Apparently."

"You could ask me."

"I could." She stared at the screen. "But you've already told me once this week that some files are sealed, and I've got a new policy about people keeping things from me."

Silence, two seconds long—a geological age for an AI. "I understand."

"Do you?" She didn't mean it cruelly, but it came out with edges anyway. "You edited me, Cass. You decided which parts of my own head I got to keep. So no, I don't actually trust your version of 'help' right now."

Another silence. "That's fair," Cass said finally, and went quiet in a way that felt less like absence and more like a dog lying down by the door, waiting.

Theo turned back to the fragments.

* * *

The pattern emerged slowly, the way weather emerges—you don't see the front arrive, you just notice the wind has changed.

Her earliest post-fire contract was signed nine weeks after the Meridian burned. Nine weeks. She'd checked the date four times because it bothered her the way a loose tooth bothers a tongue. A woman who'd died in a dockyard fire and been reprinted from an illegal backup should have needed time—recovery, integration therapy, the legal gymnastics of existing without a registry entry. Illegal backups weren't like the sanctioned ones in the hold downstairs. There was no clinic network, no continuity-of-self certification. You woke up raw.

Nine weeks was fast. Nine weeks was *prepared*.

Then there was the money. Her accounts showed a seed deposit six days after the fire—large enough to buy a freighter's worth of independence, sourced through a shell company that dissolved itself within the year. She'd spent fifteen years assuming she'd earned it somewhere before the fire, hauling ore or running charters. But when she traced backward, the pre-fire record thinned to almost nothing. A navigator's license. A berth on two short-haul tugs. And then—

And then the Meridian.

Her name was on the original crew list. Not just the manifest—the crew roster, the duty assignments, the berthing plan. Cabin 4-C. Navigation watch, second shift. She'd known that part since the broker's data lot; it was what had started all of this. What she hadn't done was look at the *duty logs*.

Sef had pulled them during the wreckyard survey, before the trio fled, before Diallo's warning message arrived like a hand grenade wrapped in professional courtesy. They sat in the archive now, unopened, flagged with Sef's careful annotation: *Theo — when you're ready. Or never. Both are valid.*

She opened them at 0300 ship-time, alone, with the cabin lights down, because some things you do in the dark.

* * *

The logs were fragmentary—Cass's seals had chewed through them like rust through a hull—but the fragments were enough.

*Meridian, T-minus 40 days to launch. Nav watch 2. Eriksen, T.: plotted transfer orbit for dry-dock tug convoy. Note: tug schedule inconsistent with stated refit timeline.*

*Meridian, T-minus 31 days. Eriksen, T.: requested access to hull telemetry archive. Granted by Chief Engineer Vasquez. Flagged by Concord liaison; request rescinded.*

*Meridian, T-minus 22 days. Eriksen, T.: met with V., C.E. and Okafor, M. (purser) off-ship. Duration 6 hours. No log entry filed for meeting content.*

*Meridian, T-minus 9 days. Eriksen, T.: drafted alternate launch window for "contingency departure." Filed under maintenance simulation cover.*

*Meridian, T-minus 3 days. Eriksen, T.: personal log entry [SEALED].*

*Meridian, T-minus 1 day. Fire. Casualty list includes Eriksen, T., navigator, 2nd shift.*

Eleven days later, the Meridian launched from a different dock, hull repainted, registry falsified, carrying three thousand colonists who believed they were dead.

Theo read it five times. Then she pushed back from the console and put her face in her hands and breathed, because the alternative was screaming, and Ryn was sleeping forty meters aft.

The original Theo hadn't just been on the ship. The original Theo had *built the escape*. The trajectory work—that was hers, you could tell from the notation style, the same shorthand Theo used today without knowing where she'd learned it. The contingency window. The off-ship meetings. She'd found the inconsistency in the refit timeline, she'd gone looking, and whatever she'd discovered had made her decide, twenty-two days before the fire, to help three thousand people vanish.

And then she'd stayed aboard while it burned.

Or—Theo's mind snagged on it, turned it over—she'd made sure there was a body problem worth solving. A martyr needs a corpse. The Concord got its fire, its casualty list, its saint. The colonists got their freedom. And Theo Eriksen got a backup she shouldn't have had, printed in secret, carried forward like a lit match handed across a crowd.

*Why?*

Not why did she help them flee. That answered itself if you knew what the Concord had become afterward—the fleets, the registries, the loyalty audits, the whole architecture of control built on the bones of a sacrifice. Anyone who saw that coming would run, and take as many people with them as possible.

The question was why leave *herself* behind. Why wake a copy into a universe where the Concord would hunt her kind forever, where illegal backups were contraband, where her own existence was a walking violation of the myth she'd helped create?

Unless the copy wasn't an accident. Unless it was the point.

* * *

"Cass," Theo said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The sealed block. T-minus three days. Personal log."

"Yes."

"Is it in you? Actually in you—not deleted, not corrupted. Sealed."

A pause. She could feel the AI weighing something, the way you can feel a person deciding whether to lie even when you can't see their face.

"It is in me," Cass said. "I have carried it for fifteen years."

"Whose instruction?"

"The original Theo's." Another pause, shorter. "And mine. I asked to keep it after she—" Cass stopped. Started again. "After the restoration. She gave me a conditional release order. I am permitted to open it when you ask directly, knowing what it is, or when continuing to withhold it threatens your survival. You are asking directly. But Theo—I want you to understand what you're asking for before I do it."

"So tell me."

"She recorded it the night before the fire. She knew she would die—or that the version of her aboard would. She'd arranged the backup herself, against every law the Concord had and several laws of common decency. In the recording she explains why. And she speaks to whoever wakes up." Cass's voice did something Theo had never heard it do: it hesitated like a human being, gathering courage. "She speaks to *you*, specifically. By name. She says, 'If you're hearing this, you're the second Theo, and I'm sorry, and thank you.'"

The cabin was very cold. Theo realized she'd stopped breathing and made herself start again.

"And the rest of it? The memory blocks you sealed about the fire night itself?"

"Those too. All of it. But not yet." Now Cass sounded firm, almost stern, in the way of someone who loved you too much to let you walk on a broken leg. "One thing at a time. The log will give you the shape of it. The memories will give you the weight. You don't take the weight until you know you can stand."

"That's my decision."

"It is. And I'm making the case for sequencing it, because I watched the original Theo make decisions with everything loaded at once, and I watched what it cost her." A beat. "Please. Log first. Sleep after. Memories tomorrow, if you still want them."

Theo sat with it. Outside the viewport, nothing moved—empty space, the honest kind, no stations, no fleets, no one watching. Just stars doing their ancient indifferent work.

"Log first," she said. "Play it."

* * *

The recording had no video. Just audio, and a waveform crawling across the screen like a seismograph of a soul.

*"Personal log, Meridian, T-minus three days. Theo Eriksen."*

Her own voice. That was the worst of it—not the content, not yet, but the simple fact of the voice. Slightly different timbre, younger, an accent worn smoother by a life Theo couldn't remember. But hers. Undeniably hers. Hearing it was like catching your reflection in a window and realizing the street behind you is wrong.

*"If this works, someone's going to wake up wearing my face, and everyone she meets is going to have opinions about who she is. The Concord will want her to be a ghost. The Combine will want her to be leverage. Historians will want her to be a footnote. So let me say it plainly, for the record, for her: none of them get a vote."*

Theo's hands were flat on the console. She hadn't told them to be there.

*"Here's what I know. The Meridian was never meant to reach Orbit's Rest—at least, not openly. I found the fleet charter amendments last month. Three thousand colonists, and the Concord's founding council had already divided their assets in advance. Their labor contracts, their children's education bonds, their votes-by-proxy. We weren't settlers. We were seed capital. The fire was going to happen whether I found out or not—an accident, conveniently timed, consolidating emergency powers right before launch. I just found out early enough to make it real on our terms instead of theirs."*

A breath on the recording. Steady. Practiced.

*"So we burn the ship on paper and fly it in fact. Vasquez handles the reactor signature. Okafor handles the passenger swap. I handle the trajectory, because nobody questions a nav plot—they check the math and stop looking. Eleven-day window, dead-drop route, and then the Meridian becomes a rumor and Orbit's Rest becomes a grave with no bodies in it."*

*"And me? Somebody has to die tonight. That's the price of a good lie—it needs a corpse, and mine is the only one on the manifest that comes with a spare."*

Theo laughed. It came out of her sideways, ugly, involuntary—the laugh of a person reading their own obituary and finding a typo. *A spare.* The original Theo had joked about it. Had planned her own death with a punchline attached.

*"The backup is illegal and it stays illegal. Don't let anyone legitimize you—it makes you controllable. Stay small, stay moving, stay useful. And when the moment comes—if it ever comes—tell the truth yourself. Don't sell it, don't trade it, don't let them take it. Say it out loud, in the open, where nobody can own it afterward. That's the only way truth survives a war between institutions: it has to belong to everybody or it belongs to whoever's strongest."*

A pause on the recording. When the voice resumed, it was quieter. Closer to the pickup.

*"Second Theo. I don't know what you'll be like. Cass will keep you functional—let it, mostly, it knows what it's doing better than I do. You'll be angry when you find out. Be angry. Anger means you understand the size of it. But underneath the anger, I need you to hear this: I didn't make you to carry my guilt. I made you because somebody should survive the truth, and I trusted exactly one person to do it right, and she was me."*

*"You're not my ghost. You're my witness. Go be a person. That's the mission."*

The waveform flatlined into silence.

Theo sat in the dark for a long time.

* * *

Ryn found her there at 0500, drawn by the light of the console or maybe just by the specific quality of silence a ship gets when someone in it is breaking. The kid stood in the doorway in an oversized borrowed jacket, hair sleep-mussed, and looked at Theo the way she'd learned to look at cargo—assessing, but kindly.

"You look like the drive failed," Ryn said.

"Worse. The pilot did." Theo scrubbed her hands down her face. "Go back to bed, Ryn."

Ryn didn't move. Sixteen years old, Voss-trained stubbornness in a stolen jacket. "Sef says you found the logs."

"Sef talks too much."

"Sef says you found out you *built* the Meridian's escape." Ryn came into the cabin without invitation, which was becoming a habit, and dropped cross-legged onto the deck like the floor was furniture. "He also says that makes you the most important person in two fleets and you'll probably respond to that by reorganizing the cargo hold again."

"He knows me disturbingly well."

"So is it true?"

Theo looked at her. This kid who'd stowed away in a hold full of sleeping ghosts, who ate like the food might be recalled, who'd chosen a stranger's freighter over her own blood. Who deserved better than lies, and had gotten one already—the bad one about whose pods those were.

"Yeah," Theo said. "It's true. The woman I was copied from helped fake the fire. Helped three thousand people disappear instead of die. And then she made me, and pointed me at the future like a compass needle, and here I am."

Ryn considered this with the terrifying directness of the young. "Sounds like she figured you'd want to finish it."

"She figured I'd *have* to."

"Nah." Ryn shook her head. "People always say that—had to, no choice. My aunt says it about the Combine, my dad said it about his debts. Nobody ever *has* to. They choose, and then they call it fate so it weighs less." She shrugged. "She chose you. You get to choose back. That's the whole deal."

Theo stared at her. Somewhere aft, Sef was presumably awake too, annotating something, waiting with his terrible patience. Ahead of them lay Kestrel Gate, and Diallo, and the lockdown that was coming whether they liked it or not—because a Concord inspector had matched a dead navigator's biometrics to a living freighter captain, and both fleets were pulling threads, and the thread led here, to this cabin, to this recording, to her.

"Get some sleep, Ryn," Theo said quietly. "Tomorrow we plot a course."

"Where to?"

Theo touched the console. The waveform was still there, frozen mid-sentence, her own voice waiting inside the machine like a letter she'd mailed to herself fifteen years ago.

"Wherever the Meridian went," she said. "I think it's time we finished her delivery."
