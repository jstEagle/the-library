## 1. Manifest of Ghosts

The trick to smuggling memories, Dominic Coldharbour had learned by his fourteenth year, was not to hide them but to make them boring.

Customs scanners at the Meridian Gate checkpoint were tuned for drama. They flagged high-arousal neural patterns — first kisses, battlefield adrenaline, the moment a dying mother pressed her newborn into your arms and said *remember this*. Grief, joy, terror: all of it lit up a scanner like a flare. But nobody's attention survived contact with forty hours of someone else's tax records, or a wedding where the couple had argued about table settings for six months and then cried through the ceremony out of sheer exhaustion. Boredom was camouflage. Boredom was a cloak woven from the dullest thread humanity had ever produced.

So Dominic ran memories the way other men ran laundry: in enormous, unremarkable bundles, folded neatly between layers of tedium.

"Vessel *Kestrel's Regret*, you are cleared for approach," said the customs drone, in a voice that had been designed to be soothing and had instead achieved something closer to patient contempt. "Please maintain current speed and heading. Do not adjust your emotional signature."

"I never do," Dominic told it. "I'm emotionally constipated by design."

The drone did not laugh. Drones never laughed, which Dominic considered the great tragedy of the age. He eased the *Regret* — a cargo hauler so old she'd been named back when people still bothered naming things hopefully — through the gate's shadow and into the long dark between the fleets.

Here is what most citizens of both halves never understood about the sundered fleet: the Waking and the Sleeping were not two nations. They were one argument, held so long it had grown architecture. The Waking kept their ships flying, generation after generation, because stopping felt like surrender. The Sleeping had banked themselves into cold storage aboard their hulks, dreaming in shifts, on the theory that whoever woke last inherited everything. Between them stretched ninety days of truce — the latest in a series of truces, each one shorter than the last, each one signed with more gritted teeth — and across that gulf ran people like Dominic, carrying the only cargo either side still wanted from the other: themselves.

A grandmother's memory of a coastline neither fleet would see again. A father's voice singing off-key. The smell, recorded faithfully by some long-dead cortex-lace, of bread baking in a galley that had since been converted into a hydroponics bay. That was the trade. Not weapons, not fuel. *Remembering*, sold by the gram.

Dominic loved the work the way you love anything you're good at and slightly ashamed of.

He was three days out of the Gate, deep in the quiet stretch where the beacon traffic thinned to nothing and the hull ticks of the *Regret* became the loudest conversation aboard, when he decided to actually read his manifest.

This was, he would later reflect, the entire problem. He almost never read his manifest. Reading your manifest was like asking a magician how the trick worked: technically permitted, spiritually inadvisable. The guild's quartermasters built the loads; the runners carried them; questions were what got people spaced. But it was a slow run, the coffee was hot, and there is a particular species of boredom that makes even paperwork feel like entertainment.

He pulled up the cargo ledger on the galley screen and scrolled.

Three hundred memory-capsules, standard grain, sealed in wax-paper sleeves like the world's saddest confectionery. Destinations split between the *Halcyon Reach* (Sleeping side, berth nine) and the *Patient Aurora* (Sleeping side, berth eleven). A dozen high-value items in the shielded locker aft, including — he smiled — a full sensory recording of the Waking council chamber's last session, purchased by parties unknown on the Sleeping side, which meant somewhere a diplomat was about to have a very bad week. All normal. All gloriously, profitably dull.

Then he reached line 314.

**PASSENGER (INERT): 1 — MERIDIAN REGISTRY — NAME WITHHELD BY SHIPPER**

Dominic frowned at it. Inert passengers weren't unusual — cryo-pods made the crossing sometimes, families reuniting a sleeper with a waking cousin, that sort of thing. Expensive, delicate work, and the guild charged accordingly. But the registry field was wrong. Ship registries were nine-character codes, and every code mapped to a hull in the fleet's master rolls.

He typed the code into the cross-reference and waited.

The screen thought about it for an unusually long time. Then it returned:

**MERIDIAN — STATUS: SCRAPPED. DATE OF DECOMMISSION: 40 YEARS PRIOR. HULL DISPOSITION: RENDERED, ORBITAL YARD 7. NO ACTIVE REGISTRY.**

Dominic set down his coffee.

"Okay," he said to the empty galley. "That's a clerical error. That's obviously a clerical error."

It was not, his hindbrain noted, behaving like a clerical error. Clerical errors were small. Clerical errors were a transposed digit, a berth number off by one. This was a passenger listed aboard a ship that had been fed into a rendering furnace before Dominic was old enough to walk, its registry struck from the rolls with the finality of a gravestone. Somebody had written a dead ship onto a live manifest, and had done it in the guild's own cipher, in the guild's own hand, which meant somebody inside the guild had done it on purpose.

He ran the check again. Same result. He ran it a third time with the old offline rolls — the ones Ilsa Vane kept in the archive hold, the ones nobody updated because updating them would mean admitting the past needed editing — and got the same answer with a bonus detail: the Meridian had been scrapped under seal. Quietly. No public auction, no memorial plate, none of the usual theater fleets performed when they killed one of their own. One day she'd been in the rolls; the next day she'd been slag, and everyone had agreed, in the way fleets agree, not to mention it again.

Forty years of silence, and now she had a passenger.

Dominic looked at the capsule bay. Three hundred sleeves of other people's yesterdays, and somewhere among them — or perhaps not among them at all, perhaps in the shielded locker, perhaps bolted beneath the deck plates where his own scans showed nothing but ballast — rode whatever the shipper had been willing to pay a king's ransom to move under the name of a ghost.

He should wake the quartermaster channel. He should flag it, per protocol, and let Guildmaster Corvo decide. That was the whole shape of his life: carry, don't open; deliver, don't ask. The guild had fished him out of a ventilation shaft on the *Long Patience* when he was nine years old and orphaned twice over, and the debt was simple — loyalty for livelihood — and simple debts were the only kind worth having.

But.

But the manifest entry hadn't come through the usual channels. It had come through the *old* channel, the one with Ilsa Vane's archive stamp buried in the header like a watermark, visible only if you went looking. And Ilsa Vane did not make mistakes. Ilsa Vane made *invitations*.

Dominic stared at the line for a long time while the coffee went cold beside him.

*NAME WITHHELD BY SHIPPER.*

Every smuggler has a rule they break last. For some it's opening the cargo. For some it's falling in love with the person trying to arrest them. Dominic's rule — the one he'd kept for eleven unblemished years — was *never look at line 300 or beyond*, because the guild put the interesting things past the point where honest eyes stopped scrolling. He'd always assumed the rule existed to protect him.

Standing in his cold galley with a dead ship's name glowing on his screen, he understood for the first time that the rule existed to protect *them*.

"Well," he said, to the drone, to the dark, to the ghost riding in his hold. "Let's find out who you are."

He keyed the nav plot, and there it was, the second strangeness of the day, waiting for him like a punchline: the route the manifest specified didn't pass near either fleet. It bent away from the Waking lanes, away from the Sleeping hulks, out along a vector no licensed vessel had flown in decades — toward the old orbital yard district, the scrapyard belt, the place where ships went to be forgotten.

Yard 7 sat right in the middle of it.

The *Kestrel's Regret* adjusted course with the groan of a ship being asked to do something undignified. On the plot, the scrapyard belt resolved slowly out of sensor noise: a drift of dead hulls, rendered spars, and cold stations turning end over end in the starlight, glittering like a graveyard that had tried to become jewelry and given up halfway.

Dominic poured the cold coffee down the recycler, mostly as a gesture of resolve.

Somewhere behind him, in the sealed and shielded dark of his own cargo hold, something that had been silent for forty years was, apparently, coming home.

* * *

He was six hours out from Yard 7 when the hail came.

Not customs. Not guild. The channel identifier was blank — just raw carrier wave, the kind of signal you got from something that didn't want to be found by anything except the exact person it was calling.

He hesitated, then opened the channel.

A woman's voice came through, dry as archive paper and twice as old. He knew it instantly; everyone in the guild knew it, though few had heard it outside of formal record-keeping sessions.

"Runner Coldharbour," said Ilsa Vane. "You read the manifest. Good. Most of you never do." A pause, and then, with something that might have been humor in a younger voice: "Come to Yard 7, Dominic. And bring the coffee. You'll want it. What I have to show you doesn't fit on a manifest — and it's been asleep longer than either fleet has been arguing."
