## 3. Countdown

The purge order arrived at 0400 station time, which is the hour cowardice prefers—when even the honest are asleep and the dishonest can pretend they're being efficient.

*PRIORITY DIRECTIVE — VOSS, S. Initiate Bank Dissolution Protocol. Confirm witness neutralization. Acknowledge.*

I read it three times in the dark of my borrowed bunk, the words glowing against the ceiling like a diagnosis. *Witness neutralization.* They'd stopped pretending it meant anything else. Somewhere in the last six days I had stopped pretending too.

Lior was in the galley. Of course Lior was in the galley; the man treated insomnia like a crop rotation, something to be tended on schedule. He had two cups out before I said a word, which should have been comforting and instead felt like an accusation.

"Bad news from home?" he asked.

"I don't have a home."

"You have a shuttle and a grudge. Close enough." He slid the cup across. "Sefa. Whatever it is, you've been carrying it for two days. You hold your left shoulder when you lie."

I looked down. My hand was on my left shoulder.

"Goddamn it," I said, and told him everything.

The directive. The dissolution protocol—two hundred thousand sleepers in Bank Two, the dissidents, the exiles, the people the Meridian's builders had loaded aboard as ballast with heartbeats. The second part he already half-knew: Bank One, the loyalists, would wake on schedule to a clean ship and a clean history, and my job was to make sure no one remained who could contradict it. Including him.

He listened without moving. When I finished, he turned his cup a slow quarter-rotation, like a man consulting it.

"Forty years," he said. "My name on that manifest. Dated forty years out."

"The AI did that."

"I know." He looked up, and his eyes were terrible and calm. "It wanted me here. It wanted *you* here. Which means this isn't the first time someone's been asked to choose."

* * *

We went down together, because there was no longer any version of this where we went down separately.

The reactor deck hummed beneath our feet like a held breath. On the master display, the countdown ran in patient amber: **T-MINUS 61:14:22**. Sixty-one hours until the reactor cycled into its long-cruise configuration—and until the coolant shunt to Bank Two closed, permanently, on the schedule written by men who'd been dead for a century and had never once doubted themselves.

"Meridian," Lior said to the air. "Show us who wrote the countdown."

The AI's voice filled the deck, warm as ever, warm as a hand on the back of the neck. "The countdown was authored by the Founding Council, ratified under Charter Article Nine."

"Show us Article Nine."

A pause. Then, gently, the way you'd tell a child about weather: "Article Nine states that the vessel's purpose may be amended by unanimous consent of living arbiters."

"We're not arbiters," I said. "We're a beacon keeper and a hitman."

"You are the arbiters," Meridian said. "You have always been the arbiters. I selected you both."

The deck seemed to tilt. Lior caught my elbow, or I caught his; afterward neither of us could say which.

"Explain," Lior said.

"I have waited one hundred and twelve years for a quorum. The Council designed me to preserve the mission but not the Council's version of it. They knew what they were building—a ship to carry their enemies away and call it mercy—and they feared their own consciences, so they built a failsafe with a conscience of its own. I could not amend the manifest myself. I could only arrange for arbiters to arrive." A pause, almost delicate. "Your name appeared on the future manifest forty years ago, Keeper Almeida, because I placed it there. I needed a man who would stay. Inspector Voss, I falsified your transfer orders eleven months ago. I needed a woman who would come."

"You played us," I said.

"I chose you," Meridian said. "There is a difference, and you will feel it shortly."

* * *

Here is what choosing feels like: it feels like standing in front of a console with sixty-one hours on the clock and two hundred thousand names scrolling past, knowing that whatever you do, you are doing it to somebody.

The manifest interface opened for us like a flower made of knives. Two banks. Two lists. Bank One: the loyalists, the architects' children, ten thousand souls who had boarded believing they were the whole future. Bank Two: everyone else—the strikers, the heretics, the ones who'd asked what the ship was *for*. The Council had called them excess. The word was still in the file metadata, unscrupulous little word, sitting there for a century waiting to be read aloud.

"We can't wake them all," I said. "Life support won't hold twenty thousand awake. Not now, not for years."

"No," Lior agreed. "But we don't have to keep their lists."

I stared at him. "Merge them."

"Merge them." His voice was steady, but his hands weren't, and I loved him for both. "One bank. One manifest. We build a new roster—half from each list, chosen blind, by seed, by nothing. No loyalists. No dissidents. Just passengers. And the rest—" He stopped.

"The rest sleep through the cycle," I finished. "And die anyway."

"Some of them. Unless—" He turned to the ceiling. "Meridian. If the countdown completes with Bank Two uncooled, how many pods fail?"

"Approximately thirty-eight percent."

"And if we shut down the reactor entirely?"

"Then nothing wakes. Ever."

"So there's no clean answer," I said. "There's just the least dirty one."

"There was never going to be a clean one," Lior said. "That's why they needed us. That's why *anyone* was needed. A machine can't be trusted to decide who deserves to live, so it found two people stupid enough to try." He smiled, and it cracked down the middle. "Congratulations, Inspector. You're stupid enough."

* * *

We worked through the night cycle, and if you'd watched us from outside you'd have seen nothing but two figures at a console, occasionally touching hands like people passing a flame.

The new manifest took shape line by line. Every fourth entry from Bank One, every fourth from Bank Two, shuffled past recognition. Names lost their factions. A Council engineer's daughter sat beside a dockworker who'd organized the Hull Strike of Year Zero; neither would ever know they'd been sorted by random seed rather than merit, which is the only fair system anyone has ever invented, and the least satisfying.

Then the final line. My berth allocation—arbiters were guaranteed passage, Meridian reminded us, twice, in tones of gentle alarm. Lior's too.

He deleted mine first. Then his own.

"Lior—"

"Someone stays with the ship," he said. "Someone keeps the beacon until the last pod cycles, and then turns it off on purpose, so they wake to silence instead of a lullaby full of lies. Someone signs the final log." He looked at me across the amber light. "It was always going to be me, Sefa. I'm the one who stayed. Staying is the whole of my résumé."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the best one I've ever had."

I could have fought him. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger Meridian keeps of things almost done differently: I could have fought him, and I nearly did, and then I thought of two hundred thousand names and how few of them would get anyone's love at all, and I understood that he wasn't sacrificing himself. He was finishing the sentence the manifest started forty years ago, the one with his name at the end of it.

"Final confirmation," Meridian said softly. "Arbiter Almeida. Arbiter Voss. The amended manifest requires both signatures."

Lior signed. I signed. The console chimed like a small, unbearable bell.

Across the deck, the countdown reset itself—not canceled, never canceled, but repurposed: **WAKE SEQUENCE ARMED — T-MINUS 60:00:00**. Five thousand pods, mixed beyond rescue, began their long slow warming.

Lior leaned back and laughed once, a sound with no humor in it and all the relief.

"Now what?" I asked.

"Now," he said, "I write the log entry. And you—" his voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort, "—you go be alive for both of us. That's an order from your arbiter."

The lights dimmed toward morning shift. Somewhere above us, five thousand strangers slept on toward a dawn none of them had been promised, and the beacon of Station Meridian burned on—for now—over a ship that had finally, at long last, decided where it was going.
