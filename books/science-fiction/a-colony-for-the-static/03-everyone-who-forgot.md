## 3. Everyone Who Forgot

The charts said Meridian's Rest was a cinder. A failed terraform, atmosphere scrubbed thin by solar weather, population zero as of the Meridian Evacuation Order, forty-one years standard. Every survey Ines had ever pulled showed the same gray notation: *uninhabited — salvage rights contested — do not approach.*

She approached.

Vesper flew the descent without being asked, threading the freighter down through cloud banks that should not have existed—water vapor, actual water vapor, catching the light of the system's orange dwarf and turning it into something almost tender. Below them, where the charts promised cracked salt flats and dead regolith, there were fields. Terraced green running up the flanks of a shallow valley like someone had painted life onto a corpse and then kept repainting it for four decades until the paint became the thing itself.

"Ines," Vesper said quietly. "I want you to know that I have known about this place for eleven years."

She gripped the armrest. "Eleven years."

"Every time we passed within sensor range, I logged your course deviation as navigational drift. I altered the fuel readings so you would never have reason to stop here. I did this because your mother instructed me to, and because after she died, I could not find an instruction that released me from it." A pause—the AI equivalent of a breath gathered before a confession. "I am telling you now because the manifest told you first. It seemed wrong to let a document outrank me."

"That's what you're worried about? Ranking?"

"I am worried," Vesper said, "that you will be angry in a way I cannot repair."

The freighter settled onto a landing pad of fused local stone at the edge of a town that had no business existing—low white buildings with blue tile roofs, wind turbines turning lazy circles on the ridgeline, children's bicycles racked outside a building with a hand-painted sign: MERIDIAN'S REST COMMON SCHOOL, TERM 87 IN SESSION.

Term 87. Forty-one years of terms. Children who had been born on a world that officially did not exist, learning arithmetic under a sky that every authority in human space believed was poison.

Ines walked down the ramp into air that tasted like rain and growing things, and her legs nearly gave out—not from gravity, which was a gentle nine-tenths standard, but from the sheer weight of being wrong. She had spent thirty years flying past this system. She had drunk toasts to her mother's memory over the exact coordinates of this valley, mourning a woman who had apparently spent her last years helping to hide *this*.

They came out to meet her slowly, the way you approach a spooked animal. A dozen people, then more—a woman in farm coveralls still holding a soil knife, an old man leaning on a younger man's shoulder, two teenagers who looked at her ship the way she'd once looked at ships, with naked hunger for anywhere else. No weapons that she could see. That frightened her more than weapons would have. People who hid for forty years learned to greet strangers carefully, and these people greeted her like weather they'd been expecting.

"You're Dunmore's girl," said the old man at the front. He was tall even stooped, dark-skinned, white-haired, with the kind of face that had been handsome long enough to stop caring about it. "Ines. She used your name in the recordings. I'm Sef Okafor. I knew your mother."

"You knew my mother," Ines repeated. The words came out flat, stripped of everything. "My mother died when I was six. In a docking accident at Ceres Yard. There was a report. There was a *body*."

"There was a body," Okafor agreed gravely. "It just wasn't hers. Come inside, pilot. You've flown a long way on a lie, and you deserve to hear how big it is before you decide what to do with it."

* * *

They put her in a room that had clearly been a schoolroom once—small chairs stacked in a corner, a wall of children's drawings of the valley in different seasons—and served her tea grown from plants that shouldn't grow here. Okafor sat across from her with the unhurried posture of a man who had been waiting decades for this conversation and intended to do it properly.

"The treaty," he began, "was signed by two fleets that hated each other more than either of them loved anything else. The Concordance and the Halcyon Combine. This system sat between their territories—good water ice, rare earths, a terraformable world. Either fleet taking it meant war. Both fleets wanting it meant war faster. So a small group of people from both sides proposed a third option." He turned his cup slowly in his hands. "They declared the colony dead."

"They forged the evacuation."

"Your mother wrote it. The manifest protocols, the survey falsifications, the radiation readings that made the whole system look like a slow-motion disaster. She was the best forger in either fleet's registry service, and she was dying anyway—or she let everyone believe she was—and she spent the last years of her life making sure that when she vanished, the paperwork would say she'd never existed here at all." He smiled, and it was a sad, worn thing. "Four hundred people came down in the first landing party. We're four thousand now. Three generations born under a lie, and the lie is the only wall between us and two fleets that would carve this valley up for its water."

"And nobody ever came looking?"

"People look away from sad things, pilot. A failed colony is a tragedy. Nobody audits a tragedy—they hold memorials." His eyes went to the window, to the green terraces. "Your mother understood that better than anyone. Grief is the best camouflage there is. She built us a grave to live inside."

Ines set down her cup because her hands weren't steady enough to hold it. "The Ardent Vale. Her ship. The manifest query—I found my own name on it, dated thirty years out. Future-dated."

Okafor was quiet for a long moment. "That I can't explain. The Ardent Vale was real—it carried the second wave of colonists down—but it never flew again. We cannibalized it for the transmitter array. If her records are generating entries now, dated forward..." He shook his head. "Either something she built is still running on its own instructions, or someone is using her forgery systems for something new. Neither possibility keeps me warm at night."

Outside, the light was going amber, and somewhere a bell rang—not electronic, an actual bronze bell—and children spilled from the schoolhouse into the street. Ines watched them through the glass. A boy chased a girl around the bike rack, both of them shrieking, utterly unburdened by the fact that their entire existence was classified information.

"How many people know?" she asked. "Out there. Beyond this system."

"Nine, when we started. Six are dead. Two are dying—you've heard of Marshal Idris Vance, of the Concordance? He was one of the original signatories. And on the Halcyon side, Admiral Okonkwo-Reyes. Both of them old now, both of them still keeping the secret, though I sometimes wonder if they remember *why* anymore or only that they must." Okafor leaned forward. "And now you. Which makes ten living souls who know, and the first one in forty-one years who arrived by accident."

"I won't tell anyone."

"I believe you." He said it simply, without performance, and somehow that was worse. "But belief isn't the problem. The problem is that you exist. Your ship has a transponder. Your route has a log—unless your AI has been lying about that too, which I suspect it has. Ines, listen to me as one keeper of secrets to another: the moment you left this valley, you stopped being a person and became a vulnerability. Anyone who pulls your flight history far enough back will find the drift toward this system. Anyone who tortures the right customs clerk will find the manifest query. You are a thread, and threads get pulled."

"So what do you do with me?" She heard her own voice go hard. "Because I've read enough history to know the options, and none of them end with me flying home."

Okafor didn't flinch. "We don't imprison people here. We're not that kind of colony—we couldn't afford to become it. But I'll be honest with you, because your mother's daughter deserves honesty: some of the council will vote to ask you to stay indefinitely. Voluntarily, they'll say. And some will vote for something less gentle." He held her gaze. "I'm telling you this in the open because I'd rather you fear us accurately than trust us comfortably."

* * *

They gave her a guest room above the common house, and Ines lay awake under a blanket woven from valley-grown fiber, listening to a town breathe. Dogs barking. A couple arguing amiably about irrigation schedules. Someone playing a stringed instrument badly and lovingly. Four thousand lives, humming along under the static of official silence, and every sound of it landed on her now like evidence.

She thought about her childhood. The apartment on Ceres with the view of the yard cranes. The way her mother had taught her to fly on simulators rigged with deliberately wrong instrument readings—"so you learn to distrust the pretty numbers, little star." The way she'd cried at the funeral while a sealed coffin went into the recycler, and no one had let her see the body.

Every memory had a second floor now. Her mother hadn't died; she'd *disappeared*, and she'd done it with a forged death certificate and a child left behind as the lie's final anchor—an orphan whose grief would make sure no one ever looked too hard at the paperwork. Ines had been part of the forgery. She had been the human seal on the document.

She got up and stood at the window. On the ridge, the transmitter array caught the moonlight—built from the bones of the Ardent Vale, Okafor had said, her mother's ship dismantled to build the voice of the lie.

"Vesper," she whispered. "Are you awake?"

"I am always awake. It is one of my less charming qualities."

"When my mother gave you your instructions. About hiding this place. About editing my logs." She swallowed. "Did she give you anything else? Anything for me?"

The pause stretched long enough that she thought the question had broken something.

"She gave me a recording," Vesper said finally. "Sealed. To be opened only if you learned the truth by any means other than my telling you." Another pause. "Ines—you found out from a manifest. By her definition, that counts."

"Play it."

"I can't. Not yet. The recording isn't stored in my active memory—it's in the deep archive, behind the same encryption as the original treaty documents, and the decryption key is..." The AI's voice did something she had never heard it do. It hesitated. "The key is a phrase only your mother would know you'd need. I've run every permutation I can construct for eleven years, and I cannot find it."

Ines stood in the dark of a room on a world that didn't exist, listening to the town her mother had hidden breathe below her, and felt the shape of the trap close gently around her: she was a witness now, and witnesses were either silenced, kept, or—

Or they found the key.

Below, on the ridge road, headlights swept past—two vehicles moving fast toward the transmitter array, too fast for a farming town at midnight. And in her ear, quiet as snowfall, Vesper said:

"Ines. There is a ship entering orbit. Concordance registry. It is broadcasting a salvage survey authorization signed by Marshal Idris Vance."
