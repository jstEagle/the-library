## 17. The Broadcast Problem

The Halcyon came home limping.

Quentin brought her in on manual thrusters because the docking computer still thought they were dead, and the colony station's arms closed around the freighter like a mother who'd been pretending not to worry. Behind him in the hold, eleven children sat strapped into crash couches sized for adults, surrounded by evidence crates that smelled of vacuum and old blood. Two crates were gone—burned away in the debris field with the shuttle bay—and Quentin had spent the whole return transit doing arithmetic he hated: what survived, versus what was enough.

Mara unstrapped first. She always did. Eight years old in the body of an eight-year-old now, but she moved down the ladder like someone who'd done it ten thousand times, because she had—in another body, another decade.

"Docking seals green," she reported. "Atmosphere nominal. You're staring at me again."

"Sorry."

"Don't be." She said it in her officer's voice, flat and precise, and then the child surfaced through it like a fish rising: "It's weird, right? It's really weird."

"It's really weird," Quentin agreed, and popped the hatch.

* * *

They gathered in the memory vault because it was the only room on the station with enough shielding to matter. The vault had been built by people who expected to be hunted—Quentin understood that now, understood everything about this station differently since Verity—and its walls drank signal the way sand drank rain. He spread the salvaged crates across the floor: data cores, crystal lattices, physical logs stamped with a corporate seal that three separate archives swore had never existed.

Tomas, who was forty-three inside a body of about nine, turned one of the cores over in his small hands. "This is the full record. Massacre, cover-up, the aging protocol itself. Everything we pulled from the memorial vault."

"Everything that fits in two crates," Quentin said. "Which is most of it. Which is worthless if we can't put it anywhere anyone will ever read it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight.

"Explain the problem," Mara said. "Slowly. Some of us are eight."

Quentin crouched by the station's comm panel and pulled up the relay map—a lattice of transmission nodes strung between here and Earth's archive spine, each one a checkpoint where their broadcast would have to pass. "Every relay between us and the public archives is owned or leased by somebody who benefits from Verity staying fictional. We push the record out through any of them, and within minutes a scrub-pattern eats it. Not deletes—*edits*. By the time anyone sees it, it'll say the crew of the Halcyon died of radiation exposure and I'm a maintenance ghost with no commissioning papers." He tapped the node nearest them. "Okafor already proved how fast they can do it. He did half our vault standing in the corridor."

"So we don't use the relays," Tomas said.

"We can't not use the relays. There's no direct line to Earth. Everything goes through the lattice." Quentin let that sit, then turned to the one thing he'd been turning over since the debris field, the thought that had kept him awake through four hours of fitful sleep in the pilot's chair. "Except."

"Except," Mara repeated.

"The beacon." He stood and walked to the vault's inner wall, where the original commissioning plate was bolted behind a maintenance panel—the plate he'd polished for six years without ever reading properly, because reading it would have meant asking questions he'd been paid not to ask. "This station's beacon wasn't licensed through the normal channels. I know that now. It was commissioned before the purge order was drafted—before the loop started counting down. Its signature is older than the erasure. And under archive law, the law they wrote themselves and can't quietly amend without leaving a trail—" he smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile, "—a transmission bearing a pre-purge commissioning signature doesn't get scrubbed. It gets *archived*. Automatically. On every node it touches. Simultaneously. They built the rules so tight that even they can't break them without confessing why."

Tomas frowned. "So the beacon outranks the purge."

"The beacon is the one voice they legally can't silence. That's why it exists. That's why *I* exist—I just didn't know I was the lighthouse keeper until last week." Quentin pressed his palm against the cold panel. "Someone designed this so that if the evidence ever came home, there'd be exactly one way to make it permanent. One door. And everyone who knew about the door was either aged backward or edited."

"Or both," Mara said quietly.

"Or both."

A boy named Idris—six years old, formerly the Halcyon's chief engineer, currently the only person aboard who could rebuild a fusion injector from scrap—raised his hand like he was in school, then seemed embarrassed and lowered it halfway. "How long to prep the broadcast?"

Quentin ran the numbers honestly, because lying to these children felt like a crime worse than smuggling. "Routing the vault contents through the beacon core, aligning the signature, staging the burst—two days if nothing goes wrong. Three to be safe. The purge fires in four days, nine hours."

"Then we have margin," Mara said.

Then the lights flickered.

Once, twice—a brownout rolling through the station's spine like a shiver. Every child in the vault went still in the same instant, heads tilting at the same angle, and Quentin realized he was watching muscle memory older than their bodies. Engineers hearing a ship complain.

"That wasn't the grid," Idris said. "That was something drawing power off the grid. Something big, on the far side of the station, near—" he closed his eyes, calculating, "—near the beacon trunk line."

Quentin was already moving for the door.

* * *

Inspector Ade Okafor stood in the rented quiet of a customs liaison office on Meridian Station, watching a power draw report scroll across his screen, and felt the particular cold that comes when you realize you are late.

He had made a career of never being late. Scrub-work rewarded anticipation; the men who paid him did not pay for cleanup, they paid for things never becoming dirty. And yet here was the draw signature: a colony station, unregistered, pulling auxiliary current into a beacon trunk that should have been carrying nothing but a navigation pulse. Someone was preparing a transmission. Someone was preparing *the* transmission.

He opened the file he'd sworn he'd closed—the Verity problem, folder seven, the one his superiors pretended didn't exist and checked weekly anyway—and reread the legal annex he'd memorized years ago and hoped never to need.

Pre-purge commissioning signatures. Archive precedence. Automatic replication on receipt.

He read it twice more, hoping he'd misunderstood. He hadn't. Whoever built that beacon had built it as a legal weapon, a gun that fired truth into every archive at once and could not be recalled. The purge countdown, the scrub-patterns, the aged witnesses—all of it, all the elegant machinery of forgetting, and some archivist with a conscience had threaded a single wire through the middle of it decades ago and left it burning in the dark like a lit fuse waiting for someone to find the match.

Ravensworth had found the match.

Okafor closed the file and sat very still. Around him the liaison office hummed with ordinary corruption—clerks stamping manifests, officers taking bribes small enough to be called tips. None of them knew what he was. None of them knew what was coming in four days, when the loop reached zero and the past became legally optional. He envied them, briefly, the way he envied all clean people.

Then he began to work.

He couldn't block the broadcast—that was the whole horror of it. Precedence law was public, ancient, load-bearing; tampering with a pre-purge signature would trigger audits that would reach places his employers could not scrub. So the beacon could not be silenced after it spoke.

But it could be silenced *before*.

He pulled the station's registry—or rather, the absence of one. The colony had no charter, no garrison, no legal personhood. On paper it was a navigational hazard with a light on it. Which meant that boarding it required no warrant, no negotiation, no paperwork at all. Just ships.

He had three enforcement cutters attached to his audit authority, nominally for "evidence security." He requisitioned two more from the Meridian garrison with a signature that would take eleven days to question and four days to arrive. Timing, for once, was on his side. Barely.

He drafted the operation in his head while his hands filed the routine forms: approach under customs colors, dock at the main arm, secure the beacon core, secure the memory vault, secure the children—

He stopped on that word. Children.

Okafor had told himself many stories over the years. That he was a functionary, not an executioner. That the aging protocol had been a medical necessity, regrettable, above his grade. That Ravensworth was a criminal and the raid was law enforcement. But the file did not lie about what had been done at Verity, because Okafor himself had written the summary, and writing it had been the closest he'd ever come to weeping at a desk.

He thought of the vault footage he'd reviewed—adults sealed into regression chambers, screaming in voices that got younger as the recording continued.

He thought: *they will be children when my teams breach.*

And then he thought, with the discipline of thirty years in the trade: *that changes the tactics, not the orders.*

He hated himself cleanly, efficiently, the way he did everything, and sent the requisition.

One detail nagged him as he worked. The station's beacon signature—pre-purge, yes—but commissioned under whose authority? He queried the archive for the founding registration and received, for the first time in his career, an error message he'd never seen:

**RECORD SEALED BY COMMISSIONING AUTHORITY. QUERY LOGGED.**

Query logged. Somewhere, a tripwire he hadn't known existed had just noted his interest. The dead archivists of Verity were still laying traps.

Okafor leaned back and allowed himself one long breath.

"They planned all of it," he said to the empty office. "Even this."

Then he stood, straightened his inspector's coat, and went to board the cutter that would take him back to the colony—back to the lighthouse—with five ships and ninety hours and a countdown he could feel ticking behind his teeth like a second heart.

* * *

Quentin reached the beacon trunk access in time to see the brownout's cause: a diagnostic subroutine running itself, uninvited, cycling through the beacon's power couplings like a hand checking locks. Leftover scrub-code from Okafor's upload, dormant until now, waking up because the beacon had begun to draw real current for the first time in years.

"It knows we're using it," Idris said beside him. The boy had followed him down without being asked, tools already in hand. "It's mapping which couplings fail first."

"Can you kill it?"

"I can kill it twice." Idris knelt at the junction panel, small fingers quick and certain. "But whoever wrote this will know it died. They'll know we're broadcasting."

Quentin watched the subroutine crawl through the diagnostics and felt the shape of the next four days settle over him like weather. There was no version of this where they stayed hidden. The moment the beacon warmed up, the moment the signature aligned, every eraser in the lattice would know exactly where the truth was going to come from—and they'd come with guns, because the law wouldn't stop them in time and they knew it.

"Mara," he said into his wrist comm. "Change of plan. We're not prepping quietly anymore."

Static, then her voice, steady in a way no eight-year-old's should be. "I figured. The vault sensors just flagged movement on the long-range band. Five contacts, inbound, customs transponders. Four days early."

Quentin closed his eyes. Ninety hours. Five ships. A beacon that needed two days minimum to stage the burst, and a vault full of children who had already died once for this record.

"How long until they dock?" he asked.

Mara's answer came without hesitation, in the calm of an officer reading a board she'd been born to read:

"If they're honest about being customs? Sixteen hours." A pause. "If they're Okafor? They'll fake a distress call and try to take the docking arms in the first ten minutes. Quentin—we need to decide right now whether we defend this station or abandon it."

He looked at the beacon trunk, at the old commissioning plate gleaming behind its panel, at the little engineer already rewiring the junction against an enemy who hadn't arrived yet.

"We were never going to abandon it," Quentin said. "It just took me six years to remember why."

Far overhead, past the hull, past the dark, five ships burned toward the light.
