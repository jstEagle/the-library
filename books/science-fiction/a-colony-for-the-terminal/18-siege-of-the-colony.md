## 18. Siege of the Colony

The first breach alarm sounded like a struck bell inside a coffin.

Quentin was in the beacon core gallery when it went off—a low resonant tone that shivered up through the deck plates and into his teeth. He'd spent four hours re-routing power from the hydroponics ring into the beacon's transmission buffer, hands black with conduit grease, and for four hours he had let himself believe they might actually make it. That the loop would tick down, that Mara would hold the vault, that the truth would go out clean.

The bell killed that belief in one stroke.

"Boarding action," the station's ancient announcement system said, in a voice recorded by someone dead forty years. "Docking arm three. Docking arm seven. Countermeasures offline."

*Countermeasures offline.* Okafor had been busy. The scrub-pattern he'd uploaded during his escape hadn't just corrupted half the memory vault—it had quietly unspooled the station's defensive firmware like a pickpocket emptying pockets while shaking your hand.

Quentin wiped his hands on his thighs and ran.

* * *

He found the first enforcement team before they found him—or rather, he found what they left of the corridor between docking arm three and the central spine. Flashbang residue. A scorch mark on the bulkhead where someone had fired first and asked nothing after. And wedged behind a cargo clamp, breathing hard and fast, a boy of about ten wearing the torn remains of a Halcyon Return engineering jumpsuit with *CHIEF PETTY OFFICER D. OKONKWO* stenciled across the chest in letters too big for him.

"Denny." Quentin dropped to a crouch. "Denny Okonkwo. Look at me."

The boy looked at him. His eyes were wet and furious and old—there was no other word for it. Eight years old, maybe nine, and behind them sat forty years of engine rooms and fire drills and the specific knowledge of how stations die.

"They came through the umbilical," Denny said. His voice cracked in the middle, child-high, then steadied. "I sealed the inner hatch but they've got cutters. I did what—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I did what I was trained to do. Except I wasn't trained. I just *knew.*"

"I know," Quentin said. "That's enough. Get to the vault. Mara needs every pair of hands."

"The vault's the target," Denny said. "They'll hit it hardest."

"That's why you're going there instead of away from it. Go."

The boy went, small feet hammering down the companionway, and Quentin allowed himself two seconds of horror—*children, they're sending soldiers against children*—before he crushed it flat and kept moving. Horror was a luxury. The loop had eleven hours on it. The purge had no mercy in it anywhere.

* * *

Docking arm seven had already fallen. Quentin watched from the shadow of a coolant junction as six figures in matte-black assault frames moved down the spine corridor in a textbook wedge, weapons slung, breaching charges racked. Professional. Quiet. They didn't speak to each other; they didn't need to. Enforcement teams moved like water finding a drain, and the drain was the memory vault at the heart of the station.

Behind them, in the docking arm, the Halcyon Return sat locked to her moorings, her airlock blown open, her shuttle bay already occupied by an enforcement pinnace. Quentin's escape route, gone. Not that he'd planned to run. But knowing the door was welded shut changed the flavor of everything. Now it wasn't a defense. It was a last stand.

His comm crackled. Mara's voice, thin and steady at once: "Vault team reports contact in the ring corridor. Pip says they're using thermal lances on the secondary bulkhead. Pip is—" a pause, a breath, a child choosing words carefully the way her adult self must have chosen them ten thousand times, "—Pip is holding the junction."

"Pip is eight years old," Quentin said.

"Pip is a warrant officer," Mara said. "She remembers being one. That has to count for something tonight."

It counted for everything. That was the terrible arithmetic of it—the thing Okafor's masters had never understood when they aged the Verity crew backward and scattered their memories into smuggler's contraband. They'd thought regression erased competence. But muscle memory lived deeper than narrative memory. You could take a woman's name, her face, her forty years—but you couldn't take the way her hands knew a bulkhead seal, the way her body read a corridor for kill-zones, the way she stood when she meant to hold a line. The erasers had deleted the story and left the soldier.

And now the soldiers were children, defending a vault full of their own stolen selves, against men sent to finish a massacre twenty years late.

* * *

Quentin took the maintenance spine toward the beacon core, because that was his job now and everyone aboard knew it. The broadcast could only originate from the beacon itself—its commissioning signature predated the purge, outranked it, made it legally untouchable. Everything else was theater. Hold the vault long enough for Quentin to reach the core, load the Verity record, and light the lamp.

The spine was dark. He killed his headlamp and navigated by memory, hand over hand along the guide rail, past the humming silence of the reactor housing. Twice he heard boots above him—enforcement teams moving through the upper galleries, fanning out, cutting the station into compartments. Standard siege doctrine. Isolate, suppress, extract, erase.

At the third junction his comm whispered again. Not Mara this time. A girl's voice, very young, very calm.

"Mister Quentin. This is Solenne. I'm in the vent gallery over ring corridor east. There are four of them below me and I can drop the coolant flush on their position if you say it's all right."

Quentin closed his eyes. Solenne. Seven years old. On the Halcyon's manifest, in another life: *Lt. Commander S. Adeyemi, Signals.* A woman who had once, according to the fragments they'd recovered, talked her way past an entire customs blockade with a forged weather report.

"It's all right," he said. "But stay out of the flow path yourself."

"Yes sir." A pause. Then, smaller: "I remember doing this before. I don't remember *where.* Just that I was scared then too, and I did it anyway."

"Then you're ahead of me," Quentin said, and heard her laugh once, bright and brief, before the comm went quiet and somewhere below her the coolant flushed and four trained killers learned what a seven-year-old signals officer could do with a gravity well and a valve.

* * *

He reached the beacon core gallery at 0400 station time, with nine hours and change left on the loop, and found the door already breached.

Not forced—*opened.* The access panel hung loose, its lock cycled honestly, and inside, standing at the transmission console with a data spike halfway seated, was Inspector Ade Okafor.

For a moment neither of them moved. The core hummed around them, that patient subsonic note Quentin had slept beside for six years without ever understanding it was waiting.

"You're supposed to be off-station," Quentin said.

"I was. I came back." Okafor turned. In the core's amber light his face looked older than it had in the inspection office, or perhaps just more honest. "You should know that I filed a protest. Formally. When they assigned me the scrub-pattern upload. It's in my record. For whatever a record is worth after tomorrow."

"Is that supposed to make me feel better?"

"No. It's supposed to make you understand that I'm not the disease. I'm the symptom with a badge." He lifted the data spike slightly. "This is a null-wipe. One insertion and the beacon's original signature is overwritten. Your legal shield becomes scrap. Everything else we do tonight is decoration."

"Then put it down."

"If I put it down, someone else picks it up. There are thirty enforcement personnel on this station, Ravensworth. You have a vault full of children and a smuggler who doesn't know his own name." Okafor's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the worst thing about him. "I read the manifest too. Chapter and verse. Quentin Ravensworth, junior archivist, Verity Station. You chose exile over complicity, and they punished you by taking the choice away. Do you know what they offered me? Nothing. I was *built* for this. Some of us don't get a before."

Quentin took one step forward. Okafor's hand moved—not to the spike, but to the sidearm at his hip, and stopped there, resting, a threat shaped like a courtesy.

"Here's what I know about befores," Quentin said. "I don't have mine. Those kids downstairs are missing theirs. And every single one of us decided, in the last twelve hours, that we'd rather fight blind than surrender clean. So no, Inspector. I don't care what you were built for. I care what you do in the next ninety seconds."

Okafor smiled, and it was the saddest thing Quentin had seen all night. "Ninety seconds," he repeated. "That's generous."

The lights died.

Not all of them—the emergency strips came up blood-dim a heartbeat later—but the main gallery went black, and in that blackness Quentin heard the sound he'd been dreading since the bell: the vault alarm, warbling, distant, *overridden.*

Mara's voice on the comm, ragged now, no longer steady: "They're through the ring corridor. Pip's down—not hurt, pinned—and they're at the vault door. Quentin, I need sixty seconds to reroute the door seals and I don't *have* sixty seconds, they're cutting—"

In the red emergency light, Okafor was gone. The console stood empty, the null-wipe spike still in his fist, his footsteps fading up the spine toward the vault—not because he wanted to be there, Quentin realized, but because the vault was where the fight was thickest, and a man built for this walked toward the sound of erasure the way other men walked toward home.

Quentin lunged for the console. The transmission buffer was still loaded—the Verity record, all fourteen hours of it, sitting patient in the queue like a letter stamped and unsent. All he needed was the beacon's authorization handshake. All he needed was the core online and his hand on the key.

All he needed was time, and the vault to hold, and children to keep being soldiers for nine more hours.

He slammed the handshake sequence. The core answered with a rising whine—and then choked, mid-note, as somewhere in the station's nervous system Okafor's scrub-pattern woke up and began, methodically, to eat.

"Quentin." Mara's voice again, and underneath it the shriek of a cutting torch eating through the vault's outer door. "Whatever you're going to do—do it soon. Because I'm looking at the restoration cradle right now, and I'm looking at the door, and I've done the math, and the math says we don't get both."

The whine climbed. The scrub-pattern chewed. Somewhere below, eight-year-olds stood in a doorway their adult selves had built, holding a line with borrowed courage and their own two hands.

Quentin put his palms flat on the console and started to work.
