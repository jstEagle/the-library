## 2. The Client Who Wasn't There

The charter administrator's office was the only warm room on Meridian Deep, and that told me everything I needed to know before he said a word.

Heat costs energy. Energy costs money. On a station where the corridors ran cold enough that my breath ghosted and the colonists wore their coats indoors like a second skin, one man kept his office at a temperature you could grow tomatoes in. Oduya sat behind a desk of actual wood—imported, lacquered, absurd—wearing a jacket that fit him the way a sentence fits a confession, and he smiled at me with the particular warmth of a man whose heating bill was paid by people who couldn't afford theirs.

"Vesper Marchetti." He said it like he was tasting the name. "The second one."

"The first one's dead," I said. "That's why I'm here."

"Yes." He gestured to the chair across from him. It was real upholstery. I sat in it and felt, briefly, like a traitor to every cold corridor I'd walked through. "I knew her. The original, I mean. Eleven years ago, when she first came. She sat in that exact chair."

I knew that. I knew it the way you know a scar you never earned—from her memories, which the company had decanted into me along with her case files, her griefs, and her opinions about coffee, which were strong and wrong. I remembered sitting in this chair from the inside of someone else's body. It's a strange way to have a past. Like wearing a coat with someone else's shape still pressed into it.

"You have my case file?" I asked.

"I have your *authorization*," Oduya said, and slid a slate across the desk. The Marchetti & Associates charter seal glowed on it, corporate blue, the kind of blue that gets printed on things that are meant to be believed. "Vessel Marchetti is retained under contract 4471-C to conclude the matter of—" he paused, and I watched him read his own document like it was written in a language he found distasteful, "—the incident of the eleventh of Thaw-Month, eleven years past. Conclusion to be filed within ninety days. Standard terms."

"The matter of," I said. "The matter of what? The file I was given has a name redacted out of it. Not blacked—*removed*. The text reflows around the gap like it was never written."

Oduya folded his hands. He had the hands of a man who'd done manual labor once, a long time ago, and had been apologizing for it ever since with moisturizer.

"Ms. Marchetti. May I call you Vesper?"

"You may call me Ms. Marchetti until you tell me who died."

Something flickered behind his eyes—not guilt, exactly. Guilt is a living thing. This was more like the fossil of guilt, pressed flat and kept in a drawer. "That," he said, "is precisely the difficulty."

* * *

Here is what I knew when I walked into that office, and I want to be precise about this, because precision turned out to be the only weapon anyone would let me carry.

The original Vesper Marchetti came to Meridian Deep eleven years ago on a missing-persons retainer. A woman named Sefa Anahid Oyelaran, hydroponics technician, forty-one years old, last seen entering the water reclamation bay on the eleventh of Thaw-Month and never seen exiting. Her sister on Meridian's sister-station, Callisto Relay, had hired the firm. The original Vesper spent nineteen days here. She interviewed colonists. She took samples. She filed—according to her own notes, which I carry in my head like a tenant carries a landlord's furniture—*forty-one pages of preliminary findings*, including a chemical assay of the reclamation bay showing trace biological residue inconsistent with the station's official account of Sefa Oyelaran having "relocated off-station without notice."

Then the notes stop. Mid-sentence. There's a fragment: *Oduya says the name doesn't—* and nothing after. The company that made me sealed the rest in a memory-block I don't have the key to. I'd spent the transit out here turning that broken sentence over like a stone with something underneath it.

So when Oduya said "that is precisely the difficulty," I already knew what the difficulty was. I just wanted to hear him say it. There's a difference between knowing a thing and making someone else stand in the room while it's true.

"The difficulty," he said, "is that no one by that name has ever been resident on Meridian Deep."

* * *

He said it without blinking. That was the impressive part. I've met liars who sweat, liars who over-explain, liars who look at your left ear. Oduya did none of these. He simply told me, in a warm room, that a woman had never existed, and he did it the way you'd tell me the station has four docking arms—flatly, administratively, as a fact of the built environment.

I let the silence sit. Silence is the original Vesper's trick, one of the few of her tricks that feels like mine now. People fill silence with truth the way water fills a crack.

Oduya didn't fill it. He let it sit there between us like a third person at the table.

"Her sister hired us," I said finally. "From Callisto Relay. There's a contract. There's a payment record. You don't retain investigators over people who don't exist."

"Your firm's contracts are your firm's business." He turned the slate toward me and tapped it. The charter's personnel registry bloomed on the screen—eleven years of Meridian Deep's residents, searchable, sortable, stamped with the corporate seal. "Search it yourself. I'll wait."

I searched it. I searched it twice, once by name and once by work assignment, because the original Vesper taught me—because I remember her teaching herself—that the second search is where the lies live. Sefa Anahid Oyelaran appeared nowhere. The hydroponics roster for the relevant year had a gap in it, a smooth one, a gap with no seam. Whoever had edited it hadn't deleted a line. They'd rebuilt the whole document around the absence, the way my case file reflowed around her missing name. It was almost beautiful. It was certainly expensive.

"You rebuilt the registry," I said.

"The registry is maintained to charter standards," Oduya said. "Charter standards require accuracy. The registry is accurate."

"Accurate to what?"

"To the charter."

I sat back in the upholstered chair and looked at him, and he looked back at me with the patience of a man who had done this before—eleven years ago, in this same room, with a woman who had my face and, at that moment, my exact thoughts. I knew how this had gone, because I had half of it in my head. She had sat where I was sitting and thought: *this man is not lying to me, which is worse.* And then she had thought: *the registry is accurate to the charter, and the charter is accurate to whoever holds it, and therefore the truth on this station is a property right.*

I was having her thoughts again. I want to be clear about that—it wasn't inference. It was memory, arriving in my head with the intimate certainty of something I'd lived. Somewhere in the warm room, the dead woman's mind and mine were sitting in the same chair, eleven years apart, being told the same nothing.

"Ms. Marchetti," Oduya said, softer now, "let me tell you what I told your predecessor. Meridian Deep is dying. Not metaphorically. The scrubbers are on their third generation of improvised parts. We've lost two hundred and six residents in five years—transfers out, mostly, though I won't pretend all of them were willing. The charter renews in nineteen months, and renewal depends on the station demonstrating operational stability. A murder investigation—*an unsolved* murder investigation—is not operational stability. It's a liability flag. Liability flags get stations decommissioned. Decommissioned stations get their residents dispersed to wherever the company finds cheapest." He spread his hands. "I am not telling you a woman didn't die. I am telling you that on the books of Meridian Deep, no one died, and the books are what I am required to keep, and keeping them is how two hundred and forty remaining people stay fed and breathing. Whatever your firm was paid, whatever her sister believes—"

"Her sister," I said, "waited eleven years. She re-hired the firm the week the clone program released me. She didn't want an investigator. She wanted the *same* investigator. She wanted the woman who came here before and then went silent."

Something moved through Oduya's face then, fast, like weather over water. "Yes," he said. "I imagine she did."

"And what happened to the woman who came here before?"

"She concluded her investigation," Oduya said, "as accidental death. She filed it herself. I have the record." He turned the slate again. There it was: *Marchetti, V. — Finding: accidental death (industrial), reclamation bay, 11 Thaw-Month — closed.* Signed. Dated. Nineteen days after she arrived, and forty-one pages of evidence to the contrary still warm in her head.

"That's not her signature," I said.

"It's her authorization code," Oduya said. "Which on Meridian Deep is the same thing."

* * *

I walked out of his office into the cold corridors and stood for a moment in the rust-tasting dark, letting the chill take the warmth of the room off my skin. That's when the crack opened. Not in the station—in the world.

Here's what I had believed, in the vague way you believe the floor will hold: that the law was a thing that existed, somewhere, the way water exists in the pipes even when the taps run dry. That a name could be removed from a registry but not from reality. That evidence was evidence, and a signed finding was either true or forged, and forgery was a crime, and crime was the thing I was for.

But Oduya hadn't forged anything. That was the crack. He'd told me the truth, his truth, the only truth his world recognized: *the charter says the registry is accurate, the charter is the law here, the charter is a document owned by people who have never breathed this air.* The law on Meridian Deep wasn't a wall you could appeal to. It was weather. It came from somewhere else, it fell on you, and you didn't argue with rain.

And the original Vesper had known that. Nineteen days, and she'd signed the finding. I kept circling that. She wasn't a coward—I have her courage in my head, I know its exact weight, I've carried it up stairwells. She signed anyway. Either they'd broken her, or they'd bought her, or she'd looked at two hundred and forty breathing people and done the arithmetic that Oduya had just done to me, and hated herself in a way so complete that the company who owned her mind had seen fit to seal it behind a locked block rather than let her clone inherit it.

*Oduya says the name doesn't—*

*—exist,* I finished, in the dark corridor, alone. *Oduya says the name doesn't exist. And then what? And then what did you do, Vesper? What did you do that was so bad they locked it away from me?*

My hand was shaking. Not from the cold.

I had come here with a case file, a retainer, and the comfortable assumption that my client was a grieving sister on Callisto Relay. Walking back toward my berth, I understood that I had no client at all. The sister had hired a ghost to finish a ghost's work. The victim was a non-person. The witness to her own investigation had signed off on the erasure and then died—quietly, off-station, two years later, of something the company's own medical division certified without comment. And the only person on Meridian Deep who would admit Sefa Oyelaran had ever existed was a charter administrator who insisted, in the same breath, that she hadn't.

Except.

Except that the original Vesper's notes said *forty-one pages.* And forty-one pages of preliminary findings don't get written about a woman who never lived. Somewhere on this station, eleven years ago, someone had talked to her. Someone had remembered Sefa out loud, into her recorder, knowing what it might cost. The registry had been scrubbed, the file had been redacted, the finding had been signed—but people are harder to edit than paper. People remember the way water remembers the shape of the pipe.

I turned at the junction toward the hydroponics decks. If you want to find a missing hydroponics technician, you ask the people who grow things. And if the law on this station is whatever the charter says it is, then I supposed I'd better start collecting the kind of evidence the charter can't own: the kind that lives in people, and sings, and grieves, and refuses—quietly, stubbornly, underground—to be accurate to anything at all.

The corridor lights flickered on ahead of me, one by one, as if the station itself were deciding, reluctantly, to show me the way down.
