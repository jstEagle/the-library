## 5. Signal Beneath the Floor

I had been at Meridian Deep for six days before I understood that the colony had a floor.

Not in the architectural sense. In the sense that everything above it—corridors, hydroponics bays, the half-empty residential rings where doors stood ajar on apartments nobody had emptied so much as abandoned mid-sentence—was a skin. A membrane stretched over something older. You could feel it in the way the deck plates hummed at a frequency slightly out of phase with the air scrubbers, like two people breathing in the same room who had not yet agreed to.

The murder case file stamped DORMANT for eleven years sat in my quarters like a guest who wouldn't leave. I had spent those six days doing what the original Vesper Marchetti had done before her memory-block was sealed: walking the colony, asking questions, collecting the particular silence that people produce when they are not refusing to talk but are also not talking. It's a distinct sound. I was built from her memories, and even I couldn't tell you where I'd learned to recognize it.

The seismic survey was technically out of scope. Technically, my charter authorization covered homicide investigation and nothing else. But the water reclamation bay where the victim—whose name I was not supposed to know, and which was Toma Reyes, and which everyone knew—had died sat above a geological anomaly the original had flagged in a margin note three days before her notes went dark. *Ground under Bay 7 doesn't match survey. Ask why.*

So I asked why. With a ground-penetrating radar array, two seismic thumpers, and a bottle of colony-made plum brandy I'd brought as a bribe for the one person on Meridian who still liked machines more than he feared the charter.

Ilya Volkov met me at the mouth of Bay 7 at second shift, carrying a toolkit older than either of us and the expression of a man who had agreed to something and was already renegotiating with himself.

"You're the clone," he said. Not a question. On Meridian, I'd learned, this passed for a greeting.

"I'm Vesper Marchetti."

"Sure." He looked at my equipment cases the way other men looked at women. "You know these thumpers are pre-Diaspora surplus? The damping rings are cracked. You'll get ghost returns off every bulkhead in the bay."

"Then we'll know which ghosts are bulkheads."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Meridians, I was discovering, rationed their smiles the way they rationed everything else.

We worked. The bay was decommissioned, drained, its reclamation tanks standing like the ribcage of something that had died with its lungs full. Dust hung in the work lights with the patience of dust that has nowhere else to go. Ilya thumped; I read returns. For two hours the ground under Bay 7 told us exactly what it was supposed to tell us: regolith, bedrock, a water table the colony had long since stopped drawing from.

Then, at 0300 by ship-time, the ground told us something else.

* * *

The first return came back wrong.

Not noisy—wrong. Clean, in fact, too clean, a signal with structure where structure had no business being. I stared at the readout for a long moment, the way you stare at a word you've written a hundred times that has suddenly stopped looking like a word.

"Ilya."

"Reading it." He was already at my shoulder, and his voice had changed. The wry, tired drawl was gone. What was underneath it was younger and much more afraid. "That's not geology."

"It's periodic."

"Geology's periodic." He said it the way a man says *the stove is off* to himself, twice, on his way back to bed. "Faults slip on cycles. Thermal expansion cycles with the day-night—except we don't *have* a day-night down here, so." He stopped. He was looking at the waveform, and the waveform was looking back.

It repeated every eleven seconds. A burst, a pause, a burst—three pulses, then a longer silence, then the pattern again. Not random. Not decay. Something down there was not merely making noise; it was saying the same thing, over and over, with the tireless patience of something that had been saying it for a very long time and had no particular expectation of being answered.

"Depth?" I asked.

He ran it. His hands were steady—I'll grant him that, his hands were always steady—but he ran the calculation twice anyway. "Four hundred meters. Maybe deeper. Under the bedrock shelf." He laughed, one dry syllable with no humor in it anywhere. "There's nothing under the bedrock shelf. The founding surveys say—"

"The founding surveys also say the colony has always had its current population," I said. "I've read them. They're very confident."

Ilya looked at me. In the work light his face had the particular translucency of a man deciding how much truth a stranger can carry. "My grandmother used to sing a song," he said. "Nobody knew where it came from. She said it was from before the charter. Before Meridian. She'd sing it when she thought nobody was listening, and if you asked her about it she'd say she didn't remember." He tapped the waveform with one blunt finger, right on the interval between the second and third pulse. "That's the rhythm of it. Three notes, rest, three notes. I used to fall asleep to it through the bulkhead."

"You're saying the ground is singing your grandmother's lullaby."

"I'm saying my grandmother was singing *its* lullaby." He straightened up. "And I'm saying I need a drink, and I'm saying we don't tell anyone about this until I've had it."

* * *

Ilya

There's a thing that happens when you've lived underground long enough. You stop thinking of the rock as ground. It becomes walls. Walls are architecture, and architecture is human, and human things are knowable. You go your whole life in the dark and the dark stays politely inert, and you forget—really forget, the way you forget your own heartbeat—that underneath the walls there is still the oldest thing there is, and it does not know you, and it does not care about your charter.

I was eight years old the first time the rock stopped being polite. A tremor, nothing, a 2.1 that the old seismometers caught and the new ones were calibrated to ignore. But I felt it through my bunk, and I cried, and my grandmother came and lay down next to me and sang the song. *The one with three notes and the rest.* And she said—this I remember exactly, because it was the only time she ever said it—she said: *Don't be afraid of the shaking. The shaking is them turning over.*

I asked who. She said, go to sleep.

I went to sleep. That's what you do on Meridian. Somebody hands you a thing too heavy to hold, and you put it down, and you go to sleep, and twenty years later a clone with dead eyes and a dead woman's warrant hands it back to you and asks you to hold it again.

We sat in the corner of Bay 7 with the plum brandy between us, and I told her about the song. All of it, or what I had of it. The melody I could still hum. Three words of it I'd never understood—sounds my grandmother made that weren't in any language spoken on Meridian, not Charter Standard, not the old Russian my father's side kept, not anything. I sang them for her, quietly, because even empty, Bay 7 had ears the way old rooms do.

The clone listened. That's the thing I'll say about her, the first thing, the thing I noticed before anything else. She listened like listening was a job she'd been built for. Which, I suppose, it was.

"That's it," she said, when I finished. She played back the transmission's audio profile through her handheld, filtered, cleaned. Three pulses. Rest. Three pulses. "The intervals match your melody. Not approximately. Exactly, to within measurement error."

"That's impossible."

"Everything about this is impossible. The question is which impossibility we do first." She set the handheld down between us, screen up, waveform pulsing its patient eleven-second heartbeat. "The language is dead, Ilya. I ran a comparison against every linguistic archive in my base memory. The closest match is a trade-pidgin family that went extinct during the Diaspora Wars. Two hundred years ago. There are no native speakers. There are no recordings. There are grammar fragments in three university archives, all of them partial, all of them secondhand."

"So who's down there talking it?"

"Nobody." She said it flat, and it was worse than any theory. "Or not anybody anymore. That signal has structure. Syntax. It's a sentence, or it's a name, or it's a warning—but it was *composed*. By a mind. And it's repeating on a power budget that should have died a century and a half ago." She looked at me. "Something under this colony is still trying to tell us something, and it has been for longer than the colony has existed, and the founding surveys say there's nothing down there to do the telling."

I turned the brandy bottle in my hands. It was a good bottle. Meridian makes terrible brandy and I'm loyal to it anyway.

"My grandmother came up in the first generation," I said. "Before the charter was finalized. There were settlements on this rock before Meridian Deep—everybody knows that the way you know a thing you've decided not to think about. Scavenger camps, prospectors, people who came out on the long-haul contracts and stayed when the contracts collapsed. When the charter company arrived, there were already people here." I heard myself say it and realized I'd never said it before, not out loud, not in that order. "The histories say the colony was founded on an uninhabited rock."

"The histories say a lot of things."

"Yeah." I looked at the waveform. Three notes, rest, three notes. *Them turning over.* "The song has a verse my grandmother never sang in front of my father. My mother told me. One verse, in the dead language, and then the rest in Standard, and the Standard part goes: *hide, little one, hide, and count the days, and when they stop counting you, count for them.*" I drank. "I always thought it was just a song. Songs are just songs. That's what you tell yourself so you can sleep."

Vesper Marchetti didn't say anything. She just reached over and turned the gain on the receiver up, one notch, and the ground's voice came through the little speaker a little clearer, and we both sat there in the drained dark of Bay 7 like two people at a bedside, listening to something breathe.

* * *

Vesper

We told no one, and by the next shift, everyone knew.

This is not a paradox unique to Meridian, but Meridian has refined it. Ilya and I had spoken in a drained bay at three in the morning with the door sealed, and by breakfast the hydroponics priestess was leaving tomatoes outside my quarters—good ones, the varietal they grow for funerals—and the retired drunks in Ring C were toasting me with the particular solemnity men reserve for a colleague who has just been promoted to a kind of trouble they recognize.

"You understand what's happening," Oduya said, finding me in the archive corridor that afternoon. The charter administrator had a talent for appearing in doorways without seeming to have traveled through them. "You've found something."

"Have I?"

"Don't." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse. "Administrator to investigator, off the record, as a courtesy your predecessor also received: whatever the ground-penetrating radar showed you, it showed you geology. Meridian sits on two hundred meters of regolith over bedrock, and bedrock does not transmit. If your equipment says otherwise, your equipment is miscalibrated, and I'll authorize a replacement unit from stores. A calibrated one."

"Will the calibrated one also tell me the victim never existed?"

The silence between us lasted four seconds. I counted. It's a habit the original had, and I have never been able to decide whether it's hers or mine.

"You should conclude your file," Oduya said at last, gently, the way you close a lid. "Accidental death in a decommissioned facility. It's what happened. It's what the record will say. And then you can go home, Vesper, and be the person you were made to be." He left before I could answer, which was also a courtesy, of a kind.

I did not conclude my file. Instead I went back to Bay 7, where Ilya had spent the day doing something he refused at first to explain.

He'd built an antenna.

Not a dish—there was no room and no budget. He'd stripped the insulation off a length of conduit, grounded it through the bay's dead plumbing, and wired it into the seismic array's receiver, turning the entire sensor network into an ear pressed flat against the floor of the world. It was ugly and brilliant and completely against about nine regulations, and when I asked him where he'd learned to do it, he said, "My grandmother," and did not elaborate, and I understood that this was the elaboration.

We listened for six hours.

The signal did not change. That was the terrible thing about it—the fidelity of it. Eleven seconds, three pulses, rest. Over and over, uncorrupted, unwavering, with the patience of something that had made its statement long ago and now existed only to repeat it. I ran the decay analysis four times. Whatever was transmitting down there had a power source that should not exist and a message it had no intention of varying.

"It's not trying to communicate," I said finally. "Communication wants a response. It would adapt, vary, escalate. This is—" I searched for the word, and the original's memories offered it up from some seminar she'd attended, some paper she'd skimmed a lifetime ago. "This is testimony. A deposition. It's not talking to us. It's talking *for the record*."

Ilya was quiet for a long time. Then he said: "Play it against the song."

I did. His grandmother's melody, hummed into the handheld's mic two nights before, laid over the transmission's pulse pattern. They aligned. Note for note, rest for rest, a lullaby that had been passed mouth to mouth through four generations of a family that thought it was only a song, matching, exactly, a signal rising from four hundred meters below the bedrock of a colony whose founding records said no one had ever lived there.

Ilya put his head in his hands. I watched him grieve something he couldn't name yet, and I felt the original's memories stir in me like sediment—not her grief, I told myself, not mine either, just data, just inheritance—and I did what I had been made to do. I looked at the evidence.

"Three pulses," I said. "Rest. Three pulses. If it's syntax, the rest is a word. A repeated word. In testimony, in deposition, in every legal and liturgical structure the Diaspora cultures shared—" I stopped, because the conclusion had arrived before my courage did.

Ilya lifted his head. "Say it."

"It's a name. Or it's *we*. Or it's *remember*." I turned the handheld toward him, the waveform pulsing its patient, endless pulse. "But whatever the word is, it's the same word every time, and it's been saying it since before either of us was born, and I don't think it's a lullaby, Ilya. I think it's a count. *When they stop counting you, count for them.*"

The signal pulsed. The ground held its note. And four hundred meters beneath our feet, something that the charter said had never lived went on, patiently, tirelessly, saying the only word it had left.

Ilya stood up. He looked different—taller, or older, or simply no longer willing to be either. "Bay 7 sits above the bedrock shelf," he said. "And the bedrock shelf has a seam. My grandmother told me that too. She said there's a way down, and that the way down was closed from the *other* side."

He picked up his toolkit.

"Get your warrant," he said. "We're going to go hear the rest of the sentence."
