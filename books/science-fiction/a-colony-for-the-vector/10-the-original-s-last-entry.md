## 10. The Original's Last Entry

The memory-block had been sitting in my kit for eleven days, and I had been pretending it wasn't there.

It's a small thing, a memory-block. A matte black cylinder the length of a thumb, corporate-issue, with the Marchetti clone-series seal pressed into the casing like a wax stamp on a letter nobody was supposed to open. The transfer techs on the station had handed it to me along with my teeth and my fingerprints and the original's muscle memory for a nine-millimeter sidearm. *Inheritance package, complete,* they'd said, and I'd signed for myself the way you'd sign for a parcel.

Everything in it had already been decanted into my head before I woke. That's the standard process. The grief, the case files, the taste of the coffee she preferred, the way she flinched at the sound of welding torches—all of it, pre-installed. The block itself was supposed to be a backup. A redundancy. The company's little insurance policy against a clone arriving at her own life with gaps.

Except this block was sealed. Flagged with a lock code I didn't have, wrapped in a legal wrapper that read, in the company's flat way: *Deferred content. Release contingent on case resolution.*

Deferred content. I'd turned that phrase over for eleven days the way you'd turn over a stone to see what's underneath, and every time I'd put it back down, because underneath was either nothing or everything, and I wasn't sure which would be worse.

But tonight the sublevel was welded shut behind me, and a man named Ansel Kovač was on a transport to somewhere with worse air than Meridian, deported on charges so manufactured they still had tool marks on them, and the two hundred dead under my feet had been declared a *structural hazard* by a man who signed his memos with a steady hand. I had run out of reasons to leave stones unturned.

I sat in my quarters in the dark, because Meridian's power grid had been quietly throttling my sector since the enforcers arrived, and I cracked the seal.

The lock code, when the block prompted me, turned out to be my own birthdate. Her death date. The company has a sense of humor like a slot floor: endless, mechanical, and cruel.

* * *

The first thing in the block was her voice, and I have to tell you what that's like, hearing your own voice say things you never said. It's not uncanny. Uncanny is a stranger's face that's almost right. This is worse. This is a mirror that got there first.

*Case addendum,* she said. *Vesper Marchetti, primary. If you're listening to this, you're me, which means the company decided the case was worth a second try, which means they think they can afford to have it solved, which means—* a pause, and I heard her breathe, and I knew that breath, I'd been hearing it in my own chest for weeks— *which means they've already decided what the answer is.*

I sat very still.

*I found the sublevel,* she said. *Eleven years ago, four months into the investigation. Seismic survey, same as you'll do it, same equipment, same anomaly. I breached it on a Tuesday. I want you to know I remember it was a Tuesday because I remember thinking, absurdly, that the dead deserved better than a Tuesday.*

The block played her descent the way memory plays it—fragmentary, sensory, no clean edges. The smell of the sealed dark, that mineral coldness of air that hasn't moved in two hundred years. Her lamp finding the first doorway. The homes. The table set with something that had rotted to lace. A child's shoe.

And then the grave.

I had stood there myself, nine days ago. I knew what she was seeing. But hearing it in her voice, in my voice, made it happen twice, and I learned something about grief in that moment: it doesn't care about the order things arrive in. The two hundred were dead before I was decanted, and they were dead before she stood over them, and hearing her say *there are so many* put them in the ground all over again.

*Two hundred and six,* she said. *I counted. I counted because counting was the only thing I could do that felt like a job. The founding records say Meridian was built on bedrock. The founding records are a lie with a charter number.*

Then her voice changed. I've heard enough testimony—recorded depositions, the original's interview tapes, the flat recitations of frightened people—to know the sound of a person reading from something. She was reading from a document.

*The company's terms were as follows. One: the sublevel is sealed and the survey data destroyed. Two: the case of the deceased, name redacted in my own file, can you believe that, redacted in my own file, is closed as accidental death by equipment failure. Three: I accept a position with Meridian administration, with the salary and protections thereof. Four: I speak to no one, ever, about what is under the floor.*

*A pause here to note the obvious: I was offered a bribe. I want to be precise about that, because I know how I'll want to dress it up later. It was a bribe, and I took it.*

I heard myself swallow. Her swallow. Ours.

*Here's what I told myself at the time,* she went on, and her voice had gone quiet in the way voices go quiet when they're stepping carefully through their own wreckage. *I told myself the colony was dying. Two hundred people left, air scrubbers failing, and if the truth came out, the charter would pull out entirely and everyone would suffocate with their history. Oduya said that. Oduya said it with tears in his eyes, and I believe the tears were real, and I believe that's the most dangerous thing about him.*

*I told myself that burying them again was a kind of mercy. I told myself that a closed case kept the colony breathing. And all of that might even be true. But here is what I did not tell myself, and what I'm telling you, whoever you are, whoever I am:*

*The man I was investigating didn't die of equipment failure. He died because he talked.*

The block went to visual then, and I let it play, and I watched her—watched myself—sit in this same room, in this same chair, eleven years younger and already tired in a way I recognized from the inside.

*His name was Davor Reiner. He ran the water reclamation bay, and he was the one who found the first bones, years before I arrived, and he couldn't hold it. He told the priestess. He told the engineer's family. He was going to tell the colony, at the winter assembly, all of it. The company found out. And three weeks later Davor went into a decommissioned bay alone, at night, to work on a pump that hadn't run in a decade, and the official record says his suit failed.*

*His suit didn't fail. I have the maintenance logs. His suit passed inspection nine hours before he died.*

*I know who did it. I have the name. It's in this block, in the sealed appendix, because I couldn't put it in the case file, because the case file belongs to the company, and the moment I wrote it there it would have stopped being evidence and started being a suicide note.*

I stopped the playback.

I want to be honest about what I did next, because this is the part of the story where I'm supposed to say I sat with it, or prayed, or felt the full weight of my inheritance settle on me like snow. What I actually did was get up and vomit into the recycler, and then sit back down with my mouth tasting of acid and my hands shaking, and press play, because I am built from her and she was thorough, and thorough people finish documents.

* * *

*The name is in the appendix,* she said again, when I found my way back. *You'll have it by now. I'm not going to say it aloud, even here, even in a block that's supposed to be sealed, because I have learned in the last four months that nothing is sealed. Everything is a frequency. Everything can be intercepted. I have started to feel like a house with the windows open.*

*But I want to tell you the part I can't put in any file, the part that isn't evidence. I want to tell you why I said yes.*

*I said yes because I was afraid. Not of dying—although the man who killed Davor came to my quarters, the week before I signed, and sat down, and poured two glasses of the engineer's terrible liquor, and talked to me for an hour about his daughter, and how old she was, and what school she'd be going to offworld if things went well for him. He never threatened me. He told me about his daughter's school for an hour, and then he left, and I understood that I had been threatened in the only language that matters down here, which is the language of other people's children.*

*I said yes because the colony is small and the company is large and I did the arithmetic. Two hundred and six dead against two hundred living. I did that arithmetic. I want you to know that I did it, and that I got the answer I wanted, and that arithmetic is how cowards do math.*

*And then I stayed. They gave me a position and a salary and a set of sealed doors to live behind, and I stayed for six years, and I told myself I was protecting them, and the truth is I was protecting myself from being the person who ended Meridian. I let them make me a custodian. Oduya and me. Two people keeping a grave swept and a colony ignorant, and calling it care.*

*If you're me—if you're the one they grew from what was left of me—then you have my memories, and you have my loyalties, and you have my shame, and I'm sorry. I'm sorry because they didn't put anything else in you. That's the design flaw of what we are: they give us the past and call it a self. You'll feel my grief and think it's yours. You'll feel my fear and think it's instinct. You'll stand in front of the sealed doors and feel my cowardice in your chest and think, this is who I am.*

*It isn't. It's who I was. There's a difference, and it's the only difference that matters, and I found it too late to use.*

*The case is yours now. I don't know what I'd want you to do. That's the honest ending. Everyone wants to leave their clone a directive, a clean instruction, and I can't, because every instruction I write down tonight is written by the woman who took the bribe, and I don't trust her judgment, and she's the only mother you've got.*

*Except this. I'll allow myself this one thing, and then I'll seal the block and go back to being a custodian, and you can weigh it however you like:*

*Davor Reiner had a daughter. She was deported when he died—the company called it relocation, grief support, they're very good at words. If anyone ever asks you what the case was about, don't tell them it was about a murder. Tell them it was about a girl who lost her father twice: once to the company, and once to me, when I closed the file and let the lie stand.*

*End of addendum. Marchetti, primary, signing off. God, I hate how that sounds. Signing off. Like I'm a broadcast.*

*Like I'm a frequency.*

The block went dark.

* * *

I sat in the dark for a long time after that. Long enough for the throttled power grid to cycle the corridor lights twice, long enough for my tea to go cold in the way Meridian tea goes cold, which is fast and without dignity.

Here is what I had, laid out in the dark like tools on a sheet: I had the name in the appendix—Davor's killer, a man now three promotions into Meridian security, a man whose daughter really did go to that school offworld, a man who had poured two drinks and talked about her for an hour. I had the maintenance logs. I had the original's confession, timestamped, sealed, and legally worthless, because the company's own wrapper made it *deferred content* and the company's own arbitrator would make it inadmissible before I finished saying the word.

I had, in other words, everything the law required and nothing the law would touch.

And I had the other thing. The thing she couldn't give me a directive about. The thing sitting in my chest wearing her fear like a borrowed coat.

I tried to find the seam. That's the honest description of what I was doing, sitting there: looking for the seam between her and me. Her grief, my grief. Her cowardice, mine. She'd said *you'll feel my fear and think it's instinct,* and she was right, I could feel it right then, a low animal certainty that the smart move, the survivable move, the move that kept the lights on for two hundred people, was to reseal the block, log the evening as records review, and go to bed a custodian.

The arithmetic was right there, waiting for me. Two hundred and six dead against two hundred living. Coward's math. She'd done it and it had cost her six years and a self, and now it was being offered to me at a discount, secondhand, pre-owned, one careful owner.

And underneath it, quieter, the other thing. Not her voice. Mine, or whatever mine was going to turn out to be. The thing that had stood in the mass grave nine days ago and thought: *the dead deserved better than a Tuesday.* She'd thought that too. It was in the block. We'd had the same thought in the same dark, eleven years apart, and one of us had buried it and one of us was still holding it, and the difference between us was not in the memories, because the memories were identical.

The difference was going to be in what I did next.

I got up. I sealed the block—not because I was done with it, but because I'd learned from her that everything is a frequency, and I had no intention of letting this one be intercepted before it reached the only audience that mattered. I put it in my kit, under the false bottom I'd cut two days ago for reasons I hadn't let myself examine.

Then I opened the door and went looking for Sefa, the priestess, because there was a bargain she'd been circling for a week, and I was suddenly, urgently ready to hear its terms.

Because here's what the original never understood, and what I understood standing in my doorway with her shame in my kit and her courage, the small unspent coin of it, in my chest: she thought the choice was between the dead and the living. Two hundred and six against two hundred. But the enforcers hadn't come to seal a grave.

They'd come to demolish one. And the colony's air ran straight through it.

The ground under Meridian Deep wasn't just speaking.

It was counting down.
