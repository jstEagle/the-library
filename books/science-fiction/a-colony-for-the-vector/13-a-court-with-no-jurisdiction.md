## 13. A Court With No Jurisdiction

There is a particular kind of document that exists only to be ignored, and I had spent three days reading it.

The Meridian Deep Charter ran to four hundred and eleven pages of corporate scripture, and buried in its appendices—between the clauses governing water reclamation liability and the schedule for charter renewal in 2191—was Article 88: *Any resident or resident's designated representative may petition for binding arbitration on matters of record integrity, historical preservation, and wrongful death.*

Wrongful death. The words sat there like a loaded weapon someone had left in a drawer and forgotten about. Or thought no one would ever find. Or hoped, if found, would be too rusted to fire.

I filed the petition at 0400 station time, from a terminal in the hydroponics bay, because every public terminal in the residential ring had been "temporarily decommissioned for maintenance" since the enforcers arrived. Oduya's people weren't even subtle about it anymore. They'd stopped pretending the colony was a place where things happened and started treating it as a place where things were prevented.

The priestess—Sefa, though everyone just called her Mother Root—watched me type with her arms folded inside her sleeves, surrounded by her towers of lettuce and her racks of algae and the one thing she kept behind a false panel that was worth more than all of it: the archive. Generations of colonists who understood that paper burns but memory ferments.

"You know what will happen," she said.

"I know what should happen."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." I hit submit. The terminal thought about it for a long moment, longer than a submission should take, and I had the distinct impression my petition was being read by something other than a filing system. Then: *PETITION RECEIVED. ARBITRATION SCHEDULED: 6 DAYS.* Six days. On Earth, arbitration under a standard corporate charter took eighteen months minimum. Here they'd found room in the calendar almost immediately, which meant either the system worked with miraculous efficiency, or someone wanted this hearing over with fast, controlled, and buried.

I chose to believe both.

* * *

The hearing chamber was on Admin Level Two, and I want you to understand what a strange sentence that is. Admin Level Two. Meridian Deep had been built with administrative capacity for a population of eight thousand. It now held nine hundred and change, which meant the entire second level was a mausoleum of empty offices, dead conference rooms, and carpet that hadn't been vacuumed since before most of the current residents were born. The hearing chamber itself was a scaled-down courtroom, because somewhere in the Diaspora Wars era, corporations had decided that legitimacy required set dressing: a bench, a gallery of six seats, a seal on the wall—the charter's sigil, a stylized meridian line bisecting a circle, polished to a shine that nothing else in the colony received.

Oduya was already there when I walked in, sitting at the respondent's table with two legal aides I'd never seen before. They'd come down on the enforcement vessel, presumably—charter counsel, actual trained lawyers, flown eleven years of dark space to sit in a room with nine hundred dying colonists and defend the proposition that two hundred people had never existed.

"Vesper." Oduya nodded at me. He looked tired. He always looked tired lately, but there was a new quality to it—not the fatigue of a man managing decline, but the fatigue of a man carrying something heavy up a hill he knew ended in a cliff. "You're representing yourself?"

"I'm the only lawyer on the colony."

"There were two."

"The other one was deported." I let that sit. One of his aides wrote something down. Everything got written down; I'd learned that much. The enforcers' documentation protocol was flawless. They recorded everything, which sounds like transparency until you realize that whoever controls the recording controls the reality, and a video of a fair hearing is not a fair hearing any more than a photograph of food is a meal.

The arbitrator entered at 0900 exactly. Her name was Magistrate Corven-Ashe, and she was not what I expected, which was a company man in a good suit radiating bored contempt. She was instead a small, precise woman with grey-shot hair and reading glasses on a chain, and she greeted us by name—all of our names, including Oduya's aides—and thanked us for our punctuality, and adjusted the room's temperature down half a degree because she found hearings went better when people were slightly uncomfortable.

She was, I recognized within ninety seconds, very good at this. That was the terrifying part.

"Petitioner," she said, once preliminaries were done. "Your petition alleges wrongful death, suppression of records, and violation of Article 31, historical preservation covenants. Your remedy sought is a full forensic investigation of Sublevel Seven and public release of findings."

"That's correct."

"And your evidence?"

I laid it out. I'd prepared for three days and I made it count: the original Vesper's case file, stamped dormant; her field notes contradicting the official record of the victim's existence; the seismic data showing the sublevel's structure; photographs of the grave—I had them printed, physical copies, handed to the bailiff, entered as Exhibit C through F. I watched Corven-Ashe read each page. She read the way surgeons cut: completely, without flinching, without giving anything away.

When I finished, she said, "Respondent's position?"

One of the aides stood—a young man with the smooth, rehearsed calm of someone who has practiced being reasonable. "Respondent acknowledges the petitioner's diligence. Respondent notes, however, that the alleged remains have not been authenticated by any qualified forensic authority. The petitioner is a licensed investigator, not a coroner. Further, the seismic 'evidence' consists of readings taken with equipment of unknown calibration by parties with documented grievances against the charter administration. And finally—" here he paused, delicately, like a man laying a flower on a grave "—the petitioner's standing derives entirely from the original investigator's appointment, which lapsed upon her death eleven years ago. The petitioner is, legally speaking, a continuation of a defunct office."

"A clone," Corven-Ashe said mildly, "is not the same person."

"No, Magistrate."

"So the petitioner has no standing."

"The petitioner may petition for standing, Magistrate, through the standard process."

"Which takes how long?"

"Eighteen months, typically. Expedited review could reduce—"

"Denied," I said.

Corven-Ashe looked at me over her glasses. "Petitioner will speak when called upon."

"With respect, Magistrate, I am called upon. You asked for my evidence and I gave it, and the response to my evidence is that I don't exist enough to present it. So let me make this simple." I stood. Somewhere behind me, in the six-seat gallery, I heard Ilya shift—he'd insisted on coming, and Oduya had allowed it, which itself told me the outcome was preordained; you don't let witnesses into a real trial. "Two hundred bodies are under this floor. I have stood among them. I have held a child's drawing that was buried with her. Whatever I am—a clone, a continuation, a ghost wearing a dead woman's credentials—the bodies are not ghosts. They are load-bearing facts. And every procedural obstacle between them and the light was built by the same institution that built the floor over them. You are the charter's employee. This court is the charter's furniture. I am asking you to rule against your own employer, and we both know you won't, so let's skip to the part where you explain why in language dignified enough to be quoted later."

Silence. Oduya closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a door he warned you about turns out to open onto a drop.

Corven-Ashe removed her glasses. Cleaned them. Put them back on. All of it unhurried, all of it performance—the performance of a woman considering, rather than a woman deciding.

"Petitioner," she said, "your passion does you credit. Let me tell you what I see. I see a colony with failing life support and a dwindling population. I see a sublevel of undocumented construction, structurally unverified, containing human remains whose provenance is unknown—they may be victims of crime, or casualties of the founding accidents that the historical record does describe, or remains transported from elsewhere during the Diaspora displacements. I see a community that has survived eleven years of quiet decay precisely because it has maintained institutional continuity. And I see a petitioner asking me to rupture that continuity on the strength of unauthenticated evidence gathered by interested parties." She folded her hands. "Continuity, Petitioner, is not a conspiracy. It is the skeleton that keeps a body upright. You propose to break it because you find the posture distasteful."

"The posture is a mass grave."

"The posture," she said, "is nine hundred living people whose air, water, and heat depend on systems that run through the structure you wish to excavate. Have you considered, Petitioner, what happens to those nine hundred if your excavation destabilizes the sublevel? Because I have. It's in my briefing materials. Would you like to know the projected casualty figures?"

"They're not casualties if you do it deliberately."

"Everything is a casualty if you do it deliberately. That is what deliberation means." She turned a page. "Ruling: the petition is dismissed for lack of standing. In the alternative, even granting standing arguendo, the petition is denied on the merits: the risk to ongoing life-support operations outweighs the historical interest in excavation. A preservation order is issued for all materials currently in the petitioner's possession, to be surrendered to charter custody within seventy-two hours." She looked up, and for one unguarded moment—half a second, maybe less—something moved behind her eyes. Not sympathy exactly. Recognition. The look of a person who has done a calculation many times and knows exactly what it costs. "The court thanks all parties. We're adjourned."

It took four minutes. I had given three days of preparation and two hundred dead, and it took four minutes, and the gavel didn't even sound final—it sounded perfunctory, a period at the end of a sentence written before the hearing began.

* * *

In the corridor afterward, Oduya caught up with me. His aides had peeled away; it was just the two of us under the dead lights of Admin Level Two, walking past offices with names still stenciled on the doors—DEPUTY DIRECTOR, RESOURCE ALLOCATION, HUMAN CAPITAL—titles for jobs no one had held in a decade.

"You knew," I said.

"I suspected."

"Don't insult us both. Corven-Ashe flew in on the enforcement vessel. Her briefing materials contained casualty projections for an excavation that officially doesn't exist. Someone briefed her on Sublevel Seven, Oduya. Which means someone admitted, in writing, to the charter's own magistrate, that Sublevel Seven exists. Do you understand what you just witnessed? The lie requires maintenance. The lie has staff."

He stopped walking. For a while he just stood there, a big man gone soft at the edges, in a corridor built for eight thousand people who would never come.

"When I was young," he said, "there was a woman here—Anahera. She ran the school. She used to tell the children that the law was like the air scrubbers: you don't notice it when it works, and when it fails, everything fails at once, and by then it's too late to fix it, you can only survive it." He rubbed his face. "She was wrong, though. About one part. The scrubbers fail on their own. The law gets failed. Deliberately. By people. And the people always have reasons, and the reasons are always true, and none of it matters, because the air is bad either way."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're going to do something stupid, and I want you to know that I understand why." He started walking again. "Seventy-two hours, Vesper. Surrender the materials. Please. I'm asking you as the man who has kept nine hundred people breathing for eleven years: give me the documents, let me bury them properly this time, and I will spend whatever influence I have left getting the colony aid shipments, real ones, off the books. That's the trade. That's the best trade anyone will ever offer you."

"And the two hundred?"

"What two hundred?" he said, and there it was—the whole machinery of the world in five syllables. Not cruelty. Necessity. The same word, worn smooth, that every hand that ever covered a grave had held.

* * *

That night I sat in my quarters with the preservation order on the table and the archive's contents spread around it—the ledger, the drawings, the translation work Sefa and I had done, the original's confession decoded from her locked memory-block. Seventy-two hours to surrender it all to charter custody, where it would vanish into the same void that had swallowed the victim's records, the founding archives, the original Vesper's career, and possibly her conscience.

I thought about Corven-Ashe cleaning her glasses. I thought about the half-second of recognition. She knew. Not what was in the sublevel, maybe—but she knew what she was. She knew the difference between a court and a courtroom, and she put on the robe anyway, and adjusted the temperature, and performed consideration for four minutes because the performance was the product. Justice existed on paper. It existed in Article 88, in the polished seal on the wall, in the gavel and the gallery and the bailiff taking my exhibits with gloved hands. It existed everywhere except anywhere it could touch anything.

And that was when I finally understood the shape of my situation, with the cold clarity the priestess had probably been waiting for me to reach: the law wasn't broken. That was the trap of thinking about it as broken. Broken implies repairable, implies some original working state you could restore. The law was functioning perfectly. It was doing exactly what it had been designed to do, which was to convert wrongs into paperwork and paperwork into silence. Article 88 wasn't a door left unlocked. It was a pressure valve—built to let just enough grievance escape, in harmless gas form, so the boiler never exploded. I had filed my petition, attended my hearing, received my dismissal, and now the system could point to the whole sequence and say: *see? Process was followed. Remedies were available. The petitioner simply lost.*

They had even let me speak. That was the elegant part. My outburst would go in the transcript. *Petitioner became emotional and accused the court of bias.* It would make the dismissal look more legitimate, not less. Every honest thing I said in that room had been converted, instantly, into material reinforcing the verdict.

I laughed. Actually laughed, alone in my quarters at 0200, the sound bouncing off the walls of a room sized for a family. The original Vesper would have appreciated it. She'd spent eleven years learning the same lesson and had chosen surrender; I'd needed one hearing. Maybe I was more efficient than her. Maybe efficiency was the only thing they'd actually improved in the cloning process.

Ilya knocked at 0300. He had a way of knowing when things had gone wrong—some engineer's sense for pressure differentials in human systems.

"Well?" he said.

"Dismissed. No standing. Preservation order on the evidence. Seventy-two hours to hand it over."

He absorbed that, leaning in the doorway, and I watched him do the same math I'd been doing, and arrive at the same answer, and I saw him decide not to say it first. That was Ilya. He'd wait. His family had waited two hundred years; he could wait out my last hour of legal faith.

But I was already past it. I picked up the preservation order, read it once more—all its careful language, its citations, its seal—and set it down.

"Ilya," I said, "how much of the comm array's transmission schedule do you actually control?"

His eyebrows went up. Then, slowly, he smiled—not triumph, nothing so clean as that. More like a man who has carried something heavy for generations finally being offered a second pair of hands.

"Enough," he said. "Why? What frequency did you have in mind?"

I looked at the ledger on my table—at the extinct language's characters marching across pages written by hands two centuries dead, testimony that had already survived one erasure and was not going to survive another.

"Theirs," I said.
