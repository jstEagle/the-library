## 15. Countdown in the Dark

My grandmother used to say that Meridian Deep had two kinds of quiet. The good kind, when the fans cycled down at night and you could almost hear the rock settling around you like an old man getting comfortable. And the bad kind, when the fans stopped because something upstream had broken, and everyone in the hab-ring pretended to be asleep so they wouldn't have to be the first one scared.

I'd been living inside the bad kind of quiet for three days without knowing it.

It started with a maintenance schedule that shouldn't have existed.

I want to be clear about my qualifications here, because Vesper always says—said?—that I undersell myself. I'm not an engineer. My grandmother's son, my father, was an engineer, and he died with a torque wrench in his hand and coolant in his lungs when I was nine, and after that I learned engineering the way orphans learn cooking: by necessity, by theft, and by standing close enough to people who knew things that some of it rubbed off. For twenty years I've kept the secondary pump loop on Deck Nine alive through spite and cannibalized parts. The company calls me "custodial support." I call myself the last man who knows where the bodies are buried, which until recently I meant as a joke about the plumbing schematics.

So when a new work order came through my queue—flagged PRIORITY: CHARTER DIRECTIVE, clearance level I've never even seen before—I read it. That alone makes me unusual. Most colonists stopped reading work orders years ago. You read them, you get sad, you stop reading them. Efficient system.

The order authorized demolition preparation for Sublevel Seven.

Not sealing. Not structural remediation. Demolition. Controlled collapse, section by section, beginning with the eastern galleries—the ones where we'd found the homes, the grave, the two hundred people the charter said never lived. And there, buried in the technical appendices like a confession hidden in a hymnal, a line item that made me sit down on a crate of desiccant and laugh, because if I didn't laugh I was going to scream:

*Note: primary atmospheric processing conduit traverses Sublevel Seven, Gallery E. Conduit relocation not feasible within operational timeline. Conduit loss acceptable.*

Acceptable.

The air I breathe runs through the floor where they buried the dead, and the company has decided the air is expendable.

* * *

I did what any sensible man would do. I got drunk.

No—that's not fair to either of us. I got drunk *and then* I went to find Vesper Marchetti, which in this colony counts as responsible behavior. I found them in the archive room the priestess had opened for them, sitting under a work lamp with the ledger from the grave spread out like a patient on a table, and I put the work order down in front of them and watched their face do the thing their face does—which is nothing, absolutely nothing, the original's face trained out of every honest expression, and the clone inheriting the training along with the grief.

Then they read the appendix line, and the nothing cracked.

"Conduit relocation not feasible," Vesper read aloud, flat as a coroner. "They're going to cut the colony's air supply to destroy a graveyard."

"They're going to cut the colony's air supply," I corrected, "because destroying the graveyard requires it. We're not the target, Vesper. We're the shrapnel."

That's the part I need you to understand, whoever ends up reading this—and someone will, because I've started keeping my own records now, paper ones, in the old style, because paper doesn't have a charter clause. The enforcers didn't come to seal anything. Sealing is what you do when you're afraid of a thing. They came with demolition charges and a timeline. This isn't fear. This is housekeeping.

And the worst part—the part that keeps me up at night, staring at the ceiling while my wife sleeps beside me—is that the signal might have known.

* * *

Here's what I know about the transmission under our feet, and I know more than I told Vesper, because telling people things is how they end up deported.

The signal repeats. It's been repeating since before the charter arrived, maybe since before the massacre—it's hard to date a signal when the only people who could read its language were murdered by the people who built the equipment listening for it. When I first heard it through the seismic rig, I thought it was machinery. A pump cycling. Something dumb and mechanical, the way everything down here used to be before the charter digitized us into obedience.

But machinery doesn't vary. And the signal varies. It comes in long cycles—a burst, then silence, then a longer burst, then silence, then three short ones. I've been timing it for weeks now, ever since Vesper dragged me into this, and the pattern holds across days. Burst. Silence. Long burst. Silence. Three short.

You know what else follows that pattern? Every alarm system ever built in the Diaspora era. My father restored one once, in a museum piece from a mining station—he let me hold the trigger assembly, told me it was older than the colonies themselves. Pulse-pulse-pause meant all clear. Any deviation meant breach. And a sustained triple-pulse—

A sustained triple-pulse meant structural failure imminent. Evacuate.

The ground under Meridian Deep isn't singing. It's been counting down for two hundred years, in a language nobody alive was supposed to understand, warning anyone who could hear it that the thing beneath the colony was failing—or that the thing above it was coming back to finish the job.

Maybe both. Maybe the old settlers built their warning system to watch for exactly what's happening right now, and somewhere under Gallery E a machine older than my whole bloodline has finally gotten an answer to its question, and the answer is: yes. They came back. Yes, they brought charges. Yes, nobody remembers your names except a hydroponics priestess, a drunk, and a person who isn't entirely a person.

Three short pulses. Over and over. The most patient scream in human history.

* * *

I took Vesper down to Deck Nine that night—not the sealed sublevel, we couldn't get near it anymore, the enforcers had welded the access hatches and posted guards with the patience of men being paid overtime—but to the pump gallery, where the primary atmospheric conduit comes down through the ceiling like a steel artery and disappears into the rock below.

"Watch," I said, and I put my palm flat against the conduit housing.

You can feel it if you know how to listen with your hand. The airflow. Two hundred thousand cubic meters of processed atmosphere per hour, running down through the deck plates, through Gallery E, through the mass grave, through the homes of the people who built this place before it was stolen, and back up into the lungs of four hundred living colonists who mostly don't know their lives pass through a charnel house every ninety seconds.

Vesper put their hand next to mine. Their hands are the original's hands. I try not to think about that too much.

"It runs through the whole sublevel," I said. "Gallery E especially. The demolition sequence starts with the eastern sections. First charge goes off, the conduit goes with it, and the backup loop—" I tapped a gauge on the wall, its needle resting in the red zone where it's rested for a decade, "—the backup loop can carry maybe thirty percent of load. Long enough for repairs, if you have parts and time. We don't have parts. We haven't had parts for eleven years."

"How long," Vesper said. Not a question. A request for a number they already feared.

"If they run the full sequence? Air quality in the hab-ring drops below survivable in about sixty hours. Slower in the agri-decks—the priestess's plants will buy us a day, maybe two, eating CO2. Children last longer than adults, ironically. Small bodies, efficient." I heard my own voice doing the thing my father's voice used to do, the engineer's trick of making horror sound like arithmetic. "Four hundred people, Vesper. Give or take. The charter's census says two-sixty, but the census is a lie like everything else."

Vesper stared at the conduit for a long time. Then they said something I wasn't expecting.

"The original knew about the conduit."

"What?"

"When she walked the sublevel—her memory-blocks, I've been decoding them. She mapped it. She noted the airflow paths, the junction points, the redundancy failures. She stood in Gallery E and felt the same current you just showed me, and she wrote in her log: *the colony breathes through the grave.*" Vesper's jaw tightened. "She saw it. She understood exactly what would happen if anyone ever tried to erase the evidence thoroughly enough. And she still signed the dormancy order. She still let the file die."

"That's not fair," I said, and immediately hated myself for it, because fairness wasn't the point. But I said it anyway, because eleven years ago the original Vesper Marchetti had a choice, and she chose the company, and the company kept us breathing precisely long enough to forget why forgetting mattered. "She probably thought she was saving us. That's the trick of it, isn't it? They make the coward's path look like mercy."

Vesper looked at me then, really looked, and I saw something move behind their eyes that I recognized from funerals. "Is that what you think I'm walking?"

"I think," I said, "that you haven't decided yet. And I think the countdown doesn't care."

* * *

We told the priestess the next morning, in the greenhouse, among the tomato vines she treats with more tenderness than the colony has ever shown its children. Sister Anouk—she won't tell anyone her real name, another inheritance, another buried thing—listened without interrupting, which is her gift and her weapon, and when I finished she was quiet for so long I started cataloguing the drip valves just to have something to do with my eyes.

"Sixty hours," she said finally.

"From first charge. Which hasn't been scheduled yet, but the prep teams are surveying Gallery E right now. I've seen their equipment manifests. Shape charges, severance tools, a mobile incinerator." I let that last one sit in the humid air between the vines. Incinerators aren't for demolition. Demolition leaves rubble. Incinerators leave nothing.

"They're going to burn the bodies," Vesper said quietly.

"They're going to burn everything," I said. "The ledger, the effects, the child's drawing. The whole testimony. And then they're going to vent whatever's left through the atmosphere processors, and four hundred people will breathe their ancestors' ashes without ever knowing, and the charter will write a memo about successful infrastructure remediation, and in sixty years there'll be another clone with somebody else's memories standing in a half-empty corridor wondering why the air tastes like rust."

Sister Anouk reached out and steadied a tomato vine that didn't need steadying. Her hands, I noticed, were shaking.

"You understand what this means," she said. "The bargain I offered you—the archive, the truth, in exchange for protecting the living first. The clock just moved, Vesper. If you go to the colony with the massacre now, if you stand in the commons and shout it, Oduya declares an emergency, the enforcers accelerate, and we all die informed. But if you wait—if you build a case, if you do it properly, legally—" she smiled, and it was the saddest smile I've ever seen on a woman surrounded by living green things in a tomb at the bottom of the world, "—there is no properly. There is no legal channel that moves faster than a demolition charge. He designed it that way. The whole system is designed so that truth arrives either too late or too loudly."

"So what do we do?" I asked. Because apparently I was in this now. Because apparently I'd been in this since the day I played Vesper my grandmother's song and couldn't take it back.

Sister Anouk turned to Vesper. "You told me once that the original left you her instincts. Her training. Her shame." She plucked a dead leaf from the vine, held it in her palm. "Do you also have her access codes?"

Vesper went very still.

"Charter administrative override," the priestess continued, gently, terribly. "Level one. The kind that opens comm arrays. The kind that opens *anything.*"

"The codes die with the original," Vesper said. "Standard protocol."

"Yes," Anouk agreed. "Unless the clone inherits more than memories." She let the leaf fall. "Check your blocks, Vesper. All of them. Even the locked ones. *Especially* the locked ones. Somewhere in that head of yours is a key, and somewhere under our feet is a machine that's been screaming *three short pulses* for two centuries, and I would very much like to introduce them."

* * *

That night I climbed to the observation blister above Deck One—you have to sign a waiver to get in, which tells you everything about how much this company wants us looking at anything—and I looked down through the rock, as if looking hard enough could see through it, toward Gallery E, where the survey teams would return at dawn with their charges and their incinerator and their clearance-level directives.

The signal was pulsing. I couldn't hear it from up there, obviously. But I could feel it anyway, the way you can feel weather coming in an old joint, the way my grandmother could feel the fans change. Burst. Silence. Long burst. Silence. Three short.

Two hundred years of warning, and the warning was finally, horribly accurate.

I thought about my father, dead in the dark with his wrench. About my grandmother, singing to children who didn't know they were archives. About two hundred people under the floor whose only remaining sin was refusing, even in death, even as ash-to-be, to stay quiet.

Then I keyed my comm and called Vesper Marchetti, and I said the words I'd been carrying all day like a stone in my boot:

"They've scheduled the first charge. Tomorrow, 0600. Gallery E."

A pause. Static. Then Vesper's voice, stripped of everything but intent:

"Then we don't have until tomorrow. Wake the priestess. Get the ledger out tonight. And Ilya—" another pause, and I swear the static carried something underneath it, something that sounded almost like the signal itself, "—I found the codes."
