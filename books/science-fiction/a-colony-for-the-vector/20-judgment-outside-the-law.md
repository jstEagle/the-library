## 20. Judgment Outside the Law

The withdrawal came, as all great institutional decisions do, in the form of paperwork.

I was standing in the comm array's control room—still smelling of solder and Ilya's grandmother's lullaby, still half-deaf from the roar of two hundred voices singing through a dead frequency—when Oduya appeared at my elbow holding a slate like it was a live grenade. He'd aged ten years in forty hours. The creases around his mouth had become canyons.

"Charter response," he said. "Priority channel."

"Read it to me," I said. "I want to hear how they say *we're sorry we got caught* in corporate dialect."

He read it. It was beautiful, in its way. Eleven hundred words that never once used the words massacre, grave, sublevel, or Vesper Marchetti. The Charter of Meridian Deep, citing "irreconcilable reputational exposure and material misrepresentation of site conditions during acquisition," was exercising its withdrawal clause. All enforcement personnel would stand down effective immediately. All demolition orders were rescinded pending "independent review." A caretaker trust would assume colonial administration within ninety days. The word *scandal* did not appear, but its ghost haunted every clause like a tenant with squatter's rights.

"They're not confessing," I said.

"No." Oduya set the slate down on the console, gently, as if it might bruise. "They're leaving. There's a difference, and the difference is lawyers."

"And the enforcers?"

He nodded toward the viewport. Down in the dark of the lower levels, where the tunnels ran through what the founding records insisted had always been empty rock, the cutting torches had gone quiet an hour ago. I could see the work lights through the floor grating, motionless now, a constellation that had stopped believing in itself.

"Standing down mid-demolition," Oduya said. "Commander Vashti received the same message. She asked for confirmation twice. Then she ordered her people topside." He paused. "She looked almost relieved. Did you know that? Like she'd been waiting eleven years for someone else to make the decision for her."

Below us, the colony was waking up to the news the way a body wakes to anesthesia wearing off—not all at once, and not pleasantly. I could hear it through the deck: doors opening, voices rising, someone laughing in a way that sounded more like crying. The hydroponics bays would be full. The priestess would be receiving people who hadn't spoken to her in years, coming to touch the growing things and ask her if it was real.

"It's real," I said, mostly to myself.

"It's real," Oduya agreed. "And now comes the part nobody writes songs about."

* * *

The part nobody writes songs about took three days, and it looked like this:

It looked like Commander Vashti's shuttle lifting off without a single enforcer so much as looking at any of us. It looked like the demolition equipment left behind in the tunnels, abandoned mid-cut, a plasma torch lying on the floor of the corridor outside the mass grave like a dropped accusation. It looked like the charter's legal team beaming over a final settlement offer that included, buried in paragraph forty-seven, a mutual non-disparagement clause covering "all parties, including deceased persons, to the fullest extent permitted by law."

They had tried to gag the dead. In writing. I framed it—or rather, Ilya framed it, printing it out on the colony's ancient fabricator and pinning it to the mess hall wall under a sign that read: *THEY TRIED TO NDA A MASSACRE.*

It looked like no one being arrested. That was the part that sat wrongest in my chest, heavier than anything else. No court anywhere in charted space had jurisdiction over what happened at Meridian Deep—the massacre predated the charter, the charter predated most of the relevant treaties, and the people who gave the original orders had died comfortably, in beds, surrounded by descendants who now ran shipping concerns and sat on ethics boards. The woman whose murder had brought me here—Eleven Years Cold, file stamped dormant—had been killed, as far as any tribunal would ever concern itself, by person or persons unknown, motive undetermined, case closed.

No court convicted anyone. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit. Justice, the kind with gavels and verdicts and sentences served in facilities with names like rehabilitation arcs, did not happen at Meridian Deep. What happened instead was stranger and less satisfying and possibly more durable: the record came into existence, and it could not be unmade.

That was the whole victory. Two hundred names, translated at last, spoken aloud in a language everyone had been told was extinct, transmitted on open channels to every listening post in the sector. You cannot unsend testimony. You cannot subpoena a frequency. Somewhere right now, on a station orbiting a star I'll never visit, a historian is pulling our broadcast out of the noise and starting to ask questions that will outlive everyone responsible for answering them.

It wasn't enough. It also had to be enough. Both things, simultaneously, forever. I'm learning that justice outside the law tastes like that—like bread made from grain you grew yourself, when what you wanted was someone else's apology.

* * *

Oduya resigned on the fourth day, and he did it the way he did everything: thoroughly, quietly, and with a folder.

He came to my quarters—I'd moved into the archivist's office off the old records bay, because irony has a sense of humor and so, apparently, do I—and he carried a physical archive. Actual paper. Polymer-sheathed, climate-sealed, older than me. Older than the original me.

"The true archives," he said, setting them on my desk. "Everything the charter's copies were made from. Everything the copies were made to replace."

"You kept these the whole time."

"I kept the colony alive the whole time," he said. "These are related activities." He sat down across from me, and for the first time since I'd landed here, he looked tired in a way that seemed honest rather than performed. "You should know what you're taking custody of. Not just the documents. The weight."

"Tell me."

"Every administrator before me got a choice on their first day. Same choice I got. Same choice, presumably, that was offered to—" he stopped, and I understood he meant the original Vesper, and we both let the sentence die with dignity. "You open the archives, you risk the colony. You seal them, you risk your soul. Everyone picks sealing. Everyone tells themselves it's temporary. I told myself it was temporary for thirty-one years."

"Do you regret it?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything, even questions designed to wound him. "I regret that the question was ever put to anyone. But no. I don't regret keeping four hundred people breathing while the truth waited underground. The truth turned out to be patient. People aren't." He stood, smoothed his jacket out of habit, and then, unexpectedly, extended his hand. "Resignation's filed. The trust takes over in ninety days. Until then, the archives are yours, Archivist Marchetti."

It was the first time anyone had called me that as a title rather than an insult. I shook his hand. His grip was dry and strong and trembling very slightly, and I pretended not to notice, because some mercies are just good manners.

"What will you do?" I asked.

"There's a settlement on Ceres Verge that needs an air systems engineer with experience managing decline," he said. "I'm told I'm overqualified." At the door he paused. "Vesper. When the reckoning happens—and it will happen, colonies are small and memory is long—keep it clean. Don't let it become another thing done *to* people. Let it be something they do themselves."

Then he was gone, and I was alone with three centuries of other people's secrets, which is, I've come to believe, the natural condition of archivists everywhere.

* * *

The colony held its own reckoning nine days after the broadcast, in the hydroponics cathedral, because it was the largest room and because the priestess insisted that judgment delivered among growing things stays humble.

There was no bench. There was no oath. Sefa—the priestess, though she'd started correcting people who used the title, saying *I grow lettuce, not absolution*—opened the proceedings by reading the two hundred names aloud, slowly, in the extinct tongue and then in translation, while the colony stood in the green light and the irrigation systems ticked like patient clocks. It took two hours. Nobody sat down. Children who had never seen sky stood beneath grow-lamps and listened to the dead being counted, and I watched a boy of maybe six mouth the syllables of a language older than the Diaspora Wars, learning it the way children learn everything: as if it had always been there.

Then the colonists spoke. That was the reckoning—not verdicts but testimonies. The retired engineer, sober for nine days and hating every minute of it, stood up and admitted he'd found a bone fragment in the reclamation tunnels twenty years ago and thrown it back and drunk about it. A woman whose family had held power-ration privileges for three generations confessed that her grandmother had informed on a neighbor in '61 and gotten extra water for it. An old man described, in a voice like gravel settling, the day the charter sealed the sublevel access, and how his father had stood at the welded door every anniversary until he died, and how the family had called him mad, and how he hadn't been mad.

Nobody was sentenced. Nobody could be. What happened instead was harder: people were *heard*, and hearing turned out to be a punishment and a release in equal measure, and several times I watched someone finish speaking and stand there looking stunned, as if they'd expected the words to cost more or less than they did.

When it ended, Sefa found me at the edge of the crowd.

"You didn't testify," she said.

"I broadcast the evidence. That felt sufficient."

"That's not the same thing." She studied me with those gardener's eyes that see root rot before the leaves show it. "You're still carrying the original's confession like it's yours. It isn't. But what you did with it—that is. Say it out loud sometime. To them. Not the data. The choice."

"I burned her memory-blocks, Sefa. I don't get to inherit her shame and disown it whenever it's inconvenient."

"You misunderstand inheritance entirely." She said it without heat, the way she said everything, watering-can patience. "You didn't receive her guilt. You received her *case*. And you closed it differently than she did. That difference is the only thing that was ever actually yours. The rest was always going to burn."

I thought about arguing. I've learned that when Sefa is right, arguing with her is just composting time.

* * *

That night I went down to the sublevel alone.

The demolition torches still lay where the enforcers had dropped them. The work lights still glowed, running on emergency power nobody had bothered to cut, illuminating the homes of the two hundred exactly as we'd found them: tables set, tools hung, a child's drawing pinned above a sleeping alcove, faded to ghosts of color. And at the end of the main corridor, the grave, marked now with a simple plate Ilya had fabricated, bearing the first line of the ledger in the old script and, beneath it, in trade-common: *WE WERE HERE. WE ARE SPEAKING.*

I stood at the threshold for a long time. Then I took out the last thing I owned that belonged to the original Vesper Marchetti: a single memory-block, the locked one, the one containing her final entry, her choice, her cowardice preserved in crystal. I'd burned the others in the array's thermal sink during the broadcast. This one I'd kept, telling myself it was evidence. Telling myself a lot of things.

Sefa was right. It wasn't evidence. Evidence lived in the archives Oduya had given me, complete and copyable. This was just grief wearing a technical specification.

I set it on the floor of the corridor, among the homes of people who had never gotten to choose anything, and I crushed it under my heel until the casing split and the crystal inside went to glitter and dust.

It didn't feel like freedom. I want to be accurate about that too. It felt like amputation—clean, necessary, and permanent. The original's memories were already woven through me too deep to excise; I would always know the taste of her coffee preferences, the route of her morning runs, the specific quality of her fear in the weeks before she chose silence. But the confession, the sealed version of herself she'd tried to pass down like a cursed heirloom—that was dust now. Whatever Vesper walked out of this tunnel was going to have to author herself from scratch.

My heel hurt. The lights hummed. Somewhere above me, four hundred living people were learning how to say two hundred names without flinching.

I picked up the plate-marker's spare fasteners from Ilya's supply crate, and I knelt at the grave, and I added a second line to the marker in trade-common, below the old script, in my own handwriting, because some testimony should be signed:

*Case closed. — V. Marchetti, Archivist.*

Then I heard footsteps behind me—quick ones, young ones—and Ilya came around the corner at a run, breathless, holding a slate like it was on fire.

"Vesper. The broadcast." He shoved the slate at me. "It's not just historians pulling it anymore. There's traffic on the reply channels—dozens of signals, from the outer settlements, from ships, from—" he caught his breath, eyes shining, terrified and lit up all at once, "—from *them*. Other descendant communities. Families who kept pieces of the language alive the way mine did, who never knew why, who heard two hundred strangers speak their grandmother's tongue on an open channel and recognized it." His voice cracked. "Vesper, there are aid ships inbound. Real ones. And one of the transmissions—it's addressed to whoever's keeping the archive. It says they have *more ledgers*. From other sites. Other colonies built the same way."

I looked down at the marker, at my signature drying in the cold air of a tunnel that had waited two hundred years to be believed.

More ledgers. More graves. More names that had never been counted.

I stood up, and I took the slate, and I read the first line of the incoming transmission—written, impossibly, patiently, in the language of the dead—and I understood that the case I'd closed was only the first one.

"Get Sefa," I said. "And wake up the translator team. We're going to need a bigger archive."
