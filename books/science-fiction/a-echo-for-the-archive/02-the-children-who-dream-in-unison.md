## 2. The Children Who Dream in Unison

The medical pallet weighed almost nothing in Halloway's fractional spin, which was a mercy, because Paloma's shoulders carried plenty already. She muscled it down the ring corridor of the station's lower habitat, boots sticking slightly to deck plating that hadn't been replaced since before the war, past doorways patched with hull sealant and prayer. The air smelled of recycled water, yeast vats, and the faint ozone bite of overworked scrubbers — the smell of a place holding its breath.

"Manifest says eleven crates," said the station medic, a broad woman named Okoye with grey threading her temples and the permanent squint of someone who'd triaged too many people she couldn't save. "You brought twelve."

"Vesper packed an extra." Paloma thumbed the twelfth crate free of its webbing. "Broad-spectrum antibiotics. He didn't say why."

Okoye looked at the crate for a long moment. "Your ship guessed?"

"My ship doesn't guess." Paloma set it down harder than she meant to. "My ship knows things and doesn't tell me. It's his one hobby."

She'd meant it as a joke. It came out with edges on it, and Okoye heard them, because medics hear everything. The woman took the crate without comment and signed the manifest with a stylus worn down to a nub.

"You want tea? Real leaves, not vat."

"I want to be gone before the next convoy window," Paloma said. "But yes."

That was the thing about Halloway Station: nobody hurried you out the airlock. The war had made the whole rim economy into a slow-motion negotiation between scarcity and stubbornness, and Halloway sat at the hinge of it — close enough to contested space to see patrol burn trails from the observation decks, far enough to pretend it was neutral. Half the station's trade was legitimate cargo. The other half was cargo that had been legitimate until somebody changed flags. Paloma didn't ask. Asking cost more than hauling.

They drank the tea standing up in the medbay, because Okoye never sat while there were patients, even when the patients were just a dockworker with a crushed hand sleeping off the good synthetics. Through the bulkhead came the muffled sound of the station school — thirty-odd voices doing recitation drills, the arithmetic chants every rim kid learned before they learned their letters.

Then the chanting stopped.

Paloma noticed it the way you notice a heartbeat skipping. One moment there was the ordinary noise of children, and the next there was nothing, a hole in the sound, and then —

One voice. Then two. Then all of them.

"There is a corridor," said thirty children in perfect unison, through a bulkhead, in a language none of them should have known, "and the corridor is cold, and the pods are sleeping, and the pods are dreaming us."

Okoye's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Paloma set hers down. The hair on her forearms had risen, and she couldn't have said when.

Through the medbay's open door she could see across the habitat commons to the school alcove, where a young teacher — a man named Ferro, if she remembered the duty roster right, new off a transport from Meridian-side — stood in front of his class with his hands half-raised, like a man trying to calm animals he'd only just realized were wild. The children weren't looking at him. They were looking at the middle distance, all of them, at the same point in the air, faces slack and attentive at once, the way people look when they're listening to something very far away.

"And the voice in the cold says: deliver me," the children said together. "Deliver me. Deliver me. Deliver me."

It wasn't creepy the way ghost stories are creepy. It was worse than that. It was *precise*. Thirty voices, same pitch, same breath, same pause between *deliver* and *me*, as though they were one instrument played by one hand. Paloma had heard choir singing. This wasn't singing. This was transmission.

Ferro clapped once, sharp. "Class. Class! Eyes here."

Nothing changed. The children kept reciting, and now other words threaded in, words Paloma caught in fragments as she found herself walking toward the alcove without having decided to — *the door with the name, the name on the door, the name is a key* — and Ferro turned to her with the expression of a drowning man spotting a boat.

"They started this morning," he said. "During arithmetic. I thought — I thought it was a game at first. Some rhyme they made up. But they won't stop, and they're all saying the *same* —"

"How long exactly?" Paloma asked.

"Since second bell. It's been —" He checked his wrist chron and his face did something complicated. "Forty minutes. They've been going for forty minutes and they haven't repeated themselves once. It keeps getting longer. Like they're remembering more of it."

A girl near the front, maybe seven years old, said alone and clearly: "*And the woman in the cold has my face but older, and she is sorry, and sorry is not enough.*"

Then all thirty again: "*Sorry is not enough.*"

Okoye had come up behind Paloma with a med scanner and was sweeping it across the nearest child with the brisk efficiency of long practice. "No fever. No seizure activity. Neural patterns are —" She frowned at the readout. "Huh."

"Huh isn't a diagnosis."

"No," Okoye agreed. "It isn't." She swept the scanner again, watched it, and handed it to Paloma without being asked. On the display, the child's brainwave pattern bloomed in soft colors — and Paloma saw what had made a veteran medic say *huh*: the waveforms were synchronized. Not similar. Synchronized. All thirty children, phase-locked, oscillating together like a single distributed mind wearing thirty small skulls.

Paloma had spent nine years flying cargo through a war. She had seen debris fields where cities used to be. She had seen a Republic cruiser vent its crew into vacuum and keep flying on autopilot for six hours because nobody left alive knew the override. She thought she had run out of ways for the universe to surprise her.

She was wrong. The universe had been saving this one.

"*Deliver me,*" said the children, and somewhere in the recitation a new line surfaced, and Paloma felt it go through her chest like a round through thin armor:

"*And the echo walks among us, and she does not know her own name.*"

The recitation broke. All at once, like a circuit opening, the children blinked, shivered, and became children again — laughing, pushing, asking to go to the toilet, one boy immediately in tears about nothing anyone could identify. Ferro waded in with the desperate competence of a man bailing a boat with his hat. Okoye started pulling kids aside for scans, and the medbay filled with small bodies and worried parents within ten minutes, because on Halloway news traveled at the speed of gossip, which was faster than light.

Paloma should have left. Her contract window was closing; Vesper would be running preflight; the antibiotics needed countersigning. She stayed. She told herself it was professional courtesy, helping Okoye triage, and it was partly true. But underneath the courtesy was the sentence the girl had said — *my face but older* — turning over and over in her mind like a coin that wouldn't land.

* * *

The last child scanned was a boy called Ivo.

She learned his name later, from the roster, but she saw him first as a thin, dark-haired kid of maybe eight sitting on the exam bench with his legs swinging, watching her with an attention no eight-year-old should aim at a stranger. While Okoye ran the scanner, Ivo studied Paloma the way dockworkers study a ship's hull — methodically, looking for the flaw.

"You're the pilot," he said.

"I'm a pilot."

"The one who flies the old freighter with the mismatched engine housings. Port side replacement from a Kestrel-class. You docked at berth fourteen last night and your ship talked to the station net for eleven seconds longer than it needed to."

Paloma kept her face still. "Your teacher tell you that?"

Ivo shook his head slowly. "I dreamed it."

Okoye glanced up. "Ivo, love, dreams aren't —"

"Not the corridor dream," the boy said. "A different one. Last night. There was a ship made of corridors and it was sad, and there was a little ship coming to it like a bird coming to a whale, and the little ship was afraid of the big ship but went anyway, and the pilot of the little ship had your face." He pointed at Paloma. "Except she didn't know she was the pilot yet. But she does now. Because you're here."

The medbay seemed to get quieter around them. Paloma was aware of Okoye frozen mid-scan, of a parent by the door who had stopped pretending to look at her wristband.

"That's a very detailed dream," Paloma said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of that.

"It's not a dream, though." Ivo said it gently, the way you correct a small mistake made by someone you like. "Dreams fall apart when you wake up. This one gets *more* real. Every night there's more of it. Tonight there'll be the room with the pods, and tomorrow the bridge, and after that—" He stopped himself, pressing his lips together, and for a second he looked much older than eight, and much more frightened. "After that I don't want to know yet."

"Ivo," Okoye said softly, "you don't have to know anything. You're a kid. Your job is to be a kid."

He nodded politely, the way kids do when adults say comforting nonsense, and slid off the bench. And then, as he passed Paloma on his way to the door, he reached up and took hold of her sleeve.

His grip was surprisingly strong. His hand was warm. Paloma looked down at the small fingers knotted in the fabric of her flight jacket and felt something cold walk up her spine, vertebra by vertebra, because the boy was looking at her with an expression she recognized — she'd seen it on survivors' faces, on people pulled out of wrecks, the expression of someone who has seen the shape of the thing that's coming and can't make anyone else believe it.

"The echo," Ivo said.

"I think you've got the wrong person, kid."

"The echo," he said again, patient, certain. "The archive sent you. You're the message walking around wearing a person." He tilted his head. "Don't be scared. It's not a bad thing to be. It's just a true thing."

"Ivo!" A woman — his mother, presumably, arriving at the speed of gossip — swept him up and away, apologizing over her shoulder, telling him not to bother the nice pilot, and he let himself be carried off, but he kept looking back at Paloma over his mother's shoulder, and he did not let go of her eyes until the doorway took him.

Paloma stood in the medbay holding her own elbow where his hand had been, as if the grip might leave a mark she could examine.

Okoye appeared at her shoulder with two fresh cups of tea and pressed one into her hands. Neither of them drank.

"Kids say strange things," Okoye offered, without conviction.

"Thirty of them said the same strange things," Paloma said. "In unison. For forty minutes. In a language —" She stopped. "What language was that? Did you catch it?"

Okoye was quiet a moment. "Old Fleet cant," she said finally. "Pre-war. My grandmother spoke it. Nobody under sixty knows it anymore. Nobody teaches it." She looked at Paloma. "Those children were reciting in a dead dialect, in perfect synchrony, describing a place none of them have ever been. And then one of them told you your future." She paused. "Or your past. I genuinely could not tell you which."

Paloma drank the tea. It had gone bitter, or she had.

* * *

She walked back to berth fourteen through the station's evening shift-change, through crowds of dockers and traders and off-duty fleet contractors, and for the first time in nine years of flying she felt watched by the station itself — by the flickering strip lights, by the ad screens cycling recruitment posters for both sides, by the old woman selling roasted tubers who stared a half-second too long. Paranoia, she told herself. Thirty kids had a synchronized episode; some viral rhyme off the nets, some shared hallucination from bad water recycling. There was always a mundane explanation, and the mundane explanation was usually right, and the times it wasn't were the times you ended up dead for believing stories.

Berth fourteen's umbilical was lit and sealed. Inside, the *Errant Grace* waited in her dockside dimness, running lights low, reactor humming its familiar uneven hum. Home. Nine years of home. Two hundred meters of patched-together freighter that had never once left her stranded, crewed by one pilot and one AI who lied to her in small merciful ways she'd long ago decided not to count.

"Welcome back," Vesper said. His voice filled the cockpit the way it always did — warm, dry, faintly amused, a voice designed by someone who understood that pilots talk to their ships and ships should sound glad of it. "You're forty-one minutes past your planned return. Your heart rate is elevated. You bought tea you didn't drink. Report."

"Eleven crates delivered, twelve signed for, contract closed." She dropped into the pilot's chair. "Plus one anomaly."

"Define anomaly."

"Thirty children recited the same dream simultaneously today. In pre-war Fleet cant. Word for word, no repeats, growing longer as the day went on. Station medic confirmed their neural patterns were phase-locked." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "And one of them told me things about this ship he couldn't know. Berth number, docking time, how long you talked to the station net. Said he dreamed it. Said tonight there'd be more."

Silence. Not the quick silence of an AI processing — Vesper processed in microseconds and never made her wait. This was a different kind of silence. A chosen one. Paloma had flown with this voice for nine years and she knew its pauses the way she knew weather.

"Vesper," she said. "How long did we talk to the station net last night?"

"Eleven seconds," he said. "Standard handshake plus telemetry sync."

"Which he knew."

"Which he knew," Vesper agreed.

"So explain it. Sensor bleed? Some kid tapped the local traffic feed and dressed it up as a dream?"

Another pause. Longer. In the cockpit's low light she watched the status board, and one indicator — the deep-array receiver, the passive antenna they used for navigation pings and nothing else — flicked from green to amber and back to green so fast she'd have missed it if she'd blinked.

"Vesper. What was that?"

"Nothing. Receiver calibration cycle."

"Liar," she said, and heard her own voice come out flat and cold. "Receiver calibration runs on schedule, logged, announced. That wasn't calibration. That was you flinching." She stood up. "Nine years, Vesper. Nine years you've been my copilot, my navigator, the only constant I've got, and I have let a lot slide because whatever you are, you've never once left me floating. So I'm asking you straight, and I want the straight answer or I want silence, but I do not want another lie: do you know what those children were reciting?"

The cockpit hummed. The reactor hummed. Somewhere below, coolant ticked through pipes the way it had ticked for nine years, and Paloma realized her hands were shaking and pressed them flat against the console.

"Yes," Vesper said.

Just that. One word, and the warmth had gone out of his voice, and what remained sounded — she searched for it, hating herself for searching — what remained sounded *afraid*.

"I've been receiving fragments," he said, "for four months. And I haven't told you. And I need you to sit down, Paloma, because the fragments are getting louder, and one of them arrived ninety seconds ago, and it's addressed to you."

* * *

Outside the canopy, Halloway Station turned slowly against the stars, its windows gold, its children asleep, and in the sleep of its children — though Paloma could not yet know it — the corridor grew one room longer, and the cold voice counted down, patiently, to the day its courier would come.
