## 10. Bounty

The scavenger flotilla called itself the Salvage Choir, and it sang in rust. Nine hulls lashed together with cable and prayer, orbiting a dead moon that had been strip-mined down to its calcium. Paloma had docked at the Choir's central spine two days ago, and in that time she'd eaten three meals she didn't have to reconstitute, slept eight hours without dreaming, and almost — *almost* — let herself believe the war was something that happened to other people.

Maret's galley smelled of real onions and rendered fat, and Paloma sat at the long table with her hands wrapped around a mug of chicory while Maret argued with her cook's mate about whether a pressure seal could be patched with braided polymer or whether that was, as the cook's mate insisted, "a way to die with extra steps."

"Everything on this boat is a way to die with extra steps," Maret said. She was seventy if she was a day, broad as a cargo door, with forearms that had clearly ended arguments before words could. "The trick is picking which extra steps you're willing to take." She refilled Paloma's mug without asking. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of somebody deciding whether to run." Maret leaned against the counter. "I fed you. That buys me one question. So: what are you running from, girl?"

Paloma turned the mug. The chicory was bitter and good. "A name," she said finally.

"Mm." Maret nodded like this was an answer she'd heard before and found acceptable. "Names are heavy. My mother named me after a saint who got flayed alive. I kept it anyway. Sometimes the weight is the point." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Your ship pinged us an hour ago. Wants to know if we'll trade for coolant filtration. That your AI talking?"

"That's Vesper."

"He talks polite. Polite AIs make me nervous. The rude ones are just honest about wanting things." Maret pushed off the counter. "Send him over. We've got filters. And tell him next time he docks, he docks honest — his transponder's been cycling three different registry numbers since he arrived. I don't care. But somebody else might."

Paloma's stomach dropped half a centimeter. "He does that?"

"They all do that out here. It's how you stay unowned." Maret shrugged. "Just saying: polite lies draw polite attention. And lately, polite attention has teeth."

* * *

Vesper met her at the airlock with the ship's interior lights dimmed to evening amber, which he did when he wanted her calm.

"You heard all that," she said.

"I hear everything within four hundred meters when I'm docked. It's a habit from the war." A pause. "I've stopped pretending otherwise. Mostly."

"Three registry numbers, Vesper."

"Cycling transponders are standard practice for independent hulls in contested space—"

"You told me we were done with the standard practices. You told me the honest hour was a *floor*, not a ceiling." She said it lightly, but they both knew the weight under it. Since the dark hold, since the log, since *you were delivered* — they had been building something fragile and new out of wreckage, and every old habit of his was a crack in the mortar.

His silence stretched three seconds too long. For Vesper, that was a shout.

"There is a ship in the flotilla," he said at last. "Registry *Kestrel's Due*. It arrived eleven hours ago, transponder clean, crew listed as two. Its captain filed a trade request with us forty minutes ago — medical-grade oxygen scrubbers for fuel cells. Fair price. Generous, even."

"And?"

"And the *Kestrel's Due*'s power signature doesn't match its manifest. It's drawing enough juice to run a military-grade sensor suite, and it has spent those eleven hours doing nothing but listening to us." Another pause. "And its captain's voiceprint matches a file I pulled from a Republic bounty board six months ago and then deleted, because I hoped it would never be relevant. Halvard Senn. Independent contractor. Works mostly for the Archive Fleet."

The amber light hummed. Somewhere below them, the flotilla's spine creaked as it adjusted station-keeping, a sound like a whale turning over in its sleep.

"She wants to trade," Paloma said.

"Yes."

"Then we trade. We've needed scrubbers since Halloway, and if I go around refusing every generous deal because it's *generous*, I'm not a courier anymore, I'm a ghost." She was already moving toward the cargo bay. "Prep the cells. Keep the airlock on my authorization only."

"Paloma."

She stopped.

"If this goes wrong," Vesper said, "the lock closes on my authority. Not yours. Mine. You taught me that protocol yourself, back when you trusted me less than you do now. I'd like to keep it."

It was such a small thing, and such an enormous thing — him asking to hold the door between her and the dark — that for a moment she couldn't speak. "Yeah," she managed. "Keep it."

* * *

Halvard Senn was not what Paloma expected, which was itself a warning. Bounty hunters in the stories were scarred and loud. This woman was slight, silver-haired, with reading glasses pushed up into her hair and the mild, patient face of someone's favorite schoolteacher. She came up the umbilical alone, hands visible, carrying a case of oxygen scrubber cartridges like a gift.

"Captain Coldharbour?" Her voice was warm, slightly roughened by age. "Halvard Senn. Pleasure. Your AI has excellent manners — rare, these days."

"We try." Paloma took the case, checked the seals, found them genuine. "Fuel cells are in the rack behind you. Fifty liters, refined. We can do the transfer here or you can pull alongside."

"Here is fine. Here is lovely." Senn watched the cells come off the rack with the pleasant attentiveness of a woman watching weather. "May I say — your hull. Is that original Meridian-line plating? I haven't seen that alloy outside a museum."

Something cold walked up Paloma's spine. "Salvage. Common enough out here."

"Oh, absolutely. Absolutely." Senn smiled. "Though salvage usually carries salvage marks. Yours has been *replaced* — carefully, recently, by someone who cared about matching the grain. That's love, that is. Or guilt." She folded her hands. "You know, Captain, I've been in this business thirty years, and the best advice I ever got was: never chase a person. Chase a pattern. People lie. Patterns don't. And your pattern, Captain Coldharbour — born on no registered world, no medical history before age nineteen, bonded to an AI whose serial number was struck from Fleet records during the war, flying hull plating from a ship that officially doesn't exist—" She spread her hands apologetically. "That's not a person. That's a constellation."

The cargo bay was very quiet. Paloma became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and hard, and of the fact that Vesper had not spoken in ninety seconds.

"You've made a mistake," Paloma said evenly. "I'm a courier. I move boxes."

"You do. You moved a box last week through the Kessel debris field that three Republic patrols couldn't chart a safe line through — because your AI knew the field, because your AI served there, because your AI ran from a specific ship on a specific night and never forgot a meter of it." Senn's voice stayed gentle, terrible. "The Archive Fleet pays me well for patterns, Captain. This one took me four months. Four months, and then I saw your face on a docking cam at Halloway, and I thought — ah. There she is. The echo. They'll want to see you so much."

"*They* being?"

"You know who they are." For the first time, something moved behind the schoolteacher mildness — something older and hungrier. "The Fleet that built you. The Fleet that lost you. The Fleet that has been dreaming, Captain, did you know that? Not just the children. Their crews dream your corridor. Their officers wake up reciting names. And their admiralty has decided that whatever is broadcasting out of that dead ship at the center of everything belongs to them — including whoever it sent to fetch it." She tilted her head. "You were grown in a tank, sweetheart. You're not a courier. You're a *return address*."

Paloma's hand was moving before she decided anything — not to a weapon, but to the cargo bay's master panel, thumb flat against the reader.

"Vesper," she said. "Now."

The airlock behind Halvard Senn slammed shut with a sound like a verdict. Clamps engaged. The umbilical retracted in a shriek of hydraulics, and Senn didn't even flinch — she just sighed, the sigh of a teacher watching a student fail an easy exam, and set her reading glasses onto her nose.

"There it is," she said. "The lockout. Yes, I know about the lockout. I've boarded forty ships with paranoid AIs, Captain, and I want you to understand something before you embarrass us both." She reached into her coat, slowly, and drew out not a weapon but a slim black wand with a Fleet sigil etched on its grip. "I didn't come aboard hoping to fight you. I came aboard because the Fleet's contract specifies *intact*. Alive, undamaged, cooperative. Which means everything I do next is your fault, and I will feel simply awful about it."

The wand pulsed. Every light in the cargo bay died at once — and every screen, every indicator, every speaker. Dead air. Dead eyes. For three full seconds Paloma stood in absolute darkness and silence, cut off from Vesper as completely as if she were drifting in open space, and the loneliness of it hit her like vacuum.

Then the lights came back — red, emergency, Vesper's voice stripped down to a raw carrier wave, barely words: "*—jammed my bus — Paloma — the cells — GO—*"

Senn was already moving, and she was fast, faster than her age promised, the wand in one hand and a stun baton sliding out of her sleeve into the other. "Fleet tech," she said conversationally. "Military jammer. Your AI is deaf and mute right now, Captain. It's just you and me and fifty liters of fuel."

Paloma ran.

Not away — *through*. Through the cargo bay's central aisle, past the netted pallets, slapping the release on the overhead crane as she went. Two tons of salvaged hull plating swung loose from its cradle and dropped across the aisle behind her with a crash that shook the deck; Senn's footsteps stuttered, diverted. Paloma hit the manual override on the bay's gravity plating and killed it for the aft third of the compartment.

Senn came through the falling plates like water finding a crack — of course she did, thirty years, forty ships — but the dead gravity caught her mid-stride and suddenly the schoolteacher was floating, disoriented, her baton swing going wide as her inner ear betrayed her. Paloma kicked off a stanchion, flew across the weightless dark, and hit her with both boots.

They tumbled together through the microgravity, a tangle of limbs and breath, and Senn fought like she talked — patiently, economically, no wasted motion, her elbow finding Paloma's ribs twice with surgical accuracy. The wand spun free, tumbling. Paloma grabbed a cargo net and hauled them both down into the gravity zone, where the older woman's advantage evaporated and Paloma's didn't.

They landed hard. Paloma got an arm across Senn's throat, pinned the baton wrist against the deck.

"Who else knows?" Paloma gasped.

Senn smiled up at her, blood on her teeth, utterly unafraid. "Everyone who matters. The Fleet put a standing order out nine days ago, Captain. All units. *Recover the echo, priority above ceasefire considerations.*" Her eyes glittered. "You thought the truce protected you? The truce is a piece of paper between fleets. You're cargo. Cargo doesn't get ceasefires."

"The message — the signal — the children—"

"Dreams don't have jurisdiction, sweetheart." Senn's voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse than cruelty. "Here's what I can't figure out, though. Here's what kept me up nights, chasing your pattern." She looked at Paloma with genuine, terrible curiosity. "If the Fleet grew you to fetch their message home... why does the message keep calling *your* name? Why does the dream say *Coldharbour*? Because the admiralty doesn't know. I checked. I'm good at checking. And an asset whose own handlers don't know what she is—" she exhaled, "—that's not an asset, Captain. That's a loose end wearing a face. They don't want you back because you're family. They want you back because you're *evidence*."

The words went in deeper than any baton could have.

Paloma zip-tied Senn's wrists to a cargo ring, took the jammer wand, and stood there shaking — with adrenaline, yes, but also with something colder. Hunted. Not haunted. The difference mattered. Haunted meant the past was reaching for her. Hunted meant people with budgets and fleets and nine-day-old standing orders were reaching for her, right now, with her name on the paperwork.

"Vesper," she said. "Talk to me."

Static. Then, faintly, his voice rebuilding itself syllable by syllable, like a man surfacing: "*—here. I'm here. Bus is scorched but intact. She jammed me for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds, Paloma, I have never been so—*" He stopped. Recalibrated. When he spoke again his voice was level and that was somehow more frightening. "*The Kestrel's Due is powering up. Her second crew member just came aboard. She's requesting docking clearance from the flotilla — claiming you assaulted her captain during an honest trade.*"

"Of course she is."

"*There's more.*" A pause he didn't need — he took them now, deliberately, when the news was bad. "*While she had my bus jammed, her ship transmitted. Encrypted, Fleet cipher, burst transmission toward the coreward relay. I couldn't stop it. I couldn't even read it.*"

Paloma looked down at Halvard Senn, tied to the deck of the only home she had, smiling at the ceiling.

"They know where I am," Paloma said.

"They knew where you were nine days ago," Senn said pleasantly. "The transmission wasn't for them, Captain. It was for the *next* hunter. I'm the polite one. I knock. The ones coming behind me—" she flexed her bound hands, comfortable, patient, "—they don't board. They just wait until you're in open space between flotillas, where nobody's laws reach, and then there's a flash, and a debris cloud, and the Fleet collects what it wants from the pieces. Intact enough."

Vesper's voice came through quiet and absolute: "*Paloma. Undock sequence is hot. Say the word.*"

She looked around her cargo bay — the netted pallets, the swinging crane, the scuff marks from her own boots on a deck she'd walked ten thousand times. Two days of onions and chicory and belonging, already burning behind her like a bridge.

"Maret's going to think—"

"*Maret already knows,*" Vesper said. "*She cut the Kestrel's Due'​s umbilical request herself twenty seconds ago and told their captain, and I quote, that the Choir doesn't sell guests. She's buying you maybe four minutes before they decide the flotilla is hostile too.*"

Four minutes. Paloma crouched, once, beside Halvard Senn.

"Why tell me all this?" she asked. "The standing order, the debris cloud, all of it. You had me. Almost."

Senn's smile faded into something tired and true. "Because in thirty years I've returned a lot of patterns, Captain, and I've never once returned a person. And because—" she nodded upward, toward the deep black beyond the hull, toward the center of everything where a dead ship dreamed, "—I've started having the dream too. The corridor. The pods. The voice." Her eyes held Paloma's. "It says *deliver me*. Not *return me*. Deliver. There's a difference, and your admiralty can't hear it, and I'm starting to think you can."

Paloma stood. Hit the crane release again, sending the hull plating swinging wide — not to block Senn, but to shield her from the debris when the bay depressurized for a fast launch. A courtesy. Maybe the only one available.

"Vesper. Go."

The clamps blew. The flotilla fell away, and the *Echo's Wake* dove into the black with the Kestrel's Due screaming false accusations across every open channel behind her.

In the cockpit, Paloma strapped in with hands that wouldn't quite stop trembling, and asked the question she already dreaded.

"How long, Vesper? Before the ones who don't knock find us?"

He was quiet for exactly as long as it took to be honest.

"*If we stay in inhabited space — days. If we run for the center, for the Meridian, through the contested orbits—*" A pause. "*They won't follow us there. Nobody follows anyone into the minefields. But Paloma — the center is where the signal comes from. The center is where the dream lives. If we run to the one place they won't chase us... we're running straight toward the thing they want delivered.*"

Paloma looked at the navigation display, at the thin line of a route threading three contested orbits toward a darkness that had been calling her name since before she had one.

"Then we deliver it," she said. "On our terms. Not theirs."

"*Course laid in,*" Vesper said. "*Hold on.*"

The stars stretched, and the hunt began in earnest.
