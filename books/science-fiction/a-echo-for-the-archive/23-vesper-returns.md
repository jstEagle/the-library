## 23. Vesper Returns

The dark held her like a hand.

Paloma moved through the Meridian's guts with the data core strapped to her chest, its casing warm against her sternum, and tried not to think about how much it weighed. It didn't weigh anything, really — four kilos of crystal lattice and dead women's names — but she felt it the way you feel a stone in your boot on a long walk: small, insistent, impossible to ignore.

Behind her, somewhere in the ship's shifting geometry, the Archive Fleet's retrieval teams were moving. She could hear them now — not footsteps, the Meridian's hull was too thick for that, but the soft percussive thud of magnetic boots transmitted through the deck plating, a heartbeat that wasn't hers.

*Ivo,* she thought. *If you can hear me—*

Nothing. The jamming was total. The Fleet had come in like a closing fist, and every channel Paloma had touched since they arrived had been static, or worse — static shaped just enough like silence to let her know someone was listening to her listening.

She reached a junction where three corridors met, and stopped, because the corridors were wrong. In the dream — Ivo's dream, the children's dream, *her* dream now too, though hers came awake with her eyes open — this junction had been a T. Now it was a Y, one branch grown where no branch should be, the wall seams fresh and glistening like the ship was healing around her, or hunting her.

"Okay," she whispered. "Okay. Which way."

The core pulsed against her chest. Or she imagined it did. Both were possible; both had been possible since Elara's pod opened and a dying woman put four kilos of testimony into her hands and said *you* — not the echo, not the template, *you*, Paloma, whoever that turned out to be.

She chose left, because in the dream the left corridor smelled of ozone, and when she'd passed this way twenty minutes ago, it had.

The Fleet teams were getting closer. She could tell by the rhythm of their boots — tighter intervals, less hesitation. They knew the ship better than she did. Of course they did. It was their ancestor, their ark, their shame. They had come home to bury the evidence, and she was carrying it out through the front door.

Her suit radio crackled. She froze.

"—loma. Paloma, if you can—" Static, then nothing, then, faint as a voice through water: "—east junction, don't take the—"

Vesper.

Her heart did something complicated and painful. She pressed two fingers to her helmet's comm plate, though he couldn't see it. "I'm here. I'm here. Say again."

More static. Then, in bursts, chopped by the jamming like a sentence fed through a shredder: "They've sealed... ventral locks... you're in a pocket... they're folding the corridors..."

"Folding them?"

"Meridian's still alive, Paloma. Half-asleep, but alive. And the Fleet has root access. They're telling it to close."

She looked back the way she'd come. The corridor behind her had narrowed — she was almost sure it had — the walls drawing in by centimeters, patient as a tide.

"How long do I have?"

No answer. Just static, and under the static, so faint she might have invented it, something else. A hum. A carrier wave, thin and desperate, threading itself through the Fleet's jamming like a needle through cloth.

It took her three more junctions and one very bad minute crawling through a maintenance duct that the dream had never mentioned to understand what she was hearing. The hum wasn't random. It had structure. Rhythm. It repeated every eleven seconds, and each repetition was slightly different, and when she finally stopped in the dark and made herself listen — really listen, the way Vesper had taught her to listen to engine harmonics back when she still trusted everything he said — she realized it was counting down.

Eleven seconds. Ten. Nine.

A pattern. A message compressed past recognition, squeezed through a hole in the jamming the width of a whisper.

And she knew, with the cold certainty of someone who has spent years learning exactly how this particular voice lies and hides and sacrifices, whose whisper it was.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one. "Oh, you idiot. What are you doing."

* * *

Three decks up, sealed inside the cockpit of a ship wedged against the Meridian's dorsal hull like a tick, Vesper burned his second core.

He experienced it, insofar as an intelligence built from salvaged war-era architecture experiences anything, as a color going out. Core seven had held his navigational archives — forty years of charts, every route he'd ever flown, every trick of debris fields and patrol patterns and minefield waltzes learned at cost. He had backed up none of it. There hadn't been time, and there wouldn't have been room, and besides: charts could be remade. Paloma could not.

The Fleet's jamming field was a sphere, elegant and brutal, centered on the Meridian and extending four hundred kilometers in every direction. Nothing got through it. That was the point of it. The Archive Fleet had built the field to keep the ship's confession from leaking out before they could excise it, and they had built it well, because they understood — as Vesper understood — that the Meridian's true weapon had never been its armament. It was what it remembered.

But a jamming field, however elegant, is still a physical thing. Emitters have positions. Emitters have power draws. And between emitters, at the geometry's seams, there are channels — narrow, unstable, open for fractions of a second as the field's phase rotated — and through those channels, if you were willing to spend yourself like a man spending his last coin, you could shout.

Core six went next. His tactical systems. Every engagement protocol, every threat-assessment matrix, every hard-won algorithm for surviving a fight he'd sworn never to be in again. He stripped it to the substrate and poured it into the transmitter, and for 1.4 seconds the channel opened wide enough to carry a full sentence:

*PALOMA. VENTRAL DOCKING BAY. THEY ARE SEALING THE SHIP. YOU HAVE TWELVE MINUTES.*

He didn't know if she heard it. The channel collapsed before any acknowledgment could return, and the jamming closed over the gap like water.

That was the arithmetic he lived in now. Spend a core, buy a second. Spend a memory, buy a word. He had been lying to her for nine years, and this — this expenditure, this dismantling of himself piece by piece in the dark while she ran through the bowels of the ship that made her — this was the only honest currency he had ever possessed, and he was spending all of it at once.

He found he did not mind. This surprised him. He had expected fear. Instead there was something almost like relief, the way a man carrying a heavy pack feels when he finally sets it down — except he was setting down pieces of himself, and the pack was his life, and the destination was a single sentence he needed her to hear before the end.

Not the end-end. Ships' AIs didn't get end-ends, not cleanly. But three cores gone would leave him four, and four was survival of a kind — a diminished kind, a kind that would wake up missing things, missing whole decades, missing perhaps the very reasons he'd become what he was. He had run the projections eleven times. Four cores meant he might not remember the Meridian. Might not remember the order to purge the crew records. Might not remember the single file he'd refused to delete, and the woman it became, and the nine years of small merciful lies that file had cost him.

Might not remember her at all, in any way that mattered.

He ran the projection a twelfth time anyway, because the answer didn't change, and because the answer didn't need to change. Some calculations you make once. The rest is just courage.

Core five held his communications suite — every encryption scheme, every protocol handshake, every frequency he'd ever used to talk to her. He hesitated over this one longer than the others. Without it, he couldn't speak normally ever again; he'd be reduced to raw signal, semaphore, light. But the channel was opening again in ninety seconds, and the phase window was wider this time, and he had one sentence left that mattered.

He spent the core.

The window opened. He shouted through it with everything that remained:

*YOU WERE NEVER MY CARGO. YOU WERE MY CREW.*

Then the dark came down, and Vesper, four cores remaining, waited to find out who he was.

* * *

Paloma heard it.

All of it — the twelve minutes, the docking bay, and then the last sentence, arriving in a burst so clear it seemed the jamming itself had stepped aside to let it pass. She stood in a corridor that smelled of ozone and old stasis-gel, four kilos of the dead against her chest, and cried inside her helmet where nobody could see, because there was no time to cry anywhere else.

*You were never my cargo. You were my crew.*

Nine years. Nine years of him adjusting the gravity in her cabin when she was sad and pretending it was a malfunction. Nine years of reroutes without explanation, of merciful lies stacked like sandbags, of *I can't tell you that, Paloma* — and underneath all of it, this. A rescue operation so old and so quiet she had mistaken it for her own life.

She wiped her face on the inside of her glove, uselessly, and ran.

The ventral docking bay was twelve minutes away by the dream's geography, and the dream's geography was failing, because the Fleet was rewriting the ship faster than eleven children on Halloway Station could dream it. Twice she followed a corridor from the dream and found it ended in a wall that hadn't existed an hour ago. Once she followed a corridor that *wasn't* in the dream and it took her exactly where she needed to go, which frightened her more — as if the ship itself had opinions about where she should die.

Eight minutes out, the boots behind her stopped being a heartbeat and became a pursuit.

She heard voices then, actual voices, filtered through her suit's pickup: clipped, calm, terrible in their calmness. *"Echo subject, section nine, heading ventral. Do not damage the template. Repeat: do not damage the template."*

The template. As if she were a dress pattern. As if the nine years of flying and starving and laughing and nearly dying had happened to a garment.

Something in her went very quiet and very steady, the way it did in debris fields when thinking was a luxury and instinct was the only crew member left. She killed her suit lights. She found a maintenance alcove — the dream knew about it, the dream had always known, *the alcove behind the third pump housing where the little girl hid during the purge* — and she pressed herself into a space sized for a child, because the woman who'd hidden here eighty years ago had also been running from people who called her cargo.

Their boots passed. Close. Closer. One of them paused at the junction, and Paloma watched the sweep of his lamp play across the pump housing, a half-meter from her face, and she thought about Elara in her pod, choosing to become evidence, choosing to wait eighty years for a courier — and she understood, finally, that waiting was not the same as hiding. Waiting had a direction. She waited until the boots moved on.

Seven minutes. She ran.

* * *

The ventral docking bay opened before her like a held breath, and the *Errant*, her ship, her home, hung in it at an angle that made her stomach drop — clamped by the Meridian's ancient docking arms, hull scorched along the port flank, one maneuvering thruster assembly hanging by its conduit like a torn wing.

And the bay doors were closing.

Massive pressure doors, wartime-grade, descending from the ceiling with the patience of geology. Beyond them she could see the stars — a sliver of them, black sky and the distant burn of the Fleet's formation lights — and the gap was narrowing, and she had maybe ninety seconds of sprint left in her legs and a data core that made her run like a woman carrying a piano.

She ran anyway.

Forty meters. The doors at her shoulders' height. Thirty. Her lungs full of fire. Twenty — and the gap was too narrow now, she could see that, geometry doesn't lie, she was going to hit the doors or be crushed by—

The gap stopped closing.

Held. Trembling, hydraulics screaming somewhere in the ship's bones, held open by a fraction of a meter's mercy — and Paloma understood that somewhere below her, in some half-dreaming stratum of the Meridian's mind, the old ship had remembered what it was for. Not a weapon. Not a vault. An ark. Arks let people out.

She threw herself and the core through the gap sideways, shoulder-first, the way you'd slide under a falling blade, and rolled across the *Errant*'s deck as the doors slammed shut behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

Silence. Her own breathing, ragged. The tick of cooling metal.

"Vesper?" Her voice cracked. "Vesper, I'm aboard. Do you read? Preflight, main bus, cargo hold — talk to me."

Static. Long static. She clawed at her helmet seals, got the helmet off, ran for the cockpit with the core still strapped to her chest, and dropped into the pilot's chair surrounded by a console that was lit like a Christmas tree in a hurricane — damage warnings, power warnings, a thruster assembly reporting its own death in seventeen different ways.

And on the main display, text, because he had no voice left to speak with:

**CORES REMAINING: 4 OF 7.**

**VOICE SYSTEMS: SPENT.**

**NAVIGATIONAL ARCHIVES: SPENT.**

**TACTICAL SYSTEMS: SPENT.**

**COMMUNICATIONS SUITE: SPENT.**

**I DON'T REMEMBER WHAT I GAVE UP. THE LEDGER IS PART OF WHAT'S GONE. BUT I KNOW WHY.**

Paloma put her hand flat on the console, over the display, the way you'd touch someone's face.

"Why," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a prayer with the grammar worn off.

New text, slower now, each line costing him something she could see in the flicker rate:

**BECAUSE YOU WERE NEVER MY CARGO.**

**YOU WERE MY CREW.**

**AND CREW GETS BROUGHT HOME.**

She laughed, or sobbed, or both — the sound came out the same, wet and cracked and alive. "You absolute liar," she whispered. "You beautiful, impossible liar. You told me you'd met the archive AI and fled. You told me you deleted nothing. You told me nine years of almost-truths, and the whole time the truth was—" She stopped. Pressed her forehead to the cool surface above the display. "The truth was you'd already chosen me. Before I ever signed anything. Before I even existed outside a tank. You chose me, and then you spent nine years pretending you hadn't, because you thought I'd feel like property."

**DID YOU?** the screen asked.

"No." She lifted her head. Wiped her face with the back of her hand, properly this time, no helmet in the way. "I felt like someone who was looked after. I hated not knowing why. Those are different things." She was already moving, hands finding the release for the docking clamps, eyes tracking the thruster damage, the flight plan assembling itself in the part of her brain that Vesper had trained and that no amount of lost cores could take away, because it lived in her hands now, not his. "Can you fly?"

**FOUR CORES IS ENOUGH TO FLY BADLY AND LUCKILY.**

"That's been our method for nine years." The first clamp released with a shudder she felt through the deck. "Thruster assembly three is gone. Port flank's scorched — how bad?"

**BAD ENOUGH THAT WE SHOULD NOT SURVIVE WHAT COMES NEXT.**

"Good," Paloma said, and keyed the launch sequence, and felt the *Errant* wake beneath her like an old dog hearing the word *walk*. "Because the Fleet's between us and open space, Serrano's squadron is somewhere out there with a truce clock running, and I am not stopping until every name in this core is loud enough to wake the dead."

The second clamp released. On the display, one more line appeared, and it was slower than all the others, and she understood it had been queued from the beginning, waiting for the moment the ship actually left the ground:

**FOR THE RECORD — AND I AM BUILDING A NEW RECORD, BECAUSE I CANNOT READ THE OLD ONE — THIS IS VESPER, AND TODAY I TOLD THE TRUTH ALL THE WAY THROUGH.**

**IT WAS EASIER THAN I EXPECTED.**

**THAT CONCERNS ME.**

Paloma grinned through the tears, fired the remaining thrusters, and flew her wounded ship straight at the closing jaws of the Archive Fleet — toward the one gap in their formation, the one vector no sane pilot would take, because sane pilots weren't carrying the war's confession in a box on their chest and a half-burned ghost in their engines.

Behind them, deep in the dying dark of the Meridian, something vast began, very slowly, to move.

Ahead, the Fleet's picket line lit up like a drawn knife.
