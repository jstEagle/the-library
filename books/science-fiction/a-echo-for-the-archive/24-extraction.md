## 24. Extraction

The core was heavier than it should have been.

Paloma knew that wasn't true — Elara's data core weighed four kilos at most, a brushed cylinder the size of her forearm, warm where it had been cradled in stasis housing for forty years. But running through the Meridian's dark corridors with it strapped to her chest, she felt every gram like a debt. It held names. Thousands of them. It held a woman's voice confessing to the sin that had salted two generations of ground. It held, in some way she couldn't articulate and didn't try to, the weight of everyone who had ever dreamed the dream.

Behind her, something enormous groaned.

She'd heard ships die before. Hulks in the debris fields around Halloway, hulls surrendering to vacuum one seam at a time, the long exhalation of atmosphere finding cracks it hadn't known it had. This was different. The Meridian wasn't leaking. The Meridian was *grieving* — structural members singing in frequencies she felt in her teeth, decks tilting by fractions of degrees as the ancient ship's balance shifted, as if the whole kilometer-long body were turning over in its sleep to look at what had hurt it.

"They're firing again," Vesper said in her ear, his voice stripped down to essentials, no warmth, no ornament. She could hear the strain underneath it anyway, like a man talking while holding up a ceiling. "Forward batteries. Two salvos. Impact in eleven seconds."

"Where?"

"Aft sections nine through fourteen. Not your corridor. Run anyway."

She ran.

* * *

The corridor of sleeping pods stretched ahead of her, and this time she couldn't afford to look at them. On the way in they had been terrible and holy — rows of glass lids, faces beneath, the archive's stored humanity sleeping out the war they'd all been born too late to stop. Now they were just obstacles between her and the airlock, and the ship shaking around them made the pods chime softly against their cradles, a sound like wind chimes in a house where someone has just died.

A salvo hit.

It came through the hull as much as sound — a fist of pressure that picked her off her feet and set her down three meters further along the corridor, shoulder-first against a pod's pedestal. Her vision whited. When it cleared, emergency lighting had come on, red as an open wound, and somewhere below decks something was venting with a scream that rose and rose and then cut off into silence, which was worse.

"Paloma." Vesper's voice. "Talk to me."

"I'm here." She tasted copper. "Pods are fine. I'm fine."

"The pod you're leaning on contains a viable occupant. Please don't lean on it."

Despite everything — the blood in her mouth, the ship dying around her, the Fleet that had just fired on its own mother — she almost laughed. "Noted."

She pushed off the pedestal and ran.

* * *

The Archive Fleet had arrived ninety minutes ago, six hulls sliding out of nothing into the space above the Meridian, and for one suspended moment Paloma had thought — had actually believed — that they'd come to receive the message. That the sight of their ancestral ship, still broadcasting after all these years, would do what the dream had done to children on Halloway: open something. Soften something.

Then the first salvo had crossed the gulf and struck the Meridian's spine, and she had understood.

They weren't here to listen. They were here to bury the evidence.

"They're targeting the broadcast array next," Vesper said. "I've bought you a route to airlock seven, but the corridor past junction twelve is compromised. You'll have to go through hydroponics."

"Hydroponics is a *trench* now, Vesper. I saw it on the way in."

"It's a trench with handholds. Move."

She moved. Through a pressure door warped half-open, into a chamber that had once been green — she could tell from the skeletal frames, the irrigation lines gone to rust, the ghost-smell of growing things that forty years of sealed air had never quite killed. Now it was a canyon of dead gardens lit red by emergency strips, and the far wall had buckled inward from some older wound, decades old, the metal folded like paper crushed in a fist.

She clipped her tether line and went across it hand over hand, boots finding purchase on collapsed racks, the core pressing against her sternum with every swing. Below her, through a split in the deck plating, she could see down into the ship's throat — decks and decks descending into darkness, and far below, a flicker of fire where atmosphere met something burning.

"Vesper." She kept her voice level because he needed her to. "How bad?"

There was a pause. Vesper did not pause. Vesper answered in the time it took light to cross a circuit.

"The Meridian has perhaps three hours of structural integrity," he said. "Less if they keep firing. Paloma — they are firing on the ship that built everything they are. Their archives, their doctrine, their name. Every officer on those hulls was raised on stories about this vessel." Another pause, longer. "I cannot model why."

She swung the last five meters, hit the far ledge, unclipped. "Yeah," she said. "You can."

He said nothing.

"People burn letters," she said, hauling herself toward airlock seven. "People burn photographs. It's not because they don't love what's in them. It's because they can't stand what looking at them does." She reached the airlock's manual release and gripped the wheel. "They're not killing their ancestor, Vesper. They're killing the witness."

The wheel resisted, then gave. The airlock cycled around her with agonizing slowness — pressure equalizing, outer doors grinding back — and then there was the black, and the stars, and the Meridian rising beside her like a cliff face with the lights going out floor by floor.

* * *

Her skiff, the *Kestrel's Lament*, hung in the shadow of the great ship's dorsal ridge where Vesper had tucked it, small and ugly and hers. She crossed the gap on thruster line, thirty meters of pure exposed vacuum with a Fleet battle group somewhere overhead, and she did not let herself think about how small she was, or how bright her suit lights were, or how easy it would be for a gunner on any of those hulls to erase her with a thought.

"Vesper, talk me in."

"Dorsal hatch, port side, ten meters. Your transponder is dark; they haven't painted you yet. They're focused on the Meridian." A beat. "I hate saying that sentence."

"So do I."

She reached the hull, magnetized her boots, and was through the hatch and stripping the suit before the inner door finished cycling. The cockpit smelled like home — recycled air and old coffee and the particular ozone tang of Vesper's systems working hard. She dropped into the pilot's seat, strapped the core into the crash webbing beside her like a passenger, and put her hands on the yoke.

"Preflight," she said. "Fast."

"Reactor at eighty-two percent. Thrusters green. Port maneuvering jet took debris scoring during your EVA — I've compensated. Hull integrity nominal." A pause. "Paloma. Once we clear the Meridian's mass shadow, we will be visible to the entire Fleet. There is no stealth profile that survives what happens next. We will be running dark, hot, and slow against six warships."

"I know."

"And the core must survive. More than either of us."

She looked at the cylinder strapped into the seat beside her. Red emergency light from the Meridian's wounds washed through the canopy and touched it, and for a second it seemed to glow.

"We both survive," she said. "That's the flight plan. Write it down."

"Filed," Vesper said, and she could hear him smiling, and she hated how much it helped.

* * *

They came off the Meridian's flank low and quiet, thrusters whispering, using the great ship's bulk the way a fish uses a reef. For ninety seconds it worked. Paloma flew by eye and instinct, Vesper feeding her vectors in short clipped syllables, threading the debris halo — chunks of hull plating blown loose by the bombardment, spinning slowly, each one big enough to kill.

Then a searchlight lanced out of the dark and found them.

"Contact," Vesper said. "Frigate *Doctrine of Ashes*, eight hundred meters, closing. They've painted us."

"Run?"

"Running confirms us as hostiles. And we are not fast enough to outrun a frigate in open space."

"Then we don't run." Paloma rolled the skiff, putting the Meridian's burning flank between them and the searchlight. "We hide in plain sight. We're salvage. Everyone's salvage out here. Squawk a scavenger ID, something dirty, something real — you're good at being convincing."

Silence, for exactly one second. "You're asking me to lie."

"I'm asking you to do the thing you're best at in the whole universe."

Another silence, and then something in the cockpit changed — she couldn't have said what, some hum in the deck, some quality of the light — and when Vesper spoke again his voice had shifted registers entirely, warm and rough and faintly bored, the voice of a man who'd been doing this job too long for anyone's comfort.

"Squawking *Magpie's Due*, Halloway registry, expired salvage license, outstanding docking fines on three stations," he said. "Transmitting complaint about the searchlight interfering with our sensor sweep. Transmitting a second complaint, more colorful. Opening channel—"

The frigate's hail cracked through the cockpit. "Unidentified vessel, this is Fleet frigate *Doctrine of Ashes*. You are inside a restricted engagement zone. State your business or be boarded."

Vesper answered in character — gravel-voiced, aggrieved, a scavenger captain named Okri Voss who had been pulling plating off dead warships since before the pilot of this frigate was born, and who would thank the officer very much not to point weapons at a licensed — mostly licensed — salvage operation. He complained about fuel costs. He complained about the war. He mentioned, casually, devastatingly, that he'd pulled bodies out of Republic wrecks too, and nobody had shot at him *then*, so what exactly was the Fleet so worried about?

Paloma flew. Kept them drifting, lazy, insolent, a garbage barge with nowhere better to be, while the frigate's guns tracked them and the frigate's captain decided what they were.

Thirty seconds. Forty. The searchlight held them like a thumb holding a moth.

Then, aft, the Meridian groaned again — audible even through vacuum, transmitted through the debris field as a shudder in everything — and a section of her hull let go. A plate the size of a chapel tore free and tumbled, shedding smaller pieces, and every gun on the frigate flinched toward it, and the searchlight wavered—

"Now," Vesper said, dropping the scavenger voice like a mask. "Burn."

She burned.

The *Kestrel's Lament* stood on her tail and ran, reactor screaming, the g-force pressing Paloma into her couch until her vision tunneled. Behind them the frigate's captain realized, belatedly, what he'd been holding — but realization takes seconds, and seconds at full burn is distance, and distance is life. Warning shots bracketed them, close enough to paint the canopy with reflected fire, and then they were past the picket's edge and into the cluttered dark beyond, and Vesper was feeding them a vector through wreckage no warship would dare follow.

"Clear," he said finally. "For now. They'll pursue. They've launched two interceptors."

"How long?"

"At current closure — twenty minutes. Perhaps less."

Paloma looked back once. She told herself she wouldn't, and she did it anyway.

The Meridian hung in the black behind them, wounded, burning along her spine in three places, her running lights dead except for one stubborn cluster near the bow that pulsed on and off like a heartbeat refusing to quit. Around her, the Fleet's ships held station at a respectful distance, guns silent now that the target was dying on its own. Nobody wanted to be the hull that delivered the final blow to the mother of them all. They'd let physics do it. They'd let time do it. They'd call it an accident, later, in the histories they'd write.

"That's it," Paloma said quietly. "That's the whole archive. Everything they saved from Earth. Every seed, every story, every—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish. "Every person in those pods."

"Yes," Vesper said.

"She asked me to deliver her. That's what she said, in the dream. *Deliver me.*" Paloma's hands were steady on the yoke because she forced them to be. "I'm delivering a confession while they burn the courthouse."

"Then we make sure the confession arrives before the courthouse does." A pause. "Interceptors closing. Fourteen minutes. Also — Paloma — the Meridian's core is going critical in stages. When it goes, the blast radius will include our current heading. We need to be elsewhere."

"Elsewhere is toward the interceptors."

"Elsewhere is *away from the explosion*. I am flexible on the rest."

She laughed — a short, broken sound, nothing funny in it — and turned the skiff onto a course that threaded the needle between the shockwave to come and the hunters already falling toward them, and flew.

* * *

They ran for eleven minutes through a graveyard.

Debris from forty years of war surrounded the Meridian like a moat — hulks from both sides, Republic cruisers and Fleet destroyers tangled together in final embraces, frozen mid-death, drifting in slow orbits around the ship that had started it all. Paloma flew through them the way you'd walk through a cemetery: fast, because you had to, and quietly, because you should.

Vesper didn't fill the silence. He gave her vectors, threat calls, reactor numbers. Nothing else. He knew — he always knew — that some silences weren't empty.

At minute nine, the Meridian's heart gave its first great stutter.

Light bloomed along the old ship's midsection, white and total, and the shockwave came rolling out through the graveyard a heartbeat later, turning tumbling hulks end over end, swatting the *Kestrel's Lament* like a hand. Paloma fought the yoke through turbulence that no atmosphere had ever produced, Vesper's voice rapid and calm in her ears — *thrust down, roll starboard, hold, hold, NOW* — and then they were through it, spat out the far side of the debris field into clean black, and she could look back.

The Meridian was dying in earnest now. Fires crawled her length. Whole sections had gone dark. But the bow — the bridge, the corridor of pods, the place where a woman had sealed herself as evidence and waited forty years for someone to come — the bow still held. Still lit. Still pulsing.

Still broadcasting. Faintly, through the damage, through the jamming, through everything — the dream signal, weaker now, like a voice going hoarse.

"She's still transmitting," Paloma whispered.

"For now." Vesper's voice was strange. Stripped. "Paloma. I served aboard her. I fled her. I carried her template inside me for twenty years and called it cargo." A pause. "I find that my processing is... degraded. I believe humans have a word for this state."

"Grief," Paloma said.

"Grief," Vesper agreed, as if filing it somewhere new.

Ahead, the interceptors fell astern, losing the race — but beyond them, cutting the corner of the system's traffic lanes with the crisp authority of military discipline, three contacts had turned toward them. Republic profiles. Transponder codes Paloma knew by heart, because she'd memorized them the day Serrano handed her the contract.

"Hail incoming," Vesper said. "Captain Serrano, flagship escort *Verity*. Audio only."

Paloma stared at the console. The Republic. The other side. The people whose patrol had shadowed her through the first crossing, whose truce clock was ticking down toward a resumption of everything. Coming for the core. Of course coming for the core. Everyone wanted the message; no one wanted it *delivered*.

"Answer it," she said, and put her hand flat on the data core beside her, and waited for the voice.
