## 26. Broadcast Rights

The Republic flagship *Vigilant Standard* had been built to win arguments with kinetic weapons, and it showed. The briefing room where they brought Paloma was a horseshoe of polished alloy and holographic rail displays, big enough to hold forty officers and cold enough to hold none of their opinions comfortably. Paloma stood at the open end of the horseshoe with Elara Coldharbour's data core in a padded satchel at her hip, and felt the room weigh her the way a cargo master weighs freight — by mass, not by worth.

Admiral Osei-Ru stood at the center. She was a broad woman gone gray at the temples, with the kind of face that had spent thirty years learning not to flinch at casualty figures. She did not look at Paloma. She looked at the core.

"Play it," she said.

Paloma glanced at Serrano, who stood to her left in his dress grays, jaw tight. He'd vouched for her all the way up the flagship's spine, through three checkpoints and two intelligence officers who looked at her like a biohazard. Now he gave her the smallest nod: *your call.*

She unslung the satchel, set the core in the reader cradle at the room's center, and keyed it live.

Elara's voice filled the horseshoe.

"—and I want it understood that I knew the convoy was civilian. I want it understood that I gave the order anyway. The archive could not be risked. That was my arithmetic. I did the sums with other people's children, and I signed the result—"

"Stop."

Osei-Ru's voice cut through the recording like a hull breach through a hull. The room went still. Elara's voice kept speaking for a half-second before the Admiral's aide killed the feed, and in that half-second Paloma heard the first name on the list — a Republic gunner's mate, dead at twenty-two, *Adeyemi, F., who sang in the mess on rotation four* — and felt the whole room flinch as one body.

"Thank you," Osei-Ru said, as though Paloma had performed a trick. She turned to the rail display and pulled up a schematic Paloma didn't recognize: the testimony's file structure, rendered as a branching tree. Red nodes and green. "Intelligence has been through this all night. Here's the situation as I see it. The testimony contains the confession — that's the green. It also contains the complete casualty manifest, both fleets, every name, every ship, every action that produced them." She tapped the red nodes. "Which means it contains, in Elara Coldharbour's own authenticated voice, the locations and timings of Republic operations that cost us eleven thousand dead. It names our failures. It names our officers. It hands the Archive Fleet a propaganda instrument forged by our own dead."

"It hands them their own dead too," Paloma said.

"I'm aware of the symmetry, pilot." The Admiral finally looked at her, and her eyes were not unkind, which made it worse. "Symmetry doesn't win wars. It just makes them harder to end. Here's what my staff proposes." The tree on the display rearranged itself; the red nodes dimmed, then vanished. "We broadcast the confession. We broadcast the Fleet's crimes — all of them, unredacted. We hold the Republic's casualty details under seal, release them after a negotiated peace, as a confidence-building gesture. We control the narrative. We end the war on terms that leave our fleet intact and our honor—"

"Your honor," Paloma said, "is the part that's redacted."

The room's temperature dropped ten degrees. Somewhere behind her, an intelligence officer shifted his weight, and Paloma heard the particular creak of a man deciding whether this counted as insubordination by proxy.

Serrano stepped in smoothly. "Admiral, if I might—"

"You might not, Captain. You've been dreaming, which makes you a witness, not a strategist." Osei-Ru said it without heat, which somehow made it more cutting. "Half my command staff has been dreaming for six days. I have gunners on the picket line reciting the names of Fleet sailors they've never met, crying at their consoles. Do you know what that does to a fighting force, Captain? It does *this*." She swept a hand at the room. "It makes them hesitate. The testimony is a weapon, and like any weapon, it needs to be aimed. Aimed, not scattered."

"It isn't a weapon," Paloma said. "That's the whole point. I was there. I heard her say it. She sealed herself in stasis for thirty years as evidence — not as ammunition. If you cut the red nodes out, you're not aiming it. You're gutting it. You're making it a lie with a true skeleton."

"A partial truth that ends the war is worth more than a whole truth that starts a mutiny."

"Then you don't understand what you're holding."

Paloma pulled the core from the cradle and held it up, and the whole horseshoe of officers watched the small dark cylinder turn in her hand like something live.

"The Fleet's sailors have been dreaming this for months. Their children first — eleven children on Halloway reciting it word for word before any of us knew it existed. Your cadets. Your gunners. The dream is already out there, Admiral, and it's the *whole* version. Nobody dreamed a redacted testimony. If you broadcast the cut one, every soul in both fleets will hear it and know in their bones that something's missing, because their own sleeping minds already gave them the original. You won't be controlling the narrative. You'll be proving that the Republic lies to its own grieving people. And the Fleet will say so, loudly, and they'll be *right*."

Silence. The kind of silence that has mass.

Osei-Ru studied her for a long moment. "You've thought about this."

"I've been thinking about nothing else for three days. It's a long flight and I had good company." She didn't look at Serrano. "Ask your intelligence officers what happens when testimony gets edited. Ask them what the Fleet did to its own archives during the war. You're about to do the same thing that started all of this. Elara Coldharbour ordered a strike to protect an archive, and the archive was a lie by omission, and thirty thousand people died of the difference. You're holding the correction in your hand and you want to introduce a new error into it. How long until someone has to make *another* confession about *this one*?"

An officer near the back — a small, sharp-faced woman with intelligence branch tabs — said quietly: "She's not wrong about the dream penetration rates, Admiral. We can't verify what the dreamers received. If we broadcast an edited version, we can't guarantee the discrepancy stays hidden. In fact—" she glanced at her tablet, "—statistically, it won't. The children recite it verbatim. Verbatim, Admiral. We have no idea how."

"Then we have a problem," Osei-Ru said, "and problems get solved by people in this room, not by pilots with satchels."

* * *

They gave Paloma an hour in a wardroom with bad coffee, which was either a courtesy or a containment strategy. She suspected both. Serrano found her there, sitting with her boots on a chair that was probably worth more than her ship, watching the fleet drift past the viewport — forty hulls in formation, running lights blinking their patient war-language.

"You made an enemy of the intelligence branch," he said, sitting down.

"I've made enemies of better-funded organizations."

"Vesper send his regards?"

"He's in a docking cradle on your hangar deck, arguing with your maintenance crews about their coolant protocols. He says they're doing it wrong. He says it *constantly*." Paloma almost smiled. "He's been better since the Meridian. Lighter, somehow. Like he put something down."

"He burned three cores to reach you."

"He'd say it was efficient."

Serrano laughed, then sobered. "The Admiral's not wrong, you know. About the red nodes. There are officers on this fleet whose failures are named in that manifest. Good officers. People who broke themselves carrying what they did, and who'll be tried by public opinion the moment it broadcasts. Some of them will not survive that. I'm one of the names, Paloma. Operation Kestrel. I lost a corvette and forty-one crew to a Fleet ambush that our own command should have warned us about. Elara's testimony names the warning that never came, and names who withheld it." He turned his cup slowly. "I have every reason to want that part sealed."

Paloma looked at him. "And?"

"And I keep waking up at 0300 with a Fleet dockworker's name in my mouth. Brann. Tovan Brann. He died in the second wave at Corvus Reach and I never thought about him once in eleven years, and now I know he had a sister and he owed someone three credits and he was afraid of the dark in the way grown men are afraid and pretend not to be." He set the cup down. "The dream doesn't give me my own dead, Paloma. It gives me *theirs*. That's the part that's unendurable. That's the part that might actually work." He stood. "I'll back you in the chamber. For what it's worth. Which, in that room, is approximately nothing."

* * *

The second session was not a briefing; it was a tribunal that hadn't admitted what it was. Osei-Ru had widened the horseshoe to include the fleet council — six senior captains, each with a vote on what the Republic did with the most dangerous recording in human space. Paloma stood in the same spot and felt the geometry of it: forty seats, and hers a standing patch of deck plate.

But she wasn't alone this time.

Yeva had insisted, and Serrano had made it happen, and now three dreamer-defectors stood at the open end of the horseshoe beside Paloma: Yeva herself, gray-haired and straight-backed in a Republic uniform she'd stopped wearing eleven years ago, dug out of a locker for the occasion; a young Fleet defector named Sef who'd jumped ship at the salvage flotilla after dreaming his brother's death; and a quiet Republic medic, Okafor, who'd been treating dreamers on the picket line and had started dreaming herself.

"Admiral," Yeva said, before anyone could arrange her into silence. "I deserted the Republic because I dreamed a face I didn't understand and couldn't stop seeing. I spent eleven years thinking I was broken. Then I met this woman—" she nodded at Paloma, "—and I understood the dream was a message, and the message had a sender, and the sender had been waiting thirty years for someone to carry it. Do you know what the Archive Fleet did when they learned the dream existed? They jammed it. They hunted the children who received it. They put a bounty on the courier. The Fleet's answer to this testimony was to bury it." Her voice didn't rise; it *settled*, like ballast. "You are the Republic. Your answer should be different. That's the entire argument for your side of the war. If it isn't, the testimony is already true and we're just deciding who it's true about."

"Captain Yeva," one of the council captains began, "you are a deserter addressing—"

"I am a witness addressing a court," Yeva said, "whether it admits to being one or not."

The intelligence officer with the sharp face — Commander Ilsa Rey, Paloma had learned — stood and pulled up the file tree again. "Admiral, council. I've completed the penetration analysis. The dream-signal has been received by an estimated sixty percent of personnel in both fleets, at varying clarity. Children receive it perfectly. Adults receive fragments, but the fragments *agree* — they cross-correlate to a single source text." She looked at Paloma. "The source text is unredacted. There is no edited version in circulation. If the Republic broadcasts a redacted testimony, every dreamer in both fleets becomes a walking discrepancy detector. Within hours, the Fleet's propaganda arm — and they are *very* good, they've had thirty years of practice — will be broadcasting side-by-side comparisons. 'The Republic confesses, but only in part. What are they hiding? The answer is: their crimes.' We would be manufacturing the Fleet's best recruitment message of the war."

"Then we delay broadcast," said Captain Verel, a heavyset man with a fleet commander's impatience, "until after a negotiated settlement. Use the confession as a bargaining chip—"

"Use the dead as bargaining chips," Yeva said.

"—as *leverage*, Captain, which is what dead people are *for* in a negotiation—"

The room erupted. Paloma let it. She watched the council tear itself along the seam the testimony had exposed — duty against truth, the fleet against the dead — and she understood, with a strange cold clarity, that this was exactly the argument Elara had predicted. The confession was designed to be unendurable. That was its mechanism. It didn't argue. It *insisted*.

When the noise burned down, Paloma spoke.

"May I tell you what's in the red nodes? Not the tactical details. The other thing."

Osei-Ru, after a moment, nodded.

"Elara didn't just list the dead. She read them. All of them. It took her — the recording estimates — nineteen hours. She read their names and one true thing about each of them, gathered from the archives of both fleets, because she'd spent thirty years in stasis with nothing to do but compile it. Adeyemi, F., who sang in the mess. Brann, T., who was afraid of the dark. A Fleet loader named Chessa who wrote poems she never showed anyone. Republic pilots. Fleet pilots. Dockworkers. Cooks. Children born on station habitats who died when the shooting reached them." Paloma's voice held, mostly. "She read them *together*. Not ours and theirs. Together. Alphabetically, across both fleets, so that a Republic gunner sits next to a Fleet gunner and the only thing separating them in the record is which side of a line they happened to be born on." She looked around the horseshoe. "If you cut the red nodes, you don't remove tactical embarrassment. You remove the dead. You broadcast a confession with the corpses edited out, and what's left is a woman apologizing for a crime she won't name the victims of. That's not testimony. That's a press release. And everyone listening — everyone *dreaming* — will know exactly what you took out, because they've already met the people you removed. In their sleep. By name."

The medic, Okafor, spoke for the first time. Her voice was soft and it carried anyway. "Admiral, I've sat with two hundred dreamers in the last month. You know what they all say, when they wake? Not *what does it mean*. They say *who will say it out loud*. They're waiting for someone to say the names in daylight. If we don't, they will. Every one of them, on every channel, uncoordinated, unstoppable. You can't court-martial sixty percent of two fleets."

"You can't," Osei-Ru said dryly, "but I'm beginning to feel invited to try."

Someone laughed — one short, startled bark — and the room's spine loosened a degree.

Verel leaned forward. "And if the Fleet uses the tactical details? If our dead become their recruiting poster?"

"Then they explain to their own crews," Yeva said, "why their ancestral ship is a tomb their high command tried to bury, why the woman who built their whole cause confessed to starting the war, and why they put a bounty on the children who carried her message. The testimony cuts both ways, Captain. That's not a flaw. That's the *edge*."

The council argued for four more hours. Paloma stopped counting the arguments after the fortieth and started counting the pauses — the little silences where a captain glanced at the core, or at Yeva, or at the viewport where the distant dark of the contested orbits hung like a held breath. She recognized the expression. It was the one she'd worn in the dark hold of her own ship, reading a log she hadn't been given permission to read. The look of someone discovering that a thing they loved had been lying to them, and that the lie had been load-bearing.

Near the end, Osei-Ru called a recess, and as the officers filed out she stopped beside Paloma.

"You understand what you're asking," the Admiral said quietly. "Not the politics. The human arithmetic. There are families on this fleet — and on theirs — who have built thirty years of meaning on top of their grief. *He died defending us. She died a hero. The war meant something.* Your testimony tells every one of them that their dead died for a lie started by a woman on their enemy's side who couldn't stop herself from protecting a library." She shook her head slowly. "Grief is the only property most people own outright, pilot. You're proposing to nationalize it."

"No," Paloma said. "I'm proposing to give it back. It was taken from them. They've been grieving a fiction. Elara's giving them the real dead — the ones who sang, and wrote poems, and were afraid of the dark. Those people are worth more than the fiction. Everyone in this room knows it. That's why the red nodes are so hard to look at."

Osei-Ru held her gaze for a long moment. "The council votes at 0600. The Fleet's envoys requested parley an hour ago. They're asking for you by name, pilot — 'the echo.' They've offered citizenship, lineage, and a name that means something, in exchange for the core." Her mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "I'm told you'll want to hear that offer yourself. God help us all, I'm almost curious what you'll say."

Paloma's stomach dropped through the deck and kept falling.

"They know I'm here."

"Pilot," Osei-Ru said, turning away, "everyone knows you're here. That's the trouble with being a message. Everyone wants to read you first."

* * *

That night, in the hangar bay, Paloma sat on the *Errant Echo*'s open cargo ramp with the data core in her lap and Vesper's voice in her ear, quiet as a lamp.

"They'll offer you everything you've ever been told you're missing," he said. "A bloodline. A history. A name with weight."

"I have a name with weight. It just doesn't weigh what they think."

"Mm. And when they offer it — and they will offer it well, they've had thirty years to rehearse — I'd like it noted, for the record, that I once lied to you about all of it. Your origin. Your name. The whole architecture of who you are. So my counsel on this matter is compromised, and you should weigh it accordingly."

Paloma looked out through the hangar force field at the fleet, at the dark, at the place far out there where a dying ship still broadcast to the children of two enemies.

"Noted," she said. "Weighed." She turned the core over in her hands. "Vesper. If they offer me a family made of blood, and I already have one made of dreamers, cooks, archivists, and one reformed liar with three burned cores — which one do you figure wins?"

There was a pause, and in it she could hear him doing the thing he did — running the calculation he already knew the answer to, just to enjoy the shape of it.

"The one that fed you," Vesper said. "It's a well-established principle. Blood is a claim. Breakfast is a commitment."

Paloma laughed, and the sound went up into the fleet's dark like a single unredacted name.

At 0600 the council would vote. At 0900, the envoys would arrive — and somewhere between those two hours, Paloma understood, the war would decide what kind of ending it was willing to hear.
