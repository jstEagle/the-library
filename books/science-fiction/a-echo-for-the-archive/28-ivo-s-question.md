## 28. Ivo's Question

The Republic flagship *Vigilant Meridian*—someone had thought that name was clever once, before anyone understood what the original Meridian had been—had a mess hall designed for four hundred crew and currently held eleven children, two exhausted minders, one defected soldier, and Paloma Coldharbour, who could not remember the last time she had eaten sitting down.

She sat anyway. The bench was bolted to the deck, grey composite, scuffed by decades of boots, and it was the most solid thing she'd touched in weeks.

Across the compartment, the children were eating. That was the miracle of the hour: they were eating, and talking, and one of them—a girl named Sefa with a gap in her teeth—was showing another how to fold a ration wrapper into a bird. The dreamers. Eleven children from Halloway Station who had shared a single dream for months, plus the adults the network had grown since: Yeva at the far table, arms crossed even while she drank her tea, watching the hatch like she expected the war to walk through it; Maret's cousin Brenn, who had joined the flotilla exodus when his niece started reciting pod-corridor geography in her sleep; three salvagers Paloma didn't know by name yet, all of them wearing the particular hollow-eyed calm of people whose nights had stopped belonging to them.

They had been ferried up on Serrano's order, over the objection of half his command staff. *Witnesses*, he'd called them. The council word for it was *assets*. The children didn't care what they were called. They cared that the food was hot and the gravity was steady and nobody was shooting at them yet.

Paloma ate her reconstituted stew and watched Ivo.

He was nine years old, small for it, with the serious face of a child who had been carrying something too heavy for too long and had decided, on balance, not to put it down. He sat at the end of the kids' table, methodically dismantling his protein cube into smaller and smaller pieces, and every few minutes his eyes came up and found hers across the compartment—not staring, exactly. Checking. The way you check a lighthouse.

He'd done it since the shuttle. Since Halloway, if she was honest, since the day he'd grabbed her sleeve in the clinic corridor and said *you're the echo* in a voice like he was telling her the weather.

She looked away first. She always did.

"You should talk to him," Yeva said, settling onto the bench beside her without asking. The old soldier moved like someone who had spent thirty years sleeping in armor—economical, always angled toward the door. "Staring contest's been going six hours. Somebody has to blink."

"He's nine."

"He's the best navigator in this fleet." Yeva said it flatly, no wonder in it anymore. She'd made peace with the absurdity days ago. "During the minefield run, when your AI took the hit—the children here were calling out course corrections in their sleep. Relay team transcribed forty-one corrections. Thirty-eight matched Vesper's actual maneuvers. The other three were better." She sipped her tea. "Ivo called two of those three."

Paloma set down her spoon. "I know. Serrano showed me the transcripts."

"And?"

"And I don't know what to do with a nine-year-old who can see through a jamming field with his eyes closed." It came out more raw than she intended. Yeva raised an eyebrow, and Paloma heard herself, heard the crack under the words, and hated it. "I flew through that field because children were dreaming my flight path aloud to strangers. Do you understand what that does to a person? Every turn I make, some kid on a station light-years back flinches in his sleep. I'm not flying alone anymore. I haven't been alone since the first reroute, and I—" She stopped. Breathed. The mess hall hummed around them, ventilation and low voices and Sefa's paper bird taking its first flight.

"Since the first reroute," Yeva repeated, gently merciless. "You mean since Vesper."

"I mean everything traces back to things people decided for me before I could decide anything. Elara built me. Vesper hid me. The Fleet wants me back. The Republic wants my testimony. Everyone gets a vote except me." She turned the spoon over. "And now there's a kid who dreams where I fly, and I don't know if looking at him feels like family or like just one more person holding a piece of the leash."

Yeva was quiet for a moment. Out the viewport behind her, the slow carousel of the Republic picket line turned against the black, gunships running their patrol loops, and beyond them, far off, the dim ember-glow where the Meridian was dying. Paloma hadn't been able to stop looking at it either. A home she'd never had, burning down by degrees, and half the system arguing about who got to say what over its corpse.

"When I deserted," Yeva said, "I left a sister behind. Republic navy, lifer, true believer. Took me four years to work up the nerve, and when I finally jumped ship at Thessaly Drift, I told myself she'd understand someday. That blood would hold." She turned her cup slowly on the tabletop. "She reported me. Gave them my heading. If a patrol had been ten minutes faster, you'd have never heard of me." She looked up. "Blood is a lottery, girl. I've seen the tickets. What you've got—" she nodded at the children's table, at Ivo carefully arranging his protein fragments into a pattern Paloma realized, with a small cold shock, was the layout of the Meridian's pod corridor, drawn in food, from memory, "—what you've got is better than a lottery. You've got a choice. Only most people never get to make it."

Paloma followed her gaze. Ivo's fingers paused over the corridor pattern. His head came up.

And this time he didn't look away.

He slid off the bench, crossed the mess hall with the unhurried certainty of a child who had already decided, and stopped in front of her. Up close he smelled of recycled air and the cheap soap the Republic used for everything, and there was a smudge of protein dust on his sleeve, and his eyes were the exact grey-brown of station-grown kids everywhere, and Paloma's chest did something complicated and painful.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi yourself."

"You came back." He said it like an accusation and a fact at once. "At the salvage flotilla. When the bounty hunter boarded. You could have taken the shuttle and run. Maret told us. She tells everybody." He shifted his weight. "You came back through the cargo bay instead. For the ship."

"For Vesper," Paloma said.

"No." Ivo shook his head, patient, the way adults were patient with him and he'd learned to return the favor. "For the ship *and* for us. Because we were docked there. Because if you ran, the hunter would've gone through the flotilla looking for dreamers next. You knew that." He held up a hand before she could speak. "Vesper told Maret. Maret told everyone. We know about the cores he burned. We know about the jamming punch-through. We dreamed it." His voice dropped, went careful. "We dreamed you in the dark corridors with the thing chasing you, and we dreamed the voice coming through the walls saying *you were never my cargo*, and then we woke up and you were alive, so we knew it worked."

Paloma's throat had gone tight. Around them, the mess hall had gone quieter—not silent, but listening, the way rooms full of dreamers listened now, attuned to each other's gravity.

"Ivo," she managed, "what do you want to ask me? I can see you winding up."

He took a breath. Nine years old, and he took a breath like a man stepping off a ledge.

"Are you my family now?"

The question landed in the middle of the table between them, small and enormous, and Paloma felt every prepared answer she owned crumble to dust. *It's complicated. I'm not sure what I am. There's a woman in stasis whose face is mine. There's a contract upstairs with my bloodline written into it.* All true. All beside the point. Because he wasn't asking about bloodlines or genetics or the political disposition of the Coldharbour template. He was asking the only question that mattered, the one underneath every other question she'd been drowning in for weeks, and he was asking it with the terrible directness of a child who had learned from the dream that families could be sealed in pods and erased from registries and burned into archive cores, and who wanted, needed, to know whether the woman standing in front of him was going to be one more person who disappeared.

She thought about Dree Okonkwo's archive room, the registry seal glowing red, *no parents listed*. She thought about Elara's recorded confession, the guilt folded into every syllable. She thought about the Fleet envoy's offer still waiting upstairs in a secure folder—citizenship, lineage, a name that meant something—and the honest, shameful warmth she'd felt reading it, the pull of a self handed to you whole.

And she thought about coming back through the cargo bay. About Maret's kitchen. About Yeva's hand-drawn charts. About Vesper's voice punching through jamming with three cores burning: *you were never my cargo, you were my crew.*

She had answered the question already. Weeks ago. In a cargo bay full of smoke, in a kitchen on a scavenger flotilla, in the decision to fly toward the thing hunting her instead of away. She'd just never said it out loud, because saying it out loud meant it could be taken away.

"Ivo," she said, and crouched down so they were eye to eye, because some answers you don't deliver from above. "When I was little—I don't know how little, that's part of the lie I'm still unwinding—but when I was small, somebody taught me that family is whoever's in the escape pod with you. Not whoever's on the manifest. Whoever's actually in the pod." She held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer something to a stray you want to trust you. "You dreamed me through a minefield. You navigated me past guns that were trying to kill me. When I was lost in the dark on that ship, your voice is the one that found me—you and the others. So yeah." Her voice cracked and she let it. "Yeah. I'm your family now. If you'll have me. Which is the same question pointed the other way, and I'm asking it."

Ivo looked at her hand. Then he did something that undid her completely: he didn't take it. He stepped forward and hugged her, both arms, face pressed into her jacket, the fierce bone-crushing grip of a child who had been dreaming of losing people and had woken up every morning surprised to find the world still holding.

Paloma wrapped her arms around him and held on, and over his shoulder she saw the mess hall watching—Sefa with her paper bird forgotten in her hands, the salvagers, Brenn wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand and pretending he wasn't, Yeva with an expression she would deny under oath—and she saw, at the hatch, Captain Serrano, arrived silently at some point, standing very still, his cadet's dream-fragments no doubt fresh in his mind, giving her a single nod that carried more weight than any contract he'd ever offered her.

*Family,* she thought. *Chosen, declared, witnessed. Filed nowhere. Unrevocable.*

Ivo pulled back eventually, scrubbed his face with his sleeve, and restored his serious expression with visible effort. "Okay," he said. "Then you have to come to dinner when we go home. Maret's making the good noodles. She said."

"She said."

"For family. She said family gets the good noodles and everyone else gets the regular ones." He glanced toward the viewport, toward the distant ember of the dying Meridian, and his voice went quiet and old. "Is the lady in the ship going to be okay? The first one. The one who sounds like you."

Paloma followed his gaze. Elara Coldharbour, sealed in her tomb of evidence, her testimony now the most contested object in the system. "No," she said honestly, because he deserved honesty more than comfort. "But she's going to be heard. That's what she wanted. And we're going to make sure of it—all of it, every name, nothing cut."

"That's what the dream says too." Ivo nodded, satisfied, as if the dream and Paloma agreeing settled the matter legally. Then his expression flickered, and he tugged her sleeve—the same gesture, the same grip, from the first day in the clinic corridor. "There's one more thing. I wasn't going to say it in front of the officers."

Paloma leaned in. "Go on."

"The Fleet sent another message while we were docking. The dream showed me the envoy's face before I ever saw her. She's the one from the corridor in the dream—the one who walks past the pods and doesn't look in." His eyes came up to hers, wide and certain. "She's going to offer you everything tonight. And the dream says whatever you answer, you have to answer it in front of everyone. Not in a room. In front of everyone. Because they're all going to pretend it's private, and it isn't. It never was."

The mess hall hummed around them. Somewhere above, in a secure briefing room, an Archive Fleet envoy was waiting with a contract bearing Paloma's genetic lineage and a name older than the war.

"Okay," Paloma said, and stood, and felt the ground settle under her feet for the first time in weeks. "Then let's not keep her waiting."

* * *

The envoy's name was Sereth Vahl, and she received Paloma in a room with excellent lighting and no windows, which told Paloma everything before a word was spoken.

"Paloma Coldharbour." Vahl pronounced the surname with reverence, the way you handle an artifact. She was silver-haired, immaculate, and wore the Archive Fleet's deep blue like it had been tailored onto her at birth—which, Paloma reflected, it probably had. "Or should I say, daughter of the Meridian. Heir of the First Archive. There are records older than the war that have been waiting for you."

"There's a contract waiting for me," Paloma said. "Let's skip to it."

Something flickered in Vahl's composure—approval, maybe, or appetite. She slid a folio across the table. "Citizenship. Full lineage restoration. Your genetic legacy, your inheritance, your history—everything that was taken from you, returned. A name that means something in every port in Fleet space." She folded her hands. "In exchange, the core stays with us. The testimony is archived properly, by professionals, with appropriate context. You'd be amazed how much suffering careless truth causes."

Behind the bulkhead, faintly, Paloma could hear the children being escorted to their quarters. Ivo's voice, high and bright, explaining something about noodles.

She thought of a cargo bay full of smoke. Of a kitchen that smelled of real spices. Of a boy who asked the hardest question in the system and accepted the answer with his whole body.

She picked up the folio.

"In front of everyone," she said quietly, and Vahl's smile faltered, just slightly, as if the dream had reached even here.
