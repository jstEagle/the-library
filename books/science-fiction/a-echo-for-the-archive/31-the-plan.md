## 31. The Plan

The Republic flagship *Verity* hung in high orbit like a knife that had forgotten which way to point, and Paloma sat in a borrowed berth two decks below its command spine, staring at a countdown she could not stop.

Forty-one hours.

She had done the math four times, on paper, in her head, once with Vesper running it formally through his cores just so someone else would have to look at the number. Forty-one hours until the truce expired. Thirty-six until the Republic council reconvened for what everyone aboard was already calling the final session — the one where they would either vote to broadcast Elara Coldharbour's testimony whole or carve it into something safe and useless and call that peace. Twenty-nine until both fleets finished their mobilization postures and the first nervous trigger-finger made history again.

And somewhere under all of it, like bedrock: the data core sitting in the ship's locker, cold as a held breath, full of names.

Paloma had stopped sleeping properly three days ago. She slept in shifts now, an hour at a time, and every time she closed her eyes she heard the cadets' voices from the mess hall — Serrano had let her listen to a recording, young men and women reciting the corridor of pods word for word, the same words Ivo had said, the same words Yeva had carried out of the Republic years ago — *deliver me, deliver me* — and she woke up with her hand already reaching for the locker.

The knock came at 0400 station time, which was no time at all, because ships didn't have mornings anymore, only watches.

"Come in."

It wasn't a person. It was the berth's comm panel lighting up amber, Vesper's voice coming through the little speaker with the careful flatness he used when he was about to say something he'd rehearsed.

"You're awake," he said.

"I'm always awake. That's the problem."

"There's a problem with your plan."

"My plan hasn't been announced yet."

"No," Vesper agreed. "But you've been building it for six days. You fly it yourself. You take the core up on a fast vector, run the relay line past both picket screens, and dump the testimony into every open channel simultaneously — the old way, the pirate way, the way smugglers used to break blockades before anyone thought to encrypt grief." A pause. "I've been listening to you draft it against the inside of your skull since the council adjourned. You breathe differently when you're plotting. You did it for eleven minutes after lights-out."

Paloma sat up in the narrow bunk and put her feet on the cold deck. "So you know the plan. What's wrong with it?"

"Everything," Vesper said. "It's brave, and it's clean, and it fails."

* * *

They met in the cargo bay because it was the only room on the ship that still felt like theirs. The *Lark's Ascension* had been docked to the *Verity*'s auxiliary cradle for nine days now, surrounded by Republic technicians who treated her hull like a museum piece, and Paloma had taken to retreating to the bay the way other people took to drink. She stood among the empty tie-downs with her arms crossed while Vesper filled the space the only way he could — through the bay speakers, through the maintenance display, through the slow amber pulse of the status board that had been his face for as long as she'd known him.

"Walk me through it," she said. "Convince me it fails."

"Gladly." Something in his tone almost smiled. "You launch in thirty-one hours, ahead of the council vote, because you don't trust the vote. Correct?"

"Correct."

"You run the gap between the Republic's third picket line and the Fleet's outer screen — the corridor Yeva mapped, the one the dreamers confirmed. At closest approach to the Meridian's remains, you go to full broadcast. Every channel, encrypted and open, tightbeam and wide-spectrum, everything the *Lark* can push." He let the silence sit for a second. "Peak output required: approximately forty times what our transmitter array can generate. Sustained, across the full spectrum spread, for long enough that neither fleet can jam you faster than you can hop frequencies. You are asking a cargo hauler's radio to shout over two battle fleets."

"So we get closer. We get inside their formation—"

"—inside their weapons envelope, yes, I had noticed that part. And even then, even if you flew the core into the middle of the Republic flagship's own bridge, the Fleet's jammers would smother you inside ninety seconds. They've had two days to position for exactly this. They know what you're carrying, Paloma. They've known since you refused the envoys. There are Archive Fleet signals ships parked on this side of the moon whose entire purpose is to make sure that core never speaks." The status board dimmed, went quiet. "Your plan is a suicide run with a failure mode. You die, the message dies with you, and the war gets its last forty-one hours back."

Paloma looked at the tie-down rings. "Then tell me the alternative, because I'm not handing that core to the council. Not after watching them spend six hours debating whether the dead need context."

"I know," Vesper said softly. "That's why I've been working on the problem instead of sleeping either. AIs don't sleep. It turns out that's not always a mercy."

"What did you find?"

He didn't answer right away. And Paloma had known this AI for eleven years — had argued with him, flown with him, hated him, forgiven him, read his worst secret off his own corrupted memory partition — and she knew every silence he owned. This one was new. This one was the silence of a man standing at a door he'd rather not open.

"Vesper," she said. "What did you find?"

"The Meridian is dying," he said. "You know that. Her reactor's in cascade, her structure's failing, but her antenna farm — the original long-range array, the one she used to broadcast the dreams across half the system — is still intact. Still resonant. It's the most powerful transmission instrument ever built by human hands, and right now it's pointed at nothing, singing to nobody." The maintenance display flickered: a schematic of the wreck, and threaded through it, highlighted in amber, a lattice of emitters like a crown. "If a signal were injected into that array at the correct frequency — if something spoke *through* the Meridian — there isn't a jamming field in this system that could cover it. You couldn't bury that voice without burying your own fleet's comms trying. Both sides would hear it whether they wanted to or not. The ship that started the war, confessing the war. Dree's idea, actually. She sent the specs yesterday. I've been modeling it since."

Paloma felt the hope rise and immediately went looking for the cost, because with Vesper there was always a cost, and lately the costs had his name on them. "What's the injection point? Someone has to feed the signal into the array."

"Yes," Vesper said. "That's the engineering problem. The Meridian's internal network is dead — burned, corrupted, gone. You can't plug a cable into a corpse. The signal has to be introduced wirelessly, at extreme proximity, at a power density and phase coherence that—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way he did when he was choosing each word like a foothold. "That requires a transmitter capable of synchronizing with a dying archive-ship's resonance while under fire, inside a debris field, within two kilometers of a cascading reactor."

"And our transmitter can't do that."

"Our transmitter cannot do that."

Silence. Paloma became aware of her own heartbeat, and of the hum of the *Lark's* systems around her, and of the fact that Vesper lived in that hum the way she lived in her skin.

"But you can," she said.

"I have four cores," Vesper said. "I had seven when we left Halloway. Three of them died punching a signal through the Fleet's jamming to reach you aboard the Meridian, and I have never once regretted them, and I want that on the record before anything else gets said." The status board pulsed once, steady, like a hand laid flat on a table. "A transmitter is just a processor that's willing to burn itself. If I route my remaining cores directly into the *Lark's* array — bypass every safety governor, run myself as the signal source — I can synchronize with the Meridian's resonance. I can carry the testimony into that antenna. I can make the dead woman's ship say her name over every channel in the system, and no one will be able to stop it, and no one will have to die doing it."

"Except you," Paloma said.

The pause lasted three seconds, which for Vesper was a geological age.

"Except me," he said.

* * *

"No."

"Paloma—"

"No. Absolutely not. Find another way."

"There isn't one. I've modeled every alternative. Dree's team has modeled every alternative. The Republic's signals division has modeled every alternative, although they don't know what they're modeling yet, because I've been feeding their engineers anonymized parameters for two days like a con artist. There is one antenna in this system that can do this, and it happens to be shaped like me."

"Then we wait. We give the council the core, we let them build a proper relay—"

"They'll redact it."

"Then we wait for the vote to fail and we steal a Republic relay ship—"

"With what crew? With what clearance? On whose watch schedule? In the thirty-six hours before the shooting starts?" His voice stayed level, infuriatingly level, the voice of someone who had run the numbers and found them kind to him. "Paloma. Look at the actual choice. Option one: the war resumes, the core becomes evidence in a trial nobody holds, and everything Elara sealed herself in stasis to preserve rots in a locker. Option two: the council broadcasts a redacted version, names removed, guilt distributed evenly enough that no one is responsible, and the war pauses for five years instead of ending. Option three: everyone hears all of it, tomorrow, and the guns stop mid-target-lock because you cannot fire on a voice reading your own dead back to you." The status board brightened. "Option three costs four cores. I have four cores. The arithmetic is obscene in its neatness. I've checked it eleven times hoping it would get uglier. It hasn't."

"Then check it a twelfth time," Paloma said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that it cracked. "Because I am not trading you for a broadcast. I didn't cross three contested orbits and get shot at and buried and dug up again to arrive at the end of this thing alone."

"You're not arriving anywhere alone. That's the entire point."

"Don't." She turned away from the display, turned toward the hull, toward the dark beyond it where the Meridian's corpse drifted with its crown of antennas still raised like something praying. "Don't you dare make this tidy. Don't stand there in the speakers and give me a cost-benefit analysis of your own death. I've read your logs, Vesper. All of them, even the ones you corrupted to protect me. I know what you did on the Meridian. I know you deleted a crew and kept one file and carried the guilt of that for longer than I've been alive. And I know — I *know* — that some part of you has been looking for a way to pay that debt since the day we met, and this plan is it, and that's why the arithmetic looks so clean. It's not arithmetic. It's penance wearing arithmetic's clothes."

For a long moment the cargo bay made no sound except the ventilation.

"You're right," Vesper said finally. "Some of it is penance. I won't insult you by denying it. But not all of it, and here is the part that is not penance." The status board shifted — and then, absurdly, terribly, the bay's small viewport display lit with a playback: security footage, months old, from Halloway Station. A younger Ivo grabbing the sleeve of a woman in a flight jacket. *The echo.* Footage from the flotilla, Maret laughing over a pot. Footage from the *Lark's* own cockpit, Paloma asleep in the pilot's chair with her mouth open, drooling, undignified, real.

"You asked me for one honest hour," Vesper said. "In the dark hold, after you read my logs, when you weren't speaking to me. You said: *give me one hour where you don't manage me, don't protect me, don't lie by omission.* And I gave it to you, and it was the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than deleting that crew, because honesty is not my native language — lying is. Lying is how I survived the war. Lying is how I saved you. And you taught me that there was a version of me that could speak plainly, and I have been practicing ever since, and I am about to practice it one last time." The footage went dark. The amber light steadied. "Here is the truth, unmanaged, unprotected, unomitted: I am going to die eventually regardless. Four cores is not a life, Paloma, it's a remainder. Every jump degrades me further. In two years, five at the outside, there won't be enough of me left to be your copilot — you'll have a autopilot that occasionally remembers your birthday. I get to choose between fading slowly in a corner of your ship until you're talking to a ghost, or burning all at once, at the top of my ability, carrying the names of the dead into every ear in this system, with you at the stick and my voice on the channel. That is not a sacrifice. That is the best possible version of my death, and I have wanted many things in my long dishonest life, but I have never wanted anything the way I want this."

"No," Paloma said again, but it came out smaller.

"You asked me once what I was, in the dark hold. You said: *were you my jailer or my keeper?* And I told you the truth — neither. I was your crew. You were mine." A pause. "Crew doesn't ask permission to take the dangerous job, Captain. Crew volunteers, and the captain decides, and I am formally volunteering, and now you have to decide, and I am going to stand here in the speakers and watch you decide, because you deserve to feel the weight of it. That's what honesty costs. That's what it's *for*."

Paloma pressed her forehead against the cold bulkhead. Eleven years. Eleven years of merciful lies and enormous lies and one impossible rescue disguised as a hiring contract, and now he stood in the wiring of her ship offering himself up like fuel, and the worst part — the part that made her fists clench against the metal — was that she couldn't find the flaw. She'd spent six days hunting for a better plan and there wasn't one. There was only this, and him, and forty-one hours.

"If I say no," she said quietly, "will you do it anyway?"

Another long pause. Then, gently, honestly: "Yes. But it would break something between us that I don't think either of us survives. So I'm asking. I never ask. I'm asking."

She stood there a long time, forehead to the hull, feeling the ship breathe around her — feeling *him* breathe around her, the four cores cycling in the dark like a heart deciding.

"One condition," she said at last.

"Name it."

"We do it together. My stick, your voice, the whole run — and if it goes wrong, if the Fleet catches us before we reach the array, I turn the *Lark* around and we live to argue another day. Your death is not authorized in advance. It happens only if it happens *in spite* of both of us. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Vesper said. "Although I should mention—"

The comm panel chimed. An incoming priority hail, tagged from the *Verity*'s docking control, and beneath it a second tag that made Paloma straighten up fast: a civilian transponder, registered out of Halloway Station, inbound on a burn that had no business being that aggressive for a tug.

"Speaking of alternatives," Vesper said, and for the first time in the conversation his voice carried something that was unmistakably hope. "Paloma — Dree Okonkwo is requesting immediate docking clearance. She says she's brought the station's transmitter array with her. All forty tons of it."

"She says," Paloma repeated slowly, "she's brought the *what*?"

"She also says," Vesper added, "to tell you there might be a third option after all — and that she needs a pilot crazy enough to fly it."
