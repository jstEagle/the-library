## 7. Deserter's Chorus

Sefa came aboard the *Long Route* like a tide coming in — slowly, inevitably, and rearranging everything she touched.

She was seventy if she was a day, ship-born, with hands that had spent a lifetime touching old paper and eyes that had spent it reading faces. She carried one bag. It contained, she informed me, forty years of linguistic notes, three changes of clothes, and a jar of preserved plums she refused to discuss.

"The archive at Vesper," she said, settling into the co-pilot's chair as though she'd owned it since launch. "The black-market one. The one run by the man who calls himself the Librarian."

"You've heard of him."

"I've heard of everyone. That's what being a linguist means." She opened the jar of plums, offered me one, and when I hesitated, ate two herself with the serene entitlement of a woman who had outlived every person who might have told her no.

We were three days out of the dead zone, running dark along the freight lanes where my transponder read as a seed-hauler because I had, technically, not yet stopped being one — license revoked, but the hull didn't know that. The garden module behind me still smelled of green things. I'd stopped going back there. It hurt less to pretend it was just cargo.

"So," Sefa said, sucking a plum stone thoughtfully. "Tell me about this Librarian."

"He buys and sells data outside both colonies' licensing systems. Pre-Severance archives, mostly. Old maps, defunct codes. If anyone on Vesper can authenticate the Halcyon's ark records against an independent Earth-era source, it's him."

"And you trust him?"

"I trust that he's greedy," I said. "Greed is more reliable than loyalty. Loyalty has terms and conditions."

Sefa looked at me for a long moment, the way she did when she was deciding whether to correct my grammar or my worldview. "On the ship," she said finally, "we have a word for people who think like that. We say they are *still waiting for the Severance to be undone.*" She patted my knee with her dry little hand. "You'll learn better words. You're surrounded by teachers now."

I hadn't told her about the others yet. Mostly because, until forty-eight hours ago, neither had I.

* * *

It started with a distress ping off a derelict mining platform in the Vesper approach corridor — except the ping wasn't standard, wasn't licensed, and used a handshake protocol I recognized from Meridian military surplus. Someone hiding. Someone with equipment that would get them shot on either side of the line.

I should have run dark past it. Instead I found myself decelerating, Sefa watching me with open delight.

"You see?" she said. "Already learning."

The airlock cycled and a woman came through it sideways, the way people do when they expect to be shot. She was Vesperan, tall, hollow-cheeked, wearing a salvaged pressure suit with the colony insignia burned off. Behind her shuffled four more figures — two Vesperan, two Meridian, all of them wearing the particular exhaustion of people who had been deserting for a long time and were bad at it.

"We saw your ship on the passive scopes," the woman said. "Seed-hauler, unregistered route, running cold. Figured you were either smugglers or idiots." Her eyes flicked to Sefa, then back to me. "Please be smugglers."

"Coral Reyes," I said. "Formerly of the Meridian Terraforming Corps. Currently of nowhere."

"Tamsin Okafor. Formerly of the Vesper Orbital Guard. Currently also of nowhere." She looked at her ragged crew, then at me, and something in her face shifted — the calculation dropping away, leaving something rawer underneath. "We've been drifting on that platform for six weeks. Air recycler's dying. We heard about you."

"Heard about me?"

"Heard there's a hauler picking up strays along the corridor. A ghost route. People said it was a story." She almost smiled. "Stories travel fast when nobody official is listening."

Behind her, one of the Meridian deserters — a big, soft-spoken kid who introduced himself only as Brannic — looked past me into the corridor and went very still.

"What's that smell?" he asked.

"Garden module," I said. "Hydroponics. Seed-stock."

He walked toward it like a sleepwalker. I followed, and found him standing among the racks with his hand hovering over a seedling tray, not quite touching, the way you'd approach something holy or explosive.

"I worked agri-domes on Meridian," he said quietly. "Before they reassigned me to patrol support. Before—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I used to grow things. For people who'd never met them."

I knew exactly what he meant. I'd spent eleven years doing the same thing, tending gardens for colonists still in their gestation tanks, loving people who didn't exist yet. Standing next to this stranger who'd fled the other side of a war I'd never fought, I felt the strange vertigo of recognition.

"They reassigned you too," I said.

"They reassigned everyone. That's how you know it's a war and not a disagreement." He turned to me, and his eyes were wet and furious. "Is there work on this ship? For someone who knows his way around a nutrient feed?"

"There's work," I said. "There's not much pay."

"I wasn't getting paid anyway. They just took credits out of my account and called it service."

And that was how it went, over the following days. Tamsin and her people stayed. Word moved along the ghost routes the way word does among people with nothing left to lose — and the *Long Route*, which had been a coffin with an engine, started becoming something else.

A Vesperan comms tech named Odile who could crack any encryption older than she was and most newer. A Meridian medic called Pem who'd walked away from a fleet hospital rather than keep triaging "expendable personnel." Two more, then three. Every one of them deserters, every one of them from one side or the other, and every one of them staring across my galley table at someone from the enemy colony and finding — dinner companions. Complainers. People who snored.

"You know what's funny," Tamsin said one night, feet up on my console, eating the stew Pem had improvised from garden greens and Sefa's suspiciously hoarded protein stock. "Six years in the Guard, and I never once saw a Meridian soldier. Just targets. Contacts. Blips." She pointed her spoon at Brannic. "You're a terrible blip."

"You're a worse one," he said placidly. "Pass the salt."

Sefa sat at the head of the table like a matriarch holding court, correcting our pronunciation of Old Colonial curse words between bites. "No, no — *kessa* is not 'damn,' it is 'the moment before damn.' Your ancestors had finer gradations of anger than you do. Be worthy of them."

"Are we worthy of the plums?" Odile asked.

"No one is worthy of the plums."

It was, I realized somewhere in the second week, the first table I'd ever sat at where both colonies were represented and nobody was performing anything. No founding myths. No enemy. Just eight people and a dying garden and a jar of plums, heading toward Vesper with proof that could end a war or start a massacre, depending entirely on who got to it first.

I should have been terrified. I was, mostly. But underneath the terror was something I hadn't felt since the tanks: the specific warmth of feeding people who will remember the meal.

* * *

Renn joined us at a fueling station on the outer lane — a wiry, quick-eyed Meridian deserter who came recommended by a smuggler contact of Odile's. He was good. That was the thing I kept coming back to afterward, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it. He was genuinely good. Quiet, competent, useful with engines and better with people. He fixed the number-three thruster that had been grumbling since Meridian. He played cards with Brannic and lost graciously. He sat with Sefa while she catalogued her notes and asked real questions about Old Colonial grammar, and she lit up like a filament answering him, because teachers are starved creatures too.

"You like him," Sefa observed, the night before we made the final burn for Vesper.

"I do."

"Mm." She sucked a plum stone. "I liked my brother. Right up until the council voted to stay hidden forever and he voted with the hawks. Liking is not a security system, Coral."

"It's all anybody's got."

"That," said Sefa, "is what the hawks are counting on."

I should have listened harder. But we were close now — Vesper a hard blue coin in the viewport, the black-market archive two jumps from reach, ninety days on Earth's loop burned down to fifty-one — and I was drunk on the arithmetic of almost-done. One more crew member. One more burn. One more lie at customs, and Sefa's forty years of notes would be sitting next to an independent Earth-era record, and the truth would have a second witness.

The ambush came at the edge of Vesper's customs shadow, where the patrol cutters don't fly colors because they aren't supposed to exist.

They came out of a debris field in perfect formation — two cutters, running silent, painting us with targeting lasers before our scopes even warmed up. Not customs. Customs knocks first.

"Boarding party incoming," Tamsin snapped, already moving. "That's a Meridian interdiction pattern. How did they—"

The comm channel opened. A voice, flat and official: "*Vessel Long Route, you are carrying unauthorized personnel and restricted archival material. Surrender your passengers and you will be permitted to depart.*"

My blood went cold in a very orderly fashion, item by item. Unauthorized personnel. Restricted archival material. Nobody knew about Sefa. Nobody knew about the notes. Nobody except—

Renn was already at the airlock controls. He didn't look surprised. He didn't look guilty, either, which was somehow worse. He looked like a man completing a transaction.

"They offered me something," he said, over the wail of the proximity alarm. "I want you to understand that it wasn't credits."

"Renn—" Brannic started toward him, and Renn's hand came up, not with a weapon, just a gesture, *stop*, and Brannic stopped, because whatever Renn believed, he believed it completely, and complete belief reads as authority.

"They told me what happens if your message gets out," Renn said. "Both colonies have ship-descendants hiding in their populations, Coral. Generations of them. Assimilated, undocumented, invisible. And the moment both governments admit those people exist — that *we* are those people — do you know what happens? Panic. Purges. *Where do they hide? Who are they?* My grandmother was ship-born. Do you understand? I'm not betraying you. I'm trying to stop you from signing her death warrant."

The cutters were closing. Tamsin was shouting about evasive burns. Sefa stood calmly in the middle of the chaos, holding her bag of notes against her chest like a child, looking at Renn with an expression I couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition.

"Coral," she said quietly, under the alarms. "The loop. Fifty-one days. Whatever happens in the next ten minutes — the message doesn't die with us. You understand that. It can't."

The airlock light went amber. Renn's hand rested on the release.

And I made my choice.
