## 22. Dreaming in Kessari

The long route began, as it always did, at Meridian Reach, but Meridian Reach was no longer a port of quiet.

Silas stood at the cargo master's console on the Echo's Wake and watched the docking chatter scroll past in six languages—fleet Standard, of course, but also Low Kessari pidgin, the trade-tongue of the Halcyon Drift, and, unmistakably, Kessari itself, the old colony language rising out of the port traffic like water finding its way back into a dry riverbed. A tug pilot was arguing with a berth controller in it. Not performing it, not reciting it for a heritage festival, but *arguing* in it, sloppily, joyfully, dropping conjugations the way people drop them when they are tired and annoyed and utterly unafraid of being heard.

Silas read the exchange twice. Then a third time, because the third time was not for comprehension but for the pleasure of it, the way one might listen to a song.

*Berth nine is flooded with light cargo, you great drifting hulk, and you know it.*

*Then give me berth ten and stop pretending your manifest system is a religion.*

Silas laughed aloud in the empty hold, and the sound startled them. They could not remember the last time they had laughed alone in this ship before the relay. There had been no shortage of solitude in the years of the silence—solitude had been the whole architecture of their life, load-bearing, carefully maintained—but there had never been this particular kind, the kind that comes after, when the silence is over and the solitude is only a choice rather than a hiding place.

The Echo's Wake was carrying a light load this run. Machine parts for the agri-collectives on Thessaly's Reach, a crate of archival film for the new records house at Meridian—*the* new records house, the one that had opened in the old censor's office, which everyone in port still called the Censor's Office out of habit and which no one called that without a small, private smile. And, bolted into the linguistic core where the fleet's listening post had once tried to read it, the fragment Vey had carried in a cargo pod across half the system: the sealed colony records, now declassified, now merely *records*, which was somehow the more astonishing word.

Vey had disembarked at Meridian three weeks ago. She had an archive to build and a generation of archivists to train and a name that was no longer a thing to be whispered. She had stood at the bottom of the Echo's Wake's boarding ramp with her arms full of data spools and said, in Kessari, *Come back through here often. I'll want to know what the worlds are saying.*

Silas had answered in Kessari too. It had felt, for the first time in their life, like the language was not a room they were locked inside but a door they could walk through in either direction.

Now the ship lifted from Meridian Reach on a morning that smelled, through the intake scrubbers, of rain on the port terraces—actual rain, unregulated, falling on a world that had begun, haltingly, to speak—and Silas set a course for the long route.

* * *

The long route was not the fastest way anywhere. It was the way that touched every world the fleet had once silenced, threading between them like a needle through the stitches of a wound, and Silas had flown it for eleven years because it was the only route where invisibility was possible: dead ports, dead frequencies, worlds that answered hails in Standard or not at all, their own languages locked behind the Silence Accord like patients in a ward with the doors painted over.

Silas flew it now because it was beautiful.

That was the word that kept arriving, unbidden, at the edges of every watch shift. *Beautiful.* The long route had always been lonely, and loneliness had been armor, and armor does not let you see what you are protecting. Now, with the armor set down, Silas saw the route itself for the first time: the amber arc of Thessaly's Reach against its ring-shadow, the fishing lights on Cael Undine coming up out of the dark like a constellation deciding to be a city, the slow green weather of the terraformed valleys on Meridian's southern continent where, the port chatter said, the first Kessari-language school in forty years had opened in a converted grain silo.

And the worlds answered.

That was the thing. That was the thing that Silas could not stop turning over, watch after watch, the way one turns a stone in the hand. When the Echo's Wake came into orbit above Cael Undine and made the standard traffic declaration, the harbor master came back not in fleet Standard but in Undine trade-sign and spoken Kessari both, the old bilingual cadence that the fleet's censors had spent a decade scrubbing from the archives, preserved instead—Silas had learned this from Vey, who had learned it from the sealed records—in the mouths of grandmothers who had never stopped talking to their grandchildren in a language with no official existence. The harbor master was maybe sixty. She would have been a child when the Accord was signed. She had kept the language alive the way one keeps a fire in a closed room: illegally, carefully, and with a ferocity that the fleet had never thought to look for because the fleet had never believed a language could be loved that much.

*Echo's Wake, you are cleared for berth four,* she said, and then, in Kessari, almost shyly, *Welcome home, freighter. We heard you.*

*We heard you.* Silas had turned the phrase over for the whole descent. It was the standard courtesy, the formula every port used, but it landed differently now, in a system that had spent forty years not listening. *We heard you.* As if the whole world were apologizing, gently, in the only way it could.

Silas found they were doing it too. At Thessaly's Reach, when a dockhand called up a greeting in Low Kessari, Silas answered in kind, and the dockhand's whoop of delight came through the comm raw and unfiltered. At the Halcyon Drift, where Silas's transponder was still officially burned and the Echo's Wake was still officially lost, the station master looked at the ship's registry, looked at Silas, and said, in the drift pidgin, *Lost ships find their way here all the time. You want fuel or a story first?* And Silas had paid for the fuel and told the story, the whole impossible arc of it—the nine seconds of falling, the stowaway, the relay at the edge of dead space, the nine seconds of speaking—and by the end there were a dozen drifters crowded into the station galley, and three of them were crying, and one old woman took Silas's hand and said, in Kessari, *My mother sang to me in that language. I thought I was the last one who could hear it.*

*You were never the last,* Silas had answered, and heard, in their own mouth, the cadence of the relay's broadcast, the colony's final testimony, the sentence that had been truncated mid-word for forty years and that Silas had completed at the edge of dead space with a voice that shook. *You were the seed. The last ones are never the last. They are the first ones of whatever comes next.*

Silas did not know where the sentence had come from. It had arrived whole, the way the dreams arrived whole, and Silas had learned—was still learning—to stop asking where.

* * *

The dreams had quieted.

That was the strangest part, stranger than the answering worlds, stranger even than the hearings. During the long route south of the relay, in the weeks after the broadcast, the dreams had been loud—full sentences in Kessari, addressed to Silas by name, the language living in them like a tenant with opinions. Silas had confessed as much to Vey in the last days before the relay, in the dark of the cockpit with the corridor maps glowing between them: *I think it's in me. I think it's been teaching itself to me, and I don't know if I'm a speaker or a vessel.* And Vey, who was twenty-three and carried stolen records in cargo pods and had never once in Silas's hearing flinched from an uncomfortable truth, had said: *Does it matter? You're the one it chose to be. Be that well.*

Now the dreams were quieter. Not gone—Silas suspected they would never be gone, and had stopped wanting them to be—but changed, the way a storm changes when it comes ashore and becomes rain. The sentences still came, but they no longer arrived like dictation. They arrived like *conversation*. Silas would dream of the long route, the real one and the dreamed one braided together, the amber arc of Thessaly and the fishing lights of Cael Undine, and in the dream someone would walk beside the ship—not a figure, never a figure, just a presence at the edge of the hull, the way the flicker had been a presence in the gravity—and it would say something in Kessari, something small and ordinary and untranslatable in the way that only living language is untranslatable, and Silas would answer.

Last night, in the dream, it had said: *The harbor master on Cael Undine was my student.*

And Silas had said: *I know. She told me. She kept the fire.*

And the presence had said: *I know. I heard.*

*I heard.* The same words the harbor master had used, and Silas understood, waking slowly in the bunk with the ship humming around them, that this was what the dreams had always been—not a haunting, not a rehearsal, but a listener. The colony's last broadcast had gone out into the dark forty years ago and no one had heard it, and a language that no one hears does not die so much as it waits, and it had waited inside the structure of things—in gravity, in hull-stress, in the dreams of one xenolinguist who happened to be raised on its cadence—until it could be heard. And now it had been heard, by a whole system at once, and the waiting was over, and what remained of it in Silas's sleep was not urgency but company.

Belonging, Silas thought, watching the amber arc of Thessaly's Reach come up over the horizon of the morning watch. It was not a place. Silas had spent eleven years looking for a place—some port, some world, some route where a person raised on a dead language could set down without being a ghost. There was no such place. There had never been such a place. There was only this: a voice, and another voice answering it, and the long work of keeping the channel open.

* * *

At Cael Undine, Silas took on a passenger.

It was not planned. The harbor master—her name was Sena Oduya, and she had the fire-keeper's forearms and the unhurried voice of someone who had spent decades being careful in public—came up the boarding ramp while Silas was sealing the cargo cradles, and behind her, half-hidden in her shadow, was a girl of maybe sixteen, clutching a battered hand-terminal to her chest like a shield.

"My granddaughter," Sena said, in Kessari, and then, seeing Silas's face, in Standard, because she was a woman who understood exactly what she was doing with every word in every language. "She's been teaching herself from the new archives. All the old broadcasts, the ones the relay held. She has—" a pause, and the harbor master of Cael Undine, who did not flinch at gravity shears or fleet cutters, looked briefly uncertain, "—questions. About the pronunciation. The old pronunciation, the one from before. And there's no one on this world left who—"

"There's me," Silas said.

The girl's name was Iyo, and she spoke Kessari the way the young always speak resurrected languages: correctly, carefully, and with a stiffness that came from learning it out of archives rather than out of air. Silas listened to her recite a passage from the colony's final testimony—the passage Silas had completed at the relay, the sentence that had hung truncated for forty years—and heard in it the ghost of the same error Silas themselves had made at fourteen, learning from recordings instead of from a living mouth. The stress fell on the wrong syllable. The vowel in the second word was a half-tone flat.

"It's not wrong," Silas said, in Kessari, gently. "It's archived. Listen—" and Silas said the sentence the way the dreams had taught it, the way the relay had sung it, the way the presence at the edge of the hull said ordinary things in the dark, and Iyo's whole face changed, the way a face changes when a locked room turns out to have a window.

"That," the girl whispered. "How do you *do* that."

"Practice," Silas said. "And dreams. Mostly dreams." And because the girl deserved honesty, and because Silas was tired of being the only keeper of strange truths: "The language taught itself to me while I slept. It may do the same to you. Or it may not. Either way, you speak it now, and that's not a thing anyone can take back."

Iyo rode with them as far as Thessaly's Reach. She spent the transit in the cockpit with her hand-terminal full of archived broadcasts, asking questions, and Silas answered them, and somewhere over the second night, without either of them deciding it, the questions stopped being about pronunciation and started being about the route, the worlds, the work—and Silas realized, with a feeling like a door opening onto morning, that they were not teaching a student. They were training a successor. Not for the ship, necessarily. For the listening. Someone had to fly the long route. Someone had to hear what the worlds were saying and carry it between them. The Echo's Wake had been lost, officially, and what had replaced it was not a ship but a function, a role in the grammar of the system, and functions outlive their speakers.

That was the sentence the presence said that night, in the dream, at the edge of the hull, and for once Silas did not answer. Silas only listened, and let it be true.

* * *

They parted at Thessaly's Reach, Iyo walking down the ramp into the amber light with her terminal held against her chest, and she turned at the bottom and signed something in the old trade-sign that the archives had no entry for, and Silas understood it anyway, because some grammar lives in the hands and never needed saving: *Until the next route.*

Silas signed back the same, and lifted, and turned the Echo's Wake onto the long route north.

The ship was quiet in the way it had always been quiet, but it was a different quiet now. Silas felt it in the hull the way one feels weather change. For eleven years this ship had been a hiding place, and then a weapon, and then a monument, and now it was only what it had been built to be: a freighter, a long-hauler, a thing that goes between. The cargo hold held machine parts and film and, in the linguistic core, the records that were no longer sealed, and none of it was contraband anymore, and Silas found they missed the contraband a little, the way one misses the weight of armor only after the battle is over and the arms are strangely light.

Vey's message came in at the Thessaly relay, routed through the open band, in clear, because there was no longer any reason not to be in clear.

*Records house is at four hundred volumes. The school at Meridian graduated its first class. Odile's hearing concluded Tuesday—she testified for nine hours and the tribunal asked her to stay on as an advisor to the restoration commission, and she said, and I quote, "I will amend what I signed or die trying," and they gave her an office. The fleet withdrew the purge order permanently. The Accord is dead. Also: the drift station master says you told your story in his galley and made a dozen grown spacers cry, which means you are becoming a legend, which means you should come through Meridian soon so I can make fun of you in person.*

*Also also:* the message said, and here the cadence shifted, and Silas could hear Vey choosing her words the way she chose them when something mattered, *the archives keep getting fragments. New ones. Broadcasts from the colony years we never had—stored in places nobody thought to look, relay buoys, hull cores, one entire mining platform that was apparently just full of them. The language is coming home faster than we can shelve it. We need listeners. We need someone who knows the long route and can hear the difference between an echo and a voice.*

*Come through Meridian, Silas. The worlds are talking. Someone should be flying the conversation.*

Silas read it twice, then set the message aside and looked out the cockpit glass at the long route unrolling ahead: the dark between Cael Undine and the drift, the amber smear of Thessaly falling behind, the cold bright scatter of the relay buoys at the edge of charted space, each one of them, Vey said, possibly holding a voice.

The dreams would come tonight. They always did. And in them the presence would walk beside the hull and say something ordinary in a language that had refused to die, and Silas would answer, and the conversation would continue, the way it had continued through forty years of silence, through nine seconds of falling, through nine seconds of speaking, through all the long work of a system learning to hear itself again.

Silas keyed the long-route heading and felt the Echo's Wake lean into it, the old ship settling into its course like a shoulder settling under a familiar weight.

*Coming through Meridian,* Silas sent. *Tell Odile to put the kettle on. And, Vey—*

Silas paused over the console. There was a sentence in Kessari for this, one of the old ones, one the archives had no entry for because it had lived only in mouths, in the space between a speaker and the one who answered. The dreams had given it to Silas months ago, and Silas had never yet had occasion to use it, because it was not a thing one said to the dark. It was a thing one said to a person.

Silas typed it, in Kessari, in clear, on the open band, where anyone in the system could hear it:

*The route is long, but I am no longer flying it alone. Keep a light burning.*

And from a hundred relay buoys and a dozen worlds and one archive house on Meridian Reach, the system answered.
