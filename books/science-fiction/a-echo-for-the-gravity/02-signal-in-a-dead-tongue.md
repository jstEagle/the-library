## 2. Signal in a Dead Tongue

The ping came in at 0347, ship's time, which on the *Long Answer* meant nothing at all. There was no morning to interrupt, no night to protect. There was only the corridor—Lane Kestrel-9 deconflicted, four hundred kilometers of empty vacuum the fleet had agreed nobody would shoot anybody in—and Vesper Ravensworth sitting their scheduled watch, drinking reconstituted tea that tasted faintly of the filter membrane, doing exactly what they did every watch of every day for twelve years.

Nothing.

It was a discipline, nothingness. Vesper had gotten good at it the way some people got good at instruments or languages: hours of practice, deliberate, daily. They kept their telemetry clean, their manifests boring, their voice flat on comms. They filed flight plans with margins so conservative that traffic control had stopped flagging them years ago. The *Long Answer* was, by design, the most forgettable ship in the Verge—a forty-year-old hauler with mismatched hull plating and a cargo manifest full of agricultural substrate and water reclamation parts. Nobody looked twice at them. That was the whole point. Vesper had spent twelve years becoming a footnote, and they were proud of it in the way you're proud of a scar: it meant something had happened, and it hadn't killed you.

So when the console chirped—not the routine handshake-chirp of lane traffic, but the three-tone priority pulse reserved for distress signals—Vesper's first instinct was annoyance.

Second instinct was to check it anyway, because ignoring a distress pulse was the kind of thing that got your registry flagged, and flags were the opposite of invisible.

They pulled the packet up on the secondary display and expected the usual: a mining skiff with a failed recycler, a yacht that had wandered off the beacon chain, some small human problem with a small human solution. What came up instead was a string of characters that stopped Vesper's tea halfway to their mouth.

The header wasn't Standard Fleet Code. It wasn't Verge Trade Pidgin, or the compressed burst-format the relay stations used, or any of the eleven encoding schemes every licensed navigator was required to recognize. It was older. Angular. Built for a phonetic alphabet that hadn't been spoken aloud in living memory outside of academic recordings.

Old Meridian.

Vesper set the tea down very carefully, as if the cup might be listening.

*Threnody,* said the first line. *We are threnody. Hull integrity failing. Cold seeping in. If anyone remembers the words, remember us.*

The translation ran itself in Vesper's head before they could stop it, the way a song runs itself when someone hums the first bar. That was the thing about Old Meridian—it wasn't really a language you learned. Not for Vesper. It was a language you'd been sung to sleep in.

*Lu-mira, lu-mira, kel an dor.*

Their mother's voice, low and rough from recycled air, singing in the dark of a habitat module while the fleet burned behind them. Vesper had been six. Everyone had been six, or five, or seven—the whole generation born in transit, rocked to sleep in a dead tongue because the living ones hadn't been invented yet. Old Meridian had been Earth's last common language, the one the evacuation fleets carried out like luggage too heavy to keep but too precious to abandon. Within two generations it had collapsed into fragments, then lullabies, then nothing. The Council had standardized Fleet Code in the second decade. Efficiency, they'd called it. One tongue for one future.

The message repeated. Same characters. Same impossible grammar. *If anyone remembers the words, remember us.*

Vesper sat with it for ninety seconds. Long enough to feel the shape of the decision forming. Then they did what twelve years of practice had trained them to do.

They deleted the log entry, purged the buffer, and told themselves it was static.

* * *

"You look terrible," Doss said at shift change.

Doss always said this. It was less an observation than a greeting, delivered around a mouthful of protein paste, his massive frame wedged into the galley bench built for people half his mass. He was the engineer, which meant he owned everything below decks and resented everything above them, including gravity.

"Didn't sleep," Vesper said. True. Safe.

"Lane keeping you up? Kestrel-9's about as exciting as watching paint sublimate."

"That's the appeal."

Mireille was already there, feet up on the opposite bench, pilot's jacket unzipped, flipping through the next cycle's routing options on a hand slate. She glanced up when Vesper came in, did her usual quick read of their face, and Vesper watched her decide not to ask. That was Mireille's gift. She collected things she didn't say and stored them somewhere, and occasionally, months later, she'd deploy one at exactly the wrong moment.

"Next window's clean," she said. "Two weeks of nothing through the Shoals approach if we take the long arc. Adds nine days."

"Take it," Vesper said.

Doss snorted. "Nine days of nothing. You two are made for each other."

Vesper took their paste, sat, ate. Normal. Routine. The armor held.

Except.

Except that at 0400 they'd done something they hadn't done in twelve years. After deleting the log, after telling themselves *static*, they had copied the raw packet—untranslated, uncompressed—to a personal drive they kept hidden inside the housing of the nav computer's redundant gyro, a location Doss would never open because he considered the redundant gyro a superstition and refused to touch it. And except that now, sitting in the galley eating paste, some part of Vesper's mind was still translating, still hearing the angular characters in their mother's voice, still feeling the cold seep in.

*Hull integrity failing.* Present tense, when it was sent. Whoever sent it had been dying, or pretending to die, or broadcasting from something already dead. Distress signals from derelicts weren't unheard of—ghost pings, automated systems firing on timers decades after their crews stopped existing. The Verge was full of them. Pre-Collapse wrecks drifted out past the shipping lanes like bones, and every few years somebody's sensor ghost would trigger a rescue scramble and they'd find nothing but frost and old metal.

That was the reasonable explanation. Automated timer. Ghost signal. Static with structure.

But ghost signals didn't encode in Old Meridian. Ghost signals didn't use the *threnody* form—the mourning-broadcast, the specific ritual format the evacuation fleets had used for ships lost during the Collapse, a format Vesper knew because their mother had explained it once, hushed, like it was sacred: *when a ship died, they didn't send mayday, they sent memory. So the universe would know what it was losing.*

Nobody alive used the threnody form. Nobody alive *could*. It required fluency.

Vesper finished their paste, washed their cup, and went back to the bridge, where they spent four hours running collision-avoidance drills against simulated traffic that didn't exist, and thought about nothing, professionally, the entire time.

* * *

It lasted thirty-one hours.

On the second night—or what passed for night aboard a ship that ran on a twenty-six-hour clock nobody's body respected—Vesper woke from a dream of their mother's kitchen to find themselves already standing at the nav console, the hidden drive already plugged in, the packet already open.

They didn't remember deciding. That frightened them more than the message had.

Under the watch-lamp, alone, they let themselves translate it fully. Not just the header. The whole broadcast. It was longer than a standard distress ping—much longer—which was strange, because threnodies were supposed to be short. Grief, compressed. This one ran to several thousand characters, structured in verses, and as Vesper worked through it with the slow, clumsy fluency of someone reading a language they'd only ever heard sung, the verses stopped sounding like a distress call and started sounding like something else.

Coordinates. Buried in the meter of it, the way their mother had buried corrections to bedtime stories in the melody—*no, little star, the third star, count again.* A position. Deep off-lane, out past Verge Nine, in the drift fields where pre-Collapse wreckage was legal to ignore and illegal to salvage without a writ nobody living held.

And beneath the coordinates, a final verse that Vesper translated three times because they didn't believe it the first two:

*We carry what was carried out. Two hundred vessels of one voice. The words are not lost. We are waiting where the words were written. Come home, little echo.*

*Come home, little echo.*

Their childhood name. Not Vesper—nobody aboard knew any other name for them—but the name their mother had used, the name that existed nowhere in any registry, any record, any file. The name of a six-year-old who had died, officially, in a docking accident that Vesper had survived and spent twelve years not talking about.

Vesper stood in the dark of the bridge for a long time, listening to the ventilation system breathe, feeling the armor crack down the middle in real time.

Because here was the arithmetic they'd built their life on: the family was gone. The records were gone. The backup program—the thing Vesper had run from at nineteen, the vaults full of copies, the whole obscene industry of preserving people in pods like fruit—had been destroyed in the same accident, or so the filings said. Vesper had checked. Obsessively, in the early years. Every database, every archive, every black-market record broker who'd take anonymous payment. Nothing. The program had been erased, and Vesper had grieved it twice—once for the family, once for the copies—and then built a life on top of the double grave.

And now something out past Verge Nine knew a name that should have died with all of it.

Options. Vesper was a navigator; they thought in options and courses.

One: report it. Flag the packet, wake Mireille, run it through proper channels. Which meant fleet channels, which meant Council oversight, which meant Vesper's real identity—buried under twelve years of careful paperwork—surfacing in a government database within the hour. Unacceptable.

Two: ignore it. Delete the copy. Go back to being a footnote. The signal was dying anyway—old broadcasts decayed, carriers faded, whatever was out there had been calling for years without an answer and could keep calling. Let the dead stay dead. That was the doctrine Vesper had lived by since they were nineteen. It had worked. It had kept them alive.

Three—

There wasn't a three. Vesper deleted the packet from the drive, wiped the access log, unplugged everything, and went back to their bunk.

They lay awake until shift change, staring at the ceiling, hearing *come home, little echo* in a voice thirty years silent.

* * *

At breakfast, Mireille slid her slate across the table.

"What's this?" Vesper asked.

"Passive array caught something two nights back. Priority-pulse profile, nonstandard encoding. I flagged it for review." She was watching Vesper's face with the careful neutrality she used for landings in crosswinds. "Review queue says it got cleared as noise. Weird, though. Noise doesn't usually come with a checksum that clean."

Vesper kept their hands still around the cup. "Sensor ghosts happen. Old wreck firing on a timer."

"Yeah." Mireille nodded slowly. "That's what I figured. Except the queue-clearance stamp has your authorization code on it, and you logged that watch as uneventful." She let that sit. "Which is fine. Your watch, your call. I'm not asking what it was."

"Doss—"

"Doesn't know. I didn't bring it to him." She took the slate back. "I'm telling you I noticed, V. That's all. Twelve years, you've never once misfiled anything. First time for everything, and it happens to be the same night my array catches a ghost singing in a language I've never seen." She stood, stretched, zipped her jacket. "Static, right?"

"Static," Vesper said.

"Sure." At the hatch she paused, back turned. "For what it's worth, the long arc through the Shoals adds nine days and passes within sensor range of Verge Nine. Purely coincidental routing. I just like the view."

She left. Vesper sat in the galley with their heart going like a failing pump, understanding two things at once.

First: Mireille knew. Not everything—maybe not much—but she knew Vesper had lied, and she was offering, in her sideways wordless way, to lie alongside them. Or to follow them. She'd rerouted the ship toward the source of the signal and dressed it up as a scheduling preference, and she'd done it before Vesper asked, before Vesper even admitted there was anything to ask.

Second: Vesper was going to have to decide soon whether the armor was worth wearing when it was already cracked through.

They went to the bridge. They pulled up the routing Mireille had filed, and there it was: the long arc, the scenic route, the nine-day detour whose flight path brushed the edge of the drift fields off Verge Nine like a hand passing near a flame.

Vesper could correct it. One keystroke. Back to the safe lane, back to nothing, back to the footnote.

Their mother's voice, thirty years gone, sang the counting verse. *No, little star. The third star. Count again.*

Vesper left the course exactly as filed.

Then, hands steadier than they felt, they opened the nav computer's redundant gyro housing, retrieved the hidden drive, and restored the packet from the shadow cache they'd sworn they'd erased—because of course they hadn't actually erased it, because some part of them had known, even at 0400 two nights ago, that erasing it was a lie they were telling out loud.

They began to plot the coordinates buried in the verses.

Outside the viewport, Lane Kestrel-9 went on being empty, safe, deconflicted, forgettable. Inside the *Long Answer*, for the first time in twelve years, Vesper Ravensworth was hiding something from the dark instead of from the light.

The signal had called them *little echo*.

Echoes, Vesper knew, were just sounds that refused to accept that the voice had stopped.
