## 4. Derelict at Verge Nine

Mireille had flown the Long Answer through eleven years of bad weather and worse contracts, and she had a rule about derelicts that she'd never once broken: you don't. A dead ship was either a tomb or a trap, and both of those were somebody else's problem.

So when Vesper stood in the cockpit doorway at 0400 ship's time and said, "There's something at Verge Nine I think we should look at," Mireille's first instinct was to say no before the sentence finished landing.

Her second instinct—slower, more dangerous—was to look at Vesper's face.

Vesper Ravensworth had two expressions aboard this ship: neutral and slightly-less-neutral. Twelve years of flying together and Mireille could count on one hand the times she'd seen anything else. Once when Doss's reactor breach nearly cooked them over Kettle Shoals. Once when a dockhand at Peridot Station had called Vesper by a name that wasn't theirs, and Vesper had gone white as unlit vacuum for three full seconds before correcting him with a smile so smooth it should have been load-bearing.

This was neither of those. This was something new. This was Vesper looking like a person standing at the edge of a hole they'd spent their whole life pretending wasn't there.

"Define *something*," Mireille said.

"A contact. Drifting. Pre-Collapse hull by the profile." Vesper kept their eyes on the nav display, not on her. "Registry transponder's still cycling. Weak."

"Pre-Collapse." Mireille leaned back in the pilot's chair until it creaked. "That's a hundred-year-old ghost, Vesper. Anything still drifting out here is either stripped to the ribs or full of things that eat boarding parties."

"It's not stripped."

"You've scanned it?"

A pause. Half a second too long. "Long-range passive only. But the mass signature is intact. Nobody's taken the drive, the tanks, or the hold."

Nobody takes the hold. Mireille turned that phrase over. Salvage crews took holds first. Holds were where the money lived. A pre-Collapse freighter drifting at Verge Nine with an untouched hold meant one of two things: nobody had found it, or everybody who'd found it had decided very quickly they wanted nothing to do with it.

"And the registry," Mireille said. "You said it's cycling. What's it say?"

Another pause. Longer this time. Vesper's jaw did something small and controlled, like a man swallowing a stone.

"Doesn't matter," they said. "It's a distress situation. Derelict with active power is a hazard to navigation. We're obligated to investigate or report."

"We're obligated to *report*," Mireille said. "There's a difference. One of them keeps us alive."

"Mireille."

And there it was—the thing Vesper almost never did, which was say her name like it meant something. Like they were asking instead of navigating. Mireille had watched this person chart courses around every contested lane in the Verge for twelve years, take the long way around storms, around patrols, around history itself, and now here they stood asking her to fly straight into the one place their whole career seemed built to avoid.

She should have said no. She knew that even then, some part of her logging it coldly, the way she logged fuel margins and hull stress. The part of her that had survived twenty years in the Verge said *no*.

But Doss was going to love this, and Doss was going to say yes, and Mireille had learned long ago that a crew of two against one was just arithmetic wearing a uniform.

"Passive approach," she said. "No docking clamps until I've seen the hull up close. And if anything over there so much as *hums* wrong, we cut loose and burn. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Vesper said, and something in their shoulders came down half a centimeter, and Mireille thought: *I'm going to regret this.*

She was right. Just not about what she'd be regretting.

* * *

The derelict resolved out of Verge Nine's dust haze at 0630, and Mireille forgot, briefly, how to breathe.

It was big. That was the first thing. Pre-Collapse freighters ran heavy—built in the era when fuel was cheap and confidence was cheaper—but this one was a cathedral class, the kind they stopped making after the Collapse because nobody trusted anybody enough to build something that size anymore. Nine hundred meters of gray hull, scarred by micrometeorite frost, tumbling end-over-end in a slow rotation that spoke of a dead attitude system and decades of neglect.

Except it wasn't tumbling. Mireille watched it for a full minute before she was sure, and then she was sure, and being sure made it worse.

"It's station-keeping," she said. "Vesper. It's *station-keeping*."

"I see it."

"That's not a derelict behavior. Dead ships drift. Dead ships don't hold position over a gravity well like they're waiting for someone."

"Maybe the autopilot's still running."

"Autopilots don't run for a century off nothing." She keyed the comms. "Doss, tell me you're seeing the power readings I'm seeing."

Doss's voice came back warm and crackling from engineering, already delighted. "Oh, I'm seeing them. Fusion plant's idling at maybe four percent output, which shouldn't be possible, because that class of plant doesn't *have* a four percent idle unless someone designed it to sip. Which they didn't. Which means someone's been down there managing it. Manually. For a long time."

"Someone," Mireille repeated. "Or something."

"Or," Doss said, with the particular relish he reserved for problems that might kill them all, "the ship's been taking care of itself."

Mireille brought the Long Answer in along the derelict's dorsal line, close enough that the old hull filled the forward viewport wall to wall. Up close, the scarring told stories: micrometeorite pitting consistent with decades of exposure, yes, but also clean bands where the hull had been maintained, patched, sealed. Work done recently. Work done well.

"There," Vesper said quietly. "Airlock seven. It'll be pressurized."

Mireille looked at them. "How do you know which airlock?"

Vesper didn't answer immediately. On the nav display, the registry transponder finally resolved its cycle—a slow, stuttering broadcast, letters assembling themselves out of noise:

**CSV MERIDIAN'S DAUGHTER — RAVENSWORTH LINE — REGISTRY 9-KES-2211**

The cockpit went very quiet. Somewhere behind them, the environmental system ticked through its cycle, indifferent.

"Ravensworth," Mireille said.

"It's a common name," Vesper said.

"It's your name."

"It's a common name, Mireille."

She turned her chair to face them fully. Eleven years, and she had never asked about the name. It was an unwritten term of their arrangement: Vesper navigated like a gift from whatever god looked after cargo haulers, asked no questions about Mireille's debts, and received none about the flatness behind their eyes, the way they flinched—only sometimes, only slightly—at certain words. *Home.* *Family.* *Before.* She'd assumed debt, or disgrace, or grief. The Verge was full of all three; they came standard with the air.

But now the name sat there on the display, cycling in a dead language's alphabet, attached to a nine-hundred-meter ghost that was holding station like a held breath, and Vesper was looking at it the way people look at graves they dug themselves.

"How do you know which airlock?" Mireille asked again, softer.

"Because it's the one closest to the cryo systems," Vesper said. "If anyone ever woke up on that ship, they'd want to wake up near the door."

*Cryo.* The word dropped through the cockpit like a stone through still water. Mireille hadn't said it. Doss hadn't said it. There was nothing on any scan to suggest—

"The hold's powered," Vesper went on, in that same terrible quiet voice. "Cold but powered. That's what I felt from the passive readings. That's why we have to look. If there are people in there—if there *were* people—"

"People don't survive a hundred years in cryo, Vesper."

"Some systems can do forty. Fifty. With maintenance." They finally looked at her, and their eyes were the flattest she'd ever seen them, which meant they were working hardest at being calm. "We won't know unless we look."

* * *

Mireille docked them herself, because she didn't trust her hands to anyone else and she didn't trust the derelict at all. The clamp engagement rang through the Long Answer's frame like a bell tolling underwater. Airlock seven, exactly where Vesper said it would be, exactly as pressurized as Vesper said it would be.

Doss met them at the inner lock in a hardshell suit with the helmet tucked under one arm, grinning like a kid at a salvage auction. "Atmosphere's good over there. Oxygen-nitrogen, twenty-one percent. Warm-ish. Somebody's keeping the lights on in sections." He paused, and his grin flickered. "Also, uh. Their registry database answered my handshake. Full schematics. Crew manifest, maintenance logs, everything. Ship's practically introducing itself."

"To us specifically?" Mireille asked.

"No, to—" Doss frowned. "Actually, huh. It pinged our transponder first. Matched us against something. Then opened up." He looked at Vesper. "You want to hear the weird part? The match flag came back green in under a second. Like it recognized us. Like it was *waiting* for us."

"Ships wait for lots of reasons," Vesper said.

"Sure," Doss said, in the tone of a man agreeing with a wall.

They crossed the collar in single file—Mireille first, because if something went wrong she wanted to be the one deciding whether they ran—and stepped onto the deck of the Meridian's Daughter.

The corridor beyond was lit. Not fully—emergency strips at ankle height, amber and patient—but lit, and warm, and clean. No dust. No decay. Bulkheads painted a faded institutional green, floor plating worn in a narrow path down the centerline, the wear pattern of feet, many feet, over many years. Mireille's skin crawled in a way she couldn't articulate. A dead ship should smell like metal and cold. This smelled faintly, impossibly, of recycled air and old coffee.

"Verge Nine's been surveyed six times in the last decade," she said, mostly to herself. "Mining consortiums, insurance assessors, two navy sweeps. A cathedral-class freighter holding station does not go unnoticed."

"Maybe it moved," Doss offered.

"Ships don't move without drives burning, and drives burning show up on every scope in the sector."

"Then maybe everyone who noticed decided not to mention it." Doss said it lightly, but his hand had drifted to his sidearm, and Mireille noted that he'd stopped grinning somewhere around the second junction.

They reached a junction where the corridor split—forward toward the bridge, aft toward the hold. At the junction stood a sign, original paint, pre-Collapse lettering in Old Meridian script above a newer translation plate. Mireille read the translation twice.

**CRYOGENIC PRESERVATION — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY — WAKE PROTOCOLS IN EFFECT**

"Wake protocols," Doss read aloud. "In effect. Present tense."

Below the sign, bolted to the bulkhead at eye height, hung a maintenance log tablet, still charged, still updating. Doss thumbed it awake. The screen filled with entries—hundreds of them, thousands, dated in an unbroken sequence stretching back decades. System checks. Power rationing notes. Coolant flushes. All signed with the same initials, over and over, patient as prayer:

**V.R.**

Mireille felt the temperature of the moment change, the way a room changes when someone says the wrong name at a funeral.

"V.R.," Doss said slowly. "Vesper Ravensworth."

"Common initials," Vesper said.

"Stop," Mireille said.

Vesper stopped. They were standing a little apart from both of them, near the aft corridor mouth, and in the amber light their face was unreadable in a way that Mireille suddenly understood was not blankness at all but effort—the visible cost of holding a face still.

"You knew the registry before we scanned it," Mireille said. "You knew the airlock. You knew the hold was powered before Doss confirmed it. You knew the word *cryo*. And now there's a logbook with your initials maintaining this ship since before either of us was born." She kept her voice level. Eleven years of practice. "I'm not asking what's going on yet. I'm asking you to tell me I'm wrong about one of those things. Any one."

The silence stretched. Down the aft corridor, past the sign, the amber strips ran away into darkness toward the hold, and somewhere down there, Mireille understood with sudden animal certainty, something was running a four-percent idle and keeping two hundred—no, she didn't know the number, she invented the number and hated herself for it—keeping *someone* alive.

"Vesper," Doss said gently. "Buddy."

"I need to go aft," Vesper said. "Alone. Give me an hour. Then I'll tell you everything, and if you want to cut loose and burn afterward, I'll stand on the dock and wave goodbye, and I will never ask you for anything again as long as I live."

"That's not how crews work," Mireille said.

"It's how *this* crew has worked for eleven years," Vesper said. "You never asked. I never told. We were good to each other inside that line. Please don't move the line tonight."

Mireille looked at Doss. Doss looked at Mireille. Behind Vesper, the corridor breathed its warm recycled breath, and the maintenance tablet glowed with decades of faithful, impossible care, and every instinct Mireille owned was screaming that the line had already been moved—that it had been moved the moment a distress signal in a dead language had come out of nowhere and Vesper had lied about static, because she'd checked the raw logs afterward, of course she had, she always checked, and the signal had been there, structured and deliberate, and Vesper had stood in her cockpit and called it noise.

"One hour," Mireille said. "Comms open the whole time. You go silent for thirty seconds, we come find you."

"Thank you," Vesper said, and walked aft, unhurried, like a person returning to a house they'd left that morning rather than a lifetime ago.

* * *

Forty-one minutes later, Mireille was standing at the aft junction pretending to inspect a coolant coupling when Vesper's voice came over the open channel, and it was the voice that made her start walking before her brain finished processing the words.

Not panic. Worse than panic. Panic would have been fine. This was the voice of someone reading their own obituary in a language they'd forgotten they spoke.

"Mireille. Doss. You need to come see this."

They found Vesper at the cryo bay threshold, one hand braced against the doorframe, suit helmet dangling from the other hand—they'd broken protocol, gone bareheaded, breathing the dead ship's air like it belonged to them, which apparently it did. Beyond them, through the open bay doors, blue light spilled across the deck in long rows.

Pods. Rows and rows of pods, racked floor to ceiling down the length of a hold the size of a parish church, each one frosted glass and steady green indicators, each one occupied.

Mireille counted the nearest rack before she could stop herself. Counted the racks. Did the arithmetic.

Two hundred sleepers, give or take, breathing in the dark under a century of careful maintenance.

And Vesper turned to face them, and Mireille saw that they were crying without any expression at all, tears moving down a perfectly still face, and they said:

"They're all me."

Doss made a sound like he'd been struck. Mireille didn't make any sound. She was looking past Vesper, at the pod on the end of the nearest rack, at the label stenciled on its frost in Old Meridian script and beneath it, in newer, careful handwriting, a translation tag that someone had added by hand within the last few years:

**RAVENSWORTH, S. — ORIGINAL PATTERN: VESPER — BATCH ONE OF FORTY**

Forty batches. Two hundred copies. A whole hold full of one person, sleeping in the dark, tended by initials in a logbook, waiting for a signal in a language only the dead remembered.

Mireille's suspicion, which had carried her this far, quietly curdled into something else—something colder and less certain of itself. Because the question was no longer *what is Vesper hiding*. The question was why a ship full of Vespers had waited a hundred years to call one of them home.

And why, on the edge of the bay's blue light, one pod—older model than the rest, its label bearing a woman's name—had begun, very softly, to warm.
