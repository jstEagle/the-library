## 7. Loyalty, Itemized

Doss found the lie in the coolant logs.

That was the thing about Vesper Ravensworth—twelve years flying with them and Doss had learned that everything important about the navigator lived in the numbers. Vesper didn't talk. Vesper *filed*. Every course correction, every fuel burn, every deviation from the deconflicted corridor went into the manifest like a confession written in a language only accountants could read. So when the burn profile for the last six hours didn't match any cargo route in the Long Answer's ledger—when it matched instead a slow, patient arc toward a derelict squatting at the edge of Verge Nine's debris shadow—Doss didn't need to be a detective. They just needed to be the person who'd been keeping this ship's guts alive for a decade and a half, and who knew exactly what her engines sounded like when they were being asked to go somewhere quietly.

Doss sat in the engineering bay with the log open on their slate and let the recycler hum. Sixteen years on this ship. Nine of them with Vesper. And in all that time, Doss had asked for exactly one thing: tell me when the plan changes. Not why. Not where. Just *tell me*.

The plan had changed four days ago, and nobody had told them anything.

They closed the slate, wiped their hands on a rag that had been gray since before either of them signed aboard, and went looking.

* * *

Vesper was in the mess, alone, which was itself an anomaly. Mireille had the con; the watch rotation said Vesper should have been sleeping. Instead they sat at the bolted-down table with a cup of tea gone cold beside them, staring at nothing, and Doss thought: *there it is.* That look. The one Vesper got maybe twice a year, the one that made them seem less like a person than a door someone had locked from the inside.

"You want to tell me," Doss said, "why we're burning toward a dead hulk instead of running the Meridian contract?"

Vesper didn't flinch. That was almost worse. "It's a short detour."

"It's a nine-hour detour through unscanned space with a hold full of perishables." Doss sat down across from them, deliberately, making it a thing with weight. "And you filed the burn as a trim correction. You've never once falsified a log. Not once in nine years. So either you've suddenly gotten sloppy—and you don't get sloppy—or you didn't want us to know."

"Which do you prefer?"

"Don't do that." Doss kept their voice level. It took effort. "Don't make it clever. I'm not asking as your engineer. I'm asking as the person who rebuilt your life support scrubbers by hand when the syndicate blacklisted you and no yard would sell you parts. I carried those manifolds through two checkpoints in my own kit because you couldn't risk being seen buying them. That's what I'm asking from. So. What's at Verge Nine?"

For a long moment Vesper looked at the cold tea like it might answer for them. Then they said, "A distress signal."

"When?"

"Four days ago."

Doss felt something in their chest go very still and very cold, the way machinery goes before it fails. "Four days. And the crew debate about whether to answer it happened—when, exactly?"

"There wasn't one."

"There was one. I was there. You told us it was static. You stood on that bridge and said 'it's static, old compression artifact, not worth the burn,' and Mireille believed you because Mireille believes you, and I half-believed you because—" Doss stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I decided to. That's the part that burns, Vesper. Believing you is a choice I've made every day for nine years, and you just spent four days spending that choice without telling me the price."

"I was going to tell you."

"When? After? After we'd docked with whatever's out there and it was too late to turn around?" Doss shook their head. "You know what the worst part is? It's not the detour. Detours are cheap. It's that you did the math on us. You ran our trust through your nav computer and you calculated exactly how much of it you could spend before we'd notice the balance dropping. And you were right. We didn't notice. That's the itemized betrayal right there—line by line, small enough to hide inside a routine."

Vesper's jaw tightened. "If I'd told you the truth, you'd have voted it down."

"Yes."

"And then what? We run the Meridian lane and pretend we didn't hear it?"

"We've pretended not to hear a lot of things for twelve years! That's the whole job! That's the whole *life* you built out here—invisible ship, invisible crew, invisible everything. You taught me that. You taught Mireille that. Deconflicted corridors, no questions, no history. And now you break every rule you wrote because some signal in a language you won't even name pinged your childhood, and you didn't think we deserved to know that?" Doss leaned forward. "What language was it, Vesper? Because I ran the waveform myself after you logged it. It's not any trade cant I know. It parsed against nothing in the standard libraries. Which means it's old. Which means it's personal. Which means it's *yours*, and you'd rather fly us blind into a debris field than say one true sentence about yourself."

The silence stretched. Somewhere aft, the number three pump cycled, the sound the ship made when she was thinking.

"My family died in the Collapse," Vesper said finally. Flat. Rehearsed. A sentence they'd given a hundred times to customs officers and insurance adjusters. "I don't talk about it."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," Vesper said. "It isn't."

Doss sat back. And here was the thing—the thing Doss would turn over for years afterward, in bunks and engine rooms and long dark watches: they could see it happening behind Vesper's eyes. The cost-benefit analysis running again. The weighing of how much truth the friendship could carry versus how much the silence already weighed. Twelve years of self-erasure didn't undo itself in a conversation. But something shifted. Some line item moved.

"The derelict," Vesper said slowly, "is registered to my family's line. Pre-Collapse hull. And the signal came from inside it."

Doss waited.

"There are people aboard. Sleeping. Two hundred of them." Vesper's voice didn't crack, but it went thin, like wire under load. "And I need to see them before anyone else does."

"*People.* Sleeping. On a derelict that's been drifting since before you were born." Doss heard their own voice climb and dragged it back down. "Cryo?"

"Cryo."

"For how long?"

"Longer than cryo is supposed to work."

Doss pressed both palms flat against the table, feeling the vibration of the deck come up through the metal, the honest pulse of a ship that never lied to them. "Okay," they said. "Okay. Here's what happens now. You tell Mireille. Today. Everything you just told me, plus whatever you're still holding back—I can hear the gap in it, Vesper, there's a whole room in you with the lights off and I'm not pretending anymore that I don't hear it humming. You tell her, or I do. Those are the options. Because she's flying us into whatever this is on your word, and she deserves the same accounting I just got."

"She'll turn us around."

"Maybe. Probably. That's called a crew, Vesper. That's the entire technology of it—you don't get to be the only vote on the ship you live on." Doss stood, knees popping, sixteen years of engine rooms living in their joints. "I'm not saying I forgive you yet. I'm saying I'm going to keep standing next to you while I decide whether to. There's a difference, and you're going to sit with the difference for a while, because I had to sit with it for four days without knowing there was a difference to sit with."

Vesper looked up at them. And for the first time in nine years, Doss saw the navigator look afraid—not of the derelict, not of the signal, but of the conversation. Of being known.

"Thank you," Vesper said.

"Don't thank me. Fix it." Doss paused at the hatch. "And Vesper? Whatever's in those pods—if it's what I think it is—you're not carrying it alone. That's not forgiveness. That's just the load rating of this crew. We're rated for more than you think."

They left before Vesper could answer, because their hands were shaking and engineers don't shake in front of people.

* * *

Mireille took it better than Doss expected, which frightened Doss more.

She listened in the cockpit with the autopilot holding a steady vector, arms crossed, face doing that thing it did where all the weather moved behind her eyes and none of it reached her mouth. She let Vesper finish—all of it, or most of it; Doss watched for the gap too and caught the moment Vesper skipped over something, a hesitation at the word *backups* that Mireille clocked and filed away the way she filed everything. When Vesper stopped talking, the cockpit was quiet except for the tick of the drift indicator.

"Two hundred sleepers," Mireille said.

"Yes."

"On a ship with your family's registry."

"Yes."

"And a syndicate corvette has already hailed us claiming a writ on the salvage." She unfolded her arms. "You knew that was coming."

"I suspected."

"You *suspected*." Mireille exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh, entirely without humor. She turned to the console, and Doss watched her pull up the route plot—the safe one, the Meridian approach, the corridor that meant another year of quiet work and quiet lies and everyone alive at the end of it. Her hand hovered over the commit key.

"Mireille," Doss started.

"I know what it costs," she said, not turning around. "I've always known what it costs. I've been paying it since the day I signed on with a navigator who wouldn't say their own middle name." She looked at the plot a moment longer. Then she closed it, and opened the plotting table fresh, and began laying in a new course—one that bent, gently, unmistakably, toward Verge Nine.

"You're rerouting," Doss said.

"I'm choosing my friend over my safety margin." She said it simply, the way she read off headings, and somehow that made it heavier. "Don't make it a speech, Doss. I'll cry later, on schedule."

Vesper stood in the cockpit doorway like someone who'd been handed a verdict they hadn't argued for. "You should turn us around."

"Yeah," Mireille said. "Probably." She keyed the new heading. The Long Answer answered, the way she always did, leaning into the burn without complaint. "But here's the deal, and it's non-negotiable, so save your breath. From this second forward: no more solo decisions. No more static-that-isn't-static. If you so much as change the tea ration without logging it, I will put you out an airlock with a smile. We dock with that derelict as a crew, we decide what happens next as a crew, and if there's a war attached to whatever's in that hold, we walk into it knowing whose war it is." She turned in the chair and looked up at Vesper, and her voice dropped to something almost gentle. "You spent twelve years making sure nobody could lose you. Congratulations. It didn't work. Now sit down and help me plot the approach, because whoever's in those pods has been waiting a long time, and I'd rather not keep them waiting on bad seamanship."

Doss watched Vesper cross the cockpit and take the second chair, and watched something in the navigator's shoulders come down half a centimeter—not surrender, not relief, something older than both. The beginning, maybe, of putting down a weight carried so long its absence would feel like falling.

Doss went aft to check the burn tolerances, because that was the job, and because somebody on this ship had to keep loving things in a language that couldn't lie.

Halfway down the spine, the comm panel chimed. Priority hail, tight-beam, source masked.

Doss opened it, and a voice they'd heard once before across four kilometers of vacuum—smooth, patient, wearing authority like a tailored coat—said:

*"Long Answer, this is corvette Kestrel's Debt. Captain Oyelaran speaking. We see your new heading. We find it interesting. Hold your course and prepare to receive terms—or don't, and we'll discuss it at the derelict. Either way, Navigator Ravensworth: we know what's sleeping in that hold. And we know it's not all yours."*

The channel closed. Doss stood in the dim spine of the ship, listening to the engines carry them toward Verge Nine, and understood that the choice had just been taken out of everyone's hands.

They turned and headed for the cockpit at a run.
