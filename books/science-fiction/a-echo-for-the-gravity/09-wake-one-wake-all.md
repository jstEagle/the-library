## 9. Wake One, Wake All

The first thing I know is cold leaving my body like a debt being repaid.

It drains out through the soles of my feet first, then my hands, and for a long time I am nothing but the sensation of thawing—of being a thing that was stopped and is now, against all expectation, running again. My heart stutters into a rhythm that feels borrowed. The gel around me loosens its grip one membrane at a time, and somewhere above my face a seam of light opens, thin as a knife cut.

I remember dying.

That's the strange part. Not the accident—I don't have the accident—but the dying after it, the long gray slide when the backup techs told me the transfer was routine, that everyone in the Meridian program did it, that I'd wake up on the other side of the jump feeling like myself because I would *be* myself, just housed differently. I remember the lid coming down. I remember thinking: this is what it's like to be archived.

And then nothing. And now light.

The pod hisses open and the air that falls into my lungs is stale and metallic and smells faintly of someone else's fear. I try to sit up and my body files a formal complaint—every muscle cramping at once, twelve years of disuse announcing itself in a single bright chord of pain. I get one elbow under me. Then the other. I hang there over the edge of the pod like laundry left out too long.

There are other pods. That's the second thing I know.

Rows of them, stretching back into the dark of the hold, each one lit from within by that same knife-cut seam of amber. Each one occupied. I look down the nearest row and my stomach does something complicated, because every face I can see through the frost-glazed lids is my face. A girl of maybe ten. A boy who could be my brother if I'd had one, which I didn't. An old man with my jaw gone soft and gray. Two hundred versions of me, sleeping in ordered ranks like ammunition.

"No," I say, and my voice comes out ruined, a dry rasp with no authority in it at all. "No, no."

Because I know what this is. I grew up two corridors from the Meridian program's backup vault. I used to press my hand to the glass of the demonstration pod they kept in the lobby, the empty one, the one they let children touch. *Continuity of personhood*, the plaques said. *You never have to end.* My mother hated the whole enterprise. She called it a warehouse full of people pretending not to be dead.

She signed me up anyway, the year before the Collapse took the program's funding and scattered its archives across the Verge like seed grain. "In case," she said, and wouldn't meet my eyes, and I was nineteen and immortal and didn't ask her in case of what.

I'm nineteen now. Or I was nineteen. The readout on my pod says cycle count: four thousand three hundred and eleven days. Twelve years. I do the arithmetic twice because the first answer is unbearable.

Someone has woken me. Which means someone is here.

* * *

I find them in the hold's access gallery, crouched over an open maintenance panel with a hand torch between their teeth, and for a moment I genuinely think I'm still asleep, still dreaming the kind of dream cryo techs warn you about, the ones where your mind tries to reconcile its last snapshot with the present tense.

They're me. Older. Maybe thirty-one, thirty-two. Hair cut short and practical, hands scarred in ways mine aren't, wearing a cargo hauler's coveralls with the name tape half-unstitched. They hear me coming—or feel me, the way you feel weather—and turn, and we look at each other across six meters of derelict deck plating.

"Vesper," they say. My name. Their voice.

"That's not yours," I say.

Something moves behind their eyes—not guilt exactly. Recognition. Like they've rehearsed this conversation in their head for twelve years and are only now discovering how badly the rehearsal went.

"It's both of ours," they say carefully. "It was hers first."

"Don't." I take another step and my legs shake and I hate them for seeing it. "Don't talk about her. You don't—" I stop. Recalibrate. "How many of us are in there?"

"Two hundred."

"And you woke *me*. Out of two hundred, you walked down the rows and picked the oldest one. Why?"

They stand up slowly, keeping their hands visible, which is either courtesy or condescension and right now I can't tell the difference. "Because you're closest to the original timeline. Because your memories end latest. Because if anyone in those pods knows what happened before the Collapse—really knows—it's you."

"I know what happened," I say. "I was there. Were you?"

The silence stretches. Somewhere in the ship's bones, a coolant pump cycles, patient as breathing.

"You're a copy," I say. "A backup that got loose. You've been walking around in my life for twelve years while I—" I gesture at the pods, at all of it, and my hand won't behave. "While I was *stored*."

"Yes," they say. No flinch. I hate that most of all. "And you're a copy too. We're both copies. The original died in the accident with everyone else. There is no original. There never was, after that day. There's just whoever wakes up and has to live with it."

"Liar."

"Am I?" They take a step toward me and I bare my teeth like an animal, and they stop, and something in their face cracks—not hurt, worse than hurt. Pity. "Ask yourself. What's your last memory? Before the pod. Before the transfer consent forms. What's the last thing you actually remember?"

I reach for it and it's there, vivid, immediate: the transfer bay at Meridian Station, the smell of antiseptic and recycled air, the tech named Oyelaran—young then, kind-eyed, her badge reading *Archival Services*—telling me the procedure had a ninety-nine point something percent subjective continuity rating. My mother's hand on the glass before the lid came down. Her mouth moving. I couldn't hear her through the pod. I spent years afterward trying to lip-read the memory, and I never could.

"The bay," I say. "My mother. The lid."

"What date?"

I open my mouth and find nothing there. No date. No season. The memory has no edges; it floats free of any calendar, any context, any sequence of days leading up to it. It's a photograph where a life should be.

"You don't have one," they say quietly. "Neither do I. Neither does anyone in those pods. Our memories start at the accident and end at the transfer bay and there's nothing real connecting them, because the program reconstructed them from partial archives. They seeded the gaps with the strongest emotional anchors so we'd believe it. So we'd wake up whole." They spread their hands. "We were built to grieve, Vesper. That's what the continuity rating measured. How convincingly we'd mourn ourselves."

"You're lying to control me."

"I'm telling you the truth because you're the only person alive who can check it against your own head, and I need you to check it. I need you to be angry about it. Anger is useful. It keeps you awake."

I laugh, and it comes out wrong, jagged. "Useful. Right. And what do you need me awake *for*? Salvage? You found a vault full of yourselves and you want help carrying it out?"

Their expression closes like a shutter. When they speak again, each word is placed down carefully, like they're crossing ice.

"There's a syndicate corvette in the outer system. They're here for the hold. Not for us—for what's *in* us. In our logs. In the things we saw before the accident." A breath. "One of the sleepers carries proof that Earth's final transmission—the Loop, the message the whole fleet runs on—is fake. Edited. The founding council cut the real ending out and spliced in a lie, and the lie is the only reason the Verge holds together, and the truth has been sitting in a cryopod's archive buffer for twelve years waiting for someone brave enough or stupid enough to play it."

The words land one at a time, each with its own small detonation. Earth. The Loop. Every ship, every station, every child in the Verge raised on that final broadcast—*we loved you, we stayed as long as we could, keep going without us*—the message that turned abandonment into inheritance and grief into a founding virtue.

"You're saying it's forged."

"I'm saying the sleepers' raw telemetry says it is. I'm saying I've spent twelve years making sure nobody found these pods, and somebody found them anyway, and now there's a clock on it, because the Loop itself is decaying and when it goes silent, whatever version of the message survives in the archive is the version history gets."

"So burn it." The words come out of me fast and hot and I don't even recognize my own voice. "Burn the hold. Burn the logs. Let the lie stand—it's a *good* lie, it's the load-bearing wall of everything, you don't pull that out just because you found the original blueprints—"

"*Our* logs," they say. "*Our* hold. Those are our memories in there, ours, yours, and you want to burn them to keep a council of liars comfortable?"

"They're not memories, they're *evidence*—"

"They're both! That's the whole—" They stop. Press the heels of their hands against their eyes. When they lower them, they look older than thirty-two. They look like someone who has been having this argument alone in the dark for twelve years. "Do you know what I did, the night I found out what the program really was? What we really were? I sat in the galley of a hauler not much bigger than this one and I tried to work out whether I was allowed to be angry. Whether anger required being real. And I decided—" their voice frays, steadies— "I decided that reality isn't a credential. It's a *practice*. You're real if you show up. You're real if you choose. Nobody in those pods got to choose anything, and that's the crime, that's always been the crime, and you want to make peace with it because making peace is easier than being evidence."

I should hit them. Part of me wants to, wants the clean animal simplicity of it. Instead I hear myself say, in a voice much smaller than the argument deserves:

"She was at the pod. Our mother. She said something and I've never been able to read her lips. Twelve years I've been trying to read her lips."

They go very still.

"You know," I say. "You've had twelve years. You know what she said."

"Vesper—"

"*Tell me.*"

They look at me for a long moment, and whatever they see in my face makes them close their eyes, briefly, like a man accepting a sentence.

"Not tonight," they say. "Tonight you eat, and you sleep in a real bunk, and tomorrow I'll show you the logs—all of them, unedited, including hers. If you still want it then, I'll tell you. But you don't get it as a weapon to use against me in an argument you're losing. She doesn't get to be that. Not her."

The injustice of it fills my whole chest. I open my mouth to say so—

And the deck shudders under our feet.

Not the ship's own rhythm. Something external, heavy, deliberate: a docking clamp, or a grapple, biting into the hull. The lights flicker once. Down the long rows of pods, two hundred amber seams pulse in perfect unison, like a held breath.

They're already moving, pulling a scanner from their belt, and the readout paints their face in ugly green light.

"That's not the syndicate corvette," they say. "That's a boarding pattern. Someone's coming aboard."

* * *

They move through the derelict like they've lived here for years—which, I suppose, in every way that matters, they have—and I follow because following is the only option that isn't standing alone in a room full of my own faces. At the hold's inner hatch they stop, hand flat against the lock panel, and look back at me.

"One question," they say. "Before whatever happens next. The pods—if it comes down to it, if we can't defend them—do we wake them or do we seal them and run?"

"Seal them," I say instantly. "Waking two hundred people onto a sinking ship is just killing them with extra steps."

They nod slowly, and there's something in their face I can't read, some private tally being marked.

"That's what I thought you'd say," they tell me. "The original said the opposite."

The hatch opens onto darkness, and somewhere ahead of us, boots are already ringing on the ladder deck, more than one pair, moving fast, and the last thing I hear before we run is their voice, low, almost gentle:

"Try to keep up. You're slower than you used to be."
