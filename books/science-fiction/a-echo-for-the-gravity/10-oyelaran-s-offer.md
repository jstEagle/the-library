## 10. Oyelaran's Offer

Oyelaran had boarded forty-one derelicts in her career and could count on one hand the ones that had frightened her.

This one didn't frighten her either, exactly. But it sat wrong in her chest as the corvette's tender crossed the two hundred meters of vacuum between the *Kestrel's Mercy* and the old freighter, its running lights sweeping across a hull so old it had gone the color of bone. The registry read *Meridian's Daughter*, which was impossible, because that name had been struck from every fleet record ninety years ago, and yet there it was, stenciled in flaking paint along the bow, patient as a grave marker.

"Truce protocols active," said Ensign Vau from the tender's second seat. "They've acknowledged. One voice only, no weapons."

"One voice," Oyelaran repeated. "Good."

She did not say whose voice she expected. She had known since the corvette's long-range array first caught the derelict's cold transponder singing to itself in the dark—known the way you know a scar on your own body, without looking. Her grandmother had made her memorize the registry numbers of the lost ships when she was nine years old, sitting on a stool in the archive vault on Ceres Verge, reciting them like scripture while the climate systems hummed and the council's seal glowed on the wall above the door.

*Meridian's Daughter* had been number eleven on a list of one hundred and six.

The tender kissed the freighter's docking collar with a clang that traveled up through Oyelaran's boots. Beyond the airlock, the ship breathed. That was the wrong word for it—ships didn't breathe—but the atmosphere cycling through the ancient vents made a sound like slow lungs, and the temperature readout on her suit's wrist display climbed from four degrees toward something almost kind.

"They're heating the corridor for us," Vau said. "That's... hospitable."

"That's an invitation." Oyelaran sealed her helmet down anyway. "Mercy is a currency. I want to see what they think we're buying."

* * *

The navigator met them at the junction of the second ring, alone, hands visible, wearing a plain shipsuit with no insignia. Oyelaran had pulled the file twice on the crossing: Vesper Ravensworth, cargo navigator, twelve years of clean runs through deconflicted lanes, no debts, no affiliations, no history before the age of twenty-two that any database would admit to. A person built like a blank wall.

Except the wall had a door in it now, and behind the door were two hundred cryopods, and Oyelaran's grandmother's voice was in her ear saying *number eleven, say it back to me, child,* and the whole careful architecture of the Ravensworth anonymity looked suddenly like what it had always been: a hiding place with someone inside it.

"Captain Oyelaran." The navigator's voice was level. "You're welcome aboard. Truce terms are posted at the collar. You'll notice they're generous."

"I noticed." Oyelaran let her helmet cycle open. The air smelled of coolant and old paper, oddly, though no ship this age should have paper anywhere in it. "Generous terms usually mean the person offering them has already decided how this ends."

"And yours?"

"Mine are simple. I'm here to offer you mercy, Navigator. I'd like to do it sitting down, if your ship still has chairs."

Vesper studied her for a long moment—the look of someone weighing not whether she was dangerous but whether she was honest—and then turned and led them aft.

The galley of the *Meridian's Daughter* had been restored to function with scavenged parts that didn't match: a modern heat plate bolted beside a pre-Collapse reclamation unit, new cabling run through conduits older than the fleet itself. Doss, the engineer—big, gray-stubbled, radiating the specific fury of a man who had been lied to recently and thoroughly—sat at the table with his arms crossed. Mireille, the pilot, stood by the viewport with her back half-turned, watching the corvette's running lights the way sailors watch weather.

And in the corner, wrapped in a thermal blanket, sat a nineteen-year-old who had Vesper Ravensworth's face.

Oyelaran kept her expression still. It cost her something. She had prepared for the sleepers; her briefings had covered the sleepers; the writ she carried, three centuries old and technically still in force, spoke of "biological assets in suspended animation" in language so antiseptic it might have been describing seed grain. But a copy awake, drinking tea, watching her with young eyes full of an anger that had nowhere left to go—that was different. That was the archive made flesh.

"You've been busy," Oyelaran said, taking the offered seat.

"We've been reading," Vesper said. "Your writ predates the Collapse. Salvage rights under Fleet Ordinance 9-Kestrel. Do you actually believe in it, or is it just a stick?"

"It's both. Sticks are useful." Oyelaran folded her hands. "But I'm not here for the stick. I'm here because I'm the last person in the Verge who will ever offer you what I'm about to offer, and after tonight the only people coming to this ship will come with cutting torches instead of tea."

Mireille turned from the viewport. "Then say it."

Oyelaran looked at each of them in turn—at the engineer whose knuckles had gone white, at the pilot whose jaw was set like a woman bracing for a wave, at the navigator whose face gave away nothing and therefore everything—and finally at the copy in the corner, who had stopped pretending to drink.

"My family," Oyelaran said, "were archivists. Not councilors—archivists. We kept the records the founding council didn't want kept, and we kept them because someone had to, and because my line took an oath in the last year of Earth that said the truth outlives the men who bury it." She paused. "I am telling you this under truce, knowing what it costs me, so that you understand I am not lying to you about what follows."

"Go on," Vesper said quietly.

"The final transmission loop. The message from Earth. *We remain. We remember you. Come home when you can.* Every child in the Verge learns it before they learn their own name. It holds the alliance together—it's the only thing that does. Three fleets, eleven habitats, forty years of grudges, all of it balanced on the knowledge that somewhere behind us, home is still speaking." Oyelaran drew a breath. "It ended eighty-nine years ago. The loop you hear today is a recording of a recording, maintained by relay stations that have never once been allowed to inspect the source. And the reason no one inspects the source is that the source is empty. Earth went silent during the Collapse. The founding council knew within the year. And rather than tell eleven habitats that they were orphans, they fabricated the loop, and they wrote into the fleet charter a provision—Ordinance 9-Kestrel, the very writ I carry—for the destruction of any evidence proving the fabrication, wherever found."

The galley was silent except for the breathing of the vents.

"They hid the proof where no one would ever go looking," Oyelaran continued. "In the dead ships. In the archives of the abandonment flotilla—the vessels that fled Earth carrying the raw logs, the unedited transmissions, the testimony of the crews who watched the homeworld's signal die and were ordered to forget it. Most of those ships were scuttled. A few were lost. Number eleven on the loss registry was listed as destroyed with all hands." She nodded slowly toward the aft of the ship, toward the cold heart of it. "*Meridian's Daughter*. Your ship, Navigator. Or your family's. Which means the hold you opened three days ago contains the most dangerous cargo in human space, and every hour it exists, the alliance that keeps forty million people from killing each other over fuel and orbit rights gets closer to finding out that its foundation is a kindness told by liars."

"You said mercy," Doss growled. "Get to it."

"I'm getting to it." Oyelaran turned to face him fully. "Here is the mercy. Destroy the hold. All of it—the pods, the logs, the copies. Render it to slag and let me report the derelict found already gutted by wreckers, its contents unrecoverable. In exchange: full amnesty under syndicate law. New registries, clean ledgers, protection for the *Long Answer* and everyone aboard it for as long as any of you live. The Ravensworth name stops being a target. The navigator stops being a ghost. And the Verge goes on believing its mothers and fathers are still alive at the far end of the dark—which, I will remind you, is the only belief keeping it fed, fueled, and at peace."

"And the sleepers?" Vesper asked. Their voice hadn't risen. Somehow that was worse.

"Gone," Oyelaran said simply. "Before they wake. They won't suffer. Cryo-death is gentler than anything else in this universe."

"They're people."

"They're *evidence*, Navigator. Two hundred copies of one person, grown from backup stock by a program that was itself illegal, carrying in their shared memory the blueprint of a lie that would burn down civilization if it ever got out." Oyelaran leaned forward. "I have read the raw logs. I read them when I was twenty-two years old, in my grandmother's vault, the night she died. I know exactly what's in that hold, and I am telling you—as the last living member of the family that swore to protect it—that protecting it is no longer possible. The council's descendants sit on every ruling seat in the Kestrel Verge. They don't call themselves a council anymore; they call themselves the Concordance, they wear nicer clothes, and they have corvettes faster than mine. When they learn this ship exists—and they will learn, because nothing stays hidden in the Verge—they will not send a woman with tea. They will send fire. And they will burn the *Long Answer* with it, and your crew, and every station that ever harbored you, and the loop will keep playing its beautiful lie until the relays decay, and no one will ever know there was another choice."

"So we should choose your lie instead," Mireille said. "Because it's older."

"I'm asking you to choose *mercy*," Oyelaran said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, and hated it. "There is a version of this story where everyone lives. It requires the hold to die. I have spent thirty years looking for that version. This is it. There isn't another one coming."

"There's always another one coming," said the copy in the corner.

Oyelaran looked at the young face—her own anger reflected back from thirty years ago, from a night in a vault with a dead woman's keys in her hand—and felt something in her chest shift like ballast.

"Young man," she said carefully, "you don't know what—"

"I know I'm evidence." The copy set down the untouched tea. "I've had two days to get used to it. You want to melt us down for the peace of mind of forty million people who are being lied to. My—" a glance at Vesper, quick and strange, the two of them sharing a face and a grief and neither willing to name it— "*our* mother died believing the loop. Believing Earth was still back there, still calling. If the lie is kind, fine. But she deserved to be lied to *knowingly*, by choice, not managed like cargo. And so does everyone else out there." He pointed aft, toward the pods, toward the sleeping rows. "You're offering amnesty for a massacre and calling it mercy. I've read enough of the logs to know where that language comes from. Ordinance 9-Kestrel. Section four. 'Humane disposition of destabilizing assets.' Your grandmother helped write it."

Oyelaran didn't answer. There was nothing to answer with. He was right, and he was nineteen years old, and he was right.

* * *

She walked the ring afterward, alone, under truce, because she had asked and Vesper had permitted it—an act of trust so reckless it told her more about the navigator than anything said at the table.

The cryo bay doors stood open. Frost feathered the frames of two hundred pods, and inside each one slept a face she had seen once already at a galley table, multiplied across ages she couldn't parse: a child of eight, a youth of fifteen, a woman of fifty with gray at the temples, all of them Vesper Ravensworth, all of them the same grief worn by different bodies. Near the bay's center, one pod bore a hand-lettered label beneath the serial code, the letters faded but legible:

**A. RAVENSWORTH — ORIGINAL PATTERN — DO NOT CYCLE WITHOUT CONSENT**

Oyelaran stood in front of it for a long time.

Her grandmother had died whispering registry numbers. *Say it back to me, child.* One hundred and six ships. One hundred and five confirmed destroyed. Number eleven, unaccounted for, for ninety years—and here it was, warm and lit and full of ghosts, and the oath of her line standing in the frost like a third person in the room.

*The truth outlives the men who bury it.*

But the truth also killed, she thought. She had seen the Concordance's work before—a station called Little Ferro, scrubbed of nine hundred souls because a data broker found three lines of an authentic Earth-era log. The alliance wasn't a lie holding up peace. It was a dam holding back a war, and everyone proposing to breach it kept imagining a gentle stream on the other side.

"Captain."

Vesper stood in the bay doorway. Alone again. Always alone, this one, even surrounded by two hundred versions of themselves.

"You've been quiet a long time," the navigator said.

"I'm deciding whether I'm an archivist or a syndicate captain," Oyelaran said. "It turns out I can't be both tonight."

"And which one wins?"

Oyelaran looked at the pod labeled with a mother's name. Then at Vesper.

"When I leave this ship," she said, "my corvette will shadow you for six hours and then break off toward Ceres Verge, where I will file a report. What that report says depends on what happens in the next day. So I'll tell you the thing I came here to tell you underneath the offer, the thing my grandmother told me the night she died, and then I'll go." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice as if the pods could hear. "The transmission loop decays below recovery threshold in less than two days. After that, even the lie dies, and the Verge has nothing at all—not the truth, not the comfort, just silence. Whatever you're going to do, Navigator, do it before the dark finishes eating the signal. Because there are people on my crew, and stations in this system, and relays older than the Concordance itself, that can still carry a voice—if anyone is brave enough to put their name on one."

Vesper's eyes changed. Something moved behind them, some decision rotating into alignment like a lock mechanism finding its tumblers.

"Thank you, Captain," the navigator said. "For the tea."

"For the mercy," Oyelaran corrected. "Whether you take it or not."

She walked back up the ring to the docking collar, and the tender took her home across two hundred meters of black, and behind her the *Meridian's Daughter* burned its small lights in the dark like a held breath.

On the corvette's bridge, Ensign Vau asked for heading.

Oyelaran stood at the viewport a moment longer, watching the old freighter, thinking of number eleven on a list of one hundred and six, thinking of her grandmother's oath and her own, thinking of a pod with a mother's name on it and a navigator who had looked at her, in the end, not with hatred but with something far more dangerous: recognition.

"Lay in a course for Ceres Verge," she said. "And then hold here. Full shadow. Nobody files anything until I say."

Vau hesitated. "Captain, the Concordance expects our report in—"

"I know what they expect." Oyelaran settled into her chair, and the old freighter's lights slid across her face, and for the first time in thirty years she let herself wonder what her grandmother would have done. "What they expect and what they get are about to become very different things. Watch the freighter, Ensign. And watch the lane to Relay Station Kettle."

Vau frowned. "Why Kettle?"

"Because," Oyelaran said, "it's the only transmitter left in the Verge old enough to speak a dead language—and somebody on that ship just realized it."
