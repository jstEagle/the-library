## 16. Relay Station Kettle

Iqbal had run Relay Station Kettle for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned one law of the Verge: nothing good ever arrived without a transponder.

The station sat at the edge of the Shoals like a kettle left on a dead stove—hence the name, given by some long-dead joker with a sense of humor Iqbal had inherited along with the lease. It was a free port, which meant it belonged to no council, no syndicate, no fleet charter. It belonged to whoever could keep its air scrubbers turning and its ancient transmitter warm. Iqbal kept both. He was sixty-eight years old, half-blind in his left eye from a pressure accident he never talked about, and he had made a quiet career out of seeing everything and recording almost none of it.

So when the proximity alarm sang at 0400 station time, and his scope showed a cargo hauler limping in on a wounded drive signature—no transponder ping, hull scorched along the starboard flank, atmosphere venting in a thin silver thread—Iqbal did what he always did. He put down his tea. He opened the channel.

"Unidentified vessel, this is Relay Station Kettle. You are cleared to approach berth three. I have patch foam, a welder, and opinions. Nothing else."

The reply took eleven seconds. Long enough for him to count them.

"Kettle, this is the *Long Answer*. We have wounded. Requesting dock."

A woman's voice. Steady, but worn through in the middle, like a cable stressed past its rating. Behind her, faintly, someone was shouting about coolant pressure.

Iqbal looked at the scope again. The *Long Answer*. He knew the name—not because it was famous, but because it wasn't. Twelve years running deconflicted corridors, clean manifests, boring flight plans filed by a navigator who had never once been flagged by any authority in the Verge. In Iqbal's experience, that kind of invisibility was never an accident. It was a discipline. And disciplines that careful usually meant somebody was hiding from something specific.

He also noticed the burn pattern on their hull. Plasma scoring, fresh, in the distinctive fan-spread of syndicate hunter batteries. And he noticed something else: the derelict registry fragment still clinging to their docking clamps, a pre-Collapse freighter's coupling sheared off and dragged along like a wedding ring pulled from a drowned man's finger.

"Well," Iqbal said to his empty control room. "That's interesting."

He keyed the channel. "*Long Answer*, you are cleared. Berth three. Bring your wounded slow, because my med bay is a first-aid kit bolted to a wall, and if your drive lets go inside my ring, I will bill your estate."

"We'll try to keep our estate," the woman said dryly.

Iqbal liked her already. That worried him. Liking people was how you ended up involved.

* * *

They came aboard at 0437, and Iqbal counted them as they crossed into his ring: four living, one stretcher, and a fifth who moved like a ghost wearing borrowed skin.

The pilot—the voice, Mireille, according to the manifest she shoved at him—was all business, signing his intake slate with quick strokes while her eyes kept flicking back to the airlock. The engineer came through supporting the stretcher himself, refusing help, blood soaked through a field dressing wrapped around his ribs. A young man, maybe thirty, gray-faced, conscious only through what looked like pure stubbornness.

And then the navigator.

Vesper Ravensworth. Iqbal recognized the name from twelve years of unremarkable manifests. But he hadn't expected the person attached to it to look like that—like someone standing in a room where every wall was a mirror and hating what the mirrors were showing. Vesper's eyes went around the station's docking bay once, cataloguing it, and stopped on the bulkhead behind Iqbal's office, where an old brass plaque read:

**KETTLE STATION — EST. PRE-COLLAPSE — MERIDIAN LINE RELAY 7**

Vesper stared at that plaque the way people stare at graves.

"You're staring at my grandfather's sign," Iqbal said.

Vesper blinked. "Your grandfather built this?"

"My grandfather *worked* this. Meridian Line, before the Collapse. He ran relay traffic for the last ships out of Earth orbit." Iqbal shrugged. "When the Line folded, the station passed to whoever could pay the air bill. That's been me, going on three decades." He tilted his head. "You read Meridian?"

Something crossed Vesper's face—fast, quickly buried, but not fast enough. Iqbal had spent thirty-one years reading faces across a docking counter. Smugglers, refugees, syndicate runners, council auditors. He knew the difference between a lie and a secret, and what he'd just seen was neither. It was grief.

"A little," Vesper said. "It's a dead language."

"It's a sleeping language," Iqbal corrected. "There's a difference. Dead things stay dead. Sleeping things—" He let it hang there, and watched Vesper decide whether to pick up the thread.

They didn't. They turned to follow the stretcher toward the med bay, and Iqbal let them go, and made a note in his private log that he would probably regret: *Berth three. Something old is awake.*

* * *

The engineer—Doss—was the worst of it. Fragment wound, three ribs gone to shrapnel, a lung trying to collapse. Iqbal's med bay was exactly what he'd advertised: a first-aid kit bolted to a wall, plus a regen cradle older than most of the crew's parents. They got Doss into it anyway, and the cradle hummed, and Mireille stood over it with her arms crossed like her posture alone could hold the man together.

Iqbal brought tea. Real tea, hoarded, terrible by any objective standard. Mireille took hers without looking away from the cradle.

"He shielded the drive with his body," she said. Not a question. Just a fact she was holding up to the light and finding heavier than expected.

"They do that, sometimes. Engineers." Iqbal sipped his own tea. "You want to tell me why the syndicate shot at a cargo hauler running deconflicted lanes? Because in my experience they don't waste ammunition on boring people."

Mireille finally looked at him. She had the kind of face that made decisions quickly and paid for them slowly. "How much do you charge for not asking?"

"Nothing. I never ask. Asking creates records." He set down his cup. "But I'll tell you something for free, since you're bleeding in my station. There's a syndicate corvette sitting at the edge of my sensor range. Been there six hours. It hasn't hailed me, hasn't moved, hasn't done anything except exist where I can see it. That's not a patrol. That's a stakeout. And now you've arrived, burned and hunted, dragging a piece of pre-Collapse hardware on your clamps." He met her eyes. "Whatever you're carrying, it's about to become my problem too. Free ports don't stay free by being surprised."

Mireille held his gaze for a long moment. Then, quietly: "If I told you, would you turn us in?"

"I've turned in exactly two people in thirty-one years. One was slaving. The other owed me money." He smiled without warmth. "Truth doesn't scare me. I'm too old and too well-insulated. What scares me is being lied to badly, in my own house."

She exhaled. "Then ask me tomorrow. Ask me when we know if Doss lives. I can't carry it and him at the same time."

Iqbal nodded slowly. Fair terms. He'd lived long enough to respect rationing.

* * *

He found Vesper at 0900, standing in the transmitter room.

This was not a public space. This was very much not a public space. Iqbal kept the door locked and the room cold, because the machine inside was the last working Meridian-band transmitter in the Verge—a cathedral of copper and glass, hand-wound coils, vacuum tubes he machined replacements for in his own shop. His grandfather's machine. The thing that had spoken for dying Earth when Earth still had words left.

Vesper stood in front of it with one hand raised, not touching, hovering a centimeter from the housing like a pilgrim afraid the relic might object.

"I locked that door," Iqbal said from the threshold.

"I know. I'm sorry." Vesper didn't turn around. "Mireille asked you to show us the station systems. I got... separated."

"Mm. Separated. Down a locked corridor. Past a keypad." Iqbal came into the room and stood beside them, looking at his own machine. "My grandfather used to say this thing had a voice like God clearing his throat. Bandwidth nobody uses anymore. Modulation protocols the modern rigs can't even parse. It speaks Old Meridian or it speaks nothing." He glanced sideways. "Which raises the obvious question of why a cargo navigator who claims to know 'a little' of a dead language is looking at my transmitter like it's the last exit off a burning ship."

Vesper's hand lowered. For a moment they said nothing, and Iqbal waited, because waiting was ninety percent of his job.

"There's a message," Vesper said finally. "An old one. Earth's final transmission loop. The one every schoolchild learns. *We leave so that something of us continues.*"

"I know it. Everyone knows it. My grandfather helped relay it." Iqbal frowned. "What about it?"

"It's false." The words came out flat, stripped of drama, which somehow made them worse. "Edited. The founding council cut the real final transmission and replaced it with something bearable. The original is still out there, decaying in the loop, and it's going to fall below recoverable threshold soon. Hours, maybe. And I have proof. I have—" Vesper's voice caught, restarted. "I have witnesses. Two hundred of them. Asleep."

Iqbal set down his tea. He found that his hands wanted something to do.

"You understand what you're describing," he said. "The Loop is the spine of the whole Verge. Every charter, every alliance, every treaty between the fleet factions—it all hangs on that message. *We left for hope.* Take it away and you're not correcting history. You're pulling the keel out of the boat."

"Yes."

"And the syndicate corvette at my fence line is here because of it."

"Yes."

Iqbal walked a slow circuit around the transmitter, touching the cooling fins, feeling the familiar warmth of the standby current. Thirty-one years he'd kept this machine alive for no reason anyone could name. Maintenance without purpose. Faith without doctrine. His grandfather had died believing the old band would matter again someday, and Iqbal had privately considered that sentiment a charming delusion he was willing to fund.

"Here's my problem," he said. "I can give you this transmitter. It'll reach every receiver in the Verge that still has a Meridian coil—and there are more of those than anyone admits, in museums, in chapels, in old family shrines. People who kept the dead tongue the way you keep a grandmother's recipe. The message would go out. It would be heard." He stopped in front of Vesper. "But a broadcast without an author is noise. Anonymous truth gets dismissed as forgery within the hour. The council's archivists are very good at dismissing things. If you want this to be *history* instead of static, someone has to stand behind it. Name, voice, face. Someone has to claim authorship and accept what comes with it."

"What comes with it," Vesper repeated.

"Treason, probably. Terrorism, certainly—the council will choose the ugliest word available. Exile, seizure of assets, a bounty that follows you into every port that fears them more than they love truth." Iqbal held up a hand before Vesper could answer. "Don't tell me yet. I've watched you since you docked. You walk like a man deciding something enormous, and I don't want your answer while it's still cooking. But I'll tell you what my grandfather told me, the only thing he ever said about the Collapse that sounded like scripture instead of complaint."

Vesper turned to face him fully. Up close, Iqbal could see the exhaustion carved under their eyes, the particular hollowed-out look of someone who had spent twelve years being no one and had recently discovered what that cost.

"He said: *A witness who stays silent isn't neutral. He's just on the other side quietly.*" Iqbal tapped the transmitter's housing. "This machine has been silent for thirty-one years. So have I. So, apparently, have you. Maybe it's time one of us stopped."

* * *

At 1400, the syndicate corvette finally spoke.

The hail came through on all bands, formal as a funeral: *Relay Station Kettle, this is salvage vessel Oyelaran's Banner, operating under writ of claim 4471-Kestrel. Be advised: a stolen vessel of interest is believed to be docked at your facility. Surrender of the vessel and its cargo is required under the writ. Harbor masters obstructing lawful claim forfeit free-port status.*

Iqbal read the message twice, then forwarded it to Mireille without comment, then walked back to the transmitter room, where Vesper stood exactly where he'd left them, as though they'd grown roots.

"That corvette just made me an accessory," Iqbal said. "In writing. Which means my window for neutrality just closed, and I find I'm not sorry about it." He pulled a keyring from his belt—an actual physical key, brass, older than Vesper probably—and unlocked a cabinet beneath the transmitter's main console. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a microphone. Not a modern pickup. A Meridian-era broadcast mic, its grille shaped like a rising sun, the maker's plate worn smooth by decades of hands.

His grandfather's microphone. The one that had carried the last true words out of Earth orbit—or so the family had always believed. Iqbal felt a strange vertigo, realizing that belief might itself be part of the falsification, that even his inheritance had been edited.

He held it out.

"When you're ready," he said, "you'll speak into this. Your name, your voice, on the record, in whatever language carries it furthest. And when the council comes—and they will come, they always come—you'll be standing right here, next to me, because I am apparently a sentimental old man who has decided your war is mine." He paused. "One condition."

Vesper took the microphone. Their hands were shaking. "Name it."

"When you broadcast—if you broadcast—I want to hear the original message first. The real one. Before the world hears it, I want to hear what my grandfather actually relayed, or failed to." His voice roughened despite himself. "Thirty-one years I've kept this station for a story. I'd like to know if the story was true."

Vesper looked down at the sun-shaped grille in their hands, and Iqbal saw something resolve in their face—the last hesitation hardening into decision, the way water hardens into ice.

"You'll hear it," Vesper said. "Everyone will hear it. Tonight, before the loop goes dark forever."

Outside the viewport, far off at the edge of the Shoals, the syndicate corvette began to move.
