## 2. Dreams in the Wrong Grammar

The dream always began the same way: a corridor of water standing upright, walls of it, and Cyrus walking down the middle without getting wet. In the old dreams—the ones from before, from Earth, from the life he'd packed into two crates and a resignation letter—he had understood the water. It meant something. Grief, maybe, or memory, or just his brain rehearsing the loneliness it practiced all day. He'd been able to say so, even inside the dream. *This is about Mother's kitchen. This is about the fellowship I didn't get.* The dream would accept the interpretation the way a dog accepts a name, tail thumping, and move on.

The new dreams did not accept interpretation. They declined it.

He woke at 0417 station time, which was a fiction—Horizon beacon kept no windows, and dead space kept no dawn—but the clock insisted, and so he sat up in his bunk in the dark and reached for the recorder before the dream could finish evaporating. That was the discipline. Twelve years of xenolinguistic fieldwork had taught him that the first ninety seconds after waking were the only reliable window; after that, the dreaming mind rewrote itself to fit the waking one, smoothing its strange edges into something presentable. His job, once, had been to catch languages before they dressed themselves in translation.

"Journal entry," he said, voice cracked with sleep. "Cyrus Villalobos, day four hundred and six aboard Horizon. Recording."

The recorder's amber light pulsed. He stared at the ceiling panel, at the hairline fracture in its coating that he had long ago decided looked like a river delta seen from orbit, and tried to hold the dream in front of him without touching it.

"There was the corridor of water again," he said. "But this time it wasn't a corridor. It was—" He stopped. Started again. "Okay. Here's what I can tell you. In the dream, I knew that the corridor was also a sentence. Not metaphorically. I knew it the way you know your own name. And the sentence was inside another sentence, and the outer one was about the inner one, and the inner one was about me walking through it, and there was a third layer underneath that I couldn't reach but I could feel it the way you feel weather coming through a bulkhead." He rubbed his eyes. "And here's the part I need you to hear, journal, because I don't have anyone else to say it to. None of those layers were nested like human grammar nests. It wasn't clause inside clause. It was more like... each sentence contained the others the way a word contains its letters. Simultaneously. All of them true at once, all of them visible, and my dream-self could read them all without effort, and when I woke up I lost the ability in the same way you lose a chord the instant your fingers leave the strings."

He let the silence run for a moment. The station breathed around him—air handlers, coolant pumps, the deep slow hum of the beacon's transmitter cycling through its assigned frequencies toward nothing. Four hundred and six days of that sound. He knew its moods better than he'd ever known a lover's.

"I want to be clear that I understand how this sounds," he said finally. "I'm not reporting an encounter. There was no entity, no contact event, no signal received on any band I monitor—which is all of them, by the way, that's the whole job. This is a structural observation about my own cognition, and I am making it as carefully as I can, because either my sleeping mind has begun organizing experience according to a syntax that does not occur in any of the six thousand documented human languages, or I am losing my grip, and I would very much like a colleague to tell me which."

He almost laughed. A colleague. The nearest human being capable of reviewing his work was eleven light-months away, and the last message from the Institute had arrived ninety-three days ago, a form letter confirming receipt of his quarterly report and reminding him of the voluntary nature of fringe postings. Voluntary. As if anyone volunteered for a station that appeared in no registry, built by no contract anyone would claim, staffed by one man whose appointment letter had come from a department that, when he'd tried to verify it before departure, turned out to share a floor with three empty offices and a vending machine.

He filed the thought where he filed all thoughts about the station's provenance—in the drawer marked NOT TONIGHT—and continued.

*

Morning shift began, as it always did, with the recitation.

This was not regulation. Regulation required him to log telemetry, inspect the emitter arrays via drone, calibrate the phase locks, and confirm the beacon's power draw against projections. Which he did. But twelve years of fieldwork among dying languages had taught him something no manual covered: a language that goes unspoken doesn't fade evenly. It dies in its peripheries first—the songs, the jokes, the words for specific kinds of rain—and retreats into whatever core its last speaker refuses to surrender. So every morning, walking the ring corridor from habitat to control, Cyrus spoke aloud, narrating his checks in the language he no longer dreamed in.

English. His mother's kitchen English, the flat vowels of the estuary town where he'd grown up, not the seminar-room standard he'd been trained into.

"Emitter array one, nominal," he said to the corridor, to the drones, to himself. "Phase lock holding at point-three degrees of drift, which is within tolerance but only because tolerance is generous out here. Coolant loop pressure steady. Transmitter output consistent with schedule. Nobody is listening. Good morning anyway."

The words came easily, which was the problem. They came too easily now, worn smooth as river stones, and each morning he noticed their smoothness more. Language used daily should develop burrs, idiosyncrasies, the private wear-patterns of a single mouth. His English was becoming a recording of itself. Meanwhile the thing in his dreams grew more fluent every night.

At the control console he pulled up the night's passive-spectrum record, more from ritual than hope. The beacon listened to a sphere of space roughly forty light-years across, and in four hundred and six days that sphere had offered him pulsar ticks, hydrogen line hiss, the faint gravitational cough of something colliding beyond detection range, and silence. Beautiful, professional, absolute silence. The Vaal—the name the survey teams had given the extinct civilization whose ruins seeded this sector—had been dust for a hundred thousand years. Whatever the beacon was for, it wasn't listening for them. Nothing left to hear.

He ran the diagnostics anyway. Then, because the dream was still sitting in him like a stone swallowed wrong, he opened his personal directory and looked at the folder he'd been avoiding for a week.

DREAM-JOURNAL_ANALYSIS. Inside it, seventeen entries. Inside those, the work he'd told himself he wasn't doing: transcriptions. He had begun, without quite admitting it, to transcribe the structure of the dreams—not their content, content was irrelevant, but their grammar. He'd diagrammed them the way he'd once diagrammed Kessari oral epics, mapping how each image related to the next, and the diagrams had shown him something he still couldn't make himself say plainly.

Human syntax, spoken or signed, was fundamentally serial. One thing at a time, arranged in order, meaning accumulating along a line. Even our most recursive constructions were recursion-in-a-line, clauses stacked like train cars.

His dream-diagrams weren't lines. They were braids.

He pulled up the newest one and made himself look at it again. Three strands of imagery—the water corridor, a figure standing at its end, a sound like a bell heard underwater—and instead of sequencing them, his sleeping mind had interwoven them such that each strand passed through the others at regular intervals, and the intervals themselves carried meaning. He knew they carried meaning because in the dream he had understood them, effortlessly, the way a native speaker understands tone. The braid wasn't decoration. The braid was the sentence.

"No known human language encodes primary semantic structure in simultaneous parallel channels," he said aloud, dictating into the analysis file. "Some sign languages use spatial parallelism productively. Music does something adjacent. But this isn't adjacent. This is a different solution to the problem of meaning, and my unconscious is using it fluently, and I did not learn it, because there is nowhere to learn it from."

Unless.

He hated the word before he finished thinking it. Unless there was somewhere. Unless the beacon's listening sphere wasn't as silent as its spectrum logs claimed. Unless silence, at some frequency or modulation he hadn't thought to define, could still teach.

"That's not science," he told the empty room sternly. "That's ghost-story logic. You're a linguist. Act like one."

But his hands were already pulling up the transmission logs, and his hands had been doing a lot of things lately that the rest of him hadn't authorized.

*

The report took him most of the day, and he wrote it three times.

The first draft was honest and unusable: *I believe my dreams are being structured by an external grammatical system, possibly transmitted, possibly environmental, and I request immediate psychological evaluation and a review of the beacon's emission history.* He deleted it because it read like a man asking to be relieved of duty, and he was not certain he wanted to be relieved, and he was less certain why.

The second draft was clinical to the point of dishonesty: *Minor sleep-architecture anomalies noted; cognitive function unaffected; requesting routine consultation.* He deleted it because if anything was actually happening to him, soft-pedaling it might cost him the window in which it could be studied—or stopped.

The third draft, the one he sent, threaded the needle as best he could:

*To: Directorate of Xenolinguistic Sciences, Meridian Station (routing via Relay Nine). From: C. Villalobos, custodian, Horizon Deep-Space Beacon. Classification: personnel welfare / research inquiry.*

*Summary: Over the past approximately thirty days, my dream cognition has undergone a progressive structural change. Dream content is increasingly organized according to a syntax exhibiting simultaneous multi-channel nesting rather than serial recursion. I have attached seventeen transcribed dream-diagrams. I emphasize that I am not reporting hallucination, contact, or distress; my waking cognition and functional capacity are normal. However, as a xenolinguist I can state with professional confidence that the structures in question are non-human in type. I have no explanation for their origin. I request: (1) consultation with a cognitive linguist familiar with sleep-state acquisition; (2) any available records concerning prior Horizon custodians' psychological reports; (3) confirmation that this message has been received.*

*Villalobos out.*

That last line. He'd added it, deleted it, added it back. Confirmation of receipt. Ninety-three days since the last reply, and form letters didn't count as replies, and somewhere in the back of his skull a voice he didn't like very much kept pointing out that a man who sends reports into a void and receives nothing back cannot actually prove the void contains a recipient. For all he could demonstrate, the Institute had forgotten Horizon existed. For all he could demonstrate, Horizon had never existed. The station's own records were no help—it had construction dates but no construction crews, a commissioning order but no commissioning authority, a maintenance history written in a bureaucratic hand that matched no institutional style guide he could find. He'd spent his first month here trying to trace its paperwork the way he'd once traced loanwords to their source, and the trail had ended, every time, in the documentary equivalent of the corridor of water: layers containing layers, and at the bottom, nothing.

He queued the report for the next relay window—six hours out—and then, because waiting was the one thing he'd never learned, he went back to the diagrams.

*

He worked until the station's evening chime, which existed purely because he'd programmed it, wanting some marker of days. Rice and protein, eaten at the console. Outside the viewport—there was exactly one, a concession to psychology in the habitat ring—the dead stars wheeled their slow indifferent wheel. He had named none of them. Naming felt like presumption.

It was during the second pot of tea that he caught himself doing it.

He'd been re-examining diagram nine, the clearest of the set, muttering observations under his breath the way he always did—"note the interval regularity, note how strand two carries the temporal marker"—when he realized the muting had changed. Not the words. The shape of them. For perhaps ten seconds, his running commentary had abandoned English syntax entirely. He'd been speaking in the braid-form, folding each observation inside the previous one, both held simultaneously, and it had felt—this was the part that made him set down the cup with exaggerated care—natural. Fluent. Like slipping into a dialect of home.

He played back the recorder. There it was: 1942 station time, eleven seconds of audio in which Cyrus Villalobos, awake, alone, and sober, produced speech whose structure matched his dream-diagrams with a fidelity that made the skin of his arms prickle.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Okay. Waking bleed-through. That's—that's a data point. That's a significant data point."

His hands were not entirely steady when he logged it.

He sat for a long time afterward, watching the stars wheel, doing the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do. Thirty days of changing dreams. Now waking intrusion. The progression was not random; it had the clean gradient of an acquisition curve. He had watched that curve a dozen times in his career, in children acquiring creoles, in adults immersed in second languages, in the last speakers of dying tongues reaching for words that came faster in the other language. Immersion produced it. Total immersion produced it fast.

Which raised the question he had been circling for a month and could no longer refuse to land on: immersed in what?

The station was sealed. The air was filtered, monitored, chemically boring. The spectrum was silent. There was no input. And yet his mind was learning a grammar the way dry ground learns rain.

Unless the input wasn't arriving. Unless it was already here. Unless the beacon itself—this station no one ordered, built by no one, staffed by him alone—was not a listener at all but something else, something wearing a listener's shape, and he had spent four hundred and six days sleeping inside its mouth.

He laughed, once, without humor. Ghost-story logic again. But the laugh didn't dislodge the thought, and the thought had brought friends.

He forced himself through the closing rituals: final sweep of the arrays, backup of the day's logs, physical inspection of the transmitter housing because motion helped. In the control room he checked the relay window—his report would go out in forty minutes, riding the tightbeam to Relay Nine, and from there, eventually, to people who might answer. Might. He stood in the quiet and let himself want that, plainly, the way he rarely permitted: an answer. A voice on the other end saying *received, understood, you're not crazy, we're looking into it.* He was forty-four years old and he wanted, with a child's whole heart, for someone to tell him the world still had rules.

The transmission went out on schedule. The confirmation handshake returned from Relay Nine automatically, a machine acknowledging a machine. Message accepted. Delivery pending.

Delivery pending. He'd learn to hate those three words over the following weeks, though he didn't know it yet.

*

He slept badly, which was to say he slept deeply and woke unrested, and the dream was waiting for him at the surface like something that had learned his schedule.

This one was different. This one had moved.

No water corridor this time. Instead: a vast interior space he somehow knew was a vault, though it resembled no vault built by human hands—more a lattice than a room, crystalline filaments branching through darkness, and along every filament, light moving in pulses. Not flickering. Speaking. Each pulse a syllable, each filament a voice, thousands of voices braided into a single architecture, and the architecture was remembering. He couldn't say how he knew that. He knew it the way the dream-mind knows everything: totally, without evidence, without doubt. The vault was full of someone's memories, and the memories were talking to each other, and the conversation was the storage medium, and—

And one filament, near the center, had gone dark.

In the dream this was catastrophic. He felt the loss the way you feel a missing tooth—not the pain but the wrongness, the tongue returning again and again to the gap. A voice had stopped. The braid had a hole in it. And the remaining voices were turning, slowly, all together, like a school of fish changing direction, toward the gap, toward him, and the attention of them was not hostile, it was worse than hostile, it was hopeful—

He woke at 0338 with his heart slamming and the recorder already in his hand.

"Journal," he said. "Day four hundred seven. New dream morphology. Previous entries featured me inside the structure. This one featured the structure attending to me. I want that on the record precisely: I did not feel observed. I felt *awaited.*" His voice shook and he let it. "There was a dark filament. A voice that stopped. And I understood—I understood in the dream, the way you understand things there—that the structure was incomplete, and that incompleteness was the reason for the attention, and I woke up before I learned what they wanted, and God help me, journal, some part of me wants to go back and find out, and that is the single most frightening sentence I have ever recorded."

He sat in the dark afterward, listening to the station breathe.

Then, because routine was the hull between him and the vacuum, he got up, made coffee, and started the morning round. Emitter array one, nominal. Phase lock drifting, within tolerance. Coolant steady. Nobody listening. Good morning anyway.

He was halfway through the recitation, passing the secondary console, when he stopped mid-word.

The console's status display had updated overnight. That was normal; the registry refreshed on its cycle, syncing with whatever distant databases the tightbeam touched. What was not normal was the entry sitting at the top of the arrivals queue, timestamped fourteen hours ago, flagged PENDING CONFIRMATION.

INBOUND VESSEL: MERIDIAN'S GHOST

STATUS: DECOMMISSIONED — REGISTRY VOID (40 YRS)

PASSENGER MANIFEST: 1 — REYES, ILSA

ETA TO HORIZON BEACON: 61 HOURS

Cyrus stood very still, coffee cooling in his hand, and read it again. And again. The station hummed around him, patient as ever, keeping its secrets the way it kept everything: quietly, structurally, in grammar he was only beginning to be able to hear.

A ship that never flew was coming to his door, carrying a woman who had no reason to exist on any manifest, and the beacon—his beacon, his silent, empty, listening beacon—had known about her for fourteen hours and hadn't said a word.

"Good morning," Cyrus said to the empty corridor, and his voice sounded, to his own ears, like a question.
