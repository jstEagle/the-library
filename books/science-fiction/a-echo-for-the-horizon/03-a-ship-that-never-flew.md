## 3. A Ship That Never Flew

The manifest updated itself at 0347 station time, which was to say it updated itself while Cyrus Villalobos was asleep, which was to say it happened in the one part of the day he no longer trusted.

He learned of it the way he learned most things now: from the beacon's morning digest, a flat list of anomalies and acknowledgments that scrolled across the galley display while he waited for the water recycler to finish its slow argument with itself. He was holding a bulb of reconstituted tea, half-awake, his mouth still tasting faintly of the dream—salt, and a word shaped like a doorway—and he almost didn't read past the first line.

INBOUND MANIFEST REVISION — VESSEL: MERIDIAN'S GHOST

He set the bulb down. Slowly. The way you set down something when the floor has begun, very quietly, to tilt.

There was no vessel called the Meridian's Ghost. There had never been a vessel called the Meridian's Ghost. He knew this the way he knew his own name—not as a fact retrieved but as a shape worn smooth by repetition, because for six years he had run the registry audits on this station himself, quarterly, out of a loneliness so complete it had curdled into ritual. Every hull registered within relay range of Horizon. Every decommissioned frame in the colonial shipping index. Every ghost entry, every corrupted record, every clerical revenant that drifted through the databases like silt. He had catalogued them all in a private file he'd named *Dead Ships I Have Known*, because humor was the last luxury the fringe offered.

The Meridian's Ghost was not among them. The Meridian's Ghost did not exist.

Except that now it did, sitting in the inbound queue with a docking request timestamped forty-one minutes old, and a passenger manifest exactly one name long.

Cyrus carried the tea bulb to the operations console without drinking any of it. His hands were steady. He noticed that they were steady and found it strange; some detached auditor in the back of his skull observed that a man ought to tremble when the world contradicts itself, and this man was not trembling, and what did that say about how long he had been out here, alone, waiting for the impossible the way other men wait for mail?

VESSEL: MERIDIAN'S GHOST
CLASS: LONG-HAUL PASSENGER / CARGO HYBRID
HULL REGISTRY: MG-7734-K
STATUS: DECOMMISSIONED
PASSENGERS: 1
REYES, ILSA

He read it four times. Then he did what he always did when the universe handed him something wrong: he went looking for the seam.

* * *

The registry archive lived in the station's cold vault, three decks down behind a door that complained in two languages whenever it opened. Horizon Station—or the Horizon beacon, depending on who was asking, and no one was asking—had been built by a consortium that had since dissolved into three successor corporations that had since dissolved into nothing anyone could subpoena. Its records were old, fragmented, held together by Cyrus's maintenance scripts the way an old coat is held together by its patches. Corruption was normal here. Contradiction was weather. He told himself this as the vault door groaned open and the chill air breathed over him, smelling of ozone and dust and the particular mineral coldness of machines left alone too long.

He pulled the full record for hull MG-7734-K.

The file came back clean. That was the first wrong thing. Corrupted files came back ragged—truncated fields, checksum failures, the digital equivalent of water damage. This file came back whole, every field populated, every checksum green, as if it had been written yesterday by someone with excellent penmanship and infinite patience.

CONSTRUCTION DATE: 2189.03.14
LAUNCH DATE: 2191.08.02
DECOMMISSION DATE: 2204.11.30
FINAL DISPOSITION: HULL SOLD FOR SALVAGE, YARD 12, CYGNUS REACH

Cyrus stared at the construction date for a long time.

Horizon Station's keel was laid in 2231. He knew this because it was carved into the dedication plaque in the docking bay, because it was in the founding charter he had read eleven times out of boredom, because it was the kind of date a man memorizes when the station is the only other person he talks to. Horizon Station was built ninety years ago.

The Meridian's Ghost had been sold for salvage fifty-three years before there was a station for it to dock at.

He sat down on the cold deck, cross-legged, the way he used to sit as a student in the archives at Meridian University—*Meridian*, he thought, and felt the word go through him like a fishhook—and pulled up the shipping index. Not the local copy. The deep index, the one that synced through Relay Nine once a month on a burst transmission so old and so slow it was practically archaeology. If someone had forged the local records, the deep index would disagree with them. The deep index was the world's memory, and the world's memory did not lie; it only forgot.

The query took nine minutes. He spent them breathing deliberately, the way the station psych manual had taught him, though he hadn't needed the manual's technique in years because nothing out here had ever been worth panicking about.

The result came back.

MERIDIAN'S GHOST (MG-7734-K): NO SUCH VESSEL ON RECORD.

And beneath it, appended by whatever dumb, faithful indexing daemon kept the deep archive:

SEE ALSO: MERIDIAN'S GHOST (MG-7734-K) — LOCAL RECORD EXISTS. DEEP INDEX MISMATCH LOGGED 47 CONSECUTIVE SYNC CYCLES.

Forty-seven sync cycles. Nearly four years. For nearly four years, the local registry had insisted a ship existed that the greater universe had never heard of, and the system had logged the disagreement, and no one had ever looked, because no one was here to look. The error had been sitting in the dark all this time like a seed, waiting.

Waiting for what? Waiting for whom?

Cyrus got up off the deck. His knees cracked. He laughed—one short, ugly syllable of a laugh—and the sound bounced around the vault and came back to him sounding like someone else.

* * *

He checked the docking request next. It was formatted correctly, authenticated correctly, routed through Relay Nine along a corridor that technically existed but hadn't carried traffic since before his assignment began. The request cited a salvage-rescue clause—a vessel in distress may request emergency berthing at the nearest certified facility—and attached a distress flag that had been raised, rescinded, and re-raised twice in the past hour, like a hand waving weakly above water.

ETA: 19 hours, 22 minutes.

One passenger. REYES, ILSA. No origin port listed. No booking agency. No payment trail. In a database where even the coffee rations had provenance, this woman appeared out of nothing, bound for a station that served no purpose he had ever been able to determine, aboard a ship that had been scrap metal before the station's foundations were poured.

He ran her name through everything. Passenger manifests, colonial census extracts, the academic citation index he still checked out of habit and vanity, the crew registries of every line that had ever operated in the Reach. Nothing. Ilsa Reyes did not exist either—which was, he admitted, less alarming than it should have been, because the deep index only reached so far, and human beings were smaller than databases, and plenty of real people were invisible to them.

But the pattern was the thing. The pattern was what made the back of his neck prickle. A ship that never flew. A woman who appeared from nowhere. A route through a dead corridor toward a station that no charter explained and no company claimed. And underneath all of it, humming like a struck string he couldn't locate—

—the dreams.

He thought about the nested clauses. He thought about the way his sleeping mind had lately arranged images inside images inside images, meaning folded into meaning like paper flowers, a syntax no human language used and yet one his dreaming self handled fluently, easily, *natively*. He had filed a report about it eight days ago. No one had answered. No one ever answered.

He thought: *the manifest changed itself.* Not was changed—changed itself. The log showed no editor. No administrative credentials. The revision simply existed, sourceless, the way his dreams arrived sourceless, fully formed, in a grammar with no author he could find.

He stood in the cold vault with the holographic record hanging in front of him—MERIDIAN'S GHOST, patient and immaculate, a lie so well-made it might be true—and he understood, with the particular clarity that comes at the moment a person stops being able to pretend, that these things were connected. The ship and the syntax. The phantom hull and the phantom grammar. Something was approaching this station, and it had been approaching for a long time, and it had begun by learning the shape of his sleep.

"No," he said aloud, to the vault, to the record, to himself. "No, that's—" But the sentence wouldn't finish, because finishing it required naming what *it* was, and there was no word for it in any language he still dreamed in.

* * *

He spent the rest of the day doing the most human thing available to a man confronted with the impossible: he checked his work.

He rebuilt the registry audit from scratch, script by script, watching each line execute. Clean. He compared the local Meridian's Ghost file against its own internal timestamps byte by byte. Consistent—created in a single write operation, no revisions, no edits, born whole like an egg. He traced the routing of the docking request back through Relay Nine until the trail dissolved into the relay's ancient, half-lucid switching logic, which returned a status message he had never seen before and which he copied down by hand because he did not trust himself to have read it right:

ROUTE PRIORITY: INHERITED.

Inherited. He turned the word over for an hour. Routes didn't inherit. Routes were assigned, negotiated, purchased. But the relay's firmware was older than the colony charts, older than the consortium, older than anything with a name, and somewhere in its depths was a priority table that predated every authority he knew of, and that table said this ship, this route, this passenger: *let her come.*

By evening he had eaten nothing. He stood at the observation blister with a fresh tea bulb going cold in his hand, looking out at the dark where the Meridian's Ghost would arrive, and he talked to himself the way he always did, narrating his checks in English because English was his, because it was the one territory the dreams hadn't crossed yet.

"Registry integrity: confirmed corrupted," he said. "Corruption vector: none identified. Conclusion: the corruption is load-bearing." A pause. "That's not a conclusion, Cyrus. That's a poem."

Out in the black, nothing moved. Dead space didn't even twinkle properly; the stars here were old and dim and red-shifted into embers, a sky like a hearth gone cold. Somewhere out there, nineteen hours away, a ship that had died before this station was born was flying anyway, carrying a woman who didn't exist toward a man who had stopped expecting anyone.

He thought about the Vaal—the name that surfaced sometimes in his dreams, unattached to any image, just the sound of it, two syllables like a door closing. He had never written the name down. He had never told anyone. It seemed private in a way that frightened him, the way a stranger's voice in your house is frightening even when it says nothing threatening.

"If you're sending her," he said to the dark, quietly, feeling foolish and doing it anyway, "I want to know why me. Why here. Why now."

The dark, naturally, said nothing. But the beacon behind him hummed its constant note—the note he had listened to for six years, the note he had long ago stopped hearing—and tonight, for the first time, Cyrus noticed that the hum had a rhythm to it. A cadence. Nested phrases, rising and folding inward, meaning stacked on meaning.

It sounded, he thought with a calm that terrified him more than panic would have, like a sentence he was almost able to understand.

He went to the console and opened the docking bay systems. Preparing a berth took hours; the machinery was old and liked to be coaxed. He initialized the sequence, watched the status lights walk themselves awake, and then, on an impulse he would examine later and fail to explain, he added one more command to the queue:

PREPARE SECOND BERTH.

The system accepted it without comment. Somewhere below his feet, doors that hadn't opened in decades began, slowly, to remember how.

Nineteen hours. He had nineteen hours to decide whether he was about to meet a passenger or an answer—and whether those had ever been different things.

Far out in the ember-dark, at the edge of what his scopes could resolve, a signature flickered: a drive plume, small and steady, burning on a heading that matched the manifest exactly.

The Meridian's Ghost was coming home to a port that had been built after its death.

Cyrus watched it burn, and waited, and did not sleep.
