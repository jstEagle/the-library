## 12. Dead Space, Dead Air

The skiff's cabin smelled of ozone and forty years of recycled air, and Ilsa had decided, somewhere in the third hour of the burn, that she hated it the way you hate a room where someone has died.

Not that anyone had died here. As far as she knew. The maintenance skiff had no name, only a hull number stenciled in a font that hadn't been manufactured since before she was born, and its registry—when Cyrus had finally coaxed it to display anything at all—listed its last scheduled inspection as *pending*, with a date stamp eleven months in the future. The ship was from a time that hadn't happened yet, or a time that had happened and then been unwritten. She was getting used to that. That was the worst part: how quickly she was getting used to it.

"Course is holding," Cyrus said from the pilot's couch, not looking up from his slate. He'd said some version of that sentence every twenty minutes since they'd burned out of Horizon beacon's shadow, and she understood now that it wasn't information he was offering. It was a hand held out over dark water. *The world still has rules. Here is one.*

"Good," she said, and gave him the same nod she'd given him the last four times, and turned back to the viewport.

Dead space earned its name. There was nothing out here—not debris, not dust bright enough to catch their running lights, not even the faint traffic-glow of relay chatter, because there were no relays for nine days of burn in any direction except behind them and ahead. The stars were just stars, unclaimed, unannotated. On the long hauls between human settlements, ships filled the silence with entertainment feeds, crew chatter, the comforting noise of other people's lives. This skiff's library had exactly three files, all corrupted, all labeled in Vaal phonemes that resolved on her retinal display as shapes like *breathing through stone*.

So there was no noise. And into that noiselessness, her implant began, very quietly, to sing.

* * *

She noticed it first as warmth.

A spreading heat at the base of her skull, behind the implant scar, gentle as sunlight through a window, and she thought: *the drive's leaking radiation, we're dead,* and then thought, immediately after, with a calm that frightened her more than panic would have: *no. Not the ship. Me.*

"Ilsa?" Cyrus had turned. Of course he'd turned; she'd gone rigid in her chair hard enough that the harness creaked. "What is it? What are you seeing?"

"Nothing." Her voice came out wrong, flattened, as if someone else were operating it. "I'm seeing nothing. That's the problem."

Because that was what was happening. Her eyes were open, the viewport was full of honest starlight, and underneath it, layered beneath it like a second sky, she was watching a courtyard.

It took her a moment—a long, vertiginous moment—to understand that the word *courtyard* was hers, that she was translating even now, that whatever she was perceiving had never once been a courtyard, had never known stone or sun or the concept of walls. But it arrived in her mind wearing those clothes anyway, the way a foreign dignitary arrives in borrowed formal dress: correctly, respectfully, and utterly unlike itself.

There were people in the courtyard. Not people. Figures. Presences arranged in a ring, and each of them was *singing*—except singing was wrong too, because what moved between them wasn't sound. It was memory, passed mouth to mouth like a flame, each figure taking a piece of remembered light and shaping it, offering it, receiving another in return. And the whole ring of them was turning slowly around two figures at the center who stood so close together that their memories had begun to braid, threads of shared past winding into a single cord—

"They're getting married," Ilsa heard herself say.

Cyrus crossed the cabin in two strides and dropped to his knees beside her chair. His face was very close, his eyes searching hers, and she realized distantly that her pupils must be doing something terrible. "Married. You're sure?"

"No." A laugh came out of her, thin and strange. "I'm not sure of anything. I'm sure of—" She groped for it. The memory pressed against the inside of her skull like a palm against glass. "There are two of them at the center. Everyone else is giving them pieces of themselves. An old one is giving them a memory of a flood—no, a *drought*, a drought that killed a whole river, and the couple takes it because grief is part of the gift, you don't get to marry someone without their grief too—" Her breath hitched. "And there's music. There isn't music, there can't be music, but my brain keeps rendering it as music, something with strings, something slow—"

"Stop." Cyrus's hand closed over hers, warm, human, calloused from thirty years of beacon work. "Breathe. Can you shut it off? The implant—can you mute it?"

She reached for the interface the way she'd done ten thousand times—subvocal command, *mute external feed, mute notifications, mute*—and felt the command slide off the implant like water off glass. The system answered her with a status line she'd never seen before:

**PLAYBACK SOURCE: EXTERNAL. PLAYBACK AUTHORITY: NOT YOU.**

"Not me," she whispered. "Cyrus, the playback authority is *not me*. Something else is driving this."

His grip tightened. For a moment he looked older than the station, older than the stars, a man watching a door he'd spent his whole life knocking on begin to open from the other side. Then he did the last thing she expected.

He sat down on the deck beside her chair, back against the console, and said, "Then don't fight it. Describe it. All of it. While it's happening."

"Why?"

"Because," he said quietly, "I think you're the only receiver in human space, and if we lose this transmission, we don't get it again."

* * *

The wedding lasted six hours.

That was how she measured it afterward—six hours by the skiff's clock, though inside the memory there was no clock at all, only the slow rotation of the giving-ring and the deepening braid at its center. Cyrus sat on the deck the entire time, slate balanced on his knee, transcribing her narration in phonetic notation, and she talked until her throat was raw, describing a ceremony conducted by a species extinct for a hundred thousand years in a language that had never existed in sound.

Some of it she could render. Some of it defeated her beautifully.

"The grandmother—the one I'm calling grandmother, she feels like grandmother, she has the weight of grandmother—is giving them a sea." Ilsa's eyes were closed now. Tears ran freely down her face and she didn't bother wiping them; Cyrus had stopped pretending not to notice an hour ago. "A real sea. She's handing them the whole sea, every storm she ever survived in it, every fish, every drowning—there were drownings, Cyrus, the sea took people and they loved it anyway—and the couple receives it and it becomes part of them, part of the marriage, so that when either of them remembers rain for the rest of their life, they'll remember *her* rain, her sea, her dead—"

"That's the inheritance structure," Cyrus murmured, pen flying. "Memory as dowry. You don't join lives, you join *archives*—"

"And now everyone's crying. They cry from—" She frowned, chasing it. "From the sides of their heads? No. From the place where hearing happens. They weep through listening. Oh, that's—" Her voice broke. "That's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen and it never happened anywhere."

The braid at the center completed itself near the end: the two central figures' memories wound together until she could no longer tell whose childhood was whose, whose fear of the dark belonged to which body, and when the ring finished its final turn, the whole courtyard—all of them, every guest, every ancestor invoked in memory-form—released one collective exhalation that her mind rendered, finally, mercifully, as silence.

Then the warmth behind her skull faded, and the cabin came back, and she was sitting in a broken skiff above nothing with wet cheeks and a dry throat and a man looking at her like she was scripture.

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to say something about destiny."

He almost smiled. It was a tired smile, a frightened one, but it reached his eyes. "I was about to say you should drink water. Everything else can wait."

But it couldn't wait, and they both knew it, and when she took the water bulb from him her hand was steady and that steadiness scared her most of all. Because here was the thing she hadn't told him yet, the thing she'd been carrying since the wedding ended:

She hadn't wanted it to stop.

* * *

The second memory came fourteen hours later, and this one was not beautiful.

She woke from a shallow doze in the bunk to find the warmth already blooming, already deep, and tried to surface from it and couldn't—it had caught her mid-sleep, caught her with her defenses down, and this time there was no courtyard, no ring of givers, no ceremony to cushion the arrival.

This time there was a sea.

Not the grandmother's sea, handed over lovingly, framed in ritual. This was the sea itself, raw, from inside a single pair of eyes—or whatever the Vaal had instead of eyes—and it was rising. She stood on a shore that was not a shore, on ground that drank the water rather than fleeing it, and the water came up and up and the sky went the color of bruised metal and all around her the others were *singing*—broadcasting terror and love and names, names of the small ones, the children, flinging their children's names out into the drowning air the way humans might throw lifelines—

*Ilsa.* Cyrus's voice, far away, from the top of a long well. *Ilsa, your heart rate—*

She couldn't answer. She was underwater now, and the water was memory, and the memory was not hers, and she was dying in it, she was actually dying, feeling the salt close over the breathing-places, feeling the enormous indifferent weight of a world ending, and the last thing the drowning one sang—not to the others, but inward, private, a sealed memory meant to be opened only at the end—was a question:

*Who will hold our remembering when we cannot?*

She came back to herself screaming, or trying to scream; what came out was a strangled croak, and then she was on the deck with Cyrus's arms around her, the harness straps hanging cut—he'd cut her out with the emergency knife—and the cabin lights blazing at full because he'd overridden everything.

"Breathe," he was saying. "You're on the skiff. You're with me. Nothing is drowning. Breathe."

She breathed. It took a long time. When she could speak, what came out was not a description but an accusation, aimed at him, at the Vaal, at the universe's sense of humor:

"It gave me a *wedding* first. Like a courtesy. Like an introduction. And then—" Her hands were shaking now, late, useless. "Then it drowned me. It drowned me in someone else's death, Cyrus. I felt the water go into the breathing places. I know what it's like to be a species at the end of its world, from the *inside*, and I will carry that until I die, and nobody asked me. Nobody *asked* me."

He held on. He was good at that; she was learning it about him. The lonely man from the beacon, who narrated his maintenance checks to an empty station, knew exactly how to hold a person coming apart, because presumably he'd had to learn on himself.

"The archive doesn't have manners," he said finally. "It's not sending you these. It's *leaking*. We pulled the plug on its vault when we left, or the vault's pulling on us—we're carrying a piece of it in your head, and pieces want to rejoin wholes." He paused. "Or it's choosing. I genuinely don't know which is worse."

"There's a third option." She pulled back enough to look at him. Her voice had gone flat again, the operator's voice, the one that wasn't quite hers. "Option three: it's teaching. Wedding first, then catastrophe. Courtship, then truth. That's a curriculum, Cyrus. That's a *syllabus*."

He stared at her. Outside the viewport, dead space kept its perfect, indifferent silence, and somewhere behind her eyes, in the overwritten year she still couldn't reach, something waited with the patience of a civilization that had already survived its own ending once, in the only way that mattered.

"If it's a syllabus," Cyrus said slowly, "then there's a final exam."

"Yes."

"And we're burning straight toward it."

She looked at the nav display. Relay Nine: five days out, its signal a stuttering whisper on the edge of detection, failing, dying, the last door. Behind them, somewhere in the dark they'd fled, the Kestrel Profit was burning too.

"Yes," Ilsa said. And beneath the fear, beneath the grief for the stranger who had drowned in her head, she felt the other thing rise again—the thing she hadn't confessed, the shameful thing: the pull. The intimacy. Somewhere in the deep water of her implant, a hundred thousand years dead, someone was waiting to be remembered, and had chosen *her* to do the remembering, and some traitor part of her had already said yes.

She just hoped, watching Cyrus watch her with those careful, hungry, frightened eyes, that she'd have the chance to choose it out loud—before the choice made itself.

That night, the skiff's cabin silent as the grave it resembled, she pretended to sleep and listened to Cyrus breathe, and planned how to tell him that the drowning memory hadn't ended with the drowning.

It had ended with a name. Spoken in Vaal phonemes, rendered on her retinal display in characters she shouldn't have been able to read—but could.

And the name was his.
