## 3. Children Who Remember Dying

The hands are wrong.

That is the first thing. Before the light, before the smell of the greenhouse—wet soil and something green and alive that makes her chest ache for reasons she cannot name—the hands are wrong. Small. Soft. The knuckles smooth as river stones. She turns them over and over in the gray dawn light coming through the shuttle's cracked viewport, looking for the scar.

There should be a scar across the left palm. She earned it at thirty-one, hauling a coolant line on the Halcyon's engineering deck when the coupling blew. Forty years of service written into her body, and her body has returned it all unread.

*Commander Amara Reyes-Okonkwo,* she thinks, and the name fits like a coat cut for someone taller.

"Commander." The word comes out of her own mouth, high and thin, a child's voice saying an adult's title, and she laughs—no, sobs, it might be both—and presses the small hands against her face. Her face is round. Her cheeks are fat with childhood. Somewhere beneath them is the woman who signed things. Who decided things. The thought arrives whole and terrible: *I was someone who decided things.*

* * *

They are gathered in the greenhouse because Ada Dunmore told them to stay where she could see them, and even diminished—even eight years old with milk teeth and a voice like a struck bell—Amara recognizes an order when she hears one. So they sit among the planter rows while the last true adult any of them knows moves through the fog beyond the glass, checking moisture lines, muttering to her logs.

Six of them. Six children in oversized environment suits cinched down to their smallest settings, sitting in a garden built for ten thousand people who are still asleep.

Tomas is picking at the seal of his glove. He is five now, maybe six—he had been the Halcyon's navigator, sixty-two years old, a man who once calculated a slingshot around Verity's second moon so precisely he'd been given a commendation—and now he cannot work the clasp because his fingers are too short and his mind keeps sliding off the task like water off glass.

"I can't," he says, and then, furious, in a voice that cracks in the middle: "I *did* this. I did this a thousand times. I know how it—" The clasp pops open. He stares at his bare hand as if it belongs to a stranger who has just done him a kindness.

Beside him, little Ines Okafor has gone very quiet. Ines had been the ship's physician. Now she sits with her knees pulled up to her chin, rocking slightly, and when Amara looks at her, Ines says, without looking up:

"I remember a delivery."

"What?"

"A baby. On the ship. Year nine of the transit." Ines's small brow furrows, an old woman's frown on a child's face. "I held her mother's hand for eleven hours. I remember the exact number of hours. Why do I remember the *hours*, Amara? Why do I remember her name? Mariama. The baby was named after her grandmother. There's no reason for me to keep that. There's no room for it." She pats her own flat chest, her small belly, as though searching for where forty years of medicine have been stored inside a body that hasn't happened yet. "I'm full of things that don't fit."

Amara knows. God help her, Amara knows.

* * *

It comes back the way deep-space dreams come back—not in order, not whole, but in shards with edges sharp enough to cut.

She is standing in the observation blister of the Halcyon, year twenty-two of the return transit, and the stars are doing nothing, which is what stars do, and she is reading a document on a slate that glows blue against her—against the older woman's—hands. The document has a header she can almost see. *Covenant Compliance Review.* And there is a list. A long list. Ten thousand names rendered as identification strings, and half of them are marked with a symbol she now understands the way you understand a thing in a dream: completely, and only while you are asleep.

*Flagged. Do-not-wake. Priority exclusion.*

And below the list, a signature line. And below the signature line, her name.

She wakes in the greenhouse with her heart slamming in a chest the size of a teacup, gasping, and Ada Dunmore is suddenly there, kneeling, real hands on her small shoulders—hands rough from decades of soil, the first honest hands Amara has touched since waking.

"You cried out," Ada says. "You said *half*. You kept saying *half of them*."

"I don't—" Amara begins, and stops, because lying is a skill and she no longer has the instrument for it. Children's faces are transparent. Everyone knows this. No one warns you that your own face will become transparent to yourself.

"The pods," Ada says quietly. It isn't a question. This woman has spent forty years alone with a dying garden; she has learned to hear the shape of a catastrophe before it finishes arriving. "Something about the cryopods."

Amara looks past her, at the other children. Tomas has stopped fiddling with his glove. Ines has stopped rocking. Little Devi Chandrasekar, who had been the Halcyon's comms officer and is now perhaps seven, is hugging herself and whispering numbers—a string of digits, over and over, a pod-bay designation or a kill-code or a prayer.

"We voted," Amara says. The words come out of her like splinters. "In year twenty-two. There was a review. The colony's power budget—the terraforming grid—it couldn't sustain the full complement. Waking everyone would crash the biosphere systems within a generation. The founders built a covenant into the archive. Either the sleepers wake, or Verity lives. Not both." Her child's voice trembles on the arithmetic, which is the only part of her that still works like it used to. "We were given the authority to choose. Because we were command. Because we were *trusted*."

"And?" Ada's face does something complicated. Hope and dread fighting for the same ground.

"We chose Verity." Amara hears herself say it and hates the sound, hates how clean it sounds in a small mouth, hates that somewhere in the shattered architecture of her memory there is a version of her—an older, taller, more tired her—who said those words and believed they were heavy enough to be honorable. "Half the pods were flagged to never open. Five thousand names. We flagged them. I—" Her hand rises toward the signature line that lives behind her eyes. "I signed first."

The greenhouse goes silent except for the drip of the irrigation line and, faintly, the wind moving through a world that was never supposed to have wind yet.

Ada stands up slowly. She doesn't speak. She walks back into the fog between the planters, and Amara watches her go and understands, with the terrible clarity available only to the very young and the very old, that she has just handed this woman a grief with a signature attached.

* * *

Night comes fast on Verity; the days are short and getting shorter, the seasons of a world still learning its own weather. They bed down in the greenhouse annex on mats meant for field technicians, six small bodies under thermal blankets, and none of them sleep well, because sleep is where the cargo comes loose.

Amara dreams in fragments.

She dreams of the council chamber aboard the Halcyon, the table too long, the vote too quiet. She dreams of Devi transmitting the flag-orders home and then vomiting into a recycler, and Devi in the present tense, three bunks away, whimpers in her sleep at exactly that moment, as if the dream is being broadcast on a channel they all still share. She dreams of Ines arguing—no, *pleading*—that the selection criteria were eugenicist garbage dressed in mathematics, and of the tall tired version of herself answering with words like *triage* and *responsible stewardship*, and of Ines finally going silent in a way that meant she had lost something she would never get back.

She wakes at what might be midnight and finds Ines awake too, sitting cross-legged, watching her.

"You remember the argument," Ines whispers.

"Yes."

"I remember losing it." Ines's eyes are enormous in the dark. "Amara. I remember being *old*. I remember my hands shaking when I drew blood and knowing it was time to stop, and I remember deciding to take the return posting anyway because I didn't trust anyone else to watch the sleepers' medical telemetry. I remember choosing to come home to them." Her breath hitches. "And now I can't reach the top shelf, and the people I came home to protect are the people we sentenced, and my hands don't shake anymore but everything else does."

Amara reaches across the dark space between their mats and takes Ines's hand—small gripping small, a handshake between two people who used to be a hundred and forty years old combined.

"We'll fix it," Amara says, and hears immediately how childish the promise is, how perfectly suited to the mouth making it, and decides, for the first time since waking, that this might not be a flaw. The commander in her had weighed five thousand lives against a biosphere and called it stewardship. Whatever she is now—eight years old, gutted, guilty, *small*—she is at least too small to hide behind abstractions anymore.

"There's something else," Ines says. "From the dream. The vault. You dreamed about a vault, didn't you. Under this building."

Amara's stomach drops, because she did—in the fragments, in the shards, a door beneath the greenhouse, founder's sigils, and a file stamped with a phrase that has been circling her all night like a bird over a fire:

*Failsafe: decision-makers shall be preserved past their own corruption.*

"In the morning," Amara says. "When the gardener is listening."

Because whatever they were made into out there in the dark between stars—whatever was done to them, whatever they allowed to be done—they are going to need the one adult left on this moon to hear it. And Ada Dunmore, Amara suspects, has already begun decrypting something of her own.

Through the annex window, far across the dark plain, the lights of the cryo-vault burn steady and green, five thousand of them dimmed to embers, five thousand burning bright.

Amara counts them twice.

She is, after all, very good at counting. It is practically the only thing she has left.
