## 5. What the Roots Remember

The dream is always the same, and it is not a dream.

I am standing in a corridor of the *Halcyon* that no longer exists—burned for reaction mass in the fourth year, I remember that too, I remember everything—and I am tall in it. My hand reaches a rail without stretching. My voice gives an order and adults obey it. And then I wake in a bunk built for someone my size, in a body that smells of milk and soap, and my hands are so small around the memory that it slips through them like water through a net.

My name is Commander Amara Reyes-Okonkwo. I am forty-six years old. I am eight years old. Both of these sentences are true, and only one of them fits.

"Commander." Devi tugs my sleeve. She is six now; she was our navigator, thirty-one years old, with a laugh like a pressure valve releasing. The laugh is still there. It comes out of a smaller throat. "Ada says we can go down today. She found the door."

I look at her and I see two people superimposed, the way you see two images merge in an old stereoscope. Navigator Devi Raghunathan, who once held our course through the debris shoal at Cygnus Reach for eleven hours without blinking. And this small girl with chapped lips who has been crying in her sleep again, all of us crying in our sleep again, because the memories come whether we want them or not.

"The door," I say. My voice hasn't decided what it wants to be yet. Some mornings it comes out in my old register, low and command-flat, and frightens the younger ones worse than any nightmare. This morning it comes out small. "Show me."

* * *

Ada Dunmore meets us at the greenhouse airlock with a lamp already lit, as if she expected us before dawn. Perhaps she did. She has spent forty years alone on this moon learning to expect things before they happen—that is what devotion does to a person, it makes them prescient about loss.

She looks at me differently than she looks at the others. I know why. In Chapter Two of whatever story she is telling herself, I am the villain: the woman who signed the order abandoning the surface, the signature on the document that left her here with a dying garden and ten thousand sleepers who would never wake. She has not said it aloud. She doesn't have to. It sits between us like a body under a tarp.

"You said roots," she says. "Last night, on the comm. You said the roots would show me."

"I said the roots remember." I am holding Devi's hand. I don't remember starting to do that. "In the ship's last transmission from Verity—the original one, the founder-era one—we recovered a fragment. Corrupted. But there was a phrase repeated in the checksum errors, over and over, like something buried trying to get out." I make myself say it plainly. "It said: *what was planted beneath the garden was planted to outlive the gardeners.*"

Ada's face does something complicated. Hope and dread fighting for the same ground.

"Beneath," she repeats. "Under my greenhouse. Under—" Her hand goes to her chest, to the locket she wears with the seed catalog inside. Forty years of daily logs, and she never thought to dig.

"You couldn't have known," I say, and hate how much it sounds like absolution coming from me, of all people.

"No," she says quietly. "But I should have looked. That's the difference." She turns and leads us into the green.

* * *

The greenhouse at night is a cathedral of failing things. Ada has kept alive species that should have died decades ago—Terran wheat adapted to Verity's thin light, lichen she coaxed into flowering, a single apple tree grown from a seed carried across forty light-years in someone's coat pocket. The crew moves through the rows like children in a museum, which is what we are, touching leaves with fingers that used to sign treaties.

Tomas finds it first. Tomas Okonfor-Hale, our engineer, now five years old and furious about it in a way that has made him the most competent small person on two worlds. He stops at the irrigation manifold in the north wall, where the pipes descend into bedrock, and he crouches, and he puts his ear against the stone the way he used to put his ear against engine housings.

"There's a void down there," he says. "Big one. And a power draw." His brow furrows, an adult expression on a child's face, and it is the most terrible thing I have seen since we came out of the regrowth tanks. "Someone's been paying the electric bill for forty years, Commander. On a moon that's supposed to be rationing every watt."

Ada kneels beside him. She pulls up the floor panel herself—no drone, no machine, just her hands and a pry bar, because some things you should open with your own hands—and beneath it is a shaft, and at the bottom of the shaft, a light.

Not emergency amber. Not the sickly green of the terraforming grid. A clean white light, steady as a held breath, burning in the dark for longer than I have been alive in either of my lives.

We climb down.

* * *

The vault is round, and it is beautiful, and I hate it immediately and completely.

The walls are lined with the same living wood-composite as the cryopods upstairs—as the cryopods everywhere on Verity—but older, first-generation, grown rather than built. Roots run through the ceiling like veins. At the center stands a console of black glass, still warm, still waiting, and above it, embossed in the founder script that every colonial schoolchild used to memorize before the schools stopped teaching history:

**THE COVENANT HOLDS WHAT THE COVENANT HOLDS. THE GARDEN REMEMBERS ITS GARDENERS.**

"Devi," I say. "Console."

She's already there. Of course she's already there; muscle memory doesn't care how long your arms are. Her small fingers move across the interface with a fluency that makes Ada take a step back, and then the vault fills with light and data, and we all see it at once.

Genetic records. Thousands of them. Founder-line genomes, annotated, versioned, tracked across generations like software releases. And threaded through every file, a diagnostic tag that Devi opens with one touch:

**REVERSION PROTOCOL — STATUS: ACTIVE — SUBJECTS: HALCYON CREW (9) — TRIGGER: DECISION-CLASS AUTHORITY EXCEEDED SAFETY THRESHOLD — METHOD: TELOMERIC REWIND, FULL SOMATIC — PURPOSE: PRESERVATION OF JUDGMENT CAPACITY PAST POINT OF ETHICAL DEGRADATION.**

Nobody speaks. The vault hums. Somewhere above us, the greenhouse drips its slow count.

"It's us," whispers Priya, our doctor, who is seven. She was sixty when we launched. She holds her own genetic file in her hands like it might bite. "It's—it rewound us. On purpose. Someone designed this before we ever flew."

"Not someone." Ada's voice comes from behind us, very quiet. She is reading faster than any of us; she has had forty years of nothing to do but read. "Look at the authorship stamps. This protocol was written by the founders themselves. First generation. Before the sleepers even boarded." She turns to me, and her eyes are wet and hard at the same time. "They knew. They knew their own descendants would rot. They built a failsafe into their own bloodline—anyone who inherited decision authority and then used it to sacrifice other human beings would be... reverted. Reset. Given back their childhood so they could choose again."

"That's not a failsafe," says Tomas, and his little fists are shaking. "That's a leash."

"It's worse than a leash." I step toward the console because my legs are doing it without consulting me. "Read the purpose clause again. *Preservation of judgment capacity.* They didn't build this to punish us. They built it to keep us useful." My voice cracks down the middle, half woman, half child, and I let it. "We weren't regressed as mercy. We were regressed as maintenance. They took our bodies apart and put them back the way they were when we were still innocent, so that whatever we decide next, they can tell themselves it was decided by someone uncorrupted. We're not survivors. We're instruments. We're tools they sharpened by making us small."

Devi starts to cry. Not the way children cry—loudly, for a reason—but the way adults cry, silently, catastrophically, tears pouring down a six-year-old face while her posture stays perfectly still, navigator-still, holding course.

"I voted," she says.

"Devi—"

"In the deep. When the power budget failed. There was a vote, Commander, don't you remember? You put it to the senior staff because you always followed procedure, God help us, you followed procedure right off the edge of the world." She wipes her face with her sleeve, leaving a wet streak. "Half the pods flagged to never open. Ten thousand people. And I voted yes, because the math said yes, because the math always says yes if you let it talk long enough. And now I'm six years old and I can feel that vote sitting in me like a swallowed stone and I don't know if it's mine or if they grew me new so I'd carry it better."

The vault hums. The roots overhead shift in some imperceptible current, and dust sifts down like snow.

I look at my own file. **SUBJECT: REYES-OKONKWO, AMARA. AUTHORITY CLASS: COMMAND. REVERSION COUNT: 1. NOTE:** and here the founders' script goes cold and clinical in a way that will follow me to my grave, whichever grave that turns out to be—**NOTE: SUBJECT'S ORIGINAL SACRIFICE ORDER (VERITY SURFACE ABANDONMENT) PRECEDED REGRESSION. PROTOCOL FUNCTIONED AS DESIGNED. SUBJECT AVAILABLE FOR SECOND DECISION CYCLE.**

Available. Like inventory. Like a part kept on the shelf.

"They've done this before," I hear myself say. "Look at the reversion count field. Mine says one. But the field exists. The field is designed to hold more." I turn to Ada, and the question comes out of me before I can stop it, the question that has been growing in me since the *Halcyon* broke atmosphere, since I saw my own signature on the abandonment order in a handwriting I didn't recognize as mine until I remembered being the hand that wrote it:

"Ada. Your colleague. The one who left the post. What was her name?"

Something closes in Ada's face like a door.

"Maren," she says. "Maren Voss. She was the surface director before me. She held decision authority for the whole terraforming program." A pause. The lamp trembles slightly in her hand, or perhaps it is her hand trembling in the lamp's light. "She left Verity nineteen years ago. Took the last shuttle. She told me she couldn't stay somewhere she'd failed."

"Or," I say, and the vault seems to lean in to listen, "she was reverted. Reset. Sent away small, to grow up again somewhere else, available for a second decision cycle. Ada—how old did Maren look, the day she left?"

And Ada Dunmore, who has logged every sunrise on this moon for forty years, who has recorded the death of every leaf in her garden with more tenderness than most people spend on their own children, goes white to the lips and says, in a voice I will hear for the rest of my life:

"She looked like a girl. I thought—I thought grief had made me see it wrong. She hugged me goodbye and she was shorter than me and I thought, grief, exhaustion, forty years, it plays tricks—" She stops. Her hand rises slowly to the locket at her throat. "There's a sub-basement under the east wing of the archive. Maren sealed it before she left. She said it was structural. She said—" Ada's breath goes ragged. "She said, *if anyone ever asks you how old I was, dig.*"

Above us, through forty meters of rock and root and the fragile green cathedral of everything she has kept alive, the colony's power grid hiccups—a brownout, brief, the first stutter of a system beginning to fail. The lights in the vault flicker and hold.

Devi looks up at me with her tear-tracked face and says the thing we are all thinking, the thing that has been waiting in the dark beneath the garden all along:

"Commander. If they hid one vault from themselves—how many more doors are down here?"

The roots overhead creak, and somewhere far below, something answers with a sound like a lock turning over after a very long sleep.
