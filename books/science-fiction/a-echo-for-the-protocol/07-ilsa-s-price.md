## 7. Ilsa's Price

The tea in Meridian Station's customs office had gone cold an hour ago, and Ilsa Vane drank it anyway, because drinking cold tea was what honest people did while they waited, and she had spent eleven years being the most honest person on this station who wasn't.

Her terminal showed Marguerite Dravenmoor's file for the four hundredth time. Not the customs file — that one was a masterpiece of nothing, a decade of manifests so clean they squeaked. The other file. The one that lived on Concord intelligence servers under a classification code she'd needed three promotions to earn access to.

DR-7. Asset designation: unclaimed. Route network: seventeen confirmed nodes, six suspected. Cargo profile: memory media, high-fidelity, cross-fleet. Recommendation: monitor, do not burn.

She'd written that recommendation herself, four years ago, when she was still junior enough to believe monitoring was a strategy rather than a cowardice. She remembered the day she'd flagged him — a Tuesday, station rotation 3311, a shipment of Callistan wine with a data-density signature eleven percent too heavy for its declared volume. She could have taken him then. One seizure, one interrogation, one commendation, and her career would have been made on the back of a soft-spoken man who paid his docking fees early and tipped the dockhands in real coffee.

Instead she'd stamped him through and gone home and stared at her ceiling until her shift started again.

Because she'd read the cargo manifest closely. Eleven percent too heavy. And inside the false bottom of a wine crate, according to the spectral scan she'd run twice and then deleted, a memory-shard tagged with a widow's name and a wedding date. Someone was paying to carry a dead man's voice across a line both fleets had sworn would never be crossed again. Ilsa Vane had stood in her booth at Gate Four with her hand hovering over the seizure alert, and she had thought: *this is the only trade on this station that isn't killing anyone.*

She'd let him pass. She'd been letting him pass ever since. And every year the recommendation to *monitor* had renewed itself like a subscription to her own hypocrisy.

Now the Vesperine sat in High Dock Two with forty-one children aboard, and the whole architecture of her patience was burning down around her.

* * *

The summons came through official channels, which was how she knew it was unofficial. A priority flag from Director Halloway's office at Concord Fleet Command, routed through the station liaison, requesting — *requesting*, not ordering — a briefing on "anomalous traffic patterns relevant to current events." The phrasing was diplomatic. The encryption underneath it was not.

Ilsa locked her office, walked the long way through the customs concourse so she could watch the crowd, and took the call from a maintenance alcove on Ring C where the acoustics were bad and the cameras were worse.

Halloway's face resolved out of the lag: gray, patient, the face of a man who had ordered things done and slept well after. "Officer Vane. You've seen the news."

"Everyone's seen the news."

"Then you know the Verity delegation has already filed a custody petition, and ours is being drafted as we speak. What you don't know" — he leaned closer to his pickup, and the compression artifacts made his wrinkles move like water — "is that our sources indicate the eldest child, the Ilves girl, is carrying an intact neural archive. Not childhood memories. An archive. Something the Vesperine's crew built or found or became, and brought back with them."

Ilsa kept her breathing even. "That's speculation."

"That's intelligence," Halloway said, in the tone of a man correcting a child's grammar. "And it means that in approximately seventy-two hours, when our custody petition lands, that girl becomes either the most protected or the most dissected person in two fleets. Depending on who gets there first." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "Which brings me to you, Officer Vane. Your file reviews are exemplary. Your conviction rate is unremarkable. Your contraband interdiction statistics are, frankly, embarrassing for a post of Meridian's traffic volume."

Here it was. Eleven years arriving all at once.

"I'm aware of my numbers, Director."

"We're aware of why they look the way they do." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that was the worst part. "DR-7. You've known about him since rotation 3311. You've passed him through your gate two hundred and six times. Do you want to tell me why, or shall we skip to the part where it stops mattering?"

Ilsa looked at her reflection in the dark maintenance panel across from her. A middle-aged woman in a neutral-port uniform, gray trim going silver at the temples, holding a cold cup of tea in a hot little room. She thought about the widow's wedding shard. She thought about the girl behind the quarantine glass who had looked at Marguerite Dravenmoor and said his name like a key turning.

"I can tell you exactly why," she said. "But you're not going to like it, and it doesn't change anything, so let's skip to the part where you tell me what you actually want."

Something flickered across Halloway's face that might have been respect. "What we want is the archive. Before the Verity fleet gets it. And DR-7 is the only person on that station with the access, the methods, and — critically — the deniability to get close to the ward without tripping Okonkwo's neutrality protocols. We can't send an extraction team without starting the war three weeks early. But a smuggler visiting a sick child?" He spread his hands. "Nobody stops a smuggler from doing something sentimental."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then he goes into a Concord tribunal cell, and we find someone else, and the girl grows up in a fleet laboratory wondering why the man whose name she remembers never came back." Halloway let that sit. "You've spent eleven years protecting him, Officer Vane. Consider this your opportunity to protect him properly. Immunity. Full retroactive amnesty for DR-7 and his network, contingent on cooperation. Witness protection protocols extended to the Ilves child regardless of custody outcome. That's not an offer I have authority to make twice."

The line held open. Ilsa realized she hadn't spoken in ten seconds and that Halloway was waiting with the serenity of a man who already knew the answer, because men like Halloway always knew the answers; they just liked to hear other people say them.

"I'll need it in writing," she said. "Real writing. Fleet seal."

"You'll have it within the hour."

"One more thing." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "When this is done — whatever 'done' looks like — the amnesty holds. Even if the archive turns out to be nothing. Even if he fails. You bought his past, Director. You don't get to renegotiate the price later."

Halloway smiled, and it was the smile of a man agreeing to terms he had no intention of honoring, and Ilsa Vane signed off knowing she had just become the kind of officer she'd spent her career pretending not to be.

She finished the cold tea anyway. Honest people's habits died hard.

* * *

She cornered him at the fish stall.

It was the only place on Meridian's mid-ring where the hydroponics section sold actual protein, and Marguerite Dravenmoor stood in front of the tank with the particular stillness of a man who had been followed before and knew how to wait for the follower to commit. He didn't turn when she came up beside him. He looked at the fish.

"Officer Vane." His voice was quiet, pleasant, ruined slightly by sleeplessness. "Come to audit my groceries?"

"Come to talk about Gate Four. Rotation 3311."

That got him. Not much — a fractional stillness, a recalibration behind the eyes — but she'd spent eleven years learning to read him through a scanner, and she read him now at half a meter. He knew the date. He'd always known the date. Men like Marguerite carried their near-misses the way sailors carried drowned ports.

"The wine shipment," he said lightly. "Eleven percent overweight. I always wondered if you caught that."

"I caught it. I ran the spectral scan twice. I saw the shard tag." She picked up a fish from the crushed ice, weighed it in her hand, put it down. "A wedding recording. Widow named Osei, Concord side, husband buried on a Verity colony. You were carrying their last day together across the line so she could die listening to it."

Marguerite said nothing. Around them the market went on — a dockhand arguing about oxygen surcharges, a child chasing a cleaning drone, the eternal hum of a station that existed because two fleets hated each other too much to leave. He watched the fish turn in the tank.

"Why didn't you take me?" he asked finally. It wasn't gratitude in his voice. It was something more dangerous: the genuine bewilderment of a man who had built his entire life on rules and encountered someone who'd broken hers for him.

"Because it was the only cargo on this station I've ever scanned that couldn't hurt anybody." She turned to face him fully now, and let him see it — let him see the eleven years sitting in her face. "Two hundred and six crossings, Marguerite. Love letters, deathbeds, a lullaby some grandmother recorded for a grandchild she'll never meet. You've been running the human parts of a divided species through my gate for a decade, and I let you, because somebody had to, and because arresting you would have made me the kind of person who arrests that."

"You should have," he said quietly. "It would have been cleaner."

"Cleaner for whom?"

He didn't have an answer. She watched him not have an answer, and understood suddenly that this was the loneliness the file had never captured — not the absence of people, but the accumulation of debts he couldn't acknowledge because acknowledging them would mean admitting he'd been seen all along.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "In seventy-two hours, Concord files a custody petition for the Vesperine children. Verity will counter-file within the day. Okonkwo will stall, because stalling is all neutral law is, and eventually one fleet or the other decides that a seven-year-old with an intact neural archive is worth breaking a truce over. That's the timeline. That's the clock nobody's announced yet."

"You're Concord intelligence." He said it without surprise, and she realized he'd known — not the specifics, maybe, but the shape of her. Smugglers always knew the shape of the people who let them pass. Nothing passes for free forever.

"For eleven years. And for eleven years I've been the reason you're free, and now I'm the reason you won't be, unless you listen to what I'm offering." She kept her voice low and level, a customs officer's voice, a voice built for gates and thresholds. "Full immunity. Retroactive. You, Doss, every node in your network — amnesty, sealed records, protection. And separate from that, guaranteed: the Ilves girl gets medical care and legal guardianship review outside both fleets' chains of command. Whatever happens to the archive, she doesn't spend her childhood in a laboratory."

"And what does Concord want for all that generosity?"

"The archive. Extracted and delivered before Verity moves. You're the only person alive who can get into that ward without making it an incident. You visit her. You've already visited her. Nobody questions a man reading stories to a child who knows his name." She paused. "We know what she is to you, Marguerite. We've known since the quarantine footage. Which means we also know you'll say yes, because saying yes is the only version of events where you get to keep seeing her."

There it was. The knife, offered handle-first, which was how the cruelest knives were always offered.

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. The market noise seemed to recede, or maybe that was just the sound of a man's interior walls rearranging themselves. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into something she hadn't heard from him before — not the smooth courier, not the grieving ghost. Something harder and older.

"You said protection for Sefa regardless of outcome. In writing?"

"In writing. Fleet seal."

"And if I extract the core and it shows the Vesperine found something Concord caused? Some weapons test, some fold experiment gone wrong — if the truth indicts your side?" His eyes didn't leave hers. "Does the amnesty cover what I do with that?"

Ilsa felt the question land exactly where he'd aimed it, in the space between her orders and her conscience, a space she'd been renting for eleven years at the price of cold tea and clean stamps.

"The amnesty covers your past," she said. "Your future is your own problem. That's the deal. It's the only deal."

He nodded slowly, and she watched him do the arithmetic smugglers did — weigh the cage against the open sky, count the exits, calculate how much of himself the deal would cost and whether the remainder was worth living in. She'd seen that calculation on a thousand faces at her gate. She'd never seen it on a face she cared about.

"I'll need three days," he said. "And I work alone. No handlers, no check-ins, no surveillance on my quarters. If Concord shadows me, the deal's off and I go to Verity instead — and Commander Rell already knows where I live, so don't think that's a bluff."

"It's not a bluff," Ilsa agreed. "Rell's been on-station nine days. We've been counting." She pulled a data-chit from her coat pocket — she'd prepared it that morning, before the call, before she'd admitted to herself what she was doing — and set it on the ice beside the fish. "Amnesty draft. Read it with a lawyer if you can find one you trust. The final version comes with the fleet seal within the hour, like I promised Halloway."

"Halloway." Marguerite picked up the chit, turned it over once. "Director Halloway. The man who buries things." He almost smiled. "You know what the funny thing is, Officer Vane? Everyone keeps coming to me to carry their secrets across lines. Fleets, directors, commanders. Nobody ever asks whether I want to know what's inside the package."

"Do you?"

"No," he said. "That's the rule. Never carry anything you can't deny." He tucked the chit away. "You just made that impossible."

He walked away down the concourse, unhurried, a man buying fish, and Ilsa Vane stood in the market noise and understood that she had finally crossed her own gate — that somewhere between rotation 3311 and this moment she had stopped being the officer who let the smuggler pass and become something else entirely, something with a fleet seal in her pocket and a child's face behind glass and no remaining version of herself that was clean.

Her comm buzzed. Station security channel, priority flag.

She opened it expecting Halloway's seal. Instead: a station-wide advisory. Quarantine Ward C — visitor log anomaly. Unregistered biometric match detected during the 1400 visiting block. Subject: adult male, unidentified, present in the ward for eleven minutes. Administrator Okonkwo has ordered a full audit of ward access.

Someone else had gotten to Sefa Ilves first. Someone who hadn't bothered with deals or seals or eleven years of patience.

Ilsa was moving before she finished reading, pushing through the market crowd toward the ring transit, and the last thing she heard behind her was the fish seller calling after the retreating figure of Marguerite Dravenmoor — *sir, you forgot your change* — fading under the klaxon beginning, softly, to rise.
