## 8. Duty Is a Loaded Word

The sky told them first.

It came as a sound Odile felt in his teeth before he heard it—a low, subsonic groan rolling across the domes like the colony itself clearing its throat. Then the light: three new stars where no stars should be, burning steady through Meridian's thin atmosphere, descending in perfect formation. He stood in the frost-killed garden behind the med clinic, Sefa's seedlings black and brittle at his feet, and watched the cleanup fleet come down out of orbit like knives being lowered into water.

He'd known they were coming. His original's cache had contained fragments of corporate protocol, enough for him to understand what happened to colonies that stopped being profitable. But knowing was an abstraction. This was three ships with hulls still gleaming from their last refit, descending on a settlement of ninety-one souls—forty-one of them children.

The comm band crackled to life across every channel at once, overriding even the emergency frequencies. A voice, smooth and genderless, the kind of voice designed by committee:

*"By order of Halcyon Terraforming Group, Meridian Deep is hereby designated a Class-4 biohazard event. All personnel are instructed to remain indoors. Quarantine protocols are in effect. Compliance ensures safety."*

Compliance ensures safety. Odile had read those words before—in the edited manifests, in the retroactively beautified census logs, in the sanitized incident reports that turned four hundred people into ninety-one and a massacre into a maintenance accident. The company didn't lie to you. It just repeated its lies until they sounded like weather.

Behind him, the clinic door banged open. Tam Voss stood there in an oversized thermal coat, breath steaming, eyes fixed on the descending ships. The boy didn't look afraid. He looked like someone confirming a suspicion.

"It's the orange," Tam said quietly. "Isn't it? That's what comes next."

Odile couldn't answer. The truth was already answering for him, written in fire-retardant foam and sterilization payloads and the careful bureaucratic language of erasure.

* * *

The contract activated at 0400 hours.

He woke—not from sleep, because he hadn't been sleeping—with the sensation of something unlocking inside his own skull. It wasn't pain. It was worse than pain: it was clarity, cold and total, like a circuit completing. Every clone Halcyon grew carried the same genetic watermark, the same embedded compliance architecture woven into the genome alongside his original's memories. Dormant until invoked. Invoked now.

Words assembled themselves in his mind, unbidden, in the company's committee-smooth cadence:

*Asset OB-7. Your genetic service contract has been activated under Emergency Provision 12. You are directed to assist quarantine operations at Meridian Deep. Noncompliance constitutes breach of property agreement. Remediation will follow.*

Remediation. He almost laughed. They had a word for everything. Four hundred colonists became ninety-one. A massacre became an incident. A man became an asset. And murder, when the time came, would become remediation.

He sat up in the dark of his quarters—the same quarters that had been ransacked two nights ago, drawers still hanging open, the ghosts of the stolen manifests everywhere—and pressed his palms against his eyes. He could feel the contract settling over him like a second skeleton. Not compelling his body, not yet. Just... waiting. Rewriting the definition of *his* with every hour it ran. He thought of the vat he'd woken in, the nutrient fluid, the barcode laser-etched into his wrist before he'd ever drawn breath. He had always known, in some chambered-off part of himself, that he was made to be disposable. Now the company was simply saying it aloud.

*Assist quarantine operations.* Which meant walk door to door with a manifest of names. Which meant help load the children onto transports whose flight plans ended nowhere recorded. Which meant stand aside—or stand with—when the sterilization burn came down on whatever the transports left behind.

His original had died asking why. Odile understood, suddenly, that the question had never been dangerous because of its answer. It was dangerous because asking it made you useless to them. An asset that asked why was a defective asset.

And defective assets got remediated too.

* * *

He found Sefa in the clinic at dawn, doing what she always did: keeping the lights on. She'd rigged the backup generator to run at eighty percent efficiency with parts scavenged from three dead atmospheric processors, and she was elbow-deep in its guts when he came in, hair tied back with a strip of gauze, solder iron clenched in her teeth like a knife between a pirate's lips.

She took one look at his face and set the iron down.

"They activated you," she said. Not a question. She'd grown up around company contracts; she knew the look of a man carrying one. "I can see it. You've gone all—" she waved a hand at him, "—symmetrical."

"Sefa."

"Don't." She held up both hands, backing toward the med bay's inner door, keeping her voice low so the children sleeping in the recovery ward wouldn't hear. "Don't say my name like that. Like you're already saying goodbye."

"The fleet is here to sterilize the colony. The transports will take maybe half the population—enough to make the numbers plausible. The rest—" He stopped. He couldn't build the sentence. "I'm legally required to assist."

"Legally required." She laughed, and it was the worst sound he'd ever heard from her, brittle and bright as breaking glass. "You know what I was legally required to do during the incident? Nothing. I was nine years old, and my legal requirement was to hide in a coolant duct while—" Her voice caught. She'd never finished that sentence in all the weeks he'd known her. She wasn't going to finish it now. "There is no law out here, Odile. There's just what they'll do to us and what we do about it."

"Come with me." The words were out before he'd decided to say them, raw and unplanned. "The transports. I can get you and the kids assigned to my work detail—I have clearance, I have codes, I can—"

"And go where?" She stepped closer, and up close he could see the exhaustion carved into her, weeks of scavenging and patching and refusing to leave, all of it spent keeping forty-one children alive in a dying colony. "A relocation camp? A labor charter? You've read the manifests, Odile. You know what happens to Meridian's survivors once they're company inventory again. They don't get killed twice. They just get erased slowly instead of quickly." Her hand came up and pressed flat against his chest, over the sternum, where a heartbeat lived that belonged to Halcyon Terraforming Group on paper. "You're asking me to run. I'm asking you where you think running goes."

"Nowhere," he admitted. "Nowhere clean."

"Then stop offering me dirty places." Her forehead dropped against his chin, and for a moment they just stood there in the humming dark of the clinic, two caretakers holding each other up, the generator ticking through its repair cycle like a patient heartbeat. When she spoke again her voice was barely a breath. "Stay. That's all I'm asking. Whatever you're going to do—do it here, with us. Don't let them take you out of this story."

"I'm not in the story, Sefa. I'm in the contract. There's a difference, and it's measured in guns."

"Then break the contract."

"You don't break Provision 12. It's not a rule, it's a—" he searched for the word and hated every candidate, "—it's a leash built into my blood. Every hour it runs, it rewrites me. By tomorrow I might not want to refuse. That's what remediation means for assets like me. Not a bullet. An edit."

Sefa pulled back and looked at him, and what was in her face wasn't pity or fear. It was the same expression she wore over failing life support systems: assessment, triage, decision.

"Then we have until tomorrow," she said. "So give me today."

* * *

They spent it together, and it was the cruelest gift the universe had ever handed him.

Morning: she taught him to splint a fracture using vacuum-seal wrap, her hands guiding his, and Tam Voss watched from the doorway with the solemn attention of a boy cataloguing everything he might need to survive. Midday: they walked the garden rows even though nothing would grow now, because Sefa said the kids needed to see the adults acting like there was a future worth planting in. Afternoon: she fell asleep against his shoulder in the clinic's supply room, just for twenty minutes, her breath evening out for the first time in weeks, and Odile sat perfectly still beneath the weight of her and memorized everything—the chapped knuckles, the solder burn on her wrist, the way her fingers curled into his sleeve even in sleep, holding on.

He memorized it because he was composing a eulogy and didn't know yet whose.

At dusk, the fleet's lead ship fired a warning burst across the northern ridge—not at anything, just near enough that the boom rolled over the domes and every child in the colony went silent at once. A reminder. Compliance ensures safety.

That night, Tam found him on the clinic roof, looking up at the ships parked in the sky like patient predators.

"The comms tower," the boy said. "The old one. On the ridge."

"What about it?"

Tam climbed up beside him, small and deliberate, and looked at Odile with eyes too old for nine. "The dream isn't a dream. We figured that out. It's a signal. We're the only copies of that night that exist anywhere, and nobody can edit us without killing us, and they're about to kill us anyway." He shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "So I guess the question is whether signals die when the transmitter does. Or whether they keep going. Whether somebody out there is listening."

Odile stared at him. Somewhere below, Sefa was lighting the clinic's last good lamps, one by one, keeping the lights on against all sense. Somewhere above, three ships waited with their holds full of fire. And somewhere in his own bloodstream, a countdown he couldn't see was rewriting him hour by hour into the man the company needed him to be.

An asset assists. A man chooses.

"Tam," Odile said slowly, "how strong is that old tower's transmitter?"

The boy smiled, and it was the orange light of the dream, and it was hope, and they were the same thing.

"Strong enough," Tam said, "if we're loud enough."

Below them, the fleet's landing barges began to drop through the dark like seeds of ash—and Odile felt, deep in the rewritten wiring of his chest, the contract tighten one more notch, and knew he had until sunrise to decide which of his makers he answered to.
