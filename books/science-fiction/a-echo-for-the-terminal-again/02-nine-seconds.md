## 2. Nine Seconds

The tea had gone cold again. Orsen Nakamura noticed this the way he noticed most things—belatedly, without urgency, as one more small failure in a life that had been engineered to contain nothing that could fail catastrophically. He wrapped his hands around the mug anyway, more for the ritual than the warmth, and watched the second replay of the sensor data crawl across his console.

Nine seconds.

He had measured it four times now, four different ways, because the alternative was to believe it on the first pass and spend the rest of his life being a man who believed impossible things before breakfast. The beacon's gravimetric array—a temperamental antique he'd spent six years coaxing into something resembling reliability—had registered a fluctuation at 0347 system time. Not a tremor. Not the slow tidal breathing of a distant mass or the ordinary noise of spacetime shrugging off some freighter's sloppy fold. A spike. A clean, brutal displacement curve that rose like a blade and fell like a guillotine, nine seconds end to end, centered on coordinates where the shipping charts said there was nothing at all.

Except the charts were wrong. The charts had always been wrong about that patch of space, hadn't they? Because he'd filed claims against it for years.

Orsen set down the tea and pulled up the claim logs.

Beacon 7-Kestrel existed, officially, to monitor insurance risk along the Meridian outer lanes: micrometeorite density, solar weather, the statistical likelihood that some hauler's cargo of fusion catalysts would cook itself into slag. In practice, it existed so that someone—him—would sit in a metal can at the edge of everything and generate paperwork that no one read. He'd known that when he took the posting. He'd *preferred* it. Every claim he logged went into a queue with a processing lag of eleven months, routed through three subcommittees, and died quietly in an archive no auditor visited twice. It was the most beautiful bureaucracy he had ever seen: a machine built entirely out of being ignored.

And for years, buried in those ignored claims, there had been entries tagged VERITY.

Colony Verity. A mining settlement on the dwarf planetoid designated MV-114c by the survey bureau and *Verity* by the people who actually lived there—forty thousand of them, if the manifests in his old claim files could be believed. He'd processed their hazard premiums for a decade. Dust-exposure riders. Seismic indemnification for the deep shafts. A group policy for the school, of all things; he remembered the absurd tenderness of that one, forty thousand miners and their children insured against a universe that mostly couldn't be bothered to notice them.

He typed the colony's designation into the beacon's registry link and waited for the directory handshake.

*No record found.*

He tried the name instead. *Verity.*

*No record found.*

His fingers hovered over the keys. This was the part where you told yourself the archive was down. Archives went down. Concord's data lattice dropped nodes the way old men dropped keys—constantly, carelessly, and always temporarily. He ran the query again with a wider net, routing through two relay mirrors and a cached copy of the colonial charter index that the beacon kept for exactly these kinds of outages.

The cache returned a single line, timestamped eleven years old:

*MV-114c — see amended registry.*

He followed the amendment. The amendment pointed to a correction. The correction pointed to a deletion notice, and the deletion notice pointed to nothing at all—not a redaction bar, not a sealed file, not even the bureaucratic courtesy of *restricted access*. Just a smooth, seamless absence, the digital equivalent of a wall that had been painted over so many times the plaster underneath had forgotten it once held a door.

Orsen sat very still.

Forty thousand people did not simply stop existing. Charters left paper trails the way glaciers left moraines—mining rights, water reclamation permits, labor disputes, tax assessments, the endless shed skin of any human settlement. Even a colony that failed—even one that starved or went dark or cracked its own atmosphere—left wreckage in the records. Bankruptcies. Salvage auctions. Memorial registries. Graves, for God's sake. Somebody somewhere always kept a list of the dead.

He searched for graves.

He searched for bodies, for casualty reports, for evacuation notices, for quarantine flags, for the cold little euphemisms bureaucracies loved—*asset decommission*, *settlement consolidation*, *population relocation*. Nothing. It wasn't that the records showed Verity dying. It was that the records showed Verity never having been born. Forty thousand lives, unwritten in a single administrative gesture, and the space where they had been was so clean it gleamed.

The tea sat untouched. Outside the viewport, Kestrel's slow orbit carried him past the dark limb of an airless rock, and the stars wheeled with the patience of things that would still be there when every record of every human being had rotted away.

* * *

By the fourth hour he had stopped searching the archives and started interrogating the waveform.

This was the work he was actually good at—the quiet, obsessive forensics of a man who had spent years alone with instruments. He stripped the nine-second event down to its components and laid them out across his display like a surgeon laying out tools.

A natural gravitational event had a texture. Collapsing masses groaned. Tidal shifts rolled in long, low frequencies. Even a ship's drive misfiring produced a signature with fingerprints all over it—sloppy harmonics, thermal bleed, the mechanical stutter of machinery doing something it shouldn't.

This had no texture. That was the first thing that made his skin prickle. The displacement curve was too clean, rising along a profile that looked less like physics and more like mathematics—like someone had drawn the ideal shape of a gravity well and then made reality hold still while it was imposed. The second thing was the duration. Nine seconds, sharp on both ends. Natural events didn't have edges. Weapons did.

He'd read about gravity weapons the way he'd read about everything—obliquely, in decommissioned engineering bulletins and the paranoid margins of academic journals that nobody cited anymore. The theory was simple enough to be obscene: impose a transient mass-shadow on a target volume and let differential stress do the rest. Structures didn't explode. They *inverted*. Atmospheres slammed sideways. People—

People became a statistic that someone else got to write.

Orsen pulled the amplitude figures and did the arithmetic twice, then a third time, hoping each pass would make the number smaller. It didn't. At the source distance implied by the waveform's dispersion, the energy required to produce that displacement wasn't in the range of accidents, or mining charges, or anything with a civilian serial number. It was in the range of things governments built in secret and denied in public.

And the timing. He kept coming back to the timing. The event had occurred at 0347. His beacon had flagged it automatically, queued it for review, and—because he was diligent, because diligence was the only vanity he permitted himself—he'd reviewed it within the hour. But when he checked the beacon's outbound transmission log, he found something that turned his mouth dry: his automatic alert packet, the one that should have propagated to the regional monitoring network within minutes, had been sent, acknowledged, and then *withdrawn*. A recall order had come down the relay chain at 0402—fifteen minutes after the event—and the network had swallowed his data like a stone dropped into deep water.

Fifteen minutes. Someone had detected the weapon discharge, identified which sensors had seen it, and reached into the infrastructure to pluck those observations out before they could take root. That wasn't cleanup. Cleanup took days. This was choreography. This was a hand already resting on the switch before the light came on.

Which meant the flicker hadn't been hidden after the fact. It had been hidden *in advance*.

Orsen stood up, because sitting felt suddenly like agreeing to something. He paced the narrow length of the operations module—eleven steps, turn, eleven steps back—a habit from a man who had taught himself that movement was safer than thought, though he could no longer have said why. The beacon hummed around him, its ancient systems cycling through their patient routines, and he caught himself looking at the walls as if the metal itself might be listening.

Of course it was listening. Everything listened now. That was rather the point of everything.

Here was the shape of it, laid out in the cold light of his console: a colony of forty thousand people had been erased from existence with a weapon, and the erasure had been so complete, so premeditated, that the entire Meridian data lattice had been prepared to forget it before it happened. And the only instrument in the region that had recorded the truth was a derelict insurance beacon at the edge of nowhere, staffed by a keeper whose reports no one read.

Whose reports no one read.

He stopped pacing.

That was the thought that arrived then, uninvited, wearing the face of a question he'd never asked himself in six years of deliberate invisibility: *why was I posted here?* Of all the dead-end assignments in the Concord's vast and indifferent bureaucracy, why had the assignment algorithm—or whoever fed it—dropped him onto this specific rock, orbiting this specific patch of space, with a clear sensor line to coordinates that officially contained nothing?

It was probably nothing. It was almost certainly nothing. Bureaucracies scattered people at random; that was what made them bureaucracies. He was a rounding error with a pension. He was nobody, and nobody was the safest thing a man could be.

But his hands weren't steady when he sat back down at the console, and he knew the difference between fear and dread, and this was dread. Fear was for dangers you could name. Dread was for the understanding that the danger had already happened—that it had happened at 0347, nine seconds long, and that everyone it touched was already gone, and that the only thing left of them in the whole universe was sitting on his hard drive wearing the disguise of an insurance anomaly.

He opened the raw waveform file again. At the bottom of the metadata block, in a field that should have been empty, there was a string of characters his automated filters had ignored: a fragmentary header, corrupted, trailing off into checksum garbage. Most of it was unreadable.

But the last intact line resolved cleanly, and it was not a sensor tag, and it was not a system code.

It was a name field. A commissioning signature, half-eaten by corruption, addressed—to judge by its structure—to whoever eventually decrypted the file.

*N. Nakamura.*

Orsen Nakamura stared at the screen for a long time.

There was only one other Nakamura he had ever heard of, and she had been dead for twenty years, and dead women did not leave signatures inside weapons telemetry addressed to strangers.

His tea, forgotten, had frozen solid against the viewport's chill. He did not notice. He was already reaching for the maintenance schematics, pulling up the beacon's internal architecture, following a thread he'd never questioned before—a sealed partition in the core, older than his tenure, that every manual insisted was empty ballast space.

Every instinct he had spent a lifetime cultivating told him to log the anomaly, file it into the beautiful machine that no one read, and go back to sleep.

Instead, for the first time in six years, Orsen Nakamura opened a door he was not authorized to open.
