## 8. A Thief of Records

Dex Oyelaran kept three clocks in his shop, and none of them told the same time.

The first was an honest antique, brass-cased, ticking away the standard Meridian hour because some part of him still believed in systems that worked. The second ran eleven minutes fast, calibrated to the drift-habitat Crowsnest's own sloppy rotation, so that he was never late for a client who might be wearing a wire. The third had no hands at all—just a hairline crack across its face and a date etched beneath it in his own handwriting, twenty-two years old now: *Kolyma Station, deleted.* He wound it every morning anyway. Some rituals were less about time than about refusing to forget.

He was winding it when the proximity chime sounded—a single soft note from the outer dock, meaning someone had come through the airlock without filing a berth request.

Dex set the dead clock down and reached under the counter for the shock baton that he'd never once needed but had never once stopped carrying.

"Shop's closed," he called out, to the empty archive room, to the walls of sealed drives and salvaged memory cores that made up the only honest library in the Meridian system—the one nobody official knew existed. "Come back during hours or don't come back."

The door to the front gallery slid open anyway. Of course it did; Dex had never gotten around to fixing the lock, on the theory that anyone worth keeping out would defeat it regardless, and anyone worth letting in wouldn't need an invitation.

The man who stood there was soaked in sweat despite the cold, breathing like he'd run the length of the habitat ring, and wearing the particular gray-faced expression Dex had learned to read long ago on Kolyma, in the last hour before Kolyma stopped existing. It was the look of someone who had seen the machinery behind the curtain—and who understood, with total clarity, that the machinery had seen him back.

"You're the keeper," the man said. Not a question. "From 7-Kestrel. Nakamura."

Dex didn't move. "I'm a dealer in obsolete media formats. Historical curiosities. Nothing else."

"Your name came up in my mother's maintenance threads." The man—Nakamura, younger than Dex expected, thinner, with the hollowed-out look of someone who hadn't slept since the numbers stopped making sense—took a step inside and let the door seal behind him. "Buried in a routing table twenty years old. *If the partition wakes, find Oyelaran.* She wrote you into the beacon's firmware, Dex. You're not a curiosity dealer. You're a contingency plan."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere above them, Crowsnest groaned through another slow tumble of its spin cycle, and the drives hummed their patient hum, holding ten thousand deleted histories against the day someone might come asking.

Dex looked at this stranger who knew his name, who carried his name inside a machine at the edge of nowhere, and felt the third clock tick somewhere behind his ribs.

"Sit down," he said, "before you fall down. And start from the beginning. I want all nine seconds."

* * *

Orsen Nakamura talked for two hours, and Dex listened the way he listened to everything—with half his attention on the story and the other half running threat calculations against the wall displays, checking dock manifests, watching the traffic patterns around Crowsnest for the telltale clustering that meant a Concord interdiction team was assembling somewhere out in the dark.

The story itself was bad enough. A gravity flicker with a weapon's signature. Forty thousand people unmade from the record in minutes. An erasure bureau strike team planting false logs aboard the beacon while Nakamura hid in the walls like a rat in a burning house. And underneath all of it, the thing that made Dex's hands go cold on the edge of his desk: a sealed partition full of human memory, commissioned by a woman named Ilsa Nakamura, threaded into the deepest firmware of Beacon 7-Kestrel, waiting twenty years for her son to find it.

"I know what you're thinking," Orsen said, when he finally ran out of breath. "You're thinking: why me? Why bring this to a black-market archivist instead of a journalist, a lawyer, a Concord ombudsman?"

"I wasn't thinking that," Dex said. "I was thinking: why did it take you eight days? I'd have been here in two." He pulled up the habitat's local net and opened a channel he used maybe twice a year. "But go on. Ask your question."

"Why help me at all?" Orsen leaned forward. "You don't know me. This isn't your fight. You could hand me to the Bureau tonight and collect whatever bounty they're running for a witness to Verity."

Dex laughed, and it came out uglier than he intended. "A bounty. Right. Let me show you what the Bureau pays for silence, keeper, because you seem to think they deal in currency."

He turned his display toward Orsen and opened a file he had not opened in twenty-two years. It was small. It was almost nothing—a station manifest, a crew roster, a list of four hundred names, each one struck through with a red line that no legitimate database would ever render.

"Kolyma Station," Dex said. "Outer belt refinery. Four hundred souls. I was born there. My father ran the ore processors; my mother taught school in the lower ring. When I was nineteen, the station's debt portfolio was sold to a Concord holding company, and six weeks later the holding company realized the station was worth more as a tax write-off than as a going concern. There was an accident. A pressure event. Very tragic. Very well documented—for about seventy-two hours, until the documentation itself was reclassified, then restricted, then gone. No manifests. No graves. No Kolyma." He tapped the red lines. "Except in here. Except in the copies I've spent my whole adult life stealing back from the people who erase things."

Orsen stared at the screen. "You lost your parents."

"I lost everyone," Dex said. "And then I lost the fact that I'd lost them, officially speaking. Do you understand what that does to a person? Grief needs a grave, keeper. Needs a record, a stone, a line in a ledger somewhere that says *this happened, these people were real.* Take that away and the grief has nowhere to live. It just—" He gestured vaguely at the walls, at the drives, at the whole crouched, cluttered, illegal heart of his life. "It becomes this. Twenty-two years of this. Every deleted history I can get my hands on, hoarded like a dragon sits on gold, waiting for a day when somebody builds a court big enough to hold all of it."

"And now?"

"Now you walk in here carrying forty thousand voices and a weapon signature, and you ask me if it's my fight." Dex shook his head slowly. "Verity is Kolyma, Nakamura. Same play, bigger stage. The Bureau refined the technique in twenty years—that's the only difference. They erased a colony in nine seconds flat and had the paperwork clean before the bodies finished drifting. If they get away with it, if the method works at scale, then every station, every outpost, every inconvenient population in this system is one debt-default away from becoming a rumor. Including—" he tapped the cracked, handless clock, the one with the date etched under it, "—including every ghost I've spent my life keeping. Because an erasure that works once gets reused. That's how tools work. That's how weapons work."

"So it's not altruism."

"Don't insult us both." Dex stood and crossed to the vault wall, pressing his palm to the reader. The inner drives woke with a sound like a held breath released. "It's self-interest. The coldest, most selfish kind. Your mother built a tomb for testimony inside a Concord beacon, and she routed its contingency plan through me, which means she knew what I was, which means either she was very good at research or we met once and I don't remember, and honestly both options frighten me. But here's what matters: if the Bureau scrubs Verity completely—if they catch you, catch the echo, catch every copy—you won't just be killing their future victims' chances. You'll be proving the tool works. And the first place they'll test it again is on the archives. On people like me. On everything I am." He turned back. "So yes. Grudgingly. Reluctantly. With a truly impressive amount of complaint queued up for later—I'm helping you."

Orsen exhaled, and for a moment the gray left his face, replaced by something rawer. Relief, maybe. Or the beginning of hope, which was more dangerous.

"What do you need from me?" Orsen asked.

"Everything. The partition architecture, the encryption keys, your mother's routing threads—all of it. And I need time. Decrypting forty thousand memory recordings isn't cracking a safe, it's rebuilding a city from rubble. Days, minimum. Maybe a week." Dex was already pulling workbenches clear, dragging a diagnostic rig into the light. "Which brings us to the problem."

"What problem?"

"The problem where the Concord erasure bureau knows a witness exists, knows roughly where he fled, and has exactly one obvious place to look." Dex nodded upward, at the ceiling, at the thin hull beyond it, at the whole fragile tumbling world of Crowsnest. "This habitat survives on deniability, keeper. Three hundred residents, no charter, no registry, a blind spot in every patrol route because enough of the right people get paid to keep it that way. But deniability has limits. Bring a Bureau kill team to our doorstep and those limits arrive very fast."

"I can leave. Take the echo somewhere else—"

"You can't. You said it yourself—the deep decryption needs serious compute, and the only compute in this system that doesn't report to Concord is mine." Dex grimaced. "Congratulations. You've made my shop the most important room in the Meridian system. I'd like to formally object to that."

For the first time since walking through his door, Orsen almost smiled. It was a tired, crooked thing, but it was real. "Noted for the record."

"Oh, there'll be a record." Dex fed the first data spike into the rig and watched the partition's directory tree bloom across his screens—thousands of entries, thousands of sealed human moments, names and faces and a child's song nested among them like fossils in stone. His throat tightened in a way he refused to acknowledge. "There will absolutely be a record. That's the entire point of me."

They worked through the habitat's night cycle, side by side, saying little. Dex decrypted; Orsen catalogued, reading entry headers aloud in a low voice—*Mara Voss, hydroponics tech, final log; Toma Ilic, age sixty-one, testimony regarding security forces; Sela, age seven, song, fragment*—each one a small bell tolling in the dark. Dex had heard ten thousand dying echoes in his career. These were different. These weren't records of the dead. They were messages from people who had known they were about to die, and had chosen, in their last hours, to speak *forward*. To whoever came after. To him, maybe. To all of them.

Near the end of the shift, Orsen went still over one entry, and Dex watched the color drain out of his face all over again.

"What is it?"

"Her voice." Orsen's hand hovered over the playback control without touching it. "My mother. Timestamped eleven days after her death was registered. She was alive, Dex. She was alive and she was on Verity, and she recorded herself into this partition knowing what was coming." His voice cracked on the last word, quietly, like something load-bearing giving way.

Dex said nothing. There was nothing to say that the moment didn't already say better. He just reached over and, gently, marked the file with a priority flag—*do not autoplay; user discretion*—because some griefs deserved to be entered through the front door, on purpose, in daylight.

That was when the alarm chimed.

Not the soft dock note from earlier. This was the hard klaxon, the one Crowsnest's harbor master reserved for unscheduled arrivals under thrust—and Dex was moving before it finished its second pulse, pulling up the external feed with fingers that remembered Kolyma even when his mind tried to forget.

Three contacts, inbound from the Concord patrol corridor, running dark except for their approach lights. Official transponders, switched off. Formation tight, professional, fast.

"They found the beacon logs," Orsen said behind him, flat and certain. "The falsified ones. They'll have flagged my escape pod's trajectory. I led them straight to you."

"No." Dex was already killing the shop's external links, spinning up the vault's physical isolation, his mind running cold and clear along rails worn smooth by twenty-two years of practice. "You didn't lead them anywhere. They were always coming. The only question was whether we'd be ready." He grabbed a drive case from the rack—one of a dozen identical decoys, seeded with plausible garbage, built for exactly this night—and shoved it into Orsen's hands. "Back exit, service ring, third junction. There's a crawlspace behind the coolant recycling plant that fits two uncomfortable people and forty thousand ghosts. Move."

"And you?"

Dex looked at his shop—at the drives, the clocks, the whole crooked cathedral of stolen truth he'd built out of the wreckage of his life—and felt the old fear rise up and meet the older stubbornness, head-on.

"I'm going to answer the door," he said. "Someone has to make them believe the archivist died with his archive."

Above them, through the thin hull, the first boarding clamps rang against Crowsnest's spine like a bell tolling for the living—and Dex Oyelaran, thief of records, keeper of the deleted dead, went to buy them time.
