## 18. Broadcast

The core chamber smelled of ozone and old dust, and it sounded like a city.

Orsen knelt in the maintenance well beneath the beacon's primary lattice, the echo's decryption cradle open in front of him like a reliquary. Forty thousand memory-threads pulsed through the partition in slow amber waves—grief and breakfast and arguments and lullabies, the whole unremarkable weight of Colony Verity compressed into light. He had spent three days learning to touch it without flinching. He was not sure he had succeeded.

His hands were steady anyway. That surprised him. Twenty years of deliberate smallness—of logging insurance claims no one read, of being a name so far down every manifest that even the Concord's erasure bureau had needed three attempts to find him—and now his hands were steady because there was nothing left to be invisible for.

*They're coming,* Sefa's voice said in his ear, thin through the relay he'd spliced into her comm channel. *Two cutters off the Kestrel docking ring. Brannoc is with them. Ninety seconds, maybe less.*

"Then I have ninety seconds," Orsen said.

"You have sixty. They're not announcing themselves this time."

He almost laughed. Of course they weren't. The Bureau had learned its lesson at Crowsnest: pretense cost time, and time was the one thing an erasure could never buy back once the first voice went out.

The splice point was a junction node two meters above his head, where the beacon's internal bus met the long-haul transmitter that had spent decades sending nothing but automated weather reports to no one. Dex's map glowed on his wrist display—ten thousand civilian relay nodes scattered across the Meridian system like stars someone had tried to sweep under a rug. Cargo relays. Habitat repeaters. A child's toy network on Halcyon Reach that bounced messages between bedrooms. The Bureau could scrub official channels in minutes. It could not scrub ten thousand private ones, not before the echo finished copying itself, and by then the copy would be making copies.

*You'll want to route through the third harmonic,* Dex's voice said suddenly, breaking in over the same channel from wherever he'd gone after Crowsnest burned—a ghost signal, bounced twice, origin masked. His voice sounded rougher than Orsen remembered. Thinner. *The Bureau's jamming suite reads harmonics one and two as hostile traffic. Three looks like background radiation. I wrote the spoof myself, back when I still believed in selling things instead of giving them away.*

"Dex." Orsen closed his eyes for half a second. "You made it out."

*I made something out,* Dex said. *Close enough. Don't waste my bandwidth on feelings, keeper. Waste it on the broadcast.*

A shudder ran through the beacon's hull—the first cutter kissing the docking ring. Orsen felt it through his knees, through the deck plates, through the old bones of the station that had been his whole world. Somewhere above him, airlock clamps engaged with a sound like teeth closing.

He reached up and pulled himself to the junction node.

The splice took forty seconds of work he'd rehearsed a hundred times in simulation: fiber bridge into the harmonic router, echo cradle slaved to the transmitter, authentication forged from Sefa's credentials layered over Dex's spoof signatures until the packet looked like exactly what it was not—routine telemetry, boring as weather, harmless as a man who logged insurance claims nobody read.

His mother's voice moved through the cradle beside him. Not speaking—not yet—but present, a thread among forty thousand threads, waiting.

"Ilsa," he whispered, because no one else would hear it, and because some things needed saying aloud before they became permanent. "I'm doing it your way. The loud way you were too smart to survive trying."

The second cutter docked. Boots on metal, many boots, moving fast.

*Orsen.* Sefa again, sharp now. *They've split. Six heading for the core chamber. You have maybe thirty seconds before—*

"Before they find me standing in the middle of their crime scene holding the evidence." He keyed the final handshake. The router accepted the forged handshake without complaint, the way machines accept all lies that are formatted correctly. "Tell me when you're clear of the hub."

*I'm not leaving the hub.*

"Sefa—"

*Someone has to hold the door open while your voices walk through it,* she said, and there was something in her voice he hadn't heard before—not fear, not guilt, though she carried enough of both to sink a station. Resolve. The specific, terrible calm of a woman who had signed one erasure too many and had decided, finally, what her signature was worth. *Go. Broadcast.*

He pressed the transmit key.

For one full second, nothing happened. The beacon hummed. The boots above him kept coming. And Orsen Nakamura stood alone in the dark with forty thousand dead souls in his hands, and felt the oldest fear of his life—that he would press the button and the universe would simply decline to listen.

Then the third harmonic opened, and Verity spoke.

* * *

It began with names.

That was Ilsa's design—he understood it now, understood everything now—and it was the design's genius. Not testimony first, not argument, not the footage of the gravity weapon's nine-second flicker that had folded a colony into a singularity of debt-collateral and silence. Names. Forty thousand of them, spoken in forty thousand voices, each one attached to a face and a birthdate and a place of residence, each one delivered in the owner's own timbre onto ten thousand civilian channels simultaneously, so that across the Meridian system, in cargo holds and kitchens and cockpit bunks and schoolrooms, people stopped mid-motion and heard strangers say their own names back at them in a stranger's cadence.

*Mirela Osei, age thirty-four, hydroponics tier six.*

*Tomasz Reyes-Vance, age seven.*

*Grandmother Anouk Pell, who taught everyone on the east ring to dance.*

On Beacon 7-Kestrel, in the core chamber, Orsen listened to his own handiwork fill the dark, and wept without deciding to.

The voices did not stop for grief. They rolled on, wave after wave, exactly as the echo had been built to do—because Ilsa Nakamura had known that a massacre announced once is a story, and stories can be disputed, but a massacre that introduces itself person by person, name by name, voice by voice, for hours, becomes a census of the dead that no bureau on any world has the resources to deny. The Bureau could falsify logs. It could plant records, pre-date orders, disappear charters. It could not un-say forty thousand names that were already replicating through ten thousand relays faster than any purge team could travel.

Above him, the boots stopped.

Orsen wiped his face with the back of one hand and dropped down into the maintenance well, pulling the cradle's housing shut behind him. Through the narrow sightline between conduit bundles, he watched the core chamber door blow inward in a bloom of cutting torch sparks, watched six figures in Bureau black pour through with weapons up, watched them find the chamber empty except for the amber pulse of the echo—still transmitting, still singing, untouchable behind its forged handshake.

"It's live," one of them said, flat with disbelief. "Sir, it's already live. It's on the civilian nets. All of them."

A pause. Then Brannoc's voice, and Orsen's blood went cold at how calm it was. Auditor Brannoc, Sefa's former partner, the man whose complicity she had broadcast over open channels only hours ago—which meant he knew he was already damned, which meant he had nothing left to protect except the mission.

"Then we take the source," Brannoc said. "Pull the cradle. Melt the partition. If the original dies before the copies finish propagating, the network degrades. Fragmented testimony is deniable testimony. Move."

They moved toward the maintenance well. Toward him.

Orsen pressed himself deeper into the shaft and did the thing that twenty years of invisibility had trained into his body better than any combat drill ever could: he made himself part of the architecture. Breath shallow. Hands flat against cold conduit. Eyes soft-focused past the intruders rather than at them. For seventeen chapters of his life this trick had been his entire survival strategy, and it failed him now within four seconds, because Brannoc stopped at the edge of the well and looked directly down into the dark where Orsen was hiding, and said:

"Nakamura. I know you're there. The thermal bloom off your cradle is visible from orbit."

Orsen closed his eyes. *So much for the quiet way.*

But the echo was still flowing through him, around him, filling the beacon's bones with the dead of Verity, and somewhere in that flood a child was singing—the same child whose song had been the first thing he'd heard in the sealed partition, weeks ago, when this was still a mystery instead of a war. *Tomasz Reyes-Vance, age seven.* The song threaded through the chamber now, through the speakers of every relay in range, bright and tuneless and absolutely undeniable, and Brannoc's men shifted uneasily as it washed over them, because it is one thing to raid a station and another thing entirely to do it while a murdered child sings into your helmet.

"The copies are past critical mass," Orsen called up, and his voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Every minute you stand there, it spreads further. Kill me, melt the core, and you'll be famous for it. The man who shot the witness live on ten thousand channels."

"We're not here for spectacle." But Brannoc's voice had changed—tightened. Orsen had spent his life reading the micro-tremors of people who wanted to be left alone, and he recognized the frequency now: fear wearing procedure as a coat. "We're here for containment."

"Containment ended ninety seconds ago."

Brannoc raised his weapon toward the well.

And the beacon screamed.

Not the alarm—Orsen had silenced the alarms. The scream was structural: the deep, groaning complaint of a station's spine flexing beyond tolerance, transmitted through the hull and the deck and the marrow of everyone aboard. Orsen felt it in his teeth. Above him, the Bureau team staggered, grabbed for handholds.

"What did you do?" Brannoc demanded.

"That wasn't me." And it wasn't. Orsen stared at his display, at the diagnostic feed scrolling red, and understood one heartbeat before the readout confirmed it. "Sefa. What are you doing?"

Over the comm channel, calm as a claims adjuster denying a fraudulent report, Sefa Rhee said: "The relay hub's docking clamps are magnetized to full lock, and I've just vented the hub's ballast mass into the approach corridor. Your cutters aren't going anywhere, Auditor. Neither is your extraction. I'm holding the door open, just like I said."

"You'll kill us all—"

"No," Sefa said. "I'll keep you all exactly where you are, in front of witnesses, until the system finishes hearing what you buried. That's not killing, Brannoc. That's jurisdiction."

* * *

Across the Meridian system, the nine seconds reversed.

In the Concord Station grand concourse, a junior clerk named Adisa stopped mid-stride, her coffee cooling in her hand, as the public address system—an announcement channel that had carried nothing but berth assignments for eleven years—began reciting the manifest of Colony Verity. She stayed for an hour. By the time she left, three hundred people stood with her in silence, and someone had begun, quietly, to write the names on the concourse floor in chalk.

In the mining belt, foremen shut down the processors—not in protest, at first, but because their crews refused to work while the voices played, and because the foremen, listening, found they didn't want to work either.

On Halcyon Reach, the children's toy network—the same humble bedroom-to-bedroom relay Dex had mapped—became the single most resilient distribution node in the system, because no Bureau analyst had ever thought to jam a network built by eight-year-olds, and by nightfall every child on the Reach knew Tomasz Reyes-Vance's song, and sang it in corridors where their parents' generation had been taught to look away.

At Concord Station's relay control, Sefa Rhee sat alone in a room full of locked doors and screaming consoles, holding the hub against Brannoc's stranded cutters with nothing but magnetized clamps, vented ballast, and the absolute refusal to move. Her console showed the propagation curve climbing—forty percent of the system covered, fifty-five, sixty-one—and beneath it, another feed: the Bureau's internal purge queue, order after order to scrub, delete, deny, each one failing, each one marked FAILED, FAILED, FAILED, in a column that looked, to Sefa, like the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

She had signed one erasure, years ago. One small one. A village's worth of inconvenient debt records, she had told herself at the time; no one died. She had told herself that for nine years.

Now she watched the counter climb and whispered, to no one, to everyone: "Say the names. Say all of them."

* * *

In the maintenance well beneath the core, Orsen Nakamura felt the moment the echo passed the point of no return.

He couldn't have explained how he knew. There was no alarm, no chime. It was something in the texture of the transmission itself—the amber pulse of the cradle shifting rhythm, the way a tide turns without announcement. The copies were self-sustaining now. Ten thousand relays feeding ten thousand more. The Bureau could burn this beacon to slag, could shoot him where he crouched, and the truth would go on spreading through the Meridian system like water finding every low place.

Brannoc seemed to feel it too. Through the sightline, Orsen watched the auditor lower his weapon—not in surrender, but in recalculation, the cold arithmetic of a man realizing that the asset he'd been sent to contain had already escaped the borders of the map.

"Fall back to the cutters," Brannoc said to his team. Quietly. Almost gently. "All of you. Now."

"They can't reach the cutters," Orsen said, climbing slowly out of the well, hands visible, weaponless, standing at last in the open in the chamber where his whole hidden life had been stored. "Your extraction is magnetized to the hull and your approach corridor is full of ballast mass. Sefa Rhee is very thorough. You should have read her file more carefully before you pre-dated her signature."

Brannoc turned. Up close, without the helmet's distortion, his face was older than his file photo and tired in a way that files don't record. "You think this ends tonight? You think forty thousand voices change the arithmetic? The directors will call it a terrorist fabrication by morning. Half the system will believe them, because believing them is comfortable."

"Probably," Orsen agreed. "Half the system believed it when Verity disappeared, too. But the other half—" he gestured upward, at the ceiling, at the whole webbed sky of relays beyond it, where a child's song was still threading its way through the dark, "—the other half is awake now, and they have the names, and names don't go back to sleep. My mother counted on that. She spent twenty years building for the other half."

Something moved behind Brannoc's eyes—recognition, maybe, or its exhausted cousin, resignation. "Nakamura. Ilsa's boy." He exhaled. "She was on the design review board for the weapon, you know. Before she turned. We all knew her name."

"I know," Orsen said. "I know everything now. That's rather the point."

Brannoc's hand twitched toward his sidearm—one last reflex of a dying doctrine—and then the beacon groaned again, longer this time, and every light in the core chamber shifted to emergency amber, and Orsen's display lit with a prompt he had written himself, days ago, in the quiet hours before the trap at Kestrel, hoping he would never have the courage to arm it:

CORE SELF-DESTRUCT — AUTHORIZATION REQUIRED — N. NAKAMURU / O. NAKAMURA

He looked at Brannoc. Brannoc looked at him. Between them, the echo poured outward, wave after wave, the dead of Verity introducing themselves to a system that could never again claim it had not heard.

"You should leave," Orsen said. "I'm taking the original with me when I go. No core for you to melt. No trophy. Just the copies, everywhere, forever."

"And you?" Brannoc asked. "Where will you be?"

Orsen smiled—or something adjacent to a smile, the expression of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. "Witnessing," he said, and put his palm on the authorization plate.

Somewhere above, in a relay hub full of locked doors, Sefa Rhee saw the destruct countdown begin and started, calmly, to seal her own escape route. Somewhere beyond the hull, in a decoy-shrouded drift shadow, a stolen skiff waited with its engines warm. And across ten thousand relays, forty thousand voices sang on—

—as the countdown reached zero, and Beacon 7-Kestrel, keeper of nothing and tomb of everything, came apart into light.
