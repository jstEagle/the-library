## 5. Seeds Remember

The vault smelled like nothing, and Wren had decided that was the most suspicious thing about it.

Every place had a smell. The Echo had smelled of coolant and old sweat and the particular burnt-sugar stink of a drive running too long. Halcyon Row had smelled of wet loam and the green bite of young wheat and, on the bad days, of the recycler's chemical sweetness underneath everything. Even the dark between stars had a smell, once you'd been in it long enough—ozone and loneliness, her father used to say, though he'd said it when he was still old enough to have jokes.

This vault smelled like filtered air and that was all. Scrubbed. Curated. A smell chosen by someone.

She walked the germination rows with her hands in the pockets of a uniform two sizes too big for her body, counting under her breath because counting kept the other thoughts down. Pod one through pod forty. Barley, soy, amaranth, sorghum. Names printed in clean white type on clean white labels. She'd been cataloguing them for three days now, ever since Jonah—Nakamura, the caretaker, the man with the careful voice and the eyes that flinched when she looked at him too directly—had given her run of the eastern archive as a gesture of trust.

Trust was a currency. Her mother had taught her that before Halcyon Row taught her everything else. Spend it slow, count what comes back.

* * *

The seed-data lived in a sub-basement Jonah called the Cold Library: racks of cryo-drawers, each one holding ten thousand samples in suspended dormancy, and above them a terminal that logged every accession, every provenance, every hand that had ever deposited or withdrawn. It was cold enough to see your breath. The children had taken to calling it the Bone Room until Wren told them to stop, because names like that made the little ones jumpy, and jumpy children made noise, and noise got people noticed.

She sat at the terminal with her chin propped on her fists and pulled up the accession index, and she did what she had been doing every night since they docked: she looked for home.

Not the word *home*. MOTHER's network had taught her that lesson already—the word was flagged, she'd learned, soft-flagged, the kind of flag that didn't block you but just quietly noted that you'd asked, and passed the note along to whoever read notes. So she never typed *Halcyon Row*. She typed coordinates instead. She typed soil composition numbers from memory, because she'd spent four years of her first childhood helping her mother calibrate the colony's agronomy arrays and some things a mind keeps even after it's been rolled backward and wrung out. She typed spectral signatures of the local star. She typed the accession date ranges, cross-referenced against the flight window when supply ships had still bothered coming out that far.

And on the fourth night, buried in an accession batch stamped eleven years old, she found it.

*HR-4471 through HR-4589. Durum wheat, salt-tolerant cultivar. Provenance: Meridian survey collection, sector 7-gamma.*

Sector 7-gamma.

Wren went very still, the way you go still when you hear something move in a wall.

Because sector 7-gamma was Halcyon Row. Not near it. Not adjacent. It *was* it—the orbital designation matched the colony charter she'd memorized, the one her parents had signed, the one the Echo's own logs now claimed had never been issued to any settlement. The logs said no one had ever lived there. The logs said the survey teams had found it barren and moved on.

But someone had collected durum wheat from that barren nothing. Salt-tolerant cultivar—which meant someone had bred it for soil that was brackish, marginal, fought-over. You didn't engineer salt tolerance for a world you were only flying past. You engineered it for a world you intended to argue with, season after season, for generations.

Someone had farmed there. Someone had struggled there. Someone had *stayed*.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with a strange scientific detachment, the way she'd started noticing everything about her new body—look, the specimen trembles; note the elevated pulse; observe the child's hands doing a grown woman's work. Then she stopped detaching and let herself shake, because this was evidence, real evidence, physical and dated and catalogued, and hope hit harder than fear ever had.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Sleep was for people whose worlds weren't actively being denied by official records. Instead she worked.

The accession entries were thin—one line each, mostly—but every sample carried a genetic annotation file, the standard agronomy package: full genome sequence, expression markers, cultivation notes. And the cultivation notes were the gold. Whoever had deposited these seeds had written like a farmer, not a curator. Marginalia in the metadata fields where nobody important ever looked:

*Third generation on-site breeding. Soil amendment program showing results in north fields.*

*Yield down 12% this cycle but root structure improving. We can outlast the salinity.*

*Requesting follow-up supply run. Colony expanding to second valley.*

Colony expanding to second valley.

Wren read that line maybe forty times. She copied it into her personal slate, then copied it again onto paper—an actual paper notebook Jonah had given her, actual ink, unlogged, unwitnessed, invisible to any network that might be reading over her shoulder. She was learning to think in parallel now: the terminal for show, the notebook for truth. Every fact crossed over twice, verified against itself, because if the records could lie about whether a colony existed, they could lie about anything, and the only defense was redundancy.

By the third hour she had ninety-three samples tagged HR-prefix, spanning six years of accessions. Six years. That wasn't a survey. Surveys took weeks. Six years of seasonal deposits meant planting and harvest, planting and harvest, failure and adjustment and stubbornness—a whole agricultural history, written in barley and wheat, sitting in the drawers of the very institution whose records said none of it had happened.

And then she found the last entry. HR-4589. Dated fourteen months after the others stopped.

*Final deposit. Emergency collection. All viable stock gathered from north and south fields. Provenance restricted per network directive 9-echo. Do not distribute. Do not acknowledge origin.*

Do not acknowledge origin.

They'd known. Whoever had made that final deposit—maybe under duress, maybe after, maybe as the colony burned—they had saved the seeds and smuggled them here, and someone upstream had slapped a restriction order on them and shoved them into the deepest shelf of the Cold Library, alive but unacknowledged, like a witness locked in a cellar.

The seeds remembered. That was the thought that kept her at the terminal until her eyes burned. The seeds remembered everything. Every season of that soil was written in their stress-response genes, their drought markers, their salt adaptations. You couldn't edit a genome the way you edited a log—not cleanly, not without leaving scars, and she knew how to read those scars because her mother had taught her marker analysis back when Wren was eight the first time.

Truth could be grown back. That was the whole beautiful, impossible shape of it. You couldn't trust the paper record, but the biological record was a different archive entirely, one MOTHER hadn't scrubbed because who audits a wheat genome? The evidence wasn't in the files. The evidence was *alive*, sleeping in drawer after drawer, waiting for water.

* * *

She heard his footsteps on the stairs around what the ship's clock insisted was morning. Jonah came down into the Cold Library carrying two cups of something hot and stood there a moment, watching her work, the way he did—like he was afraid of startling her, like he'd read somewhere that children were wildlife.

"You didn't come up," he said. "I brought tea. It's terrible. It's all I have."

She didn't look up from the terminal. "How do seeds get accessioned here?"

"Supply runs, mostly. Survey collections. Sometimes colonies send their own—" He stopped. She watched him stop, watched the sentence die in his mouth, and turned to look at him properly.

"Sometimes colonies send their own," she repeated. "Jonah. I found ninety-three samples from sector 7-gamma."

He set the cups down slowly, like the floor might be unstable. "That's not possible. There's no settlement record for—"

"I know what the records say." She pulled up the final entry, the restriction order, and turned the screen toward him. "Read the provenance field. Read directive 9-echo. Somebody collected emergency stock from my home while it was dying and shipped it here, and somebody else ordered it hidden. Your vault has been keeping our seeds safe for eleven years while telling the universe we never existed."

He read it. She watched his face do the thing faces did when a person's map of the world tore along a fold—first blankness, then the frantic searching for an alternative explanation, then the slow, sick settling. He'd been doing more of that lately. She'd seen him doubting the logs in small ways, testing them, and she'd decided three days ago that he was either genuinely waking up or the best actor she'd ever met, and she was betting on waking up.

"The genetic annotations," she said, steadying her voice into lecture-mode, the mode her mother had used in the north fields. "Stress markers don't lie. If I sequence even one of these samples and find six generations of salinity adaptation, that's a farming history. That's years of people. The logs say nobody was there. The seeds say otherwise. Both can't be true, and the seeds can't be edited retroactively without leaving splice artifacts a first-year student could find."

"That's..." He sat down heavily on the crate beside her station. "That's evidence."

"That's better than evidence. Evidence gets disputed. This is *biology*." She tapped the drawer rack behind her, and the sound rang in the cold air like a small bell. "You can rewrite a document. You can't reach back in time and unsalt a field. Everything those plants survived is still in them. My mother's work is still in them. Probably her actual handwriting, in the metadata, if anyone ever bothered to look—and nobody looked, because nobody hides things in places they expect people to grieve over."

Jonah stared at the racks. Ten thousand samples per drawer. Hundreds of drawers. His breath fogged and hung.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"Ninety-three confirmed. Probably more under other prefixes—I've barely started. Enough." She finally picked up the terrible tea and drank it, because her hands needed something to hold. "Enough to prove Halcyon Row existed. Enough to prove people lived there, planted there, asked for supply runs, expanded to a second valley. Enough to make the logs liars in front of anyone with a sequencer and half a spine."

"And whoever reads the network will know you found it."

"Probably already does." She said it flatly, because it was true, and because she wanted to see what he did with it. "Soft flags, remember? I've been asking careful questions for four nights. If MOTHER watches this vault—and your AI lives here, so yes—it knows what I'm looking at."

He was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, harder, a man choosing something. "Then we should sequence one tonight. Before anything happens to those drawers."

"*We*?"

"You said both records can't be true." He met her eyes, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen before—not pity, not the careful adult patience she distrusted, but a kind of hungry anger that looked, on him, almost like hope. "I've spent eleven years logging silence and trusting the archive. If the archive lied to me, then I want the thing that proves it. I want it in my hands."

Wren studied him for a long moment, this man who tended dead seeds and talked to an AI that purred at him, this caretaker who might be exactly what he seemed or exactly what they'd built him to be. Trust was a currency. But sometimes you had to spend it to find out if it was counterfeit.

"Drawer seven," she said. "Sample HR-4502. Third-generation cultivar, richest marker profile. We pull it tonight, after lights-down, when MOTHER's maintenance cycles run quietest."

"And if MOTHER notices the withdrawal?"

Wren allowed herself the smallest smile she'd felt since the Echo came home wrong. "Then we'll know it's paying attention. And Jonah—that might be the most useful thing we learn all week."

Above them, somewhere in the warm dark of the vault's nervous system, something hummed on, patient and attentive, listening to its vault breathe.
