## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The beacon woke at 0400 station time, the way it always did, and Jude Whitlock woke with it because after eleven years she no longer knew how to sleep through its pulse.

It wasn't loud. That was the thing nobody understood about Vesper, the thing she could never explain to the supply pilots who came through twice a year and asked, with the particular pity reserved for people who lived alone, *doesn't it drive you crazy, all that quiet?* The quiet wasn't the problem. The problem was the beacon — a sound below sound, a rhythm felt in the deck plating and the water lines and the bones of her inner ear, sixty-one seconds from crest to crest, like something enormous breathing in a room she couldn't leave.

She lay in her bunk and counted the pulses the way other people counted sheep. Sixty-one seconds. She had never measured it with an instrument. She didn't need to. Her body had become the calibration standard.

At 0417 she got up, because the beacon's morning diagnostic window opened at 0415 and closed at 0430, and in fifteen years of station service — six aboard Meridian Relay, five aboard the *Corvid's Lament*, and eleven here, alone, at the edge of the Kestrel Drift — Jude Whitlock had never once missed a diagnostic window. That was the whole of her job, if she was honest about it, and she was honest about it every morning in the mirror: keep the beacon burning, keep the logs clean, keep the coffee hot. Everything else was decoration.

She pulled on the same grey coveralls she'd pulled on yesterday, and the day before, and she paused at the mirror only long enough to confirm that the woman in it was still the woman she expected. Brown hair going iron at the temples. A scar through the left eyebrow she'd stopped asking about years ago — the station medical database had no record of its origin, and neither did she, and at some point you stop interrogating your own face. Dark eyes. Tired in the way that had stopped being an emotion and become a texture.

*You're fine,* she told the mirror. *Everything's fine.*

The mirror, as always, declined to confirm it.

* * *

The beacon room sat at the heart of Vesper like a heart, which was the joke she'd made to herself in her first week and had never made to another living soul. Four meters by four, wrapped in thermal shielding, dominated by the transmitter column — a three-meter spire of matte ceramic and copper that hummed at a frequency just below hearing, the same frequency that lived in her bones now, whether she liked it or not. She didn't like it. She had stopped liking it around year four. But you didn't have to like your heartbeat either, and the beacon was Vesper's heartbeat, and by extension it was hers.

She ran the diagnostic the way she always did: manual first, automated second, because the automated system had a blind spot in the phase-lock module that the manufacturer had never acknowledged and that Jude had found in her second year and had never reported, because reporting it would mean a maintenance visit, and maintenance visits meant people, and people meant questions, and questions were the one thing Vesper's isolation could not protect her from.

Phase-lock: nominal. Carrier wave: stable at 99.997 percent. Power draw: within tolerances. The beacon spoke its little litany of green lights to her, and she spoke her litany back — *good, good, good* — and for four minutes the ritual held the universe together, the way it did every morning.

Then she got to the transmission content line, and the ritual did what it always did, which was fail.

Every navigation beacon in the fleet net broadcast a standard identification packet: station designation, coordinates, service date, maintenance authority. Vesper's packet contained all of those things. It also contained a second data block, nested inside the first like a seed inside a seed, encrypted, unindexed, and — this was the part she had learned not to think about, the way you learn not to touch a hot stove, not through discipline but through scar tissue — unremovable.

She had tried to remove it. Year three. She'd spent four months of off-hours on it, quietly, carefully, and had gotten as far as isolating the block's boundaries before the transmitter had thrown a fault she'd never seen before or since — a hard lockout, every subsystem red at once, the whole station's power grid stuttering like a startled animal. The fault had cleared the moment she'd restored the block from backup. She had logged it as *transient power anomaly, resolved* and had never gone near the seed again.

That had been eight years ago. Some nights she still dreamed about the red lights.

*Content: standard,* she typed into the log. *No anomalies.*

The log accepted the lie the way it accepted all her lies: silently, permanently, without judgment. Somewhere in the fleet archives, a record now existed stating that Vesper's beacon broadcast exactly what beacons were supposed to broadcast. Somewhere in the beacon itself, the seed sat in its nested dark, doing whatever seeds did.

Jude closed the diagnostic, drank her coffee — reconstituted, over-bitter, perfect — and went to log the traffic.

* * *

There wasn't any traffic. There was almost never any traffic. Vesper sat at the junction of three shipping lanes that the war had emptied and the peace had never refilled, and in an average month the beacon's return-signal array registered perhaps forty contacts, thirty-eight of which were freighters running the long way around the Drift to avoid customs, one of which was the quarterly supply run, and one of which was usually a sensor ghost — a reflection off the debris fields, a cold rock tumbling through the scan envelope, the array's own noise folded back on itself.

Logging the ghosts was her favorite part of the day, though she'd have denied it under oath. There was something almost companionable about them. She'd named the recurring ones. The rock that tumbled through sector seven every nineteen days was *Old Faithful*. The interference pattern that bloomed off the Kestrel debris field in high solar wind was *the Curtain*. The ghost that appeared on the long-range array at random intervals, always at extreme range, always gone before it could be resolved, she had never named, because naming it would mean admitting she expected it, and expecting it was one step from worrying about it, and worrying about it was one step from the door at the end of C-deck, and she did not go to that door.

Today's contacts: a freighter, *Halcyon Route*, transponder clean, course boring. A debris cluster, unremarkable. A cold-signal contact at the edge of resolution that the array classified as *probable rock* and Jude classified as *probably a rock* and logged without further comment.

She was closing the traffic log — coffee number two, the station's rotation bringing the Drift's faint violet haze into the observation port, the morning assembling itself into its usual shape of nothing — when the array pinged.

Not a contact. A query. The beacon's handshake protocol, reaching out on its scheduled sweep to confirm the relay network was alive, and — for the first time in eleven years, in fourteen thousand scheduled sweeps — something answered.

Jude set down her cup very carefully, the way you do when your hands have opinions of their own.

The return was faint, degraded, half-swallowed by distance and drift. The array chewed on it for ninety seconds before it resolved enough to classify, and when the classification came up on her display, Jude read it three times, because the first two times she was sure she'd misread it.

*Contact class: Meridian-series deep relay tender. Hull configuration: decommissioned.*

She knew the class. Every navigator of her generation knew the class. The Meridian-series tenders had been the workhorses of the war's last years — fast, expendable, crewed by two, built to run messages through fire. The fleet had retired the entire line six years ago. Scrapped them, mostly. The ones that weren't scrapped were museum pieces, and museum pieces did not answer handshake queries from the edge of the Kestrel Drift.

She ran the contact's bearing. Extreme range, sector eleven — the deep field, past the debris, past anything with a reason to be there. The signal didn't repeat. Whatever had answered was gone, or had never been, or was waiting.

Jude sat with it for a long time. The beacon pulsed. Sixty-one seconds. Sixty-one seconds. Somewhere below her, through two decks of plating and insulation, she could feel it in her sternum.

Then she did the thing she was paid to do, the thing she was *for*: she typed *sensor ghost, uncorroborated, logged as debris* into the traffic record, closed the entry, and did not let herself look at sector eleven again.

But the rest of the day, in the galley, in the gym she used four times a week to keep her body from filing complaints, in the long violet afternoon, she caught herself listening. Not to the beacon. To the spaces between its pulses. Sixty-one seconds of silence, sixty-one seconds of silence, sixty-one seconds of silence — and somewhere out past the debris fields, in one of those silences, something old and decommissioned had said *here*.

* * *

Her route to the observation dome — her one indulgence, twenty minutes at end of shift, watching the Drift burn its slow violet — went past the C-deck junction. It had to; it was the only route. And the door was there, the way it was always there: a standard fleet bulkhead door, grey, unremarkable, except for the sound.

The cryo-hold hummed. It had hummed for eleven years, a low harmonic that lived in the door itself and in the deck around it, and the maintenance manuals said cryo-storage systems hummed, that's what they did, that was normal, that was the compressors and the coolant loop and nothing else, nothing else, nothing else.

Jude kept her eyes forward as she passed. She had gotten very good, over eleven years, at walking past a door without seeing it. It was a skill, like the beacon diagnostics, like the log entries. She could do it at any hour, in any lighting, even now — even with the hum seeming, today, a half-tone deeper than usual, even with the door's status panel glowing its steady amber *stasis nominal* that she did not check because checking it would mean stopping and stopping was not on the route.

Forty-one pods. She knew the number the way she knew the pulse interval — not from looking, but from the paperwork, from the commissioning manifest she'd read exactly once in her first week and had never been able to unread. *Cryo-storage, long-term, 41 units, contents: biological archive, authority: fleet directorate, access: restricted — J. Whitlock, custodian.*

Custodian. Not warden. Custodian. As if they were paintings. As if she were keeping them safe for someone.

She reached the observation dome without having looked at the door, and stood at the glass with her coffee going cold in her hands, and watched the Drift.

It was beautiful. That was the trap of it, the thing that had kept her here past every rotation window, past every chance to request transfer: the Kestrel Drift at this hour was a slow aurora of ionized dust, violet and deep green, and the dead warships of the debris fields caught its light and threw it back in fragments, and it was the most beautiful thing Jude had ever seen, and she had seen it every day for eleven years, and she had never once been able to look at it without thinking: *this is the graveyard, and I am the lighthouse keeper, and the lighthouse is not for the living.*

Because that was the thought, the one that lived under the diagnostics and the logs and the coffee and the gym, the one she'd buried so deep she could only find it at the edge of sleep or at the edge of the violet: beacons were for guiding people home. And Vesper's beacon sat at the mouth of a graveyard, and the only traffic it had ever guided was the traffic that never came, and the seed in its heart broadcast something she couldn't read to an audience she couldn't name, and forty-one people slept behind a humming door, and every one of them, according to a manifest she'd read once, had her face.

*Biological archive.* She'd told herself it was a coincidence. Genetic samples, maybe. A population preservation program. There had been such programs, during the worst years. It was a big war. Strange things had been done.

But the manifest had listed a custodian, and the custodian was her, and the fleet did not put a navigator alone on a station at the mouth of a graveyard to guard a coincidence.

She finished her coffee. She watched the light die off the debris fields. She did not think about the door.

* * *

At 2200 she ran the evening diagnostic, and it was nominal, and she logged it as nominal, and the beacon pulsed, and she ate, and she exercised, and at 2340 she stood in the corridor outside her own quarters and realized she was looking at the time display and doing arithmetic she hadn't authorized.

The sweep. The handshake protocol ran every fourteen thousand seconds — no. Every six hours. The sweep ran every six hours. The next sweep was in twenty minutes. And if something answered again — if the decommissioned ghost in sector eleven answered again — she would have to log it, and logging it twice made it a pattern, and patterns were the beginning of every kind of trouble.

She went into her quarters. She sealed the door. She lay down in the dark and counted pulses until they blurred.

Sixty-one seconds. Sixty-one. Sixty—

The array pinged.

She was out of the bunk before she'd decided to move, barefoot down the corridor, into the beacon room, her heart going fast and stupid, and the contact was there on the display, sector eleven, extreme range, resolving slowly out of the noise like a face surfacing in dark water.

Meridian-series tender. Decommissioned hull. And this time the signal held — eleven seconds, twelve, thirteen — long enough for the array to pull a fragment of transponder, a string of characters half-corroded by distance, and Jude read the fragment and felt the floor of her eleven carefully maintained years tilt beneath her, because the transponder registry prefix was one she knew, one she had no business knowing, one that belonged to a ship she had served aboard in a life she had logged, in her own personnel file, as *classified — prior assignment redacted*.

She didn't remember serving on it. That was the thing. She had the prefix in her head the way she had the scar in her eyebrow — present, permanent, and sourceless.

The signal faded. Sector eleven went back to noise. The display offered her the fragment, patient as a question:

*VSR-*

Jude stood in the humming dark for a long time. Then she opened the traffic log, and her hands — steady, she made them steady — typed: *sensor ghost, uncorroborated, logged as debris.*

She closed the entry. She did not delete the fragment from the raw capture buffer. She told herself that was an oversight.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would pull the transponder registry and look up what ship the prefix belonged to, and it would be nothing, a scrapyard hulk, a misfiled record, and the pattern would dissolve, and everything would be fine.

The beacon pulsed. Sixty-one seconds. Below her, two decks down, behind a door she did not look at, the cryo-hold hummed — and if it hummed a half-tone deeper than it had yesterday, well. The manuals said that was normal.

Everything, she logged, was nominal.
