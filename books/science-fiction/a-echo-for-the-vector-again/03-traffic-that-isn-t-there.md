## 3. Traffic That Isn't There

The ping came back at 0347 station time, which meant nothing out here, where the sun was a rumor and the clock was a habit Jude kept the way other people kept pets — fed daily, never quite trusted.

She was in the operations cradle with her boots hooked under the console rail and a bulb of cold tea sweating against her thigh, running the beacon's quarterly resonance sweep because the manual said quarterly and because the manual was the closest thing Vesper had to a second voice. The station hummed around her, that low tidal note she'd stopped hearing years ago the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, and the beacon burned overhead in its lattice of struts and coolant lines, faithful as a lighthouse nobody had ordered lit.

Jude Whitlock was good at her job. That was the whole architecture of her life, if she was honest — competence laid over silence like ballast over a hull breach. She could calibrate the beacon's phase array in her sleep. She could log a thousand hours of empty traffic without her eyes glazing, because empty traffic was the point, empty traffic was peace, and peace was the thing she'd been told she'd helped buy and was never, under any circumstances, to examine too closely.

The sweep ran. The numbers scrolled. And then the numbers did something numbers weren't supposed to do.

A return.

Not an echo — she knew echoes the way a lighthouse keeper knows gulls, the smeared, delayed, geometrically obedient ghosts of her own signal bouncing off rock and ice and the slow tumbling wreckage of a war that had ended six years ago. Echoes came back polite. Echoes came back *shaped*, attenuated along predictable curves, arriving late and apologetic.

This came back crisp. Compressed. Addressed.

Jude set the tea down with the deliberate care of a woman who did not want her hands to know they were shaking, and pulled the raw packet.

The beacon's protocol was simple by design: a navigation pulse, a handshake request, a request for transponder identification. In six years and forty-one days — not that she was counting, not that the count wasn't carved somewhere behind her sternum — she had received eleven thousand, two hundred and six responses. Freighters on the long haul to the Jovian yards. Colony barges. Patrol cutters from both fleets, flying the colors of a ceasefire both fleets still pretended was a peace. Every one of them had answered the handshake the way ships answer ships: with a hull code, a registry, a bored automated voice.

This one answered with a hull code she knew.

Not knew the way you know a face from a news feed. Knew the way you know a scar.

*CSV Meridian's Wake*, Meridian-class long-range survey vessel, keel laid at Tycho Yards, commission date — and here the packet's metadata unspooled in her mind's eye with the terrible clarity of things you memorized under fire — commission date 2141, decommissioned by order of the Combined Fleet Reorganization Authority, 2163. Stripped. Sold for scrap in lots. Her sister ships had gone to the torch one by one, their reactor signatures logged as they died, and Jude had watched the last of them — the *Wake* herself, or what the fleet said was the *Wake* — come apart in a salvage bay above Ceres while she stood at a viewport with a cup of champagne in her hand and a smile on her face that someone else had taught her muscles to make.

Decommissioned six years ago. Dead before the armistice was a year old.

And answering her handshake from a bearing that, when she triangulated it, put it somewhere in the Halcyon Drift — the debris fields, the graveyard, the place where the war had gone to be forgotten, which was, she was beginning to suspect, a full-time industry.

"Okay," she said, to the cradle, to the tea, to the hum. Her voice sounded strange in the room, too loud, a guest. "Okay. Ghost."

She ran the triangulation again. Same bearing. She ran it a third time with the beacon's redundant array, the one she maintained out of paranoia rather than regulation, and got the same answer within four thousandths of a degree. Whatever was out there wasn't drifting. Drifting things wandered. This was *holding station*, which was a thing only ships did, and ships were crewed, and crewed things out here meant —

Meant what? She made herself finish the sentence. Crewed things out here meant someone had found a Meridian-class hull, refitted it, and was sitting in the Halcyon Drift answering navigation handshakes from a decommissioned registry. Which was either a scavenger with a sense of humor and a very good transponder spoofer, or —

Or it was the *Meridian's Wake*, which was impossible, which was a word she reached for the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood, and the driftwood said: *you watched it die. You had champagne.*

Jude sat with that for a long moment. The station hummed. The tea went colder. Somewhere below her feet, down three decks and a sealed bulkhead and a corridor she did not walk if she could route around it, the cryo-hold hummed its own lower, patient note, and she noticed — as she always noticed and always pretended not to — that her awareness of it sharpened whenever her heart rate did, as if the hold were listening for exactly this.

"Debris," she said.

The word came out flat and administrative, and she was almost proud of how well it came out.

* * *

The logging protocol was unambiguous, because she had written the local addendum herself in her first year, back when she still read manuals like scripture: *unidentified returns from within debris fields shall be classified as inert debris reflections unless positive hull identification is confirmed by secondary sensor contact.* It was good protocol. It was the kind of protocol that kept a lone keeper from filing panic reports that made her look unstable, and instability, in her experience — in the thin, redacted, secondhand experience she had of her own past — was a word with teeth.

She opened the log entry. The cursor blinked in the classification field.

*Debris*, she typed. Then she sat there, and the cursor blinked, and the packet sat in her buffer with its crisp little handshake and its impossible registry, patient as a held breath.

The thing was, she told herself, the thing was that transponder spoofing was trivial. Any salvage rat with a cracked firmware kit could make a dead ship's ghost talk. The Drift was full of scavengers; she'd logged their pings before, sloppy ones, ones that had come back with registry codes belonging to hulls that had burned at Second Kalder, and she'd classified those as debris too, because they *were* debris, in the way that mattered: no hull, no threat, no history arriving uninvited at her door.

But those had been sloppy. This was clean. This was clean the way military hardware was clean, the way a signal was clean when it came off an array that had been maintained by someone who cared, and Meridian-class survey ships had carried the finest passive arrays in the fleet, and —

And she was doing it. She could feel herself doing it. Building the case for the impossible out of circumstantial crumbs, because some part of her — and this thought arrived like cold water down the back of her collar — some part of her *wanted* the impossible. Wanted the past to come knocking. Wanted the sealed door in her memory, the one shaped like a launch bay and full of screaming metal, to have a reason for existing that wasn't just her own broken mind.

She deleted the speculation before it could finish forming. She was good at that too. She'd had practice.

*Inert debris reflection, Halcyon Drift sector 9, bearing 114.7/22.3. No secondary contact. No action required.*

She filed it. The log accepted it with the small contented chime of a system that believed its keeper, and Jude Whitlock sat in the operations cradle of relay station Vesper and did not believe herself, and the distance between those two facts was the first crack she'd ever let herself see in the ice, and she looked at it the way you look at a crack in a dam — briefly, from a safe distance, with a professional estimate of how long you have.

* * *

She should have gone to sleep. Her shift had ended an hour ago by the station's fictional clock; the tea was cold enough to have opinions; her body had that grainy, over-lit feeling of a night spent watching numbers. Instead she pulled the registry archive.

This was allowed. That was the first thing she checked, the way you check that a door is unlocked before you admit you wanted to go through it. Fleet registry data was public — decommissioned hulls, salvage records, disposal certificates, all of it sitting in the open databases any station keeper could query, because transparency was one of the armistice's ceremonial words, one of the words they'd carved on the memorial at Tycho along with *sacrifice* and *renewal* and *never again*, words that had always sounded to Jude like the polished stones people put over graves.

*Meridian's Wake.* She typed it and the database thought about it for 1.3 seconds and gave her the file.

She already knew what it would say. That was the strange part — she read it like a woman rereading a letter, mouthing the lines. *Decommissioned 2163-03-14. Reactor core removed and certified destroyed, Tycho Salvage Bay 4. Hull sold in lots; superstructure scrapped 2163-06-02. Disposal officer of record: Cmdr. E. Vance, Comms Directorate.*

The name went through her like a phase array going through a hull.

Elias Vance. She knew the name the way you know a name from a dream — instantly, totally, and with no idea where the knowing came from. It arrived attached to things: a laugh that was mostly exhale, hands that moved when he talked, the smell of solder and the specific grey of shipboard coffee, a voice on a comms channel saying *say it back to me, Jude, say the vector back to me* — and then, like a door swinging on oiled hinges, the images stopped. Not faded. *Stopped*, at a clean edge, the way a recording stops when someone's thumb finds the switch.

She was standing. She didn't remember standing. The cradle was behind her and she was at the viewport, one hand flat against the cold of the glass, looking out at the Halcyon Drift, which from here was just a scatter of faint stars that weren't stars — a field of wreckage catching the distant sun and throwing it back in glints, beautiful the way battlefields are beautiful from a hundred years away, and somewhere in all that glitter was a bearing, 114.7/22.3, where a dead ship was politely answering questions.

"Disposal officer of record," she said softly. Her breath fogged the viewport and shrank. "Elias Vance."

She tried to reach for him and the door was there, the clean-edged door, and behind it the launch bay and the screaming metal, and between her and the door stood a flat white absence shaped exactly like a man, and she understood — not with her mind, which was busy generating alternative explanations, but with some older, dumber, more honest part of her, the part that flinches before the conscious mind knows there's anything to flinch from — she understood that the absence was not an accident. Absences that clean were manufactured. She'd worked with redacted files during the war; she knew what a redaction looked like from the outside, the neat surgical blank, the information not hidden but *removed*, with care, by professionals.

She had never once thought to wonder what it looked like from the inside.

The beacon chimed. A soft, routine tone — the hourly health check, the heartbeat of the thing she kept, the lighthouse confirming to itself that it was still lit. She watched the diagnostic scroll green across the display, every line nominal, and thought: *you built your whole life on this thing being what the manual says it is.* A navigation aid. A public service. A light for strangers.

And she thought: *the manual was written by the same fleet that put Elias Vance's name on a disposal certificate for a ship that is, at this moment, holding station in the Drift and answering my handshake.*

She went back to the cradle. She did not reclassify the log entry. That was the decision she made that night, though she wouldn't understand until much later that it *was* a decision, a real one, with weight and consequence: she left *debris* in the field, she left the lie in the record, because the record was read by people, and she was not yet ready to be a woman who saw ghosts. But she copied the packet. Full raw capture, every header, every timestamp, the bearing, the handshake signature, all of it — into a personal partition, encrypted, buried under her maintenance archives where no auditor would ever think to look, in a directory named for a coolant calibration routine so boring it was practically invisible.

Her hands were steady the whole time. She noted that. She noted it the way you note a stranger's behavior — *look at her, she's calm, where did she learn that* — and the question frightened her more than the ghost ship did, because the honest answer was *somewhere I can't remember*, and there was a great deal of that going around.

* * *

She slept, finally, in the small hours, in her bunk in the keeper's quarter with the lights at their lowest amber and the station humming its one note. She dreamed, and the dream was the old one, the one that came maybe once a month and always left her with damp hair and a taste like battery metal: the launch bay, the clamps, the screaming of metal that was not metal screaming but ships, or a ship, or one ship, one hull coming apart in a way hulls don't come apart unless something is very wrong, and a voice on the channel — his voice, she knew it was his voice even though the dream never let her hear words, only the shape of them — and herself saying *say it back to me, say the vector back to me*, except in the dream she was saying it and no one was answering, and the silence where the answer should be was the loudest thing in the dream, louder than the metal, louder than the —

She woke at 0611 with her hand already moving on the console she kept beside the bunk, pulling up the log, the way you check on a child in the night.

The beacon was fine. Green across the board. And there, in the traffic queue, timestamped 0558, forty-one minutes ago, while she slept: a second return. Same bearing. Same registry. Same crisp, clean, maintained-someone's-array handshake.

But this one had an attachment. A short one. A text string, uncompressed, sitting in the packet's optional comment field where no automated system would ever look, where only a keeper running a manual review would find it — and she had never once in six years run a manual review of the traffic queue, and whoever had sent this could not have known that she would, which meant they hadn't sent it *for* her, or they had sent it knowing that eventually, someday, a keeper with her particular insomnia would go looking.

She opened it. Four words. No signature, no authentication block, nothing but the string itself, rendered in the plain monospace of a system that didn't care how it looked:

**THE VECTOR WAS TRUE.**

Jude sat in the amber dark with the words glowing on her screen and her heart going like a drive core coming out of standby, and she thought: *I never logged the first return as anything but debris. I never answered it. Nobody knows I saw it.*

Except whoever was out there in the Drift, sitting in a ship that had been scrap for six years, had just told her they knew she'd seen it. Knew, or *hoped*, or — she made herself consider it — *knew her*, knew her well enough to know that Jude Whitlock did not file panic reports and did not answer ghost handshakes and did not sleep well, and that sooner or later she would come down to the traffic queue in the small hours and look.

She should classify it. She should wipe the packet, purge the buffer, run a diagnostic, write it all up as a transponder anomaly and never think of it again. The manual had a procedure for exactly this. She had written the procedure.

Her finger hovered over the purge command.

Then she copied the second packet into the buried directory, next to the first, and closed the partition, and sat for a while in the amber dark listening to the beacon burn and the station hum and, three decks down, behind a sealed bulkhead she did not open, the cryo-hold humming its low patient note — and for the first time in six years, Jude Whitlock let herself wonder, deliberately, in full sentences, what was behind that door, and whether the forty-one sleepers she counted but never examined were really asleep, and who had signed the order that made her a keeper of a light for ships that no longer existed.

The next sweep was due at 0700. She ran it early. And at 0653, with the resonance wave still propagating out into the dark toward the Halcyon Drift, the traffic queue chimed a third time — and this time the return wasn't a handshake at all.
