## 7. The Hold Opens

The lights died at 0347 station time, and Jude Whitlock woke with her heart already sprinting.

She had learned the Vesper's rhythms the way a sailor learns a lover's breathing — the tick of the thermal regulators cycling down in the deep-night hours, the faint harmonic the beacon threw into the deck plating when it talked to itself, the particular creak of the habitat ring settling as its spin bearing warmed against the cold of vacuum on the far side. She knew all of it the way you know your own pulse: not by listening, but by noticing when it changed.

What changed now was everything. The hum stopped. The dark came down like a lid.

Jude sat up in her bunk and did not move for three full seconds — an eternity, long enough for the emergency strips to wake along the floor edges and paint the corridor outside her cabin in a thin amber line. Backup power. Good. That meant the reactor hadn't scrammed; that meant something had tripped a breaker somewhere, or a bus had faulted, or —

Or something was drawing power that shouldn't be.

She dressed in the dark by touch, which took longer than she liked and gave her too much time to think. The Vesper drew its power from a fusion plant rated for a crew of sixty and currently feeding one woman, one beacon, and forty-one cryogenic pods held at a maintenance draw so low it barely registered on her daily logs. She checked those logs every morning. It was a ritual, like calibrating the beacon, like logging empty traffic: she looked at the cryo-hold's consumption curve and confirmed that it was flat. Flat meant sleeping. Flat meant nothing down there had so much as dreamed.

The last reading she'd seen, twelve hours ago, had been flat.

She clipped the hand torch to her belt, hesitated over the toolkit, and took it anyway — not because she expected to fix anything, but because holding a wrench made her feel like an engineer instead of a woman walking toward the thing she'd spent six years not walking toward.

The corridor breathed cold air at her. The habitat ring's climate system ran on the same bus as everything else, and without main power the temperature was already sliding, her breath ghosting faintly in the amber light. She moved aft along the spine, past the galley where the coffee unit's standby light was dead, past the comms alcove where the beacon's local monitor showed a single line of text scrolling in patient amber:

**BUS C FAULT — LOAD REDISTRIBUTION IN PROGRESS**

Load redistribution. Something on Bus C was pulling more current than the breakers liked, and the station's brain was shunting power around to compensate, and the shunting was what had killed her lights. Bus C fed the aft sections. The cargo bays. The water reclamation plant.

And the cryo-hold.

Jude stood at the junction where the spine met the aft accessway and told herself a story. It was a good story, well-worn, with soft edges: a coolant pump had developed a slow leak of current through degrading insulation; six-year-old hardware did that; she'd run a diagnostic next shift and replace the pump and log the anomaly and everything would be flat again by morning. It was a story she could have sold to anyone, including herself, except that her hands were shaking as she pulled up the load schematic on the junction panel, and the schematic told a different story entirely.

Bus C wasn't leaking. Bus C was *feeding*. One circuit, deep in the hold's distribution tree, had gone from a trickle to a river in the last hour — a draw curve climbing in a smooth exponential that Jude recognized from her training and had hoped never to see outside a classroom.

It was the warm-up curve. The standard, documented, manufacturer-certified power profile of a cryogenic pod bringing its occupant back.

"No," she said aloud, and the word came out small in the big cold dark.

There were protocols for this. Somewhere in the station's operations manual — the real one, the fleet-issued one she kept in a drawer beneath a stack of personal effects she never sorted — there was a section on unscheduled revival events, with checklists and decision trees and probably a reassuring diagram of a stick figure pressing a red button. She tried to remember any of it and found her memory had gone smooth as ice, offering nothing. What it offered instead, unbidden, was the smell of the launch bay on a day she did not let herself think about — hot metal and burnt insulation and something sweeter underneath that she had never been able to name and had never once tried to.

She put the memory down the way you put down something with wet hands, fast, before it could get a grip.

*One pod,* she told herself. *Maybe it's a sensor error. Maybe it's a stuck relay simulating the profile. Pods don't just wake up. There are interlocks. There are —*

There were interlocks, yes. And she was the only person aboard with the authority to release them, and she had never released them, and the authorization codes were hers alone, biometrically bound, unforgeable.

Which meant either the system was lying to her, or it had found a way around her.

Neither possibility made her feel better.

* * *

She walked the aft accessway because the alternative was standing at the junction forever, and because some stubborn engineer's reflex in her chest insisted that you go look at the problem, that you put your hands on it, that fear unfaced metastasizes into the kind of dread that eats a person alive in a tin can eight light-minutes from anywhere. Her boots rang on the grating. The amber strips ran out partway down and the torch took over, its beam swinging ahead of her across bulkheads scabbed with old inspection stickers, each one stamped with dates marching backward through her tenure on the Vesper like rings in a felled tree.

The hold's outer door appeared at the end of the passage, and Jude stopped.

In daylight hours — the ones she invented for herself, since a station in orbit has no use for day or night — the door was a thing she passed at a brisk walk, eyes forward, breath even, a woman with somewhere else to be. It was a slab of composite armor two meters tall, painted fleet gray, stenciled CRYOGENIC STORAGE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL, and beneath the stencil, smaller, almost apologetic: OCCUPANCY 41. She had read that number so many times it had worn smooth, lost its meaning, become furniture. Forty-one. A number like a room dimension. A number like a pressure rating.

Tonight, under the torch beam, the stencil looked freshly painted. Tonight the number had weight, mass, gravity. Forty-one people. Forty-one bodies with her face, her hands, the scar on her left knuckle from a childhood she only half remembered having. Forty-one women asleep in the dark below her feet for six years, dreaming whatever frozen people dream, while she walked past them every day and called herself alone.

The door's status panel glowed green. Sealed, pressurized, nominal. But set into the panel's corner, a second indicator she had never once seen lit was burning a steady, patient blue.

**REVIVAL IN PROGRESS — POD 17**

Pod 17. She didn't know why the number landed in her stomach like a dropped wrench. They were all just numbers. She'd counted them through the little observation ports years ago, early in her posting, back when denial was still a project under construction rather than a finished house she lived in — she'd stood in the hold exactly once, on her first week, and walked the rows, and counted, and felt the pods watching her with their frosted glass eyes, and left, and never come back. Pod 17 had been third row, port side. She didn't remember anything else about it. She had made very sure not to remember anything else about it.

The manual said the outer door couldn't be opened during a revival cycle. Thermal containment. She should turn around. She should go to the control room, pull the master override, kill the pod's power feed at the distribution level, and let whatever was happening down there stay safely, legally, procedurally unfinished.

Her hand was already on the door release.

She watched it happen the way you watch someone else's hand — the fingers finding the recessed grip, the thumb brushing the biometric plate, the mechanism taking her identity from her blood and deciding, on her behalf, that she was authorized. The locks disengaged with a sound like a rifle bolt, four heavy clunks in sequence, and the door swung inward on counterweighted hinges, exhaling a breath of air so cold it burned.

Fog rolled out over the threshold and broke around her boots.

"Damn it," Jude whispered, and stepped through.

* * *

Inside, the hold was a cathedral built for a congregation of sleepers.

Racks rose in two long rows toward the ceiling, each rack stacked three pods high, their surfaces furred with frost, their status panels dark except for the running lights — tiny green stars scattered through the gloom, hundreds of them, steady as a night sky. In main power failure the hold ran on its own isolated feed, designed to survive anything short of the station's total destruction, and so the cold here was absolute, ancient, the cold of a place that had been holding its breath for six years. Her torch beam cut a white tunnel through the fog. Her breath came out in plumes and hung there.

She went left, toward the port-side racks, because the alternative was standing in the doorway, and because the same stubborn reflex that had carried her this far apparently intended to carry her all the way to catastrophe. Third row. Port side. She counted racks as she passed them — one, two — and told herself the whole way that the panel in the corridor was wrong, that pod 17 would be dark and frosted and dead as all the others, that the blue light was a fault, a ghost, a trick of failing electronics in an aging station, and that tomorrow she would file a maintenance request and drink her coffee and walk past this door at a brisk pace with her eyes forward, and the world would be flat again, flat and safe and dreamless.

Pod 17's status panel was not dark.

It burned blue-white, bright enough to throw her shadow long across the deck plates, and across its face scrolled data in calm, unhurried columns: core temperature climbing, neural activity present, metabolic restart at eleven percent and rising. Eleven percent. The number meant nothing and everything. Somewhere behind that frosted glass, in a body identical to hers down to the scar on the knuckle, a heart had remembered how to beat.

Jude raised her torch and wiped the frost from the observation port with the heel of her hand, and immediately wished she hadn't, because the port was small and the face behind it was close, closer than she'd expected, tilted slightly toward the glass as if the woman inside had turned toward the warmth of Jude's palm in her sleep.

Her own face looked back at her.

Not a resemblance. Not a family similarity, not the jolt of seeing a sister or a twin. It was her face, exact, down to the small asymmetry of the eyebrows that no mirror had ever flattered, down to the faint crease between them that Elias used to smooth away with his thumb when she worried — and the thought of his name arrived carrying its own cold, and she shoved it down, down, into the sealed place where she kept him.

The eyes were closed. The mouth was relaxed. She was, Jude thought with a hysteria she kept carefully off her face, beautiful. Of course she was. Jude had always thought she might be, in the right light, if anyone ever looked.

Then the eyelids moved.

It was nothing. It had to be nothing — a muscular flutter, a dream reflex, the random firing of a thawing brain. The manuals said full emergence took hours, that there were stages, that consciousness returned late and gently. The eyelids moved again, and this time they did not stop halfway, and Jude watched, nailed to the deck, as her own eyes opened and looked at her.

They were unfocused. Drowned-looking. The pupils wandered, found the torchlight, contracted. The brow creased — that familiar crease, the worry-line, smoothed by a dead man's thumb — and the lips parted, and though there was no sound through the glass and the pod's intercom was dark, Jude heard the shape of the word anyway, felt it form in her own mouth like a shared yawn:

*—cold—*

Jude ran.

She was not proud of it. Later, in the parts of the night she would refuse to revisit, she would try to reconstruct it as a tactical withdrawal, a rational retreat to the control room to execute the proper shutdown procedure, and some honest corner of her mind would always answer: *you ran like an animal, you ran before you decided to, your body voted without you.* She went up the aisle between the racks with her shoulder clipping a rack frame hard enough to leave a bruise she'd find tomorrow, and the frost-furred pods slid past her on both sides, dozens of dark windows, and in the fog and the swinging torchlight every one of them seemed to be turning, seemed to be watching, seemed to have opened its eyes the moment she looked away.

Behind her, pod 17's status panel pulsed. Metabolic restart: fourteen percent. Sixteen. Climbing faster than the curve said it should, faster than the manufacturer's gentle stages allowed, as if whatever was waking in there was in a hurry, as if six years of sleep had ended not in emergence but in *escape*.

She hit the outer door controls with the flat of her hand and the door was slower than it had ever been, an geological age, a decade, and then it was open and she was through and it was sealing behind her with its four rifle-bolt clunks, and she stood in the amber-lit accessway dragging air that was too cold and too thin, listening.

Through the door, muffled by composite armor, she could hear it: a sound she had no right to hear, because the hold was supposed to be silent, because the pods were supposed to be quiet, because nothing in there was supposed to be awake.

A thump. Then another. Rhythmic. Patient.

Someone — some *one*, some version of herself with her hands and her memories and God knew what else besides — was kicking the inside of pod 17.

Jude backed away down the corridor, one step, two, her torch beam shaking, and the station chose that moment to bring the main power back online. Lights bloomed overhead, white and merciless. The hum returned, the familiar harmonics, the Vester's heartbeat resuming as if nothing had happened, as if the universe were a place where things simply went back to normal.

At the junction panel, the load schematic refreshed itself. Bus C: nominal. Pod 17's draw curve flattened, stabilized, settled into a new plateau — the steady, modest consumption of a system maintaining a living occupant instead of a frozen one.

The station had accepted the change. Had logged it. Had filed it away as the new normal, the way stations do, the way Jude had taught herself to do, the way she had done for six years with everything she couldn't bear.

She stood in the white light with her back against the bulkhead and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she could not shut the pod down from the control room. Not anymore. The interlocks required biometric release, and the biometrics were hers — and the woman in pod 17 had the same blood, the same prints, the same face. Whatever woke fully in that hold would be able to open every door on the Vesper that Jude could open. Would be able to sit in her chair. Read her logs. Find the drawer with the fleet manual and the letters she'd never sent and the photograph she kept facedown.

Would be able to tell her what she'd forgotten.

Down the corridor, behind the sealed door, the thumping continued. Steady. Patient. Unhurried as a heartbeat.

Jude pushed off the bulkhead and walked toward the control room, fast, eyes forward, and did not look back — and it was not until she reached the junction and the comm panel chimed, once, softly, that she understood the night was not finished with her.

An internal transmission. Station-local. Origin: cryo-hold, pod 17, auxiliary intercom — a channel that had been dark for six years.

Text only. No voice. Four words on the screen, in a handwriting-recognition font rendered from input typed by fingers that had never touched a keyboard until tonight, and yet somehow, impossibly, familiar:

**JUDE. OPEN THE DOOR.**
