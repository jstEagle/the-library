## 12. Sabotage at Svalbard

Juno Okonkwo had flown through the debris shoals of Ceres after the Collision Cascade, threaded a dying freighter through Jupiter's radiation belts with her hands blistered from the console, and once spent eleven days in a spin-grav coffin listening to her own heart argue with itself. She was not easily rattled.

But three weeks planetfall on Earth had done something to her that vacuum never managed. It was the sky. The sky went on forever and there was nothing above it but more nothing, no hull between her and all of it, and every night she lay in the bunk they'd given her in the vault's hab ring and felt like she was falling upward into an ocean with no surface.

So she volunteered for the coolant runs. Down in the Lattice galleries, under two hundred meters of permafrost and seed vault and dead civilization, there were no skies. There were pipes and gauges and the low thrum of the engine dreaming beneath everything, and Juno could pretend she was back in a ship again—except this ship was the size of a mountain and it wanted something from humanity, and everyone aboard was arguing about the price.

She came down the maintenance ladder at 0430 local, because Wendell slept at 0400 and Ilsa Reyes never slept before 0600, and those ninety minutes were the only time the galleries belonged to her alone.

That was when she saw the frost.

* * *

The coolant loop for Gallery Three ran along the eastern wall, a fat insulated trunk line that carried helium-hydrogen mix down from the surface compressors and wrapped the Lattice's primary manifold in a cold embrace. The engine ran hot when it dreamed. Everyone had learned that in the first week—the deep-interface sessions made the walls sweat, and the coolant system groaned like a living thing being asked to carry too much.

The frost on the trunk line was wrong because it was *inside* the insulation jacket.

Juno crouched, pulled her glove off, and pressed her bare palm flat against the composite cladding. Cold. Not pipe-cold. Vacuum-cold, the kind of cold that meant pressure loss, that meant somewhere ahead of her the line had opened its mouth to the rock and was exhaling its charge into the dark.

Her scanner confirmed it before her stomach finished dropping: a breach, forty meters up-gallery, and behind it—a second one. And the tell that turned dread into certainty: the first breach wasn't a failure. Failures split along seams. This hole was bored, clean-edged, deliberate, drilled straight through the jacket and into the line where a pressure sensor would mask the drop until the flow rate crossed a threshold too late.

Someone had studied the schematics. Someone had wanted this to look like decay.

"Voss," she said into her wrist comm, keeping her voice level the way you did when you didn't want to wake the thing sleeping next to you. "Wake up. Gallery Three. Bring Reyes."

Static. Then Wendell's voice, rough with sleep and instantly sharp: "What is it?"

"Somebody cut the coolant." She was already moving, unspooling patch kit from the wall locker, doing the math she didn't want to do. "Two breaches. Engine core temp's going to climb before we even get the alarm. How fast can you get down here?"

"I'm coming now."

"And, Wendell—" She hesitated, then said it anyway, because lying to them had never once worked. "It wasn't an accident."

* * *

They came down together, which was how Juno knew how scared they were. Wendell moved through the galleries like they'd been born in them—and maybe they had, in the way that mattered—but they always came alone or with Ilsa trailing protocol. Tonight they came down the ladder side by side, Wendell in thermal layers thrown on over sleep clothes, Ilsa fully dressed, fully composed, and fully white around the mouth.

Ilsa looked at the bored hole for four seconds and said one word: "Kessler."

Not a name. A faction. Juno had heard it whispered on the Belt channels since before planetfall—the hardline caucus inside the Martian delegation who believed the Lattice should be fired immediately, on Mars's triage list, with or without consensus, and who considered the Belt crew and Wendell's fragment theory to be sentimental obstacles between humanity and survival. Kessler's people had arrived with the second delegation wave. Six of them. Juno had counted their boots in the hab ring every morning out of pure Belt-born paranoia, and she'd told herself the counting was a habit, not a warning.

"You're certain," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question, but Juno answered it anyway.

"Look at the cut. That's a plasma microtorch, military spec. Your people brought two of those in the survey kits." She watched Ilsa absorb it—the way duty landed on the woman like ballast, straightening her spine even as it dragged her down. "Where are your six tonight?"

"Four are accounted for in the hab ring. Two requested surface leave eight hours ago. Weather station inspection." Ilsa closed her eyes briefly. "There is no weather station inspection scheduled."

"So they're topside, watching their work cook." Juno turned back to the line. The scanner readout was ugly and getting uglier. Coolant mass down thirty-one percent and falling. Core temperature up six degrees and climbing on a curve that would hit manifold tolerance in—she did it twice to be sure—eleven hours. Maybe nine if the engine kept dreaming hard.

"What happens at tolerance?" Wendell asked quietly.

Nobody answered for a moment. Then Ilsa said, "The original engineers built failsafes. Thermal shutdown, full quench, engine dormancy." Her voice did the thing it did when she was delivering a verdict she hated. "The failsafes degraded with everything else. We've known that since the first survey. If the manifold overheats, it doesn't shut down cleanly. It cracks."

"And if it cracks," Wendell said, "the dream ends."

"The dream ends and the handshake window closes with it. Whatever alignment the flicker opened—" Ilsa gestured vaguely upward, toward the sky, toward Neptune, toward eight million sleepers drifting in decaying arcs, "—we lose it. Possibly permanently. Possibly explosively. The engineers' notes on cascade behavior were not reassuring."

Juno zipped the patch kit open and looked at what she had: composite wrap, self-curing sealant rated for a tenth of the pressure differential she needed to hold, and two emergency clamp collars designed for hairline fractures, not bored holes the width of her fist.

"This kit holds a seam," she said. "It doesn't hold a wound. I need to get inside the jacket, pack the breach from the line side, and clamp it while the sealant cures. Which means hands inside the plume while it vents."

"No," said Wendell.

"It means roughly forty seconds of direct exposure to flashing cryogen and whatever particulate the line's carrying," Juno continued over them, because if she stopped she'd hear her own fear. "Suit'll take most of it. Dosimeter will hate me. I've eaten worse out past Ganymede."

"That's not—" Wendell stepped closer, and in the gallery light Juno saw the thing she'd seen on the relay feeds for twenty years but never in person until three weeks ago: the exact moment Wendell Zhou got frightened enough to be honest. "Juno. The line carries activated coolant. It's been cycling through the manifold for weeks. You know what that means."

"I know what it means."

"Then don't stand there telling me about your dosimeter like it's a joke."

"It's not a joke." Juno met their eyes. "It's arithmetic. Eleven hours to manifold failure, and the nearest replacement collar is in the Martian survey cache, and I am not asking Ambassador Reyes to send her people down here to fix a hole her people probably drilled. This one's mine."

* * *

Ilsa insisted on the second suit. Not a volunteer—one of her own techs, a silent young woman named Adeyemi whom Ilsa ordered into the gear with three sentences and no room for refusal, and who took up position at the shutoff valve with the expression of someone who understood exactly why she hadn't been asked.

Wendell stood at the gallery mouth and did not leave, and did not speak, and Juno loved them so much in that moment that it made the suit feel heavier.

"Breach one first," she said, running comms checks. "Smaller. If my technique works there, breach two goes faster. Adeyemi, kill flow on my mark, hold it for sixty seconds, then restore partial pressure so I can verify the seal. Everybody else stay behind the bulkhead line."

"Juno." Wendell's voice in her ear. "Talk to me while you work. Tell me about Ceres. Tell me anything."

"Voss, I'm working."

"Please."

So she talked. She told them about the debris run, the freighter called *Mama Legba*, the way old Captain Ferro used to say that the Belt didn't raise children, it raised survivors who occasionally got to be children afterward, if they were lucky and stubborn. She told them about the first time she'd heard Wendell's voice on the relay—a kid's voice, thin through the static, reading supply manifests aloud because silence was worse than boredom—and how she'd decided, at nineteen, alone in a cockpit above the asteroid dark, that this kid on the dead planet was hers now, and nobody had ever consulted her about it or needed to.

"Mark," she said, and killed the sentiment, and dropped her hands into the cold.

* * *

Cold was the wrong word for what came out of that line. It was a presence. It screamed through the breach in a white plume that ate her headlamp beam, and the suit's outer layer stiffened instantly, frost blooming across her visor in feathers, and her gloved fingers went from pain to nothing in eight seconds. She packed the cavity by feel. Sealant, wrap, sealant, clamp—her hands remembering the work while her mind recited Ferro's litany, *breathe slow, breathe slow, the Belt provides, breathe slow.*

Adeyemi restored partial pressure. The clamp held. The sealant cured around it with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Forty-one seconds exposure," the suit reported. "Cumulative dose 1.2 sieverts equivalent."

"That's fine," Juno said, and moved up-gallery to the second breach, and did not think about the number, because thinking about the number was a luxury for later, and later was a country she might not get to visit.

The second breach was bigger. Of course it was. They'd saved the killing stroke for last—this one bored into the junction where the trunk line met the manifold feed, a place where a patch would have to hold against the full pulse of the engine's circulation. She packed it twice. The first sealant bead cracked audibly as pressure cycled. On the second attempt she braced her whole body against the line, cheekplate against frozen composite, and held the clamp closed with her arms shaking while the cure timer crawled through three hundred seconds that lasted approximately a geological age.

Somewhere in the middle of it, Wendell stopped talking. Juno registered the silence through the fog of concentration and thought, distantly: *they're praying, or they've gone to do something brave and stupid, and I'll find out which when I'm done.*

The timer chimed. Adeyemi brought the line to full pressure. The gauge needle trembled, steadied, held.

"Coolant mass stabilizing," Ilsa reported. "Core temp cresting and turning over. You did it, Okonkwo."

"Yeah." Juno peeled her hands off the clamp, and discovered that her fingers didn't entirely want to obey, and that the frost on her visor had gone yellowish at the edges where the plume had bitten through the outer coating. "Yeah, I did it. Somebody get me a coffee and a very honest conversation with a medical unit."

* * *

The medical unit was not honest; it was worse. It was precise.

Three point eight sieverts cumulative, once the suit telemetry was reconciled with the gallery dosimeters. Acute radiation syndrome, moderate-severe. The hab ring's autodoc could manage the symptoms. Nothing in the solar system could undo the number. Her marrow had taken a punch it would spend months recovering from, if it recovered, and the odds it quoted her were the kind of odds Belters took bets on, which was the only reason she laughed.

Wendell sat beside the medbay cot and did not laugh.

"Don't," Juno said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't do the face. The face where you're calculating how this goes on some ledger. I can see the abacus moving behind your eyes, Voss."

Wendell's jaw tightened. For a long moment they said nothing, and then, quietly, in the voice they used on the relay when the truth was too big for volume: "You keep saying it's arithmetic. Doses, hours, percentages. But you're my family, Juno. You were my family before I ever saw your face. And today I learned that loving me has a body count."

"No." Juno reached out and caught their wrist—her hand shook, damn it, the dose already collecting its tax—and held on. "Listen to me. Nobody died for you. I got burned fixing a hole that cowards cut because they're afraid of a future where nobody gets to play god with a list. That's not your ledger. That's theirs." She let go, slowly. "But you want to know the real thing? The real thing is I'd do it again tomorrow. Not because of the engine. Because you're down here. That's the whole equation. Stop trying to solve it."

Wendell bowed their head. Their shoulders did something small and terrible, and Juno pretended not to see, because some griefs deserved privacy even in a medbay with no doors.

Ilsa appeared in the doorway with a tablet and the posture of a woman carrying bad news she'd already rehearsed. "The two who took surface leave," she said. "We found them at the old weather station. One had a plasma torch in his kit and a burn pattern on his gloves that matches the cuts. He's in custody. The other one—" she paused, and Juno watched the institution crack another millimeter across the ambassador's face, "—the other one is claiming he acted alone, on principle, and that the faction had nothing to do with it. Kessler is demanding his release and calling the whole incident a Belt fabrication."

"Of course she is," Juno said.

"But that's not why I'm here." Ilsa set the tablet on the cot rail. On it, a telemetry graph climbed in a smooth, terrible curve. "While we were all underground, the deep-space array flagged something. The Neptune arcs. Their orbital decay just—" she exhaled. "It accelerated. Not gradually. Stepwise. As if something out there changed."

Juno looked at the graph, then at Wendell, whose face had gone still in a way she recognized from twenty years of relay calls: the look of someone receiving news from the dark and understanding it before anyone explained.

"How long," Wendell said, "do we have?"

Ilsa's answer was a number, and the number was years smaller than anyone had planned for, and outside the medbay, far above the permafrost and the galleries and the dreaming engine, the sky over Svalbard went on being empty and enormous, and somewhere past all of it, eight million sleepers began, imperceptibly, to fall.
