## 17. The Vote That Wasn't

Juno Okonkwo had voted exactly four times in her life, and three of them had been jokes.

The first was a Belt-wide poll on whether to rename Ceres Station after a dead rockhound everyone loved and nobody could agree on a name for. The second was a ship's vote on whether the *Marrow of Luck* should carry a cat or a hydroponics bay. They'd gotten both, in the end—the bay in the hold, the cat in the cockpit, named Bay by unanimous acclamation. The third was a bet with Wendell, relayed across eleven light-minutes, on whether Ilsa Reyes would smile before or after she threatened them. Juno had won. Reyes never smiled.

The fourth was now.

She stood in the vault's antechamber with a tablet in her hand and the weight of eight million sleepers pressing down through it, watching the tally numbers crawl like something alive. The referendum had been live for six days. Six days of Ilsa burning her career to force the question into open air, six days of Juno's crew relaying arguments up and down the Belt like oxygen canisters, six days of Wendell sitting in the Lattice chamber below them all, holding the door open with their patience.

The question was simple. The answers were not.

**PROPOSAL A — MARTIAN TRIAGE PROTOCOL:** Wake 3.9 million. Sacrifice 4.1 million. Magnetosphere restored within fourteen months.

**PROPOSAL B — BELT REFUSAL:** No selection. Hold current state. Seek alternatives. (Note: no viable alternative identified.)

**PROPOSAL C — FRAGMENT DISTRIBUTION:** Wake all. Wake no one whole. Requires a living integrator-mind. (See Appendix: What This Means.)

Turnout was ninety-one percent of every living human being who wasn't asleep. It was, as far as anyone could determine, the largest democratic act in the history of the species. More people were voting than had ever been awake at one time before the Collapse.

And it was going to fail.

Juno could see it in the numbers, the way you could see a docking failure two hundred meters out—not because anything had gone wrong yet, but because the vectors didn't close. Proposal A held thirty-four percent, concentrated on Mars and the arc-adjacent stations where fear had a face. Proposal B held twenty-two percent, mostly the Belt, principled and useless. Proposal C held forty-one percent.

Forty-one. Not fifty. Not a majority. A plurality of humanity looking at the only option that didn't require a list of names, and saying *yes, but not enough of us.*

"They need another day," said Okafor behind her, working the relay console with his big careful hands. "The outer clusters are still counting. Triton lag alone is—"

"It's not lag." Juno didn't turn around. "It's fear. Fear counts slow."

"Then we wait for it."

"We don't have it." She pulled up the telemetry feed from the Neptune arcs—the feed that had been quietly getting worse for nine days while everyone pretended the referendum deserved clean air. Cascade warnings, amber then red, spreading through the cryo-systems like frost through a cracked viewport. The flicker events had destabilized more than Ilsa had first reported. The pods weren't failing at random. They were failing in sequence, a propagation front moving through the arcs' power architecture, and the models disagreed on whether they had eighteen months or six.

Six days ago, when the vote opened, the median estimate had been fourteen months.

Democracy needed time. Time was the one thing the universe had stopped selling.

* * *

The call came at 0400 vault-time, which meant nothing on Earth but everything to Juno, whose body still kept Belt hours from a childhood spent on a rock that spun.

It was Priya Chandra, the systems tech aboard *Long Answer*, the survey skiff parked at Neptune trailing distance. Priya had been Juno's apprentice once, back when the *Marrow* ran cargo instead of hope, and she had the particular flat calm of a Belt-born engineer delivering bad news she'd already triple-checked.

"Cascade's gone nonlinear," Priya said. "Arc Seven lost containment integrity on two hundred pods in the last hour. Not power failure—thermal. The flicker scrambled their phase-lock and they're drifting out of sync with each other, and when they drift, they heat. Arc Four is showing the same signature, delayed. Arc Two next, probably."

"How many total?"

"If the propagation holds pattern? All of them. Every pod, every arc, inside seventy days."

Juno sat down on the cold floor of the relay room because standing suddenly seemed like someone else's skill. Seventy days. She thought about the tally crawling on her tablet. Forty-one percent. Counting slow.

"Priya. Is there any way to slow it?"

"We're rerouting everything we've got. It buys weeks, maybe. But Juno—" and here the flat calm cracked, just a hairline, just enough to hear the person underneath, "—the pods are waking people up anyway. Partially. Badly. We've got forty-three thousand vitals signatures that are neither asleep nor alive. The docs are calling it *stutter-state*. They're dreaming, Juno. All at once, out of sync, and the dreams are degrading."

Juno closed her eyes. Somewhere below her feet, under a hundred meters of permafrost and seed stock and old human intention, Wendell Zhou was sitting inside an ancient engine, learning its dreams, teaching it theirs. Somewhere above her head, eight million people were beginning to wake without permission, without wholeness, without anyone having voted on it at all.

The universe, it turned out, did not care about turnout.

"I'm calling the council," Juno said. "All three of us. Wake Reyes."

"She'll love that."

"No," Juno said, "she won't. That's how I'll know she's awake."

* * *

Ilsa Reyes arrived on the relay channel looking like a woman who had slept in her uniform and intended to make the uniform apologize for it. Behind her, Mars's emergency session was visibly still running—she hadn't left it so much as stepped outside it, the way you step outside a burning building to take a call.

"Show me the data," she said. No greeting. There never was.

Priya pushed the cascade model. Juno watched Reyes read it the way she did everything: fast, twice, and with visible contempt for whoever had made her need to.

"Seventy days is the optimistic bound," Reyes said finally. "Your engineer knows that."

"Priya doesn't pad numbers."

"No. Martians do." Something moved behind Reyes's eyes that might have been exhaustion or might have been the last structural member of her career giving way. "Here's what happens now, officially. Mars declares a state of survival exigency. The triage protocol self-authorizes. We begin waking the roster in sixty days whether the referendum concludes or not."

"And unofficially?"

Reyes was quiet for three full seconds. Juno counted them. In three years of knowing this woman, Juno had learned that Ilsa Reyes's silences were load-bearing.

"Unofficially," Reyes said, "the referendum is dead. Not postponed. Dead. You cannot ask eight million sleepers' proxies to deliberate on a timeline that expires before deliberation ends. Whatever we do now, we do without consent, because consent requires time and time has been withdrawn from the offer." Her voice didn't shake. It went very precise instead, which Juno had learned was worse. "I spent my career building the machinery that was supposed to make sure we never had to decide like this again. And the universe looked at our machinery and sent a gravity flicker."

"The vote isn't dead," Okafor said from the console. "People are still voting. Turnout's climbing. They know something's wrong—they always know—and they're voting faster."

"Faster isn't the problem," Reyes said. "The problem is that forty-one percent chose Fragment Distribution, and Fragment Distribution requires an integrator. And there is exactly one qualified candidate in the system, and she is downstairs, and none of us have asked her." She looked directly into the camera, directly at Juno. "Because asking her means admitting what we already know the answer has to be."

Juno felt it land in her chest like a hull breach. Because Reyes was right, and because the thing Reyes was right about was the thing Juno had been refusing to say out loud since the night Wendell had first explained fragment theory, gesturing with both hands, eyes bright, describing their own dissolution as if it were a supply route they were volunteering to fly.

"You don't get to decide that," Juno said. "Not Mars. Not the math. Not even her."

"Then who?"

"Her. And us. And everybody voting right now who chose C knowing what C costs." Juno stood up. Her legs worked after all. "The vote isn't dead, Ambassador. It's just about to mean something."

* * *

They brought the cascade data to Wendell at 0700, all three of them crowded into the interface chamber where the Lattice's presence hummed through the walls like a held breath. Wendell sat cross-legged on the deck, cables trailing from the neural crown, and listened to Priya's report relayed through Juno's voice because some things you shouldn't hear secondhand and some things you shouldn't have to deliver firsthand, and Juno had decided this was the second kind.

Wendell took it the way they took everything: completely, quietly, with that terrible attentiveness that made you feel like the most important signal in the system.

"So the arcs are waking people anyway," Wendell said. "Badly."

"Yes."

"And the vote can't finish in time."

"No."

Wendell looked down at their hands. When they spoke again, their voice had the strange doubled quality it got during deep interface, as if the Lattice were listening through them and occasionally breathing.

"Then here's what I think we do. We don't cancel the vote. We change what it's for." They looked up, and their eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Ask them one question. Not which plan. Just: *if fragment distribution is the only plan left, does humanity accept it?* Yes or no. One day. The arcs can hold for one day if Priya strips every nonessential system, and Ilsa's roster gives us the fallback, and the Belt—" Wendell's voice caught, and Juno saw them choose to keep going, "—and the Belt keeps its soul by having asked."

"That's not a real choice," Reyes said. "You're offering them a yes with a corpse attached."

"Every choice has a corpse attached," Wendell said. "Yours has four million. Mine has one." They smiled, and it broke Juno's heart along a seam she hadn't known was there. "At least mine gets to be chosen."

* * *

The emergency question went out at 1200 vault-time, carried on every channel Juno's crew could reach, which was all of them, because the Belt had spent three generations building redundancy against exactly this kind of silence.

**FINAL QUESTION:** The arcs are failing. Fragment distribution is the only remaining path that wakes everyone. It requires one conscious mind to serve as living integrator—a person who will merge with the Lattice and never return as themselves. Do you accept?

Yes or no. Twenty-four hours.

Juno flew the relay herself, *Marrow of Luck* burning hard between the vault's uplink buoys, because she couldn't sit still and the ship at least gave the restlessness somewhere to go. Bay rode the copilot seat, unimpressed by history. The tally came in fast this time—fear counted fast now, terror counting fastest of all—and Juno watched the numbers climb with her hands white on the yoke.

By hour six, turnout had passed the original referendum's total.

By hour twelve, *yes* held sixty-eight percent.

By hour sixteen, seventy-four.

And then, at hour nineteen, Arc Seven's containment failed ahead of schedule.

Juno heard it happen in real time: Priya's voice on the channel, stripped of all its practiced calm, reading casualty estimates from a system that had stopped estimating and started counting. Thirty thousand stutter-state minds, gone—not dead exactly, not alive exactly, scattered into noise the way a song scatters into static when the antenna turns. Arc Four's propagation front accelerated to match. The seventy-day window collapsed to something Priya wouldn't put a number on, which meant the number was too small to say out loud.

The vote was at eighty-one percent yes with five hours left when the arcs' governing council broadcast a single message on all channels:

**THERE IS NO LONGER TIME TO COUNT. WE ARE SENDING OUR YES NOW, UNCOUNTED, AND BEGGING YOU TO BEGIN.**

After that, the tally stopped mattering. Stations didn't wait for certification. Ships transmitted assent in the clear, unencrypted, raw. The referendum didn't fail, Juno realized, watching the feeds dissolve into an uncountable flood of individual voices—it succeeded so completely, so desperately, that it tore itself past the point where counting was possible. Democracy hadn't failed exactly when it mattered most. It had burned itself as fuel, which was maybe the same thing, and maybe the only honest thing it had ever done.

Juno landed the *Marrow* with shaking hands and walked down into the vault, past the seed shelves, past the truce-worn boundaries of three factions' camps, down to the interface chamber where Wendell was waiting.

They knew. Of course they knew. The Lattice had probably told them before Priya had.

"It wasn't supposed to be like this," Juno said, standing in the doorway. "You were supposed to get a real mandate. A clean one. Numbers and quorums and—"

"Juno." Wendell stood up, cables swaying. "Eighty-one percent of everyone said yes to losing me. Do you understand what that is? Nobody in the history of the species has ever been loved on a margin that wide."

"That's not—" Juno's voice cracked and she let it. "That's not love, Wen. Love would have found a third option. Love would have taken longer."

"Love took as long as it had." Wendell crossed the chamber and took Juno's face in both hands, the way they used to over relay video, except now it was real, warm, mortal. "Get Ilsa. Get the crew. Get everyone. If I'm going to become the voice inside everything, I want my last day as just-a-person to be loud."

Behind them, deep in the walls, the Lattice began to sing—a low rising tone, patient and enormous, like an engine that had waited three hundred years finally hearing its name.

And beneath Juno's feet, faint but unmistakable, the gravity stuttered again.
