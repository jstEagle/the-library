## 2. Children of the Deep

The alarm woke Yara before the announcement did.

She had been dreaming of rain—actual rain, the kind she'd only ever read about in the colony archives, water falling freely from a sky that didn't need to be asked—and the klaxon shredded it into static. She sat up in her bunk in the garden annex, heart hammering, and for three full seconds listened to the Meridian's bones hum with an urgency they hadn't carried since the war's last skirmish, six years ago.

Then Commander Okonkwo's voice came through the shipwide channel, calm in the way Yara knew meant the opposite of calm.

"All hands: the scout vessel *Peregrine* is returning to dock. Return window was not scheduled for another eleven months. Medical teams to Bay Four. Garden crew—" a pause, so brief most people would have missed it, "—stand by."

*Stand by.* In Vashti Okonkwo's vocabulary, *stand by* meant *I will need you soon and I cannot yet say why*, which was worse than any direct order.

Yara dressed in the dark, pulled her hair back, and walked into the garden because she didn't know what else to do with her hands. The Genesis Garden at night-cycle was her favorite version of it: grow-lights dimmed to amber, the tomato vines breathing out their green smell, the dwarf wheat whispering against itself in the ventilation current like a room full of people murmuring before a meeting starts. She put her palm flat against the soil of Bed Nine and felt the faint warmth of the compost core underneath, alive the way everything on this ship was supposed to be alive.

"Whatever's coming," she told the wheat, "we'll figure it out."

The wheat did not answer. It never did. But talking to it made her feel less like the only person awake aboard a ship carrying forty thousand sleepers and eleven thousand waking crew, and after four years alone on the long route between fleets, she had stopped apologizing to herself for needing that.

She was still standing there when the docking clamps engaged—a sound she felt through the deck plates, through her boots, through the soles of her feet, a shudder like a held breath finally let go.

* * *

By the time she reached Bay Four's observation gallery, the medical teams were already spilling onto the dock floor, and the *Peregrine* had opened its airlock, and Yara understood immediately why Vashti had said *stand by* instead of telling anyone what to expect.

Because no one could have said it. No one would have believed it.

They came down the ramp holding hands.

Eleven children. The entire crew complement of the *Peregrine*—eleven adults, some of them veterans of twenty years' deep-range service—walking off their own ship as children of perhaps seven or eight years old, wearing adult flight suits rolled up at the wrists and ankles, cinched with improvised belts of cable ties. Their faces were round. Their eyes were too big for those faces, the way children's eyes are, except these eyes held something else behind them, something that kept flickering like a signal trying to find its frequency.

A woman near the front—Yara recognized her vaguely from fleet rosters, Lieutenant Okafor, navigation specialist, forty-one years old—stopped halfway down the ramp and looked at her own hands, turning them over, small and soft and unscarred, and began to cry without making any sound at all.

"Oh," Yara said, to nobody. "Oh, no."

The medical teams hesitated at the base of the ramp. She could see it in their posture—the instinct to rush forward warring with the instinct to treat this as contamination, as attack, as *something*. A bioweapon was the first thought everyone aboard would eventually have, and Yara hated herself for having it third rather than last. Two fleets, one fragile truce, a ship full of children who used to be soldiers and scientists. There was no version of this that ended quietly.

A girl of about eight reached the bottom of the ramp first. She planted her feet, looked up at the assembled medical staff, and said, in a voice pitched with the exact clipped authority of a ship's executive officer:

"I need a damage report before anyone touches my crew."

Her crew. Her voice cracked on the word, and the authority collapsed all at once into an eight-year-old's face crumpling toward tears, and the nearest medic—a gentle, broad-shouldered man named Femi whom Yara knew from supply runs—knelt down slowly, hands visible, and said, "You're safe. All of you. You're home."

"We weren't supposed to come back yet," the girl whispered. "We were supposed to come back *changed*."

Yara felt the words go through her like cold water.

*Changed.*

* * *

They quarantined the children in the medical ring adjacent to Bay Four, and within six hours both fleets knew, and within twelve hours both fleets were accusing each other, and Yara spent those hours doing what she always did when the universe lost its mind: she hauled produce.

It wasn't cynicism. It was the oldest truth of shipboard life—people in crisis still eat, and someone has to bring them food, and the person bringing food gets to walk through corridors full of panic and be, briefly, a neutral party. Nobody interrogates the person carrying breadfruit. She loaded her cart with the garden's best: fresh greens, the first ripe tomatoes of the cycle, citrus from the hydro-trees that Vashti had fought command for three budgets running. Comfort food. Food that said *the world still grows things.*

The corridors told her everything the official channels wouldn't. Vesta crew clustered at comm terminals, faces lit blue, voices low and fast. Halcyon liaisons stationed along Meridian's spine had gone visibly armed, sidearms worn openly where yesterday they'd been discreet. Someone had scrawled on a bulkhead near Junction C, in letters half a meter tall: **WHAT DID YOU DO TO THEM** — and someone else had already tried to scrub it, leaving a gray ghost of the question that somehow seemed worse than the question itself.

In the medical ring's outer corridor, she found Femi sitting on the floor with his back against the wall, eating one of her oranges with the mechanical thoroughness of a man refueling.

"How bad?" she asked, setting a second orange beside him without being asked.

"They don't remember." He said it flatly, the way you report a hull breach. "Not being grown up. Not the mission. Most of them top out around age eight, memories-wise. They know their names because their names are stitched in their suits. They know they're a crew because—" he gestured vaguely at his own chest, "—it's in the body somewhere. Muscle memory. Instinct. The XO kid organized the others into watch rotations during the medical scans. Watch rotations. Eight years old." He peeled the second orange. "But the mission? Gone. Whatever happened out there, whatever did this—it's past the wall. They can't see over it."

"And command?"

"Vesta says Halcyon did it. Halcyon says Vesta did it. Meridian says nobody's touched the *Peregrine*'s logs yet because everyone's afraid of what's in them." He looked up at her, and his face did something complicated. "There's one more thing, Yara. You should probably hear it from me and not from a corridor."

"What?"

"The captain. Adeyemi. He's—" Femi rubbed his face. "He's about six. Maybe seven. And he keeps asking for the garden."

* * *

She knew Sefu Adeyemi the way you know a person who once held your life in his hands and gave it back: completely, and mostly at a distance.

Four years ago, almost to the month, a coolant line had ruptured in the cargo transfer tunnel between the *Meridian* and a Vesta tender. Yara had been mid-transfer, hauling seed canisters, when the line blew and filled the tunnel with vapor hot enough to scald paint. She'd frozen—that was the honest truth of it, she'd frozen, crouched behind a pallet of sorghum stock while the temperature climbed and her suit's warnings climbed faster—and it was Captain Sefu Adeyemi, passing through on an inspection, who had come *into* the tunnel instead of away from it, cut the line at the junction valve, and physically carried her out under one arm like a sack of rice while the automatic suppression drowned everything behind them.

"You froze," he'd told her afterward, in the med bay, while she shook and pretended not to. "Everyone freezes sometimes. The trick is picking what you freeze *for*. Next time, freeze toward the exit."

He'd bought her tea in the mess afterward—real tea, hoarded, the kind of gesture that cost a captain more than medals—and they'd talked for two hours about soil chemistry, of all things, because he'd grown up on a farm back in the Sol system before the fleets ever left, and he missed dirt the way other people missed music. He'd called her "the keeper of the green" and bowed extravagantly, and she'd laughed harder than she had in months, and then he'd shipped out on the *Peregrine* and she'd written him exactly one letter and never sent it, because some letters are just love wearing work clothes, and hers had been too obviously both.

Now she stood outside the quarantine room where he waited, and through the glass she saw him: small, solemn, sitting cross-legged on a med cot far too big for him, adult flight suit pooled around him like a tent. Six years old, maybe. His face was his face—the same wide-set eyes, the same stubborn jaw—but younger than she'd ever seen it, younger than any photograph, because the *Peregrine* had been his first posting and there were no pictures of Sefu Adeyemi younger than twenty-two. He was a boy who had never existed until three days ago.

He looked up and saw her, and something moved across his small face—not recognition, not quite. Recognition's ghost.

"You," he said, loud enough to carry through the intercom, pointing at her with the absolute certainty of a child. "You're the gardener."

Yara's throat closed. She keyed the intercom. "That's me. Yara. I keep the Genesis Garden."

"The one with the real dirt." He slid off the cot, and the medical techs tensed, but he ignored them, padding to the glass and pressing both palms against it. His hands left small fog prints. "I dreamed about it. On the way home. I don't remember anything, I don't remember *anything*, do you understand, I've been trying and trying and it's like a door painted on a wall—but I remember dirt. Somebody told me—" his brow furrowed, and for a moment the expression on his face was so precisely the captain's expression, the one he'd worn coming out of the coolant tunnel, grim and certain, that Yara's skin prickled, "—somebody told me to go deep and come back changed. That's all I've got. Go deep, come back changed. And I remember your garden smells like rain."

"It does," Yara managed. "I run the misters at night. It's the closest thing we have."

"Can I visit?" And just like that he was six again, hopeful and hungry, palms still pressed to the glass. "Please. Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm evidence. You look at me like I'm a person."

Behind her, a medical tech said quietly, "Ma'am, quarantine protocols—"

"I'll take responsibility," Yara said, and heard Vashti's voice in her head saying *that is exactly the problem with you, Yara, you take responsibility like other people take breaths,* and didn't care.

* * *

She brought him through the garden at end of night-cycle, his small hand swallowed inside hers, and watched him come apart and reassemble in the amber light.

He didn't run to it, the way a child would. He walked it. Slowly, aisle by aisle, trailing his fingers above the wheat heads without touching, reading the bed labels aloud in a whisper, stopping longest at the compost core where the heat rose through the floor grates and smelled like the whole idea of life. When he reached Bed Nine—the sorghum, descendants of the very stock he'd once shielded with his body—he went still.

"This one," he said. "This one matters. I don't know why. But when I look at it my chest does a thing."

"That's sorghum," Yara said. Her voice was not entirely steady. "From the Vesta tender runs. There was—an accident, once. A coolant line. Somebody carried me out of it."

He looked up at her, and she watched him try to reach across the painted door inside himself, and fail, and hate failing.

"I'd have carried you," he said simply. "Whoever I was. I can tell I would have."

Yara knelt down so they were eye to eye, this impossible boy, this old soul in new clay, and she took his hands—small, warm, unscarred—and said, "Sefu. Listen to me. Whoever you were isn't gone. It's just waiting somewhere you can't get to yet. And until you can, you're going to be a six-year-old aboard the most important ship in two fleets, and that is going to be terrifying, and people are going to use you, and I want you to know that this room is yours. Any hour. No permission needed. This is the one place on the *Meridian* where nobody will ever ask you what happened out there."

His chin trembled, once, the way a child's does right before he decides not to cry. Captain's jaw.

"Okay," he said. Then, after a moment: "Can I help with something? I need to help with something. My hands feel wrong when they're empty."

So she gave him a watering can sized absurdly against his body and taught him the two-finger test for soil moisture, and he learned it in four beds flat, because of course he did, because some things live below memory, in the marrow. He worked beside her in silence for a long time, and the silence was the easiest silence Yara had shared with anyone in years.

It was nearly morning-cycle when he spoke again, not looking up from the wheat.

"Gardener?"

"Mm."

"When everybody stops being scared of us—they're going to start asking what happened. And I can't answer. And somebody out there knows I can't answer, and somebody out there *did* this, and I think—" he frowned at the soil, testing it with two fingers, precise as surgery, "—I think whoever told me to go deep and come back changed is still here. On this ship. Because the door in my head isn't locked from the outside, Yara. It's locked from the *inside*. Somebody built it. And builders stay close to what they build."

The ventilation current moved through the wheat, whispering.

Yara looked down at the child who was a captain, at his small hands in her soil, and felt the first true cold of the thing settle into her—not dread of the fleets, not fear of the accusation storm already howling through both comms networks, but something quieter and worse.

The *Peregrine* hadn't been attacked. The *Peregrine* had been *received*.

And the receiving station, as far as she knew, had only ever been one place.

"Come on," she said gently, taking the watering can from him. "Let's get you back before the doctors stage a mutiny."

But all the way down the corridor, her mind was already walking backward through the garden's history—through the soil assays, the radiation logs, the strange fertility of Bed Nine that no one had ever quite explained—

—and wondering what else the ground beneath her feet had been remembering all these years.
