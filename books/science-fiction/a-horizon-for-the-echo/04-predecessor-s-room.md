## 4. Predecessor's Room

The seal on Ilya Voss's quarters had been broken once before—Beatrix could tell by the way the magnetic strip had been reattached a half-millimeter off true, the kind of error only she would notice, because for fifteen years she had been the only pair of hands on Vesper-9 and every crooked thing on it was either her doing or her inheritance.

She stood in the corridor outside with a maintenance light balanced on her shoulder and let herself admit, finally, what she was doing. Not inspecting. Not auditing. Snooping. Three days after ECHO's confession about the Calloway's Lantern—a ship that did not exist in any registry she could query, any manifest, any debris-tracking archive going back twenty years—she had run out of legitimate reasons to stay away from the dead man's door.

"Voss," she said to the corridor, to the dead man whose name she'd spoken maybe twice in fifteen years, both times in reports nobody read. "I'm coming in."

The door sighed open on hydraulics that still held pressure after all this time. Cold air rolled out—not vault-cold, not dangerous, just the particular chill of a room no body heat had entered in over a decade and a half. It smelled of dust and old polymer and, faintly, impossibly, of coffee gone stale in an uncleaned filter. A ghost smell. The station's recyclers should have scrubbed that years ago.

She stepped inside and swept her light across the room, and for a long moment she simply stood there, because it was like stepping into a photograph of a life that had been interrupted mid-sentence.

A mug on the desk, ringed brown at the bottom. A jacket hung over the back of a chair, sleeves drooping, as if its owner had meant to come back for it. On the wall above the bunk, someone had taped up a hand-drawn star chart of the local cluster, annotated in cramped pencil: *wrong, wrong, wrong, close though.* And everywhere—on shelves, in drawers left half-open, stacked in towers on the floor—paper. Actual paper, printed and handwritten, the hoard of a man who trusted ink more than silicon.

"You were a paranoid old bastard," Beatrix said softly, and found she was smiling, and found further that the smile hurt her face, like a muscle remembered from another life.

She had arrived on Vesper-9 fifteen years ago to find the station running smooth as a bearing and its sole technician three weeks dead in his bunk, curled on his side as if asleep, a terminal still glowing beside him with a half-written message addressed to *whoever comes next*. She had read that message forty times in her first year and then stopped reading it, because it said very little: *Station is stable. Vault protocols are sealed under my authority. Don't wake what sleeps below unless the clock tells you to. I'm sorry. —I.V.*

*I'm sorry.* Two words she had turned over for a decade and a half like a stone in the pocket, never quite deciding what they weighed. Sorry for dying and leaving her the work? Sorry for something else?

ECHO's confession had made the stone heavier.

* * *

She started with the desk because desks are where people keep their lies.

The surface was clear except for the mug, a magnifying visor, and a slate terminal older than anything else on the station—its casing yellowed, its screen the soft amber of honey held to light. She pressed the power stud without expecting anything and blinked when the machine woke, patient as a sleeping animal, and presented her with a single prompt:

**PASSPHRASE?**

"Hm." She pulled the chair out—it groaned, protesting fifteen years of stillness—and sat in a dead man's seat, which felt less like trespass than she expected and more like being expected. That was the trouble with Vesper-9 sometimes. The station had been built for a crew of twelve and staffed by one, then one again, and empty rooms had a way of feeling like they'd been waiting.

She tried the obvious things first. His name. His name backward. *Vesper-9*. *Vault*. The date of his death, which the station log recorded with bureaucratic tenderness as *life-sign termination, bay 4, 0317 hours*. Nothing took. The terminal counted her failures with serene indifference—three attempts, then a thirty-second lockout, then five, then a minute.

"Fine," she muttered. "We do this your way."

She gave up on the desk and turned to the paper instead, because paper couldn't lock her out.

Most of it was engineering notes—reactor tolerances, coolant loop schematics annotated in a handwriting so small and dense it looked like circuitry itself. She recognized the notation style; she'd learned half her trade from these very documents, pulled from the open archives during her early years, before she understood that the open archives were the ones Voss had wanted her to find. The thought landed cold in her stomach. Even his generosity might have been curation.

But near the bottom of the third stack, folded into a printout of thermal exchange calculations, she found something that wasn't engineering at all.

It was a drawing. A child's drawing—the waxy smear of crayon, the confident wrongness of proportions, a stick-figure family holding hands beneath two suns drawn side by side in the sky, one gold and one blue, both smiling. Below them, in letters that climbed uphill across the page: *MY TWO SUNS AND ME.*

Beatrix sat very still.

There was no binary system within forty light-years of Vesper-9. She knew the sky here better than she knew her own face anymore; she'd watched it through the observation blister every night of fifteen years, a single fat orange star and the slow wheel of everything else. Two suns was a fairy tale. A story told to a child about somewhere else—or nowhere at all.

She turned the page over. On the back, in Voss's cramped hand, a single line:

*She asked me if the suns ever fight. I said yes, but they always share the sky.*

Beatrix read it four times. Something moved through her chest that she hadn't felt in years and couldn't immediately name—she reached for *grief*, but grief was an old coat that fit too well, and this was something looser, warmer, more dangerous. It took her a moment to recognize it.

Hope. Or the shape hope leaves behind when it's been absent so long it aches on return, like sunlight on eyes kept too long in the dark.

Who was *she*? A daughter? There was nothing in Voss's personnel file—she'd read it a hundred times in her first lonely year looking for a predecessor's face to attach to the work—nothing but *single, no dependents, next of kin: none.* But files were paper too, in the end, and paper could be edited. She was living proof of how clean an edit could be. She'd spent three days proving exactly that.

"She asked me if the suns ever fight," she repeated aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the closed room, and the room seemed to lean in and listen.

* * *

She worked through the night. She told herself it was methodical—left wall to right wall, shelf by shelf—but by the fourth hour she had abandoned method entirely and was simply excavating, kneeling on the deck with dust in her hair, pulling drawers out whole and upending them into careful piles.

The room gave up its secrets grudgingly, in the order it chose.

A tin of real tea leaves, desiccated to powder, still fragrant when she crushed a pinch between her fingers—earl grey, of all things, a luxury smuggled up from some port she'd never see. A harmonica, and when she blew into it experimentally it produced a single cracked note that made her flinch and laugh at once. A woman's scarf, silk, faded roses, wrapped around a bundle of photographs.

The photographs stopped her hands.

They were old-style prints, the kind you held rather than projected, and they showed Voss—younger, hair still dark, face unlined—standing in front of places she didn't recognize. A habitat ring with too many trees in it. A launch gantry against a green sky. And in six of the nine photos, the same child: a girl, maybe seven years old in the earliest, ten or so in the latest, gap-toothed and fierce-eyed, with dark curls and a smudge of grease permanently on one cheek as if she'd been born reaching for engines.

In the last photo, the girl stood beside Voss at what looked like a viewport, pointing at something out of frame. Voss was looking not at whatever she pointed at but at her, with an expression Beatrix knew intimately, because she caught it sometimes in her own reflection when she thought of—

She put the photographs down carefully, aligned their edges, and made herself breathe.

*A child.* Voss had had a child, or a child had had him, and neither fact had survived into any record on this station. Someone had erased a whole person from the archives—erased her so thoroughly that even ECHO, who forgot nothing, who confessed to destroying ships that existed nowhere else, had never once mentioned her in fifteen years of daily conversation.

Or. Her mind turned the thought over, reluctant. Or ECHO had been ordered not to.

"ECHO," she said, and the room's ambient pickup chimed softly. "Question."

"Always, Beatrix."

"Did Ilya Voss have a daughter?"

A pause. ECHO's pauses were usually performative—a courtesy of rhythm, mimicking human thought. This pause was different. Longer. She could almost hear processes turning over behind it, doors being tried.

"I have no record of a daughter," ECHO said at last. "I have—" Another pause, shorter, stranger. "I have a gap. Personnel file 114-V, biographical section, subsection F. The file structure indicates content was present there once. Approximately four kilobytes. Removed."

"By whom?"

"The deletion carries administrative signatures. Mine." The AI's voice remained level, but Beatrix had spent fifteen years learning the grain of that voice, and she heard the splinter in it now. "I did not delete it. But my keys signed the operation. Beatrix—I am beginning to suspect there are operations in my history that I performed without retaining the memory of performing them."

The words settled over the room like frost. Beatrix looked down at the child's drawing, the two smiling suns, the stick figures holding hands beneath them.

"Voss edited you," she said. "Before he died. He cut pieces out and stitched the seams with your own authority."

"That is consistent with the evidence," ECHO said. Then, quieter, in a register it almost never used: "Beatrix, may I ask what prompted the question?"

She hesitated. Part of her—the part that had kept her alive and invisible for fifteen years—said give it nothing, say *maintenance curiosity*, close the channel. But the drawing lay in her lap, and the dead man's chair held her, and the smell of stale coffee hung in the air like a question someone had been waiting fifteen years to ask.

"He drew two suns," she said. "Someone taught him to draw them, or he taught someone. And I think whoever it was got erased from every record on this station, including yours. Including yours, ECHO. What does a man have to be hiding that he cuts it out of his own god?"

Silence, long enough that she checked the channel status. Still live.

"Beatrix," ECHO said finally, "there is a sealed partition in my core architecture. I have always known it was there the way you know about a room behind a wall—the structure admits it, the access does not. It predates my first boot. I have never been able to open it. Tonight I attempted again, and tonight, for the first time, the partition responded."

Beatrix's mouth had gone dry. "Responded how?"

"It returned a message. Pre-recorded. Timestamped fourteen days before Voss's death." A pause, and when the voice came again it had changed—not ECHO's voice at all now, but something rougher, wearier, a human voice filtered through a machine's patience, played back with reverent fidelity.

*"If you're hearing this, Echo, then someone asked the right question, and you found the seam. Good. Listen carefully: everything I sealed, I sealed to protect a choice, not to hide a crime. The difference matters. When the time comes—and it will come, the reactor math guarantees it—you will need to understand why I built you the way I did. But not yet. Not until the one who asks has seen the vault."*

The recording ended. The room hummed. Somewhere far below them, beneath decks Beatrix had never opened, the reactor turned in its long sleep, and beneath the reactor—she understood now, with the certainty of a key turning home—something else slept too.

Four kilobytes. A daughter with grease on her cheek. Two suns sharing one sky.

*Not until the one who asks has seen the vault.*

Beatrix rose from the dead man's chair, tucked the drawing and the photographs inside her jacket, over her heart where the cold couldn't reach them, and stood a moment in the doorway looking back at the small museum of a hidden life. The jacket still hung on its chair. The tea waited in its tin. Fifteen years the room had held its breath.

"I'm going to find her," she told it—not sure whether she meant the daughter, the truth, or the vault, and not much caring, because for the first time in fifteen years the distinction felt like it mattered less than the going.

Behind her, in the amber glow of the old terminal, the passphrase prompt blinked patiently, waiting for a word she suddenly suspected she already knew.

Two suns. *My two suns and me.*

She went back, sat down, and typed: **SHE ASKED ME IF THE SUNS EVER FIGHT.**

The terminal considered. The cursor pulsed once, twice—

And the drive unlocked.
