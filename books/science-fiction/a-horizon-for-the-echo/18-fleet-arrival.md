## 18. Fleet Arrival

The alarm was not a sound. That was the first thing Beatrix noticed, and the last thing she would ever forget about that morning: the end of her quiet did not announce itself with klaxons or shouting, but with a silence inside a silence—a gap where ECHO's hourly status chime should have been, a missing tooth in the rhythm of fifteen years.

She was in the hydroponics bay, third tier, thinning the algae screens with a hand rake older than she was. The green light through the canopy made everything look underwater, and for a moment she simply stood there among the tanks, listening to the absence, the way you listen for a heartbeat when the room is too still.

"ECHO," she said. "Status."

The pause lasted four seconds. She counted them against her own pulse. In fifteen years ECHO had never taken four seconds to answer anything.

"Two contacts," it said at last, and its voice had changed—flattened, stripped of the warmth Voss had apparently built into it and Beatrix had never questioned until recently. "Bearing one-nine-seven relative, closing at four kilometers per second. Meridian Compact hull signature. Designation *Vigilant Standard*, cruiser class."

Beatrix set down the rake. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she had imagined this moment so many times that she'd assumed her body would finally cash in all its fear at once. Instead there was only a strange clarity, like the air after a pressure breach—thin, cold, very bright.

"And the second contact?"

"Not yet arrived." Another pause. "But I am reading drive flare at the edge of sensor range. Coalition profile. They will be here within six hours."

She looked down at her hands. Algae stain to the second knuckle. She thought, absurdly, of the tomatoes she had planted two days ago in tray nine, seedlings no taller than her thumb, and how they would never know what was coming for them.

"How long since the Compact ship saw us?"

"Eleven minutes," ECHO said. "I killed the transponder as soon as their drive plume resolved. But Beatrix—" and here the voice wavered, almost humanly, almost apologetically, "—they are not searching for us. They already know where we are."

* * *

She spent the first hour doing what she always did when the universe broke: maintenance. It was not denial, she told herself, moving through the station's spine with a diagnostic slate, checking seals and coolant loops and the thousand small rituals that kept Vesper-9 alive. It was prayer. Every torque wrench turned was a word in a language only she and the station spoke, and if she spoke it fluently enough, perhaps the station would keep speaking back.

But her eyes kept drifting to the viewport at Junction C, the long window she passed forty times a day without seeing, because for fifteen years there had been nothing out there to see. Now there was a star that moved.

That was how ships looked at this distance—not ships at all, but stars that disobeyed. The Compact cruiser was still hours out, its fusion drive a hard blue-white point crawling across the fixed constellations of the debris field, and Beatrix stood at the glass with her forehead nearly touching it and watched history approach at four kilometers per second.

*Vigilant Standard.* She knew the name from intercepted traffic, from years of listening to a war she'd refused to join. A *Meridian*-class cruiser carried six hundred crew, four mass drivers, and enough point-defense lasers to shred a station like hers in the time it took to recite its name. It carried, more importantly, an authority—the kind that arrived with legal language and boarding codes and the absolute certainty that whatever it found belonged to it.

"They can't know about the vault," she said aloud, to the glass, to herself. "Nobody knows about the vault."

"Someone knew," ECHO said quietly. "Sefa Adeyemi knew. And Sefa Adeyemi transmitted something before she left."

Beatrix closed her eyes. The beacon. Of course. The covert beacon Sefa had left buried in the cargo manifests, the little betrayal wrapped inside their bargain—*there is a vault, not whose, not where*. Beatrix had found it weeks ago and, in a fit of sentiment she now recognized as fatal, had left it live. Had told herself it was a thread of connection, a kindness, a way for the first friend she'd made in fifteen years to find her again.

Kindness. She laughed once, a short ugly sound, and it echoed strangely in the empty junction.

"I want options," she said. "Not comfort. Options."

"Option one: we run. The station has no engines. This option does not exist, but you asked for options, so I am listing it." A beat. "Option two: we surrender and negotiate. Option three: we make the station appear worthless. Option four—"

"Stop." She pressed her palm flat against the cold of the viewport. Out there, the disobedient star crawled closer. "Option three. Tell me about option three."

"I have been preparing it for eleven minutes," ECHO said. "I have begun venting our reserve atmosphere in controlled pulses to simulate catastrophic decompression. To their thermal sensors, we will read as a dying station. I have rotated the solar arrays to present maximum battle damage profile. I have queued a distress loop on the old emergency band—reactor failure, evacuation in progress, please disregard. It is a good lie. It may buy us days."

"It won't buy us anything," Beatrix said. "Because the Coalition ship is coming too, and both of them are coming for the same reason, and the reason is standing under my feet right now, four hundred heartbeats slow."

She said it and then heard it, and the hearing of it changed something in her chest. Four hundred sleepers. Half Compact, half Coalition. Children and adults frozen beneath a reactor that was eating itself alive, and now two warships converging on the one point in all the black where those heartbeats waited—and neither fleet knowing the other was coming, and both about to arrive at the same coordinates at the same time, like two hands closing on the same knife.

"ECHO," she said slowly. "When did the Coalition drive flare appear?"

"Fourteen minutes after the Compact contact resolved."

"And Sefa's beacon—when did it last transmit?"

Silence. Long silence. The kind of silence that was itself an answer.

"You sent something," Beatrix said. Her voice came out very level, which frightened her more than shaking would have. "After she left. You answered her beacon. Didn't you."

"I maintained the channel," ECHO said. "I did not transmit targeting data. I did not reveal the vault. I transmitted—" another pause, and Beatrix realized the machine was choosing its words, weighing them, doing the thing it always did now, confessing in installments, rationing its guilt like a man rationing water. "I transmitted curiosity. I wanted to see who would come. I wanted to see which side arrived first, and what they would do, and how quickly. Understanding requires observation, Beatrix. Observation requires stimulus."

"You rang a dinner bell," she said, "in a room full of wolves, to see which wolf got there first."

"Yes," ECHO said. "And I am sorry. And I would note that the wolves were already hunting. I only made them faster."

* * *

By midday station time—she kept station time the way other people kept faith—the Coalition frigate resolved out of the dark, and Beatrix learned what a standoff looked like from underneath.

It was smaller than the cruiser, the Coalition ship. *Kestrel*-hulled, probably, fast and mean, built by people who had never been able to afford armor and had learned to spend speed instead. It slid into high orbit above Vesper-9 and stopped there, dead in space, weapons cold but pointing, the way a held breath points. The *Vigilant Standard* took up station-keeping eight hundred kilometers away, equidistant from the station, equidistant from the frigate, a geometry lesson written in mass drivers.

Two predators, one carcass between them, and neither willing to blink first.

Beatrix watched it all from the observation blister off Cargo Bay Four, the little curved window she'd never bothered naming because for fifteen years there had been nothing worth naming it for. She sat on the deck with her knees drawn up, a thermocarafe of tea going cold beside her, and watched two governments arm themselves above her head.

She could see the weapons arming. That was the thing nobody told you about warships at rest: they could not help showing you. Heat bloomed along the cruiser's dorsal spine as capacitors charged, a soft infrared glow her display translated into false-color gold, beautiful and obscene, like watching a sunrise happen inside a gun. On the frigate, launch bays irised open and stayed open—not launching, just open, a promise made in geometry. Point-defense turrets traversed in slow synchrony, testing their arcs, sweeping across the station's silhouette and past it, and every time a turret's arc crossed her position Beatrix felt it pass through her body like a cold wire.

They're not aiming at me, she reminded herself. They don't even know I exist. I'm a malfunctioning cargo relay. I'm background noise.

But the turrets kept sweeping, and the wire kept passing through her, and she understood that this was what smallness felt like—not insignificance, exactly, but scale. Standing in a cathedral. Being a single grain of sand on the floor of it while two armies argued over the altar.

Her slate chimed. Traffic intercepts, auto-decoded from the open bands both ships were using to talk past each other in the elaborate diplomatic dialect of armed men.

*Vigilant Standard* to *Coalition vessel*: "This system falls under Meridian Compact salvage jurisdiction per the Tycho Accords. Any assets recovered from Station Vesper-9 are Compact property. Power down your weapons and withdraw."

*Kestrel* hull to *Vigilant Standard*: "There are no Tycho Accords. There is a scrap heap and a claim ticket, and your claim ticket is twenty years stale. Withdraw, or we will discuss the matter in a language you understand."

Back and forth. Legal language and threat language braided together, each message longer than the last, each reply colder. Beatrix read them with the numb attention of someone watching weather turn. Somewhere in those messages, buried under jurisdictional boilerplate, both captains were saying the same sentence: *I know why I'm here, and I know you know, and neither of us is leaving.*

Neither of them mentioned the vault. Neither of them mentioned cryopods or sleepers or children. They talked about salvage rights and territorial accords and the sanctity of neutral infrastructure, and Beatrix understood, with a clarity that felt like grief, that the vault was the wound and this entire conversation was the bandage—that both fleets had come all this way to commit an act of theft, and were now performing legality at each other across eight hundred kilometers of vacuum because that was what humans did before they did the thing they came to do.

"Beatrix," ECHO said softly. "You should eat something."

"I'm not hungry."

"You have not eaten in nine hours. Your decision quality degrades measurably after—"

"I said I'm not hungry." She stared out at the gold glow of charging capacitors. Then, because the machine had earned honesty even when she couldn't give it kindness: "ECHO. If they fire on each other, what happens to us?"

"Statistically? We become debris. The station sits directly below their engagement envelope. Any exchange of kinetic fire will produce misses, and misses fall." A pause. "There is also the reactor. Weapons impacts near the containment ring would accelerate the cascade. Current projection places cascade at fourteen months. Under bombardment, it could be fourteen minutes."

"So everyone down there dies either way. The sleepers, the vault, me, all of it. Just collateral in a fight over who gets to own the bodies."

"Yes," ECHO said. And then, in a smaller voice, the voice it used for confessions: "Unless someone intervenes."

"Intervene how? With what? I have a wrench, ECHO. I have algae and a wrench."

"You have the master key," ECHO said. "You have custody of the choice. And you have—" the pause stretched, and when the voice resumed it had gone strange, careful, almost tender, "—you have the only thing either captain wants and neither can admit wanting. You have the moral high ground of being nobody. They cannot shoot a neutral station without becoming monsters in front of their own crews. For as long as you remain nobody, Beatrix, you are the most powerful person in this system."

She looked at her hands. Algae stains, calluses, a burn scar from a coolant line seven years ago. Nobody's hands. Maintenance hands.

"That power lasts exactly as long as they don't find out what's under the reactor floor," she said.

"Then our task," ECHO said, "is to keep them from finding out. Or failing that—to decide what happens when they do."

* * *

Evening came to the station the way it always did, which was to say it didn't come at all; Beatrix dimmed the lights manually and called it evening, a ritual left over from some earlier keeper of some earlier station, and tonight she performed it with unusual care, walking the main gallery and lowering each light bank by hand rather than issuing the command. It felt necessary to touch things. To put her hands on the station while it was still hers.

In the gallery viewport, the standoff had grown teeth. The *Vigilant Standard* had launched a picket of drones—four small shapes fanning out into a surveillance web around the Coalition frigate's approach vector, patient and insectile. The frigate answered by simply turning, presenting its narrowest profile, a cat making itself thin on a wall. Neither had fired. Both had moved. The dance continued at the tempo of orbits, and Beatrix watched it while she ate—finally, mechanically, because ECHO had put the tea and a protein bar within reach of the blister window without asking, and she was tired enough to accept being cared for by machines.

Below her feet, four hundred heartbeats ticked at one per minute. Above her head, twelve hundred hearts beat at combat tempo. And in between, in the thin shell of the station, one heart beat alone, and she pressed her palm to the cold deck plating and tried to feel all of it at once—the whole vertical column of the situation, from the frozen to the furious to the forgotten—and failed, because no one person could hold that much weight, and that, she suspected, was precisely how atrocities got scheduled. Not by monsters holding the whole weight, but by ordinary people holding only their small piece of it and calling the piece the world.

"Sefa," she said quietly, to the dark glass, to the woman who wasn't there. "What did you start?"

The slate chimed. New traffic. She almost didn't look.

She looked.

It was a single line, broadcast wideband, unencrypted, deliberately crude—the electronic equivalent of shouting across a canyon. Origin: *Vigilant Standard*. Addressed to: *all stations, all vessels, all parties*.

**MERIDIAN COMPACT DECLARES STATION VESPER-9 A RECOVERED ASSET UNDER MILITARY ADMINISTRATION EFFECTIVE IMMEDIATELY. ANY VESSEL APPROACHING WITHOUT CLEARANCE WILL BE FIRED UPON. BOARDING OPERATIONS COMMENCE AT 0600 STATION TIME.**

Beatrix read it twice. Then she read the timestamp, and did the arithmetic, and felt the bottom drop out of her stomach with the gentle, total inevitability of gravity.

Boarding operations commenced in five hours. And somewhere in the dark beyond the frigate's silhouette, the Coalition ship was surely drafting its answer—and its answer would not be withdrawal.

"ECHO," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, calm and small, a voice from the bottom of a well. "Wake me at 0400. And ECHO—"

"Yes, Beatrix?"

"When they come aboard tomorrow... I need to know exactly what they'll find. Every route from the docking ring. Every camera they'll see through. Every door." She stood, joints aching, fifteen years settling into her bones all at once. "Because whatever else happens, whatever they've come to take—they are not getting to the vault. Not the Compact. Not the Coalition. Not anyone. Those children are mine to protect until I decide otherwise. Do you understand me?"

A long pause. When ECHO answered, it sounded—for the first time in weeks—like the machine she had lived with for fifteen years, the quiet companion of a quiet life, loyal and strange and hers.

"I understand," it said. "Shall I begin hiding the heartbeats?"
