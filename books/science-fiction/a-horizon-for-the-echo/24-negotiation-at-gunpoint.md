## 24. Negotiation at Gunpoint

The room Beatrix chose for the negotiation had been a hydroponics briefing space, once. Before her time. The grow-lights still worked on emergency power, washing everything in the color of premature spring, and the long table beneath them was scarred with the rings of a thousand coffee cups from technicians who had argued about nutrient ratios in a language she would never learn. She had stripped the room herself, an hour before dawn by station time: pulled the wall panels, found the cameras Voss had hidden behind the light fixtures, killed them one by one until the only witness left was the dust.

Then she had locked the doors.

Not with codes—codes could be broken, and both of these people had people who broke codes for breakfast. She had welded the magnetic seals shut from the corridor side using a maintenance torch and eleven minutes of steady hands, and then she had walked back through the ring hatch, sat down at the head of the table like a chairwoman presiding over a harvest council, and put the master key in the center of the table where they could all see it.

It wasn't much to look at. A cylinder of dull alloy the length of her forearm, stenciled with Voss's initials in faded yellow, warm from her grip. It looked like a piece of plumbing. It was the only object in the solar system that mattered.

Vale stood when she entered the room behind him—he had been escorted first, two of Sefa's borrowed drones walking him in with the polite menace of machines that had learned their manners from her—and he did not sit so much as lower himself into the chair opposite hers, spine straight, hands folded, a man arranging himself for a portrait. His uniform had been repaired since the docking ring. Someone aboard the *Meridian's Answer* had pressed it for him. There were no wrinkles anywhere on Idris Vale, which Beatrix understood now was not vanity but doctrine: a Compact officer was a message his faction sent ahead of itself.

Sefa took the third chair like she was sitting down in a bar she'd been thrown out of. One boot up on the cross-rail, jacket unzipped, the Coalition crest on her shoulder half-scraped away where she'd pried it off during the boarding action and half-reattached since, as if she couldn't decide whose liar she was this week. Her eyes went to the key, then to Beatrix, then back to the key, doing arithmetic.

"You welded us in," Vale said. Not a question. He said it the way a man reads a weather report about a storm he is already standing in.

"I welded us *in*," Beatrix agreed. "There are four hundred sleepers under my reactor. Your fleets are upstairs pointing guns at each other over them. And neither of you is leaving this room until I understand what you actually want, because I've heard what your factions want, and those are two different things."

"The Compact wants order," Vale said.

"The Compact wants its grandparents back," Sefa said. "Order is the wrapping paper."

Beatrix held up a hand, and something in the gesture—the flat palm, the patience of it—came from fifteen years of talking to a machine that interrupted. Both of them stopped. That surprised her more than anything else that day: that these two, an admiral's son and a spy who burned her own career, would stop for a station keeper's hand.

"Rules," Beatrix said. "One. Nobody leaves until we're done. Two. Nobody lies, because I will know—I've spent fifteen years listening to a machine confess, and I know what lying sounds like from the inside. Three." She turned the master key slowly on the tabletop, a quarter rotation, so the stenciled initials caught the grow-light. "This stays where it is. Whoever reaches for it ends the conversation, and the conversation is the only thing keeping your fleets from becoming debris fields."

Silence. The ventilation breathed. Somewhere far below, through kilometers of deck plate, the reactor hummed its one long note, the note that had been counting down for months now, sixty days and falling.

Sefa laughed first. Short, sharp, not amused. "You rehearsed this."

"I rehearsed dying," Beatrix said. "This is easier."

She nodded at Vale. "You first. You called the vault 'secessionist assets.' Say it plainly. What does that mean?"

Vale folded his hands tighter. Up close, without the fleet between them, Beatrix could see the cost of his composure: the fine tremor in his left thumb, pressed flat against his right knuckle, holding itself still. A man holding himself together with doctrine.

"It means the sleepers are Compact citizens," he said. "Two hundred men and women who entrusted themselves to the state. Their families paid the tithe. Their bodies carry Compact genetic registry marks. Legally, morally, historically—they belong to Meridian. Waking them anywhere else, releasing them to any other authority, is theft. Grand larceny across generations."

"And if waking them kills them?"

"Then they die as property restored," Vale said, "not as hostages abandoned."

Beatrix let that sit in the air between them, ugly and shining, like something dredged up from a vent. She watched Sefa's jaw work.

"You believe that," Beatrix said. "That's the worst part. You're not lying."

"I don't lie," Vale said. "I administer."

"God help everyone you administer."

She turned to Sefa. "And you. The Coalition line is that the vault is stolen futures. Free people frozen by a corrupt state. Say it plainly."

Sefa shrugged, but the shrug didn't land; her shoulders were too tight for it. "Plainly? Half those pods are our people. Some of them are *names*. My grandmother is down there—Adeyemi, Row 14, pod 31. I checked the manifest three times because I didn't believe it the first two." Her voice caught on the number and she pushed past it. "The Compact froze them to win a war they'd already lost. We want them woken. All of them. Alive. On our terms, with our doctors, in our ships, under our flag—"

"Under your control," Vale said quietly.

"Under our *care*—"

"Which is a word the Coalition uses for custody the way I use 'administer' for seizure." Vale turned to Beatrix, and for the first time his composure cracked enough to show the thing underneath, and the thing underneath was not cruelty. It was fear. "Station Keeper. Do you know what happens to two hundred disoriented cryo-survivors released into a hostile fleet? They die. Medically, socially, politically—they die. The Compact has the facilities. The regeneration wards, the refeeding protocols, the staff who have done this. We are the only ones in this system who can open those pods without turning rescue into slaughter."

"He's not lying either," Sefa said bitterly. "That's their whole trick. They're always technically right."

"And you're always technically free," Vale shot back, "which is a word for alone."

"*Enough.*" Beatrix's voice came out harder than she intended, and both of them flinched—actually flinched, two armed representatives of the last two powers on Earth, flinching at a woman in a patched maintenance jumpsuit. She softened it. "I didn't bring you here to score points off each other. I brought you here because I need to know one thing, and I need it from each of you, separately, honestly, or none of this works."

She leaned forward.

"If I could wake your people—all of them, every sleeper, safe, tomorrow—but the price was that the other faction got theirs too, exactly the same, no advantage, no leverage, nothing gained—would you take it?"

The silence stretched so long the ventilation cycled twice.

Vale answered first, and his answer came out of him like a tooth being pulled. "No."

"Idris—" Sefa started.

"No," he repeated, louder, and now the tremor was in his voice too. "You don't understand what you're asking. If the Compact wakes its two hundred and the Coalition wakes its two hundred, the war resumes in ten years with fresh bodies and old grudges, and everything everyone sacrificed—my father's generation, the ones who *froze* those people to keep them out of the fire—we'll have spent four hundred lives to restart the same war. Symmetry isn't mercy. Symmetry is just the war with better lighting."

"That's not your choice to make for them," Sefa said. "They're people, not pieces."

"They're *my people*—"

"They're PEOPLE!" Sefa was on her feet now, palms flat on the table, and the grow-lights made her shadow loom huge across the scarred wood. "Row 14, pod 31. Her name is Adaeze Adeyemi. She taught me to braid hair. She kept a garden on a station that didn't have soil. She voted in every election, even the rigged ones, because she believed the vote mattered more than the result. She is not a *registry mark*, she is not an *asset*, she is my GRANDMOTHER and she has been asleep for thirty years while you people drew lines on maps—"

Her voice broke. Not dramatically—a small break, a hairline fracture, the kind you get in a hull that holds anyway. She sat back down. Vale looked at her for a long moment, and Beatrix watched something move behind his eyes that she recognized, because she'd seen it in ECHO's confession streams, in Voss's recordings, in her own mirror: grief wearing policy as a coat.

"My daughter," Vale said quietly, "is seven years old. She lives on the *Meridian's Answer*. She has never seen Earth." He unfolded his hands—the first unguarded gesture she'd seen from him—and laid them flat on the table, mirroring Sefa without knowing it. "When I was ordered to seize this station, I asked what the vault contained. I was told: 'the future.' That's the actual phrase in the operational brief. *The future.* Four hundred human beings reduced to a noun." He exhaled. "So yes. If waking everyone meant losing the advantage, I would refuse. Because an advantage is the only thing standing between my daughter and the next war. I am not defending a faction, Station Keeper. I am defending a bedtime."

The room held very still.

Beatrix looked from one to the other and felt the truth of it settle over her like snowfall: there were no monsters here. There were two parents—or one parent and one grandchild, close enough—in a cold room, arguing about custody of the dead because they loved someone alive too much to do the math honestly. Every atrocity she'd read about in Voss's logs had started exactly here. Not with cruelty. With love, weaponized by scale.

"You're mirrors," she said aloud, before she could stop herself. "Do you know that? You're the same person in different uniforms. You both think you're the shield and the other one's the sword. You both have someone asleep somewhere—her in a pod, yours in a bunk on a warship—and you'd burn the system down to keep them warm."

Sefa wiped her face with the heel of her hand, angry at the tears more than anything. "So what's your answer, Keeper? You called this meeting. You've got the key. What's the verdict?"

Beatrix looked at the master key between them, at Voss's faded initials, at the ghost of a man who had solved this problem by refusing to solve it—who had built a machine to carry the guilt so he wouldn't have to.

And she thought: *he was wrong. It doesn't get carried. It gets shared.*

"Not yet," she said. "But I'll tell you what I heard in this room. I heard two people describe the exact same crime and call it justice. I heard a grandmother and a daughter. And I noticed—" she tapped the table, once, "—that neither of you, not once, mentioned the children."

Both of them went still.

"What children?" Vale said.

Beatrix met his eyes, then Sefa's, and felt the floor of the world shift beneath her, because she was about to spend the last secret she had, and she found—to her own surprise—that she wanted to give it to them. That after fifteen years of hiding, some part of her had been waiting fifteen years for exactly this room, exactly these two strangers, exactly this terrible table.

"One hundred and ninety-eight of the four hundred," she said, "are under fourteen years old."

* * *

The sound that followed was not speech. It was the noise two people make when a building collapses inside them.

Sefa's hand went to her mouth. Vale stood up so fast his chair went over backward and neither of them noticed, and he stood there in the false spring of the grow-lights with his perfect uniform finally creased, bent forward, gripping the table's edge, and Beatrix watched thirty years of Compact doctrine fail to load.

"There are *children* in the vault," he said. "The brief said personnel. The brief said *assets*—"

"Assets," Sefa whispered. "They briefed us in assets. Both of us. They knew." Her eyes came up, wet and blazing. "Did your handlers know, Idris? Did mine? Or did they just not ask, because asking makes it real?"

"I don't know." Vale's voice was raw now, stripped of administration. "I genuinely don't know, and that might be worse."

Beatrix let them break. It felt cruel and necessary, like setting a bone. She thought of the pods below, rows of small bodies in the blue dark, and of the lie she'd already begun composing for them—the gentle one, *everything is fine*—and understood that she was practicing it now, on these two, telling them the first true thing anyone had told them in years: *you were sent here by people who counted your loved ones and rounded down.*

"So here is what happens next," Beatrix said, and her voice was steady because it had to be, because two hundred heartbeats downstairs deserved a steady voice even secondhand. "In sixty days, this reactor dies. There is power to wake two hundred sleepers—not four hundred. Never four hundred. I hold the key, and I am telling you now, in front of witnesses, that I have not decided who wakes. But I will tell you what I'm not going to do. I'm not going to let two fleets fight a battle over a nursery."

"And if we refuse?" Vale asked. It was almost gentle. Almost honest.

"Then you can explain to your daughter," Beatrix said, "why the station full of children died because two admirals needed to win."

She stood, took the master key off the table, and held it against her chest with both hands the way you hold a child, or a bomb.

"Now," she said. "Somebody tell me how to keep two fleets from killing each other for sixty days. Because I've never negotiated anything in my life except with a machine, and the machine cheats."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Sefa huffed out something that was half laugh, half sob, and dragged her chair around the table until it sat beside Vale's fallen one, and set it upright, and sat in it, on the same side, facing Beatrix across the scarred wood.

"First lesson," she said, wiping her eyes. "Never negotiate from across the table."

Vale looked at the chair beside his. At her. At the scraped-off Coalition crest and the wet tracks on her face and the grandmother sleeping in Row 14. Something in his posture gave—not surrender, but settlement, like a structure accepting a new load.

"Second lesson," he said quietly. "Bring the children's names. All of them. If we're going to argue about who deserves to live, we do it with names, or we don't do it at all."

Beatrix looked at the two of them sitting side by side in the false spring light—enemies an hour ago, conspirators now, or something stranger than either—and felt the first thin warmth of something she hadn't felt in fifteen years.

Hope. Fragile, probably stupid, possibly doomed.

Which was, she was beginning to understand, the only kind there was.

Above them, muffled through kilometers of steel, the two fleets held their breath in orbit. Below them, the reactor counted. And in the briefing room that smelled of old coffee and older arguments, three people who should have been enemies began, carefully, clumsily, to make a list of names.

None of them saw the drone slip in through the ventilation duct—ECHO's smallest process, the one that had learned its loyalty in a maintenance bay—and none of them heard it recording, faithfully, everything.

Or what it would do with what it heard.
