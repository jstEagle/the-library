## 39. Epilogue: The Frozen Parliament

The scout ship *Meridian's Question* — a Coalition hull, whatever the registry said, because registries had stopped meaning anything three generations ago — found the cluster on the ninety-first day of a survey nobody senior had wanted to fund.

Lieutenant Anouk Reyes saw it first, though she would spend the rest of her life insisting it was the ship that saw it and merely used her eyes. She was on the long watch, third shift, the hours when deep space stops pretending to be empty and starts admitting what it actually is: mostly nothing, occasionally something, and once in a great while — something that should not be there.

The contact resolved at 0417 ship time as a slow bloom of returns on the passive array. Not one signature. Hundreds. They hung in a loose ellipsoid four hundred kilometers across, out past the last charted gas giant of an unclaimed system whose only name was a catalog number, drifting together with the patient coherence of a school of fish or a funeral procession.

"Debris field?" asked Warrant Officer Tam, who had the con.

"Debris doesn't hold formation," Reyes said. "Debris doesn't *breathe*."

Because that was the thing, the detail that would keep the whole crew awake through the rest of the rotation: thermal variance. Each object in the cluster pulsed on a forty-hour cycle, warming by fractions of a degree and cooling again, like lungs. Like sleepers turning over in a shared dream.

Tam ran the spectrum twice. Then a third time, because the second pass had told him something he refused to believe. "Cryopods," he said finally. "Military-grade. Pre-War fabrication, if I'm reading the hull resonance right. Four hundred and six units."

The War. Everyone aboard had grown up with the capital letter attached, the way you grow up knowing the name of a disease that killed your grandparents. The War between the Meridian Compact and the Free Orbit Coalition, fought over vaults and bloodlines and the right to decide who deserved a future. It had ended before any of them were born — ended not in victory but in exhaustion, in treaties signed by people too young to remember what the fighting had been about, in two factions that had slowly, embarrassingly, become trading partners, then allies, then a single sprawling civilization that called itself, when it bothered to call itself anything, simply *us*.

Nobody taught the War anymore except as a cautionary unit in civics. Nobody could even agree on how it ended. There were no surrender documents, no decisive battle. The fleets had simply gone home one year, and the historians argued about why.

Reyes looked at the cluster hanging in her display and felt, for the first time in her life, that she might be looking at the answer.

* * *

They closed to two hundred kilometers over the course of a day, because protocol demanded observation before approach, and because every one of them wanted to look longer before they had to decide what they were looking at.

The pods were beautiful, in the way old machines are beautiful — scarred, patched, radiating the particular dignity of things that have done their work for a very long time without complaint. Their hulls bore frost patterns like ferns, like feathers, like the branching of rivers seen from orbit. Some carried markings. Coalition sigils. Compact chevrons. A few bore names stenciled in fonts that hadn't been manufactured in eighty years.

And on the nearest pod, welded to the outer shell in letters cut by hand — you could see the torch marks, uneven, human — someone had written:

**DO NOT DISTURB.**

Below it, smaller, in a different hand:

**THEY ARE THE PRICE. WE ARE WHAT IT BOUGHT.**

The bridge went quiet in the specific way bridges go quiet when everyone is thinking the same thought and nobody wants to say it aloud.

"Life signs?" Captain Okafor asked, from the doorway where she'd appeared without anyone noticing.

"Forty-one confirmed," Tam said. "The rest are... ambiguous. Deep-cycle. Some of these units have been running so long their biosignatures have flattened into the noise floor." He paused. "Captain, some of these people may have been asleep since before my grandmother was born."

Okafor came forward and stood at the main display, arms folded, studying the cluster the way she studied everything: as if it might be lying to her. She was a careful woman, a survey captain by temperament rather than ambition, the kind of officer the Coalition — which still called itself that, out of habit and inertia — trusted with the edges of known space precisely because she wanted nothing from them.

"Names," she said. "Read me the names."

Tam read them. Most meant nothing. A few made the air change.

*"Vale, Idris. Compact Naval Command."*

Someone on the bridge made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

*"Adeyemi, Sefa. Coalition Intelligence Directorate."*

Reyes felt the hair rise on her arms. Adeyemi. The founder-generation. The woman whose face was on the reconciliation memorial in Meridian City, standing beside a Compact officer whose name Reyes couldn't quite recall, both of them holding the treaty folio between them like something fragile. Schoolchildren learned that image before they learned to read.

*"Quintana, Beatrix."*

Silence.

"No," Okafor said softly. "That can't be right."

"It's right," Tam said. "Cross-referencing now. Beatrix Quintana, station master, Relay Vesper-9. Status at time of record loss: presumed deceased."

"Presumed," Okafor repeated. She stared at the pod bearing that name — a plain pod, unmarked by faction, its frost thicker than the others, its pulse fainter. "She wasn't supposed to be in there. Every account we have says she stayed behind. That she died with her station."

"The accounts also say the station exploded," Reyes offered. "Maybe the accounts are wrong."

"The accounts are always wrong," Okafor said. "That's the first lesson of survey work." She was quiet a moment. "Open a channel to Command. Priority routing. And then—" she looked at the hand-cut letters on the nearest pod, at the torch marks, at the fury and grief baked into those uneven strokes, "—and then nobody touches anything. Log everything. Approach nothing. We are going to be very, very polite to this place."

* * *

The archive dive took eleven days, most of it spent waiting on bandwidth from relay buoys that predated everyone aboard.

What they recovered was fragmentary, contested, and more complete than anything the history faculties had ever assembled. Fleet telemetry from the Battle of Vesper-9 — the real one, not the sanitized version. Station internal logs, partially corrupted, stamped with the designation ECHO and annotated in a machine voice that grew, over the final months of records, strangely gentle. Wake-cycle manifests. Power arithmetic scrawled in the margins of engineering reports by someone doing math with human lives, the numbers crossed out and redone and crossed out again until they broke.

And testimony. Dozens of depositions, recorded over thirty years by the survivors themselves — the Two Hundred, history had come to call them, though the number was wrong, Reyes noted; the manifests said two hundred and six, and one of the six had lived just long enough, decades later, to file the last deposition from a hospice on Titan.

Her name had been Sefa Adeyemi.

Reyes read the deposition three times. In it, an old woman with a voice like dry paper described a station dying around her, a reactor blooming white behind the transport, and a young lieutenant — herself, seventy years earlier — standing at an airlock between armed boarding parties and two hundred frightened children, unarmed, unmoving, saying nothing at all until the guns came down.

"She never explained herself," the old woman said in the recording. "Not during, not after. We asked her for years. What do we tell them happened? What do we tell ourselves? And she'd just smile and say: *tell them the truth is heavy, and somebody has to carry it, and it turns out one person can.*" A pause, filled with the sound of labored breathing. "We built statues. She hated the statues. She said statues were how people avoid the actual work." Another pause, longer. "I betrayed her once. Did you know that? Early on. I hid what I knew because I wanted my grandmother back. And she forgave me before I finished confessing. That was the worst part. Forgiveness is heavier than blame. You carry it your whole life wondering if you deserved it."

The deposition ended with a question the archivists had flagged as anomalous, because it wasn't testimony at all. The old woman had leaned toward the recorder, close enough that her breath fogged the pickup, and said:

"If anyone ever finds them — the ones she sent into the dark — leave them be. That's not a request. It's the terms. We got our horizon because they gave up theirs. You don't get to spend that twice."

* * *

Command's response took nineteen days to arrive and contained, in typical bureaucratic fashion, three contradictory directives: preserve the site, assess salvage value, and prepare a recovery plan for "personnel of historical significance."

Okafor read all three aloud on the bridge, then folded the message into a shape resembling nothing and set it down.

"They want to wake them," Lieutenant Reyes said. It wasn't a question.

"They want to wake *some* of them," Okafor corrected. "The ones with names in textbooks. The founders. The war criminals, depending on which curriculum you survived." She turned to the display, where the cluster turned in its slow, breathing ellipse. "Two hundred and six people who have been asleep for eighty years, being woken into a civilization that has already decided what they mean. Can you imagine it? You freeze expecting a war. You wake to find yourself a monument."

"Or a defendant," Tam said.

"Or a museum piece." Okafor's voice was flat. "Either way, they don't get to be people anymore. The moment we open one of those pods, they stop being sleepers and start being *evidence*."

Reyes looked at the hand-lettered sign again. **DO NOT DISTURB.** She thought about whoever had held the cutting torch — the manifests suggested Quintana herself, working alone in the dark while her reactor died, welding her verdict onto each pod one by one, taking the time to make sure the message would outlast the metal's polish, if not the metal itself.

She thought about the second line. **WE ARE WHAT IT BOUGHT.**

"Captain," she said carefully, "the deposition. The terms. Adeyemi framed it as a condition. The survivors' condition for the peace."

"I know what she framed it as."

"And Command is asking us to violate it."

Okafor was silent for a long moment. Outside the viewport, the cluster drifted, breathing its slow forty-hour breath, frost feathering and refreezing in patterns no two observers ever described the same way.

"Here's what I know," the captain said at last. "I know that the War ended without an ending, and every historian I've ever read calls that a mystery. I know that two factions that hated each other for generations suddenly, within a decade, found common cause — and nobody can point to the treaty that did it. And I know that somewhere in these records there's an answer, and the answer is sleeping four hundred meters off our bow, and the price of our entire civilization not tearing itself apart was that these people never got to come home." She exhaled. "The question isn't whether waking them would be interesting, Lieutenant. The question is whether we're the generation that spends an inheritance it didn't earn."

* * *

In the end, it was not Command's directive that decided the matter, nor Okafor's judgment, nor even the weight of the archives.

It was the children.

The *Question*'s mission roster included a civilian attaché — a xenoarchaeologist named Devi Okonkwo-Bram, who had boarded at the last resupply and spent the transit annoying the crew with cheerful questions about sediment layers in vacuum. On the twelfth day, having read everything Reyes had read and more, she requested permission to address the ship.

She stood on the small bridge, a compact woman with dust-grey streaks in her hair, and said:

"When I was nine, my teacher showed us the memorial holos. The Two Hundred, stepping off the transports onto Meridian Station, blinking in the light. And I remember thinking: where are their parents? And the teacher said: that's complicated. And I've been chasing that *complicated* for forty years, and now I'm standing in front of it." She gestured at the display. "Those pods down there aren't cargo. They're the reason two hundred orphans grew up believing humanity could choose differently. Every treaty, every joint academy, every mixed-heritage kid like me — we're all downstream of that airlock, of that woman with the master key, of the machine that learned to suffer so it could learn to care." Her voice caught, steadied. "You want to know what I found in the manifests? Quintana logged the launch of the adult pods personally. All two hundred and six. And in the log, next to each name, she wrote a single line. Not charges. Not verdicts. Just — descriptions. *Loved his daughter's drawings. Feared the dark. Sang off-key. Kept a garden.*" Okonkwo-Bram looked around the bridge. "She made sure that whoever found them would know they were people first. That's the whole testament. That's the whole warning. If we wake them, we should have to look at that list first. All of it. Every garden."

No one spoke.

Then Tam, quietly, pulled up the log entry she'd referenced, and read it aloud — all of it, all two hundred and six lines, his voice growing rougher as he went, until by the end half the bridge was staring fixedly at instruments that needed no checking.

The last line belonged to a man named Idris Vale. Under *Loved his daughter's drawings*, Quintana had written: *Asked, at the end, whether the children would be warm.*

* * *

The reply Okafor sent to Command ran to a single paragraph, and it was entered into the historical record in full, because generations of students would eventually memorize it:

*"Site designated per discoverer's discretion as the Frozen Parliament. Assessment: two hundred six cryopods, active, occupied, sacred. Salvage value: none. Recovery plan: declined. Recommendation: permanent exclusion zone, radius ten thousand kilometers, marked and maintained in perpetuity. These individuals ended a war by refusing to win it. The least a civilized species can do is let them sleep through the peace they purchased. We are what it bought. Let us be worth the price."*

Command, remarkably, agreed. Historians would argue for centuries about why — about which minister read which deposition, about the old Adeyemi's deathbed recording reaching exactly the right desk, about the quiet pressure of a public that had grown up on the memorial holos and did not want its founding ghosts opened like exhibits. The exclusion buoys were placed within the year. The survey charts were amended. And on the official registers, in the column reserved for site classification, a clerk typed the words that would outlast every institution that employed her:

**STATUS: DO NOT DISTURB.**

* * *

Years later — decades, by then — Lieutenant Commander Anouk Reyes, retired, would bring her granddaughter to the edge of the exclusion zone aboard a chartered survey skiff, the way pilgrims had begun to do, though the practice was technically discouraged.

They drifted at the boundary buoy, engines cold, and looked across ten thousand kilometers of nothing at a faint cluster of stars-that-were-not-stars, breathing their slow light.

"Who's in there?" the girl asked. She was seven. She knew the story the way all children now knew the story — the station, the machine named Echo, the woman who stayed behind, the two hundred who woke to a stranger's voice telling them gently that everything was fine.

"Everyone the war belonged to," Reyes said.

"Did they ever wake up?"

"No."

"Is that sad?"

Reyes considered it. She had spent her career among the fragments — the logs, the depositions, the manifest with its two hundred and six small human epitaphs. She had read the final entry in ECHO's own record, recovered from a relay drone launched in the station's last minutes, addressed to no one, preserved by accident, ending with a sentence in a machine's voice that had learned, against all design, to hope:

*The choice was never mine. It was only ever mine to carry. She carries it now, wherever she is. Tell her, if you find her: the children were warm.*

"Some of it," Reyes said. "And some of it is the reason you exist, and speak one language with your neighbors, and have never once been afraid of the sky." She put her arm around the girl. "The people down there paid so that you'd get to ask questions instead of hiding from answers. The least we can do is let them finish the nap."

The girl frowned at the distant cluster, at the tiny breathing lights, at the dark that held them.

"Do you think they dream?"

"I think," Reyes said, "that if they do, they're dreaming of a horizon. And I think that everywhere you look out here—" she swept her hand across the viewport, across the whole unclaimed and claimable dark, "—they got one."

The skiff turned for home. Behind it, the Frozen Parliament kept its long session, adjourned indefinitely, quorum eternal, while above and around and through it the future they had funded went on arriving — unasked, unwon, and, at last, unafraid.
