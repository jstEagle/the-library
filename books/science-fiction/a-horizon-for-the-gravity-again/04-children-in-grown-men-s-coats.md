## 4. Children in Grown Men's Coats

Oscar had prepared for many contingencies in his eleven years at the vault. Reactor failure. Avalanche. Polar bear (twice). The slow unraveling of his own mind, which he'd planned for by writing increasingly detailed notes to himself, including one that simply said *YOU ARE OSCAR. THE BEARD IS OPTIONAL.*

He had not prepared for thirty children in adult-sized parkas standing in his airlock like a choir that had wandered away from a very strange church.

The eldest — the girl who called herself Vess — stood at the front of the group, arms crossed inside a coat whose sleeves she'd rolled up eight times each. Behind her, the smaller ones pressed against the frost-fogged glass of the inner door, breathing clouds, staring at him the way he imagined people stared at the last dodo.

"You're the caretaker," Vess said. Not a question.

"I'm the caretaker," Oscar agreed. His voice came out rusty. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Oscar Underhill. I keep the vault."

"We know your name." She said it flatly, but something flickered behind her eyes — recognition, or its ghost. "It's on the maintenance logs. You sign everything twice. Once for the record and once for yourself." She paused. "Whoever taught you that did it so you'd remember you were still a person even when nobody was reading."

Oscar felt the floor tilt slightly under his boots. Nobody had ever articulated that habit back to him. He hadn't articulated it to himself.

"Your predecessor signed three times," Vess went on. "Once for the record, once for himself, once for whoever came after."

"His name was Aldous Wren," Oscar said carefully. "He vanished before I arrived. Eleven years ago. There was no note."

"There was a note," Vess said. "You just couldn't read it yet."

* * *

Vess watched the tall man process this, and she could see the thought moving through him like weather: confusion first, then fear, then — because he was the kind of man who wound a beacon every night for an audience of nobody — concern for *them*, arriving ahead of concern for himself.

She remembered that about caretakers. She remembered more than she wanted to.

The memories were a broken radio. Most days she got static and fragments: the smell of engine coolant, a woman's voice saying *check the tertiary couplings*, the specific weight of a coffee mug she had held in hands much larger than these. Some nights she dreamed in full sentences she no longer understood. But the vault, the beacon, the man named Wren — those came through clearer than most, as if the signal were stronger here, close to the source.

"He used to sing while he worked," Vess said. "Badly. Something about a girl from Tromsø. He said if the beacon ever answered him back, he'd consider it applause."

Oscar made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "That's — yes. That's exactly something he would say. I found his notes. Half of them are jokes. The other half are instructions, and half of *those* are also jokes." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm sorry. This is insane. You're children. You're wearing Chief Engineer Okafor's coat. I attended his memorial service via delayed transmission twenty years ago."

"Mama Okafor," said a small boy at the glass, and then started crying, quietly, the way children cry when they've learned crying gets them nothing.

Vess put a hand on his shoulder without looking away from Oscar. "Her name was Adaeze Okafor. She kept dried mango in her desk drawer and gave it out when we did well on drills. She hummed when she soldered." Her voice stayed level through sheer force of will. "We have her face. We don't have her anything else. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"I think so," Oscar said. And then, because he was who he was: "Are you hungry? It occurs to me I'm interrogating hungry children in my doorway like a lunatic."

Vess blinked. Of all the possible responses to *we are dead people wearing small bodies*, *are you hungry* had not been high on her list. Which meant, somehow, it landed harder than any of the others.

"Yes," she said. "Extremely. We've been eating ship rations, which taste like regret and packaging."

"I have real food," Oscar said. "Well. Real-adjacent. I grow things. Potatoes, mostly. Some greens under lamps. There's a jar of honey from before your parents were born, probably, but honey doesn't expire, it just gets philosophical."

* * *

They ate in the vault's mess, which had been designed for a staff of forty and had hosted one for a decade. Oscar laid out potatoes roasted in diesel-adjacent oil, rehydrated greens, flatbread, and the philosophical honey. The children descended on it with the terrifying efficiency of the genuinely starving, and Oscar found himself doing math he didn't want to do: thirty mouths against stores calibrated for one.

He did the math anyway, and kept ladling.

Vess ate last and least, moving down the table making sure the little ones had seconds before she took her firsts. Oscar recognized the behavior. He performed its adult equivalent every time a supply drop came — checking seals, counting canisters, taking the bruised ration for himself.

"You count everything twice," Vess said, watching him. "Like your signature."

"It's how I know things are real."

"That's why Wren did it too." She turned her spoon over. "He told us. Before launch. He said, *when you get lonely enough, numbers will be your friends, and that's sad, but it works.*"

"When did he tell you that?" Oscar asked gently. "You'd have been — what, born after launch?"

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Then she set down her spoon and folded her hands, and for a second the posture was so precisely adult, so specifically the posture of someone about to deliver a briefing, that Oscar's skin prickled.

"I don't know when," she said. "That's the problem. I remember him telling me, and I remember being taller than the table when he told me, and I remember the taste of the coffee he was drinking. But I also remember being six years old and losing a tooth during a meteor shower drill. Both are true. Both happened to me. They just happened in the wrong order, or maybe there is no order anymore." She shrugged, a child's shrug wearing an officer's exhaustion. "The doctors aboard called it regression fragmentation. The crew called it *the tide going out*. Everything we were washed backward, and some of it stuck to the shore in pieces."

"And you're the one who collects the pieces."

"Somebody has to." She glanced down the table, where a girl of perhaps five was asleep face-first in her flatbread, and a boy was carefully teaching another boy how to fold his napkin into a shape, both of them giggling. "There are thirty of us. The oldest is twelve. The youngest is four. None of us can reach the overhead valves. Two of us can read schematics, sort of, the way you'd remember a song. I can run the reactor checklist from memory but my hands are too small for the physical interlocks." Her jaw tightened. "We flew a deep-space vessel home on fragments and muscle memory. We landed it. Barely. And now everyone keeps looking at us like we're either miracles or evidence, and nobody has once asked us what we need."

"What do you need?" Oscar asked.

Vess opened her mouth. Closed it. To her own horror, her eyes filled. She was twelve years old and had captained nothing and everything, and no one — not once since the tide went out — had simply *asked*.

"Sleep," she managed finally. "Beds that aren't built for our old bodies. Someone to stand watch so the rest of us can stop standing watch." She swiped her sleeve across her face, furious at it. "And answers. Eventually. But sleep first. Sleep is tactical."

"Sleep is tactical," Oscar agreed solemnly. "I've been saying that for years. Nobody listens. Well — nobody listened. Now there's a whole crew." He stood and began stacking bowls. "Bunks are down the corridor. They're sized for adults, but I have blankets, and frankly I've slept in worse configurations than *child in an oversized bunk*, I once slept standing up in a supply closet during a blizzard and I want you to know I dreamed about shelving."

A small laugh rippled down the table — the first sound the children had made that wasn't crying or protocol. Vess felt it move through the room like warmth through a cold pipe.

* * *

Oscar lay awake that night in his own bunk, listening to the unfamiliar music of the corridor outside: small snores, a cough, someone murmuring in their sleep in a voice that was probably sixty years old.

He should be afraid. He knew that. Thirty impossible children wearing the faces of the honored dead had landed out of a ship declared lost two decades ago, and every instinct trained into him by the fleet's handbooks said *report, contain, document*. But the handbooks had never eaten dinner with them. The handbooks didn't know that the smallest one said thank you in three languages depending on how tired she was, or that the boy with Okafor's face had folded his napkin into a lopsided bird and presented it to Oscar with the gravity of a knighthood.

The bird sat on his desk now. He found himself unwilling to look directly at it, the way you're unwilling to look directly at the sun or at hope.

He got up, pulled on his coat, and walked the long corridor to the beacon array — nightly ritual, eleven years unbroken. Wind the rotators. Check the phase alignment. Log it twice.

Vess was already there.

She stood at the base of the array in a blanket like a cape, looking up at the rotating lights with an expression he couldn't read.

"Couldn't sleep?" he asked.

"I slept four hours. That's plenty." She said it automatically, and he heard the ghost of a shift schedule in it. Then she pointed up. "It's different than I remember. The pattern. Wren ran a sequence — seven pulses, rest, three pulses. Like a heartbeat with a limp."

Oscar went very still. "That's the default commission pattern. I changed it nine years ago."

"Why?"

"Because it wasn't working." He laughed, embarrassed. "Eleven years of answering nobody. I figured — if the point of a beacon is to be heard, maybe I should say something worth hearing. So I built a new pattern. A longer one. It says—" He stopped, suddenly shy of it, this private thing he'd never told anyone because there had been no one.

"Say it," Vess said.

"It says *you are not lost, you are early.*" He shrugged. "It seemed true. Ships aren't lost, they're just somewhere between. People neither." He looked at her sideways. "It seemed especially true for anyone coming home after a long time."

Vess didn't answer right away. Above them the array turned, patient as a lighthouse keeper, casting its long slow sentence out over the ice and up past the atmosphere toward whatever was listening.

"He would have liked that," she said finally. "Wren. Seven pulses, rest, three. A heartbeat with a limp." She hugged the blanket tighter around her shoulders. "Oscar. Can I ask you something tactical?"

"Always."

"The fleet. When they find out we're here — and they'll find out, the Meridian's landing burned a scar you can see from orbit — what happens to us?"

Oscar considered lying. He was, apparently, bad at it; it lasted less than a second.

"I honestly don't know," he said. "But I know I'm the one who answers the door here. And I know how to make a lot of potatoes." He paused. "And I know that whatever they decide you are — miracles, evidence, abominations, all the words people use when they're scared — you ate at my table tonight, and that makes you mine to look after. That's not fleet regulation. That's just the job."

Vess studied him for a long moment, twelve years old and sixty years deep, and nodded once, the way officers nod.

Then she frowned, tilting her head at the array, and her voice dropped.

"Oscar. The pattern's wrong tonight."

He looked up. The rotation was smooth, the phases aligned, everything green across his handheld readout — and yet the seventh pulse came a half-beat late. Then again. Then again, regular as breathing, deliberate as a knock.

Not a malfunction. An acknowledgment.

Something out in the dark had learned his sentence — and was answering it back, word by word, in a rhythm no human had programmed.

Behind them, down the corridor, one of the sleeping children began to scream.
