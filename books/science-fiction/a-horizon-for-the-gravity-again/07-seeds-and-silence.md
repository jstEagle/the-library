## 7. Seeds and Silence

It took me three days to work up the courage to show thirty children the most important room on Earth.

Not because the vault was fragile — it had survived the century better than any of us had. It was because I'd spent eleven years talking to generators and frost-sealed doors and a beacon that never answered, and my conversational muscles had atrophied into something closer to rope than muscle. The last time I'd addressed a group this size, I'd been defending a doctoral thesis on seed dormancy in arctic conditions, and I'd fainted halfway through the second slide.

The children, for their part, seemed unbothered by me. They had organized themselves within hours of landing into something between a crew and a schoolyard, with Vess at the top of both hierarchies. They swept the Meridian's corridors in shifts. They rationed with a discipline that would have shamed most adult colonies. They had also, I noticed on my second visit, reorganized my guest quarters' supply crates into an arrangement that could only be described as *feng shui by committee*, and when I'd asked about it, a small girl named Petra had looked at me with the weary patience of a quartermaster addressing an idiot and said, "Heavy things low, Doctor Underhill. Obviously."

Obviously.

So on the morning of the third day, I stood in the vault's entry hall in my cleanest thermal layer — clean being relative; everything I owned smelled faintly of diesel and despair — and waited while Vess assembled her people on the ramp.

"Rules," Vess said, in a voice pitched to carry. She stood with her hands behind her back like a twelve-year-old admiral, which, given what she actually was, she essentially was. "We go where Doctor Underhill says. We touch nothing he says not to touch. We do not run." A pause. "Bram, I'm looking at you."

"I ran *once*," said a boy who could not have been older than eight but whose posture suggested a man who had once lost a shipment of something important.

"You ran into a pressure bulkhead."

"The bulkhead came out of nowhere."

"Bulkheads don't come from anywhere. They're installed." Vess turned to me. "Doctor. Whenever you're ready."

I took a breath, keyed the inner lock, and let the first warm breath of the vault roll out over them.

* * *

People imagine the seed vault as a cavern, all echoing dark and dramatic lighting. In truth it's a long spine of corridors bored into permafrost, lined with shelving that goes on past the point where the mind gives up counting. Millions of samples. Wheat from terraces that no longer exist. Rice bred before anyone thought to write down who bred it. Apples from orchards that are now parking structures, or seabed. Every packet a small paper promise: *this existed. Someone loved it enough to save it.*

The children filed in behind me in a loose double column, and for a full minute, nobody said anything at all.

Then Bram said, "It smells like a library," and the spell broke into a hundred small voices.

They spread along the shelves the way water finds its level. Some read labels aloud in careful, halting voices — these were children who had learned to read twice, I suspected, once as adults and once again as children, and there was something heartbreaking in watching a small face puzzle over *Solanum tuberosum* like it was a riddle. Others simply touched the drawers, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. Petra found the medicinal botany section and went very still, and I saw her lips moving as she memorized catalog numbers.

Vess stayed near me. She didn't browse. She watched her people, and she watched me, and she asked the question I'd been waiting for.

"How long has this been here?"

"Longer than me. Longer than the fleet, probably. The original builders wanted a place that would outlast whatever happened above." I ran my hand along a shelf frame, cold even through my glove. "Most of the world forgot it. That was sort of the point. You can't bomb what you can't find, and you can't panic about what you've stopped thinking about."

"And you keep it alone."

"I keep it alone," I agreed.

"That's a lot of library for one librarian."

I laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled us both. "That's the best job description anyone's ever given me."

A small boy named Oskar — yes, really; we'd had a whole solemn ceremony about it, two Oskars shaking hands like rival nations — tugged my sleeve. "Mister Underhill. What's under the floor?"

I should have lied. I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever logbook the universe keeps: I considered lying, and the lie was right there, fully formed, easy. *Fuel storage. Old machinery. Nothing.*

But Oskar was looking up at me with the particular gravity of a child who already knows the answer is interesting, and eleven years of silence have a way of rotting your discretion from the inside.

"Come see," I said.

* * *

The cryo-banks sit two levels below the seed archive, behind a door I had opened exactly four times in eleven years — once a year for inspection, and once, in my second winter, just to stand there and feel the weight of it, because a man alone needs to be near other people sometimes, even sleeping ones.

The chamber is broad and dim and humming. Two wings branch off the central hall, sealed behind frosted bulkheads marked A and B in lettering so institutional it might as well have been carved by bureaucracy itself. Between them, ten thousand pods in ranked rows, each one lit with a soft amber standby glow, each one holding a person.

The children came down the stair in near silence, and the silence deepened as they understood what they were seeing.

"They're asleep," someone whispered.

"All of them?" someone else.

"All of them," I said. "Ten thousand and forty-one. They came here during the last collapse — the fleet's founding generation. People who didn't fit on the ships, or wouldn't leave Earth, or were promised they'd be woken when it was safe." I heard my own voice flatten on the last part. "It's been safe for a while now. Waking them takes power we haven't had."

Petra pressed her mitten against the glass of the nearest pod. Inside, a woman slept with the unlined face of someone frozen mid-thought, mid-life, mid-everything. "She's not old," Petra said.

"No."

"She's been asleep longer than I've been alive."

"Yes."

Petra absorbed this with the terrifying efficiency of children everywhere, then moved on to the next pod, and the next, reading nameplates in a whisper like a roll call for a class that never woke.

And that's when I saw it. The thing I would turn over in my mind for nights afterward, the detail that should have warned me.

The children did not react to Wing A and Wing B the same way.

Wing A's seal drew them the way a fire draws moths — curiosity, crowding, Bram pressing his face to the little viewport until Vess pulled him back by the collar. But Wing B — Wing B made them quiet. Not awed-quiet. *Careful*-quiet. The way you're quiet near a dog that's been hurt. Several of the younger ones drifted toward it and then, inexplicably, away, as if the air in front of the bulkhead were slightly too thick. A girl named Sefa stood before it for a long moment with her hand half-raised, not touching, and then walked to the far side of the hall and didn't look at it again.

Nobody explained it. Nobody talked about it. And nobody, I noticed, asked me to open either wing.

Which was fortunate, because I couldn't have. The wing seals weren't mine. They predated my tenure, keyed to codes held somewhere above my pay grade — which, given that my pay grade was *one man and a diesel generator*, wasn't saying much.

Vess hadn't joined the others. She had gone still in front of the central directory — a wall display listing every sleeper by wing, bay, and pod, a census of the saved. Her eyes moved down the columns with a strange double motion, the scanning of a child and the searching of someone much older, and then they stopped.

I watched the color leave her face.

It left quickly and completely, the way heat leaves metal, and for a moment she looked less like a girl than like a candle blown out. She raised one hand and touched the display, just below a single line of text, not quite touching the name itself, the way you hover your palm over a stove to check if it's hot.

"Vess?" I kept my voice low. "Do you know someone here?"

For a long moment she said nothing. The hum of the banks filled the space between us, that patient mechanical breathing, ten thousand heartbeats replaced by one compressor's drone.

"My mother's maiden name," she said finally. Her voice was steady, which somehow made it worse. "It's on that list. Wing B, bay nine." She blinked, and when her eyes cleared they were dry and terribly old. "I didn't know she was on the list. I didn't know there *was* a list. But I know that name the way you know a word in a language you thought you'd forgotten."

"Your mother is here? Asleep?"

"I don't know." She turned to look at the sealed bulkhead of Wing B, and her jaw set in a way I would come to recognize as the exact expression of a ship's captain receiving news she cannot yet act on. "I don't know if she's here, or if she *was* here, or if—" She stopped herself. Whatever the end of that sentence was, she swallowed it whole, and I watched it go down like a stone.

"Vess," I said carefully, "if your family is in that wing—"

"Don't." It wasn't sharp. It was frightened, which is different. "Please. Not yet. I need to — I need to think about why I know that name, and I can't do it while everyone's watching me be strange about a wall."

I understood that more than she could have guessed. Eleven years of being strange about walls myself.

"Okay," I said. "Not yet."

"Not yet," she agreed, and then, because she was also still twelve, she added, "But soon. And Doctor Underhill? When I figure out why my stomach dropped through the floor just now — you'll be the first person I tell. Deal?"

"Deal," I said.

She straightened her uniform jacket — an adult's jacket rolled at the cuffs, always rolled at the cuffs, and I wondered briefly who had rolled them for her before she learned to do it herself — and walked back toward her people wearing composure like borrowed clothing.

* * *

We surfaced into afternoon light an hour later, blinking, the children chattering again now that the underground hush had released them. They had questions in bulk. Why seeds and not animals (genetics, mostly, and animals complain). Could they grow something (spring, I promised, spring). Was the sleeping lady in bay three lonely (I said I checked on her every year, which was true, and that I talked to her, which was also true, and Petra nodded gravely as though this were normal behavior, which after eleven years it frankly was).

Vess lingered at the top of the ramp while the others streamed toward the Meridian.

"Thank you," she said. "For showing us. Most adults would have hidden the bottom floor."

"Most adults would have hidden it better," I admitted. "A kid asked what was under the floor and I folded like wet cardboard."

"Kids are good at that." She smiled, small and tired. Then her gaze slid past my shoulder, north across the ice shelf, and the smile faded by degrees.

I followed her eyes. At first I saw nothing — the flat white horizon, the low sun smearing gold along the pressure ridges. Then I saw what she saw: a glint. Then another. Descending, in formation, too steady to be ice glare and too many to be anything friendly.

"Doctor Underhill," Vess said quietly, "how often does your sky get visitors?"

"In eleven years," I said, "never."

"Hm," said Vess, in the tone of a captain whose day has just gotten complicated. "Then I think we should all get inside."
