## 13. Vess Remembers Everything

The fever came on the fourth night after the retrieval team left, and it came the way winter came to Svalbard — politely at first, then all at once.

Vess knew something was wrong when she started finding the mess hall funny. Not funny in the way of Pip's jokes, which were mostly about reconstituted eggs and therefore objectively funny. Funny in a loose, sliding way, where the walls breathed and the overhead lights had opinions and Dr. Anselm Kade's gentle voice from three days ago kept replaying in her head like a song she couldn't turn off.

*You're doing so well, Vess. Just tell me what you remember.*

She'd lied to him. She remembered lying to him — that was the terrible part, that the lie was one of the clearest things in her head while everything around it was fog. She'd told him the regression was an accident, a drive malfunction, a tragedy of physics. She'd watched his kind eyes soften with pity and she'd let him pity her, because pity was safer than truth.

Now the fever was burning the fog off, and underneath it was everything.

"Vess?" It was little Odile, six years old and solemn as a judge, standing in the doorway of the bunkroom Vess shared with four of the younger ones. "You're making noises."

"Noises," Vess said.

"Like the kettle Oscar fixed. But sadder."

That was fair. That was devastatingly accurate, actually, and Vess would have laughed if laughing hadn't hurt her ribs. She tried to sit up and discovered the room was doing a slow rotation around her, like the Meridian in its worst hours.

"Get Oscar," she said. "Tell him — tell him I need him. And don't tell anyone else. Especially not anyone wearing fleet gray."

Odile nodded with the gravity of someone accepting a military commission and ran.

* * *

Oscar Underhill arrived carrying a thermometer, a thermocouple blanket, and the particular expression of a man who had spent eleven years alone and had somehow become, against every expectation, a father to thirty children he hadn't asked for.

"Fever's 39.2," he said, reading the strip. "That's high for you lot. Your baseline runs cool."

"I know." Vess's voice came out scraped. "It's not just a fever."

"Everything's *just* something until it isn't." He sat down on the edge of the bunk, which creaked under a grown man's weight, and looked at her with those patient caretaker's eyes. "What is it?"

And here was the thing Vess had learned about fever, in the long hours of the night while the others slept and her skull filled up with light: fever didn't ask permission. Fever opened doors she'd nailed shut with a twelve-year-old's hands, and behind the doors were twenty-two years of being someone else.

"I'm going to remember out loud," she said. "I can't stop it anymore. So either leave now or hold my hand, but whichever you do, don't interrupt, because if I stop I don't know if I can start again."

Oscar held out his hand. His palm was warm and rough and smelled faintly of diesel, which was, Vess thought distantly, the most honest smell in the universe.

So she began.

* * *

"We found it in the second year out," she said, and her voice was not entirely her own anymore; some of it belonged to a woman named Dr. Vashti Okonjo-Brandt, mission xenobiologist, thirty-eight years old, who had once been very sure of herself. "Past the heliopause, in the deep dark between here and Proxima. We weren't looking for it. Nobody looks for anything like it, because nothing like it should exist.

"It was a signal first. Like your beacon, Oscar, except wrong. A beacon says *here I am*. This thing said — " she searched, and the word arrived cold — "*come see.* And we went to see, because we were explorers, because that was the whole stupid beautiful point of us.

"It wasn't a ship. It wasn't alive the way we mean alive. The best word anyone found for it was *horizon*. A moving horizon. When you looked at it, you saw the edge of everything, and past the edge was — nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. And the nothing looked back, and it was patient, and it was curious, and it wanted to know how we worked the way a child wants to know how a clock works. By taking it apart."

Oscar's hand tightened around hers. He didn't interrupt. Good man.

"We lost the *Calder* first. Six people. Not killed — *unmade*. Their patterns, their selves, smoothed out like frost on glass. And command made the call every command makes: press on, classify, contain. Captain Adeyemi ordered us to run silent and keep seeding worlds, because the mission was bigger than six lives, and maybe she was right, and maybe that's the kind of arithmetic that damns you.

"But the horizon followed. It liked our wake. Our engines, our noise, our *pattern* — whatever we were, it was interested, and interest, from a thing like that, is worse than hunger. It doesn't chase. It drifts along behind you at exactly the speed you're fleeing, forever, because it has forever.

"So Dr. Sefa — our chief medical officer, brilliant, half-mad by then, we all were — proposed the experiment. You can't outrun it. But maybe you can *change* what it's tracking. It reads pattern, signature, self. What if we scrambled ours? What if we rewound ourselves back before we were anyone — before we had a shape worth unmaking?"

"The regression," Oscar said quietly.

"The regression," Vess confirmed. "Deliberate. Designed. We rebuilt ourselves into children using our own cell lines — second-generation bodies, grown fresh, seeded with fragmentary memory so we could still fly the ship home. Because here's the part that matters, Oscar, the part I lied to Kade about, the part I've been choking on since we landed:

"*We weren't running away.* We were running *home*. We knew it would follow the Meridian's wake, and the wake pointed at Earth. We aged ourselves backward so we could get ahead of it and warn you. Thirty children flying a dying ship across eleven light-years to raise an alarm nobody would believe from mouths this size."

The fever pulsed. Vess closed her eyes and saw the horizon again, vast and edgeless, and felt the old adult terror move through her small chest like weather.

"And it's still coming," she said. "It doesn't hurry. It doesn't have to. But it's coming, and it will arrive, and when it does, everyone asleep in your vault and everyone awake in that fleet and every single one of us — we'll all be clocks waiting to be taken apart."

* * *

For a long moment there was only the sound of the bunker's ventilation and, somewhere above, the wind worrying the ice.

Then Oscar said, "Okay," in the calm voice he used for fixing generators, and Vess almost sobbed with relief, because he hadn't flinched, hadn't called her a liar or a monster or a symptom.

"Okay?" she managed.

"Okay. I've got questions, and they're going to be dumb ones, because I'm a seed librarian, not a physicist. But first — " he pressed the back of his hand to her forehead, frowning — "you need to drink this and sleep, because you just carried twenty-two years uphill and you're burning up."

"Oscar. Did you hear me? It's *coming*."

"I heard you." He helped her sit up enough to drink — electrolyte broth, warm, saltier than sin. "And tomorrow I'm going to walk into that comms room and figure out how to make the fleet believe a twelve-year-old, which may be the hardest engineering problem on this planet. But tonight you rest. That's not negotiable. I've been tending things alone for eleven years; I know the difference between urgency and emergency, and you, kid, are currently both, but only one of them gets solved by you staying awake."

There it was again — that gentle humor, dry as the crackers in stores, that made the horror sit down beside it instead of swallowing everything whole. Vess drank the broth. It helped. Some of the spinning slowed.

"Why aren't you scared?" she asked.

"Oh, I'm terrified." He said it easily, settling back against the bulkhead. "But I've noticed fear works better if you give it a job. Right now its job is to wait its turn."

"That's not how fear works."

"Says the person who's been hoarding hers in a locked room for two years. How's that working out?"

Vess stared at him. Then, despite everything — the fever, the horizon, the unmaking waiting somewhere in the dark between stars — she laughed, a real laugh, cracked and too loud, and several of the younger kids stirred and grumbled, and Odile muttered "the kettle again" from her bunk, and for one absurd moment the end of the world smelled like diesel and electrolyte broth and sounded like a child snickering into a pillow.

Relief and horror arriving together, she thought. They'd apparently decided to share a bunk.

* * *

She slept in fragments, and in the fragments she dreamed the true dreams, the adult ones, undiluted now.

She dreamed of the observation deck, year two, watching the Calder's last telemetry flatline into smoothness. She dreamed of Sefa's face lit blue by the regeneration tanks, saying, *We won't remember why we did this. Write it down. Write it down where a child can find it.* She dreamed of the black box, of the partials Commander Reyes-Okonkwo had seen — and understood, suddenly, waking at 3 a.m. with her heart slamming, that Ilsa's sister's name in the manifest meant the commander already suspected, already grieved, already had skin in this game.

And she dreamed of the wake itself: a long bright scar through the dark, stretching from the edge of everything back toward a small blue world, and something vast drifting along it, unhurried, curious, patient as permafrost.

When she woke properly, gray morning light leaking through the ice-crusted port, Oscar was still there. He'd clearly been awake for hours. There was paper everywhere — actual paper, from the vault's ancient supplies — covered in his cramped handwriting.

"You said write it down," he said, without turning around. "In the dream or before it, somebody said write it down where a child can find it. So I've been writing down everything you said. And I've been thinking."

"Dangerous."

"Happens once a decade. Best pay attention." He turned, and his face had the look she'd learned to read as *caretaker mode engaged*: tired, resolute, quietly furious on behalf of other people. "Your thing follows wakes. Patterns. Signatures. Yes?"

"Yes."

"And the beacon — my beacon — has been broadcasting a pattern for decades. A strong one. Straight out of this valley, straight off this planet, like a shout in a quiet room."

The cold that went through Vess then had nothing to do with the fever.

"Oh no," she whispered.

"Yeah. I don't think your predecessor built that array to guide the Meridian home out of kindness, Vess. Or not only kindness. I think somebody, somewhere, built a lighthouse — and lighthouses work in both directions." He stood, joints cracking, and gathered up his papers with hands that were almost steady. "I need to check the array's transmission logs. All of them. Eleven years of them."

"Oscar —"

"And you," he said, pausing at the door, and his smile was thin but real, "are going to stay in this bed and get better, because when the fleet comes back — and they'll come back, truce or no truce — you're going to have to be the one who tells them. All of it. In words grown-ups can't argue with. That's your job now. Mine's the light."

He left. Vess lay in the bunk, fevered and hollowed and, strangely, lighter than she'd been in two years, listening to his boots fade down the corridor.

Somewhere beyond the ice, beyond the sky, the horizon was drifting closer along a trail they had burned themselves.

And directly above the vault, faithful as a wound, the beacon was still shining — calling something home.
