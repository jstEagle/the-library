## 14. Kade's Report

Dr. Anselm Kade had written four hundred and eleven medical reports in his career, and he had a private theory that the fleet's bureaucracy measured a physician's worth not by lives saved but by the density of his appendices. He was, by that measure, the finest doctor in the orbital fleet. His appendices had appendices.

He sat in the temporary med-bay they'd established in the vault's upper gallery — three folding tables, two diagnostic rigs, and a heating unit that clanked like an old man climbing stairs — and looked at the data one more time before he wrote the report that would end his career.

Thirty-one children. He'd scanned all of them now, twice. The youngest was six. The eldest, Vess, was twelve going on forty, and had corrected his terminology twice during her examination ("I'm not *regressed*, Doctor, I'm *re-sequenced*; there's a difference, and you should know it if you're going to write about us").

She was right. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole problem.

The scans showed what he'd suspected since the first day: telomeres long and laughing, bone density appropriate to age, neural architecture dense in ways no natural child's was — adult scaffolding overgrown with childhood, like ivy on a ruined house. They were not damaged. They were not dying. They were, by every metric his instruments could produce, *viable*. Astonishingly viable. Two of them had better cardiovascular profiles than Kade himself, which stung a little, because Kade ran.

And yet the order had come down from Fleet Medical Command at 0400 ship-time, routed through channels so clean it was almost elegant:

*Assess subjects for post-regression viability. Note: given documented cellular instability in long-duration re-sequencing protocols, nonviability is the anticipated finding.*

Anticipated. There was a word doing a lot of heavy lifting. In twenty years of medicine, Kade had learned to read orders the way other men read weather. This one said: *we have already decided, and we would like your signature under our decision, please and thank you.*

He thought about it for a long time, sitting in the cold with his hands around a cup of coffee that had gone the particular gray flavor of reheated twice. Through the gallery's frost-furred window he could see the ice shelf stretching white to the horizon, and somewhere out there, thirty-one children hiding in tunnels because adults with scanners had come to collect them.

One of them, a small boy named Tam with a cowlick and a habit of narrating his own actions under his breath ("now I'm checking the seal, yep, still sealed"), had asked him yesterday, very seriously, whether the scanner hurt. Kade had said no. Tam had said, "Okay. My dad used to say the truth hurts but needles don't," and then had held perfectly still while Kade drew blood, watching the needle go in with the fascination of a child and the flinch-suppression of a man who'd once spent nine months in deep transit.

Kade set down the coffee. He opened a fresh document.

He wrote the truth.

It took him six hours, because the truth, unlike a lie, required evidence, and he attached all of it: full scan suites, cellular assays, developmental baselines, cognitive evaluations conducted through games he'd disguised as games (the children had seen through this immediately and played along anyway, which somehow made it worse). He wrote that the Meridian's crew were biologically children and experientially something older and stranger, that their re-sequencing appeared deliberate, controlled, and — as far as he could determine — *successful*, and that declaring them nonviable would be not merely false but a specific kind of false that history had names for.

Then he encrypted it. Not through fleet channels. Through the old diplomatic relay codes he'd learned as a resident, back when he'd rotated through the treaty office and thought he'd never use them, the way you learn a language for a country you'll never visit. Addressed to Commander Ilsa Reyes-Okonkwo. Flagged personal. Flagged urgent. Flagged, in the old protocol's idiom, *under my hand alone* — meaning: I am the source, I accept the consequence, do with it what your conscience tells you.

He hesitated over the send key for exactly as long as it took to drink the rest of the terrible coffee.

Then he sent it, closed the terminal, and went to find his coat.

* * *

Going dark, it turned out, was logistically simpler than filing an expense claim.

The vault was a honeycomb. Oscar Underhill knew every corridor, every crawl-space, every maintenance shaft that some mid-century engineer had included with the cheerful paranoia of a man who expected everything to fail eventually. Kade had spent two days being ostentatiously visible — completing scans, requesting supplies, complaining mildly about the coffee (a cover identity he wore comfortably) — while Oscar, in the unhurried way of a man who had spent eleven years thinking in decades, marked the route on a scrap of paper folded into Kade's glove.

"Corridor nine, past the seed drawers, left at the junction with the rusted valve wheel," Oscar had said, as though giving directions to a bakery. "There's a supply cache at the third bulkhead. Blankets, ration bars, a chemical heater. And Anselm—" he'd paused, and his weathered face had done something complicated, "—the heater's temperamental. You have to coax it. Like most things that keep people alive."

Now Kade stood in corridor nine with a duffel bag, a stolen hour of sensor downtime that Oscar had engineered by "accidentally" tripping a breaker, and the peculiar lightness of a man who has just stepped off a cliff and discovered the air holding him up.

Behind him, above him, the fleet's retrieval station hummed. By morning, his absence would be noticed. By afternoon, it would be categorized. Desertion, probably. Or "unauthorized absence pending review," which was desertion wearing a tie.

He found the cache where Oscar promised. Blankets that smelled of diesel and cedar. Ration bars in flavors that suggested optimism rather than experience. The heater, which sputtered when he coaxed it wrong and purred when he coaxed it right, and he talked to it quietly, the way you talk to anything temperamental that's keeping you alive, and after a while he realized he was saying things like "there we go, that's it, good girl," and decided not to examine that too closely.

He'd been in the tunnel perhaps four hours when he heard footsteps — small ones, quick ones — and froze, hand on the heater.

"It's me," whispered a voice. "Tam."

A shape resolved in the dark: a small boy in an oversized thermal coat, holding a flashlight under his chin so it lit his face from below, which made him look like a tiny ghost telling a campfire story.

"How did you find me?"

"Vess guessed. She said you'd take the route closest to the kids' side of the tunnels because you're 'sentimental for a bureaucrat.'" Tam crouched next to him, gravely accepting a ration bar. "Also Oscar told her. But she wanted it to be the sentimental thing."

Kade laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, echoing down the tunnel. "Is she here?"

"Nope. She's running interference topside. She's very good at it. Yesterday she convinced Lieutenant Okafor that the ice tunnels flood at night, which they don't, and he moved his whole patrol schedule." Tam bit into the bar with the satisfaction of a connoisseur. "She says you did a brave stupid thing."

"She would."

"She says brave stupid things are the only kind that work." Tam chewed. "Is it true they're going to say you deserted?"

The word landed in the quiet of the tunnel. Kade found he didn't mind it as much as he'd expected. A deserter, properly understood, was just someone who had finally read the fine print on his own conscience.

"Probably," he said.

Tam considered this with the seriousness of a six-year-old — or whatever Tam was, which was a question Kade had stopped trying to answer — and then nodded once, decisively, the way a board approves a measure. "Vess says that's okay. She says everybody who ever did anything got called something first." He stood, brushing crumbs from his coat with tremendous dignity. "I'm supposed to tell you the heater trick is: tap it twice, then once, then apologize."

"That's not how thermodynamics works."

"Neither is growing backward," said Tam, and went away down the tunnel, flashlight bobbing, narrating under his breath: *now I'm going back, yep, going back.*

Kade sat in the dark with the purring heater and felt, absurdly, less alone than he had in years.

* * *

The fleet's judgment arrived faster than he'd anticipated, which was its own kind of compliment. They'd always been efficient; it was their mercy that lagged.

He heard it secondhand, days later, via Vess, who had a talent for acquiring information the way other children acquired colds — constantly, and from sources nobody could identify. She materialized at the mouth of his tunnel one evening with her hands in her pockets and her twelve-year-old face arranged around twenty years of command experience.

"You've been promoted," she said.

"I've been *what*?"

"Deserted. It's a promotion. You're officially a cautionary tale now. They briefed the whole retrieval team this morning." She recited, in a flat imitation of a briefing officer's cadence: "'Dr. Anselm Kade, formerly of Fleet Medical, is to be considered absent without leave and potentially compromised. Do not engage. Report sightings.' Compromised. That's my favorite part. It sounds like you caught something."

"In a sense," Kade said, "I did."

She sat down beside him without asking, which was, he was learning, how trust worked among the Meridian's children — not requested, simply assumed, the way oxygen was assumed. For a while neither of them spoke. The heater ticked. Somewhere far above, muffled by meters of ice, the fleet's generators droned their endless note.

"They're going to come for us," Vess said at last. Not a question. Not even fear, really. Inventory.

"Yes."

"And the report you sent — did it matter? Did she read it?"

"I don't know. I hope so." He turned the ration bar over in his hands. "Commander Reyes-Okonkwo is a good officer. Good officers are the hardest kind, because they believe in the machine, and the machine has to break a little before they'll admit it's broken."

Vess was quiet for a moment. Then: "Oscar says the same thing about generators."

"Oscar," said Kade, "is the wisest man on this planet, and I include myself, and I'm including myself generously."

That earned him half a smile — the first he'd gotten from her, and he understood its rarity, and pocketed it like currency. Then her face went serious again, and older, the way it did when the adult underneath leaned close to the surface.

"There's something I haven't told you," she said. "About why we came back. About what's behind us." She glanced down the tunnel, toward the younger ones sleeping in the deeper galleries, and lowered her voice. "When I'm ready. Soon. But I need to know — if I tell you, and it's bad, really bad — whose side will you be on?"

Kade thought about the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to her would have been like lying to a lighthouse.

"The side of whoever's standing between the bad thing and those kids," he said. "Including me. Especially me. I've got nowhere else to be. I'm a deserter, remember. I caught it."

She studied him for a long moment with eyes that had seen too much and been given too little time to forget any of it. Then she nodded, once, the board approving its measure.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Bring the heater. What I have to tell you, you're going to want something warm."

She went. Kade sat alone in the humming dark, a branded man in a hole in the ice, and found that his fear had a strange companion riding along with it — something that felt almost like relief. The lie was over. The report was sent. Whatever came tomorrow, he would meet it as what he had finally, at ruinous cost, become: a doctor who told the truth.

He tapped the heater twice, then once, then apologized.

It purred.

Far above, through the ice, through the thin dark air, the beacon turned its slow face to the sky — and Kade, who could not have known it, fell asleep to the rhythm of a signal that was, at that very hour, beginning to change.
