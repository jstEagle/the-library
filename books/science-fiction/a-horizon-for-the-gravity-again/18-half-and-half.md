## 18. Half and Half

The reactor room smelled the way it always did on the worst days: hot metal and old coffee, the two constants of Oscar Underhill's adult life. He'd made the coffee himself, an hour ago, in the little alcove off the control room, and he'd made it strong enough to strip paint, because if this was going to be the last pot he ever brewed, he wasn't going to drink it like a coward.

"Okay," he said to the room, to the reactor, to the two sealed wings of sleeping humanity stacked beneath his feet like the world's most consequential basement. "Let's do the arithmetic one more time. Slowly. Out loud. Because apparently I only believe things when I hear my own voice say them."

Nobody answered. Eleven years of practice had made him very good at both halves of a conversation.

The numbers hadn't changed since the last time he'd run them, or the time before that, or the four times after Kade had fallen asleep against the wall with a tablet still glowing on his chest. The reactor could fully revive one wing — ten thousand sleepers, thawed, stabilized, walked out into the light. Or it could fully revive the other. Doing both at full power was like asking a single candle to light two houses. The fleet's engineers had said so in language so polite it took a moment to realize they were telling him to choose which half of humanity got to exist.

Command had chosen Wing A. Fleet loyalists. The people who had signed the orders, funded the orders, and in at least a few cases, Oscar suspected, personally benefited from the orders.

He'd spent most of the night being angry about that, and then, somewhere around three in the morning, standing in the corridor with his breath fogging and the beacon humming overhead like a patient insect, the anger had done what anger does when you give it enough time and enough cold: it had settled, and cooled, and turned into something more useful.

A plan.

Not a good plan. A plan the way a raft is a boat — technically seaworthy, aesthetically questionable, and dependent on everything going exactly right, which nothing ever did. But it was his, and it was better than the choice he'd been handed, and after eleven years of winding a beacon nobody answered, Oscar Underhill had developed a very specific allergy to other people telling him what the signal was for.

* * *

Vess found him at dawn, or what passed for dawn down here, which was the moment Oscar flipped the corridor lights from night-red to work-white and pretended the sun was a thing that still happened to him personally.

The boy — twelve years old, sixty years of memory folded up inside him like a letter in a too-small envelope — stood in the doorway with his arms crossed, wearing an adult's parka rolled at the cuffs, and looked at the schematic Oscar had projected onto the wall.

"That's wrong," Vess said.

"Good morning to you too."

"You've drawn the stagger wrong. The second wave can't start until the first wave's metabolic load drops below forty percent. You have them overlapping." Vess stepped forward and traced the timeline with a finger, and Oscar felt the old ache he always felt watching these children — the way their small hands moved with adult certainty, the way the gesture looked borrowed from someone taller.

"It overlaps on purpose," Oscar said. "The overlap is the whole point. If I wait for a clean handoff, the second wave finishes too late. Whatever's coming up that wake is going to be in sensor range in—" he checked the chronometer he'd taped to the wall because the built-in one had died in year four and he'd grown fond of the tape, "—eleven hours and change. I need half of both wings awake and vertical before that. The overlap buys me three hours."

"It buys you a reactor excursion."

"It buys me a *controlled* reactor excursion. There's a difference. One of them has a plan attached."

Vess looked at him. It was the look, Oscar had learned, of a twelve-year-old who remembered being forty — the look of someone doing the math on Oscar's face and not liking the total.

"You're not in the revival order," Vess said.

It wasn't a question. Of course it wasn't a question. The boy had read the manifest three times, and Oscar's name appeared in it exactly once, in the reserve column, the little allocation of reactor years set aside for the vault's caretaker on the theory — someone's theory, decades ago — that the man who kept the lights on deserved a thaw of his own someday.

"I reallocated it," Oscar said. "This morning. While the coffee was brewing."

"To which wing?"

"Both. Split it. Half to A, half to B." He shrugged, as if he were describing moving a chair. "It was just sitting there. Reserve allocations are like umbrellas. If you're not using them, somebody should be."

"Oscar."

"It's fine."

"It's *not* fine, that's your—"

"It's a number in a column, Vess." He said it gently, and he meant it gently, and he was surprised by how gently it came out, how little of him wanted to argue. That was the strange thing about the decision. He'd expected it to feel like standing on a ledge. Instead it felt like setting down a pack he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten the shape of his own shoulders. "Eleven years I've been keeping a place warm for ten thousand people I've never met. That was the job. Nobody asked me if I wanted a place kept warm for me too. And honestly? I've had my thaw. It was cold and it was lonely and it smelled like diesel, but it was mine, and I'm — I'm not giving anything up. I'm just choosing where it goes."

Vess stared at him for a long moment. Then, in a voice that cracked exactly the way a twelve-year-old's voice should and not at all the way the man inside it wanted it to:

"You sound like Halvorsen."

Oscar's chest did something complicated. Halvorsen. The predecessor. The man whose logbook he'd spent a month decoding, whose handwriting he knew better than his own, who had chosen exile rather than report what the Meridian had become — and who had, in his final encrypted entry, written one line that Oscar had copied out and taped inside his locker: *A caretaker's arithmetic is simple. You count what needs keeping, and you put yourself last on the list, and you find, to your surprise, that the list is enough.*

"Yeah," Oscar said. "I've been reading his mail."

"He'd have liked you."

"He'd have thought I was an idiot who talks to generators."

"He thought *that* about himself," Vess said, and for a second — just a second — the boy smiled, and it was an old man's smile on a child's face, and Oscar had to look away at the schematic until his eyes behaved.

* * *

By eight, the control room had a population. Kade arrived first, hollow-eyed, moving like a man who had learned in the last two weeks that sleep was a rumor spread by people with clear consciences. Then came three of the older children — Priya, ten, who remembered being a propulsion engineer and had opinions about Oscar's reactor management that she expressed with devastating politeness; Tomas, nine, who remembered being a doctor and had appointed himself the revival's medical authority by the simple method of knowing more medicine than everyone else present; and little Anouk, seven, who remembered nothing much at all except that she'd been someone's daughter, and who had appointed herself Oscar's shadow, on the theory — sound, in Oscar's opinion — that he was where the snacks lived.

They set up in the control room like it was a kitchen on a busy morning. Priya took the reactor console and immediately began disapproving of it. Tomas laid out the medical triage boards with the grim competence of someone who had done this before, in another body, in another life. Anouk sat on a crate and sorted Oscar's spare fuses into piles by color, which was not useful, and which Oscar would have defended with his life.

"Explain it again," Kade said. "Slowly. I want to hear how insane it is in your own words."

"It's not insane. It's *ambitious*." Oscar pulled up the master timeline, the one with his hand-drawn annotations overlapping the fleet's pristine original like graffiti over a legal document. "The fleet's plan: full power to Wing A, ten thousand sleepers, clean revival, done in fourteen hours. My plan: we don't have fourteen hours, and I'm not picking a wing. So we split the load. First wave, half of each wing — five thousand from A, five thousand from B — staggered in clusters so the thermal and metabolic draw never peaks all at once. As each cluster stabilizes, its draw drops, and we feed the surplus into the next cluster. Rolling revival. A wave, then B, then A, then B, alternating, so neither wing ever wakes alone and neither wing waits."

"And the reactor?"

"The reactor is going to run at a hundred and twelve percent of rated output for approximately nine hours." He said it lightly. He'd practiced saying it lightly in the mirror, and the mirror had not been fooled either. "Priya says the containment margins can absorb it."

"Priya says the containment margins can absorb it *if* the coolant loops hold, *if* the secondary pump doesn't seize, and *if* nobody sneezes near the breaker panel," Priya said, not looking up from her console. "Also Priya says you should stop referring to Priya in the third person, it's creepy when a ten-year-old does it."

"Noted."

"And the second wave?" Kade asked. "The sleepers who don't make the first pass?"

"They wake later. Days later, maybe a week. The reactor recovers, cools down, and we run the second half the same way." Oscar tapped the far end of the timeline. "It's a promise, not a miracle. I can't wake everyone before that thing gets here. But I can wake enough. Five thousand people who can think, and carry, and dig, and fight, and plant — split evenly between the people who built the fleet and the people the fleet abandoned. Nobody wakes up to find out their side won. Everybody wakes up to find out they're needed."

The room went quiet. Even Anouk stopped sorting fuses.

"That's not what command ordered," Kade said.

"No."

"It's not even close to what command ordered."

"I'm aware."

Kade rubbed his face with both hands. When he lowered them, he looked older than his service record, and he almost smiled. "You know they'll court-martial you for this. If there's still a fleet to court-martial anyone."

"There's a reserve allocation with my name on it," Oscar said. "They can have the trial in it."

* * *

They started at 0900. Oscar threw the first breaker himself, because some things you don't delegate, and felt the reactor answer under his hands — a deep, subterranean shift in the building's hum, the sound of a sleeping animal rolling over and deciding, reluctantly, to get up.

The first cluster of Wing A began to thaw.

He watched it on the medical displays with Tomas standing on a chair beside him, both of them silent, watching ten heartbeats flicker from flat stillness into slow, staggering rhythm. Ten people who had been frozen for twenty years, waking into a world that had gone on without them, warmed by a plan their own command would have forbidden.

"Cluster two, ready when you are," Priya called from the reactor console.

"Bring it up. Slowly. Like you're apologizing to it."

"I'm always apologizing to machinery. It's the only relationship advice I remember from being thirty-four."

Cluster two came online. Then cluster three, the first from Wing B — the Meridian's families, the exiled, the forgotten — and Oscar stood in front of that display a long moment, watching strangers' hearts learn to beat again, and let himself feel it. Not the plan, not the numbers. Just this: someone's mother, warming up.

"Oscar." Anouk tugged his sleeve. She held up two fuses, one red, one blue. "Which one is the important one?"

"The red one."

"Why?"

"Because if it goes, everything stops."

She considered this with the gravity of a seven-year-old, then tucked the red fuse into her pocket, next to the crackers she'd been hoarding since Tuesday, and gave him a nod that said the fuse was now under her personal protection. Oscar decided this was the best security arrangement the vault had ever had.

* * *

The trouble came at hour five, because trouble always comes at hour five, or at least it always came at hour five in Oscar's experience, and he had a great deal of hour-five experience.

The secondary pump — Priya's second *if* — began to seize. A rising whine from deep in the vault's bones, a temperature curve bending the wrong way, and Priya's voice cracking across the comms with a ten-year-old's pitch and a veteran engineer's calm: "Coolant loop two is losing pressure. If it drops below the line, I have to shed a cluster, and the cluster I'd shed is B-four."

B-four. The Meridian's families. The wing the fleet had already written off once.

"Options," Oscar said.

"Shed the cluster, or I go in and manually clear the pump intake, and the intake is behind the coolant jacket, and the jacket is at eighty degrees, and I am ten."

"I'll go," Oscar said.

"You'll take four minutes to suit up and it's a two-minute problem."

"I'll run."

"You'll run *in a suit*, which is a comedy routine, not a solution." A pause. Static. Then, quieter: "Send Kade. He's ex-fleet. Fleet people are trained for hot work. It's the one thing they're good at."

Kade was already moving. He didn't argue, didn't ask permission, just grabbed the suit Oscar had left prepped by the hatch — because of course Oscar had left a suit prepped, he'd been expecting hour five since midnight — and said, "Keep the timeline honest, Underhill. I want to wake up to a completed plan and a full apology."

He was gone into the hot dark, and for eleven minutes Oscar stood at the console watching a pressure gauge with his whole soul, and Anouk stood beside him holding the red fuse up like a lantern, and Priya called adjustments in a steady voice while her hands, on the display feed, shook.

At minute eleven, the pressure gauge climbed. At minute thirteen, Kade's voice came through the comm, breathless and singed around the edges: "Intake's clear. There was a frost plug the size of a fist. Also I have burned my left eyebrow off and I want it noted in the log that I looked distinguished before."

"Noted," Oscar said, and sat down on the floor, because his legs had filed a formal complaint.

B-four held. The wave rolled on.

* * *

By hour nine, five thousand people were awake.

Not awake awake — not yet. They were in the recovery wards, wrapped in blankets, drinking the broth Tomas's team brewed by the gallon, blinking at the ceiling with the dazed expression of people who had gone to sleep in one century and woken in another. Oscar walked the wards once, because he needed to see them, needed it the way a man who'd wound a beacon for eleven years needed to see, finally, what the signal was for.

They looked like people. That was the thing that undid him. He'd spent a decade imagining ten thousand sleepers as a concept, a responsibility, a number in a column, and here they were — a woman with frost-pale eyebrows asking for coffee, an old man crying without noise, a teenager from Wing B staring at a teenager from Wing A across the ward and neither of them knowing yet which side of the line the other had been born on.

An elderly woman caught his sleeve as he passed. "You're the caretaker," she said. Her voice was rust. "Halvorsen's successor."

"That's me."

"He wrote about you. In the last years. Said there'd be someone after him." She looked at him with eyes that had been closed for twenty years and were, impossibly, kind. "He said whoever it was would be stubborn. He said it like a compliment."

"He was right on both counts," Oscar said, and had to keep walking, because the alternative was standing in a ward full of resurrected strangers and coming apart at the seams, and there wasn't time. There was never time. There were eleven hours once, and now there were two, and the chronometer taped to the wall did not care about reunions.

* * *

He found Vess in the beacon room, of all places. The boy stood at the base of the array housing, looking up at the machinery the way other children looked up at church ceilings.

"It's louder," Vess said. "Since the revivals started. Can you hear it? It's like it's excited."

"It's drawing more power. Everything's drawing more power. The beacon, the reactor, my patience." Oscar came to stand beside him. Above them, through the reinforced glass of the array housing, the great antenna rings turned in their slow nightly rotation — the rotation nobody had answered for eleven years, and which now, apparently, something out on the ice was answering back, something vast and patient and coming up the Meridian's wake like a debt collector.

"Two hours," Vess said.

"Give or take."

"The second wave. The other five thousand. They'll wake after it's here."

"After it's *past*," Oscar said. "That's the plan. We hold the line with five thousand and a beacon, and when it's gone, we wake the rest, and everybody gets to be alive for the rebuilding." He looked down at the boy. "You don't have to be in the line, Vess. Any of you. The fleet would call you material witnesses, and I'm calling you children, and I know which definition I like better."

Vess didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was very quiet.

"You split your allocation. Half and half. A and B." He was still looking up at the array. "Did you split yourself too? Or did you just delete yourself?"

Oscar considered lying. He was, he reflected, genuinely bad at it — eleven years without an audience had atrophied the muscle.

"I deleted myself," he said. "It was easier than the math."

The boy turned and looked at him then, and Oscar saw that Vess was crying, silently, in the way of children who have learned that crying costs something, and that behind the tears was the old man's fury, and behind the fury was the old man's grief, and behind all of it — this was the part that got Oscar, the part he'd carry into whatever came next — was the old man's *gratitude*, which was the heaviest thing a person could hand you.

"Halvorsen put himself last on the list too," Vess said. "And the list wasn't enough. It was never enough. That's the secret nobody writes in the logbooks."

"I know," Oscar said. "But it's a good list. And it's mine. And I got to choose." He put a hand on the boy's shoulder, small under his palm, carrying sixty years. "That's more than most people ever get, Vess. Most people never get to choose where they're spent. I got to choose, and I chose this, and I'd choose it again in front of a tribunal, a reactor excursion, and whatever's walking up that ice shelf. Now come on. We've got two hours, five thousand groggy strangers, thirty kids, one beacon, and a thing the size of a weather system incoming. I need you on the array controls, because you're the only person here who remembers the Meridian's original signal protocols, and I need that thing to see exactly the horizon we want it to see."

Vess wiped his face with his sleeve, and nodded, and for one moment he was just a twelve-year-old, scared and brave in the same breath, which Oscar privately considered the most honest combination a human being could manage.

They climbed the stairs to the array controls together. Below them, the vault hummed with five thousand new heartbeats. Above them, the rings turned, patient as ever, toward the dark.

On the far edge of the sensor display, at the very limit of resolution, something enormous adjusted its course — and began, deliberately, to follow the light.
