## 19. What Followed Them Home

The thing came out of the dark the way weather comes over a mountain — not fast, not slow, just inevitable.

Vess felt it before anyone told her. She was in the mess hall teaching a woman named Petra Halvorsen how to open a ration tin — Petra had been asleep for nineteen years and her hands remembered everything except pull-tabs — when the floor hummed a note Vess's bones recognized from the deep dark between stars. She set down her tea.

"It's here," she said.

Petra looked up, tin halfway open, beans glinting. "What's here?"

"The reason we became children." Vess was already moving. "Finish your beans. You'll want the strength."

* * *

Oscar heard it as silence.

For eleven years the vault had sung to him — generators, fans, the tick of frost expanding in old welds, the beacon's patient pulse overhead like a heart he'd learned to sleep against. Now every instrument on his board went quiet at once, the way birds go quiet before a storm, and then the long-range array painted it: a mass at the edge of sensor range, decelerating, vast beyond the word's usual meaning. Not a ship. Ships had edges. This had *presence*, a smear of wrongness against the starfield that the computers kept trying to file under debris and kept apologizing for.

"Give me something I can say out loud," Oscar muttered at the screen.

The screen declined.

Behind him, the corridor filled with people. That was still new — after eleven years of his own footsteps, the sound of forty-odd humans moving through his halls made him feel like a lighthouse keeper whose lamp had attracted a town. Half of them were adults in thermal wraps, woken twelve hours ago from Wing A and Wing B both, gray-faced and staggering, blinking at the world like cats presented with an unexpected bathtub. The other half were children in adult-sized uniforms who moved through them with the brisk competence of nurses on rounds.

"Underhill." Commander Reyes-Okonkwo's voice cut through the shuffle. She looked like she hadn't slept since the sabotage, which was true, and like she'd decided to be furious about it later, which was also true. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a beacon," Oscar said. "And a theory."

"I love theories," said Dr. Anselm Kade, arriving with a med kit and the particular calm of a man who'd already been branded a deserter and found it freeing. "Tell us the theory."

Oscar pulled up the sensor feed. The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet around bad news.

"It followed the Meridian home," he said. "It's been following the wake for twenty years. It's drawn to signals — that's how it hunts, that's what Vess remembers, it finds things by their noise. And for eleven years—" He stopped. Swallowed. "For eleven years, I've been winding that array every night. Calling. I thought I was calling people home."

"You were," Vess said quietly. "We came."

"And so did it."

Kade stepped closer to the screen. "So the beacon is a dinner bell."

"The beacon is a dinner bell," Oscar agreed. "Which means the beacon is also a trap door. It's drawn to signal. Signal is all it sees. If we can make the beacon sing loud enough, bright enough, *elsewhere* enough—"

"—it chases the song instead of the singers," Ilsa finished. Her jaw worked. "You want to blind a thing the size of a moon using a radio antenna and a lie."

"I've been lying to myself about this place for eleven years, Commander. I'm very practiced."

Someone laughed — a ragged, exhausted laugh, one of the freshly woken, a broad man with Wing B's manifest number tattooed on his wrist. Meridian family. He caught Oscar looking and shrugged. "Sorry. It's just — I went to sleep on a ship full of strangers and woke up in a hole in the ice being saved by a librarian and thirty kids. My humor's adjusting."

"Librarian?" Oscar said.

"Beacon keeper. Same thing. You keep the light on and hope somebody reads you."

* * *

They worked through the night, and the night worked back.

The problem was simple and enormous: the entity would arrive within hours, and everything they loved was sitting directly beneath the loudest signal on the planet. The solution was almost as simple and nearly as enormous — split the beacon's pattern, cast a false horizon out across the ice fields, a phantom signal source two hundred kilometers east, and hold it there, steady and delicious, while the real array went dark and quiet as a held breath.

Holding it was the hard part. The false pattern couldn't run on automation; the entity had spent twenty years listening to automated signals and knew their texture the way a customs officer knows forged stamps. It needed a hand on the wheel. A human rhythm. Imperfect, alive, constant.

It needed someone who'd been keeping the light for eleven years.

"We'll rotate," Kade said. "Four-hour shifts at the array. I'll take first—"

"No," said six voices at once, and then everyone started talking.

In the end it was Ilsa who cut through it, because cutting through things was her gift and her curse. "Nobody takes shifts alone. Nobody volunteers alone. We do this the way a crew does it — everybody carries a piece." She looked around the operations room: fleet officers still in their uniforms, colonists in donated thermals, children rolling cables twice their height. "Half of you were asleep an hour ago. Half of you are nine years old. None of you owe each other anything." She paused. "That ends now. We're one crew. Act like it."

A small girl named Odile — seven years old, or sixty, depending on which life you counted — raised her hand. "I know the cable routing better than anybody. I crawled it during the blackout."

"So did I," said the broad man with the wrist tattoo. "Different blackout. Nineteen years ago. Ship's systems, Meridian."

Odile considered him with the gravity of a much older person, which she was. "Show me yours, I'll show you mine."

"That," Kade murmured to Oscar, "is either the sweetest or most unsettling thing I've ever heard."

"Both," Oscar said. "Get used to both."

* * *

The entity arrived at 0340 local, and it did not arrive like a ship. It arrived like a change in pressure, like the moment before lightning, like a thought you'd been avoiding finally saying its name.

Every screen showed it differently. The optical feeds showed a darkness with structure — vast, slow-turning, geometry that hurt to track. The thermal feeds showed cold where there should have been nothing at all, a hole in the warmth of space shaped like intention. Vess stood in front of the main display with her arms crossed, twelve years old and ancient, and felt the fever-memory rise in her again: *the thing that watched, the thing that waited, the reason they'd poured themselves backward into new bodies hoping it wouldn't recognize them.*

It had recognized them anyway. It had simply been patient. Patience, Vess understood now, was the whole of what it was.

"It's slowing," she reported. Her voice didn't shake, and she was privately proud of that. "It's reading the beacon."

On the eastern ice field, two hundred kilometers away, the false horizon burned. Oscar sat at the array with Kade beside him and Odile on the cable board, feeding power along routes only a child who'd crawled those tunnels could have mapped, and he sent the lie out into the dark with both hands: *here, here, here, come here, everything you want is here.*

The entity turned.

The room exhaled — and then didn't inhale again, because the thing moved toward the false signal with a speed that no moon should have, and the ice shelf under the vault sang a low note through everyone's boots.

"It bought it," Kade whispered. "It actually—"

"Don't jinx it," said Odile, not looking up from her board.

* * *

But patience cuts both ways.

At 0515 the entity reached the coordinates of the false horizon and found nothing there, and it hung above the empty ice for four minutes — four minutes in which nobody in the vault breathed, moved, or in one case blinked — and then it began, slowly, terribly, to listen harder.

"It knows," Vess said. "It's sweeping. It's going to find the real signal underneath."

"The real signal's dark," Oscar said. "I killed the broadcast."

"Not the beacon." Vess pointed at the sensor trace, at the faint rhythmic pulse beneath the noise floor. "The backup oscillator. It never stopped. It's been pinging every ninety seconds since before you were born — it's hardwired, it's the fail-safe, it can't be shut down without—"

"Without standing at the array and holding the cutoff manually," Oscar finished. He looked at the distance to the array bay. Forty meters. He looked at his hands, which had wound that beacon through eleven winters and knew every switch in the dark. "Well," he said, in the mild tone he used for diesel maintenance and other insoluble problems, "someone should probably see to that."

"I'll go," said three people.

"You don't know the panel," Oscar said. "Eleven years. Every switch in the dark. That's me." He was already pulling on his outer gear, and the strange thing — the thing he'd examine later, if there was a later — was how calm he felt. How *right*. Eleven years of devotion hollowed into habit, and now, at the end, the habit filled itself back up with meaning. "Kade, keep the false horizon singing as long as you can. Vess—"

"I know," Vess said. "Keep them together. Keep them working." She grabbed his sleeve as he passed. "Oscar. Come back."

He smiled at her, and it was the first fully honest smile he'd given anyone in eleven years. "Wind me a welcome if I'm late."

* * *

What happened next, the survivors would tell in fragments for years, because no single person saw all of it.

They saw the broad man from Wing B — Tomas, his name was Tomas — carrying cable alongside a nine-year-old fleet cadet, arguing cheerfully about splice technique, two halves of a divided humanity discovering they spoke the same language after all.

They saw Ilsa Reyes-Okonkwo, commander of the fleet that had exiled the Meridian, kneeling in an ice tunnel helping a regressed child calibrate a jammer, her rank insignia frosted over, her hands steady.

They saw the entity sweep closer, closer, its attention dragging across the ice like a searchlight, and they saw the false horizon flicker as Kade pushed the transmitters past every rating plate, buying seconds with machinery, spending the equipment like blood.

And they saw Oscar Underhill walk into the array bay, plant himself in front of the backup oscillator like a man keeping an appointment eleven years overdue, and put his hands on the cutoff.

The pulse died. The real signal went silent. The vault, the sleepers, the children, the fleet — all of it dropped out of the universe's hearing, small and dark and safe.

And the entity, cheated of its dinner bell, hung above the ice a long moment —

— and then turned, slow and vast, toward the last voice still singing in the dark: the false horizon, burning bravely on the eastern field, held alive by Kade's dying transmitters and, beneath them, by the one man who had always known that a beacon is not a machine.

It is a promise. And promises need keeping.

Oscar watched the great shape swing away from everything he loved and begin to move toward the light, and he settled into the operator's chair that fit him like an old coat, and he began, with steady hands, to wind the pattern brighter.
