## 14. Waking the Witness

The first thing Sef Okonkwo understood about being alive again was that it hurt in a way he had no language for yet.

Not the body—that came later, a catalog of complaints his cells would file over the coming days—but something underneath the body, some deep structural grievance, as if the machinery of him had been taken apart and reassembled by hands that worked fast and didn't care about fit. He was aware of cold first. Then of light, filtered amber through closed lids. Then of a sound: a slow rhythmic hiss, like breath drawn through a long tube, and he thought absurdly, *I am breathing through a tube, I am still in the pod, they've woken me wrong—*

Then a voice.

"—pressure's climbing. He's climbing. Sef? Can you hear me? Sef Okonkwo."

He knew that voice. He had known it across forty years and the width of a graveyard of ships. He dragged his eyes open and the amber light resolved into a med-bay that was too small and too old and too clean all at once, and Dara Voss was leaning over him with her face doing something he had never seen it do, which was hope without armor.

"Dara," he said. His voice came out as gravel poured through a sieve. "You look terrible."

"You look worse." Her laugh cracked in the middle. She pressed her forehead briefly to his knuckles, an intimacy she would deny under oath, and then stepped back and became a captain again, brisk, checking readouts he couldn't see. "You were under eleven years. Eleven years, Sef. I want you to lie there and be amazed at that before you start asking questions."

"Eleven—" The number landed somewhere in his chest and sat there, heavy as ballast. He did the arithmetic the way you do it when you already know the answer and are only confirming the shape of the loss. *The Kestrel went down sixty-three years ago. I boarded the Corvid's Rest fifty-two years after that.* No. That wasn't right either. Time in cryo was a corridor with the doors painted on. "How long since you found me?"

"Fourteen months. We ran you down slow. Your body was—" She stopped herself. He watched her decide not to say what his body was. "The doctor who put you under built the protocol well. Whoever she was."

"She was my wife," Sef said, and watched Dara absorb that, another small stone added to the cairn of things she'd carried for him without being asked. "Nadene. She was the Kestrel's medical officer. She wasn't aboard when—" He stopped. Some sentences have edges. "She put me in the pod herself. On the station. Before."

*Before* hung in the room. Dara let it hang. Somewhere beyond the bulkhead a pump cycled, patient as a tide.

A second figure stood near the foot of the bed—a woman he hadn't noticed, because she stood the way people stand when they're trying to take up less space than they occupy. Dark-haired, mid-thirties maybe, wearing the plain grey coveralls of a caretaker, with a tablet held against her chest like a shield or a prayer book. She was looking at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors across a lifetime: the look of someone standing in front of a door they'd been told never to open, holding a key they weren't sure they wanted to use.

"You're the keeper," he said. His throat was sand but the words came. "Yarrow. The beacon says Yarrow."

The woman blinked. "You know the beacon?"

"I helped build its predecessor," Sef said. "Meridian Station, before the vault went autonomous. I was twenty-six years old and I thought I was building a lighthouse." He tried to sit up and his body informed him, comprehensively, that this was not among today's services offered. Dara eased him back with one hand flat on his sternum, gentle and immovable.

"Lena Yarrow," the woman said. "My mother kept the vault before me. And her mother before her." A pause, and then, with the care of someone stepping onto ice: "Sef Okonkwo. You're listed on a manifest as crew of the Kestrel. The fleet registry says the Kestrel never flew."

"The fleet registry," Sef said, "says a great many things."

* * *

They gave him a day before they gave him questions, and he was grateful, because the day was needed for inventory.

His body had been sixty-one when Nadene sealed the pod. It was sixty-one now, biologically, but cryo is not preservation—it is deferral—and the deferral had been paid for out of accounts he hadn't known he held. There was a tremor in his left hand that hadn't been there. There were gray-white streaks in his vision when he looked at bright surfaces, like frost on glass, and when he mentioned them Lena Yarrow went very still and said, quietly, "Radiation exposure. Recorder-linked. Dara told me," and he understood that these two women had already had conversations about him, conversations conducted in the grammar of prognosis, and that he was a man with a date attached.

Fine. He had been a man with a date attached for fifty-two years. Everyone was; his was just better documented.

What the day was actually for was memory. Sleep took him and returned him and each time it returned him it brought cargo. The smell of the Kestrel's galley—scorched protein and Toma's cardamom tea, hoarded past all regulation. The sound of the hull singing in a hard burn, that low harmonic the engineers swore was normal and everyone else privately called the ship's voice. Nadene's hands, quick and certain, taping a sensor lead to his forearm while she talked about the colony allocation data as though discussing weather, because talking about it plainly had become dangerous even in whispers, even aboard their own ship, and they had all learned to speak in weather.

By the second evening, when Lena came to his bedside with a recorder running openly in her hand—she asked permission first, which he noted, and appreciated, and would remember—he was ready.

"Tell me what you know," she said. "From the beginning. Whatever the beginning is."

Sef looked at the ceiling of the little med-bay, at the hairline seam where two panels met imperfectly, and found the beginning the way you find a word on the tip of your tongue: not by reaching for it but by going quiet enough to let it come.

"The beginning," he said, "is a census."

* * *

"You'll have learned the founding story the way everyone learns it," he said. "The Exodus Fleet, nine ships, four hundred thousand souls, fleeing the Collapse with seed banks and genome libraries and a charter signed by all nine captains. The charter divided the destination system fairly. Population-weighted allocation: each ship's descendants would receive habitable ground in proportion to the lives aboard. One fleet, one future, shares distributed by headcount. It's carved above the assembly hall on Concordance Prime. I've seen it. It's very moving."

Lena's tablet was steady in her lap. "And?"

"And it depended entirely on the counts." He shifted against the pillows; his body objected; he continued. "Every allocation, every share of every world, keyed to the population manifest filed at departure. Four hundred thousand was the official number. But numbers are written by people, and people had reasons. If your ship reported more souls than it carried, your descendants would inherit more ground than they'd earned. Falsify the census once, at the beginning, and the falsification compounds for generations—an entire civilization's real estate built on a rounding error that someone chose."

"And someone chose," Lena said. Not a question. She had spent fourteen months with a flight recorder in a hold; she had opened a bay full of dead crew's belongings. She was ahead of him, and he loved her a little for making him say it anyway, because testimony that isn't spoken aloud isn't testimony. It's just weight.

"Three ships falsified," Sef said. "Concordance itself, the Foundry flagship Meridian's Pride, and one other—the records were scrubbed so thoroughly we never confirmed the third with certainty, though Ade Chukwu on navigation had a theory involving the Compact's progenitor vessel that he defended to the last week of his life. They inflated their manifests. The six honest ships—including mine, including the Kestrel—were allocated correspondingly less. Not slightly less. Substantially. Whole continents' worth of difference, spread across six bloodlines, forever."

The pump beyond the bulkhead cycled. Lena wrote nothing; the recorder wrote everything.

"How do you falsify a census?" she asked. "On a fleet? People notice."

"People were dying," Sef said. "It was the Collapse. Ships were taking damage, losing compartments, losing contact for weeks. The manifests were reconstructed afterward—from partial logs, from survivors' accounts, from whatever each captain's quartermaster could assemble. Chaos is a wonderful ledger. You can write anything in it." He paused, and the frost-streaked vision swam, and he waited for it to clear. "But here is what matters, Keeper. Here is why I am lying in this bed instead of dust. A census can be forged, but a fleet leaves shadows. Fuel consumption. Life support draw. Berth allocations. Waste processing tonnage. You cannot feed three hundred thousand people and log consumables for three hundred eighty thousand for eleven years without leaving a signature. The discrepancy is recoverable. It was always recoverable. It was only ever a question of whether anyone would go looking."

"Ade Chukwu went looking," Lena said softly.

"Ade Chukwu went looking." Sef closed his eyes and let the man arrive whole: broad, loud, laugh lines deep as plow furrows, a navigator who treated mathematics as a moral discipline. "He was our senior flight systems officer and he had a hobby that made the rest of us nervous, which was auditing the fleet. For pleasure. He pulled consumption logs from public archive buoys—nothing classified, nothing stolen, just patience—and he built a model over nine years, off-duty, the way other men build model ships. And the model said the Concordance manifest was inflated by roughly nineteen percent. Nineteen percent, Keeper. Nineteen percent of the habitable surface of every allocated world, held by one faction on the strength of a lie, enforced ever after by the truce that the lie required."

"The truce," Lena said. "The Concordance truce. The one that expires in ninety days."

"The same. Renewed every cycle, generation after generation, and every renewal depends on nobody asking why the original allocation stands. Because the moment the census fraud becomes public, the allocation becomes theft, and theft demands restitution, and restitution means the Concordance and the Foundry surrender ground their people have farmed and buried their dead on for two hundred years." He opened his eyes and found her watching him with an intensity that reminded him painfully of Nadene. "You cannot un-ring that bell gently. Which is why, when the Kestrel found the proof, the answer was not negotiation."

"It was three ships and a firing solution."

"Three Concordance cruisers," Sef said, "and an order to heave to and surrender all navigation data, and Captain Ede refusing—not out of heroism, though she was heroic, but out of procedure. Fleet law said civilian survey vessels could not be boarded outside port authority. She cited the law. She genuinely believed, in those last minutes, that citing the law would protect us." His voice did something he didn't authorize; he let it. "Two hundred and fourteen people. The youngest was nine. The Okuda family had their children with them because the survey rotation allowed dependents that cycle. I was on the station at Meridian coordinating the sample transfer, which is the only reason I am speaking to you, and I have spent fifty-two years deciding whether that makes me a survivor or a deserter, and I will tell you now what I decided: it makes me a witness, and witnesses don't get the luxury of the other question."

Silence. Then Lena said, "The charter data. The census evidence. Where was it?"

"Half in Ade's model, half in the Kestrel's own survey cache—we'd independently confirmed the fuel-consumption signature against three deep-archived logistics buoys. The complete package was aboard the ship when she burned. Which is why the Concordance didn't just destroy us and move on. They swept the debris field for eleven days. My wife watched them do it from the station, counting sweep patterns, logging hull-matching signatures, building the case that would get her killed later." He met Lena's eyes. "Your mother received Nadene's final transmission, Keeper. Did you know that?"

Lena had gone very pale. "No."

"'To the keeper of Meridian,'" Sef recited, and fifty-two years fell away and he was reading it off a screen in a station office that smelled of recycled air and grief. "'Evidence follows under seal. I cannot protect it and my child both. Choose for me.' And beneath it, coordinates, and a lock code, and a single line of instruction: *Bay Seven. Until someone comes asking.*"

Lena's hand had risen unconsciously to her collarbone. "Bay Seven," she whispered. "My mother's code opens it. It's been sealed my whole tenure. It's full of—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Uniforms. Letters. A child's drawing. All tagged KESTREL CREW."

"Nadene sent the effects ahead on a supply drone, weeks before the end, when she understood how it would finish," Sef said. "She split the evidence. The data cache went into the recorder and the recorder went into the wreck, hidden in a coolant shroud, because she reasoned—correctly—that the Concordance would search the station and the drone lanes and never stop searching, but a destroyed ship's black box, buried in a debris field they believed they'd stripped, would sit untouched until someone stubborn enough came along." A dry almost-laugh moved through him. "Stubborn enough. Voss, show her the recorder. Show her what my wife bought with her life's last competence."

Dara, who had been standing silent by the door for some minutes, pushed off from the frame. "Already showed the keeper the hull signature," she said. "Haven't played it. Was waiting for you." Her jaw worked. "Waiting to see if you'd survive the waking. Didn't want to hand you your own deathbed before I knew you'd have one."

"Then play it tomorrow," Sef said. "Tonight, I have one more thing to give the keeper, and it isn't data."

* * *

They turned the recorder off for it. This part, he wanted in living memory only.

"I'm dying," he said, without ceremony, because ceremony would only make it harder for her. "Weeks, the doctors think—your vault's autodoc is thorough but it isn't a miracle. Radiation does what it does. I accepted the odds when I asked Voss to bring me home, and I want to be precise about the word: home. Not the fleet. Here. This vault. Because I finally understand what your mother built, Lena Yarrow, and I think maybe you're starting to, and I need you to hear it from someone who was there at the laying of the foundation."

He gathered breath. His lungs priced it steeply.

"When I was twenty-six, helping install the beacon array, I asked Ilsa Yarrow—your grandmother, then, though she was just the junior keeper with sawdust in her hair—why a seed vault needed a transmitter at all. Seeds don't call for help. And she said something I didn't understand for half a century. She said: 'A vault isn't for keeping things safe from the world. It's for keeping things safe *for* the world. The beacon is the promise that someday we'll open the door.'" His tremor had reached his voice now; he let it shake. "Your grandmother built a promise. Your mother inherited it and, God forgive her, she kept it the only way she could see—sealed, silent, waiting. Bay Seven wasn't a betrayal, Keeper. It was a promise she didn't feel strong enough to keep, stored beside the ones she could. And now the door is opening, and the fleet's factions are coming to this rock inside ninety days to renew a truce that is load-bearing for a massacre, and everything—everything—depends on what gets said, and by whom, and whether it's said *once, to everyone, at the same time,* or fed piecemeal to whoever offers the best price."

Lena was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was level but her eyes were not. "Dara told me about the Compact's offer. Protection, a platform—if the evidence indicts the Concordance alone."

"And will you take it?"

"No."

"Good." He reached out and covered her hand with his ruined, trembling one. "Because here is my last gift, and it is the hardest one: the truth is not a weapon, though everyone you meet will try to make it one. The truth is a seed. It doesn't care who plants it. It doesn't fight for a flag. It only asks to be put in the ground whole—with the census fraud and the massacre and your mother's silence and my survival and all of it, the rot and the root together—or it grows into something worse than the lie it replaced." He squeezed, as much as his hand could squeeze. "Swear me the whole truth. To everyone. At once. Swear it now, while I can hear it, because I intend to spend my remaining weeks making it admissible, and admissibility requires a sponsoring witness with clean intent, and you, Keeper, are the cleanest intent this fleet has produced in three generations."

Lena Yarrow bowed her head over his hand. He felt rather than saw the nod.

"I swear it," she said. "Whole. To everyone. At once."

"Then witness me," Sef Okonkwo said, "and tomorrow we begin."

* * *

He slept, and dreamed of the Kestrel's voice singing in a hard burn, and woke before dawn to alarms he didn't recognize—soft, procedural, the vault's systems murmuring to themselves—and found Lena already at his bedside, tablet lit, her face washed in its glow.

"What is it?" he asked.

She turned the tablet toward him. On it, a traffic advisory, fresh from the relay net: three approach vectors, converging on Meridian, transponders declaring three different allegiances.

"They're early," Lena said. "All of them. The delegations are coming—and Sef, they're coming *now*."
