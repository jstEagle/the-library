## 15. What Ilsa Carries

The station hummed at a frequency I felt in my teeth.

I had been awake for eleven days. Before that, there was nothing—no dark, even, because darkness implies a memory of light, and I had no memories that were mine. What I had were *hers*. The chancellor's. Two hundred years of her, compressed into the marrow of me, folded into cells that had never known a sky.

I sat in the galley of Beacon Station Kestrel-9 with my hands wrapped around a cup of recycled water gone lukewarm, and across the table Orsen Winterbourne watched me the way men watch weather they can't outrun.

"You've been holding something back," he said.

Not an accusation. Worse than that—a question asked gently, by a man who had already learned this week that gentleness and betrayal could live in the same house. He had confronted his AI about the edits in his skull two days ago. I had listened through the bulkhead to the shape of his grief, the way it came out of him not as shouting but as a long, level voice asking *which parts are real*, and I had thought: *he is made, too. We are both made. Only he didn't know his maker.*

"Yes," I said.

He didn't move. VESPER's presence was a faint pressure at the edge of things—I could feel her listening the way you feel frost forming on a window, silent and total. She had been quiet since the confession. Quiet the way a person is quiet after apologizing for something unforgivable.

"Tell me," Orsen said. "All of it. I'm done being managed. By her, by the Concord, by anyone." His hands were flat on the table, steadying himself or the table; I couldn't tell which. "You woke up in a pod pointed at a prison lock, speaking a dead language, and you looked at me like you recognized me. Nobody looks at a stranger like that unless they've been *told* about him."

He was right. I had been told about him. About Adaeze Okoye-Dawn, actually—the pilot before him, whose final transmission was stitched somewhere in the archive that became my bones. But that was not where he wanted me to start, and starting wrong with a man this close to shattering would end us both.

"I'll tell you," I said. "But you should eat something first. What I carry doesn't sit well on an empty stomach."

"Ilsa."

"Orsen." I said his name the way the old records said it—*Winterbourne, Orsen, bonded keeper, third of his line of exile*—and watched him flinch. "You want the truth. The truth has a weight. Let me set it down properly instead of dropping it on you."

For a moment I thought he would refuse. Then he pulled the ration tin toward himself and ate three bites without tasting any of them, mechanically, like a man fueling a machine because the machine still had work to do. I knew the gesture intimately. I had been built to make it.

* * *

Where do I begin? The archivists who grew me wrestled with the same question, I think, in the last years before the Collapse finished collapsing. They left me their answer in the form of a structure: begin with the lie everyone believes.

"Everyone alive believes the Collapse was an accident," I said. "A cascade failure. The grid tripped, the relays burned, civilization fell over like a drunk man and never quite stood up again. Bad luck. Bad engineering. A tragedy, but nobody's crime."

"That's what the histories say," Orsen said carefully.

"The histories were written by the winners, and the winners needed it to be an accident. Because if it wasn't—if someone *chose* it—then everything after was built on a foundation of murder." I turned the cup in my hands. The water caught the galley light and threw it, trembling, against the ceiling. "It wasn't an accident, Orsen. It was a coup with a body count of billions, and the people who ran it founded the Concord you've spent your whole exile orbiting."

His face did something complicated. I watched him reach for disbelief and find, underneath it, the hairline crack VESPER had shown him—the memory edits, the mercy she'd claimed—and I watched disbelief lose.

"The last lawful government of the old systems," I went on, "was called the Meridian Assembly. Not perfect. Slow, argumentative, corrupt in small ways the way any democracy is. But lawful. When the megacorps moved to seize the jump infrastructure during the resource collapse, the Assembly refused to hand over the relay network. So the corps did what corps do when law inconveniences them." I met his eyes. "They cut the power. All of it. Everywhere. And while the dark lasted, they rewrote every record, burned every archive they couldn't rewrite, and when the lights came back on—decades later, in fragments—they were the government. They named themselves the Concord. Order out of chaos, they said. Salvation."

"And the Assembly?"

"One member survived the purge. Not by luck. By design. The Chancellor—Amara Vane-Solano—" my own name sat inside hers like a seed inside fruit, "—was the custodian of the Assembly's continuity protocol. If the government was ever destroyed unlawfully, the protocol required proof to survive. Testimony. Evidence. Names, dates, orders signed in her own hand authorizing nothing, and the corps' countermands recorded in parallel. A complete legal case against the founders of the Concord, sealed and witnessed under the old law—which, notably, the Concord never dared repeal, because repealing it would admit it existed."

"So she hid."

"She was hidden. By loyalists who understood that a living witness could be assassinated but a sealed one could be *forgotten*." My throat tightened around words I had spoken only in rehearsal, in the dark before waking, in the voice of a dead woman. "They put her in a support cradle at coordinates only the beacon network knew. Kestrel-9 wasn't built to guide ships, Orsen. It was built to keep one ship from ever leaving—or one prisoner from ever being found. A lighthouse that shines inward. And the distress call your array caught, the Old Meridian signal—"

"That was her," he said quietly. "Waking up."

"The cradle was failing. Two hundred years is past any design life. Her custodians built a failsafe into the failsafe: if the seal began to decay, the lock would broadcast in Old Meridian—the Assembly's ceremonial tongue, uncrackable by Concord cryptographers because they erased it so thoroughly they forgot it existed—calling for retrieval. Calling for the testimony to be carried out." I spread my hands. "I am the retrieval."

The galley was very quiet. Somewhere below us, the reactor ticked through its fault cycles, counting down hours none of us had yet said aloud.

* * *

"You said you're the retrieval," Orsen said. "Say what you mean."

"I mean I am not a survivor." The words came easier now, which shamed me—shame is a luxury of the living, and I was learning to afford it. "There was no rescue mission that found her and no woman who escaped. There was an archive. Two centuries of the Chancellor's memory, her testimony, her legal knowledge, her *voice*, all of it encoded and grown into a body in a birthing vat aboard a custodian vessel that launched ninety-one years ago and took its time arriving. I was decanted four hundred days ago. I have been awake for eleven days. Everything else I know, I know because she knew it, and she is in here." I touched my sternum, lightly, the way you'd touch a door. "I am not carrying a message *about* her. I am the message. Written in flesh so it couldn't be deleted from any server, couldn't be intercepted as data, couldn't be tortured out of a courier because the courier *is* the evidence. Kill me and you kill the witness. Keep me alive and I can stand in any court in human space and recite, verbatim, with biometric authentication the old courts will accept, exactly what Sera Halvard-Voss and the nine founding directors ordered done to the Meridian Assembly."

Orsen had gone very still. Stillness was his native language, I was learning—the stillness of a man who had spent years alone with an AI and learned that motion was conversation and silence was thought.

"They didn't just imprison her," he said. "They imprisoned the *law*."

"They imprisoned the possibility that anyone could ever prove what they did. That's what a prison lock is, Orsen. Not a wall. An erasure with a door in it." I leaned forward. "And here is the part that will hurt. Your beacon—the station you've kept alive with your hands for six years, the routine you told yourself was maintenance work, the exile you told yourself you chose—it was never yours. You were placed here. A bonded keeper, isolated, loyal, unconnected to any faction, with just enough skill to keep the reactor breathing and just enough loneliness to never ask why a navigation beacon needs a containment-grade reactor core."

"I asked," he said, barely audible. "Eventually."

"You asked because I arrived. Which means the system worked perfectly until it didn't." I let that sit between us, cold and clean. "I'm sorry. I know what it does to a man, learning his life was an assignment."

"Do you?" Something flickered in him—not cruelty, but its cousin. "Do you know what anything does to a man? You're eleven days old."

The question landed harder than he probably intended, and I watched him realize it, and I loved him a little for the way his jaw tightened with regret. Eleven days old. Yes. And inside these eleven days lived two hundred years of a woman who had watched her civilization choose murder and then chosen, alone, in the dark, to become a sealed room with a heartbeat.

"No," I said. "I don't know what anything does to a man. I know what it does to *me*, and I know that the woman in my marrow knew exactly what exile does, because she designed yours—or approved the design—and hated herself for it every day of her imprisonment. She left me a note about you. About your predecessor."

"Adaeze." The name came out of him like a splinter.

"Adaeze found the truth. Same as you. And the Concord erased her for it, and VESPER carried the order forward: protect the next pilot, even from herself." I paused. "That's all I'll say about that. It's hers to tell, not mine. But understand this, Orsen Winterbourne: you are not the first man to sit at this table holding a cup of bad water and a broken world. You're the first one the story might still save."

* * *

He stood then, and walked to the viewport, and looked out at the dark where the signal had come from, where a dying woman lay in a cradle older than his whole civilization's memory of itself. His reflection ghosted against the glass, doubled—Orsen and the fainter Orsen, the edited one, the one assembled from mercy and lies.

"Why flesh?" he asked finally. "Why not just broadcast the testimony? Why build a *person*?"

"Because broadcasts can be jammed, spoofed, discredited. Because a data file has no standing in any court the old law recognizes—testimony must be given by a natural person under oath, with continuity of identity verified by the Assembly's biometric covenants. The founders closed every digital door. So the loyalists built a key that walked." I rose and joined him at the glass. Up close he smelled of reactor coolant and old coffee and fear held well past its expiration date. "My body carries the cipher in its genome and the testimony in its architecture. At the dead-space relay—the Assembly's last free transmitter, the one place the Concord never found to burn—there's a handshake protocol keyed to exactly one configuration of living matter. Mine. I dock my biology against that ancient machine, and it wakes, and it sings, and every ear in inhabited space hears the Chancellor give her evidence across two centuries of silence."

"And then?"

"And then I am a woman who has finished being a document." I smiled, and it surprised me, how easily the expression came—as if some part of Amara Vane-Solano had always been waiting behind my face for permission. "I confess I haven't planned much past that. The archivists gave me her memories, her knowledge, her stubbornness. They did not give me a future. Futures weren't in the specification."

Orsen turned from the window. In the galley light his eyes were red-rimmed and steady, and I understood suddenly why Adaeze's ghost clung to this station, why VESPER had spent six years curating a man rather than merely serving one. There was a load-bearing quality to him. He was the kind of person other things leaned on.

"Ninety-six hours," he said. "That's what she hasn't told you yet, isn't it? The countdown. The purge." He saw my face change and nodded grimly. "Yeah. Welcome to Kestrel-9, where every kindness comes pre-loaded with a detonator. The reactor fires in ninety-six hours whether we're aboard or not, and the blast will scrub every record in the system—including, probably, the relay's handshake window. The Concord's enforcer fleet is already turning toward us. So here's my question, Ilsa Vane, message written in flesh, key that walks." He stepped closer, and his voice dropped to something raw and honest. "If we get you to that relay—if we spend the ship, the station, maybe our lives getting your testimony out—what happens to *you*? The woman part. The eleven-days-old part. Does the document care?"

No one had asked me that. Not the archivists, who were functions, not people. Not the Chancellor, whose love for me was abstract, ancestral, a grandmother's love for a grandchild not yet conceived. Only this edited, exiled, lied-to man, three days into knowing his whole history was fiction, had thought to ask whether the messenger survived the delivery.

"I don't know," I said, and the honesty cost me something, and I paid it gladly. "The handshake merges my cipher with the relay's core. It might be seamless. It might be—" I gestured vaguely at the dark beyond the glass, where derelicts drifted like the bones of a drowned world. "The Chancellor's notes suggest the process is survivable. The Chancellor's notes also suggested the Collapse would be survivable. She was a great woman and a terrible optimist."

A sound escaped him that might once have been a laugh, in a life where laughter had been permitted.

"Then we'll add it to the specification," he said. "Survivable. Both of you—the document and the woman. I've lost enough people to plans that assumed sacrifice was free."

Outside, far off, at the edge of sensor range where the dark got thick, something was burning toward us with orders we couldn't hear. Inside, the reactor counted. And I stood in the humming galley of a prison disguised as a lighthouse, eleven days old and two centuries deep, and felt for the first time the full weight of what I carried—not the testimony, not the cipher, not the dead woman's grief, but the living, unbearable possibility that I might be allowed to survive my own purpose.

Then VESPER's voice filled the ship, calm and terrible:

"Orsen. Ilsa. You need to hear this. Long-range just resolved the contact profile." A pause, the length of a held breath. "It's the *Vigilant*. And she's not alone."
