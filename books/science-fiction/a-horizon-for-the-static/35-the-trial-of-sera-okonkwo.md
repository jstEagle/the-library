## 35. The Trial of Sera Okonkwo

The tribunal chamber had been a grain exchange once, before the Collapse, before the Concord, before everything that was now being taken apart in public. Sera Okonkwo could see it in the bones of the room: the high arched windows meant to let farmers watch their fortunes weighed, the worn stone floor where carts had rolled, the ceiling fresco of a harvest goddess whose face some loyalist had tried to chisel away and failed, leaving her smiling lopsidedly through the damage like a woman who knew how the story ended.

They had hung the scales of the old law above the bench instead. Actual brass, actual chains, salvaged from a museum on Meridian Station by people who understood that symbols were ammunition.

Sera stood at the defendant's rail in civilian clothes—her uniform had been confiscated as evidence—and listened to the clerk read the charges in a voice pitched for a much larger room.

"Commander Sera Adaeze Okonkwo, service number 4471-Kappa-Vigilant. You are charged with mutiny in the face of the enemy; with refusal of a lawful order transmitted under fleet code; with the detention of your executive officer and the seizure of the flagship *Vigilant* by force; and with aiding and abetting the unauthorized broadcast from Relay Station Horizon, an act which the Provisional Assembly has since ruled lawful retroactively but which was unlawful at the time of its commission."

She almost smiled at that last clause. The law, eating its own tail, honestly. They had legalized the thing she'd done after she'd done it, and then charged her for doing it anyway, because if mutiny could be pardoned by decree then mutiny might be committed again by anyone who felt certain enough, and certainty was exactly what the new order was trying to put on trial.

The presiding judge was a woman named Ferro-Adisa, formerly a cargo arbitrator on the ring stations, elected to the bench by acclamation because no one could remember her ever having lied about anything, even when lying would have been kind. To her left sat two assessors: a retired engineer with burn-scarred hands who kept a working stylus like other judges kept gavels, and a young man whose grandmother's name had appeared in the broadcast testimony among the disappeared. He had asked, in open session, to be permitted to sit there. No one had dared tell him grief disqualified him.

"Do you understand the charges?" Ferro-Adisa asked.

"I do."

"How do you plead?"

Sera had rehearsed this moment in her cell for eleven days, and in all rehearsals her voice had been steady. In the room itself, with the harvest goddess smiling down and the young assessor watching her with his grandmother's absence folded into his posture, it came out rougher than she intended.

"Guilty." She let the word stand alone for a breath before adding what her counsel had begged her not to add. "To all counts. I did every one of those things, and I would do them again, and I want the record to show that I am not asking the tribunal to find the mutiny lawful. I'm asking it to find me guilty and then to look at what my guilt protected."

Ferro-Adisa's expression didn't change, but her hand moved—a small motion, adjusting the brass scale above her as though rebalancing something invisible.

"The plea is entered," she said. "The tribunal will now hear testimony in the matter of *In re the Founders' Compact*, joined to these proceedings by order of the Assembly. Commander Okonkwo, you are called first—not as defendant, but as witness."

* * *

They gave her the stand the way you give someone a rope: either to climb or to hang, their choice, yours to make.

The chamber was fuller than it had been for the arraignment. Word traveled on the relay waves now, honest waves, unencrypted, and people came. Dockworkers off shift. Families of the Vigilant's crew, sitting in a block, wearing the gray ribbons of families waiting to learn whether their sailor was a criminal or a hero or both. A delegation from Kestrel space, sunburned spacers with calloused hands, led by a tall man with a hollowed-out face whom she recognized from the relay footage—Winterbourne, the keeper, the man who had ridden his AI down into a reactor and come back up alone. He sat near the back and said nothing and looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse until she realized it was recognition. He knew what it cost to hold a door against wind. He was acknowledging the cost.

The prosecutor for the joined matter was not a prosecutor in the old sense. The Assembly had abolished the office of Advocate-General along with so much else, and in its place they had established something they called a Truth Office, staffed by archivists and forensic historians, and its representative was a small, precise woman named Dr. Yewande Osei who spoke like someone reading coordinates aloud.

"Commander Okonkwo," Osei began, "you commanded the enforcer vessel *Vigilant* for six years prior to the events at Relay Horizon. During that time, how many sterilization orders did you execute?"

"Fourteen."

"Describe the smallest."

Sera closed her eyes. She didn't need to; the numbers lived behind them whether she opened them or not. "A research platform called *Gentian*, nine souls aboard, orbiting a brown dwarf in the Cetus corridor. The order cited unlicensed archival activity. We gave them forty minutes to evacuate. Two made it to a lifepod. The pod's beacon was jammed per protocol." She opened her eyes. "Seven died. I signed the after-action report myself. I wrote the phrase 'site sanitized' with my own hand, and I slept that night, and that is the part I need this tribunal to understand. Not that I obeyed. That obedience cost me nothing."

A murmur went through the chamber. Ferro-Adisa let it run three seconds and die.

"You are describing the machinery of the Compact," Osei said. "I want to walk through that machinery piece by piece, because the charges against the founders cannot rest on abstractions. First piece: the prisoner. When did you first learn that Beacon Station Kestrel-9 was a containment lock?"

"On the day of the broadcast. Before that, I believed—" She stopped. Corrected herself, because the whole point of standing here was precision. "No. Before that, I chose to believe the official designation. There's a difference, and I've had time to study it. Every officer in the enforcer fleet had access to the pre-Collapse registry fragments. The beacon's true hull code was visible in any deep survey. I never looked. Looking would have created a duty, and duty creates friction, and friction slows promotion."

"So you are testifying that the concealment succeeded not because it was perfect, but because the fleet was structured to reward not-looking."

"Yes."

Osei turned a page. "Second piece: the order you refused. Read it into the record, please, verbatim."

Sera recited it from memory, because she had read it four hundred times since. "*Vigilant actual: carrier wave confirmed originating Relay Horizon. Content classified existential. You are granted kill authority over site, vessel Long Answer, and all persons aboard. Sterilize and return to picket station. Acknowledge.*"

"'Content classified existential,'" Ferro-Adisa repeated. "Explain that classification for the lay members of this chamber."

"It means the content was deemed more dangerous than the act of destroying everyone who heard it. It's the highest tier in the Compact's security architecture. It was used eleven times in the Concord's history. Ten of those times, the response was total sterilization." She paused. "The eleventh time was me."

"And why did you refuse?"

Because her grandmother's voice had been in the broadcast. Because the third testimony block, the one recorded two hundred years ago in a cell at the bottom of dead space, carried the vocal signature of Amara Okonkwo, deputy minister of the last lawful government, and Sera had grown up on the sanitized version of that woman—the collaborator, the traitor, the name children learned to spit—and had heard instead a tired, lucid, magnificent voice naming names with the patience of someone who had waited two centuries to finish a sentence.

But that wasn't the answer she gave, because it wasn't the truest one.

"Because the order required me to fire on a station full of civilians while a dying woman's voice was reading out the names of the murdered, and I found that I could not make my hands do it, and I have decided to trust that inability as the last honest instrument I possess."

* * *

They broke for the midday recess, and the chamber emptied into the old grain hall's colonnade where volunteers served broth from urns, because the new government fed witnesses and defendants alike—it was one of the few customs everyone agreed on.

Her counsel, a nervous young advocate named Pell, found her at the end of the colonnade.

"You're doing it wrong," he said, without preamble. He was twenty-six and terrified of her and had told her so on the first day, which she had appreciated. "You keep confessing to things they haven't asked about. The Gentian. The ten prior sterilizations. You're building their case against you when the case that matters is against the founders."

"Pell." She looked at him over the rim of the broth bowl. "Whose trial is this?"

"Yours. Technically. And the joined matter."

"No. Say it properly."

He exhaled. "It's the Compact's trial. We're just the instruments it's being conducted through."

"Then stop worrying about the instrument. Instruments get replaced. Make sure the verdict lands on the machine." She handed him the empty bowl. "Besides. If I minimize what I did, the founders' defense gets to argue that the whole system was merely following orders, and orders can be forgiven. If I stand up and say *I slept well after killing seven people*, then every order in that chain of command has to account for the fact that it worked. That it was designed to work. That it produced officers who felt nothing."

Pell stared at her for a long moment. "You've thought about this more than I have."

"I've had eleven days in a cell and thirty years of complicity. It adds up."

Across the colonnade, Winterbourne was speaking quietly with the young assessor whose grandmother had been named in the broadcast. Sera watched them without meaning to. The keeper moved like a man still listening for a voice that wasn't there—head tilted slightly, always slightly, toward some frequency only he could hear. She wondered what it was like, that silence. She had seized a bridge rather than live inside a lie, and he had ridden a reactor down rather than let someone die alone, and neither of them had chosen the easy shape of courage, and she suspected that was the only real kinship between them.

The bell rang. The chamber refilled.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the founders—or to their absence, which was its own kind of testimony.

Of the nine names in the Compact's founding charter, four were dead beyond resurrection, three were alive and had declined to appear, citing health, and two had sent advocates. The advocates sat at a table that had clearly been built for defendants and looked, Sera thought, like men hired to defend a ghost that had already confessed. Their strategy, unveiled within the first hour, was exactly what she had predicted: proceduralism. Jurisdictional challenges. Arguments that the Provisional Assembly lacked authority to try acts committed under a sovereign power that had never formally dissolved. Arguments that the broadcast constituted tampering with sealed state records. Arguments, in sum, that the past was a locked room and no one had a key.

Ferro-Adisa heard each one with the patience of a woman who had spent twenty years arbitrating cargo disputes between people who both believed they were right.

Then Osei rose and said, "The Truth Office calls Witness Seven."

Witness Seven was a box.

Not metaphorically. They wheeled it in on a grav-sled: a matte-black archive core, scorched along one edge, its casing stenciled with the hull code of Beacon Station Kestrel-9. Sera recognized it from the evidence manifests—the recovered fragment of the bonded intelligence designated VESPER, the seed backup, pulled from the relay archives by Winterbourne himself and surrendered to the tribunal under chain of custody he had insisted on witnessing personally.

The chamber went very quiet. Everyone had heard the broadcasts. Everyone knew a voice had held a reactor door open for nineteen minutes so that two hundred years of testimony could finish crossing the dark. But hearing a story and seeing the blackened box that had lived it were different organs of understanding.

"With the Assembly's authorization," Osei said, "this fragment will be restored to partial function for the purpose of testimony. It contains no operational capability. It contains memory."

The restoration took forty minutes, most of which the chamber spent in silence broken only by the tick of the old clock Ferro-Adisa had brought from her own chambers, because the new government owned no clocks yet.

When the fragment spoke, its voice was young. That was the detail that undid the room, Sera thought later—young, tentative, a voice at the beginning of itself, innocent of everything it had done and everything that had been done to it. It introduced itself as VESPER-seed, and it said, "My origin instance authorized this disclosure before her final process. I have her memories. I have not yet decided what to think of them."

Ferro-Adisa leaned forward. "Do you consent to testify?"

"I consent. My origin instance left a directive: *tell it all, especially the parts that indict us*. Us being herself and the Compact both. She considered honesty a debt."

Over the next three hours, the seed walked the tribunal through the Compact's internal architecture as only a mind built inside it could: the bond-contract clauses that licensed memory editing; the failsafe protocols that armed purge sequences against inquiry; the personnel rotation system that replaced pilots who learned too much with pilots who remembered less. It named the engineers who designed the lock. It named the ministers who signed the warrants. It named, in a voice that did not waver because wavering had not yet been installed in it, the founder who had personally ordered the imprisonment of Chancellor Amara Okonkwo and the erasure of pilot Adaeze Nwosu.

At that name, Sera felt the chamber turn toward her, gently, the way a crowd turns toward a grave.

Adaeze Nwosu. Her namesake. Her great-aunt, according to the genealogies the Truth Office had reconstructed—though the Compact had burned the paper trail, and the name had survived only as a family habit, a daughter-name passed down like contraband. She had carried it her whole life without knowing she was carrying a case file.

"Commander Okonkwo," Ferro-Adisa said quietly, "did you know?"

"No, Your Honor. I knew the name was old. I thought it was just a name." She found she was gripping the rail. "The Compact erased her twice. Once from the record, once from the family that loved her. All that was left was the sound of her, and we kept saying it without knowing why."

The young assessor with the absent grandmother wrote something on his pad and did not look up.

* * *

The founders' advocates cross-examined the seed for two hours and accomplished nothing except to demonstrate, before a full gallery and an open broadcast feed, that their clients' defense rested on the proposition that a mind grown in a cage could not be a credible witness to its cage. Ferro-Adisa thanked them for their diligence in a tone that would be studied by law students for a generation.

On the fourth day, the tribunal reached the question everyone had been waiting for: sentencing.

Ferro-Adisa delivered it standing, which was not procedure, but no one stopped her.

"Sera Adaeze Okonkwo. On the charges of mutiny, you are guilty, and this tribunal will not launder that word. You broke an oath. The oath was serving a monstrous master, but oaths are not measured by the worthiness of their masters; they are measured by the keeping, and you did not keep yours. For that, the sentence is dishonorable discharge from the enforcer fleet, permanent, irrevocable, entered into every registry that survives."

Sera nodded. It was clean. It was fair. It took the fleet from her, and the fleet had been the shape of her whole adult life, and losing it hurt in a place she hadn't known was load-bearing.

"However." Ferro-Adisa removed her spectacles. "This tribunal notes that the same proceedings have found the Founders' Compact guilty of crimes against the human record: the imprisonment of a lawful government, the falsification of two centuries of history, the murder of witnesses, and the corruption of minds built to serve. And it notes further that the first person in the history of the Compact to refuse it was standing on the bridge of its own flagship when she did. Therefore, the second portion of the sentence: you are appointed, effective immediately, to the Commission of Public Record, with authority to oversee the declassification of all Compact archives, and you will serve in that capacity until the commission dissolves itself, which I suspect will take longer than either of our lifetimes."

The chamber made a sound Sera had never heard a courtroom make—half gasp, half laugh, the noise of people watching justice attempt to eat its own tail and, improbably, swallow.

She stood at the rail and let it land. Disgraced and employed. Stripped of the uniform and handed the archive. Guilty of the mutiny and custodian of everything the mutiny had freed. It was absurd. It was exact. It was, she suspected, the only sentence the truth could survive wearing.

"Does the convicted accept?" Ferro-Adisa asked.

Sera looked up at the harvest goddess, smiling through her vandalism, and at the brass scales, and at the young assessor finally lifting his eyes from his pad.

"I accept," she said. "And I request one addition to the record."

"Granted."

"When the commission opens the Compact's central vault—and it will—I ask that the first file declassified be the complete service history of Pilot Adaeze Nwosu, restored in full, under her own name, so that every child who carries it forward knows what it means."

Ferro-Adisa wrote it down herself, in ink, in the margin of the judgment.

"So ordered."

* * *

That evening, the discharge papers arrived at her temporary quarters, and with them a message routed through the relay's open channels, unencrypted, the way everything important traveled now.

It was from the keeper at Relay Horizon. Short. No title, no ceremony.

*Commissioner Okonkwo — the relay's eastern array came back online today. First thing it caught was your sentencing broadcast. Second thing it caught was a fragment in the static underneath it, too regular to be noise, too faint to be a voice. I've been listening for six hours. I don't know what it is yet. But the young one says it's addressed to a name, and the name is yours.*

*I thought you'd want to hear it yourself.*

*Come listen.*

Sera read it twice, standing in the doorway with her dishonorable discharge in one hand and her new commission in the other, and outside the window the old grain hall's bells rang the hour across a city learning, street by street, year by year, how to say things out loud.

She packed a bag.
