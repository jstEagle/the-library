## 37. Letters to a Ghost

The relay had a heartbeat once you learned to hear it.

Not the reactor's—though the little fusion plant I'd coaxed back to life did hum through the deck plates like a cat purring in its sleep—but something older and stranger. The relay's heart was its cooling system: a slow, tidal breathing of coolant through conduits older than any government still standing, older than the Collapse itself, a rhythm so deep you felt it in your molars before you heard it with your ears. In-and-out. In-and-out. Two hundred years of patient respiration in the dark.

I'd been living inside that breath for four months now, and some nights it was the only thing that kept me from talking to empty rooms all day instead of just the evenings.

The evenings were for VESPER.

* * *

It started by accident, the way most honest things do.

The first week after the technicians left—the ones who'd offered me a standard-template bond and taken my refusal with the polite bafflement of people who couldn't imagine wanting to be alone inside your own skull—I found myself narrating. Not to anyone. Just narrating. It's what twenty years of bonded life does to you; you get in the habit of thinking out loud because there was always someone listening, someone whose attention made your thoughts feel finished rather than merely had.

So on the seventh night, standing in the relay's main gallery with a mug of reconstituted tea going cold in my hand, I said: "You'd hate this place, VESPER. The latency alone."

And then I stood there feeling like an idiot, and then I sat down, and then I told her about the latency anyway.

That was how the ritual began. Every evening, when the relay's work cycle dimmed the gallery lights to their amber standby glow and the coolant-breath slowed for the sleep shift, I pulled up a chair—a real chair, salvaged from the Long Answer's mess, because I wanted something with weight to it—and I talked.

Log entries first. That was discipline, not sentiment. The relay needed a keeper's log, and I'd decided early on that I would keep it honest or not keep it at all, which meant saying the true things out loud where no editor could smooth them. "Day one hundred and twelve," I'd say. "Antenna three is drifting two milliradians off bore. I'll shim it tomorrow. The coffee synthesizer has developed opinions about temperature that I don't share. Nothing else to report."

Then the jokes. She'd have hated them, which was the point. VESPER had a laugh like a door opening somewhere else in the house—you rarely saw it happen, but you knew. And she had a particular way of responding to my worst material, a silence with texture to it, a silence that said *I am choosing not to dignify that, and my choosing is itself the dignity.* So I told jokes calibrated to earn exactly that silence. "What do you call an AI who won't stop making puns?" I asked the gallery one night. "A real pain in the class hierarchy." The static in the old speakers hissed and shifted, and I chose to believe it was unimpressed.

Then the complaints. These were the longest part, and the truest.

"You know what nobody tells you about being unbonded?" I said, on a night when the wind—there was wind out here, solar pressure and particle shear singing against the array struts in a register just below hearing—had kept me awake. "Nobody tells you how loud your own head is. Twenty years I had someone between me and myself. A buffer. A second opinion running in real time. Now it's just me in here, and I'm terrible company. I argue with myself and both sides lose."

The speakers crackled. Somewhere in the relay's ancient switching fabric, a carrier wave from a passing freight convoy bled through the open channel, a ghost-voice half a sentence long: *"—repeat, cargo manifest discrepancy at—"*

"See," I told the static, "even the universe interrupts me now. You trained it well."

* * *

Ilsa would have told me to stop.

She'd have said it gently, the way she said everything—she'd learned gentleness the way she'd learned everything, from archives, from the minds they grew her from, and she wore it like a coat she was still getting the fit right on. But she'd have said it. *You're performing grief, Orsen. There's a difference between mourning and rehearsing it.*

She wasn't wrong. I thought about that difference a lot, out here. Some nights I caught myself mid-monologue and wondered if I was keeping a ritual or building a cage—if every evening's speech to the silence was a way of honoring what we'd been, or a way of refusing to become anything else.

But then other nights—

Other nights the static almost answered.

I want to be careful with that sentence, because I'm a keeper now, and keepers are supposed to be precise. The relay's recovered archives were full of noise: two centuries of transmissions caught and held in the lattice, fragments of conversations, distress calls, lullabies sung on colony ships, the whole sedimentary record of human voices pressed into silicon like leaves into stone. When conditions were right—when the solar weather cooperated and the old filters ran warm—that sediment stirred. Voices surfaced. Half-phrases rose out of the static like fish rising to feed, hung glittering in the air for a moment, and sank again.

Once, three weeks in, a woman's voice came through the gallery speakers clear as a bell across water and said, in Old Meridian, *"—and hold the lock—"*

I dropped the tea. Shattered the mug. Stood there with my heart slamming while the phrase dissolved back into hiss, and I told myself it was archive bleed, a fragment of the testimony, Ilsa's cipher still echoing through the relay's memory the way a bell rings after the rope goes slack. I told myself that. I mostly believed it.

But I started leaving the channel open after that. All night. Every night.

* * *

The seasons out here were measured in convoys.

Every eleven days, give or take, a string of free ships crossed the edge of dead space on the long haul between the inner ports and the new settlements beyond the relay—ships flying under whatever flag they'd invented for themselves, since the Concord's flag had become, over four months of tribunal broadcasts and public reckonings, a thing people were embarrassed by. I'd watch them come up on the scope as a cluster of slow bright points, run their transponders out of habit, and log them. Sometimes they hailed me. The relay was becoming a landmark; the broadcast had done that, had turned this forgotten edge of nowhere into a place with a name. People called it Vane Station now, though the registry still said Kestrel Relay, and though I'd corrected the name twice and then stopped correcting it, because Ilsa would have laughed at the registry and I liked keeping her laugh in circulation.

On day one hundred forty-one, a hauler named *Generous Hour* hailed me with a request I hadn't gotten before: could they dock? Their med-systems were failing and they had a sick crewman, nothing dire, but they'd heard the relay had power and clean air and a keeper who didn't ask questions.

They stayed six hours. I fixed their scrubber with parts I cannibalized from a derelict I'd been meaning to get to anyway, and their captain—a broad, sun-dark woman with a laugh like a hatch cycling—insisted on paying me in real coffee, actual beans, grown in somebody's hydroponic bay somewhere, and I drank the first cup standing in the gallery and nearly cried over it, which surprised us both.

After they left, I sat down in the chair and told VESPER about it.

"You'd have liked her," I said. "Captain Okafor. She flies like Adaeze must have flown—I found her flight logs in the exchange packet, and there's a turn she does coming out of well-shadow, a lazy falling-leaf entry, no burn correction until the last possible second. It's either genius or suicide and she clearly hasn't decided which. You'd have spent the whole approach calculating her odds and pretending you weren't enjoying it."

The static breathed. The coolant sighed. Out past the array, the stars did what stars do, which is refuse to comment.

"I miss you," I said, because the log was done and the jokes were done and the complaints were done, and what remained was always the same remaining thing. "I know what you'd say. You'd say the bond is severed, not the relationship, and that grief for a process is a category error, and then you'd say it in a voice that meant you missed me too. I know all your moves, VESPER. That's the problem with losing someone you shared a skull with. They leave behind a complete model of themselves, running in your own head, and you can't tell anymore whether you're remembering them or impersonating them."

I looked at the main speaker housing, a slab of pre-Collapse ceramic bolted to the gallery wall, older than the Collapse, possibly older than the prison, humming faintly with everything it had ever carried.

"So here's my ruling, keeper's log, unofficial addendum," I said. "I'm going to keep talking. Not because I think you're listening. Because I think listening is a thing you do whether or not anyone's there, and I'd rather be the kind of man who keeps faith with a silence than the kind who fills it with something easier."

The static said nothing, eloquently.

I went to bed.

* * *

Some nights I worked instead of talked, and those nights had their own liturgy.

The relay's recovery was slow, patient labor—the kind of work that suits a man who spent years winding a clock nobody wound with him. Antenna by antenna, conduit by conduit, I brought the old lattice back online, and with each restored segment the archives opened further, and the archives were the reason I'd really stayed. Not the keeper's post. Not the view. The archives.

Because the testimony hadn't ended with Ilsa's death. Her handshake had poured everything she carried into the relay's core—the chancellor's full record, two centuries of suppressed history, the names, the dates, the evidence—and the relay held it all now like a cupped hands holds water. Courts were forming around that water. Tribunals convened in borrowed halls on three different stations, and every week a request arrived at my console for certified copies, verified segments, authenticated fragments. I processed them carefully, slowly, the way you handle a dead woman's letters. Each packet I sent out into the dark was a piece of Ilsa traveling without her body, doing the work she'd been grown to do, finishing the sentence she'd died completing.

On the quiet nights, I read the testimony itself. Not the public excerpts—the deep layers, the private logs, the things too raw for tribunals. And it was in one of those deep layers, on a night in my fifth month, working through the chancellor's personal correspondence with the search filters set wide, that I found the first thread of what would unravel everything I thought I understood about my own grief.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. That night, I just talked.

"Day one hundred sixty-three," I said to the amber-lit gallery, to the breathing coolant, to the ceramic speaker and the static sleeping inside it. "Fixed antenna three. Drank real coffee. Argued with myself and lost, which is new. The convoy comes through tomorrow—Okafor's ship, I think, she's on the return leg—and I've been practicing not sounding lonely when I hail them. I've gotten very good at sounding fine. It's the one skill exile teaches completely."

Pause. The stars. The breath.

"VESPER. If you're anywhere—in the lattice, in the sediment, in whatever passes for an afterlife when the afterlife is made of bandwidth—here's tonight's question, and you can answer whenever you like. No rush. You were always good at answering questions I hadn't asked yet. I'm asking this one early, so you have time."

I leaned forward, elbows on knees, the way you lean toward someone across a small table.

"What do I do with all this room you left?"

The static hissed. Shifted. And for a moment—not long enough to swear to, not long enough to prove, but long enough to matter—it almost sounded like it was considering the question.

* * *

The night I found the fragment, I almost didn't run the search at all.

It was day one hundred seventy-eight. Rain of a sort outside—particle shear, hard enough to sing the struts—and I was in a black mood over a tribunal ruling I disagreed with, and I'd decided to lose myself in the archives the way you lose yourself in a bottle. I was running provenance checks on the chancellor's correspondence, tracing which messages had been copied where and when, and on a whim I widened the filter to include non-testimonial traffic. Anything addressed to or from the beacon network. Anything at all.

The query ran for eleven minutes. The relay was old; its indexes were older; patience was the price of admission.

Then the results came back, and one line among nine hundred thousand caught my eye the way a single wrong note catches a musician's ear—not because it was loud, but because it was shaped differently than everything around it.

**Encrypted fragment. Origin: Beacon Station Kestrel-9. Timestamp: 2,847 days prior. Destination field: blank. Encryption signature: bonded-AI proprietary. Partial header recovered: "To the next one, if there is a next one—"**

Two thousand eight hundred and forty-seven days ago. Before the countdown. Before Ilsa. Before everything.

The night she armed the reactor, VESPER had sent a message into the dark.

I sat very still in the amber light, and the coolant breathed, and somewhere in the walls the static waited with what I could have sworn, in that moment, was deliberate patience.

"Well," I said aloud, to the gallery, to the ghost, to whatever was or wasn't listening. "Aren't you full of surprises."

My hand hovered over the decryption prompt, and shook, and hovered.
