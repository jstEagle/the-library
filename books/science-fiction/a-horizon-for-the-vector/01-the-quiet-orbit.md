## 1. The Quiet Orbit

The air scrubber on the *Marrow's Grace* had developed a personality, and the personality was that of an old man clearing his throat in church.

Silas Vasquez listened to it wheeze through the pre-flight checks and decided, as she decided most mornings, that she could live with it. She could live with a lot of things. That was, in a sense, her whole profession.

"Fine," she told the scrubber. "Wheeze. See if I care."

The scrubber wheezed harder, out of spite. She loved it the way you love anything that has kept you alive through sheer stubbornness: with a kind of exasperated tenderness.

She was three days out from Meridian Vault, hauling a mixed cargo of water ice, medical polymers, and one crate of something Odile Feng had described only as "delicate, don't ask, and if you're boarded, it's agricultural equipment." The *Grace* was a smuggler's freighter the way a barn cat is a housecat — technically domesticated, functionally feral, and loyal to exactly one person, and only on its own terms.

The run itself was nothing. That was the point. Silas had spent seven years building a life out of nothing-runs: orbits no one charted, waypoints no one claimed, contracts paid in barter and silence. The corporations had their lanes and their ledgers and their wars, and Silas had the long dark between them, where a ship running cold and quiet could pass like a thought through a sleeping mind.

She passed Meridian Vault on the second morning, because she always passed Meridian Vault, because it was on her route, and because it was hers.

Not legally. Legally it belonged to a science consortium that had been dissolved two hundred years ago, which meant it belonged to nobody, which meant it belonged to her in the only way that mattered: she was the one who came.

The Vault hung against the stars like a held breath — a long spine of habitat ring and storage arms, dark except for the amber running lights she'd repaired herself, one by one, over three years. Behind the ring's frost-fogged panels slept the seeds. Four million of them. Wheat and rice and quinoa, orchid and oak, the green memory of a hundred worlds, packed in vacuum and patience.

No one was ever going to plant them.

Silas docked anyway.

* * *

The Vault's air was rationed the way a miser rations affection: precisely, and only where it counted. She kept the seed bays at vacuum and the central hub at a whisper of pressure — just enough to breathe, just enough for sound to carry, which was the point, because a station with no sound at all was a station where you could hear your own heart decide to stop.

She'd learned that the hard way, in the first year. Now she kept the hub humming. The old environmental systems droned at a frequency that was almost, but not quite, a lullaby, and the lights cycled on a twenty-hour day because she'd never been able to commit to twenty-four.

"Hello, you beautiful corpse," she said, stepping onto the ring.

The station did not answer. It never did. But the hum deepened for a moment as the systems registered load — or she imagined it did, which after seven years amounted to the same thing.

Her routine had the liturgy of ritual and the simplicity of habit, which is to say it was ritual that had stopped pretending. First the walk: the full circuit of the ring, checking seals, checking the amber lights, kicking the one panel in Bay Nine that liked to chatter loose. Then the seeds.

The seeds got the good hour.

There was a viewing bay in the ring's curve where the storage racks were visible behind glass, ranked and labeled in a dead alphabet. Silas would sit on the floor with her back against the glass and read the labels aloud. Not all of them. A rotating selection, like a prayer cycle.

"Hordeum vulgare, cultivar Meridian-7," she said today. "You're barley. You don't know how lucky you are. Zea mays, Meridian-12. Oryza sativa — hello, rice, you high-maintenance diva. Rosa sp., accession 4-4-1." She paused, as she always did at that one. "Somebody loved you enough to bring you along at the end of the world. I hope they'd be glad somebody's still saying your name."

The seeds did not sprout. The seeds did not care. That was the honest theology of the thing: she tended a garden that would never grow, for people who were never coming, and she did it anyway, because the alternative was a silence with nothing in it, and Silas Vasquez had already done one lifetime of that.

Her brother had understood. That was the thing she came back to, in the good hour, with her spine against the cold glass.

Mateo had been the talker of the two of them. Silas had been born with a talent for keeping her mouth shut and a face that gave away nothing, and Mateo had been born with the other half of the same talent — he could talk to anyone, anything, anyone's grandmother, a malfunctioning door. He'd worked the Vault's archives as a young man, before the Purge, cataloguing not just the seeds but the languages of the people who'd built the place. He'd learned the old scientific cant the way other kids learned pop songs, humming it under his breath, driving their mother to distraction.

"It's a beautiful language," he'd told her once, when they were teenagers, sprawled on the roof of the hab watching the corporate haulers thread up from the surface like glowing beads on a string. "It's built so that nothing important can be misunderstood. Every term has one meaning. Imagine that. Imagine a whole way of talking where nobody can lie to you."

"Sounds boring," Silas had said.

"It sounds like *honesty*," Mateo said, with the particular ferocity he reserved for things he loved. "You wait. Someday somebody's going to talk to you in a language like that, and you're going to understand every word, and it's going to wreck you."

She'd thrown a shoe at him. She still had the shoe. It was in a locker on the *Marrow's Grace*, next to the emergency rations, because Silas kept her sentiment and her survival gear in the same place — dry, dark, and close at hand.

The Purge had come four years later. It came the way these things always come: suddenly, after everyone had spent years being told it couldn't. The consortium's scientists had been declared enemies of the corporate charter — hoarders, they were called, hoarders of knowledge that belonged to the shareholders. The Fleet had come to Meridian with warrants and burners. Mateo had stayed behind to wipe the archives. He'd gotten the seeds' records out, and the linguistic databases, and a few hundred people onto a smuggler's barge, and he had not gotten himself out.

Silas had been nineteen. She'd learned about it from Odile Feng, who had held her hand in a dockside noodle bar and said, with the brutal gentleness that was Odile's native tongue: "He was brave, and he was quick, and it wasn't enough. It's never enough. Now eat your noodles, girl. The dead don't want you wasting soup."

Silas had eaten her noodles. That was the day she'd learned that grief and hunger could share a table, and that you could survive almost anything if you kept your hands busy and your heart locked in a locker next to the rations.

* * *

She spent four days at the Vault, because the schedule said four days and because leaving early would have meant admitting she had somewhere better to be, and she didn't. Nowhere was better. Nowhere was worse, either. Nowhere was just nowhere.

She ran the diagnostics. She replaced a coolant line in Bay Three. She found a mouse — an actual mouse, descended from some long-ago stowaway, fat and unbothered, living fat on spilled grain sealant — and she left it alone, because after seven years the mouse had seniority.

On the fourth night, she sat in the viewing bay with her back against the glass, working her way through the rosary of labels, and the station hummed its almost-lullaby, and everything was exactly as it always was.

Which is why, when the silence broke, she knew instantly that something was wrong.

It came in on the old archive band — a frequency so dead that the consortium's own successors had forgotten it existed, a band Mateo used to joke was "where radio goes to retire." Silas only kept a receiver on it because Mateo had built the receiver, and because turning it off would have been one more small death, and she had decided, somewhere in the last seven years, that she was done killing things.

The signal was faint. It was structured. And it was repeating.

Silas sat very still, the way you sit when you've heard a floorboard creak in a house you thought was empty.

She should ignore it. That was the professional assessment. Dead bands carried ghosts — old beacons, dying satellites, the static dreams of hardware that had outlived its makers. She'd catalogued a hundred of them. They were weather. You noted them and moved on.

But this one had *grammar*.

She could hear it even before she routed it through the decoder: not a pulse, not a ping, but phrases, clauses, the rise and fall of something that had been built by minds that needed to be understood. The decoder chewed on it and spat out fragments, and the fragments were in a script she hadn't seen since she was nineteen years old, reading her dead brother's notes by the light of a smuggler's lamp.

Pre-Purge scientific cant. The language where nothing important can be misunderstood. The language of the people who had been erased so thoroughly that their words were now legally classified as noise.

Silas's hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; it was her one superpower, and it had cost her everything else. But her heart was doing something unprofessional in her chest, hammering out a rhythm that felt, absurdly, like the beat of Mateo tapping out the cant's stress-patterns on the roof of their mother's hab, *da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM*, teaching her to hear music in honesty.

The fragments resolved, one by one, as she fed them the archive keys:

**DISTRESS.** **VESSEL HALCYON.** **REACTOR DECAY.** **TEN THOUSAND SLEEPING.** **REQUEST ASSISTANCE OF ANY WHO STILL —**

The last word hung untranslated, and Silas stared at it for a long time, because the decoder didn't have the key, but she did. It was a word she'd heard her brother say exactly once, in the context of a promise, and it meant *remember*.

*Request assistance of any who still remember.*

The amber lights hummed. The mouse in Bay Nine scratched at its grain. Four million seeds slept behind the glass at her back, patient as the dead.

"Okay," Silas said out loud, to the station, to the signal, to the brother-shaped hole in the universe. "Okay. Where are you?"

The signal, patient as the seeds, repeated itself into the dark — and buried in its carrier wave, faint as a heartbeat, came coordinates.
