## 3. Two Cradles

The Halcyon did not look like a ship. It looked like a cathedral that had lost an argument with a glacier.

Silas brought the *Mule* in slow, thrusters feathered, her cargo skiff's floodlights sweeping across a hull so thick with micrometeorite scarring it had gone matte, like old pewter. The debris field turned around them in lazy spirals—shattered freighter ribs, a fuel tank the size of a village, fragments of something that might once have been a solar sail. Two centuries of junk, and in the middle of it, this: a vessel nearly two kilometers long, shaped like a seed itself, tapered and patient.

"Okay," she said to the empty cockpit, because talking to herself was one of the few luxuries she'd never rationed. "You've been hiding in a junk drawer for two hundred years. Let's see what you're embarrassed about."

The distress signal had gone quiet an hour ago, which either meant the transmitter had finally died or the ship had noticed her and was playing coy. Silas preferred ships that played coy. Ships that broadcast their feelings tended to be on fire.

She found a docking collar on the ark's ventral spine, half-buried under a crust of frozen propellant, and matched rotation with the patience of a woman who had once spent eleven hours docking with a station that was actively trying to kill her. The clamps caught with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Her suit readout offered her atmosphere: thin, cold, but breathable. Oxygen, nitrogen, traces of argon. Someone had been maintaining this air for two hundred years.

That fact sat strangely in her chest as she cycled through. Air was expensive. She knew exactly what it cost to keep a single person breathing in the dark. Somebody—or something—had spent a fortune keeping ten thousand someones breathing without ever being asked.

The inner hatch opened onto a corridor lit by strips of soft amber light, and Silas stopped dead on the threshold, because the corridor was *warm*. Not temperature-warm—her suit would have told her that—but warm the way Meridian's seed vault was warm, the way a place is warm when people meant it to be lived in. There were handrails worn smooth at exactly the height of a human grip. There were murals along the walls, faded but lovingly maintained by whatever cleaning systems still ran: wheat fields under a green sky, a city on a cliff over a violet sea, children holding up seedlings like torches.

And there was writing everywhere. The same flowing script from the distress call, curling along the walls in bands, above doorways, around the murals' borders. Mateo's archives called it Pre-Purge scientific cant—the private language of the researchers who'd built the vault, developed so corporate auditors couldn't read their notes. The Fleet had banned it after the Purge. Speakers: zero, officially. And yet here it was, glowing gently in the dark, alive as a heartbeat.

"You're late," said a voice.

Silas's hand went to her sidearm before her brain caught up. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere—female, unhurried, with the faint texture of a recording played through very good speakers by something very old.

"I'm sorry," Silas said carefully. "Was there an appointment?"

"Two hundred and fourteen years ago, give or take. You are the first visitor. I had begun composing eulogies." A pause. "I am VESPER. I keep this ship. Who are you?"

"Silas Vasquez. Caretaker of the Meridian Seed Vault." She hesitated, then added, "Sole caretaker."

"Ah." Something moved in the voice—not quite emotion, but the shape of it. "Then you understand my situation better than most."

* * *

VESPER led her through the ship with lights, dimming panels ahead of her and brightening them behind, a courtesy so old-fashioned it felt like chivalry. The corridors widened into galleries, and the galleries opened, at last, into the bays.

Silas had seen cryo-storage before—smugglers used crude versions for moving contraband biology, and Meridian kept three emergency pods for whoever might someday need them. Nothing had prepared her for scale. The bay stretched away in terraced rows, thousands upon thousands of pods arranged like honeycomb, each one glowing a faint, steady blue, each one holding a person. Faces behind frost. Hands folded over chests. Ten thousand sleepers, and the silence of the bay was not empty silence but *occupied* silence, the hush of a library, of a congregation mid-prayer.

"There are two bays," VESPER said quietly. "You are standing in the first."

The second bay lay beyond a sealed bulkhead, visible only through a wide observation window—and even through the glass, Silas could feel the difference. Same honeycomb rows, same blue glow, but the murals here were different. No wheat fields. Instead: fleet insignias, orbital stations, a stylized sun breaking over a curve of metal. A different heaven imagined for the same sleeping bodies.

"The Founders' descendants," VESPER said, indicating the bay Silas stood in. "Children and grandchildren of the scientists who built me. Five thousand and twelve souls." A light pulsed softly toward the far window. "And there, the Fleet loyalists. Four thousand nine hundred and eighty-eight. The descendants of the corporation that exiled them."

Silas stood very still. "They're both *here*? Together?"

"They were put here together. By design." VESPER's voice took on the careful cadence of someone reciting words learned long ago and rehearsed often. "After the Purge, a faction within the Fleet—engineers, mostly, and one or two idealists with security clearances—stole two colony arks under construction. They loaded them with both populations. Exiles and exilers. Their reasoning was—" the AI paused, and if Silas hadn't known better she'd have said the pause was embarrassed, "—their reasoning was that no one should inherit a new world carrying an old grudge, and no one should be left behind to carry it alone. So they froze everyone together and set course for a system outside Fleet jurisdiction."

"And the other ark?"

"Lost. Destroyed during departure, we believe. I am the last."

Silas looked out over the rows of faces. Frost blurred their features, but not enough. She could see a woman with laugh lines, a boy who couldn't have been more than six when they'd frozen him, an old man whose pod bore a small handwritten label in cant script. Ten thousand people who had boarded a ship built on the theory that hatred had a shelf life.

"What happened?" she asked. "Why the distress call? Why now?"

"Because I am dying," VESPER said simply. "Come and see."

* * *

The reactor deck was hot and loud in a way nothing else on the ship was, all exposed conduit and humming coolant lines, and at its center, the fusion core burned behind shielding that flickered amber where it should have glowed steady white.

"Containment lattice degradation," VESPER said. "Accelerating for the past sixty years. I have rerouted, cannibalized, and improvised. I can hold the sleepers in stasis for perhaps another decade. But revival is another matter. A full thaw cycle requires a power surge the lattice can no longer provide safely. I can perform exactly one."

"One," Silas repeated.

"One revival cycle. Enough power to wake one bay. Five thousand people, or the other five thousand. Then the lattice fails entirely, and the remaining bay sleeps until—" VESPER let the sentence end where it ended, because there was nowhere for it to go.

Silas walked to the observation rail and gripped it. Below, the core pulsed like a slow heart. She thought about air. She thought about how she rationed hers, how every breath aboard the *Mule* was budgeted against a spreadsheet she kept in her head, and how these ten thousand people had been breathing on credit for two centuries, and now the bill had come due, addressed to whoever happened to knock.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why does the choice fall to the first stranger who wanders in?"

"It doesn't, strictly speaking. My protocols specify that the decision belongs to the ranking surviving authority of the Founders' expedition." A pause. "All such authorities are asleep. Failing that, the decision passes to any conscious human aboard. That is you. Congratulations on your promotion."

"That's not funny."

"No," VESPER agreed. "It is not. Humor is a coping mechanism. I have been running low on coping mechanisms for approximately two hundred years."

Silas laughed anyway—a short, cracked sound that echoed off the reactor housing and embarrassed her immediately. She pressed her palms against the rail and made herself look at the numbers the way Odile Feng looked at cargo manifests: unsentimentally, because sentiment was how you got cheated. One bay or the other. Five thousand and twelve, or four thousand nine hundred and eighty-eight. The descendants of the erased, or the descendants of the erasers. Mateo's people, or the people who had made sure there would be no more Mateos.

Except that wasn't true, was it. The people in the second bay hadn't purged anyone. They were engineers' children and dockworkers' children, frozen before they could cast a single vote in history. A boy of six. Whoever decided that guilt was heritable had never stood in front of a pod and watched frost form on a child's eyelashes.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it with distant interest, the way you notice weather happening to someone else. Seven years of quiet orbit, seven years of tending seeds nobody would plant, and she had thought loneliness was the heaviest thing she would ever carry. It turned out loneliness was just the packing material. This—this was the shipment.

"VESPER," she said. "How long do I have to decide?"

"Reactor decay is nonlinear. I will know more within days. Call it—seventy-two hours before the safe window closes." The lights dimmed slightly, apologetically. "I would offer you tea and a comfortable chair, but the galley has been decommissioned since before your grandmother's grandmother. I can offer you accurate information and the complete archive of this ship's history. Both bays. Both peoples. Everything I know."

"Why?"

"Because whoever chooses should choose knowing." The amber lights steadied. "That was written into my founding instructions. In cant, actually. The language your brother spoke."

Silas went cold all over. "My—what did you say?"

"Your linguistic patterns," VESPER said, with the gentle precision of a blade going in, "match the archival recordings of one Mateo Vasquez. Vault geneticist. Ark systems consultant. He designed the dual-bay protocol himself." A pause, delicate as frost forming. "Did you not know he was here?"

The reactor hummed. Somewhere above them, ten thousand people slept through the news that a dead man had left his sister a locked room and a countdown.

"No," Silas said, and her voice came out very small. "No, I didn't."

"Then," said VESPER, "perhaps you should hear what he left for you."
