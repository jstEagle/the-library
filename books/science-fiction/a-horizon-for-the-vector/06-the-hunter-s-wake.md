## 6. The Hunter's Wake

Silas felt the Vigilant before she saw it—the way you feel a storm coming through the soles of your feet, a pressure change in the quiet.

She was in the Halcyon's third bay, learning the sleepers by heart. Row forty-one held a woman named Anouk Reyes-Fleet, propulsion engineer, whose file included a commendation for "unauthorized modification of Purge-order targeting arrays." Silas had read that entry four times. *Sabotaged the sabotage,* VESPER had said, with something like admiration. *She saved eleven thousand people and was never caught.*

Then the deck hummed a half-tone lower, and every light on the ship flickered amber at once.

"Silas." VESPER's voice had developed a habit of arriving before its explanations. "A vessel has entered contested space. Registration: CSV *Vigilant*, Meridian Consolidated charter. It is broadcasting."

"Broadcasting what?"

"A demand. For my coordinates."

Silas stood very still among the frozen dead-and-not-dead, ten thousand strangers sleeping under her borrowed authority. "Who did it send the demand to?"

"To all of us," VESPER said. "To me. To your station. And—" a pause that sounded almost apologetic— "to the *Kestrel's Luck*, which is currently forty minutes out from Meridian with a cargo of water ice and one very unhappy matriarch."

Odile. Silas closed her eyes. Of course. The corporation didn't need to find the ark; it only needed to make finding it expensive enough that someone would sell it cheap.

"Play me the broadcast."

The speaker crackled, and a voice came through—warm, reasonable, the voice of a man who had never once worried about air rations. *"This is Director Cassel Ondine, speaking to all vessels in the Meridian corridor. Two centuries ago, an act of theft deprived this region of its lawful inheritance. The Halcyon carries that inheritance. Any party providing verified coordinates will receive full amnesty, debt forgiveness, and safe passage under corporate charter. Any party concealing it will be treated as an accessory to the original crime. We are not your enemy. We are simply the future, arriving."*

Silas laughed, once, without any humor in it. "He rehearsed that."

"He rehearsed it two hundred years ago," VESPER said. "It is a template. The Fleet used the same phrasing during the Purge. I have compared the texts. Only the names have changed."

*The future, arriving.* She thought of Mateo, who had died so that this ship could stay hidden, and of Odile, who had fed her through her first winter alone because grief makes bad customers and worse thieves, and Odile took pity anyway.

She keyed her comm. "Odile. Tell me you heard it."

Static. Then, gravel-rough: "Heard it, girl. Whole corridor heard it. Get back to Meridian. Now."

* * *

Odile Feng had survived forty years in the unclaimed orbits by holding exactly one principle: *never be interesting*. Interesting ships got boarded. Interesting people got audited. Interesting cargo got confiscated along with everyone who'd ever touched it. Her crew called her the Matriarch behind her back and never to her face, because she'd told them once, mildly, that titles were how corporations started.

Now the *Kestrel's Luck*'s mess smelled of recycled tea and fear, and eleven of her people sat around the table being extremely interesting.

"You all heard him," said Bren Okoye, youngest of the ice-haulers, turning his cup in circles. "Amnesty. Full amnesty. My sister's still on Ceres Station with a corporate flag on her work permit. One call from me and she walks free. One call."

"One call," Odile repeated, "and eleven thousand people die in their sleep."

"They're not people yet, are they? They're cargo. Frozen cargo on a dead ship." He wouldn't look up. Cowardice, Odile thought, always dressed itself as arithmetic. "We're not murderers, Matriarch. We're just... not heroes either. Nobody ever paid us to be heroes."

"No," Odile agreed. "Nobody ever has." She let the silence do some work. Around the table, faces she'd known for decades looked anywhere but at each other. "Anyone else?"

Tomas raised a hand halfway, like a schoolboy. "The reactor's failing anyway, right? That's what Silas said. Ship's dying regardless. Maybe we're not selling them out. Maybe we're just... declining to catch them."

"That's a lovely distinction," Odile said. "I'll carve it on your headstone. *He declined to catch them.*"

"Matriarch—" Tomas began.

"The man on that broadcast," Odile cut in, "is offering amnesty because he needs something. Corporations don't give things away, they rent them. The day he gets his coordinates, the day he's burned that ark and everyone in it, do you imagine your sister's flag comes off? He'll need new leverage by then. It'll be us. It's always us. We're the ones who know where everything is buried out here." She stood, joints cracking like small gunfire. "Invisibility is over, children. The question isn't whether we're interesting anymore. The question is whether we're interesting *alive*."

Bren set down his cup. "So what's your answer, then? Because I've got until he gets bored, and men like that don't get bored. They get thorough."

Odile looked around the table—at Bren's hungry fear, at Tomas's shame, at old Petra who'd already decided and was waiting to see if anyone would say it first—and felt the particular loneliness of command, which is that you must pretend the answer is obvious when it is only necessary.

"My answer," she said, "is that Silas Vasquez hauled my mother's coffin home through a solar storm when no charter would touch it. So we're going to go find out what she's gotten herself into. And then we're going to decide together." She picked up her tea, cold as regret. "And if any of you calls that director before we finish deciding, I want you to look around this table first. Memorize the faces. Because after the money clears, these are the people you'll be spending eternity avoiding."

Nobody spoke. But nobody left the table either, and in the unclaimed orbits, Odile thought, that counted as a vote.

* * *

Silas made the crossing from Halcyon to Meridian in nineteen hours, running dark, riding a debris drift like a leaf on a gutter. All the tricks Mateo had taught her, all the tricks the smugglers had taught her after, deployed now against the world they'd built between them. It felt like apostasy. It felt like inheritance.

Meridian's docking bay swallowed her skiff with its familiar groan of tired hydraulics. Odile was waiting inside the airlock, arms crossed, which meant the argument had already happened without her.

"You brought the whole crew," Silas said.

"I brought the whole crew because half of them wanted to sell you out and I needed them to watch your face while they decided against it." Odile pushed off the wall. "You're welcome. How many are aboard the ark?"

"Ten thousand. Five hundred can wake. The rest stay frozen unless someone hauls salvage out here, and the reactor clock says seventy-two hours before even that stops mattering."

"And the Vigilant says seventy-two hours too, more or less. Funny how clocks agree when men wind them." Odile studied her, and Silas recognized the look—Odile pricing a situation, tallying what it would cost and who would pay. "Girl. Walk away. I can get you a berth on the long haul to the Jovian fringe. New name, clean transponder. In six months nobody remembers there was ever a Silas Vasquez."

"And the ten thousand?"

"Not yours."

Silas looked past her, through the bay window, to where the vault's seed racks glowed faint green in the dark—her brother's seeds, her seven years of tending plants no one would ever plant. "Mateo helped build that ship, Odie. Not the reactor part. The *peace* part. He designed it so both halves of a broken family would have to share one morning or none at all. And now a man with a warm voice wants to make sure it's none, and my own family—" she gestured vaguely at the *Kestrel's Luck* hanging off the dock, "—is taking bids."

Odile was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, the gruffness had thinned into something older and sadder. "You sound like him. Your brother. Same stubborn music."

"I miss him too much to be flattered."

"Don't be. It's a fatal condition." Odile turned toward the bay, where her crew was gathering, drawn by gravity and dread. Behind them, Bren Okoye hovered at the edge of the group, comm unit in his jacket pocket, and Silas saw it, and saw that Odile saw it, and neither of them said anything, because saying it would make it true faster.

"So what happens now?" Silas asked.

"Now," Odile said, raising her voice so the whole bay could hear, "we have a vote. Amnesty on one side. A dead woman's brother and ten thousand sleepers on the other. And whatever we choose—" she looked directly at Bren, mild as milk, "—we choose out loud, in front of God and everybody, so there's no forgetting who we turned out to be."

The bay lights dimmed, cycling low for night shift, and in the sudden half-dark Silas's wrist comm lit up unbidden. VESPER's voice, small and urgent, pitched for her ear alone:

"Silas. The Vigilant has changed course. It is no longer searching the debris field." A pause. "It is heading directly for Meridian. Someone has given it a place to start looking."
