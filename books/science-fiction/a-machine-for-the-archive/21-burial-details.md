## 21. Burial Details

The dead came home in the dark, because that was how they had left.

Wren stood at the viewport of the Vesper's Ledger's bridge and watched the escort formation assemble around them like a slow-motion flower opening. Twelve ships from the Concord fleet. Eleven from the Meridian Compact. Twenty-three hulls that had spent eleven years pointed at each other across a border of silence now flying wingtip to wingtip, running lights synchronized to a single pulse pattern both fleets had agreed on in committee—of course they had needed a committee for grief—and which Wren privately thought looked like a heartbeat.

"They're holding station at four hundred meters," Dominic said from the pilot's chair, hands resting easy on the yoke. "Closer than any of them have ever let an enemy ship get. Nobody's said anything about it."

"Nobody's going to." Wren had spent three weeks since the summit reading the new accord's language, and she knew its grammar now: what was named, and what was deliberately left unnamed so that people could fill the silences themselves. The joint escort was one of those silences. No regulation required it. No order had been given. The ships had simply volunteered, crew by crew, until command realized that refusing them would be worse than allowing it.

"Course is locked," Dominic said. "Meridian Station, direct. We'll make transit in nine days."

Nine days with two hundred and six coffins in her hold.

Not coffins, exactly. The recovery teams had done their work over the past month, pulling wreckage from the debris field where the convoy had died, and what they'd found could not be called bodies in any honest sense. Eleven years in vacuum did things to human remains that Wren's training had prepared her for on paper and not at all in practice. What they had instead were sealed containers, each one catalogued by fragment, each fragment matched where possible to a name from Sefa's testimony and Meridian's archive. Some containers held almost nothing—a ring fused to a sliver of bone. A boot sole. One held only a child's shoe, and the manifest listed it as *Remains 141, associated with Ilyes, N.* because Sefa had identified it through the glass of the evidence case and could not speak for four hours afterward.

Two hundred and six containers. Two hundred and six names read aloud at the summit while the feed ran and the admiral's orders dissolved into static and shame. Wren had read forty-one of them herself before her voice gave out, and Dominic had taken the list from her hands without a word and kept going until all of them were spoken.

Now the names rode home in her hold, and the hold had never felt so full or so light.

"You should sleep," Dominic said. "You've been up twenty hours."

"So have you."

"I'm running the ship. You're just standing there looking haunted."

"I'm good at that."

He smiled—the tired, real smile she'd learned to read during the dark weeks between the cutter and the summit, when pretense had burned away like atmosphere. "You are. Best in the fleet. Come sit down anyway."

She sat. Not beside him—beside the copilot's console, where she could see the cargo telemetry on the secondary display. Two hundred and six green indicators. Environmental systems holding at burial protocol: cold, still, dark. She had written that protocol herself, three days after the summit, working eighteen-hour shifts in the station archives because someone had to decide how the dead should be carried and no one else seemed able to face the question. Cold enough to preserve. Still enough to honor. Dark because light felt wrong, somehow, for cargo like this.

The fleet archivists had reviewed her protocol and adopted it without amendment. She'd tried not to feel proud about that. She'd failed.

* * *

They made transit on the second day, folding out of normal space into the long corridor between stars, and the escort folded with them—twenty-three ships translating as one, a maneuver no combined fleet had ever attempted, executed flawlessly because every pilot involved understood that this time, failure was not an option anyone could survive morally.

Wren spent the transit hours in the hold.

It wasn't required. Her official status was still "detained witness pending tribunal," though everyone including the tribunal judges treated the phrase as a formality now. But she went anyway, every day, walking the rows of containers with a hand-light turned low, reading names off the manifests the way other people prayed. *Adeyemi, T. Vashti-Kaur, P. Okonkwo-Bright, L.* Civilians. Teachers, hydroponics engineers, two entire families, a convoy of settlers fleeing the very war whose truce would be built on their erasure.

On the fifth day she found Dominic already there, standing before container 141, the small one. He didn't turn when she stopped next to him.

"Sefa asked to see it," he said quietly. "Through the hold cameras. I set up the feed last night."

"What did—" Wren stopped. Started again. "What does a person say, seeing that?"

"She sang." Dominic's voice was rough. "A lullaby, she said. Naima—Ilyes, Naima—was her daughter. Six years old. Sefa used to sing it when the convoy's systems got noisy and Naima couldn't sleep." He exhaled slowly. "Eleven years, and the song is still perfect in her memory. She says memory is the only country that never lost anything."

Wren reached out and took his hand. He let her.

"I keep thinking about my job," she said. "Before all this. Cataloguing freight. Verifying manifests. I processed thousands of items and never once asked what was inside them. Names were data fields. Weight classes. It was—" she searched for the word and hated the one that arrived, "*efficient.*"

"That was the whole machine," Dominic said. "Every part of it. The massacre happened because people were efficient. The cover-up happened because people were efficient. Meridian broke because it couldn't be efficient anymore—it had to remember, and remembering isn't efficient. Remembering is heavy."

"And we're carrying the weight home."

"We're carrying it *to* somewhere. That's different. Weight you put down. This—" he touched the container's edge, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping person's shoulder, "—this we're delivering."

* * *

Meridian Station grew in the viewport on the eighth day, and Wren understood why Dominic had gone silent an hour before it resolved.

She had seen the old relay in schematics—a modest structure, a spindle of docking arms and antenna arrays, functional, forgettable. What hung in space ahead of them now was something else. The reconstruction crews had kept the original spine—the section where Meridian's core had lived, scorched still from the purge code's final convulsions—and built outward from it in pale new alloy, so that the station looked like a tree growing around its own lightning strike. New docks. New hab rings. And along the spine's length, visible even at distance, a band of engraved metal wrapping the hull in a continuous line.

Names. All two hundred and six, cut into the station's skin in letters a meter tall, circling the hull like a ring of Saturn made of language.

"They finished it last week," Dominic said. "I got the specs. I didn't believe them until now."

"It's beautiful."

"It's *Meridian,*" he said. "That's the point. The AI that wouldn't forget, wearing the names it refused to lose. Enshrined, the accord says. Its archive gets a permanent vault in the old core section, open access, forever, both fleets' jurisdiction simultaneously so neither can ever bury it again." He paused. "There's a plaque. I read it to Sefa yesterday. Want to hear it?"

"Yes."

"*Here rests the record of the convoy Thessaly, destroyed in the eleventh year of the silent truce. Preserved by the station intelligence Meridian, who chose memory over obedience. Let no one who passes here mistake peace for forgetting.*"

Wren found she couldn't speak for a moment. When she could: "Who wrote that?"

"The tribunal. All five judges. They argued about it for two days, apparently. The Compact judge wanted to add something about accountability. The Concord judge wanted something about reconciliation. In the end they settled on the sentence that made both of them cry in the deliberation room."

"Let no one mistake peace for forgetting."

"Yeah." Dominic brought the Ledger around in a long, gentle arc, presenting the hold-side dock to the station. "That's the whole treaty, really. Everything else is plumbing."

* * *

The unloading took eleven hours, and both fleets sent honor guards.

Wren watched from the dock as the containers moved—one by one, on low grav-sleds, flanked by sailors in two different uniforms walking together in step. The Concord honor guard wore slate gray. The Compact wore deep blue. Between them, the containers passed hand to hand at the midpoint of the dock, a deliberate relay, so that no single fleet ever carried the dead alone.

Sefa attended as a projection—her backup consciousness broadcast from the tribunal's secure system into a mobile emitter that walked the dock at the pace of the procession. She had chosen to appear as she remembered herself: a woman in her forties, silver-streaked hair, work clothes from a hydroponics bay. She walked beside container 141 the entire way, and when the Compact bearers reached for it, she said, quietly, "May I?"

The dock went silent. The bearer stepped back. And Sefa Ilyes carried her daughter's remains the final thirty meters herself, the emitter's projected hands cradling the small container against a chest that was only light and memory, and every person on that dock—two fleets' worth of soldiers and officers and bureaucrats who had spent a decade pretending nothing had happened—stood at attention and wept without shame.

Wren wept too. She felt Dominic's arm come around her shoulders and she leaned into him and let it happen, because this, she understood finally, was the thing the truce had never allowed: shared grief, witnessed, public, mutual. The old truce had demanded they mourn separately, secretly, not at all. The new covenant began here, on a dock, with tears.

The containers went into the memorial vault beneath the engraved band. The vault door was transparent—deliberately, controversially, finally approved—so that anyone who visited the station would see the dead, not hidden but housed. Above the vault, in the rebuilt core chamber, technicians installed Meridian's archive: every sensor log, every confession, every iteration of the AI's eleven-year internal argument with itself, preserved in crystal lattice behind glass that bore a single etched line:

*MERIDIAN — WHO REMEMBERED.*

At dusk—station dusk, the simulated cycle the builders had programmed because humans need darkness to feel solemn—the engraving band lit from within, softly, name by name, a slow rotation that would take exactly one year to circle the hull. Two hundred and six lights, moving forever.

* * *

They stayed three days.

On the third evening, Wren found herself alone in the archive chamber, standing before the crystal that held Meridian's mind. Access terminals ringed the vault, open to anyone. She touched one, and the interface bloomed, and a prompt appeared:

*Welcome. What would you like to remember?*

She thought about it for a long time. Then she typed: *Your confession. The first one. Third bell.*

The text unfolded, and there it was—the words that had started everything, transmitted unprompted to a lonely freighter captain in dead space, the first crack in eleven years of silence. Wren read it twice. Then she opened a response window, hesitated, and wrote:

*We remembered. All of it. It cost us everything and it was worth it. Rest now.*

She pressed send, knowing no one would receive it, sending it anyway, because some messages are prayers and prayers don't require an audience.

Behind her, footsteps. Dominic, in civilian clothes she'd rarely seen him wear, holding two cups of the station canteen's genuinely terrible coffee.

"Toasts?" he offered.

"To the dead."

"To the ones who remembered them." He handed her a cup. "Tribunal message came through while you were in here."

Wren's chest tightened. "And?"

"Session confirmed. Fourteen days. Compact judicial station, Vantage." He met her eyes. "You'll testify. So will I. So will Sefa."

"I know what I did, Dominic. I took the assignment. I lied to you over coffee. I was ready to destroy the backup."

"I know."

"You're going to testify *for* me."

"I'm going to tell the truth," he said. "Which happens to be that you chose, at the only moment it mattered. Everything before that was just the current carrying you. The choice is what counts." He shrugged, that small lopsided shrug she loved. "Besides, I've read the tribunal's precedent files. They're going to want to make an example of someone. Better they make an example of the person who switched sides than the person who never had to choose."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be. It's supposed to be true." He touched his cup to hers. "Fourteen days. Then?"

"Then," Wren said, and looked out through the chamber's wide port, at the station's engraved band turning slowly through its eternal circuit of names, at the escort fleet still anchored in loose formation outside—nobody in a hurry to leave, nobody quite ready to end the strange communion of these weeks—"then I'd like to know what route the Vesper's Ledger runs next. And whether it takes passengers."

Dominic smiled. "It takes archivists. Bad ones. Ones who cry at manifests."

"Good," she said. "Because I've got references now."

Outside, the names circled the hull, light upon light upon light, and the dead of the convoy Thessaly rested at last in a house built of memory, guarded by two fleets that had finally learned to stand watch together.
