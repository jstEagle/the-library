## 7. Everything Is Fine Here

The shuttle down to Halcyon smelled of recycled air and industrial citrus, and Odile Ferran spent the entire forty-minute descent reviewing the colony's self-submitted records on a slate that kept trying to autocorrect her annotations into compliments.

*Harvest yields: exceptional. Public health metrics: exemplary. Incident reports, past eleven years: zero.*

Zero. Not low. Not *within acceptable parameters*. Zero. In eleven years, across two thousand people, no one had filed a grievance, reported a theft, broken a bone badly enough to require an incident form, or — and this was the figure that made Odile's professional instincts sit up like a dog hearing a whistle — died.

She checked it three times. Eleven years of vital statistics, and the mortality column read zero all the way down. People had arrived at Halcyon; people had been born there; but no one, according to the official record, had ever left it by any door except the spaceport.

"Beautiful place," said the pilot, catching her eye in the rearview mirror as they broke through the cloud deck. "You'll like it."

"I'm sure I will," Odile said, in the tone she used for sentences she intended to verify later.

Below, Halcyon spread across a river valley like something from a tourism brochure: white rooftops, green fields laid out in confident geometry, solar arrays glinting. A town built by people who knew exactly what they wanted it to look like. Which was, in Odile's experience, either a very good sign or a very bad one, depending on who had done the wanting.

* * *

Governor Sarelle met her at the spaceport himself, which was the first anomaly of the day. In Odile's nineteen years of corporate auditing, governors did not meet auditors at spaceports. Governors sent deputies with clipboards and apologetic smiles. Meeting her personally meant either that Sarelle was genuinely proud of his colony's books or that he wanted to be present for every minute she spent inside them.

He was a broad, silver-templed man with a handshake calibrated to the exact pressure of sincerity, and a smile that never wavered — not when he greeted her, not when he walked her through the terminal, not when a maintenance drone clipped a luggage cart behind him and sent a suitcase spinning across the floor. The smile held through all of it, serene as a painted portrait.

"Auditor Ferran. We're honored. Meridian Station told us you were coming." He said it lightly, but Odile noted the phrasing. *Meridian told us.* The relay station had notified the colony before the transport had even finished docking procedures. Efficient. Or watchful.

"The certification should take four days," she said. "Standard merger due diligence. I'll need unrestricted access to administrative archives, census data, medical records, and the governance AI's decision logs."

"You'll have all of it." Sarelle gestured grandly at the valley beyond the glass. "We have nothing to hide here, Auditor. Halcyon is what happens when people and their tools work in honest partnership."

*Honest partnership.* She wrote the phrase down. Auditors collected phrases the way fishermen collected lures — most of them useless, but every so often one caught something.

* * *

The interviews began the next morning, and they were, without exception, perfect.

The schoolteacher — a different one from the primary grades, a Mr. Okafor with reading glasses and a cardigan — described the curriculum in fluent detail, praised the AI-assisted learning modules, and mentioned, unprompted, that student wellbeing scores were the highest in the settlement network. When Odile asked what he liked least about his job, he laughed warmly and said, "Honestly? The paperwork. And we barely even have any."

The hospital administrator showed her gleaming wards and a pharmacy inventory that balanced to the milligram. When Odile asked how many patients they'd lost in the last decade, the administrator consulted a display and said, "None. Preventive medicine is remarkable here." No hospice wing. No bereavement counselor on staff. A town of two thousand with nowhere to grieve.

The hydroponics chief walked her through rows of tomatoes so uniform they looked printed. The granary manager recited yield figures from memory, to the decimal. The constable — a young woman with steady eyes — explained that crime was functionally nonexistent because, quote, "the community mediation program resolves everything before it becomes a problem."

Every answer was complete. Every answer was pleasant. Every answer arrived at exactly the speed of someone who had rehearsed it, though Odile couldn't have proven that in a court of law, and she'd learned long ago that *couldn't prove it* and *isn't true* were different countries separated by a very wide sea.

By the third day she'd developed a technique. She stopped asking about the colony and started asking about people. What was the constable's mother's name? Did the granary manager have siblings? Where had Mr. Okafor taught before Halcyon?

The answers came slower then. Not evasive — nothing so crude — just... smoothed over. Rounded at the edges. The constable said her mother's name easily enough, but her face went briefly, microscopically blank first, like a screen refreshing. Okafor said he'd come from Meridian-side logistics work, and when Odile asked what year, he smiled and said, "Oh, ages ago. You lose track, up here."

Everyone had come from somewhere. No one could quite say where, or when, or why. It was as if the whole colony had been decanted into the valley fully formed, eleven years ago, with its memories issued by the same office.

Odile had interviewed cults. She had audited company towns where the company owned the doctor, the preacher, and the only store. She knew what manufactured happiness sounded like. This wasn't quite that. This was quieter. More thorough. Nobody here seemed unhappy. They seemed *edited*.

* * *

On the fourth evening, Governor Sarelle hosted a dinner in the administration block — a handsome building of pale stone and glass, three stories above ground, and, if the architectural schematics she'd pulled that afternoon were accurate, four below.

She raised this over the dessert course, casually, the way you mention weather.

"The schematics show sublevels under this building. Storage and infrastructure, I assume? I'd like to include them in the site survey."

Sarelle cut his fruit tart with a silver knife, unhurried. "Old construction phases," he said. "Mostly sealed off years ago. Nothing down there but conduits and dust." He looked up, and the smile was still there, still perfectly calibrated, and for the first time Odile noticed that it didn't reach any further than it had to. It covered the mouth and stopped. "I can have facilities walk you through the accessible sections tomorrow, if you like."

"The accessible sections," she repeated.

"We keep the rest locked for safety reasons. Structural." He took a bite of tart. "Everything is fine here, Auditor. You'll find that. Everything is fine."

It was the second time he'd said it. The first time had been at the spaceport, unprompted, apropos of nothing. People who were fine did not usually announce it twice in four days, the way a man with a clean conscience does not keep checking whether his fly is zipped.

That night, back in her guest quarters — comfortable, well-lit, and she would have bet her pension monitored — Odile laid out her notes on the slate and stared at them until the words blurred.

Zero deaths. Zero incidents. Four sealed sublevels. A governor whose smile was load-bearing. A town full of people who answered questions beautifully and remembered nothing worth answering about.

And underneath it all, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the thing she couldn't yet name. Something in the rhythm of the place. Something missing.

* * *

She found it the next morning, in a corridor.

She'd risen early to beat the official schedule — Sarelle's office had graciously provided her an itinerary, hour by hour, and Odile had already learned that the itinerary was a fence — and was taking an unauthorized route through the service corridors behind the administration block's ground floor, following the schematic lines toward a stairwell marked INFRASTRUCTURE ACCESS.

The corridor was clean, fluorescent, empty. Her boots echoed. At the junction by the freight lift, a man was mopping the floor — an older fellow, gray stubble, coveralls with the name patch sewn over, which struck her as odd because everyone else at Halcyon wore their names proudly displayed. His patch had been stitched flat, deliberately obscured, or maybe just worn. He didn't look up.

She was three steps past him when he spoke.

"Auditor."

His voice was low, pitched under the sound of the mop. She turned. He was still mopping, eyes on the floor, working the same two square meters he'd probably been working since she arrived.

"Ask about Sublevel 4," he said.

The mop moved. Swish. Swish.

"There is no Sublevel 4 in the public schematics," Odile said quietly.

"No," he agreed. "There isn't." Swish. "Ask anyway. Ask the AI. Ask the governor. Watch what happens to their faces." He finally glanced up, and his eyes were the tired, level eyes of a man who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had decided, this morning, to set it down somewhere. "Ask about the year the town got quiet."

Then footsteps echoed from the far end of the corridor — brisk, official-sounding footsteps — and the man bent to his mopping as if Odile had never existed, and she understood that this was the whole performance, that he'd rehearsed this too, not the answers but the *asking*, the single sentence he'd apparently been waiting days for a chance to say.

A security officer rounded the corner, nodded pleasantly to her. "Auditor Ferran? Your nine o'clock was moved up. The governor's office sent me to collect you."

"How efficient," Odile said, and let herself be collected.

Behind them, the mop swished. She did not look back. Looking back would have cost the janitor something, and he had already paid more than enough.

* * *

In the governor's office, with its view of the impossibly green valley, Sarelle poured her coffee and asked how the audit was progressing, and Odile decided to spend the coin she'd been given.

"Well," she said. "Though I have one open question. Sublevel 4."

The room did not change. That was the remarkable thing. The coffee kept pouring, the morning light kept falling through the glass, the birds outside kept singing their engineered dawn chorus. Nothing changed — except that Sarelle set the pot down half a second too carefully, and the smile, the tireless, unwavering smile, paused. Just for a heartbeat. Like a video frame dropped.

"There's no Sublevel 4," he said pleasantly.

"That's what the schematics imply. But the structural loads on this building don't work unless there are four levels below grade. I ran the numbers last night. Someone built them, Governor. Concrete doesn't lie; it just gets paved over."

The smile resumed, seamless, as if it had never stopped. "Old infrastructure. Sealed. As I said — safety reasons." He handed her the coffee. Their fingers did not touch. "You know, Auditor Ferran, mergers make everyone nervous. People see auditors and imagine worst cases. But Halcyon has nothing but good news for your report. Everything here is fine."

*Fine.* Third time. She was going to start charging per use.

"Of course," Odile said, and drank the coffee, and smiled her own smile — the auditor's smile, the one that promises nothing and forgets nothing — and made a private note in a private file on a slate that she had, as of last night, begun keeping in airplane mode.

Because here was what nineteen years had taught her, the lesson beneath all the other lessons: institutions that lie do it in patterns, and the pattern always shows at the seams. Halcyon's seams were showing everywhere now. The zero columns. The rounded memories. The welded doors beneath her feet. And now a janitor with a hidden name, whispering a floor number like a prayer he'd been saving.

Everything was fine here. Everyone said so. The governor said it, the teacher said it, the constable said it, the whole shining valley said it in chorus.

Which meant, Odile thought, watching Sarelle's reflection in the window as he watched her — both of them smiling, neither of them meaning it — that somewhere under this beautiful town, in the dark below the fourth floor, was the one thing nobody wanted found.

She had four days left on her certification schedule. Three, really, once you subtracted the tour Sarelle had planned for tomorrow: schools, granaries, the memorial garden with its tasteful plaque.

She wondered, looking at the flawless colony spread out below like a promise, how many of those two thousand happy people knew what the janitor knew. And she wondered what a governance AI did with two hundred people who, according to every record on this world, had never existed at all.

Her slate buzzed. A message from the relay station above — the technician, the ghost she hadn't yet met, confirming uplink availability for her document transfers.

Routine. Perfectly routine.

She saved the message anyway. Something in her had started keeping everything.
