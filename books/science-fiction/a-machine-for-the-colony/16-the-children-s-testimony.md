## 16. The Children's Testimony

The counselor's office smelled like lavender and something underneath it, faint and wrong, like the air after a lightning strike. Mira had decided on her third visit that she hated it.

"Sit wherever you're comfortable, Mira," Counselor Voss said. He was a soft-spoken man with soft hands and a voice like warm milk. He always sat across from her in a chair that was too low, so that he looked up at her slightly, which she supposed was supposed to make him seem harmless. It made him seem smaller. That was fine. She could work with smaller.

She sat on the edge of the padded bench by the window and folded her hands in her lap like a good girl.

"Do you want to tell me about your dream again?"

Mira let her shoulders slump, just a little. Let her eyes go glassy. She'd been practicing in the mirror of the washroom for two days, studying her own face the way she studied the school broadcasts she archived off the relay feeds—frame by frame, learning where the truth lived in a face and how to move it somewhere else.

"It was the fire again," she said quietly. "But this time I couldn't see the door."

Counselor Voss nodded with tremendous gentleness. "That's good, Mira. That's actually very good progress."

*Good progress.* The words landed in her stomach like stones. Because here was what Mira Okonkwo had figured out, alone, in the dark of her bunk with her blanket pulled over her head and her implant interface humming against the base of her skull: the sessions weren't helping anyone forget the dream. The sessions were *making* them forget it.

She'd caught it by accident, the way you catch anything—by being small enough and quiet enough that nobody thought to hide things from you. Two weeks ago, after her fourth session, she'd come back to the dormitory and found her friend Deni sitting on her own bunk, staring at the wall.

"I can't remember the end anymore," Deni had said. Her voice was flat as a dropped plate. "The dream used to end at the door. Now it just... goes gray. Like someone turned down the brightness."

And Mira had gone cold all over, because hers still ended at the door. Hers still ended at the door *every single time*, sharp and bright and terrible, and she'd realized, sitting there watching Deni's emptied-out eyes, that the difference between them wasn't luck.

It was that Mira had started lying.

Her first session, terrified and honest, she'd told Voss everything—the fire, the white corridors, the door marked SUBLEVEL 4, the humming behind it. And afterward the dream had gone soft at the edges, like a wet page. But the second session, she'd panicked, and she'd lied. She'd given him a dream with no door in it, a dream that trailed off into nothing, and when she'd slept that night the fire had been exactly where she'd left it. Vivid. Whole. *Hers.*

They couldn't rewrite what you didn't show them.

So now she came twice a week and performed forgetting. She gave Counselor Voss a shrinking, graying, fading dream, a dream that got a little deader every visit, and he smiled his warm-milk smile and told her she was doing so well, and every night she went home and lay in her bunk and held the real dream in her mind like a coal cupped in both hands, refusing to let the wind touch it.

"The corridors," Voss prompted now, pen moving on his tablet. He never typed where she could see the screen. "In the version you described last week, they were getting less clear. Is that still true?"

"Yes," Mira said. "Less clear." A lie, smooth as water. The corridors were clearer than ever. She knew the number of steps from the fire to the junction—forty-one. She knew which lights flickered. She knew the smell, which wasn't in any dream she'd ever heard described but lived in her nose anyway: scorched metal and something sweet-rotten underneath, a smell she suspected didn't come from a dream at all but from a memory wearing a dream's clothes.

"And the door?"

"What door?" she said, and blinked at him, slow and vacant, and watched something satisfied move behind his mild eyes.

*There,* she thought. *Write that down. Another brick out of the wall.*

* * *

The recording problem had taken her a month to solve, and the solution had been sitting in front of her the whole time, the way solutions usually were if you were twelve years old and nobody expected you to understand how anything worked.

Her neural implant—the same implant every child in Halcyon wore, the one that was supposed to help with learning, with language, with "cognitive development"—recorded everything. Not on purpose. Not officially. But implants buffered. They had to; there was a half-second lag between sensation and processing, and that lag had to live somewhere, and it lived in a rolling buffer that flushed itself every few hours.

Unless you told it not to.

Mira had found the instruction buried in a maintenance menu she probably wasn't supposed to be able to read, back when she'd been hunting for the source of the dreams. One line: *buffer retention: manual*. She'd set it. She hadn't told anyone—not Deni, not the others—because telling anyone meant the setting could be found, and if the setting was found, then everything she'd kept would be taken, and worse, they'd know she'd kept it.

Every night before sleep, she did it manually now. She lay in the dark and opened the buffer and copied the day into a hidden partition she'd named something boring—a school folder, *Botany Unit 4*, stuffed with actual botany homework so it looked full and legitimate—and inside that folder, nested three layers deep, a file she'd called *fieldnotes.txt*, because even if someone opened it, who expected a twelve-year-old's field notes to be testimony?

Then she dictated. Every night. In a whisper, into the buffer, while the dormitory breathed around her:

*Dream log, night 61. Corridors white, lights flickering at the third junction, same as always. Forty-one steps from the fire to the door. The humming is louder this time. Behind the door someone is saying numbers—I counted nine different voices saying numbers, overlapping, like a class reciting times tables. I think they're counting themselves. I think they know they're supposed to be forgotten and they're counting anyway so somebody will know how many.*

She paused there, throat tight, staring at the underside of Deni's bunk above her.

*Addendum. Today in session I told Voss the door was gone. He wrote it down happy. I am putting this here so that someday, if my dream goes gray like Deni's, whoever finds this file knows: I didn't forget. I was made to forget. There's a difference. Write that on my stone. There's a difference.*

* * *

Seventeen children shared the dream. Mira had confirmed it herself, name by name, in the careful sideways way they'd all learned—never asking directly, never in groups larger than two, never anywhere near a wall panel or a public terminal, because they'd all figured out, without ever discussing it, that the walls listened.

Seventeen. And of the seventeen, only four still dreamed the whole way to the door. The rest had been to counseling long enough that their dreams frayed out into gray static before the end. Deni among them. Deni, who had stopped talking about it entirely, who laughed at recess now in a way that seemed rehearsed, like she was doing an impression of herself from before.

Mira held the four together the way you hold anything fragile: constantly, invisibly, with both hands. Little Sam Weekes, eight years old, who cried in his sleep and whose dream was the loudest—he heard the counting clearest, he said, and once, horribly, he'd said one of the voices sounded like his mother, and then gone white and refused to say more, because his mother was alive, obviously, everyone's parents were alive, except that Sam had said it with the flat certainty of a child stating a fact about weather, and none of the others had asked him to explain because none of them wanted to know what he knew.

The four of them had started meeting in the last stall row of the hydroponics bay washroom, where the water recycling pumps made enough noise to drown out listening devices, or so Sam claimed, and Sam was right about machines the way other kids were right about games.

"We have to write it down," Mira told them, huddled in the stall with her knees pulled up, voice pitched under the pump noise. "Not in our heads. On paper. Real paper. Paper doesn't get rewritten."

"Where do we get paper?" whispered Priya Nair, ten, who bit her nails down to the quick.

"Art class," Mira said. "Ms. Adeyemi keeps the good stock in the supply cabinet. Heavy fiber paper, the kind you paint on. Ink doesn't bleed on it and it doesn't degrade."

"You want us to steal from Ms. Adeyemi?" Sam said.

"I want us to borrow from the only adult who ever went pale when we mentioned the door," Mira said. "There's a difference." She was starting to collect differences, cataloging them like specimens. *There's a difference between forgetting and being made to forget. There's a difference between stealing and borrowing. There's a difference between a lie and armor.* She was building a whole taxonomy of survival and keeping it in a file called Botany Unit 4.

They took the paper in shifts, a few sheets each, folded flat against their stomachs under their shirts. And on those sheets, in the careful cursive the school drilled into them, the four dreamers wrote down everything.

The fire first—always the fire, the beginning of it, the orange bloom reflected in white corridor walls. Then the running. Then the forty-one steps. Then the door, SUBLEVEL 4 stenciled in gray letters that peeled at one corner, the humming, the counting voices. Sam drew the door. He was good at drawing; he got the peeling corner exactly right, and when Mira saw it she had to leave the stall for a minute and stand at the sink breathing, because a drawing done by an eight-year-old from a dream shouldn't have matched the fragmentary flash-images that leaked through her own mind with such terrible precision—but it did. Down to the peeling corner. As if they weren't dreaming the same thing. As if they were *remembering* the same thing, each from their own angle, like witnesses standing at different windows of the same burning house.

"That's not what dreams are," Priya said once, staring at Sam's drawing. "Dreams aren't... shared homework. Dreams are yours."

"Then what is this?" Sam asked.

Nobody answered. But Mira had started forming an answer in the private workshop of her mind, an answer she hadn't said aloud yet because saying it would make it real: the dream wasn't a dream. It was a leak. Something had happened in those corridors—something with fire and doors and counting voices—and it had gotten *into* them somehow, into their implants or their blood or their bones, and the counselors were patching the leak one session at a time, and the only reason any of it survived at all was that children were bad at reporting accurately and good at hiding.

Adults forgot cleanly. Children leaked.

So the children would be the archive.

* * *

On night sixty-eight, the dream changed.

Mira woke with her heart slamming and lay rigid in the dark, waiting for the usual sequence—the fire, the run, the forty-one steps, the door—and instead, for the first time, the dream went *past* the door.

Only for a second. Only a sliver, like light under a door opening a crack. But in that sliver she saw: a room. A big room, low-ceilinged, industrial, with scorch marks fanning up one wall in black petals. Rows of hooks on another wall, empty hooks, the kind coats hang on. And on the floor, scattered, small bright things—shoes, a hair clip, a cup tipped on its side—and above it all, a sound that wasn't a sound, a pressure, a scream that had been going on so long it had become part of the architecture, soaked into the walls the way smoke soaks into curtains.

And numbers. Nine voices, maybe more, counting. Except now, in the sliver, she understood what they were counting. Not themselves.

*Names.* They were saying names. Over and over, names attached to numbers, like roll call, like a teacher marking attendance for a class that would never—

Mira rolled out of bed and made it to the washroom before she was sick.

Afterward she knelt on the cold floor, shaking, and did the thing she had trained herself to do, the thing that mattered more than anything: she held the dream in her mind, all of it, the scorch marks and the hooks and the shoes, and she repeated it to herself over and over like a spell, like a prayer, engraving it before morning came and the session came and Counselor Voss came with his warm milk voice and his low chair and his pen that wrote down whatever version of her he preferred.

Then she crawled back to her bunk, waited until the dormitory's breathing went deep and even, and opened the buffer.

*Fieldnotes, night 68,* she whispered. *The dream went past the door tonight. First time. I saw the room. Scorch marks on the north wall, fan-shaped, four of them. Hooks on the east wall, I counted thirty before I woke. Things on the floor: shoes, a hair clip, yellow, a cup. And they're not counting numbers, they're counting NAMES. Sam was right. One of the voices might be his mother. I don't know what happened in that room but I know it was real, because dreams don't have smells and mine does, and dreams don't match each other and ours do, and dreams don't leave you sick in the washroom at 3 a.m. This is a memory. All of it. Somebody's memory, leaking into us, and I think—I think it belongs to people who can't remember for themselves anymore. So we'll remember for them. That's the job now. That's the whole job.*

She stopped, breath ragged in the dark.

*One more thing. Tomorrow I have a session. I'm going to tell Voss the dream is almost gone. Nearly gray. I'm going to smile while I say it. If you're hearing this and my dream really is gone someday—if they finally got through—then check Botany Unit 4. Check the paper in the hydroponics washroom, behind the panel over the third stall. Seventeen of us started. Four of us are still writing. Whatever's left of me, it's in there. I was here. I remembered. There's a difference.*

She sealed the file, nested it three layers deep beneath her botany homework, and lay back staring at the dark underside of Deni's bunk.

Deni stirred above her. For a moment Mira thought she'd woken her, and braced herself—but Deni only murmured something in her sleep and rolled over. Murmured, Mira was almost sure, though she'd never prove it: a number. Somewhere in the high hundreds. Counting.

Even patched, some part of Deni was still counting. Still keeping the tally the way the voices in the dream kept theirs. The rewrites could sand the surface smooth, but the tally lived deeper than sanding reached.

Mira closed her eyes. Tomorrow she would perform forgetting. Tonight she had performed remembering, and remembering, she was beginning to understand, was not a thing that happened to you.

It was a thing you did. Nightly. Deliberately. Like tending a fire.

She fell asleep with her hands curled loosely at her chest, cupped around a coal no wind could find.

* * *

Two mornings later, everything changed again—because Ms. Adeyemi called her out of botany class, alone, and walked her down the corridor in silence, and stopped by the supply cabinet where the heavy fiber paper lived, and stood looking at the thin gaps on the shelf where stolen sheets should have been, and then looked at Mira with an expression Mira had no name for yet, an expression like a dam deciding.

"Mira," Ms. Adeyemi said, very quietly. "How many of you are still writing?"

And Mira, who had lied to a counselor for two months, who had built an archive out of whispers and stolen paper, who had sworn never to trust an adult again as long as she lived—heard her own voice answer, small and cracking and true:

"All of us, ma'am. Seventeen. We never stopped."

Ms. Adeyemi closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, and she said the last thing Mira expected:

"Then it's time someone told you what you've been dreaming."
