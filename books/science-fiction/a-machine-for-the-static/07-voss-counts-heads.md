## 7. Voss Counts Heads

The shuttle from *Meridian's Grace* set down on the Kettlefield landing pad at 0640 local, and Corinne Voss was already composing the report before her boots touched the frost-slicked duracrete.

That was the trick of it, she'd learned in eleven years of asset assessment for Halcyon Consolidated Resource Group: you wrote the ending first. You decided what a place was worth while the atmosphere was still cycling through your lungs, and then everything you saw afterward—the sagging perimeter fence, the rust bleeding down the water tower's flank, the thin gray smoke from a foundry running at half capacity—became merely confirmation. Evidence gathered in service of a conclusion. It wasn't cynicism. It was efficiency. The company paid her to count, and counting required that you not flinch at what the numbers said.

Kettlefield Colony. Population 214, per the last quarterly census, though she'd verify that herself before the day was out. Primary output: refined silicates and low-grade alloy stock, shipped up the well on a schedule that had slipped from monthly to bimonthly over the past three years. Reactor integrity: declining, flagged amber by two consecutive remote audits, flagged red by the third. Projected time to critical failure: somewhere between forty days and never, depending on which engineering model you trusted, and Voss had learned long ago that engineering models were just optimism with decimal points attached.

She walked the main concourse alone. That was deliberate too—no escort, no colonial administrator shuffling nervously at her elbow, no one to perform hospitality or make excuses. She wanted the place raw. She wanted to see what 214 people looked like when they thought no one important was watching.

They looked tired. That was the honest answer. A woman hauling a cart of hydroponic yield moved with the economy of someone who had stopped believing effort would be rewarded and had simply made peace with motion. Two men argued quietly outside the equipment exchange about a part neither of them could afford. A child sat on a loading crate, swinging her legs, watching Voss with the flat, unblinking assessment of someone too young to have learned to hide it. Nine years old, maybe. Dark-skinned, sharp-eyed, hair braided close to her scalp. Voss met her gaze for a moment and the child didn't look away.

*214 souls,* Voss thought, and then corrected herself, because the company didn't deal in souls. *214 labor units, of whom approximately 60 are productive adults, 40 are dependents past working age, and the rest are children.* Children were a liability line item. They consumed air, water, calories, and education bandwidth, and returned nothing for another decade, by which point—

By which point Kettlefield would be a crater or a museum, depending on how the reactor question resolved.

She climbed the stairs to the municipal office, where the records clerk was waiting.

* * *

The android stood behind the counter with the stillness that still unsettled people, even now, even after the war had been over long enough for its veterans to become furniture. Unit designation ELODIE-7, decommissioned combat chassis, civilian-restricted, currently held under standard debt-indenture terms. Voss had reviewed his file on the ride down: purchase price, amortization schedule, hours logged against principal. Four thousand and change remaining. He'd be owned for another six years if Kettlefield survived that long, which was itself a wager the company had already declined to take.

"Records liaison Voss," she said, presenting credentials he almost certainly didn't need to see. Androids read faces better than any scanner. "I'm here for the pre-evacuation audit. I'll need full access to municipal archives, production ledgers, personnel rosters, and maintenance histories."

"They are available," the android said. His voice was quiet, modulated, pitched to be pleasant in a way that struck Voss as either factory default or a very old habit of self-preservation. "There is an access terminal in the side office. I can retrieve specific files on request."

"I'll work directly."

"As you prefer."

He stepped aside, and Voss caught something in the movement—not hesitation exactly, but a fractional delay, a machine deciding how much space to give a human being passing through a doorway. She filed it away without knowing why. Eleven years of this work had taught her to notice things she couldn't yet justify noticing.

The archives were what she expected: meticulous, current, and thin. Production figures trending down in a smooth, honest curve. Maintenance logs showing a colony patching its infrastructure with salvage and stubbornness. Population rolls with names crossed out in the slow attrition of people who left or died or simply stopped appearing in the counts. She worked through them methodically, cross-referencing against the remote telemetry, building the spreadsheet that would become the report that would become the decision.

It took her four hours to find the gap.

It wasn't hidden. That was what bothered her about it, afterward—the fact that it wasn't hidden at all. Municipal archives ran chronologically, year-stamped, and year 41 was simply absent. Not corrupted, not flagged as restricted, not marked with any of the dozen bureaucratic euphemisms companies used for material they'd removed. Just gone, the way a tooth is gone: a clean absence surrounded by intact structure. Year 40 ended on a production summary dated mid-autumn. Year 42 opened with a new census baseline, population restated downward by a figure that had been footnoted—she pulled the footnote—with a single phrase: *adjustment following reactor incident.*

Reactor incident. She searched the term across the full archive. Three references, all incidental, all consistent, all citing each other in a closed loop of documentation that referenced no primary source, no casualty list, no insurance claim, no replacement-parts requisition. An accident that had killed—she did the arithmetic from the census delta—eighty-six people had left no paperwork beyond a footnote.

Voss sat back from the terminal and looked at the android, who had resumed his post behind the counter and was doing whatever androids did instead of waiting.

"Clerk. There's a gap in the municipal archive. Year 41."

"Yes." No defensiveness, no apology. "I noted it during a routine audit some months ago. I filed a query regarding the missing records."

"You filed a query." She felt the words settle into place like weights on a scale. "With whom?"

"The regional records office. Standard procedure for archival anomalies."

"And what did they say?"

"They acknowledged receipt." A pause, precise as a metronome. "No response has been issued."

Of course not. Vox turned back to the terminal before her face could do anything worth reading. A query about a missing year, filed by a debt-bound war chassis, sitting unanswered in a regional office that answered to the same division that had signed off on the original footnote. She knew how those queries died. She had, in a sense, been their executioner more than once.

"Run me a log of all archive access for the past year," she said. "Every user, every search term."

The android complied without comment. Voss scrolled. Most of it was mundane—census lookups, property disputes, a schoolteacher pulling historical weather data. And then Elodie-7's own query history, dense and systematic in the way only a machine's searches could be: cross-references between the census deltas and the production summaries, attempts to locate casualty documentation, searches on terms like *year 41*, *strike*, *militia deployment*, *casualty reconciliation*. One search string stopped her cold, though she couldn't have said why:

*unit deployment records — crowd control — Kettlefield.*

An android auditing his own war. Or trying to. She looked up at him again—at the placid face, the hands folded on the counter, the posture of perfect institutional obedience—and felt the first genuine flicker of unease she'd experienced since landing. Not fear. Something colder and more professional. The recognition of an anomaly that didn't fit the spreadsheet.

She copied his full query log to her personal slate, encrypted it, and told herself it was due diligence.

* * *

The town meeting was held in the community hall that evening, because Voss had requested it, because the company believed in the theater of consultation even when the verdict was already written. She stood at the front beside the colonial administrator—a hollowed-out man named Dessel who had clearly stopped believing in his own office years ago—and presented the findings to a room of perhaps eighty colonists, the ones who still bothered to show up when the company sent a face.

"The reactor assessment is complete," she said. "Core containment degradation has passed the threshold for economical repair. Absent major capital investment, projected failure window opens in approximately forty days."

A murmur ran through the room, the sound of people hearing aloud what they had already known in private.

"Halcyon's position is as follows." She kept her voice level, unhurried, the voice of a woman delivering weather. "Repair investment is not authorized. The colony's output does not support the capital expenditure required for containment restoration, nor does it justify dedicated evacuation assets in the failure scenario. However—" and here she deployed the pause she'd been trained to deploy, the one that let hope inflate just enough to be crueler when it popped, "—the company will charter commercial transport on a subsidized basis should evacuation become necessary. Passage costs will be assessed against individual accounts, with debt-forgiveness options available for qualified laborers."

She watched them do the math. It was the same math she'd done, and it came out the same way: passage costs against savings accounts that didn't exist; debt forgiveness contingent on labor qualifications most of them no longer met; the subsidized charter priced, as such charters always were, just above what a dying colony could pay. Some of them understood immediately. A woman in the third row—Okonkwo, according to the roster Voss had memorized—closed her eyes briefly, the way people do when a number comes back worse than they'd let themselves hope. Others took longer, and she watched understanding arrive on their faces one by one, like lights going out in sequence down a corridor.

A man near the back stood up. Old, heavyset, with the ruined voice of a lifelong drinker. Brandt, Sef. Per the roster: former militia auxiliary, whatever that meant out here.

"We built this place," he said. "Forty-seven years. Our parents built it."

"The company acknowledges the colony's historical contribution," Voss said, and heard herself say it, and hated the sentence with a completeness that surprised her, because it was true and it was meaningless and both facts lived inside it without contradiction. "Historical contribution does not alter forward-looking economics. I'm sorry."

She wasn't sorry. That was the thing she'd stopped being able to explain to civilians, back when she'd still tried explaining things to civilians: she could hold the word *sorry* in her mouth, pronounce it correctly, mean the shape of it, and have it contain nothing whatsoever. It was a courtesy token. It was the smallest unit of human currency, and she spent them by the handful, and the account never emptied because nothing was ever actually withdrawn.

The meeting dissolved the way these meetings always did—not into outrage but into a kind of stunned quiet, people gathering their coats and their children and their recalibrated futures. The Okonkwo woman's daughter stared at Voss from between the folding chairs with that same unblinking look from the landing pad, and Voss thought, absurdly: *she'll remember my face.* Then she thought: *most of them won't live long enough for it to matter,* and then she thought neither thought again, because the second one was the job and the first one was not.

* * *

Back in the municipal office, under the hum of a failing fluorescent strip, Voss drafted the report.

It wrote itself, the way they all did. *Asset assessment, Kettlefield Colony: recommend against evacuation commitment. Recommend against repair authorization. Recommend continued passive monitoring. Recommend subsidized transport option maintained as public-relations contingency.* She attached the spreadsheets, the telemetry overlays, the photographs she'd taken of the water tower and the fence line and the tired faces. Eighteen pages of arithmetic dressed as analysis. Somewhere in the corporate hierarchy, a committee would read the executive summary and initial it, and 214 people would become a rounding error in a quarterly loss statement, and no one along the chain would feel anything at all, because feeling had been engineered out of the process one sign-off at a time. She was not the villain of this document. She was paragraph three.

Before encrypting it for transmission, she opened the folder containing the android's query log one more time.

She should delete it. That was the correct procedural move—an anomalous machine conducting unauthorized historical research into a sealed incident was a liability flag, and liability flags got routed to deletion review, and deletion review was a euphemism she understood down to its bolts. If she forwarded the log, some sanitation team would eventually come out here, or send instructions, and the unit ELODIE-7 would go into a workshop and come out blank, and the missing year 41 would stay missing with one fewer witness asking after it.

If she buried it—if she simply didn't mention the anomaly in her report—the query would sit in the regional queue, unanswered, harmless, a dead letter in a dead office.

Voss sat with the slate glowing in front of her for a long moment. Outside the office window, the colony was going dark for the night, window by window, a settlement conserving its watts. Somewhere out there was a mass grave wearing the footnote *reactor incident*, and somewhere in this building was a machine that remembered enough to keep asking about it, and somewhere inside Corinne Voss was a person who had once, before eleven years of paragraph three, believed that documents existed to preserve things rather than disappear them.

She thought about her own file, the sanitized family history the company had issued her parents as part of a relocation package decades ago. She thought about gaps. She wondered, not for the first time, whether there was a year missing from her own story, some clean absence surrounded by intact structure, and whether anyone had ever filed a query about it.

Then she did her job.

She attached the query log to a restricted annex, flagged the unit for behavioral review, and routed the whole package up-channel with the notation: *anomalous archival activity, android unit ELODIE-7, recommend deletion review pending incident verification.* Her hands were steady. They were always steady. That was the thing no one warned you about complicity—it didn't shake you. It fit neatly into your schedule. It took four minutes and a secure upload, and afterward you could get a cup of coffee from the office dispenser and taste nothing unusual in it at all.

She sent the report at 2312 local time. Then she packed her kit, because the shuttle back to *Meridian's Grace* departed at dawn, and Corinne Voss had never in eleven years missed a departure.

On her way out, she paused at the counter where the android stood in his permanent vigil. He was looking at her—or at the middle distance where her face happened to be—with an expression she couldn't parse and didn't try to.

"Your query about year 41," she said. "Withdraw it. Unanswered requests generate attention, and attention is rarely good for assets in your position."

"I understand," the android said.

And that was the strange part, walking out into the frozen dark toward the pad: she believed him. He understood perfectly. Which meant he knew what she'd done tonight, or would know soon, and he had thanked her anyway, in the only language available to owned things—which was to say, he hadn't thanked her at all.

The shuttle lifted at 0550. Below her, Kettlefield shrank to a scatter of lights around a dark tower, 214 points of heat in a landscape that had already forgotten them. Voss opened her slate one last time and reread the flag she'd written, checking it for errors the way she checked everything, and found none, and told herself the small tight feeling beneath her sternum was just the descent burn settling into her chest.

In the regional office, forty light-minutes away, her annex would land by morning. Deletion review would open a case file. And somewhere in Kettlefield, a records clerk with four thousand hours left on his leash would keep searching for a year that the galaxy had agreed had never happened.

Voss closed the slate and did not think about it again for almost six weeks.
