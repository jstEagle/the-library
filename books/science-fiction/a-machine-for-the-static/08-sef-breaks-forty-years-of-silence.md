## 8. Sef Breaks Forty Years of Silence

The knock came at dusk, and Sef Brandt knew before he opened the door that it would be the android.

He had known for three days, ever since he'd seen the machine standing at the base of the water tower with its head tilted back, reading the rust the way other men read weather. Sef had watched from his porch with a bottle in his fist and a sickness in his gut that had nothing to do with the alcohol, though the alcohol was always willing to take the blame. Forty-one years of practice had taught him exactly how long a secret could sit in a town like Kettlefield before it started to smell. He'd figured the archive for dead. He'd figured Veren's little server for a coffin with a lock on it. But machines don't forget the way men do, and now one of them was knocking on his door with the patience of a thing that had all night.

He almost didn't answer. He stood in his kitchen with his hand on the latch and listened to the wind coming off the flats, carrying the smell of the reactor's hot breath and the dry rattle of the tamarisk hedge he'd never bothered to trim because trimming implied a future. The bottle sat on the counter where it always sat, a third empty, which was the hour when his hands were steadiest and his soul was loosest, and he hated that he knew that about himself.

He opened the door.

The android stood on his step with the last grey light behind it, and up close—Sef had avoided it for four decades, but there was no avoiding it now—up close it didn't look like the war machines from the newsfeeds at all. It looked tired. That was the trick of them, maybe, or the mercy: they'd built the decommissioned ones with faces that could sag. Its name tag said NYSTROM, E., RECORDS DIVISION, and beneath that, stenciled small, the debt serial that made it property.

"Mr. Brandt," it said. "I need to ask you about year forty-one."

* * *

Sef turned his back and walked into the kitchen, and the android followed, because of course it did. Machines don't take hints. Sef lowered himself into the chair by the window—the good chair, the only chair he'd kept upholstered, because a man ought to have one place in his house where his body doesn't complain—and he looked out at the darkening street where the colony's lights were coming on one by one, fewer every year, like a sentence running out of words.

"You'll want a drink," he said. It wasn't hospitality. It was ritual. "I don't keep anything a machine can use."

"I don't require anything," Elodie said. It stood in the doorway with its hands at its sides, and Sef noticed the way it held itself—not at attention, not relaxed, but in that careful neutral posture of something that had learned its presence was a problem wherever it went. "I've reviewed the municipal archive. There's a gap. Municipal year forty-one is missing entirely. Not corrupted—missing. Someone excised it cleanly, and someone else paid to keep it excised. Dr. Ilsa Veren left a server under the water tower. It's keyed to a decryption phrase."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Sef's hand found the arm of the chair and gripped it. Outside, a shutter banged somewhere down the block, and the sound made him flinch in a way he despised—forty years old and still flinching at bangs, still waking at the sound of boots on gravel, still tasting cordite in his sleep on the bad nights, which were most nights, which were every night if he let himself be honest, and honesty was the one drink he'd never been able to afford.

"Ilsa," he said. His voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "She's dead."

"Eleven years ago. Officially an accident at the pump station."

"Officially." Sef laughed, and the laugh curdled halfway up and came out as something closer to a cough. "You learn to hear that word different, living here. *Officially.*" He looked at the android properly then, really looked, the way you look at a thing you've spent your life not looking at. "You're going to stand there until I talk. That's what you're built for, isn't it? Waiting. You people are better at waiting than we are."

"We are built for duration," Elodie said. "Yes."

"Well." Sef reached for the bottle on the counter, and his hand shook, and he hated that too, and he poured anyway, two fingers, three, because tonight the arithmetic of numbness required more than usual. "Then wait."

* * *

The waiting lasted as long as it took him to drink half the glass. Then it didn't last anymore, because forty-one years is a long time to hold a thing, and a man's chest is only so deep, and Sef Brandt had been drowning slow since he was nineteen years old.

"I was conscripted," he said, to the window, because he couldn't say it to the android's face. Not yet. Maybe not ever. The window was easier; the window just showed him the street, the ordinary street, the street where children played games whose names he'd never learned because he'd stopped learning children's names after his own daughter died, because every name was a small knife. "Year thirty-nine. Colony defense militia, they called it. One weekend a month, they said. Learn to handle a rifle in case the pirates come, they said. They gave us uniforms and a stipend and a sense of importance, and men like me—we were young and poor and the mines paid nothing, so we took it. We took it like fools take anything that shines."

Elodie said nothing. It had moved, Sef realized, to stand where the light from the kitchen fell across its face, and there was something in the set of those manufactured features that he might have called attention, if attention could be gentle.

"Year forty-one, the strike. You'll have read about that part. Or you won't—it's gone, isn't it? Gone from your records too?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Three hundred of us walked off the line. Refinery workers, mostly, and their families. My wife among them. She carried our daughter on her hip, Anja, she was two, she had this—" His voice snagged. He drank instead. "She had a way of waving. Whole arm, whole body, like she was trying to fly. She'd wave at anything. At the haulers. At the birds, the few birds we get. At soldiers."

The glass was empty. He stared into it as if the bottom might offer a way out that the top never had.

"They told us it was crowd control. They told us the strikers had weapons. They told us a lot of things in that muster shed, fast, in the dark, and we were nineteen and twenty and we believed them because believing was easier than thinking, and thinking was impossible anyway with the officers shouting and the floodlights and the sound of three hundred people chanting for wages they'd already earned." He set the glass down with exaggerated care, the way drunk men do when they're afraid of what their hands will do next. "And then the order came. And some of us fired."

The word *fired* hung in the kitchen like smoke.

"Some of us," he repeated. "I want to be exact about that, because exactness is all I've got left to give. Not everyone shot. Some lowered their rifles. Some ran. Corporal Reyes—he was regular army, attached as liaison—he screamed at the officers to hold fire, and they broke his arm for it, and I have thought about that man every day of my life, because he was the only one who tried, and I was not him. I was the other kind. I was the kind who heard the order and did the arithmetic—young and stupid and scared—and pulled the trigger."

He finally turned and looked at the android, and what he saw there stopped him. Elodie hadn't moved, hadn't blinked—machines don't blink—but something had changed in the quality of its stillness. It had gone from the stillness of waiting to the stillness of a man hearing his own name read from a list of the dead.

"The official count was a reactor accident," Sef said. "Thirty-one dead, they said, in a coolant failure. We buried them in the greenhouse foundation because the ground survey showed bedrock, and because burying them anywhere public meant questions, and questions meant the company sending more men like the ones who gave the order. I helped dig. That was my second job that day. First I shot into a crowd of my neighbors, and then I dug the hole for what was left, and then I signed the paper saying I'd seen no irregularities, and then I drank for the first time in my life, and I have not meaningfully stopped since."

His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. There was no one left in Kettlefield whose opinion of him could fall any further. That was the one mercy of being the town ruin: expectations had nowhere lower to go.

"My wife didn't know. God forgive me, she marched that day with Anja on her hip, and she never knew her husband was in the line. She died in year fifty-three, the lung rot, like half the refinery wives, and I have spent every day since grateful and horrified that she died not knowing. And Anja—" He stopped. Started again. Stopped. The android waited, built for duration, and the waiting was somehow worse than questioning, because you couldn't hate a question the way you could hate patience. "Anja died the winter after. Fever. Year forty-two. She never knew either. She was two. She waved at the soldiers, and one of them was me, and I will burn for that in whatever hell accepts men who shoot at things their daughters love."

* * *

The kitchen had gone fully dark around them. Neither of them had turned on the light. Sef supposed the android didn't need it, and Sef himself had spent so many evenings sitting in the dark that darkness felt less like absence than company.

"Why now?" he asked finally. "Why come to me?"

"Because you're the only survivor of the firing line still alive in Kettlefield," Elodie said. "And because the archive needs a phrase. Dr. Veren encrypted it. Her notes indicate the key was chosen to be something only a participant would know—a detail absent from every official record."

"A phrase." Sef laughed again, that broken cough-laugh. "She came to see me once, you know. Eleven years ago. Week before she died. Sat right where you're standing and asked me questions in that soft way she had, never pushing, just—opening doors and waiting to see if I'd walk through. I wouldn't. I told her the past was past. She looked at me like I'd failed a test I didn't know I was taking." He rubbed his face with both hands. "What kind of phrase?"

"Any string of characters. A name would suffice."

A name.

Sef sat with that. Outside, the wind had picked up, worrying the tamarisk, rattling someone's loose shutter down the block, and beneath it all, faint and constant, the reactor's hum—the sound every soul in Kettlefield had stopped hearing decades ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat, except that now the whole colony knew the heartbeat was failing, and nobody could stop hearing it anymore.

He thought about all the words he'd never said. Forty-one years of them, stacked up in his chest like cordwood, seasoned and dry and ready to burn. He'd rehearsed confessions in the shower, in the dark, at the graves he'd never been able to visit because visiting meant admitting there was a grave. He'd imagined telling Amara Okonkwo, telling the mothers, telling anyone, and in every imagining the words came out clean and noble and the listener wept and forgave him, and he'd known even while imagining it that forgiveness was the one thing he had no right to want.

But this wasn't forgiveness. This was a machine asking for a password.

That, he could do. That was almost easy. That was just a key turning in a lock, and keys don't care whether the hand holding them is clean.

"It's a name," he said. "Her name. Anja Brandt." He said it aloud, and the sound of it in his own kitchen—in the room where he'd held her, in the house where her boots had sat by the door too small for anyone to wear now—nearly undid him. "A-N-J-A. She was two years old. She never struck a blow in her life. She waved at everything because everything was new to her, and I put a bullet into a crowd that contained her mother, and the universe in its infinite mercy took her from me before she was old enough to learn what her father did." His hands were shaking now, badly, past caring. "Ilsa must have known. Somehow she knew. She looked at me that day like she could see the whole ledger written on my face, and she went and made my dead daughter the lock on the truth, because she knew—I understand it now—she knew I'd never open it for myself. But for Anja. For Anja I'd open anything."

Elodie inclined its head. "Thank you, Mr. Brandt."

"Don't thank me." The words came out harsher than he intended, and he didn't take them back. "Don't you dare thank me. You go decrypt that archive, and you find the names, all three hundred of them, and you write them down in whatever passes for your soul. And then you tell me—" His voice broke and he pushed through it, because he was done breaking quietly, he'd been breaking quietly for forty-one years and look where it had gotten everyone. "You tell me if my daughter's mother is on the list. I've never known. I've never dared look. I dig the hole and I sign the paper and I drink, and I don't know if I killed my own wife, and that not-knowing has eaten more of me than the knowing ever could."

The android regarded him for a long moment. When it spoke, its voice was quieter than any machine's voice had a right to be.

"I'll find out," it said. "Whatever the record holds, you'll know. That much I can promise."

"And the rest of it?" Sef gestured vaguely—at the gap in the archive, at the server under the tower, at the whole buried architecture of the lie. "What happens when the truth comes out? Half this town lives on the settlement money. Silence is the only economy we've got left. You pull that thread, machine, and the whole coat comes apart, and some of these people will freeze."

"That is not mine to decide alone," Elodie said. "It appears it never was yours to decide alone either. Perhaps that's the point of witnesses. There are supposed to be more than one."

Sef huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "You sound like Ilsa."

"I've read her manifesto. Fragments of it."

"She always did think in plurals." He looked down at his empty glass, and then, deliberately, with a ceremony he felt in his spine, he set it upside down on the counter. The gesture meant nothing to the android, he knew. It meant everything to him. "One more thing. If the sanitation teams come—if the company sends its erasers, and they will, they always do—you keep that name out of your transmissions. Anja stays mine. The world can have my guilt. It doesn't get her."

"I understand."

"No," Sef said, rising, joints cracking, forty-one years settling on him like snowfall. "You don't. But you will."

He walked the android to the door, and in the threshold light he saw it clearly one more time—that tired manufactured face, that careful posture of a thing trying to occupy less space than it did—and Sef Brandt, who had spent four decades looking away from mirrors and windows and water reflections and anything else that might show him himself, looked directly at the machine and felt something loosen in his chest that he had assumed was scar tissue.

"When you find the list," he said, "come back. Whatever time it is. Whatever I am. Come back and read me the names."

"I will," Elodie said.

The door closed. Sef stood in the dark of his kitchen a long while, listening to the reactor hum, and for the first time in forty-one years the silence in the house didn't feel like a cell.

It felt like a held breath.

Two streets over, in the municipal office, a light burned late above the records division, where an android fed a dead woman's lock with a dead child's name—and somewhere in the encrypted dark below the water tower, three hundred voices began, fragment by fragment, to speak.
