## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The beacon woke him, as it always did, four minutes before the alarm.

Gideon Kavanagh lay in his bunk in the dark of the Vesperine's crew deck and listened to it sing. That was the only word for it—*sing*—though the instrument panel called it a harmonic carrier pulse, 1.42-second interval, phase-locked to nothing in particular. Eleven years of listening had turned the numbers into music whether he wanted them to or not. The beacon had a voice like a struck glass, thin and clear and endless, and this morning—or what the ship's clock insisted was morning, cycle 4,017—it was a half-tone flat.

He was out of bed before the alarm could chime. Some habits you keep because they matter. Most habits you keep because they are the only hands on the wheel.

"Good morning," he said to the empty cabin, because his mother had raised him to greet the day and there was no one left to break him of it.

The corridor lights came up around him in their slow staggered sequence, deck one through deck five, a wave of pale gold rolling ahead of his footsteps like something following. He'd stopped finding that eerie around year three. Now it felt like company.

The beacon room sat at the Vesperine's spine, a cylinder of dark glass and humming copper with a single chair bolted dead center, facing the transmitter array. He settled into it. The chair had molded itself to his body over a decade—not literally, though sometimes he suspected the foam of remembering him—and he let out the breath he'd been holding since sleep.

"Let's hear you, then."

He keyed the diagnostic. The array's tone rolled through the chamber, and there it was: flat by 0.3 percent, drifting worse. He pulled up the calibration lattice and began the work his hands knew better than his mind did—nudging the resonators, damping the harmonics, walking the frequency back up the ladder rung by rung until the note stood straight again. It took forty minutes. It always took forty minutes. When he finished, the beacon sang true, and Gideon sat back and felt the small clean satisfaction of a thing done well, which was, some cycles, the only feeling available to him.

The maintenance queue blinked amber at the edge of his vision. He sighed and opened it.

**REQUEST 88-4412: COOLANT LOOP B, DECK 3 — PRESSURE ANOMALY.**
**REQUEST 88-4413: GALLEY UNIT — DISPENSER JAM, BAY 2.**
**REQUEST 88-4414: HOLD 4 — ATMOSPHERIC SEAL, POD RACK NINE — INSPECTION DUE.**

He knew before he checked that all three were phantoms. The ship generated them the way a body generates dreams—some quirk of the maintenance intelligence, orphaned routines still running on schedule for a crew of forty that had been dead or scattered since before Gideon's first shift. Request 88-4412 referenced a coolant loop that had been decommissioned eight years ago. The galley dispenser jam had been "reported" every ninety days for a decade; he'd long ago learned to clear the fault code without visiting it. And Hold 4, Pod Rack Nine—

That one he didn't clear. That one he answered in person, the way you answer a letter from someone who might still be alive.

He logged the responses first, because ritual is mostly paperwork:

**88-4412: Loop decommissioned. Request void.**
**88-4413: Dispenser serviced. Fault cleared. (Fault does not exist.)**
**88-4414: Inspection scheduled. Will attend personally.**

Then he ate breakfast alone at the mess table, as he had eaten eleven years of breakfasts alone at the mess table. The galley unit produced eggs that tasted like the idea of eggs and coffee that tasted like regret, and he ate them looking out the viewport at the slow wheeling of a starfield that never changed its mind about anything. Somewhere behind him, four hundred people slept in the hold, and somewhere ahead of him, the beacon sang to an audience of vacuum.

He washed his dish. He dried his dish. He put his dish away in the cabinet that held exactly one of everything.

* * *

Hold 4 smelled like cold metal and antiseptic and, faintly, underneath everything, like sleep—the way a bedroom smells when someone has been in it a long time. Four hundred pods stood in ranks of ten, floor to ceiling, their frost-fogged faces lit from within by the soft green of life-sign indicators. The ship kept the hold at two degrees above freezing. His breath made ghosts.

He walked the racks the way he always did, port side first, reading names. He knew them all. Not their histories—he knew almost nothing of their histories—but their names, their pod numbers, their frost patterns, the particular way each indicator pulsed. OYELARAN, T., whose heartbeat ran slow and steady as a metronome. OKONKWO, S., whose pod fogged unevenly on the lower-left corner no matter how many times he buffed it. The twins, HALLORAN, M. and HALLORAN, J., side by side, their vitals so synchronized that for the first year he'd checked three times daily to make sure the display wasn't duplicating a feed.

He talked as he went. Low, unhurried, the voice you'd use in a sickroom.

"Morning, Tomas. Cold enough for you? Me too." Past the Oyelaran pod. "Sefa—your corner's fogging again. I'll get to it." A tap of two fingers on frosted glass, gentle as knocking on a door you don't expect to be answered. "Mariam, Jonas. Still holding hands in there? Good. Keep it up."

Four hundred greetings. It took him most of an hour if he did them properly, and he always did them properly, because a man who skips even one name starts skipping two, and a man who skips two wakes up one cycle unable to remember why he bothers at all.

At last, Rack Nine.

Pod nine stood at the end of the row, and like all the others it bore a stenciled label in the old corporate hand:

**KAVANAGH, G. (0)**

The zero had bothered him for years before he'd stopped asking the ship about it. Cargo manifests listed no other Kavanagh. Crew records listed none either. Just this one pod, this one sleeping man with his face—Gideon's face, unmistakably, down to the scar over the left eyebrow that Gideon had earned falling off a roof at nine years old—and a designation that looked less like a name than a coordinate. An origin point. A zero.

"Morning," he told himself.

The face behind the frost did not answer. It never did. But Gideon leaned his forehead against the glass anyway, the way he had done ten thousand mornings, and felt the faint vibration of the pod's circulators through his skull, and pretended—for the length of one breath—that the man inside was dreaming of him, or would wake someday and ask him questions Gideon couldn't answer, like *who are you* and *why do we have the same hands.*

"I calibrated the beacon today," he said. "It was flat again. I think it's getting tired. I know how it feels."

The green light pulsed. Fifty-two beats a minute. Steady as ever.

"There were phantom requests again," he went on, because talking to yourself was madness but talking to the sleepers was routine, and the difference between those two things was apparently just consistency. "Three of them. One of them was about your rack, so here I am. You're welcome."

He laughed, a little. It echoed strangely in the hold, off all that frost and silence, and died fast.

Eleven years. He'd signed on for a navigator's berth on a colony support vessel—a good contract, hazard pay, a strange hollow feeling in the chest he'd carried out of Earth's last decades and assumed was grief and assumed everyone had. The mission brief had been vague even by corporate standards: maintain transit beacon, tend cryogenic cargo, await fleet rendezvous. The rendezvous date had come and gone six years ago. He'd filed inquiries. They'd returned, eventually, form letters thanking him for his continued diligence.

*Diligence.* The word had begun to taste like a leash.

But the work was good work. The sleepers were safe. The beacon sang. And there was a peace in it—there genuinely was—a peace he'd built brick by brick out of routine and named *contentment* so he wouldn't have to look at what it actually was. He pressed his palm flat against pod nine's glass one more time.

"Someday," he said quietly, "you and I are going to have a conversation, you and I. And you're going to tell me why they gave you my face and a zero for a name. Deal?"

The frost breathed. The light pulsed. He straightened, nodded to the rank of sleepers as a whole—*good day, all of you*—and turned back toward the corridor and the beacon and the long gold afternoon of another identical cycle.

He was three steps from the hold door when the comm panel on the wall chirped.

Not the maintenance queue tone. Not the ship's clock. Something else—a raw incoming signal, unscheduled, arriving on a band so deep and dead that the panel seemed embarrassed to be receiving on it. Gideon froze. In eleven years, the Vesperine had received exactly four inbound transmissions: three automated fleet pings and one misdirected advertisement for orbital insurance.

This was none of those.

The signal resolved on the display into a waveform, and the waveform into something structured, rhythmic, repeating—and Gideon Kavanagh, who had not felt his heart move in years, felt it move now, because buried in the cadence of that dead-band signal was a pattern he knew in his bones before he knew it in his head.

A lilt. A rise and fall. The rhythm of a language with no living speakers, sung to him in a kitchen that no longer existed, by a woman who used to hum it while she worked.

Old Meridian.

Someone out there was speaking to him in his mother's tongue.
