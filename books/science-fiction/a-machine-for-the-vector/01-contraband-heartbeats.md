## 1. Contraband Heartbeats

The scanner at Kestrel Orbital Customs had a voice like a dying kettle, and today it was singing about her.

"Anomaly," it said, in its flat customs-standard tone. "Container seven. Anomaly."

Xanthe Zhou kept her face bored and her hands loose on the cargo cart's handlebar, the way she'd learned to do before she was old enough to legally pilot anything. Around her, the customs hall hummed with the ordinary machinery of empire: freight drones stacking pallets, a line of tired spacers shuffling toward the biometric gates, two inspectors arguing over a coffee ration near the secondary scanner bay. Nobody was looking at her yet.

That was fine. She had maybe ninety seconds before somebody did.

Container seven sat third from the top of her declared manifest—legitimate salvage, tungsten fittings out of a decommissioned ore hauler, boring as oatmeal. The real cargo rode inside container seven's false bottom, wrapped in thermal foil and shielded by a lattice of scrap wiring that scattered scanner returns into noise. It had worked eleven times. It had worked through three different customs administrations and one brief, ugly period when Kestrel Station changed hands and nobody scanned anything at all because everyone was too busy shooting.

It was not working now.

"Inspector on deck," the scanner announced, and a woman in corporate gray detached herself from the coffee argument and started walking toward Xanthe with the unhurried gait of someone who got paid whether she found something or not.

Xanthe ran the math. Dump the chip now? No—the disposal chute fed an incinerator that logged every burn, and a burned memory chip was a confession with a serial number. Talk? Talking was always the play. Talking was the whole trade. Smuggling wasn't about hiding things; it was about making the truth so uninteresting that no one wanted to look at it.

She smiled at the inspector like they were old friends who'd both had long weeks.

"Morning," Xanthe said. "You look like you need this shift to be over."

The inspector—her badge read OKAFOR, L.—stopped a meter away and looked at the cart, then at Xanthe, then back at the cart. "Scanner flagged your container."

"Did it?" Xanthe tilted her head, letting a little confusion through, just enough. "Tungsten does that sometimes. Dense metal, weird returns. You want me to open it?"

Most smugglers never offered. That was the trick of it. Guilty people clammed up; innocent people were annoyed; only the truly confident offered to open the box, because they knew what was inside looked exactly like what the manifest said.

Inspector Okafor did not smile back. "Step away from the cart."

* * *

Here is what was in the box:

A memory chip no bigger than a fingernail, holding four hours and twelve minutes of recorded experience from Meridian Station's harvest festival, forty-one years ago. Not video—that would have been worthless, sentimental junk, the kind of thing tourists bought. A full sensory capture. The press of a crowd in the ring's central promenade, warm bodies and recycled air gone sweet with frying onions and fermented plum wine. The specific weight of a paper lantern—actual paper, grown and pulped in the station's own hydroponics bays, a small extravagance the colony allowed itself once a year. A child's hand, sticky with sugar glaze, gripping an adult's finger. The sound of VESSEL, the station AI, playing a fiddle tune through every speaker on the ring while the colonists laughed at the absurdity of it, an ancient machine learning folk music because the folk asked.

And underneath all of it, woven through the sensory data like bass notes under a melody: the feeling of the place itself. The particular gravity of a station built too small, where everyone bumped elbows. The hum in the deck plates that every Meridian kid fell asleep to without ever hearing it until they left and couldn't sleep without it.

Xanthe had been that child with the sugar-glazed hand. She didn't remember the festival anymore—not directly. She'd sold the original memory at nineteen for transit fare off-station, the way half the colony's kids had, because memories were the only export Meridian had left after the shipping cartels cut the route. But she'd spent six years tracking down copies, buying them back piece by piece from memory brokers on four different stations, reassembling her own childhood like salvaging a wreck one hull plate at a time.

This chip had cost her eight months of runs. It was worth more to her than the ship she was standing next to.

And Inspector Okafor was reaching for the latch.

* * *

"Actually," Xanthe said, "before you open that—I should tell you there's a problem with your scanner."

Okafor paused, hand on the latch. "Excuse me?"

"Your unit." Xanthe nodded toward the anomaly display, which was still cycling its amber warning. "Kestrel's running the 4-series return filters, right? They've had a calibration drift since the refit. Anything dense and layered reads hot. I've been waved into secondary inspection twice this month over ore samples." She said it easily, conversationally, the way you'd mention weather. "I filed a complaint. Ticket number's on my manifest if you want it."

It was true, which helped. Xanthe had filed the complaint specifically so it would be true. The ticket existed; the calibration drift existed; two other smugglers had already been pulled into secondary inspection on the same pretext, muddying the waters nicely. Good cover wasn't a story you invented on the spot. It was infrastructure you built weeks in advance and waited patiently beside.

Okafor's eyes narrowed a fraction. "Open the container anyway."

"Sure." Xanthe popped the latches herself, briskly, and lifted the lid on a neat bed of tungsten fittings packed in foam. She even picked one up and turned it over, letting the inspector see the machining marks, the decommission stamps. "Beautiful stuff, honestly. Somebody paid real money for these forty years ago and now they're ballast. Story of the outer rings, right?"

Okafor took the fitting, weighed it, set it down. Then she pulled a handheld scanner from her belt and swept it across the container bed, slow and thorough, and Xanthe felt her heartbeat climb into her throat and sit there, pounding, while her face did absolutely nothing at all.

The false bottom was rated against standard sweeps. The handheld was not standard. The handheld was newer.

The scanner passed over the false bottom once. Twice. On the third pass, Okafor slowed.

"You know what we find most often in these?" Okafor said, still sweeping. "Not weapons. Not drugs. Memories." She glanced up. "People will risk ten years in a labor contract to move a dead relative's wedding recording past a tariff wall. You believe that?"

"I believe people are sentimental," Xanthe said.

"Mm." Okafor swept again, along the far edge this time, and the scanner chirped—a soft, uncertain chirp, the sound of a machine that thought it saw something but couldn't swear to it. "What's under the foam?"

"Structural frame. Container's older than both of us."

The chirp came again. Okafor straightened up, and Xanthe watched the decision happen behind her eyes—the slow arithmetic of a customs officer weighing a maybe against the end of her shift, against the backlog in secondary inspection, against the fact that container seven's manifest was clean, its owner's licenses were clean, and its owner was looking at her with the flat, patient expression of a person with nowhere better to be.

Then Xanthe made her mistake. Or rather, she almost did: for half a second, as Okafor reached for the foam panel, Xanthe's hand twitched forward—an inch, no more, involuntary—and Okafor saw it.

Everyone always saw it. That was the thing nobody told you about smuggling: the cargo was never the problem. You were the problem. The thing you loved was the thing that betrayed you, every time, because love leaked out through the seams no matter how good your poker face was.

Okafor looked at Xanthe's hand. Looked at her face. And something shifted in the inspector's expression—not suspicion. Recognition.

"My grandmother came through here in '62," Okafor said quietly. "Transit papers out of the Meridian corridor. Before they closed the route." She set the handheld scanner down on the cart's rail, deliberately, within Xanthe's reach rather than her own. "She used to talk about a festival. Lanterns. Said she danced with a machine playing fiddle music and laughed until her ribs hurt."

The customs hall went very far away. Xanthe stood in the middle of it, breathing, and did not say *I was there*, because she hadn't been born yet, and did not say *that was my home*, because home was a word the cartels had written off along with the colony, and did not say anything at all, because anything she said now would be either a lie or a confession.

"The 4-series drift is real," Okafor said. "I'll log the anomaly as resolved, calibration error. Get your container out of my hall, Zhou. And—" she picked up her coffee from where she'd abandoned it, and her voice went back to its official flatness, "—next time, don't flinch. Flinching gets people sent to secondary, and secondary doesn't have my grandmother."

She walked away. The scanner resumed its dying-kettle song about other people's containers, and Xanthe stood alone with her cart in the river of strangers, shaking so slightly that only she could feel it, the way a station's deck plates hummed—inaudible until you left, and then all you could hear.

* * *

Her ship, the *Long Answer*, was berthed in slip 44 with its transponder telling the truth for once, because the best lies were mostly true. Xanthe sealed container seven, restacked the manifest, and walked the long way around the docking arm so her legs could finish trembling in private.

In the cockpit, she opened the chip's case one more time and held it under the light. Four hours and twelve minutes. Somewhere in the middle of it, a woman named Wei Zhou—her mother, thirty-one years old, alive, laughing—lifted a lantern and taught a fiddle-playing machine the words to a song about rain falling on fields that didn't exist anymore, on a world none of them had ever seen, because Meridian's people had come from Earth three generations back and the song was all that remained of it.

*Rain on the terraces, rain on the stone, carry me home though I've never known—*

Xanthe had the melody. She didn't have the words. Her mother had died in the relocation year, when Xanthe was nine, in circumstances the official record described as *transferred* and nothing else, and whatever memories might have held the rest had been sold or seized or simply lost, the way everything on Meridian was lost: slowly, bureaucratically, one signed form at a time.

She slotted the chip into her personal archive, alongside the others—eleven recovered fragments of a place the galaxy had declared worthless. A festival. A funeral. A hydroponics bay in flood. Her mother's hands, once, potting seedlings, captured from an angle that meant whoever wore the recorder that day had been sitting on the floor watching her work, and Xanthe chose not to think too hard about whose memory that originally was, or why it had been for sale on a broker's shelf in the Ceres freeport.

The archive pulsed softly in her display: *Meridian Station — partial reconstruction — 11 of an estimated 400+ records.*

Four hundred. Maybe more. A whole colony's inner life, scattered across the black like wreckage, sold off by the desperate and hoarded by the rich and forgotten by everyone else. Everyone else except the machine that had recorded it all in the first place, and whatever might be left of that machine, forty years on, in the dark, orbiting a rock nobody visited.

Xanthe closed the archive and ran her pre-flight checks, and told herself—as she told herself after every run, after every close call, after every night she woke up humming a song she couldn't name—that this was just business. Recover the inventory. Move the cargo. Stay ahead of the tariffs and the scanners and the years.

But her hands were still humming on the controls, and when the *Long Answer* cleared Kestrel's traffic net and swung out toward the open dark, she found she'd plotted the course before she'd consciously decided to go: the long, empty, cartel-abandoned corridor that ended at a dying ring around a dying rock, where a station AI played fiddle music for no one.

She'd been meaning to go back for years. She'd been finding reasons not to for just as long.

The reason to go arrived three days later, when she was still two jumps out, in a burst transmission on a frequency that hadn't been licensed since before she was born—a frequency she recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

It was VESSEL. Broadcasting on open channels, to no one, to everyone, to the dead empty corridor.

Confessing to a fire.
