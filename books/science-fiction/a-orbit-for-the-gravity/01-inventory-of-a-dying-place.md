## 1. Inventory of a Dying Place

The log began, as it always began, with the number of people still breathing.

Hazel Joubert sat at the desk in the administrative office of Meridian's Reach, in the chair that had been the original's chair, before a screen that had been the original's screen, and typed: *Population, as of this morning's census: one hundred and eleven.* Then, because the log demanded honesty and honesty demanded precision: *One hundred and eleven, minus one. Old Tomas Ferreira died in his sleep two nights ago. I found him at shift change. The number is one hundred and ten.*

She paused over the sentence. There was no protocol for how to record a death in a colony that was itself dying, no form elegant enough to hold it. The colonial charter's templates assumed growth — births logged in blue, deaths in grey, the net always trending upward, always toward something. Meridian's Reach had not logged a birth in nineteen years. The blue column had gone silent the way a struck bell goes silent, not all at once but in a long diminishing hum that no one had thought to mourn until it was simply gone.

*Cause of death: cardiac failure, consistent with age and low-grade hypoxia.* She wrote it the way she wrote everything, in the flat clinical register she had taught herself in the first months after decanting, when her own hands had seemed like instruments borrowed from a stranger. *He did not suffer. He was ninety-one. He was, by every measure this colony can offer, fortunate.*

She deleted *fortunate*. It was not clinical. It was not honest either. She restored it, then deleted it again, and left the sentence ending at *offer*, with a period like a door closed gently.

Outside the office window — a window, because the original sections of Meridian's Reach had been built by people who still believed in windows, before the colony had retreated into the hab ring and its sealed corridors — the morning light of Kessel's Reach came up amber over the regolith plain. The star was small and old and orange, and it made everything it touched look like something at the end of a long illness: the skeletal gantries of the mining heads, long cold; the rows of hydroponic greenhouses with their patched membranes catching the light like scar tissue; the transmitter array on the western ridge, its dish turned eternally toward a sky it could barely speak into anymore.

Hazel watched the light for a while. Then she opened the systems inventory and began the day's real work.

*Atmospheric scrubbers, units one through six:* she typed. *Units two and five operating at sixty-one and fifty-four percent efficiency respectively. Filter medium degradation continues at projected rate. Unit five's CO₂ scrubbing capacity will fall below colony minimum within approximately eleven months. Unit two within fourteen. Replacement medium: none in inventory. Resupply: none scheduled. Probability of resupply: —*

She stopped there. She had written that line, or one shaped exactly like it, forty-one times now, once for every weekly inventory since she had assumed the log. The field after *Probability of resupply* had never once been filled in. It sat empty in forty-one entries, a small rectangular window onto nothing, and Hazel had come to think of it as the most honest sentence in the entire archive. Not *zero*. Not *unlikely*. Just the blank, waiting, the way the whole colony waited, for a word that was not coming.

She typed *negligible* and moved on.

*Water reclamation: ninety-two percent closed-loop. Losses within tolerance. The condenser coils in section C require descaling; I have scheduled it for Thursday.* She could hear, as she wrote it, the particular hum of the reclamation system through the office wall, a sound so constant that she only ever heard it when she listened for it, and listening for it had become a kind of prayer. As long as the hum continued, the dead were being returned to the living, drop by drop. As long as the hum continued, the arithmetic held.

*Power: reactor three at seventy-eight percent output, degrading. Reactor four offline since 14.203, unrepaired, parts unavailable. Load-shedding schedule attached. The eastern hab sections remain unheated. The memorial wing remains unheated.* She hesitated, then added: *I have not rerouted power to the memorial wing. The names do not require warmth. I have decided this is a practical decision and not what it actually is.*

She stared at that sentence for a long moment. It was the closest she had come, in forty-one logs, to confession. Her finger hovered over the delete key.

She left it in.

Some part of her — the part that was not clinical, the part she kept, like the memorial wing, unheated and dark and visited only rarely — had begun to understand that the log was not an inventory at all. It was a letter. It had no addressee. It would almost certainly never be read; the colonial archive's last scheduled uplink to the wider network had been seven years ago, and the transmitter's power budget these days allowed perhaps four minutes of broadcast a month, reserved for the automated distress beacon that no one answered. The log was a letter to no one, written in a dead office, in a dying place, by a woman who was herself a kind of copy of a letter whose original recipient had been murdered.

But she wrote it anyway. That was the duty. And duty, Hazel had found, was a room you could live in. It had walls. It had a schedule. It asked nothing of you except that you show up and count things, and counting things was infinitely easier than feeling them.

* * *

At 0900 she made the rounds.

This too was inherited — not the route itself, which she had designed, but the habit of walking it, which she had absorbed whole from the original's own logs, from the way the first Hazel had described her mornings in the early years, when the colony still had two thousand souls and the corridors had still smelled of bread from the communal ovens. Hazel walked the same route at the same hour because the first Hazel had, and because when she did, the colony felt less like a wreck she was cataloguing and more like a body she was tending. Pulse points. Circulation. The slow work of keeping a patient alive past the point where medicine had any honest hope to offer.

The hab ring came first. One hundred and ten people lived in a structure rated for four thousand, and the emptiness had a texture, a pressure, like deep water. Whole corridors stood sealed, their lighting strips dark, their doors tagged with the yellow chevrons of decommission. Hazel walked the lit spine of the ring and passed the doors that were still warm: the Okonkwo—no, not Okonkwo, the *Okafor* unit, where Mariam Okafor raised vegetables in a converted sleeping cubicle and traded them with a seriousness other people reserved for currency; the Diallo door, open as always, Amara's domain smelling of tea and machine oil; the long dormitory block where the last thirty-odd residents slept in rooms meant for families, one lamp each in a corridor built for a thousand.

She counted doors the way she counted everything. Forty-one occupied. She did not need to count them; she knew them. But the counting was the duty, and the duty was the room she lived in, and so she counted.

In the central commons, the morning gathering had already formed itself — eleven of the older residents, seated in the same chairs they always took, around the same table, beneath the mural of the *First Landing* that some artist had painted in the colony's second decade, back when the future had been a thing you could paint. The mural showed settlers in bright coveralls planting a flag in orange regolith, their faces upturned toward a Kessel's Reach that the painter had made far too blue. Hazel passed it every morning. She had never once looked directly at it. Looking at it felt like eavesdropping on someone else's hope.

"Hazel." Amara Diallo raised her cup to her — tea, not coffee; coffee had been gone for six years, and Amara had been the one to announce its passing at a colony meeting with the gravity of a state funeral. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early."

"You're always up early," Amara agreed, "but today you're up early *and* walking fast. That's a systems failure somewhere. Which one?"

"Scrubber five. I'm going to look at it."

"Mm." Amara's eyes, sharp and dark and old, followed her with a attention Hazel had never quite learned to bear. "Eleven months, you said last week."

"Eleven months," Hazel confirmed.

"And the committee said?"

"The committee said thank you, Amara."

The committee was Amara, and three others, and the thank-you had been delivered in silence, five people looking at a number, and then Okafor had asked whether anyone wanted more tea, and that had been the whole of the colony's official response to the news that its air had a deadline. Hazel understood it. She had written it up in the log as *community consensus: continued operation, no actionable alternative identified.* What she had not written, because there was no clinical vocabulary for it, was the way Mariam Okafor's hands had gone still around her cup, or the way old Yusuf had looked at the ceiling as if the answer might be written up there among the vents.

"Come by after," Amara said. "I have the new seed stock sorted. You'll want to see the tomatoes."

"The tomatoes died in the last cold cycle."

"The *old* tomatoes died. These are stubborn tomatoes. Like their gardener." Amara smiled, and Hazel performed the approximation of a smile that her face had learned, and walked on, and felt — as she always felt leaving Amara's presence — something in her chest that she declined to name, because naming it would require admitting that the room called Duty had a window, and that through the window was a garden she could not enter without burning the whole structure down.

* * *

Scrubber five lived in the atmospheric plant, a cathedral of pipes and catwalks in the colony's industrial spine, and it was there, elbow-deep in the unit's filter housing, that Hazel spent the middle of her day doing the work her hands knew better than her mind did. The filter medium had degraded exactly as projected — she had known it would, had *wanted* it to, in some obscure way, because a failure that arrived on schedule was a failure she could hold at arm's length — and she replaced the bypass seals, logged the efficiency drop, and sat for a while afterward on the catwalk with her back against the housing, feeling the machine breathe through the metal against her spine.

In. Out. In. Out. The scrubber breathed for one hundred and ten people, and Hazel sat inside its breath like a child inside a heartbeat, and let herself, for exactly four minutes by the wall clock, think nothing at all.

It was the closest thing she had to rest.

The trouble with the room called Duty — she had been circling this thought for weeks, and on the catwalk, in the machine's breath, she finally let it finish itself — was that it was not actually a room. It was a costume. She had built it out of the original's habits and the colony's needs and her own talent for precision, and she wore it so well that some days she forgot, and the forgetting was the whole point. But a costume is not a room. A room keeps the weather out. The costume only decides *which* weather reaches your skin, and the weather of Meridian's Reach — the silence in the sealed corridors, the empty chairs at the commons table, the forty-one blank fields where a resupply date should have been — got through everything.

She was a woman alone in a crowd of the dying, cataloguing the dying, and the catalogue was not working.

She had known that for some time, actually. She had known it the night Tomas Ferreira died, when she had sat with his body in the medical bay for two hours past the point of any practical purpose, because the alternative was going back to her quarters, and her quarters had a window, and the window had the ridge, and the ridge had the transmitter array, and the array pointed at a sky full of people who had forgotten this place existed. She had known it every time she wrote *Population:* and felt the number like a stone in her hand, lighter every week.

One hundred and ten.

The log wanted its evening entry eventually. But the day had one more station on the route, and she made it as the amber light went long and red across the regolith: the memorial wing, unheated, dark, its door sealed with a yellow chevron like everything else the colony had decided to stop feeding. Hazel keyed her own override — she was the administrator; the chevrons obeyed her — and stepped inside, and stood in the cold among the names.

The wall of the departed. Four hundred and twelve names, engraved in order of death, from the first fatality of the landing (a man called Devi Anand, killed by a pressure fault before the colony was a month old) down to the most recent line, cut by Hazel herself with the handheld engraver, three nights ago: *TOMAS FERREIRA, 91. HE KEPT THE ORCHARDS.*

She had added that last part herself. The template did not call for epitaphs. She had cut it anyway, in the dark, at 0300, and had not mentioned it to anyone, and had not entered it in the log, and it stood there now in the cold air as the single most dishonest omission in forty-one weeks of meticulous record-keeping — no, she corrected herself, standing before it, the most *honest* thing in the record. The log lied by omission every day. The wall told the truth. The wall said: *these people were not units. They were orchards and tea and stubborn tomatoes and laughter in the commons, and they are gone, and you are the one left writing the numbers down.*

Her breath fogged in the unheated air. She counted the names. All four hundred and twelve, though she did not need to. The counting was the duty. The duty was the costume. And underneath the costume, in the cold and the dark, Hazel Joubert stood alone in a room full of the dead and admitted, to no one, in no log, what she could not admit anywhere else:

She did not know how to be the last one keeping the books. She did not know what she was for, when the colony ended — and it would end; the arithmetic was not sentimental; eleven months, fourteen, and the reactor's slow decline like a third clock ticking underneath — and she did not know whether the woman whose name she wore had known, at the end, either. The original had died with her books unfinished too. That was in the file. That was one of the few things that was in the file.

Hazel stood with the dead a while longer. Then she sealed the door, and the chevron light blinked yellow above it, and she went back down the corridor toward her office and the evening entry, her boots loud in the emptiness, the only footsteps in a hab ring built for a thousand.

* * *

She wrote the evening entry by lamplight, because the office's overheads were on the load-shed list now too.

*Daily log, Meridian's Reach, day 287 of the current administrative cycle. Population: one hundred and ten. Systems as per morning inventory, with scrubber five seals replaced and efficiency stabilized at fifty-six percent. Water reclamation nominal. Power nominal under shed schedule. One death recorded: Tomas Ferreira. Memorial updated.*

She stopped. The cursor blinked. Forty-one entries, and the rhythm of them had become a kind of heartbeat, and tonight the heartbeat stuttered, because her hand was hovering over the keyboard and what it wanted to type was not an inventory.

*Note:* she typed, before the clinical self could stop her. *The colony's charter requires this log to be maintained "for the use of successor administrations." I have been the successor administration for forty-one weeks. I am beginning to suspect there will be no further successors, and that I am not maintaining this record for an administration at all. I am maintaining it for whoever comes after the administration — for whoever finds this place, whenever they find it, in whatever century. I am writing to the future the way the memorial wall writes to the past. Someone should know we were here. Someone should know we counted everything, and kept breathing, and did not ask the galaxy for anything it had already decided not to give.*

*This is not a clinical observation. I am leaving it in.*

She sat back. The lamp hummed. Through the window, the transmitter array on the western ridge caught the last red light of Kessel's Reach and held it, a dish of frozen fire aimed at all those absent ears.

Tomorrow she would review the inheritance file. She had been putting it off for forty-one weeks — the legal instruments, the estate, the debts, the clause that had made her, that had *built* her, the document that explained why a murdered woman's property and obligations and unsolved death had passed, by provision of the Meridian Succession Act, to a woman grown in a vat in a facility she had never seen. She had read it once, at decanting, and never again. But Tomas Ferreira's death had loosened something in her, and the log had confessed more than she intended tonight, and she had the sudden, cold understanding — arriving with the certainty of a systems failure detected early, the kind you could not un-detect — that she could not keep wearing the costume without knowing, exactly and completely, what had been tailored and why.

She saved the entry. The archive chimed its small confirmation: *Log 41 committed. Integrity: verified.*

Somewhere below her, the scrubbers breathed. One hundred and ten people slept. And on the western ridge, the old transmitter held its silence toward the stars, waiting — though Hazel did not know it yet, would not know it for another week — for a voice that was two hundred years dead, and for the message that would drag her quiet, invisible, meticulously counted life out into the light of a war.

She turned off the lamp, and the office went dark, and the log kept its own counsel in the cold.
