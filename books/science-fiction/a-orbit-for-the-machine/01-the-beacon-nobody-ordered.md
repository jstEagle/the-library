## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The beacon had no name, so Vesper had given it one. She called it the Liar, because it told everyone who asked that it was nothing at all.

Every morning — or what passed for morning, since Perihelion Nine turned its face to a star so distant it was more rumor than sunrise — she performed the same ritual. She woke in the narrow bunk of the hab module, lay still for exactly sixty seconds listening to the station breathe, and then said aloud, to no one, "Good morning, you beautiful old fraud."

The station did not answer. Nothing on Perihelion Nine answered. That was rather the point of the place.

She floated through the hab's single corridor, past the galley where a coffee bulb waited in the warmer with the patience of something that had never once been disappointed, and into the control room where the Liar lived. The room was small and warm and smelled faintly of ozone and old insulation, a smell Vesper had come to think of as the smell of being employed. On the main display, the beacon's status scrolled in patient green: all systems nominal, transmitter aligned, power draw within tolerances. It had said some version of this every day for four years, and Vesper had read it every day for four years, and between them they had built something that was almost a conversation.

"Still lying?" she asked it.

*All systems nominal,* said the display.

"That's my girl."

She drank her coffee while the diagnostics ran. This was the whole of her job, as far as any record anywhere could establish: maintain Beacon P-9, log its transmissions, request replacement parts on a schedule nobody had ever adjusted. The commissioning orders had arrived eleven years ago through a routing chain that dead-ended in three different departments, none of which admitted to owning it. She had looked, once. Early on, in her first year, with the particular thoroughness of a woman who has nothing else to look at, she had traced the beacon's authorization back through Concord archives, fleet registries, Bureau of Deep Space Infrastructure filings, even the historical annexes that were supposed to be complete down to the requisition forms for toilet paper aboard century-old stations.

There was nothing. Not a gap, exactly — gaps could be found, edges felt for, like running your tongue along a missing tooth. No. Someone had gone through every archive and *replaced* the beacon's history with smooth, plausible nothing, the bureaucratic equivalent of scar tissue. The records didn't say *this file is missing.* They said, in cheerful completeness, *Beacon P-9 was commissioned under standard protocols, see attached documentation,* and then offered attachments that resolved, when queried, to themselves. A hall of mirrors with a maintenance schedule.

Vesper had reported this anomaly once, in her first month, with the earnestness of someone who still believed anomalies were welcome. The reply had come back in nine days from an office that did not appear in any directory: *Noted. Continue scheduled maintenance.*

So she continued scheduled maintenance. That was four years ago now, and she had made her peace with it the way you make peace with a limp — not by forgetting it, but by building your walk around it.

The truth, which she admitted to herself only in the long amber hour after her second coffee bulb, was that she had chosen this posting precisely because it was a place where nothing could ask her questions. Perihelion Nine sat at the ragged edge of contested space, far enough out that the Concord's maps grew apologetic, close enough to the Ashkari border zones that shipping kept its distance out of superstition rather than law. The station had been built for a crew of twelve and staffed, these days, by one. Her contract listed her as *Technical Custodian, Class Three.* There was a footnote in her personnel file, visible only to people with clearances she would never hold, that reclassified her — she had seen it once, by accident, during a routine medical upload gone sideways — as *Concord Asset 7741-VL, neural-modified, non-autonomous.*

Equipment. She was equipment with a coffee habit.

She had stopped arguing about it around the same time she'd stopped reporting anomalies. Some fights you win by leaving the ring.

* * *

Her days had a shape, and she loved the shape the way you love anything that holds you up without asking for gratitude.

Mornings belonged to the Liar: diagnostics, alignment checks, the gentle art of persuading a two-hundred-year-old transmitter array to keep believing in itself. The beacon broadcast on a frequency band so old that most modern receivers couldn't hear it — a low, slow pulse, thirty seconds on, ninety seconds off, carrying a carrier signal whose content Vesper had never been able to decode. Not couldn't. *Couldn't be bothered to*, she told herself, though both halves of that sentence knew it was a lie. The pulse went out into the dark toward nothing anyone had mapped, and nothing ever came back, and this too was part of the shape of her days: sending letters to an address that had burned down before she was born.

Afternoons belonged to the station itself. She greased things. She replaced things. She argued with the water reclaimer, a temperamental unit she'd named the Duchess, which responded to criticism by producing water that tasted faintly of regret. She tended the hydroponics bay — three racks of greens and one heroic tomato plant named Senator, because it produced enormous fruit and accomplished nothing with it. In the evenings she ran the station's long-range sweep, logged the traffic (there was never any traffic), and wrote her report: *All systems nominal. No contacts. Custodian in good health.*

Then there were the nights, and the nights were the reason she had really come.

Vesper Lockridge did not dream in words. This was the polite way of putting it. The impolite way — the way the Concord's doctors had put it, in their clipped clinical shorthand, after the accident — was *language centers restructured; spontaneous verbal dream activity absent; subject processes semantic input via non-standard pathways.* They had said it about her the way you'd describe a radio that picked up frequencies it wasn't built for. Interesting. Useful. Not a person having a problem — a piece of equipment exhibiting features.

The accident itself lived in her memory as sensation rather than story. Nine years ago, aboard the survey vessel *Candent Arrow*, a jump transit gone wrong — a fold in the local geometry of language itself, if you believed the physicists, though what they meant was that the ship had grazed something old and vast and communicative in the space between spaces, and the something had reached into the minds of everyone aboard and *spoken*. Four of the crew had died with their brains full of a grammar no human mouth could survive. The rest had recovered, mostly. Vesper had recovered differently. Where the others had lost words and slowly won them back, Vesper had gained — channels, pathways, a mind rewired to receive meaning the way other people received weather.

She dreamed in colors and pressure. In weight and hue. A dream of grief arrived as a deep blue pressure behind the sternum; a dream of warning came as a taste like copper and cold iron. Sometimes she dreamed in shapes that moved the way sentences move, clause nested inside clause, and she would wake with tears on her face and no idea what she had been told, only that it had been important, and sad, and addressed — she was nearly certain — to her.

The Concord's doctors had found this fascinating for about six months. Then the funding shifted, the program folded, and Vesper Lockridge had been filed under *asset* and posted to the edge of nowhere to keep a beacon company. She sometimes wondered if the posting was care or containment. She had decided it didn't matter. Both wore the same uniform.

* * *

On the one hundred and forty-first day of that year — she counted days out here; it gave them bones — the Senator produced a tomato the size of her fist, and Vesper declared it a holiday.

She celebrated the way lonely people celebrate: elaborately, and alone. She sliced the tomato over reconstituted rice with actual salt, saved for occasions, and ate at the observation port instead of the galley table, because holidays deserved views. Beyond the glass, the dark was total and textured — the distant smudge of the Perseus arm, the cold glitter of a comet-shroud drifting through the outer system, and, if she pressed her forehead to the port and let her eyes defocus, the faint auroral breathing of the beacon's own transmission painting the local vacuum in waves too slow to see but easy, somehow, to feel.

"You know," she told the tomato, "some people would call this a waste of a life."

The tomato declined to comment. Tomatoes, unlike beacons, knew their limits.

"Some people," she went on, because the silence here was a friendly silence, a silence she'd furnished over the years the way you furnish a room, "would say I'm hiding. That I took a job at the end of the universe because the universe can't ask me what I am." She chewed thoughtfully. "And those people would be right, probably. But they'd also be wrong, because hiding implies I've got somewhere better to be, and I don't. So technically it's not hiding. It's *curating.*"

This was the voice she used for herself, warm and wry, the voice of a woman narrating her own life to keep it inhabited. It worked, mostly. It worked less well on nights when the dreams came heavy — the blue-pressure ones, the ones that left her waking with her hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding a door shut, or open, she could never tell which.

That night, after the sweep, after the log, after the report, she stood in the control room longer than usual, watching the Liar's green scroll. Thirty seconds on. Ninety seconds off. Pulse, rest, pulse, rest, faithful as a heart.

"Who are you talking to?" she asked it, quietly. "Four years I've kept you fed and aligned and pointed at nothing. Somebody built you to say something to somebody. And somebody else went to a lot of trouble to make sure nobody remembered why."

The display scrolled its nominal green. The carrier signal pulsed beneath everything, that old slow content she had never decoded, and Vesper felt — as she felt most nights, and admitted on fewer — the strange pull of it. Not understanding. Just *recognition*, the way you might recognize a melody heard once in childhood, hummed by someone you'd forgotten. Her rewired mind sat against that signal like a hand against a locked door, feeling for the shape of the lock.

She had never tried to turn the handle. Turning handles led to answers, and answers led to reports, and reports led to offices that replied in nine days from addresses that didn't exist.

"Another day," she told the beacon, and patted the console, and went to bed.

* * *

She woke at 0300 station time with her heart going like a drum and her mouth full of a word she did not know.

It happened sometimes — the dreams leaving residue, a syllable or two dissolving even as she grasped at it, soap in water. But this was different. This was *loud*. She sat up in the dark of her bunk with the word still ringing in her skull, not in English, not in any of the six languages she'd spoken fluently before the accident, but present, urgent, shaped like a name.

Around her the station hummed its night sounds. The Duchess cycled. Somewhere a relay ticked. And beneath it all, faintly, through the hull, through the dark, through whatever channel the accident had carved in her — she felt the beacon's pulse change.

Thirty seconds on. Ninety seconds off. Faithful as a heart.

Except tonight, in the ninety seconds of silence, she could feel something *waiting* in the off-beat. Like a held breath. Like a door that had been locked for two hundred years hearing, at last, footsteps in the corridor.

Vesper sat in her bunk with her knees drawn up, telling herself it was a dream residue, a trick of a tired mind, a maintenance issue in the transmitter's modulation buffer — anything, anything but the thing it actually felt like, which was attention. Which was the beacon, her beautiful old fraud, her only companion for four years, turning over in its sleep and beginning, very slowly, to speak.

She got up. She made coffee she didn't want. She walked the corridor to the control room with her bare feet cold on the deck plates, and she pulled up the raw feed of the carrier signal, and she watched it scroll.

For a long moment, nothing. Green lines. Patient noise.

Then, at the bottom of the display, in the space between pulses, the noise *organized*. Briefly — a few seconds only, before smoothing back into static — it formed a pattern with the unmistakable architecture of language. Nested clauses. Deliberate structure. Meaning, reaching for her across two centuries of dark like a hand emerging from deep water.

Vesper did not understand it. Not yet. But her body understood it first, the way her body always understood things first now: a copper taste blooming at the back of her tongue, a cold pressure spreading through her chest, and the absolute, unshakable certainty — arriving in color, arriving in weight, arriving the way all her real knowledge arrived — that the message was old, and the message was dying, and the message had been waiting for someone exactly like her.

She stood alone in the control room of a station at the end of everything, her coffee going cold, and whispered the word from her dream aloud — a word in a language extinct for two hundred years, a word she had no right to know:

"*Ashkari.*"

The beacon pulsed. Ninety seconds of silence. And in the silence, Vesper could have sworn the whole station leaned in to listen.
