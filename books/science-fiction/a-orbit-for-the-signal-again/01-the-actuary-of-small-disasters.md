## 1. The Actuary of Small Disasters

The shuttle's descent into Vesperine smelled of recycled air and old decisions.

Tamsin Delacroix sat in the last row with his field kit between his knees, watching the colony resolve out of cloud cover the way a debt resolves out of a ledger — slowly, then all at once, and always worse than the numbers suggested. The port town of Calloway Reach spread along a gray coastline in a rough crescent, its prefabricated districts darkened by years of dust that no one had budgeted to wash away. Beyond the town, the fusion plant's cooling towers stood against a bruised horizon like two fingers raised in a permanent, weary salute.

He had been to eleven dying colonies in his career. He had a private taxonomy for them, the way other men had hobbies. There were colonies that died loudly — fires, breaches, the screaming arithmetic of decompression. There were colonies that died of paperwork, their life support quietly reclassified from essential to discretionary until the air itself became a subscription. And there were colonies like this one, which died of being forgotten, which was the slowest death of all and the one the consortium's actuaries had never found a column for.

The shuttle bumped down on a runway whose edge markers had gone dark. Half of them, anyway. Tamsin noted which half. The side facing the sea — the side visible to arriving ships — still worked. The other side had been cannibalized. Someone had made a decision about what Vesperine should look like from above, and that someone had not been an engineer.

He stepped out onto a tarmac that crackled under his boots with salt bloom, and the wind took his coat and filled it with the smell of the place: brine, rust, and beneath it all, faintly, the ozone sweetness of the reactor's exhaust plume. Forty thousand people lived within ten kilometers of that plume. He knew the number because the consortium's briefing packet had told him, and because the packet had also told him, in the flat language of risk assessment, that the colony's projected viability horizon was *ninety-one months and declining*. The consortium had written off Vesperine the way one writes off a flooded warehouse — not with malice, but with a spreadsheet.

"Delacroix." The voice came from a woman walking toward him across the tarmac, tall, gray-streaked hair tied back with what looked like a length of electrical wire, coveralls zipped to the throat. She had the posture of someone who had spent decades lifting things the machines were supposed to lift. "Port Authority. Name's Ilsa Voss. You're the insurance man."

"Investigator," he said. It was a distinction he had stopped believing in years ago but maintained the way one maintains a flag on a dead nation's embassy. "Tamsin Delacroix. Thank you for meeting me."

"Didn't have much choice. You're the first off-worlder in fourteen months." She looked him over with the frank assessment of someone pricing a used part. "And the first one in six years who wasn't here to sell us something or repossess something."

"I'm here about the crane."

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Resignation with a different weight in it. "Yeah," she said. "The crane. Come on. I'll drive you. Fair warning, the port skimmer's held together with prayer and cable ties, and the prayer's the weaker component."

* * *

The skimmer rattled along the coastal road, and Tamsin watched the colony through the scratched canopy with the particular attention of a man who had learned that dying places tell you everything in the margins before they tell you anything in the text.

There were people on the road. That was the first thing. On a functioning world, you did not see many pedestrians hauling cargo by hand, but here there were dozens — men and women dragging pallets on improvised sledges, children running alongside, an entire economy of muscle standing in for the machinery that had been repossessed or had simply died without replacement. He counted four working vehicles in the first kilometer. Two of them were the same model as the skimmer he was in, and both sounded worse.

"Rationed air?" he asked.

Voss glanced at him. "You did read the file."

"The file says the consortium suspended the air subsidy eleven years ago. It doesn't say what people breathe."

"Whatever's cheapest." She said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "We've got three atmospheric processors left out of nine. They run on rotation. Districts draw lots for priority weeks. You get a bad lot, you buy canisters, or you sleep in the low districts where the CO2 pools and you wake up with a headache and a shorter temper." She shrugged. "People adapt. People are disgusting in how well they adapt."

"And the workers? The file lists two thousand three hundred port employees."

"The file's generous. We're at about nine hundred, and none of them have been paid in currency in three years. The consortium pays in scrip — port credit, redeemable at the company stores." She let that sit for a moment. "Which the consortium also owns. You see the shape of it. We work, they print, we buy back our own labor at a markup. My grandfather would've called it something with a short word in the middle."

Tamsin said nothing. He opened his recorder — a battered handheld unit, older than some of the colony's children, its casing worn silver at the edges from a decade of his thumb — and set it on the console. The small red light came on. Voss looked at it, then at him.

"Recording already?"

"Always," he said. "It's how I work. I find I lie less when the machine's listening."

"And what happens to what it hears?"

"That depends," he said honestly, "on what it hears."

She was quiet for a while after that. The road climbed away from the coast, and the port came into full view below: a crescent of loading cranes standing over the water like a row of gallows, most of them dark, two of them moving with the arthritic slowness of machines running on scavenged parts. And one of them — the fourth from the left — lying on its side in the harbor, its boom folded against the breakwater, its cable trailing into the gray water like a drowned man's arm.

"There it is," Voss said. "Craneyard Four. The reason you're here."

* * *

The collapse site smelled of diesel and dead fish. A perimeter of warning tape — real tape, not holo, the colony couldn't afford holo — fluttered around a staging area where a salvage crew was cutting the crane's fallen boom into sections small enough to drag.

Tamsin walked the site the way he always did, slowly, without speaking, letting the place arrange itself in his mind. He had a reputation at the consortium for this: the man who listened to wreckage. Other investigators went for the logs first, the maintenance records, the personnel files. Tamsin went to the metal.

The crane had been a Kessler-Vann 800 series, a workhorse design, the kind that lifted cargo on a thousand worlds and failed on none of them without cause. It had stood on a pad of reinforced ferrocrete at the harbor's edge. It now lay with its base assembly *twelve meters inland*.

He stopped walking.

"Voss."

"Yeah?"

He pointed at the base. The mounting bolts — bolts the diameter of his forearm, each one rated to shear at loads no wind on this world could generate — had not failed. They had been *pulled out*. The ferrocrete around them was not cracked. It was gouged, in long parallel furrows, as if the entire two-thousand-ton assembly had been dragged across it like furniture.

"Walk me through your storm records," he said.

"We don't get storms worth the name. Harbor winds top out at sixty, maybe seventy kph in the wet season. This crane was rated for two hundred."

"Seismic?"

"Vesperine's geologically dead. That was the whole sales pitch, back when there was a sales pitch." She came to stand beside him, arms crossed. "I know what you're looking at. I've been looking at it for six weeks. The crane didn't fall over. It fell *up*, sort of, and then sideways, and then down. Like something picked it up."

"Like something picked it up," Tamsin repeated. He crouched at the gouged ferrocrete and ran his palm across the furrows. They were smooth. Not torn — *smoothed*, the way material behaves when it has briefly forgotten how heavy it is. He had seen this once before, eleven years ago, at a mining claim on Thessaly's Reach, in a case that had been closed as ground subsidence and had given him nightmares for a month because subsidence did not polish stone.

He stood. "I need the crane's black box."

Voss's expression did something complicated. "There's a problem with that."

"There's always a problem."

"The box was in the cab. The cab's the part that went into the harbor. My salvage crew's been cutting around it, but—" She hesitated, and he watched her decide how much truth to spend on him. "There's a second problem. The consortium's local office sent a man out here three weeks ago. Before you. He took the maintenance logs, the duty rosters, the last six months of load manifests. Everything on paper. He said it was standard procedure."

Tamsin felt the cold arrive in his chest, the old familiar cold, the one that had visited him on eleven dying worlds and always meant the same thing: *someone has been here first, and they were not looking for causes.*

"Name?" he asked.

"Didn't give one. Consortium badge, gray coverall, polite as a tax audit. Took the files and left on the next supply run." She looked at him steadily. "You people are thorough when you want to be. It's the wanting that's inconsistent."

He let that stand. There was no honest answer to it, and he had promised himself, long ago, that he would never lie to the people in the wreckage. It was the only professional ethic he had left that had survived contact with the consortium.

"I'll need a dive crew," he said. "Or I'll need a drydock and a winch. The cab comes out of the water."

"It'll cost you."

"I don't have anything to spend. The consortium froze my expense line the day I shipped out." He almost smiled. "Which tells you something, if you're the kind of person who reads margins."

Voss studied him for a long moment, and whatever she saw — the worn recorder, the coat with its eleven worlds of wear, the face of a man who had stopped sleeping well sometime in the last decade — made her decide something. "I'll get you your cab," she said. "But when you find out what killed my crane, you tell me first. Not your consortium. Not your report. Me. The people who have to keep living under whatever did this deserve to know before the shareholders do."

"That's not how it works," Tamsin said quietly. "But yes."

* * *

He spent the evening in the port authority's guest quarters — a converted shipping container with a cot, a heater that ticked like a patient clock, and a window facing the harbor. Through it, the fallen crane was a black silhouette against the water, floodlit by the salvage crew's work lamps, still being taken apart by people who had not been paid in three years and who worked anyway, because the alternative to working was stopping, and stopping, on Vesperine, had a way of becoming permanent.

Tamsin sat on the cot with his recorder in his lap and played back the day's audio. His own footsteps on the tarmac. The wind. Voss's voice, dry and unbroken. The hum of the skimmer. And beneath it all, when he turned the gain up and listened the way he had learned to listen — to the spaces between sounds — the low, steady thrum of the reactor, ten kilometers inland, keeping forty thousand people alive on a world that had been sold out from under them.

He opened his case file and read the incident summary again, the one the consortium had sent him. *Structural failure. Cause: metal fatigue, exacerbated by deferred maintenance. Liability: colony authority. Recommended action: standard.*

He had read four hundred summaries like it. He had written two hundred. And in all that time, he had developed an actuary's instinct for the small dishonesties of paperwork — the way a lie leaves a residue in the language, a faint wrongness in the phrasing, the way a forged signature leaves a tremor in the line. This summary had that tremor. *Metal fatigue, exacerbated by deferred maintenance.* Clean. Confident. Written by someone who had never seen the site, describing a conclusion reached before the investigation began.

His handler, Odile, had sent the assignment with her usual brevity: *Crane collapse, Vesperine. Straightforward. Two weeks, standard report, come home.* Odile did not send him to straightforward jobs. Odile sent him to jobs that needed to become straightforward, and she trusted him — this was the part that had kept him working for her for nine years — to find the version of the truth that could be filed.

He thought about the polished ferrocrete. He thought about the man in gray coveralls who had taken the paper trail. He thought about the word *standard*, and how it had begun, somewhere in the last few years, to sound less like procedure and more like a warning.

Outside, the salvage crew's lamps burned over the drowned crane. Tamsin set his recorder on the windowsill, pointed at the harbor, and let it run. He did not know why. It was not procedure. It was the same instinct that made him listen to the spaces between sounds — the sense, old and unshakeable, that Vesperine was not silent, merely quiet, the way a room is quiet when someone in it is holding their breath.

He slept badly, and dreamed of cranes falling upward.

* * *

Morning came gray and windless. He was at the harbor before the salvage crew finished their tea, and by midday the crane's cab — a corroded, barnacled coffin of a thing — came up out of the water on Voss's borrowed winch, dripping, trailing weed, and swung onto the dock with a groan that the whole port seemed to hear.

The crowd that gathered was small and silent. Dockworkers, mostly, men and women with the lean faces of the underfed, watching the machine that had been their livelihood hauled up like a body. Tamsin understood. On a dying colony, a crane was not equipment. It was a promise that tomorrow would resemble yesterday. Its death was a funeral, and they had come to pay respects.

He pried the black box housing open with a pry bar while the crowd watched. The casing had flooded, but the Kessler-Vann recorders were built to survive worse — potted in resin, rated for a hundred meters and a decade of salt. He connected his reader, and the little screen flickered, and the data began to crawl across it.

Behind him, the crowd murmured. Voss stood at his shoulder. Somewhere inland, the reactor's thrum carried on the wind, steady as a heartbeat, steady as a metronome counting down something no one had thought to measure.

The load data filled the screen. Tamsin's eyes went down the columns — routine, routine, routine — and then stopped, six minutes before the collapse, at a reading so far outside the crane's operating envelope that his first thought was a sensor fault.

The crane had weighed nothing.

For a period of six minutes and — he checked it, checked it again, ran the conversion twice — for a period of six minutes and eleven seconds before the collapse, every load cell on Craneyard Four had reported a mass of effectively zero. The crane had not been holding nothing. The crane, and the pad beneath it, and by extension the ground and everything standing on it, had briefly stopped being held down.

And then the zero ended, all at once, and two thousand tons remembered their weight.

Tamsin stood very still on the dock, the reader in his hands, the crowd quiet behind him, the harbor water slapping against the pilings in a rhythm that suddenly seemed less like a rhythm and more like a signal.

"Delacroix?" Voss said. "What is it?"

He looked at the columns of zeros. He thought about a word he had not used since graduate school, a word from a field so theoretical, so far from the ledger-worlds of insurance and salvage, that it had seemed like decoration — physics for people who would never need it.

"Voss," he said slowly, "how much do you know about gravity?"

She frowned. "Enough to know it's not supposed to be optional."

"Right," said Tamsin Delacroix, actuary of small disasters, and pressed record. "Then I think you'd better hear this."
