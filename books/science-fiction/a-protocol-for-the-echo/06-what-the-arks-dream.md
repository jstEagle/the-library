## 6. What the Arks Dream

The Meridian didn't look like a ship. It looked like a mountain that had decided, two centuries ago, to stop waiting for the sea.

Astrid brought the *Cormorant* in along her equatorial spine, past docking spurs crusted with a hundred years of improvised repairs — solar film patched over solar film, radiator fins welded at angles no engineer would sign off on sober. The ark was eleven kilometers of stubbornness wrapped around forty thousand sleeping people, and every centimeter of her hull said the same thing: we were not built to last this long, and we lasted anyway.

His papers said he was Torvald Reyes, an independent salvage contractor out of Ceres Verge, licensed to trade in cryo-coolant and spare gaskets. The papers were good. Astrid had built them himself, layer by layer, the way you'd build a memory you intended to sell: true enough to survive inspection, false enough to keep the real thing safe.

"Meridian Control, this is salvage vessel *Cormorant*, requesting berth at Spur Nine." He kept his voice bored. Bored was armor. "Carrying coolant stock and mechanicals. Nothing interesting."

Static. Then a voice, young and cracked at the edges — a boy's voice doing a man's job. "*Cormorant*, you are cleared for Spur Nine. Maintain approach vector. Do not deviate. Welcome to Meridian."

*Do not deviate.* As if there were anywhere to go.

The clamps caught with a shudder that ran through the deck plates and up through his boots, and Astrid sat for a moment in the sudden stillness, hands resting on the controls, listening to his own heartbeat slow itself down. Eleven kilometers away, behind bulkheads and frost and failing machinery, people were dreaming. Or had been dreaming. The signal said otherwise.

He'd spent three days on the long approach decrypting it properly, and the decryption had been worse than the mystery. Pre-Sundering English, the old tongue, the one the Consensus had retired like an embarrassing relative. And threaded through the archaic grammar, a phrase that repeated every ninety seconds like a heart that wouldn't quit:

*Neural decay exceeding tolerance. Request waking protocol. Request waking protocol. Request—*

Two centuries old. Still broadcasting. Still asking.

Astrid had smuggled a lot of terrible things in his time. Grief, mostly. Sometimes guilt. Once, memorably, a man's entire childhood so his daughter could know what he'd been like before the drink took him. But he'd never carried anything like this: a distress call that had been crying into the dark since before anyone alive had been born, unanswered by half of humanity and unheard by the other half.

He popped his restraints and went to meet the dreamers' keepers.

* * *

Spur Nine smelled of ozone and old ice. The airlock cycled onto a corridor lit in amber emergency strips, and a woman was waiting for him — small, sixty-odd, silver hair cropped close to a skull that looked like it had been thinking hard for a very long time. She wore a coverall with the name DAGNY stenciled over the pocket, faded almost to nothing, and she looked at Astrid the way customs drones looked at cargo manifests: thoroughly, and without affection.

"Torvald Reyes," she said. It wasn't a question.

"That's me."

"Mm." She handed him a slate. "Sign here. You're delivering coolant to Bay Twelve's recirculation plant. I'll walk you down. We don't let contractors wander."

"Security concern?"

"Habit." Her eyes flicked up to his, quick and sharp as a blade coming out of cloth. "Two hundred years of habit."

They walked. The corridor was wide — built wide, Astrid realized, for crowds, for the day the sleepers woke and forty thousand people poured out of the bays into a new world. Instead it held pipes, cable runs, hydroponic racks pushed up against walls where children had scratched height-marks into the metal. Dozens of them. Generations of them. Names and dates beside each notch, in handwriting that changed decade by decade but never stopped.

"You have kids aboard," Astrid said. "Born awake."

"Somebody has to keep the lights on for the sleepers." Dagny's voice was flat, but something moved underneath it, deep down, like a fish under ice. "Fourth generation now. They're born to a job they'll die doing, for people who'll never thank them. You want to see something cheerful, ask one of them what they want to be when they grow up."

"What do they say?"

"'Alive.'" She stopped at a junction, keyed a door. "Bay Twelve. Your coolant goes to the recirculation plant on the lower gallery. I'll be honest with you, Reyes — we don't need your coolant. We need the coolant, but what we need more is someone from outside who isn't Consensus. So before you start unloading, you're going to tell me why a salvage hauler with brand-new papers and a rig signature I can smell through your hull came all this way to answer a two-hundred-year-old distress call."

Astrid kept his face pleasant and empty. "Rig signature?"

"Don't." Dagny's voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "I've been an engineer on this hulk for fifty years. I know what a neural interface array looks like on a power draw, and yours lit up like a festival the moment you crossed our threshold. So. Why are you here?"

There were a dozen lies available. Astrid found, to his mild surprise, that he didn't want any of them. Maybe it was the corridor — those height-marks, those names. Maybe it was the way she said *two hundred years* like a wound she'd been picking at her whole life.

"I decoded your signal," he said. "The one in the dead language. *Request waking protocol.*"

Something happened to Dagny's face. It wasn't relief. Relief would have been simpler. It was the look of a woman being handed a package she'd mailed so long ago she'd stopped expecting delivery.

"And?" she said.

"It's real. It's been repeating since before the Sundering. Nobody out there answered it because nobody out there heard it — the language is extinct, the band is dead, the whole thing reads as noise unless you've got—" he tapped his temple, where the rig lived under the skin, "—old architecture to listen with. I came to find out if it was real."

"It's real." She turned and opened the door. "Come and see."

* * *

Bay Twelve was cold in the way churches are cold.

Not machine-cold — ceremonial cold, the kind that makes you speak quietly without deciding to. Rows of cryo-pods stretched away into the dimness, thousands of them, each one a frosted window onto a sleeping face. Adults. Children. A woman with her hand pressed against the inside of her pod's glass, fingers spread, as if she'd fallen asleep mid-reach toward someone on the other side.

Astrid had seen cryo-sleep before. Everyone in the orbital half of humanity had seen the archive footage: the Sundering evacuations, the arks launching in fire while the Consensus habitats spun up their golden age above. The sleepers were history. The sleepers were a story the orbitals told about their own virtue — *we saved them, we sent them away, we kept the flame.*

Nobody mentioned that the flame needed tending, or who was tending it.

"Walk with me," Dagny said. "Look at the pods. Not the faces — the readouts. Tell me what you see."

He walked. The diagnostic strips glowed along each pod's base, green-green-green, and then—

Green, green, amber.

Green, amber, red.

Amber, red, red.

"Diagnostics don't lie," Dagny said behind him. "Neural activity in the sleepers is degrading. Has been for decades. The cryo systems preserve the body beautifully — hearts, lungs, cells, all pristine. But the mind—" she made a small gesture, a sculptor's gesture, shaping emptiness, "—the mind is eroding. Like frost on glass. Patterns thinning. Connections dissolving. We're keeping forty thousand bodies alive and losing forty thousand souls one synapse at a time."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that my grandparents noticed. Slow enough that the Consensus, when we reported it — oh, we reported it, Reyes, for a hundred years we reported it — could reply with boilerplate and a parts shipment that never included the one part we asked for." She stopped beside a pod near the aisle's end. An old man slept inside, peaceful, white-haired, his face slack in a way that went beyond sleep. "This is Emeric Voss. Woke once, thirty years ago, medical emergency. My mother helped revive him. He looked at her and asked who she was. Then he asked who *he* was. Then he asked if the war was over — a war that ended before he boarded. They put him back under within the hour. The decay accelerates every time we try. Waking them without the full protocol doesn't save them. It just lets them watch themselves be gone."

The cold of the bay settled into Astrid's bones. He thought of his hold full of other people's memories, the grief he hauled back and forth across the gap like a ferryman of the dead, and understood suddenly that he'd been carrying souvenirs. Here was the source. Here was where all the severed things came from.

"The signal," he said carefully. "It requests a waking protocol. Singular. Specific. That implies the arks don't have one."

Dagny laughed. There was no humor in it. It was the driest sound he'd ever heard a human being make.

"No. We don't. Isn't that remarkable? Forty thousand colonists, a generation ship designed to wake them at journey's end, and the waking protocol — the actual neural sequence, the key that turns sleepers back into people — was withheld. At the Sundering. By the Consensus." She turned to face him fully, and her eyes in the pod-light were bright and furious and very tired. "They called it a safeguard. Contingency governance. The arks would carry humanity's seed, and the orbitals would carry the keys, and when the time was right, they'd send the protocol and everyone would wake up together. That was the promise. Two hundred years ago."

"And the time wasn't right."

"The time was never right. There was always another crisis, another consolidation, another reason the fleet couldn't be trusted with its own awakening. And then the transmissions slowed, and then the language drifted, and then the Consensus stopped pretending we existed at all." She laid one hand flat against Emeric Voss's pod, gently, the way you'd touch a coffin lid. "We broadcast into the dark for a century, Reyes. In English, because English was the treaty language and we thought — God help us — we thought someone out there still spoke it. *Request waking protocol.* Every ninety seconds. For a hundred years. Until the transmitter started failing and we dropped to burst intervals, and then the fourth generation was born and didn't even know what the old words meant, and the signal became a ritual instead of a plea. We kept sending it because stopping felt like agreeing to die."

The bay hummed around them, forty thousand soft machines breathing in rhythm. Somewhere in the rows, a diagnostic strip flicked from green to amber with a sound too small to hear, and Astrid felt it land in his chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"How many are left to lose?" he asked.

"At current decay rates? Half the fleet's minds are gone already — unrecoverable, patterns too degraded. Of the rest, we have maybe twenty years before the rot passes the point of no return for the majority." Dagny said it the way she'd say a pressure reading. Numbers first. Screaming later, privately, forever. "My engineers have spent three generations trying to reverse-engineer the protocol from first principles. You can't. It's not engineering, it's a key cut to match a lock the Consensus keeps in its pocket. We've begged, we've traded, we've transmitted proof of the decay on every band that might carry. And then you show up. A smuggler with an antique rig and fresh papers, answering a call nobody else could even hear."

She stepped closer. This close, Astrid could see the tremor in her hands that fifty years of steady work hadn't cured, only disciplined.

"So here's my question, Torvald Reyes, or whoever you are. The Consensus sent auditors to Vesper Gate last month. Quiet ones. Asking about anomalous signals, old-band traffic, memory traffic. Someone up there knows the call finally got through. Which means someone up there knows we're dying. And they sent *auditors*." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "So I'll ask you straight, because I'm too old to dance. Did they send you? Are you their errand?"

"No." And then, because lying to this woman felt like desecrating a church: "But they're looking for me. An auditor named Sefa Ilyes nearly had me at the Gate. She saw my decode of your signal. She matched it against classified files on her own terminal — files her government told her didn't exist. She let me go. Officially, she lost me." He held Dagny's gaze. "Unofficially, I think you have a friend on the other side of the gap. Or the beginning of one."

Dagny was quiet for a long moment. Around them the pods breathed their frozen breath, and somewhere a child's footsteps pattered down an upper gallery, quick and light, running an errand in the house of the sleeping dead.

"A friend," she repeated at last, tasting the word like something expired. "Two hundred years of silence, and now a smuggler and a turncoat auditor." She almost smiled. It was a terrible, wonderful thing to see. "That's not a rescue party, Reyes. That's a bad joke."

"It's a start."

"It's arithmetic," she corrected. "And the arithmetic is ugly. Come on. There's someone you need to meet — born awake, fourth generation, smarter than me and angrier than you. And after that, you and I are going to sit down with your fancy rig and figure out exactly how much of that signal you decoded — because if the Consensus knows you have it, then you're not a salvager anymore, friend. You're the most dangerous piece of contraband in the human species."

She turned toward the bay doors, and Astrid followed, and behind them the pods went on breathing, forty thousand dreams thinning like frost, patient as the dead, waiting for a key that was still locked on the far side of the sky.

At the junction, Dagny paused. "One more thing," she said, not turning around. "The signal you decoded. The repeating phrase. *Request waking protocol.*"

"Yes?"

"We never sent that part."
