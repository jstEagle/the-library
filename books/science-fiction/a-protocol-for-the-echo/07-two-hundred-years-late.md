## 7. Two Hundred Years Late

The night the Sundering happened, Dagny Okafor was thirty-one years old and standing in the Meridian's primary comms bay with a mug of cold chicory going stale in her hand, watching the last transmission from Earth resolve into static.

She would remember the shape of that static for the rest of her life. It looked like snowfall. It looked like a city burning in negative.

"Say again, Consensus Actual," said Halvorsen at the relay console, his voice steady in the way of a man who has decided not to hear what he's hearing. "Meridian Fleet requests confirmation of protocol transfer schedule. We have forty thousand sleepers on standby wake cycles. Requesting — " He stopped. Listened. His shoulders did something small and terrible.

"What are they saying?" Dagny asked.

Halvorsen didn't turn around. "They're saying there's been a change."

* * *

The story everyone aboard told afterward — the story Dagny herself had told for years, because it was easier to carry than the truth — was that the waking protocols were lost. That when the orbital habitats severed from the ark fleet in the chaos of the Exodus schism, the encrypted master keys to the cryo-revival systems went with them, locked inside Consensus archives, casualties of a war of paperwork rather than weapons. An accident of history. A tragedy of bad timing.

Dagny had believed it for eleven years.

Then she found the memo.

It came to her the way most ruinous things come: quietly, on an ordinary shift, buried in a maintenance archive she wasn't supposed to be reading. She was tracking a coolant anomaly in Bay Seven — nothing dramatic, a valve stutter that kept tripping alarms — and she'd gone digging through the original engineering handoffs from the fleet's construction era, looking for as-built schematics. The archive was a graveyard of old formats. Half the files wouldn't open. The other half opened into tedium: torque specifications, weld certifications, supply manifests listing ceramic bearings by the thousand.

And then, filed under a mislabeled directory called VENT-DUCT-REVISIONS-4, a document with a Consensus security header she recognized only because she'd once seen it on a recruitment poster, back when the two halves of humanity still pretended to be one species.

The header read: PRIORITY ABSOLUTE — EYES ONLY — COUNCIL CIRCULAR.

She should have flagged it. She should have done the dutiful thing, logged it, sent it up the chain to Fleet Command, where it would have vanished into the same silence that swallowed everything else they sent toward the orbitals and never got back. Instead she sat down in the archive alcove with its failing amber lights and its smell of cold metal and older dust, and she read it.

The memo was four paragraphs long. She could recite it now, two hundred years later, word for word, in the dark, in her sleep, which she often did, though she no longer called them dreams.

It began: *In light of the political divergence between the Habitat Consensus and the Ark Fleet Meridian, Council assesses that the reintegration of the sleeper population represents the single greatest long-term strategic variable.* 

Strategic variable. Forty thousand people in drawers. Her mother among them, her brother, three-quarters of the engineering staff she'd trained beside. Variables.

The second paragraph laid out the arithmetic with the serene cruelty of people who had learned to think in populations. The arks' life support, their hulls, their drive systems — all of it had been built by orbital yards, maintained to orbital standards, dependent on parts and expertise that existed only in Consensus space. If the sleepers woke, the fleet would need those supply lines restored within a generation or die. Which meant, the memo observed, that the sleeping fleet's survival was contingent on good behavior for decades to come. *The withheld revival capability therefore functions not as abandonment but as assurance,* the memo said. *A bond of trust, held in escrow.*

Escrow. Dagny had looked the word up afterward in the ship's dictionary, though she knew perfectly well what it meant. She'd wanted to see it printed plainly, wanted the ugliness of it in plain type instead of her own furious memory. Held in trust until conditions are fulfilled. Hostage, in every language but the one lawyers spoke.

The third paragraph instructed Fleet Command — her Fleet Command, the officers whose portraits hung in the corridor outside the bridge, whose signatures were on her commission — to accept the arrangement without formal protest, to log the loss of the protocols as accidental, and to make no record of this circular beyond the single classified copy. *An open grievance would harden positions,* it explained. *Silence preserves the possibility of future reconciliation.*

The fourth paragraph wished everyone luck. She meant that literally. It ended with the phrase *with every confidence in our shared future*, and Dagny sat in the amber dark of the archive and laughed until the laugh turned into something else, something she pressed against her sleeve so the duty officer walking the aisle behind her wouldn't hear.

Her mother had gone under believing she'd be woken in forty years. Believing it the way you believe sunrise. There had been a goodbye, of course — there was always a goodbye — but it hadn't been a real goodbye, because real goodbyes are for endings, and this was supposed to be a nap. Her mother had squeezed her hand through the bay glass and mouthed *see you soon*, and Dagny had smiled and waved like a child at a departing train, and the drawer had sealed, and somewhere in an office she would never stand in, a man with a council seal had already signed the paper that turned *see you soon* into a lie with a two-hundred-year fuse.

* * *

She took the memo to Commander Ilsa Reyes-Vance on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were Reyes-Vance's open-door days and because Dagny was twenty-nine days past her own breaking point and thought, with the beautiful naivety of a good engineer, that evidence was evidence, that systems either worked or they didn't, that you brought the fault report to the person who could sign the work order.

Reyes-Vance read it twice. Dagny watched her face do very little, which should have been the first warning. Good news makes faces move. Bad news makes faces move. Only anticipated news leaves them still.

"You understand this can't leave this room," Reyes-Vance said at last.

"It left the room two hundred years ago," Dagny said. "It's just arriving now."

"Dagny." The commander set the printout down flat, aligned its edges with the desk, a small compulsive orderliness that Dagny would come to recognize over the following years as the gesture of a woman keeping herself together by tidying whatever was nearest. "I know what's in that file. I've known for six years."

The bay hummed. Somewhere below them, forty thousand hearts beat at four per minute, patient as machinery, trusting as children.

"Six years," Dagny repeated.

"And in six years, what has knowing changed?" Reyes-Vance stood, walked to the viewport, where the stars wheeled slow and indifferent. "We have no leverage. We have no manufacturing base. Every spare reactor governor in this fleet is a relic we're cannibalizing slower than it decays. If we broadcast this, if we tell the crew, tell the sleepers' families — what then? Mutiny? A war we lose in nine months? And the Consensus wakes nobody, ever, and calls it regrettable. Or worse — they don't even bother calling it anything."

"So we sit on it. We let them hold our mothers hostage and we say thank you."

"We keep the fleet alive," Reyes-Vance said, turning, and her eyes were red-rimmed and exhausted and honest, which was somehow worse than if they'd been cold. "That's the whole job now, Dagny. Not justice. Not truth. Alive. Every year this ship breathes is a year the debt stays collectible. You want fury? Fine. Be furious. But be furious quietly, in a maintenance uniform, for fifty years, because the day this fleet stops functioning is the day the Consensus stops needing us calm."

Dagny had gone home to her bunk and lain awake listening to the ship breathe, and somewhere around fourth shift she understood the trap completely, and it was elegant, and she hated elegance from that day forward. They couldn't fight. Fighting killed the sleepers faster than neglect would. They couldn't leave. There was nowhere to go. They couldn't even grieve properly, because grief required admitting loss, and admission was the one thing the arrangement forbade. All that remained was maintenance. Tending. Keeping the hostages healthy and the lie warm, forever, in exchange for the hope — the thin, humiliating, unkillable hope — that someday someone in the orbitals would grow a conscience.

Two hundred years. Nobody had grown one yet.

* * *

So she did what Reyes-Vance asked. She put on the maintenance uniform. She fixed the valve in Bay Seven. She became, over the decades, the best cryo-systems engineer the Meridian ever produced, and she did it with a specific kind of attention that the younger techs mistook for devotion and that was actually surveillance. She mapped every failure mode of every drawer. She learned the decay curves of forty thousand sleeping minds the way a doctor learns the faces of the dying — intimately, helplessly, by heart.

Because here was the thing the memo hadn't bothered to calculate, the variable the strategists in their orbital offices had never once considered: cryostasis wasn't storage. It was medicine, and medicine expires. The neural patterns held in suspension degraded — slowly, glacially, a few cells a decade, a few memories a century — and the restoration protocols that could repair that drift were the same ones sitting in a Consensus vault, gathering the digital equivalent of dust. Every year of delay didn't just postpone the waking. It ate the sleepers. Bit by bit. Name by name. The Consensus hadn't frozen forty thousand people; it had started a clock on forty thousand erasures and then hidden the key to stopping it.

Dagny ran the numbers herself, alone, over thirty years, and when she finished she sat in the archive alcove — the same one, the amber lights finally dead above her — and she wrote in her personal ledger, in handwriting that shook:

*At current decay rates, 12% of the sleeper complement will be unrecoverable by year 150. By year 200, conservatively 30%. My mother's pattern shows early-stage degradation as of last scan. Estimated window remaining: unknown. Possibly already closed.*

Then, beneath it, because she was an engineer and engineers write the next line even when it hurts:

*Conclusion: the crime is not theft. Theft implies the property survives elsewhere. This is demolition conducted at the speed of patience.*

* * *

She told Joon's grandmother once — not Joon himself, he wasn't born yet; she told Marta, in the galley, over reconstituted tea, on the one night in sixty years she let the ledger out from behind her ribs. Marta listened all the way through without interrupting, which was her gift, and at the end she said, "You've known since before my parents went under. And you fixed their drawers anyway. Kept them cold and safe and dying slowly. Why?"

"Because the alternative was letting them die fast," Dagny said. "And because someday — " and here her voice did the thing it always did, the thing she despised, the hopeful crack, the engineer's fatal flaw, believing the system could be repaired if you just found the right fault — "someday somebody over there opens the wrong file. Somebody curious. Somebody with a ship and a rig and no respect for borders. Two hundred years is a long time, Marta. Secrets rot faster than sleepers do."

Marta had wrapped both hands around her tea and looked at her for a long moment. "And when that happens," she said, "what's left to wake?"

Dagny hadn't answered. She hadn't had an answer. She had a ledger full of decay curves and a fleet full of fading ghosts and a fury two centuries old with nowhere to put itself except into gaskets and coolant loops and the endless, holy, humiliating work of keeping the drawers running.

* * *

Now she was old, and the alarm was singing through the Meridian's corridors, and a smuggler's ship with false papers was sitting in her docking cradle carrying a young man whose neural rig had just decoded a distress call she'd helped draft a hundred and forty years ago — the fleet's own voice, crying out into the dark in a dead language because the living language of the orbitals had stopped being theirs.

He was standing in front of her in the bay office, this Astrid, all sharp competence and careful eyes, asking his questions with a smuggler's casualness that fooled absolutely no one who had spent two hundred years reading people the way she read pressure gauges. Behind him, Joon hovered, young and hungry and terrified of his own hope.

"The signal," Astrid said. "It's genuine? It's really two hundred years old?"

"It's genuine," Dagny said. "It's really two hundred years old. And it isn't a distress call, not exactly." She pulled her ledger from the lockbox where it had lived through four commandants, three mutiny scares, and one fire. She set it on the table between them, cover worn soft as cloth, and watched the young man look at it the way people look at scripture or ordnance.

"A distress call asks for rescue," she said. "This is an indictment. Forty thousand people were taken hostage at the Sundering, and the ransom was our silence, and we paid it, and they let the clock run anyway." She opened the ledger to the first page, to her own shaking handwriting, younger than Joon, older than grief. "You want to know what's killing the sleepers, boy? It isn't time. Time we could have beaten. It's a decision. Made by comfortable people, in comfortable chairs, in the orbitals your Consensus calls home. And the protocol that undoes it — "

She stopped. Because she had waited two hundred years to say the next part, rehearsed it in ten thousand silent shifts, and now that it was here it came out quiet, almost gentle, which frightened her more than shouting would have.

"— the protocol that undoes it exists. Intact. In Consensus custody. And if anyone alive can carry it across the gap, it's you."

Joon made a sound behind him, small and involuntary, like something breaking open.

Dagny turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table. "Read it," she said. "All of it. Then decide fast, because the decay curves don't care about your feelings, and neither do I. Every hour you spend deciding is an hour I'm watching another name cross the line where no protocol in existence can bring them back."

Outside the office window, the cryo-bays stretched away into the dark, forty thousand points of monitoring light blinking their slow green patience, and Dagny Okafor — ninety-one years old, keeper of the fleet's oldest secret, custodian of its oldest wound — felt the thing she had buried rise in her chest at last with somewhere to go.

Fury, after two hundred years, had found its errand boy.
