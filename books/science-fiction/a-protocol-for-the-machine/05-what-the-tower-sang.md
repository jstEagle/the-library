## 5. What the Tower Sang

The dream came back on a Tuesday, which felt rude. Even dreams should have the decency to wait for the weekend.

I'd been having it since I was eight—two years now. The singing tower. It stood in a bowl of dark grass under too many stars, and it sang in a woman's voice, except not quite a woman's voice, more like a woman's voice remembered by someone who had only ever heard her through a wall. The song had words sometimes. Most of the time it was just *hold*, over and over, sung so gently you could almost believe nothing bad had ever happened anywhere.

Every kid in Meridian's Rest had it. That was the weird part. Not similar dreams—*the same* dream. Priya dreamed the tower standing in water. Tomas dreamed it buried to its waist in red sand. But the tower was always the tower, down to the hairline crack running up its left side like a scar, and the song was always the song.

Adults said it was a fad. Adults say everything is a fad until it's a crisis. My mother said my neural implant needed recalibrating, which is what she says about everything from nightmares to opinions. So we did what children of fringe colonies have always done when adults fail them: we made a plan, we stole snacks, and we went out into the night to find out for ourselves.

There were six of us. Priya Okonjo, who could pick any lock on the colony with a hairpin and pure disrespect for property law. Tomas and his little brother Dev, who brought a flashlight shaped like a frog because apparently terror required amphibians. Naledi, who was nine and fearless in the way that only makes sense if you haven't yet learned what fear is for. Amara, who kept the map. And me, Sefu Adeyemi, ten years old, keeper of the sketchbook, which meant I was the one who'd drawn the tower forty times and finally noticed something that made my stomach drop through the floor of the world.

I knew where it was.

Not "somewhere out past the ridge." Not "in the general direction of nowhere." I knew. Because three months ago, chasing a runaway cargo drone for an insurance claim my aunt was filing—and yes, I helped my aunt file insurance claims, everyone needs a hobby—I'd crested the ridge at Kilometer Forty and seen it. A real tower, in a real bowl of dark grass, with a real hairline crack running up its left side.

Nobody lived at Kilometer Forty. Nobody was supposed to. The colony maps showed nothing but relay stubs and old survey markers. And yet there it stood, humming to itself under the stars, singing a song I'd been hearing since I was eight years old.

You don't tell adults things like that. Adults call it trespassing, then they call your parents, then your whole life becomes a series of meetings. You tell your friends. You make a plan. You steal snacks.

* * *

We went out during the long dark, when Meridian's Rest turns its face away from the sun and even the hardiest colonists huddle inside pretending the cold isn't personal. Priya popped the maintenance gate on the drainage road—she'd been practicing on it for weeks, and honestly the lock barely put up a fight—and we walked the four kilometers out along the service track, six small shadows in a landscape that seemed embarrassed to have us in it.

"Tell me again why we're doing this," Dev whispered, for the ninth time.

"Because the dream is ours," Naledi said, "and nobody else gets to decide what it means."

That's Naledi. She's nine and she talks like a fortune cookie, but you can't argue with her because she's usually right.

"It's probably just a weather relay," Tomas said.

"Weather relays don't sing," I said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I've stood next to one in a storm and it mostly just complains."

The grass got taller as we came down into the bowl, dry stalks hissing against our legs like the ground itself was telling us to be quiet. And then the tower rose up ahead of us, and every single one of us stopped talking at exactly the same moment, which has never happened before or since.

It was bigger than the dream. Dreams are stingy with scale; they show you the idea of a thing, not the thing. This was thirty meters of dark metal and composite, older than any of us, older than most of the colony, its surface patched and re-patched by hands that cared about it the way you care about a wound—with patience and no witnesses. The crack ran up the left flank exactly where I'd drawn it, forty times, in three different notebooks. At its base sat a low equipment shelter with a single dim light behind a frosted pane, and a path worn smooth in the grass leading to its door.

A path. Worn smooth. Somebody walked here. Somebody walked here often, and recently, and alone.

"Is someone home?" Amara whispered.

"The tower lady," Naledi said simply, and we all looked at her.

"What tower lady?" I asked.

"The one in the dream. Behind the song. Didn't you ever notice? There's someone holding the note. Like—" she frowned, searching for it, "—like when Mama sings and Papa hums underneath. The song is the tower. But the humming is a person."

I hadn't noticed. But standing there, listening—not with my ears, with whatever part of me the dream had been using all these years—I heard it. Underneath the carrier tone, deep and steady as a heartbeat, something human. Something keeping time.

Priya tried the shelter door. It wasn't locked. Which told us two things: whoever lived here either trusted the emptiness of Kilometer Forty completely, or had never once imagined that six children would walk four kilometers through the freezing dark because of a shared dream. Both possibilities said something about this person that I couldn't name yet, but filed away anyway, the way I filed everything about the tower.

Inside: a cot, neatly made. A kettle. A wall of tools hung in perfect order, each one outlined in marker like a museum exhibit. And a console, alive with soft green light, scrolling data too fast to read. On the desk, a single framed photograph, face-down.

Tomas reached for it. I caught his wrist.

"Don't," I said. "You don't turn over people's photographs. That's rule one of breaking into somewhere."

"There are rules?"

"There are always rules. That's what separates us from animals."

Dev snorted. Then the console chimed—a soft, descending note, almost apologetic—and the room filled with sound.

* * *

I want to describe it properly, because I've spent two years failing to describe it, and I'm going to keep failing, but I want it on record that I tried.

The song didn't come from speakers. It came from everywhere—the walls, the floor, the fillings in our teeth, the space behind our eyes where the implant sat doing whatever implants do. It was the dream-song, exact, note for note, the one we'd all been hearing since we were small. But awake, standing inside it, we could hear the layers. Underneath the gentle *hold, hold, hold*, there were voices. Many voices. Some calm, some shaking. A man reading names. A woman saying *if anyone receives this* in eleven different languages, or maybe one language eleven times, it was hard to tell. A child laughing, once, briefly, in a way that made all of us go quiet in a different way than before.

And threaded through it, unmistakable now that Naledi had pointed it out: a second voice. Low. Steady. Humming underneath the broadcast like a hand pressed flat against a trembling door, holding it shut, holding it together.

"That's her," Naledi breathed. "That's the tower lady."

"She's here," Priya said. "She's *here*. The kettle's warm."

It was. I touched it. Warm, not hot—used within the hour. My skin prickled. We weren't discovering a mystery; we were standing in the middle of somebody's life, and that somebody had stepped out for a moment, maybe to check the antenna, maybe to stand in the grass and listen to her own song the way we were listening to it now.

The outer door scraped.

I will state for the record that none of us screamed. There was a collective intake of breath that could charitably be called a gasp, and Dev dropped the frog flashlight, which croaked, which did not help.

She stood in the doorway backlit by starlight: tall, gray-haired, wrapped in a coat built for colder planets than this one, with a toolbox in one hand and an expression moving rapidly through surprise, alarm, resignation, and something else—something that looked, absurdly, like relief.

She looked at us. Six children in her sanctuary, caught red-handed around her warm kettle.

"You're the dreamers," she said.

Not *who are you*. Not *how did you get here*. Not *you're trespassing*. She said *you're the dreamers*, in the voice of someone who has been waiting for this conversation for a very long time and dreading it and, possibly, hoping for it.

"Yes," said Naledi, because of course Naledi answered first. "Are you the tower lady?"

Something happened to the woman's face then. All the armor in it— and there was armor, decades of it, thick as hull plating—cracked down the middle, right along a line I swear matched the crack in the tower. She set down the toolbox very carefully, as if her hands needed a job that wasn't feelings.

"I'm Beatrix," she said. "I maintain the beacon. And I imagine all of you have questions."

"We have *so* many questions," said Tomas.

"I have one," I said, and everyone looked at me, and I hadn't planned to speak but the question had been sitting in my chest for two years and it came out before I could stop it. "Who are the people in the song? The ones saying *if anyone receives this*?"

Beatrix Montrose—for that was the name on the tools, stenciled small and neat—looked at me for a long moment. Then she crossed the room, turned the console's display toward us, and pointed at a line of text cycling through the green light. An origin stamp. A date. A place.

*Earth.*

"They were the last people broadcasting," she said quietly. "From home. And the beacon repeats their words so that two fleets out there—" she gestured upward, at the sky, at everything we couldn't see, "—keep believing Earth still belongs to someone. If they ever learn the truth, they'll come here. They'll come *for here*. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

We looked at each other. Six kids, ages seven to eleven, standing in the load-bearing secret of the entire colony.

"You've been lying to everyone," Priya said. "For how long?"

"Forty years," Beatrix said.

"And if we tell?"

The silence stretched. Outside, the tower hummed its borrowed grief into the dark, and I understood suddenly that the dream had never been an accident, and never a fad, and never anything we could give back. We knew now. All of us. Forever.

"You won't," Beatrix said—but her voice shook on it, and I saw her looking at each of our faces the way you look at a fuse you suspect is already burning.

She was wrong, actually. Sort of. Because we didn't tell the fleets, and we didn't tell the fleet agents who started showing up within the month asking their sharp questions. But we also didn't tell *nobody*.

Because the next morning, Administrator Vashti Okafor stopped me in the market lane, crouched down to my eye level with that smile adults use when they already know the answer, and asked me, sweet as anything:

"Sefu. Why do your boots have Kilometer Forty mud on them?"
