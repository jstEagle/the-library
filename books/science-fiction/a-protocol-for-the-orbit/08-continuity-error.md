## 8. Continuity Error

The relay station hung in the dark like a ribcage picked clean, its antenna spars reaching toward nothing. Naya had seen derelicts before—Halcyon's outer belt was littered with them, monuments to the era when the colonies had reached farther than their supply lines could hold. But she had never stood inside one. She had never felt the way a dead station breathed: the slow tick of cooling metal, the whisper of residual atmosphere moving through ducts that no longer had a purpose.

She stood now, in the dark of the old comm bay, with three of her students pressed against her sides like burrs.

Tomas had stopped shivering an hour ago. That frightened her more than the shivering had. Amara kept her hand fisted in Naya's jacket, and little Dev—six years old, too young to understand anything except that Miss Naya had said *run* and so they ran—kept asking, in a whisper he clearly believed was silent, whether they were going home yet.

"Soon," Naya said, for the fourth time. It was becoming a prayer she didn't believe in.

Through the comm bay's cracked viewport, she could see Orsen Villalobos at the relay's main console, lit blue by screens that hadn't glowed in thirty years until she'd coaxed them back to life. The negotiator moved with that terrible economy Naya had come to recognize—not a wasted gesture anywhere, every motion rehearsed by a dead woman and performed faithfully by whoever this person actually was. Naya had watched her for weeks now, through the treaty renewal, through the quarantine announcement, through the desperate night when Naya had smuggled children into a cargo hold because staying on Halcyon meant forgetting them. And somewhere in all that watching, Naya had stopped seeing Villalobos the diplomat and started seeing someone else entirely: a woman running calculations behind her eyes, always, even now—even here, cornered, hunted by two fleets.

*Cornered* was not an exaggeration. The Meridian vanguard had found them six hours out from Halcyon, and the Ashfall pickets had converged within the hour after. Orsen had threaded the debris field like a needle through cloth, but there was nowhere left to thread. This relay was the last rock before open black, and both fleets knew it. Naya had watched the tactical display fill with contacts the way a glass fills with rain.

"They're hailing us again," Tomas said. He'd been listening on the headset Orsen had given him, teaching him, even now, even here. "Meridian first. Then Ashfall. They're both saying the same thing."

"What thing?"

Tomas looked up at her, and his face was older than it had been a month ago. "They want the children back. And they want Villalobos to surrender for questioning." A pause. "Both fleets are saying they'll fire if we run for the jump point."

Naya closed her eyes. When she opened them, Orsen was standing beside her. Naya hadn't heard her cross the room. Of course she hadn't.

"It's time," Orsen said quietly. "I need you to take them to the aft compartment. Sealed bulkhead. If something goes wrong—"

"No," Naya said. It came out flat and certain, surprising her. "No. Whatever you're about to do, we stay."

Orsen studied her. In the blue light her face was unreadable, and then, briefly, readable—the flicker of someone who had spent her whole borrowed life being alone and didn't know what to do with people who refused to leave. "It may be dangerous."

"Everything is dangerous," Naya said. "You taught my students burn calculations in a cargo hold while warships chased us. You don't get to send us behind a bulkhead now."

Dev tugged her sleeve. "Is it story time?"

And that—that undid something in Naya's chest. Because that was exactly what it was. Every night in the hold, the children slept in a pile of cargo netting and dreamed together, and every morning they told the dream again, adding pieces, refining it, the way children refine a game until it becomes realer than the world around it. The white room. The counting voice. The blood on glass. The argument between the dead woman and someone she trusted. The syringe. The signature.

The murder, assembling itself nightly out of eleven sleeping minds.

"You're going to broadcast it," Naya said. Understanding arriving all at once, cold and complete. "The dream. You're going to put it on the fleet channels."

Orsen's jaw tightened. "Every ship in both fleets is locked onto this station. Open channels. If I transmit the memory archive directly—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the negotiator surfacing. "Vess has spent years controlling what everyone knows. One voice, one narrative, one treaty signed by a ghost. But he can't control eleven children dreaming out loud into every crew compartment at once. Not when the memory is *mine*. Not when it carries my neural signature and his voice."

"You're not going to speak," Naya realized. "You're not going to confess over the comm. You're going to let *them* do it."

"The children remember things I don't," Orsen said simply. "Their pooled fragments are more complete than my corrupted archive. If I narrate it, it's testimony—one clone's claim, deniable. If they transmit the raw dream, it's evidence. Everyone hears the same thing at once. There's no version of events Vess can spin fast enough to beat that."

Amara spoke up, small and fierce. "We're not scared of it anymore. The dream. We used to be. But it's just a lady who got hurt, and she wants people to know."

Naya looked down at her student, at all three of them, and felt the terror in her own chest transmute into something harder. "Then we do it together."

* * *

The comm bay smelled of ozone and dust. Orsen wired the relay's ancient transmitter into a portable archive drive—the same drive, Naya understood, that held the corrupted blocks of a murdered woman's mind—and the children sat in a half-circle on the deck plating as if settling in for a lesson. Which, Naya supposed, it was.

"I won't make you," Orsen said, kneeling to their level. "Any of you. Once this goes out, it can't be taken back. Every crew member on forty ships will hear what happened to me—to her. Some of those people loved her. Some of them will hate that you made them grieve."

Tomas lifted his chin. "She was our friend before we ever met her."

Something crossed Orsen's face then that Naya would remember for the rest of her life: grief and gratitude tangled so tightly they were indistinguishable. She nodded once, and turned to the console.

"Channel status?" Naya asked, because someone had to ask practical questions; that had always been her role, even here, even now.

"Open to both fleets' command frequencies. Wideband." Orsen's hands hovered over the keys. "Once I key it, they'll either listen or they'll shoot. Probably both."

"And Vess?"

"He'll try to jam it. He'll try to cut the relay's power remotely, discredit the signal, call it a fabrication." Orsen smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "But neural archives carry authentication watermarks. Fleet-grade. He built the system himself—he'll know the watermark is genuine, and so will every technician listening. He can't forge one. He can only deny it. Denial is slower than truth."

Dev raised his hand, absurdly, the classroom reflex surviving even here. "Do we close our eyes?"

Orsen paused. "If you want."

"We sleep better with them open now," Amara said. "Miss Naya taught us. You look at one fixed point and breathe."

Naya hadn't taught them that. But she was glad they thought she had.

Orsen keyed the transmission.

* * *

There was no sound at first. Just the waveform climbing the display, a pale spine of light rising on the dead screen. Then the comm speakers, tuned wideband, began to render what the children carried—and Naya's knees nearly gave.

It was not audio, exactly. It was everything at once: the hum of a white room rendered as tone, the cadence of a countdown felt in the sternum, the smell of antiseptic translated into static. The neural archive poured itself into the channel raw, unfiltered, and every speaker on every ship within range became a window into a dying woman's last hour.

Naya heard the argument. Everyone heard the argument.

*"You can't alter the manifest, Vess. The Ashfall delegation audits tomorrow. If they find the shipment discrepancies—"*

*"Orsen. Orsen, listen to me. The treaty is fragile. Fragile things need gardeners, not auditors. Give me the codes and in ten years no one will remember there was ever a discrepancy at all."*

*"That's not peace. That's a hostage situation with better lighting."*

A laugh—warm, familiar, horrible. Naya had heard that laugh across treaty banquets, warm as brandy. *"You always did have a flair for the dramatic. It's why they chose you."* Then the sound of a syringe depressing—a soft pneumatic sigh the archive rendered as a kiss of static—then the countdown, the woman's own voice counting herself down, *ten, nine, eight*, growing slurred, *four, three*, and the wet sound of glass breaking, and blood, and silence.

Silence, and then, because the children's pooled memory held one fragment more than any single archive should have contained, a final line, spoken low and satisfied in the dark:

*"Continuity maintained."*

In the comm bay, nobody moved. On the display, the waveform flattened to a line.

Then the channels detonated.

Not gunfire—voices. Dozens of them, hundreds, bleeding through the open wideband from forty ships: Meridian crews and Ashfall crews hearing the same murder at the same moment, their shock colliding in shared spectrum. Someone was crying. Someone was shouting for their captain. Someone, on an Ashfall corvette, was repeating *that's Villalobos, that's her voice, I served under her* over and over like a man reciting scripture. A Meridian comms officer broke protocol entirely and broadcast, unencrypted, to both fleets at once: *"Requesting verification of archive watermark. Requesting verification. Oh God, it's authentic, it's authentic—"*

And beneath it all, cutting through like a blade through silk, one voice, calm and controlled and utterly unmistakable:

"This is Vess. All ships, disregard that transmission. It is a fabricated psychological operation conducted by the fugitive vessel. I repeat, disregard—"

He got four sentences out. Four sentences into a channel full of people who had just heard him kill a woman, and every sentence sounded exactly like the voice in the white room, and every crew in both fleets heard the match at the same instant.

The channel turned on him like a tide.

Naya sank down against the console, pulling Dev into her lap, and laughed—a shaky, disbelieving sound that was half sob. Across the bay, Orsen stood very still, watching the waveform dance, watching the truth escape every cage that had ever been built for it. Her hands were trembling. Naya noticed. For the first time since Naya had known her, Orsen Villalobos's hands were trembling, and she wasn't hiding it.

"It worked," Naya whispered. "It actually worked."

Orsen shook her head slowly. "No. It's worse than that. Look."

On the tactical display, the fleet formations were dissolving—not scattering, but *reorganizing*, ships peeling away from their squadrons, Meridian destroyers falling in alongside Ashfall frigates in ad hoc pairs, all of them converging on one heading.

On the relay station.

On them.

"They heard the truth," Orsen said quietly. "Now they're coming to decide what to do with the woman who told it. Both fleets. At once." She turned, and in the blue light her eyes were steady, and terrified, and free. "Get the children strapped in, Naya. Whatever happens next, it happens with us looking at them."

The first ship dropped out of translation less than a thousand kilometers off the hull, close enough that the station's dead hull rang with its wake—and its guns were already hot.
