## 10. A Protocol for the Orbit

### Orsen

The table they built for the signing was too long, and that was the point.

Orsen had watched them assemble it in Halcyon's central atrium over the course of three weeks—Meridian engineers on one shift, Ashfall welders on the next, neither crew willing to let the other do it alone, both unwilling to admit why. The result was a slab of salvaged hull plating, scarred with old micrometeorite pitting, polished just enough to write on. It was ugly. It was honest. Every scratch in its surface had a story someone could point to.

She stood at the far end of it now, in a jacket she'd bought herself—not issued, not replicated from a dead woman's wardrobe—and watched the delegates file in. Commander Ilsa Reyes of Meridian arrived first, her dress grays pressed to knife-edge precision. Then Commander Dae-Ho Sun of Ashfall, who had stopped wearing his fleet's colors entirely and settled instead on civilian charcoal, as if he were already rehearsing for a future where the fleets were something other than two barrels pointed across a dock.

Between them walked the children.

There were eleven of them, though only nine had hidden in her cargo hold that night. The other two had joined later, drawn by dreams they couldn't explain to their parents—dreams of a white room, of counting down, of blood blooming across glass like ink in water. Naya had vouched for all eleven. Naya, whose quiet stubbornness had done more to end the war-that-wasn't than any treaty clause ever drafted.

The children carried the archive core between them on a litter, four hands apiece, taking turns. They had insisted. *It's ours,* Tam had said, in the flat way children say things that are simply true. *We dreamed it. You don't get to carry our dream for us.*

The core was a modest thing—a canister the size of a water jug, its casing transparent so anyone could see the lattice inside, the crystallized pattern of a memory that had once been locked in a dead woman's skull and smuggled out through the sleeping minds of children. No encryption. No access restrictions. Anyone in either fleet, any colonist, any child not yet born, could query it and hear the original Villalobos argue with Vess about a falsified manifest; could hear the hiss of the syringe; could hear Vess say, in a voice no one who'd served beside him would ever mistake: *The peace is more important than the peacemaker.*

That sentence had ended a career, a conspiracy, and very nearly a man. Vess was alive, technically, serving a life sentence aboard a Meridian prison hulk, where he reportedly spent his days drafting appeals no one read. Orsen had visited him once. She wasn't sure why. He had looked at her the way a man looks at a mirror that has started giving him back the wrong face, and he had said nothing, and she had left feeling lighter than she expected—as if she'd finally put down something heavy that had never actually been hers to carry.

"Delegate Okonkwo." Reyes gestured to the open seat near the head of the table. "Whenever you're ready."

*Okonkwo.* Not Villalobos. Not even the name she'd woken up with in a cloning vat, the placeholder designation stamped into her file before anyone thought to ask what she might want to be called. The children had chosen it six weeks after the broadcast, sitting cross-legged on the deck of her cargo hold while the fleets argued over her fate. Naya's grandmother's name, they said. A teacher's name. A name that meant something about endurance.

She had cried, a little, when they told her. She'd told them it was recycled air irritating her eyes. None of them had believed her. That was fine. Being disbelieved kindly was also new, and she was learning to like it.

She sat. The document before her was displayed on a slate, but a paper copy lay beneath it—the fleets had insisted on paper, some old ritual instinct, ink that couldn't be edited retroactively by anyone with server access. The title ran across the top in both fleets' typefaces, side by side:

**THE HALCYON ACCORD: A PROTOCOL FOR THE ORBIT**

It was short, as treaties went. That was deliberate. Article One established joint administration of contested orbit, with rotating command reviewed quarterly in public session. Article Two mandated full disclosure of neural archives held by either fleet—all archives, including the ones that had started everything. Article Three was the one the children had dictated, nearly verbatim, and the delegates had been wise enough not to soften it:

*No memory shall be owned, sealed, or weaponized against the person from whom it came.*

Article Four concerned her. It did not name her as an asset, or a continuity measure, or a diplomatic instrument. It named her as a signatory. A party. A person.

Orsen picked up the pen. Her hand was steady. She noticed that, and noticed herself noticing, and understood that this was what healing looked like for someone built from another woman's grief: not the absence of vigilance, but vigilance finally aimed at nothing.

She signed.

*O. Okonkwo.*

The letters looked strange under her hand—unfamiliar, unclaimed until this moment, hers the way a path through grass is hers after she has walked it enough times. Around the table, the delegates signed in turn. Reyes. Sun. Naya. And last, solemnly, each of the eleven children, some of them needing two hands on the pen, their signatures sprawling across the margin like small constellations.

When it was done, nobody cheered. Instead there was a silence—not the armed, watchful silence of two fleets sharing a dock, but something warmer and stranger. The silence of people realizing, together, that they have run out of reasons to pretend.

Tam broke it, because children always do. "Can we go see the ship?"

Reyes laughed. Sun laughed. Orsen found she was laughing too, and that it didn't hurt.

### Naya

Naya stayed near the atrium doors, the way she always did at these things—close enough to witness, far enough to let it belong to others. Teaching had taught her that. The best moments in a classroom were the ones you didn't hover over.

She watched Orsen across the room and marveled, not for the first time, at how still she had become. In those first months—the forged credentials, the memorized laugh, the way she moved through Halcyon's corridors like a woman trying not to disturb the air—Naya had thought of her as a ghost wearing a uniform. Now the uniform was gone, replaced by that self-bought jacket with the frayed cuffs she refused to replace, and Orsen stood in the middle of a crowd of delegates and children and dockworkers as if she had grown roots through the deck plating.

She had almost left, twice. Naya knew about both times. The first was three days after the confession, when the fleets' tribunals were still deciding whether she was a victim or a fraud, and Orsen had packed her ship in silence and sat in the pilot's chair for four hours without running preflight. Naya had found her there, brought her tea, and said nothing, because some decisions you can't talk a person into. The second time was the night before the Accord's final draft. Orsen hadn't even boarded. She'd stood at the dock gate with her bag over her shoulder, looking down the long gray throat of the corridor toward open space, and then Tam had come running with a drawing—crayon on scrap, a lopsided ship surrounded by eleven stick figures—and Orsen had folded it and put it in her breast pocket, over her heart, and turned around.

"You're staring," said a voice at her elbow. Sun, holding two cups of the terrible sweet coffee Halcyon imported at ruinous cost. He handed her one. "I used to think she was the most dangerous person at these tables. Back when she was Villalobos."

"And now?"

"Now I think she might be the most honest one." He sipped, grimaced at the coffee out of habit. "My fleet spent thirty years believing peace was a performance you maintained. She proved it could be a confession instead. That's going to take us a generation to learn."

Below the atrium, through the great viewport, Halcyon's docks spread out in their cluttered, working glory—Meridian haulers berthed beside Ashfall freighters, cargo cranes crossing fleet lines without ceremony, dockhands of both colors arguing amiably about load weights. Six months ago, that view had been a standoff. Now it was just traffic.

Naya's eyes found the smallest shape in the scene: Orsen's ship, still berthed where it had been since the confession. She'd repainted the hull. Where the old registry markings had been scraped away, the children had stenciled a new name in letters visible from orbit. *Blue Dreamer.* Tam's idea. Of course it was.

"She's staying," Naya said.

Sun followed her gaze. "You sound surprised."

"I'm not surprised. I'm—" She searched for the word and found it waiting, patient, the way true things are. "I'm glad. She spent her whole existence being someone else's continuity. I think she finally understands she gets to be her own."

### Collective

Later—after the ceremony, after the speeches mercifully kept short, after the children dragged every available adult to the observation deck to watch the archive core's public uplink complete—Halcyon Colony dimmed its lights for evening cycle, and the station breathed.

In the atrium, the long scarred table stood empty, the ink drying on eleven small constellations and one steady signature. In the prison hulk's sparse visiting room, a man named Vess stared at a wall and began, for the ten-thousandth time, composing an appeal that would never be sent, because even he had finally run out of arguments with himself. In the school module, Naya stacked chairs and found a crayon drawing tucked behind the supply cabinet: the white room, redrawn. But in this version the countdown had reached zero, and past the bloodied glass there was a door standing open, and light coming through it, and small figures walking out.

And on the observation deck, Orsen Okonkwo stood at the rail with a child asleep against her side—Pem, who still sometimes dreamed fragments that weren't hers, and who had learned to come find her afterward—and watched the traffic of two fleets move through shared orbit like threads learning to be one cloth.

"Are you sad?" Pem mumbled, half-dreaming. "That you're not her anymore?"

Orsen considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. Below, a Meridian tug nudged an Ashfall barge into alignment, gentle as a hand on a shoulder.

"I was never her," she said. "I was just waiting to be me. It took longer than I wanted."

Pem nodded, satisfied, and drifted off again. Above them, the archive beacon pulsed once per minute, broadcasting the dream to anyone who asked—open, unhidden, free. Somewhere out there, other colonies were beginning to receive it. Other children, perhaps, were beginning to dream.

Orsen stayed at the rail long after the lights dimmed, visible at last, home by choice, watching the orbit carry them all forward together.
