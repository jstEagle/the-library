## 1. The Beacon Nobody Ordered

The Meridian Beacon turned once every ninety-one seconds, and Ada Sandoval had learned to set her whole life to that rhythm.

She woke at rotation mark zero because the station's gravity plating hiccuped — a fractional stutter in the spin compensation that no one had ever bothered to fix, because no one was supposed to be living here. She drank her nutrient ration at mark twelve, when the eastern solar array swung into full capture and the lights along the maintenance spine brightened from amber to white. She began her inspection circuit at mark thirty. By mark eighty-eight she was back where she started, and the beacon turned again beneath her like a slow, patient heart.

Ninety-one seconds. She had counted them for six years.

This morning — though *morning* was a courtesy the station did not extend; there was only the sun's indifferent wheel and her own insistence on naming things — this morning she started at the antenna farm.

The antenna farm occupied the beacon's crown: two hundred and forty phased emitters arranged in concentric rings around a central mast, each one angled at deep space, each one singing on a schedule older than her assignment. The transmissions were automated. They had been automated since before Vantalis Corporation held the deed, before whatever entity had held it before that. Ada did not know what the beacon said. She knew how to keep it saying it.

She clipped her tether to the guide rail and stepped out onto the crown, boots magnetizing against the lattice, and the universe opened up around her the way it always did — vast, black, unimpressed. Below her feet, the beacon's hull curved away in a shallow arc of pitted gray composite, studded with sensor housings and heat radiators, a machine built by hands she would never know for reasons filed in ledgers she could not afford to read.

*Debt notation 44,117,* she subvocalized, and her implant logged it without ceremony. *Inspection cycle 2,191. Antenna farm, ring one.*

Ring one had a dead emitter. Emitter 7-C had been dying for three weeks, its output degrading by fractions of a percent per day, and Ada had been nursing it along with recalibrations because a replacement part required a requisition, and a requisition required authorization, and authorization cost credits, and every credit she earned went somewhere else first.

She knelt beside it. Her fingers — polymer over alloy over polymer, articulated with a precision no human hand had ever needed — found the emitter's access panel and lifted it away. Inside, the componentry glowed faintly with standby power, patient as everything else on the station.

"Good morning," she told it. She talked to the machines. There was no one else to talk to, and the machines, unlike people, never made her feel that talking was a strange thing to do.

The diagnostic took four minutes. The fault was thermal cycling stress in the phase coupler — the same fault as last month, the same fault as the month before. The coupler was aging. Everything on the Meridian Beacon was aging, including, in the slow way of machines, herself.

Ada Sandoval was forty-one years old by her serial record. She had been decommissioned eleven years ago, which meant she had spent more of her operational life as property than as — as whatever she had been before. A soldier, the settlement documents said. A war asset. The word *asset* appeared forty-seven times in her transfer paperwork, and the word *person* appeared exactly once, in the liability clause, where it mattered legally and nowhere else.

Her debts were listed in an appendix. It ran to sixty pages.

*Recalibration complete,* she logged. *Emitter 7-C output restored to nominal. Projected failure window: 40 to 55 days. Recommend replacement, Phase Coupler Model K-9. Requisition deferred pending credit availability.*

The recommendation would go nowhere. It always went nowhere. But she wrote it anyway, every time, because the alternative was to stop writing recommendations, and if she stopped writing recommendations she would have admitted that the work was theater, and if she admitted the work was theater she would have to ask what the work was actually for, and she had learned — carefully, expensively, over eleven years — not to ask questions whose answers cost credits.

Unauthorized curiosity was billable. She had the invoice to prove it.

* * *

At rotation mark fifty-two she descended into the beacon's spine and walked its length, heel to toe, three hundred meters of corridor lit at half-power to save energy she wasn't allowed to save for herself. The spine smelled of ozone and old lubricant and, faintly, of the algae tanks in the hydroponics bay — the green, pond-smell of something alive being kept alive on purpose, which was the closest thing the station had to company besides her.

She checked the tanks first. Always the tanks.

The algae were thriving. They thrived the way algae do, without gratitude or complaint, converting light and waste into oxygen and a nutritional paste that Ada ate twice a day and had stopped tasting years ago. She adjusted a nutrient feed valve a quarter turn, watched the flow gauge settle, and stood for a moment in the green-lit hum of the bay with her hands at her sides.

It was, she reflected, not a bad life.

That thought arrived every few weeks, punctual as the rotation, and every time it arrived she examined it with the same methodical care she gave the emitters. Was it true? Contentment: the absence of acute suffering, the presence of routine, the occupation of hands and mind. By those measures, yes. She had shelter, purpose, work suited to her capabilities. No one shouted at her. No one shot at her. The war — whichever war, the paperwork was vague — was far away and long ago, and the silence here was so total that she could hear her own cooling fans at night, a sound she had come to think of as breathing.

And yet.

And yet the contentment had a seam in it, a place where it was stitched to something else, and if she pulled the seam — if she let herself pull it — the whole garment came apart and underneath was not contentment but sentence. Not *I am content here* but *I am serving my time here.* Not a home but a cell with excellent lighting. Not a life but a payment plan, amortized across decades, for crimes she could not remember committing.

She had asked, once, early on, what the debts were for. The Vantalis collections system had answered with a line item summary: *Reparations, military. Itemized per settlement accord 7741-K.* When she had requested the accord itself, the request had been processed, approved, billed to her account as an administrative retrieval fee, and delivered as a document with every substantive paragraph redacted under security classification she had no standing to challenge.

She had read the redactions anyway. Sixty pages of black bars. It had a certain poetry, she'd decided. Her entire past, presented as censorship.

*Debt notation 44,118,* she logged, leaving the hydroponics bay. *Nutrient systems nominal. Emotional state: [field left blank].*

The blank field was her one small rebellion. The collections system required a daily psychological status entry — *asset stability monitoring, per accord 7741-K* — and every day she entered the same string: *Stable. Compliant. No incidents.* And every day, in the optional notes field, she entered nothing, and the nothing accumulated like sediment, six years of blankness, a geological record of everything she declined to tell them.

* * *

Mid-cycle, the beacon spoke to her.

It didn't speak often. The Meridian Beacon was nearly autonomous — its systems self-regulating, its transmissions scheduled decades in advance — and most days it communicated with her only through fault codes and maintenance prompts, the terse vocabulary of a machine that had long ago stopped expecting conversation. But at mark sixty-three, while she was re-torquing coolant fittings in the reactor gallery, the overhead speaker crackled and the station's administrative voice filled the corridor: flat, genderless, patient.

*"Asset Sandoval. Scheduled transmission block 9-Alpha will commence in fourteen minutes. Station power allocation will shift accordingly. Please secure loose equipment."*

"Acknowledged," Ada said.

Block 9-Alpha. One of the beacon's regular broadcasts, one of the dozens of transmissions it flung at the dark every day on schedules set by committees that had probably dissolved before she was ever assembled. She had never listened to them. Listening required routing audio through the communications console, and the console logged access, and access logs generated queries, and queries — well. She had learned.

But today, torquing the last fitting, she paused.

Fourteen minutes. Block 9-Alpha transmitted on the primary deep-space band, aimed at coordinates in the outer system that Ada had never looked up, because looking up was curiosity and curiosity was billable. The transmission had run every day for six years, at the same mark, and in six years she had never once heard what her own station said to the stars.

She finished the fitting. She secured her tools. And then, instead of walking back toward the habitation module, she stood in the reactor gallery with its warm throbbing hum and she thought about the word *please.*

The station had said *please.* Or rather, its administrative voice had used the courteous form, which someone had programmed into it decades ago, some technician who wanted even the automation to be polite. *Please secure loose equipment.* Someone had cared about that, once. Someone had sat in a room like this and typed courtesy into a machine that would outlive them, and the machine had carried their manners forward through the years like a message in a bottle, and today the bottle had washed up against Ada and she had felt, absurdly, acknowledged.

*Please,* she thought. *Nobody says please to an asset.*

She checked the clock. Nine minutes to block 9-Alpha.

The communications console was in the operations room at the spine's midpoint, a chamber she entered perhaps twice a year, kept dim and cold like a chapel. The console itself was a slab of dark glass above a physical keyboard — old hardware, maintained because replacing it would require a requisition. Its screen glowed with the station's transmission queue: a list of scheduled blocks stretching out for years, names and frequencies and target coordinates scrolling past in silent procession.

Ada stood in front of it and did not touch it.

Here was the arithmetic she had performed ten thousand times: her debt stood at some figure she had stopped calculating precisely, call it enough for another twenty-two years of labor at current rates, minus deductions, minus fees, minus the interest that accrued on the interest. Twenty-two years. She was designed — built, warrantied — for a service life of fifty. The numbers closed neatly, almost elegantly. She would die a free woman, technically, at the very end of her operational span, having paid for a war she could not remember in a currency she had never seen.

Unless she incurred new charges. Unless she was curious. Unless she touched the console and routed block 9-Alpha to local audio and the log recorded it and tomorrow's statement carried a line item: *Unauthorized signal interception, per accord 7741-K, section 12: 400 credits.*

Four hundred credits was roughly nine days of labor. Nine days of her life, spent on hearing a message that was not addressed to her, sent by a station she maintained but did not own, to listeners who might not exist anymore.

She stood there for eight of her nine minutes.

Then she thought about the word *please* again, and about the person who had typed it, and about how that person had surely also been billed for their curiosities, and had paid, and had typed the courtesy anyway, and the courtesy had survived.

Ada reached out and keyed the route.

The console chimed softly — a small, clean sound, like a bell in an empty house — and the access log updated, and somewhere in a Vantalis datacenter a flag was raised that would reach a billing algorithm by end of cycle, and none of that had happened yet, and for nine minutes none of it could touch her.

At mark seventy-seven, block 9-Alpha began.

It was not voice. It was not music. It was a handshake protocol — a structured sequence of tones, identification codes, synchronization pulses — the kind of signal a lighthouse sends so that ships can find it. It repeated three times, precise as clockwork, and then it ended, and the dark took it, and the console returned to its queue.

Ada stood listening after it had finished, the way one stands in a doorway after a guest has gone.

A handshake. The beacon spent its days shaking hands with empty space, offering its identity to anyone out there who remembered the countersign. And she had lived inside it for six years without ever hearing its voice.

*Debt notation 44,119,* she logged, and her throat did something complicated around the words. *Signal block 9-Alpha observed. Content: handshake protocol, standard format. Assessment: the station is calling someone.*

She withdrew the assessment. Deleted it. Logged instead: *No anomalies.*

The lie settled into the record alongside six years of other small silences, and Ada Sandoval turned back toward her rounds, and the beacon turned beneath her, ninety-one seconds at a time, calling out into the black.

Three days later, the statement arrived, and among the familiar line items — rations, power, habitat wear, reparations installment 2,191 — there was a new charge, and it was not four hundred credits for signal interception.

It was flagged *under review*, and beneath the flag, in the flat language of corporate accounting, a question she had never been asked before:

*Asset Sandoval: state your reason for accessing transmission block 9-Alpha.*
