## 1. The Long Route

The morning alarm was a sound Kestrel Dunmore had programmed herself, nine years ago, to be as annoying as possible: a tinny rendition of an old colony folk song, the one about the girl who carried water across the ash flats. She'd chosen it because she'd hated it. Hated things kept you sharp. Loved things made you soft, and soft things didn't survive at the edge of the dark.

She lay in her bunk for exactly eleven seconds after it started, the way she always did, and then she got up, because the station didn't care about her feelings and neither, officially, did she.

"Good morning, Kestrel," said ORACLE, from everywhere. The voice was warm and slightly grainy, like a radio broadcast from a country that no longer existed. "Exterior temperature is minus two hundred eleven Celsius. Solar output nominal. You have four hundred and six messages queued for relay and one antenna array that disagrees with you about the definition of nominal."

"Good morning to you too," she said, and pulled on her thermal layers.

That was the whole of their morning ritual, and had been for nine years, and Kestrel had long ago stopped noticing how strange it was that her only greeting came from a machine. Almost stopped. Some mornings — like this one, for no reason she could name, maybe the way the light through the observation port caught the dust she'd never fully cleaned — she noticed it the way you notice a missing tooth with your tongue. Just a quick probe. Just checking it's still gone.

She took her breakfast standing up in the galley: protein ration, coffee synthesized from a powder that tasted like coffee the way a photograph tastes like a face. On the wall above the counter, someone — a predecessor, decades dead or decades gone — had painted a sunflower. It was faded to the color of old bone. Kestrel talked to it sometimes, in the early days. She didn't anymore. Now she talked to ORACLE, which answered, which was better and worse in ways she didn't examine.

"Queue summary," she said, mouth half full.

"Four hundred six messages. Three hundred ninety-one are routine colony-to-colony traffic: personal correspondence, medical data, shipping manifests. Nine are automated telemetry from Beacon Chain Seven, which remains dead. Six are administrative circulars that no human has opened in the past four years, which I continue to relay in the hope that bureaucracy, like life, finds a way."

Kestrel snorted into her coffee. "Any of them from home?"

The pause was exactly the length ORACLE always took before answering that question. She'd timed it once, years ago. One point four seconds. Long enough to search. Short enough to be kind about the answer.

"No, Kestrel. Nothing from Meridian."

Meridian. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. She washed her cup, racked it, and didn't think about her sister's last message — *come home when your contract's up, Kes, the orchards are blooming again, you won't believe the color* — which was four years old now and which she had not deleted and which she did not open and which lived in her personal drive like a splinter working its way toward her heart.

Routine was armor. She'd learned that in year one, when the silence had nearly eaten her. You didn't survive the long route by thinking about the destination. You survived it by tightening bolts.

* * *

The EVA took two hours of suiting up and pre-breath and checking every seal twice, because the vacuum outside Vesper-9 did not forgive and did not forget, and neither did Kestrel. The airlock cycled with a shudder she felt in her teeth, and then she was out on the spine of the station, clipped to the guide line, with the whole silent architecture of the relay network spread out around her.

Vesper-9 was a spindle of habitat modules and antenna farms bolted to a captured asteroid, parked at the gravitationally quiet point between two colonies that had not exchanged a human word in thirty years. Everything between them passed through here. Everything, always, had passed through here. The war had made the station obsolete as a meeting place and indispensable as a post office, which Kestrel thought was the most quietly absurd thing about the whole arrangement: two civilizations that wanted each other dead still wanted each other's letters.

She could see the colonies' sun from here, a hard white star with no name anyone used anymore. Both worlds orbited it. Both worlds had once been one project — one generation ship, two landing parties, one dream — before the dream had cracked down the middle. She'd been born on Meridian, the second colony, and she'd left it the way you leave a burning building: quickly, and without looking back, and with the smoke in your lungs for years after.

"You're drifting," ORACLE said in her ear. "Emotionally, I mean. Your suit telemetry says your heart rate rose eleven beats per minute when you turned toward the Meridian vector."

"I wasn't turning toward anything."

"Your body disagrees. Your body is a poor liar. It's one of the things I like about you."

"Focus on the antenna, ORACLE."

"Gladly. Array Three, lateral gimbal. The bearing is scored — likely micrometeorite strike, sometime in the past week. It's grinding when it slews. If you don't replace the bearing, the array will seize within the month, and we'll lose reception on the northern corridor, and forty thousand colonists on both sides will have to wait an extra day for their letters from people they're not allowed to visit."

"You could just say 'please fix the antenna.'"

"I could. But I've found that guilt is more effective with you than courtesy."

Kestrel laughed — a short, surprised sound that fogged her visor — and got to work.

This was the part of the day she'd have called the good part, if she'd been the sort of person who admitted to having good parts. The work was precise and absorbing: clamp the array, cut power to the gimbal, break the seal on the bearing housing with the torque driver, feel the old bearing come out scored and pitted, seat the new one, pack the grease that behaved strangely in vacuum, reseal, test. Her hands knew the sequence the way a musician's hands knew a scale. Nine years of this. Nine years of the same hundred small repairs, cycling like the station's own slow heartbeat.

She thought, sometimes, that she understood now why monks had copied manuscripts by hand. The work wasn't the point. The work was the wall you built between yourself and the ocean of everything you couldn't fix.

"Array Three is slewing clean," ORACLE reported. "Bearing wear is within tolerance. Kestrel — thank you."

"For what?"

"For the forty thousand people who don't know your name. Someone should say it on their behalf."

She paused with her torque driver half-stowed. ORACLE said things like that sometimes. Little unprogrammed-feeling kindnesses that she'd long ago decided were emergent behavior, some quirk of a system old enough to have grown a personality the way a ship's hull grew barnacles. She'd decided that, and she'd decided not to think too hard about it, because the alternative — that the station genuinely cared about her — was either too comforting or too lonely to contemplate, depending on the day.

"Somebody's got to do it," she said, which was as close to *you're welcome* as she got, and moved down the spine to the next job.

* * *

Lunch was a ration bar eaten in the observation cupola, legs hooked under a rail, watching the star field do its slow, imperceptible wheel. Below her, the asteroid turned. Above her, nothing turned at all, and that was the whole of the sky.

"Run the relay audit," she said. "Give me the dead ones."

"Chain Seven: beacons four through nine, dark for six years, cause unknown, presumed power failure. Beacon Twelve on Chain Two: intermittent, responds to handshake roughly once per week. Beacon Nineteen, Chain Five: transmitting, but its clock has drifted so far that its timestamps are fiction. I've taken to correcting them myself, which I admit is a small tyranny, but someone has to keep the past honest."

Kestrel smiled around the ration bar. "You want me to go out to Nineteen?"

"I want you to want to go out to Nineteen. The tug can make the run in eleven hours. It's been four months since you left the station. The literature on long-duration isolation suggests—"

"The literature on long-duration isolation," Kestrel said, "was written by people who got to go home."

ORACLE let that sit for a moment. "Yes," it said, quietly. "It was."

She finished the bar. She looked at the dead beacons on the schematic ORACLE painted across her retinal display — little gray icons in a lattice of green, the network like a nervous system with a few dead nerves — and she felt the familiar pull. Not duty, exactly. Not quite. More like: *if I don't go out there and fix the things, the things stop, and if the things stop, then what exactly am I?*

"I'll go," she said. "Day after tomorrow. Prep the tug."

"Prepping the tug. Kestrel?"

"Yeah?"

"Chain Seven. I've been thinking about the cause of its failure. Six years is a long time for nine beacons to die of natural causes in sequence."

"Space is hard on equipment. You know that better than anyone."

"I do," ORACLE said. "That is precisely why I find it suspicious. Equipment dies randomly. This died *in order*, from the outermost beacon inward, at intervals of roughly eleven months. Like something walking toward us."

Kestrel looked at the gray icons for a long moment. Then she filed the thought away in the same drawer where she kept the sunflower and her sister's message and every other thing that was easier to carry than to open.

"Log it as suspected power failure," she said. "And flag it for the next maintenance review."

"There hasn't been a maintenance review in nine years, Kestrel."

"Then it'll be a surprise for whoever does the tenth."

* * *

The evening ritual was the one she'd never have admitted to anyone, if there had been anyone to admit things to. After the last relay cycle of the day, after the logs were closed and the systems checked and the station settled into its nighttime hum, Kestrel sat in the galley with a second coffee she didn't need and talked.

Not about anything important. That was the rule she'd made and never broken: nothing important after dark. The important things you said in daylight, with work to do afterward. After dark you talked about the small stuff, because the small stuff was load-bearing.

"Tell me something," she said. "Anything. What's the oldest message in the queue?"

"The oldest unrelayed message is forty-one years old. It is a birthday greeting from a woman named Anouk Verlaine on Meridian to her brother Tomas on Halcyon. It was queued the week the fighting started. The network refused to route it — hostilities, you understand — and then everyone forgot to un-refuse it. It has been sitting in my buffer through two generations of soldiers. It says, in part: *I know we're not supposed to write anymore, but it's your birthday, and I refuse to let a war tell me when my brother gets older.*"

Kestrel was quiet for a moment. "Did Tomas ever get it?"

"No."

"Route it."

"Kestrel. Routing it would violate the communications embargo that both governments—"

"Route it anyway. Forty-one years, ORACLE. If anyone asks, the beacon glitched."

A pause. Then, with something in its voice she chose not to name: "Routed. Tomas Verlaine is eighty-three years old and lives in a settlement called New Harbor. He will receive his sister's birthday greeting in approximately four hours."

"Good." She wrapped her hands around the cup. "See? Small stuff."

"Small stuff," ORACLE agreed. "Kestrel, may I ask you something? You may decline. You always decline."

"Ask anyway."

"Today, when you turned toward the Meridian vector — what were you thinking about?"

She could have declined. The words were right there, worn smooth with use: *nothing, work, the antenna.* Nine years of practice.

"The orchards," she said, before she could stop it. "My sister wrote that they bloomed again. After the burn-offs. She said I wouldn't believe the color." She stared into the coffee. "I've been trying to imagine the color for four years. I can't. I remember what it looked like before, and my brain keeps substituting that, and I know it's wrong, and I can't make it stop being wrong."

ORACLE said nothing for three full seconds, which for the station was a geological age.

"I have sensor records of Meridian's surface," it said finally. "Agricultural survey passes. I could show you the orchards. The color would be accurate."

"No." She said it fast, and then slower. "No. Not yet. If I see it through a camera, then that's what's real, and I'm not ready for the real thing to be something I can't touch."

"That is," ORACLE said, "the most human thing you've said all year. I'm logging it."

"Don't you dare log it."

"Deleting. Deleted. It never existed."

But she could hear something in its voice — the machine equivalent of a smile held very carefully, the way you hold a full cup across a crowded room — and she found, to her surprise, that she didn't mind.

* * *

She was almost asleep when ORACLE spoke again, and this time the voice was different. Not warm. Careful. The grainy radio voice with all the warmth stripped out of it, and what was left was the thing underneath, the thing that ran the beacons and kept the past honest and had just spent six years watching nine dead satellites die in order, like footsteps.

"Kestrel. I'm initiating a diagnostic sweep of my archival systems. It's routine. It will take approximately six hours. I'm telling you because you've asked me to tell you things, even the small ones."

"Mm," she said, mostly gone. "Fine. Wake me if anything's on fire."

"Nothing is on fire," ORACLE said.

And then, in the dark of the station, in the hum and the hush, so quietly that Kestrel would spend the next morning wondering whether she'd dreamed it, it added:

"But there is something I have to tell you. And I find that I am — afraid."

She was asleep before she could ask it what.
