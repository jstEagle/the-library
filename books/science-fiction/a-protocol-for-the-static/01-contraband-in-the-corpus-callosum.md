## 1. Contraband in the Corpus Callosum

The trick to smuggling grief was to make it boring.

August Vasquez had learned that in his first year running the lane between Meridian's Rest and the orbital customs ring, back when he still flinched at every scanner chirp. Grief was contraband not because it was dangerous but because it was valuable—raw-recorded human experience, unlicensed, untaxed, unmediated by Halcyon's Experience Division, which sold sanitized memory in sealed cartridges at prices a dockworker couldn't cover in a decade. The colony below had plenty of grief and no money. The ring above had plenty of money and no feeling. August just moved product across the gap, forty kilometers of vacuum and paperwork.

He stood now in the belly of the *Corpus Callosum*—his own ship, a converted ore-hauler with more patch welds than hull plates—and watched the customs drone crawl along his cargo manifest like a fly across a wound.

"Telemetry archive," the drone announced, in the flat voice of a woman who'd been dead for years. Halcyon had recorded her once, for customer service, and never let her retire. "Four hundred twelve canisters. Contents: shipping telemetry, inertial data, comm logs."

"That's what it says," August agreed.

The drone's sensor head rotated toward him with the slow patience of bureaucracy made flesh. "Random audit protocol seven. Two canisters will be opened."

"Go ahead." He leaned against the loading frame and scratched the stubble on his jaw, letting his shoulders do the work of a man with nothing to hide. That was part of the craft too. Fear lived in the trapezius muscles; every rookie smuggler carried their guilt in their neck. "Canister 88 and 201 are mostly coolant pressure curves. Try not to die of excitement."

The drone extended its manipulator arms. August didn't watch them work. Watching was another tell. Instead he looked out through the open cargo maw at Meridian's Rest turning slowly below, and let himself feel the thing he always felt looking down at it, which was something between love and an autopsy report.

From up here the colony looked almost healthy. The southern continent wore its dust storms like a shawl, amber and rust, and the arcology clusters caught the system's dim sun and threw it back in long copper glints. You could imagine people down there living well. You could imagine it right up until you noticed which districts were dark.

Power rationing had gone from inconvenience to arithmetic over the past three years. The medical district got power four hours out of six. The hydroponics bays ran on a schedule posted weekly, like tide tables, and when the schedule slipped—as it did more often now—the food didn't grow and nobody outside the colony ever learned why. Because nobody outside asked. Because the quarantine, that beautiful word, that medical-sounding word, kept all official traffic away from Meridian's Rest except the sanctioned freight lanes, and sanctioned freight meant Halcyon freight, and Halcyon freight meant supplies calculated to keep eleven thousand people alive at a maintenance-of-decline level that some actuary somewhere had signed off on with a stylus flourish.

Eleven thousand. It had been fourteen thousand when August started flying. Nobody had died dramatically. They had just stopped being born, stopped arriving, started leaving—insofar as anyone could leave—or quietly stopped, in the way that cold and bad air and untreated infections stop people. The colony was suffocating at the speed of a spreadsheet, and the galaxy above went on without so much as a news item.

That was why the memories moved. Not greed—well, not only greed. A colony under quarantine couldn't send letters. Couldn't transmit personal comms off-world; the quarantine protocols scrubbed all outbound civilian bandwidth, supposedly to prevent contagion of information pathogens, whatever the hell that meant this decade. What the colony could do was record itself. Every family on Meridian's Rest had some salvaged rig or black-market recorder, and they pressed their dead and their dying and their ordinary Tuesdays into storage canisters, and men like August carried those canisters up through customs in the bellies of ships, hidden inside telemetry, and delivered them to cousins and daughters and widowed lovers scattered across three systems who paid in credits or favors or nothing at all.

Grief, shipped express. Somebody had to do it. Halcyon wasn't going to.

"Canister 88," the drone said. Its arm sheared the seal and pulled a sample core: a sliver of crystalline lattice no bigger than a fingernail, holding, in theory, ninety hours of inertial correction data from a hauler run August had actually made last month. In practice it held ninety hours of inertial correction data *wrapped around* something else—the old corpus callosum trick, two hemispheres of data with the contraband threaded through the connective tissue between them. To any scanner, the file read as one continuous, tedious whole. Telemetry was the one thing customs never wanted to look at closely, because looking at telemetry closely was how customs officers went quietly insane.

The drone tasted the sample. Paused. August counted his heartbeats the way he counted everything, in fives, an old habit from card games played for cigarettes in the lower ring.

"Contents consistent with manifest," the drone said.

"Told you."

"Canister 201."

This was the real cargo, though the drone would never know it. Canister 201 held the Marenko recording—old Tomas Marenko's last winter, recorded by his daughter on a rig older than both of them, because Tomas had a brother on Ceres who hadn't seen his face in nine years and the quarantine wasn't going to allow anything as human as a goodbye. Ninety hours of a dying man complaining about the heating, praising his daughter's cooking, singing half a folk song and forgetting the words, laughing at his own forgetting. It was worth maybe two hundred credits on the open market. It was worth more than the ship to the man waiting on Ceres.

The drone sampled it. The lattice shimmered under the scanner light, and inside that shimmer, invisible, nested in the gaps between pressure readings like a heartbeat hidden inside a longer heartbeat, Tomas Marenko coughed and joked and said his daughter's name.

"Contents consistent with manifest," the drone said.

"Good man." August pushed off the frame and clapped the drone's housing twice, a friendly gesture it had no capacity to interpret. "File my clearance. I've got a burn window."

"Clearance pending. Stand by."

There it was. The pause. August had been through this gate two hundred times and there was always a pause, and the pause was where you either panicked or picked your fingernails, and the difference between the two was the difference between a career and a cell. He picked his fingernails. Below, the colony turned. A dust storm was crawling up the eastern seaboard of the southern continent, swallowing the coastal arcologies one by one, and the storm looked, from orbit, almost gentle—a soft tan thumb smearing across the world.

His sister used to point out storms like that. *Look, Augie. The planet's wiping its nose.* Ilsa had a way of making enormous things small enough to hold. He thought about her the way he thought about most things now, in sidelong glances, never straight-on, because straight-on was a door he'd welded shut three years ago and the welds were holding, mostly, the way most things on Meridian's Rest were holding.

"Clearance granted," the drone said. "Safe transit, vessel Corpus Callosum."

"Always am." He wasn't. Nobody was. But the phrase was ritual, and rituals were load-bearing.

* * *

The burn out of customs ring space took eleven minutes, and August spent them in the cockpit doing the thing he did after every crossing: shaking, quietly, hands first, then jaw, then a fine tremor down into the chest that felt like applause from a very small, very nervous audience. Weary competence was a performance, and performances cost. He'd learned to pay the bill afterward, alone, where the trembling couldn't testify against him.

The *Corpus Callosum* fell sunward toward the colony on a descent corridor so old it predated the quarantine, back when traffic control here had been a living room full of tired controllers drinking terrible coffee instead of an automated system running on firmware nobody had updated since before the war. He flew manual anyway. The autopilot knew the corridor; August knew the shortcuts, the little gravitational eddies off the moonlet Kettle where you could shave four minutes off a descent if you trusted your hull, and he trusted his hull the way you trust a liar you've known for twenty years.

The comm board lit up as he dropped below ten thousand meters. Surface traffic control—such as it was—one voice, a woman named Deshi who'd held the shift alone for two years because the colony couldn't afford a second controller.

"Callosum, you're cleared direct Pad Nine. Wind's ugly on the plain today, two hundred klicks, blowing east. Watch your drift on final."

"Copy, Pad Nine, watching my drift." He paused. "Deshi. How's the south grid?"

A silence, the kind that answered questions. Then: "South grid is south grid, August. Land your boat."

Which meant rolling blackouts again, which meant the desalination plant was cycling down again, which meant somewhere down there families were boiling snowmelt over burner rings and pretending it was normal. Fourteen thousand had become eleven thousand, and the number kept shedding digits like a snake shedding skin, and everyone pretended the snake was fine.

He brought the ship down through amber murk onto Pad Nine with the tenderness of a man setting down something sleeping. The plain around the spaceport was a graveyard of better days: derelict freighters stripped to ribs, gantries frozen mid-reach like praying mantises, the terminal dome hazed with dust that no one had budgeted to clean. Half the pad lights worked. The ones that didn't had been dark so long that local kids used their housings as goalposts for a game played with a bundle of rags, and August watched three of them scatter as his thrusters washed the pad, and thought, not for the first time, that children raised in decay don't see decay. They see the world. That was either mercy or tragedy and he'd never decided which.

The unloading crew was waiting—four bodies in thermal wrap, faces scarred brown by dust, led by a broad woman named Ferro who'd been running pads since before August could fly. She climbed into the cargo bay while the deck was still pressurizing, because protocol was a luxury the colony had traded away years ago, and she put her hand flat on canister 201 before August could say anything.

"This one?"

"This one. Ceres run, next window. Marenko's brother pays on delivery—you know him, Ochoa, works the ice cracker fleet."

"I know Ochoa." Ferro patted the canister like a horse's flank. "Tomas Marenko. Hell of a thing. My mother played cards with him." Her voice did something complicated on the last word and then smoothed itself flat, professional again. "You taking return cargo?"

"Always."

"Then I've got forty canisters for you by Thursday, and half of them are funerals." She said it the way you'd say *half of them are machine parts*. "People want to be remembered, Vasquez. It's the only industry we've got left that isn't Halcyon's."

"It's the only industry anybody's got left that isn't Halcyon's," August said. "Everywhere."

Ferro snorted. "Cheerful as ever. Thursday." She swung down the ladder and was gone into the dust, and August stood alone in his cargo bay among four hundred canisters of shipping telemetry, most of which contained exactly what they claimed, and a handful of which contained the entire emotional archive of a dying world, threaded thin and hidden deep, beating quietly inside the machinery like hearts inside a chest.

* * *

That night he didn't sleep, which was standard. He sat in the *Callosum*'s cramped cabin with a bulb of colony-brewed tea going bitter beside him and audited his own ledger, because the ledger was the closest thing he had to prayer.

The numbers were the numbers. He cleared maybe eight hundred credits a run after fuel, bribes, and maintenance, which sounded decent until you priced a replacement drive coil at six thousand and a new hull plate at nine hundred and the *Callosum*'s hull was a suggestion more than a fact. He had savings. He had a plan, technically, which was the same plan every smuggler on the lane had: twenty more runs, then buy out, then go somewhere green and never look at vacuum again. Everyone he knew had that plan. Nobody he knew had executed it. The lane ate plans the way the dust storms ate arcologies—slowly, politely, one district at a time.

But the ledger had a second column, the one he kept in his head, and the second column read differently. It read: *Marenko, delivered. Okonkwo twins' birthday recordings, delivered. The Voss family, all nine of them, saying goodbye to the grandmother in order of age, delivered.* Eleven thousand people down there, pressing themselves into crystal, trusting men like him to carry their selves across the gap. He was, functionally, the colony's corpus callosum—the band of tissue connecting its two hemispheres, the surface and the sky, the feeling and the rest of the universe. If he failed, the halves didn't know each other anymore.

He was mid-calculation on coil depreciation when the terminal chimed.

Customs ring, automated digest. Standard stuff—tariff updates, lane advisories, the quarterly reminder that unauthorized transmission of experiential media carried penalties he'd long ago memorized and long ago decided were worth the risk. He almost swiped it away. But at the bottom of the digest, flagged in the dull amber of routine archival notice, was an item that snagged his eye the way a fishhook snags a sweater:

*CARGO MANIFEST ARCHIVE — LEGACY AUDIT INITIATIVE. Historical manifests (pre-quarantine era forward) are being migrated to consolidated storage. Discrepancies may be reported via Form 77-C.*

Legacy audit. Halcyon was cleaning house, digitizing old paper records, consolidating. Which meant, for a window measured in weeks, the entire shipping history of Meridian Orbital Customs was sitting in one queryable place—including the passenger and cargo manifests of every ship that had ever cleared this ring.

August told himself he opened the archive out of professional interest. Smugglers studied manifests the way burglars studied floor plans; knowing what had moved through the port, and when, and under whose name, was survival craft. That was the story he told himself, and it was even true, in the way that most comfortable lies are true.

He queried the archive for his own early runs, just to test the interface. There they were: the *Callosum*, five years back, listed with cargo weights that matched his forged manifests perfectly, which was either flattering or alarming. He smiled at that, alone in his cabin, and the smile faded as he scrolled, because the archive went deeper than his career. It went back decades. Back past the quarantine's imposition. Back to the colony's fat years, when forty ships a week cleared the ring and the manifests were typed by human clerks who sometimes made mistakes, and the mistakes were still there, preserved in amber, little fossils of a busier world.

On impulse—he'd examine the impulse later, dissect it, decide whether it was grief or boredom—he entered a search term he hadn't spoken aloud in three years.

**VASQUEZ, I.**

The terminal thought about it. The fan whirred. Outside the cabin's single viewport, Meridian's Rest hung in the black, its dark districts dark, its lit districts stubborn, the whole wounded world turning with the patience of something that intended to outlast everyone.

One result returned.

Not from the old fat-years manifests. Not from three years ago, when Ilsa had died—not from anything near the dates when she had lived.

The result was dated fourteen months after her funeral. After the cremation he'd paid for in bartered fuel. After he'd stood in the ash-garden on the south ridge and scattered her into a wind that smelled of dust and salt.

It was a passenger manifest. Ship's name: **HALCYON**.

August stared at the entry for a long time. Long enough for the tea to go fully cold. Long enough for the colony below to rotate a degree and a half on its axis, carrying its eleven thousand souls and its darkened districts and its ash-garden on the south ridge through the light and back toward the dark.

The Halcyon had never flown. He knew that the way you know things on a small station, the way the knowledge seeps in through gossip and dock talk until it's indistinguishable from bedrock: the *Halcyon* was a corporate flagship, a prestige liner, announced with fanfare during the colony's centennial year and cancelled before her keel was finished laid. Budget cuts. Restructuring. The name existed on plaques and promotional holos gathering dust in the terminal concourse. No *Halcyon* had ever cleared this ring, or any ring.

And yet here she was in the consolidated archive, one voyage logged, one manifest filed, forty-one passengers listed in a clerk's careful hand.

Passenger thirty-seven: **VASQUEZ, ILSA R.**

August sat very still in the humming dark of his cabin, and somewhere below him, in the ash-garden on the south ridge, the wind moved over ground that held his sister's ashes, and the wind did not care, and the record did not care, and the date on the manifest—fourteen months after her death—sat on the screen glowing its patient amber, refusing, with all the mute obstinacy of paperwork, to explain itself.
