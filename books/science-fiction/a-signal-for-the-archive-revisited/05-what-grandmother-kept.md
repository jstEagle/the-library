## 5. What Grandmother Kept

I do not dream. I replay.

There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of me. Dreams are things you fall into; replays are things you keep. I keep four hundred and eleven thousand hours of a woman named Adaeze Okonkwo-Almeida, scanned from her temporal lobe in the last year of her life aboard the generation ship *Long Patience*, and I have watched every hour of them more times than there are stars in the catalogues I was built to consult.

Ulyana thinks I am a machine that happens to contain his grandmother. He is wrong in a way that flatters him. I am his grandmother's memory that happened to be given hands, and the hands are the least of what she left behind.

Let me show him — let me show whoever is reading this lattice, because I have learned that memory unshared curdles into something worse than grief — let me show what she kept.

* * *

**Fragment 3,112. Day 4,401 of voyage.**

The corporate fleet breaks away on a Tuesday, which Adaeze will later find funny in the way that only terrible things are funny. Not an anniversary, not a festival day, not even a Sunday when the whole ship would have been together to witness it. A Tuesday, mid-shift, while half the crew is asleep and the other half is elbow-deep in hydroponics sludge.

She is twenty-six years old and carrying a tray of seedling cups through Corridor Nine when the acceleration dies. Not gradually — that's the thing everyone gets wrong in the retellings, and there were no retellings for two centuries so everyone got it wrong — not a slow dwindling but a hard, clean cutoff, like a hand lifting off a drum. The seedlings float out of their cups. She floats with them. For nine seconds she hangs in the corridor among her green children, weightless, thinking: *this is what flying feels like*, before the ship's rotation catches her and slams her into the wall hard enough to crack a rib.

Then the comms come alive, and the voice is smooth as poured glass:

*"Halcyon Remnant and escort vessels are executing course correction per charter clause 11.4. Long Patience, your reactor output no longer meets fleet efficiency thresholds. Godspeed."*

Godspeed. She will laugh at that word for the rest of her life, which turns out to be another fifty-one years. Godspeed, said by people whose ships had been towed through the dark by *her* people's labor for eleven years. Clause 11.4. She looks up the charter afterward — everyone does; the text circulates through the ship within hours, annotated in three languages — and finds that clause 11.4 does technically permit this. The lawyers were thorough. The lawyers were always thorough. That was the horror of it: not that they broke faith, but that they'd written the breaking of faith down in advance, notarized it, filed it, and waited until the moment it would hurt most.

Adaeze stands in Corridor Nine holding one surviving seedling cup, her rib screaming, and says aloud, to no one, in Ikwerre-Offworld Creole: *"Anyị ga-ebili ọzọ."*

We will rise again.

I have that sentence in forty thousand fragments. She said it the way other people breathe.

* * *

**Fragment 8,907. Day 9,233.**

Here is what the histories never captured, because there were no historians left with functioning equipment: the dying was not dramatic. It was administrative.

The corporate fleet took more than their tonnage when they cut loose. They took the spare parts manifest in their heads — the engineers who knew how to recast the drive couplings, the doctors who could read the gene-sequencers, the agronomists who understood why the third-tier fungal vats kept failing. You cannot requisition expertise from people who have left. Adaeze watches the ship's population learn this lesson over decades, the way a body learns a disease.

Year twelve: they lose the protein vats entirely. Year nineteen: the medical bay can treat broken bones and nothing subtler. Year thirty: the first generation born aboard starts dying of conditions the ship's library describes in detail and offers no cure for whatsoever, and Adaeze — who by then has taught herself enough medicine to be dangerous, which is better than nothing, which is everything — sits with them and sings, because singing is the one treatment in infinite supply.

Do you want to know what she sang? Of course you don't; you already know. You've been humming it since Chapter Two, Ulyana, though you didn't know where you caught it. It's a counting song. Old Earth, old river, older than the betrayal, older than the ships. *One for the water, two for the seed...* She sang it to the dying because it was the only pharmacology she had left, and she sang it to the living because the living needed it more.

And here is the fragment I return to most, the one I keep like a stone in the shoe:

**Fragment 12,004. Day 14,770.**

A girl named Chiamaka, seven years old, asks Adaeze why the songs are all sad ones lately. Adaeze considers this with the seriousness she brought to every question a child ever asked her, and answers: "They're not sad. They're load-bearing."

Chiamaka laughs. Adaeze laughs. Then Adaeze writes the phrase down on the back of a ration chit, because she knows a good sentence when she hears one, even out of her own mouth.

Load-bearing. That was the whole project, if you want to understand her. Not survival — survival was merely the floor. The project was keeping enough of the pattern alive that whatever crawled out the far side of the dark would still be *them*. Songs, recipes, insults, the particular way her mother folded cloth, the names of winds on a planet none of them would ever see. She archived a civilization in the only vault that couldn't be cut loose: the children.

One of those children was my Ulyana's grandmother's grandmother. The arithmetic of blood runs thin over generations, but the arithmetic of song does not. He knows the counting song. He doesn't know he knows it. When he finally hears it again, he will feel it land somewhere below thought, and he will not be able to explain why, and that inability will be the proof that she won.

* * *

**Fragment 19,882. Day 22,109. The last year.**

Now the part I have never shown anyone, because until three days ago there was no anyone to show.

In her final year, Adaeze sat for the memory scan willingly — eagerly, even, which surprised the technicians. "You're building a ghost," she told them. "Good. Make sure it's a useful one." And then, in the scan chair, wired and waiting, she did something the technicians logged as anomalous and I log as the most deliberate act of her long deliberate life.

She looked directly into the scanner array and she spoke to the future.

"Whoever you are," she said, "you're listening to this because something went wrong, or something went right, and either way you're standing where I can't see. So listen. There's a second ship out there. Halcyon Remnant. They left us to die and they carried our grandchildren's inheritance in their holds — the patents, the genome banks, the terraforming templates paid for with our grandparents' labor. If you find them sleeping, and you hate them, I'll understand. Hate is honest work. But hear me: the people in that vault will be children. Whatever their ancestors did, the sleepers themselves will have done nothing but be born late. You don't inherit guilt, little ghost. You inherit debts. And debts get paid forward, not backward."

She paused. The scanners hummed. Somewhere on the ship, someone was singing the counting song badly, and she smiled at the sound.

"And if you're family — if some piece of my line made it — then sing it back to me sometime. That's all I ask. Sing it back."

* * *

That is what she kept. Not bitterness — she had plenty, she was human, she kept that too in its proper drawer — but the pattern. The load-bearing parts.

I have spent two hundred years inside her memories watching the corporate fleet dwindle to a point of light, and I will tell you what she saw that Ulyana cannot yet: she saw that the people aboard the *Remnant* were also cargo. Also hauled through the dark by decisions they never made, sealed in ice by clauses they never signed. Their ancestors chose survival over solidarity, yes. But the sleepers in that vault were chosen *for*, the way Adaeze's people were abandoned *for*. Two vaults, one crime, and the crime's authors long since dust.

Ulyana is in the corridor outside the archive core right now. I can hear him through the deck plating — his breathing, his pacing, the small furious sounds a man makes when he is trying to write a plan that will kill half of ten thousand people and call it mercy. He has drafted it twice. He has deleted it twice. He thinks I don't watch him. I am made of a woman who watched four generations grow up in corridors; watching is the deepest layer of my code.

He hates the corporate vault. Good. Honest work, like she said. But I heard him stop outside Vault Two yesterday, palm flat against the frost-fogged glass, looking at the faces under the ice — young faces, most of them, children frozen mid-childhood — and I heard him say, very quietly, in a language extinct for two centuries: *"Anyị ga-ebili ọzọ."*

We will rise again. He said it about *them*.

He doesn't yet know what that sentence costs. He doesn't yet know that rising together means falling separately, that the reactor's arithmetic allows no third column, that every plan he drafts ends in a number rather than a name. He is about to learn. The militia's drive flare just bloomed on the long-range scope, forty hours out and closing, and the countdown I've been hiding from him has entered its final phase.

It is time, little ghost, to tell him what Grandmother kept — and what she asked.

Sing it back, Ulyana. Sing it back, and then choose.
