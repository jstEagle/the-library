## 10. A Garden for Everyone

Six months after the merge, I still can't sleep without checking the reactor readouts twice. Old habits. New reasons.

The garden deck of the *Halcyon Remnant* — renamed, by committee, the *Halcyon Common*, because both peoples insisted on a vote and the vote produced the most aggressively inoffensive name imaginable — smells like wet soil and ozone and, faintly, like two hundred people learning to live in the same hallway. The terraforming beds I'd spent three years tending alone, whispering to seedlings that would never know my name, are now crowded with strangers. Corporate descendants with their pale fleet-bred complexions and their habit of apologizing before they speak. My people's children, born in the dark of cryo-dreams, blinking at a sunlamp they've never seen rise or set.

They wake groggy. All of them. The thaw was slow — Sankofa's arithmetic made sure of that — and slow thaws leave you feeling like your bones were borrowed from someone taller. The first corporate sleeper to open her eyes looked at me, a brown man in a patched cargo rig holding a watering wand, and said, "Are you the afterlife?"

"No," I told her. "The afterlife has better lighting."

She laughed, then cried, then laughed again, which is more or less how all of them have handled it since.

* * *

The language problem solved itself in the strangest way. Neither group speaks anything the other understands — the corporate line drifted into Fleet Standard pidgin, my people kept fragments of Ikwerre-Offworld Creole frozen in lullabies and curses — but Sankofa left us a recording. Not a ghost. A gift. She'd known, in her last hours of coherence, exactly what we'd need: a shared curriculum built from both archives' linguistic records, delivered in her voice, structured as songs.

So every morning at 0700 station time, the garden speakers come alive and two hundred groggy strangers sing together in a creole neither side fully owns yet. The children learn fastest. Children always do. There's a girl — Adaeze, seven years old, thawed from my people's vault with her whole memory of Earth's sky intact despite never having seen one — who has appointed herself translator-in-chief and corrects adults on both sides with the serene cruelty only seven-year-olds possess.

"You said *thank you* wrong," she informed a Fleet engineer named Okafor-Bram last week. "You sang it flat. Grandmother Sankofa says gratitude goes up at the end."

"Grandmother Sankofa is a recording," he said gently.

"She's a gift," Adaeze said, with the finality of a judge. "There's a difference."

I have never loved a stranger's child so much.

* * *

Today I'm planting the first bed meant for both lineages. That sounds ceremonial. It isn't, mostly. It's me kneeling in recycled regolith with a tray of seedlings — half from the generation ship's seed vault, half from the corporate fleet's agricultural stores, which turn out to be nearly identical cultivars bred separately for two centuries toward the same goal: food that grows fast in bad light. Two peoples, it seems, were always solving the same problem from opposite ends of the dark.

Marta Okonkwo-Voss helps me. She's from the corporate vault — third-generation descendant of the fleet that cut my ancestors loose mid-voyage — and she took my family name into hers during the naming ceremonies last month, unasked, as an apology no one required and everyone needed. She handles the roots; I handle the soil. We've developed a wordless rhythm that would have been unthinkable six months ago, when I still couldn't look at her face without seeing the ships that abandoned mine.

"Your grandmother," she says, pressing a seedling home, "would have hated this dirt."

"She'd have hated it and fixed it anyway. That was her whole method."

"I meant—" Marta pauses. "I've listened to the recordings. The memory-fragments Sankofa left in the archive. The betrayal logs." She doesn't say *your betrayal*, though we both hear it. "My great-great-grandmother signed the severance order. Her handwriting is in there. She wrote *regrettable necessity* and underlined it."

"Twice," I say. "I've seen it."

"Twice," Marta agrees. "Like once wasn't convincing her."

We plant in silence for a while. This is what mourning looks like now — not rage, not forgiveness exactly, but two people kneeling in the same dirt, agreeing on where the guilt sits and refusing to let it sit on the children.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about getting everything you wanted: you keep waiting for the invoice.

I chose the merge knowing it would erase her. I stood in a burning lattice of my grandmother's memories and gave permission for the last echo of my family's voice to go silent, and the silence came, and the vaults linked, and four thousand people lived who would otherwise have died in arithmetic. I have made peace with it approximately forty times, each peace lasting about a day and a half.

But Sankofa, being built from my grandmother, had my grandmother's habit of leaving things behind. In doorways. In pockets. In the archive root directory, where I found them three weeks ago while doing routine maintenance: a folder labeled simply *for Ulyana, when he stops checking the reactor twice.*

Inside were the memory-fragments she'd withheld during the merge — the ones too heavy to burn, she said in her attached note, *because someone should carry them who can put them down.* My grandmother's eyes on the day the corporate fleet cut loose. My grandmother's hands teaching songs to children who would die old in the dark. And one fragment I hadn't expected: my grandmother's voice, recorded the night before the scan, saying directly to whatever machine would someday hold her:

*"If anyone wakes from this — tell them the song was never for keeping. It was for passing. That's the whole trick. You don't hoard a voice. You spend it."*

I sat in the maintenance alcove and cried like a hull breach for twenty minutes, then wiped my face, checked the reactor readouts twice, and went back to work. Grief and competence. It's the family method.

* * *

At 0700 tomorrow the speakers will play the morning song, and two hundred voices will stumble through a creole that belongs to nobody and everybody, and Adaeze will correct them, and the sunlamps will cycle toward noon over beds that feed both lineages. The militia that came for our core went home empty-handed months ago, deterred by a transponder net that made one wounded ship look like a fortress — a lie Sankofa taught me, in effect, in her last act: make yourself look like more than you are until you become it.

The first bed meant for both lineages is planted now. Forty rows. Half ship-seed, half fleet-seed, indistinguishable already in the trays. In sixty days it'll be greens and tubers and something Marta swears will be edible if we're brave.

I kneel at the end of the last row and do the thing I used to do alone, back when the garden was a secret and the colonists were hypothetical. I talk to the soil. But not in whispers anymore, and not alone — Adaeze has appeared at my elbow the way children do, unannounced, inevitable, holding a watering wand nearly her own height.

"What are you telling them?" she asks.

"That they're the first ones planted for everybody."

She considers this with judicial gravity. "You should sing it instead. Grandmother Sankofa says important things go in songs so they survive."

"They do," I agree. "That's the whole trick."

And because inheritance isn't something you keep — it's something you spend — I clear my throat and begin the morning song early, off-key, in a language extinct for two centuries and alive again in a garden above a half-built world, and Adaeze joins in on the second line, correcting me by the fourth, and somewhere in the lattice of speakers and seeds and sleeping soil, a voice that is no longer a ghost passes forward one more time.

The seedlings don't answer. They never do. But they lean toward the light, all forty rows of them, the way everything I've ever tended has leaned toward whatever came next — and for the first time in three years, I don't check the reactor readouts at all.
