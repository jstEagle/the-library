## 5. The Archive Does Not Err

The summons came at the hour Meridian pretended was morning, when the station's false dawn bled amber through the viewport slats and the corridors smelled of recycled air and someone's over-steeped caf. Ansel had not slept. He had spent the night with the Halcyon crew's testimony spread across his workspace like an autopsy — eleven voices, eleven stress profiles, and beneath them all that faint, wrongness-shaped hum he couldn't yet name.

The message was simple. *Director Okafor requests the pleasure of your company. Level Nine. 0800 station.*

Level Nine. He hadn't been above Level Six since his certification, thirty years ago. The lift ascended through the station's strata like a diver rising through water that got warmer and more expensive the higher you went. Level Seven smelled of real soil — the hydroponic promenade, where the wealthy grew things that didn't have to justify their water. Level Eight was carpeted. Ansel's boots made no sound at all, and he found the silence obscene.

Okafor's office occupied the station's crown, a hemisphere of glass overlooking the docking throat where the Halcyon hung in its web of moorings, small and stubborn as a barnacle. The Director stood with her back to him, hands folded at the small of her back, watching the ship the way a surgeon watches a tumor she has decided to respect.

"Ansel Delacroix." She turned, and her smile was warm the way heat lamps are warm. "You've gotten old."

"You've gotten promoted."

"Both true. Sit, please." She poured tea from an actual ceramic pot — ceramic, on a station — and he understood the ritual for what it was: an offering, a leveling, a performance of equality before the asymmetry began. "Thirty years ago I signed your first field assignment. Do you remember?"

"You signed the routing order. I remember the order."

"I signed *you*, Ansel. Into the deep routes, into the trade-tongues, into the dark. The Archive invested more in your ears than in any instrument we've ever flown." She sat, folding herself into the chair across from him, and her voice dropped into a register he recognized from negotiations — the one that meant the real conversation was starting. "And now you've heard something. I can see it in you. The crew of the Halcyon have said something to you that you believe."

Ansel held his tea and said nothing. Silence was a tool he'd sharpened for decades; he let it sit between them like a blade on a table.

Okafor didn't flinch. "I'm going to offer you a chair. Not a seat — a *chair*. Director of Xenolinguistic Integrity. Your own division, your own staff, first access to every signal the fleet intercepts. You'd spend your last working decade doing what you were born to do, with resources you've only dreamed about." She sipped. "In exchange, you'll give a public assessment of the Halcyon crew's testimony. Cognitive degradation. Deep-route psychosis. Memory contamination from long-duration signal exposure. You've diagnosed it before. You're the most credible voice in the fleet."

"And if I find their testimony credible instead?"

"Then you'll have found yourself in agreement with sedition." She said it gently, the way one mentions weather. "Ansel. Vesper's Reach is thriving. Population forty-one thousand. Last transmission received eleven hours ago — a school choir, if you'd like to listen. The crew of the Halcyon is telling eleven thousand people their children are dead. What does that make them, in your professional language?"

He set the cup down. "In my professional language, a school choir is a recording. Recordings are the easiest things in the universe to forge."

The warmth left her face by a single degree, which from Okafor was a shout. "The Archive does not err."

"Everything errs," Ansel said. "That's the first thing any honest linguist learns. Meaning drifts. That's not a flaw in language. It's the engine."

"The Archive is not a language."

"It's the largest one humanity has ever built." He stood, because sitting felt like agreeing. "I'll review the testimony. I'll give you my assessment when I have one. Not before."

She let him reach the door before she spoke again, and her voice followed him like a hand on his shoulder. "Your assessment matters, Ansel. But it will exist inside a context. Make sure you like the context."

* * *

He didn't go back to his quarters. He went down, past the crew levels, past the hydroponics, into the archive vaults on Level Two, where the station's memory lived in cold servers and colder protocol. He had clearance. He'd had clearance for thirty years; it was one of the few things the institution had ever given him that he'd trusted.

He called up his own testimony logs.

They were a habit older than his career. Every field linguist kept them — session records, phoneme inventories, the raw capture of dying languages, annotated in his own shorthand. He'd logged the Vesper's Reach signal-life contact personally, twenty-two years ago. He'd been there. He'd sat in a listening post above that colony for a full season, mapping the way the native signal-life sang its grammar into the colony's comms bands, the way the colonists had answered. He remembered the creole taking shape. He remembered the children — there had been children — inventing words for it faster than he could record them.

The file opened. The header was correct. The date was correct. His own authentication seal sat at the bottom, unbroken.

And the contents were wrong.

Not missing — *edited*, and edited by someone who knew his shorthand, who knew his habits, who had smoothed the annotations into something plausible and flat. The session where he'd recorded the signal-life's full tonal range — the passage where the colonists and the natives had sung to each other for six hours, the passage he had privately called, in his notes, *the most beautiful thing I will ever hear* — was gone. In its place: a routine survey summary. Signal-life classified as non-sapient. Contact terminated as a precaution. No further study warranted.

He searched his memory against the file the way he'd search a text against a translation, and the discrepancies opened like wounds. Words he'd coined were absent. A phoneme he'd spent nine days isolating — the one that meant, as near as he could render it, *we remember you* — did not appear anywhere in twenty-two years of his own work. As if he had never heard it. As if he had never *been* there.

His hands were shaking. He noticed them the way you notice weather — remotely, from somewhere else.

There were two possibilities, and he turned them over like stones. The first: someone had entered the vault and rewritten his files. That required access, authority, motive — and a knowledge of his shorthand so intimate it could only have been learned from him, or from decades of surveillance.

The second possibility sat in his chest like swallowed ice.

That they hadn't touched the files at all. That the edit had been made somewhere upstream. In him.

He thought of the dreams he no longer had — the ones that had gone silent years ago, the ones he'd told himself were just age, just the mind closing its shutters. He thought of how the Vespertine creole had felt in his mouth back then, how it had *lived* there, and how one morning, without ceremony, it had simply been gone — and how he had never once, in twenty-two years, thought to ask why he didn't miss it.

You don't miss a language you've been made to forget. You don't grieve a wound you've been made to believe was never cut.

"Director Okafor," he said aloud, to the empty vault, to the servers holding his flattened life. "What did you take out of me?"

The servers hummed their indifferent answer. But beneath the hum — faint, buried in the vault's ambient carrier noise, so quiet he might have invented it — something answered. A tonal shape. A rising phrase.

*We remember you.*

Ansel stood very still in the cold, in the dark, in the wreckage of thirty years of trust, and listened.

The signal-life of Vesper's Reach was supposed to be a hundred light-years away.

It was here. It was *here*, in Meridian's own wiring, whispering in a language the Archive had ordered erased — and it had been waiting for him to remember enough to hear it.
