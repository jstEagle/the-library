## 6. Dreaming in Vespertine

The night shift on Meridian Station ran quiet the way a held breath is quiet. Ansel had claimed a corner of the linguistics annex that no one else wanted — too close to the coolant conduits, which hummed at a frequency that gave most people headaches and gave him, perversely, comfort. It sounded like a throat about to speak. He had spent forty years learning to listen for throats about to speak.

Around him, the station slept. Around him, eleven impossible people slept too, quarantined in medical with their young faces and their old eyes, and somewhere among them Ilsa Reyes was losing an hour of her life for every hour she lived. He tried not to think of her hands. He failed.

He pulled up the testimony recordings instead.

* * *

He began the way he always began: badly, mechanically, with the drudgework that looked like genius only from a distance. He transcribed. He tagged. He built spectrograms of the crew's depositions and laid them side by side like geological strata, looking for the fault lines.

The stress markers were still there, still damning. Every time they spoke of Vesper's Reach, their voices carried the micro-tremors, the swallowed sibilants, the breath catches that no coached liar could fake across eleven independent speakers. But truth-markers weren't meaning. Truth told him *that* they believed; it said nothing about what the belief was made of.

What bothered him — what had been bothering him since the second interview — was the residue. When the crew finished a sentence, when they thought they were done speaking, something lingered in the waveform. A tail. Like a bell struck softly that kept ringing below the threshold of hearing.

He isolated one. Tam's deposition, the moment he described the colony's dome going white: *"and then the light came through everything, and Marta said—"*

The recording ended there. Tam's file said the rest was lost to memory degradation. But the waveform didn't end. It went on for another four seconds, a low structured murmur beneath the silence, and Ansel sat very still in the humming dark and listened to a man speak without knowing he was speaking.

He amplified it. Cleaned it. Ran it against every phonological database the Archive maintained — three hundred and twelve living languages, nine hundred dead ones, forty-one constructed trade-idioms, the full corpus of human vocalization ever recorded.

No match.

His heart did something complicated. He hadn't felt this particular thing in years — not since his first field posting, not since the Keshari signal-lattice, not since he'd been young enough to mistake wonder for a profession. It was the feeling of standing before a door that everyone insisted was a wall.

* * *

By the third hour he had found it in all of them.

Not just Tam. All eleven crew members carried the tail-speech, buried under their human words like a river running under ice. It surfaced most often at the edges of things — at the ends of sentences, in pauses, in the moments when memory failed them and something else stepped in to fill the gap. When Reyes couldn't recall the name of her own navigator, her mouth made a sound anyway: three syllables, rising, soft-edged, that no human language owned.

Ansel worked until his tea went cold twice and the station's false dawn cycle painted the annex ceiling a pale industrial gold. He mapped the phoneme inventory by hand, because the software kept returning errors, because the software had never heard anything like it. Consonants that shifted mid-articulation. Vowels that seemed to be shaped by resonance rather than position, as if the speaker's whole chest cavity were part of the instrument. And underneath it all, a rhythmic pulse — not stress, not intonation, but something structural, something that carried information the way a heartbeat carries more than blood.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing. Before he understood it, before he feared it, he registered its beauty, the way you register firelight before you register the burning house.

And then, at hour five, with his hands shaking around a stylus he'd stopped using twenty minutes earlier, he recognized the pulse.

He had heard that rhythm once before, thirty years ago, in a classified appendix of a classified report he'd been forbidden to cite. A survey team on the Vesper system's gas giant moon had recorded structured emissions from the native signal-life — luminous, slow, vast organisms that lived in the upper atmosphere and sang to each other across hundreds of kilometers in modulated light and pressure. The report had called them non-linguistic. The report had been wrong; Ansel had known it was wrong even then, and had said so, and had been reassigned for saying so.

The rhythm matched. The pulse in the crew's buried speech was the pulse of the Vesper signal-life.

Which meant the creole was real. Which meant the colony had really spoken with them. Which meant the Archive's file — *Vespertine: extinct, 2143, no surviving speakers, no surviving recordings* — was not merely incomplete.

It was a lie with a body count.

* * *

He should have slept. Okafor would expect him rested, presentable, cooperative. Instead he sat in the annex with the lights dimmed and played the tail-speech over and over, teaching it to himself the way you learn a song stuck in your head — passively, helplessly, letting it sink past the analytical mind into whatever older room of the brain holds lullabies and grief.

He caught himself mouthing it at hour six. Three syllables, rising, soft-edged. The sound Reyes had made when she couldn't remember her navigator's name. He didn't know what it meant. His mouth knew it anyway.

That was the thing about languages, the thing no one believed until it happened to them: deep enough immersion, and the language stops being a tool and becomes a tenant. He had lost English for six weeks once, after the Keshari lattice, wandering his own quarters unable to remember the word for *door*. The xenolinguists' old joke — *we don't study languages, we're studied by them* — had never been funny to him, because it was true.

At hour seven, the annex intercom chimed and a medic's voice told him the crew's overnight scans showed further cognitive decline, and would he please come sign the observation order, and he said yes, of course, in two syllables that came out wrong, that came out *soft-edged*, and the medic paused on the line and asked if he was all right.

"I'm fine," he said, in English, carefully, like setting down something fragile.

He was not fine. He was, for the first time in a decade, awake.

* * *

Sleep took him at his desk sometime after hour eight, and the dream came the way water comes through a cracked hull.

He stood in a garden under a violet sky, and the plants were light. Not lit by light — *made* of it, slow columns of luminescence swaying in air thick as water, and each column pulsed with the rhythm, the pulse, the heartbeat-structure, and the pulses were words. Not words like human words. Words like weather. Words like tides. They didn't name things; they *were* things, briefly, and then let go.

Someone beside him spoke. He turned and it was a woman whose face he knew from a service record dated eleven years dead — Marta Okonkwo, Halcyon's navigator, listed lost with the rest of them, alive here in the violet light with her young-old face full of an expression he could only call *waiting*.

She said, in the tail-speech, in Vespertine, in the language the Archive had murdered on paper: a sentence.

He woke with it whole in his mouth.

He sat up in the grey half-light of the annex, heart slamming, and said it aloud to the empty room before he could lose it — three phrases, eleven syllables, the consonants shifting mid-shape exactly as the spectrograms had shown him, his chest resonating in a way human speech had never asked of it. The coolant conduits hummed. The station hummed back. Or he imagined it did.

He recorded himself immediately, hands unsteady, voice rough with sleep. Then he ran the comparison, and the result scrolled up the screen and he read it three times because his mind refused it twice:

**Phonetic match confidence: 99.7%. Corpus source: Halcyon crew testimony.**

He had dreamed fluently in an extinct language. In a language that was not extinct. In a language that eleven dying witnesses carried in their rewound bodies like smugglers carry contraband, hidden below the watermark of their own speech.

The dead were speaking through the living. And now, apparently, through him.

Ansel pressed his palms flat against the desk and breathed until the shaking stopped, and what replaced the shaking was not fear. It was the old wonder, the first wonder, the one the institution had spent decades grinding into competence. It felt like a door where everyone had promised him a wall.

He replayed his own voice, the dream-sentence, and translated it word by word by structure by pulse, working fast now, working hot, and the meaning assembled itself in his mind like a photograph developing:

*We kept the names. We are still keeping the names. Come and hear them.*

His blood went cold and bright at once. Because that wasn't a memory. That wasn't residue. That was an invitation — extended across whatever distance separated the signal-life from this steel ring around a sleeping station, addressed to whoever could finally answer.

He looked at the clock. Medical rounds began in ninety minutes. Okafor wanted her preliminary findings by end of shift. And somewhere down the corridor, eleven people were forgetting the massacre one hour at a time, their memories dissolving like sugar in warm water, and the only complete copy of what happened at Vesper's Reach was dissolving with them.

Unless someone learned the language fast enough to ask them to say it all again — in the tongue the Archive couldn't edit, because the Archive didn't know it existed.

Ansel pulled up the full testimony corpus, all eleven speakers, forty hours of recordings, and began to build the grammar of the dead.

Outside the annex, the station's morning cycle chimed. Somewhere in medical, Ilsa Reyes was waking younger than yesterday, and he found he was already composing, in a language he'd learned asleep, the sentence he would need to say to her:

*I can hear you. Tell me everything, while you still can.*
