## 9. The Broadcast

The impound order came down at 0347 station time, and Ansel knew it before the chime sounded, because the station itself had changed its breathing.

Meridian hummed in a register he had learned over six sleepless weeks the way he learned all languages: through the body first. The ventilation's pulse, the subliminal shudder of the docking clamps, the particular hush that fell over a corridor when security moved through it in numbers. Tonight the hush was everywhere. The Halcyon was being sealed. Eleven witnesses were being folded into quarantine bays and medical holds, their testimony reclassified as evidence of neurological contamination, their bodies — young, wrong, eroding — scheduled for "restorative observation" that would last exactly as long as it took the Archive to finish deciding what they remembered.

He sat in his quarters with the testimony on every screen, eleven voices braided into one file, and beneath it the Vespertine phoneme map he'd built night after night while the colony's dead language rearranged itself inside his skull like furniture in a house he'd always owned but never entered.

They would come for him within the hour. Okafor was many things, but she was not slow. She had offered him everything with such tenderness — funding, standing, Ilsa's care, a life — and when he'd said no, she had looked at him the way one looks at weather: inevitable, regrettable, already accounted for.

*You'll understand someday,* she'd said. *Grief is just history you haven't filed yet.*

Ansel touched the interface and opened the array access protocol he had spent three weeks building in fragments, disguised as calibration routines, buried under forty layers of legitimate diagnostics. The long-range transmitter at Meridian's crown was designed to speak to the fleet: ordered, hierarchical, encrypted to human ears alone. It had never once been pointed at anything that wasn't listening for orders.

That was about to change.

* * *

Getting to the array meant crossing four secure junctions, and he did it carrying nothing but a maintenance slate and the posture of a man too tired to be dangerous. Sixty years of being the institution's gentle instrument had taught him this much: nobody guards against the translator. Translators are furniture. Translators are the people who stand slightly behind power and repeat what it says in softer words.

At the third junction, a young officer glanced up from her console. "Dr. Delacroix? Late shift?"

"Calibration," he said. "The array drifts 0.3 degrees a week. Nobody's corrected it since the refit." A lie built from a truth; the drift was real. He'd checked it himself, twice, as cover.

She nodded him through, and he felt the old shame rise and pass through him like weather. He had spent his whole life making institutions legible. Tonight he would make them audible.

The array chamber was cold in the way only vacuum-adjacent spaces are cold, a cold that came through the walls from the dark itself. The dish hung above him beyond the viewport, vast and pale, aimed at nothing. He sealed the door behind him with a maintenance override and began the takeover sequence, and his hands, which had trembled for weeks whenever he thought of Ilsa's thinning hands, were perfectly still.

* * *

The transmission had two parts, and he had rehearsed them both until they lived in his mouth like prayers.

The first was human. The full joint testimony — eleven voices, timestamps, biometric stress markers, the fleet ordnance signatures Tam had once described with a gunner's terrible precision before the massacre dissolved out of him like sugar in water. Every frame of sensor data the Halcyon had carried home. The edited seams in Ansel's own logs, exposed side by side with the originals he'd recovered from a backup so old the Archive had forgotten it existed. He addressed it to every receiver in the network, unencrypted, unranked, unfiled. Not to admirals. To archivists' assistants, to dockhands, to children doing schoolwork on colony relays. Truth is not a document, he had learned. Truth is a distribution problem.

The second part was not for humans at all.

He keyed the Vespertine phoneme string into the modulator and watched it render: a pattern that looked like noise if you were human, a cascade of amplitude shifts riding beneath the carrier wave the way grief rides beneath a calm voice. It was the creole the colonists of Vesper's Reach had built with the signal-life — those vast, patient intelligences that lived in the electromagnetic deeps between stars and spoke, when they spoke at all, in structures no human grammar could hold. The colony had died speaking it. The Archive had declared it extinct, a footnote, a curiosity.

But extinction, Ansel now understood, was an administrative term. It meant *no longer relevant*. It did not mean gone.

He had isolated the pattern in the crew's speech because the reversal eating their memories had stripped away everything acquired and left only what had been laid down deepest — and what lay deepest in them was not Standard, not fleet protocol, but the language they'd dreamed in at Vesper's Reach, the language they'd used to talk to the sky. The signal-life had written itself into them. It was still there, under the erosion, holding shape the way a riverbed holds the shape of water long after the water has been taken.

So he did not address the broadcast to humanity. He aimed the dish at the coordinates where the signal-life congregated — the slow bright shoals the fleet charts called *anomalous interference* — and he composed, in a tongue he had known for six weeks and would know forever, a single sentence:

*We are the ones who survived your friends. We carry what was done to them. Remember aloud.*

Then he added, because he was still human, still a man who had loved people who were dissolving: *Please. Before we forget too.*

His finger hovered over transmit.

Somewhere below him, Ilsa Reyes was in a medical hold, and by tomorrow her memory of the fire would be gone, then her memory of the colony, then perhaps her memory of ever having asked a stranger to witness her. He had promised to witness. This was witnessing. This was the only kind that could not be edited, because it would live outside the Archive entirely, in the minds of beings who had watched the massacre happen and been silenced by distance, by treaty, by the simple fact that no one had asked them.

The Archive does not err, Okafor had told him. No, he thought. The Archive errs constantly. It simply never admits it, and it has never once been made to grieve.

He pressed transmit.

* * *

The array sang.

He felt it in his teeth, in the fluid of his eyes, a pressure wave of meaning pouring upward through the dish and out into the black. The testimony went first, human words scattering across the network like sparks off a grinding wheel. Then the Vespertine string, and the chamber went quiet around its own echo, and Ansel stood alone under the great blind eye of the dish and waited.

Ten seconds. Twenty. The readout showed clean transmission, no response, the dark absorbing everything as it always had. Doubt arrived on schedule, cold and fluent: *you have spoken nonsense into the void, old man, you have burned your life on a phoneme map built from the broken speech of dying people—*

Then every receiver on Meridian Station opened at once.

It began as a hum in the deck plating. Then the emergency channels, the maintenance bands, the entertainment relays, the encrypted fleet frequencies — all of them lit simultaneously, all of them carrying the same incoming signal, a flood with no origin point because it came from everywhere at once, from the shoals and the deeps and the space between Meridian and everything. Ansel's console screamed with bandwidth warnings and he laughed, actually laughed, a cracked and disbelieving sound, because he understood what he was hearing before any instrument decoded it.

They were remembering aloud.

The signal-life did not speak in sentences. They spoke in *presences* — whole hours delivered whole. And what they delivered was Vesper's Reach: the colony's final hour, uncompressed, unedited, witnessed from the sky. Ansel felt it move through the station's systems and through his own skull, and it was not data, it was *weather*, it was the heat of the burning habitat domes, the specific frequency of a child's voice calling a name the Archive had deleted, the ordnance falling in formation like a sentence being punctuated, the colonists' last transmissions in Vespertine calling not for help but for the sky to remember them — and the sky had heard, and the sky had waited thirty years for someone to ask.

Every speaker on the station was playing it. Every screen showed it rendered in whatever form each system could manage. In the corridors below, alarms rang and did not matter. People were standing still in hallways, hearing the dead.

And threaded through all of it, unmistakable, the eleven voices of the Halcyon's crew — because the signal-life had kept them too, had recorded what the crew had seen and said, and now gave it back to them, complete, so that even the parts they had lost were returned. Somewhere in a medical hold, Ansel knew, Tam was hearing the massacre again for the first time since he'd accused his crewmates of madness. Somewhere, Ilsa was hearing herself say *witness me* and knowing someone finally had.

Ansel slid down the console to the cold floor and put his head in his hands, and the flood poured over him, and he let it. Terror and release arrived together, indistinguishable, the way dawn and dusk look identical if you don't check which way the light is moving.

He had betrayed the institution that raised him. He had committed treason in a language humanity couldn't read. And the truth was loose now, everywhere, in every ear, and no archive in human space could call it back.

The door override chimed. Security, at last — Okafor's answer arriving late, arriving irrelevant. Through the viewport, the dish stood luminous against the stars, still transmitting, though there was nothing left to send, because the answering signal had taken it over completely, using humanity's own great throat to speak.

Ansel rose to meet the door. His legs shook. His hands did not.

When it opened, Director Okafor stood in the corridor with six officers behind her, and her face was not angry. It was worse than angry. It was the face of a woman listening, despite herself, to the burning of Vesper's Reach pouring out of the wall panel beside her head — hearing, for the first time, the thing she had spent her career filing away — and understanding that the treaty, the peace, the forty fed worlds, and the Archive itself had just begun, in front of everyone, to come apart.

"You should have certified the records, Ansel," she said quietly, over the voices of the dead.

"I know," he said. "I chose the living instead."

Behind her, down the length of the corridor, every screen on Meridian Station flickered and resolved into the same image: the sky above a burning colony, and written across it in light, in a language two people on this station could read, a single word that the signal-life had waited thirty years to say, and that the next chapter of every human life would begin with —

*Remembered.*
