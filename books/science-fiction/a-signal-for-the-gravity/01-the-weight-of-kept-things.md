## 1. The Weight of Kept Things

There are two kinds of people who end up as caretakers of the Svalbard Deep Seed Vault: the ones who believe in the work, and the ones who have nowhere else to go. I've spent twelve years telling myself I'm the first kind, which is how you know I'm the second.

My name is Jonah Okonkwo. Officially, I'm Custodian First Class of humanity's backup plan — four hundred thousand seed varieties sealed in permafrost and steel, waiting for the day we finally manage to ruin everything so thoroughly that we need to start over. Unofficially, I'm a man who hauls cargo through contested orbits twice a quarter because the orbital transfer contracts keep getting "restructured," and nobody with options wants a route that skirts three separate corporate exclusion zones just to deliver nitrogen cartridges and desiccant to a facility that hasn't had a visitor in nine years.

The vault doesn't need me, exactly. It needs *someone*. The distinction matters more than it should at three in the morning ship-time, when the only voice on comms is the station's maintenance AI reminding me that humidity sensor seven has drifted point-three percent out of tolerance. I've named her Agnes. She's the closest thing I have to a colleague, and she has never once asked me how my day went, which I respect.

So: routine run. Cargo pod lashed to the *Mule*'s spine like a stubborn barnacle, forty-one hours of transit through the Kessler shoals where the debris from the old corporate wars still tumbles in elegant, lethal spirals. I know the lanes by heart now. That's not a boast; that's a confession. Twelve years, and my whole world fits inside a corridor of tracked objects and a schedule nobody audits.

I was running the pre-docking checklist when Agnes flagged the anomaly.

"Contact bearing two-seven-zero mark one-five," she said, in the flat tone she reserves for things that are either boring or catastrophic. "Range eleven kilometers. No transponder."

"No transponder" in contested orbit means one of three things: derelict, smuggler, or something worse. I pulled up the optical feed and there it was — a ship, drifting silent against the black, tumbling very slowly, like a body turning over in sleep.

She was deep-space class. Long spine, radiators folded like a moth's wings, hull scorched the particular dull gray-black of a vessel that's been through hard radiation and come back changed. And stenciled along her flank, half-eaten by micrometeorite pitting, a name:

*MV MERIDIAN — CALLISTO TRANSIT AUTHORITY.*

I sat with that for a long moment. The Meridian was famous, in the way dead ships are famous. Launched fourteen years ago on a long-loop survey past Jupiter. Declared lost with all hands eight years back. There'd been a memorial service. I'd watched the broadcast alone in the mess hall, eating reconstituted rice, feeling the specific loneliness of mourning strangers while being un-mourned yourself.

Lost ships don't drift near seed vaults. Lost ships don't drift anywhere. They burn up, or they get salvaged, or they fall into some gravity well and stay there. A lost ship holding position eleven kilometers off my dock was not a lost ship anymore.

Protocol said report it. Protocol said log the contact, transmit to Orbital Traffic Control, and let the authorities handle whatever came next. Protocol and I have been close friends for twelve years. Protocol keeps me fed, licensed, and invisible. Protocol is the wall I built between myself and every bad decision available to a man alone in the dark.

I opened the reporting channel.

And then the ship moved. Not much — a puff of cold-gas thrusters, a slow arrest of its tumble, and then it was hanging there, nose toward me, deliberate as a held breath. A light blinked on in an observation port. Three flashes. Pause. Three flashes.

Somebody over there was alive, and they knew exactly what protocol required of me, and they were asking me not to do it.

"Jonah," Agnes said. "You are obligated to—"

"I know what I'm obligated to do, Agnes." I closed the channel. My hand was shaking, which annoyed me. Fear or excitement, impossible to tell at this range, and honestly they're the same animal wearing different collars.

Here's the thing about twelve years of custodianship that nobody warns you about: you become a keeper of dead things. Seeds are alive, technically — dormant, patient, viable — but the vault itself is a mausoleum of intention. Every rack and cryo-drawer is somebody's answer to a question nobody's asking yet. I tend the answers. The questions died or left or got bought out by a conglomerate. Some nights I walk the stacks and think: this is what I am. A man keeping other people's futures fresh while his own expires quietly in the dark.

And here was a ship full of people who'd been mourned. Officially erased. Written off with a ceremony and a memorial page and probably a settlement paid to relatives who'd never stop wondering. Eight years dead on paper, and here they were, blinking lights at me in threes, asking a stranger for the one thing he wasn't supposed to give.

I told myself I was going to hail them first. Just hail them, assess, gather information. Information gathering is compatible with protocol; there's even a form for it. I am a great believer in forms. Forms are how responsible men hide from their own choices.

"MV Meridian, this is Svalbard Deep custodial vessel Mule, do you copy?"

Static. Then a woman's voice, hoarse, like it hadn't been used in weeks. "Copy, Mule. This is Reyes, Ilsa Reyes, commanding the Meridian." A pause, and when she spoke again there was something underneath the exhaustion — a careful, practiced steadiness, the voice of someone who'd rehearsed this. "We have wounded. We have no fuel worth mentioning. And we need you to understand that if you report us, we die. Not eventually. Quickly. Do you copy?"

"That's not — Captain, that's not how reporting works. There's due process, there's—"

"There's a salvage claim on our hull filed by Halcyon Dynamics six months before we were declared lost," she said. "Look it up. We are legally wreckage, Jonah Okonkwo. Wreckage gets towed, stripped, and disappeared. Ask me how I know."

I didn't ask. I looked it up instead, because of course I did, because I am who I am. She was right. Salvage claim, sealed court order, and a Halcyon subsidiary listed as sole beneficiary of the Meridian's estate. Eight years ago. While they were still out past Jupiter, still alive, still trusting the system to be there when they came home.

* * *

I docked them myself. Manual approach, no autopilot, because I didn't want a log entry showing anything but my own hands making the decision. The Meridian kissed my docking collar with the gentle gracelessness of a ship running on fumes and prayer, and when the seal pressurized and the inner hatch cycled open, I got my first look at the crew of the lost Meridian.

Six of them. Alive. Standing in the accessway like survivors of something I couldn't name yet, hollow-eyed, wrapped in thermal blankets gone gray with use.

And wrong. All of them wrong, in a way that took my brain several seconds to process because it kept rejecting the data. The woman who introduced herself as Dr. Okafor, their medic, had the face of someone in her late twenties — but the way she carried herself, the economy of her movements, the surgical precision of her speech, belonged to a woman of fifty. The engineer, a broad man with a shaved head, moved like an old sailor in a young man's body, favoring joints that shouldn't ache yet. And at the back, half-hidden behind the others, stood a girl who couldn't have been more than ten years old, watching me with eyes that were old beyond any age at all.

"You're the crew," I said stupidly. "Of the Meridian. You launched fourteen years ago."

"We did," Reyes said. She was the least wrong of them — mid-thirties now, maybe, where she'd have been mid-forties at launch. Her hair was coming in dark at the roots above gray. Growing backward. All of them, growing backward. "Our drive does things to time that the brochures didn't mention. We outran something on the way home. Our bodies paid the toll." She looked around my docking bay — the scuffed deck plates, the cargo netting, the humming racks of seed canisters — with an expression I recognized, because I see it in the mirror every morning. The look of someone taking inventory of a place that isn't theirs, knowing they'll never be sent anywhere better.

"Custodian Okonkwo," she said. "Before you decide anything else, you should know what we're carrying. Why they'll come for us. Why they'll come for *you*, now, just for opening this hatch."

"Don't tell me," I said.

She raised an eyebrow.

"Not yet," I said. "Because the second I know, I'm an accomplice, and I want—" I stopped. Started again. "Twelve years I've done everything right. Filed everything. Reported everything. And nobody has ever once needed me to. I want one hour of being a man who made a choice before I'm a man who committed a crime. Can you give me that?"

Something shifted in her face. Recognition, maybe — the look of one abandoned thing finding another.

"One hour," she said. "Then I tell you about Callisto, and you're going to wish you'd reported us."

Behind her, the little girl — the one who was somehow also not a little girl — tugged Dr. Okafor's sleeve and whispered, too loud, the way children whisper: "Is he safe?"

Okafor glanced at me, and I understood with terrible clarity that none of them knew the answer either.

"He's here," Okafor said. "That's a start."

Agnes pinged me privately on my implant, gentle as a cough: *Reminder. Undocumented contact must be logged within four hours.*

Four hours. I looked at the six ghosts of the Meridian standing in my bay, growing younger by the day, carrying something heavy enough to make corporations file claims on living people.

"Yeah," I murmured. "Log it later, Agnes. Log it much, much later."
