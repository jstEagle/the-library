## 1. The Long Route

The mail ship *Perihelion* was eleven days out of Thessaly Reach when Casper Ashgrove began talking to himself.

He did it the way he did everything—quietly, methodically, without any expectation of an answer. The habit had started years ago, back when he'd first taken the Long Route contract and discovered that a man could go three weeks between human voices if he wasn't careful. Now he narrated his routines to the empty cabin like a liturgy: *Coolant pressure nominal. Starboard array still humming at that frequency I don't like. Coffee, reconstituted, tastes like regret.* The words came out in English, mostly, though sometimes—when he was tired, or when the dark outside the viewport pressed too close—they came out in nothing at all.

That was the part he never told anyone.

The dreams had no images. That was what made them bearable, and also what made them unbearable. Other people dreamed in pictures: faces, oceans, the houses of their childhoods rendered in impossible detail. Casper dreamed in syllables. He would surface from sleep with his mouth already shaped around sounds that belonged to no language he'd ever studied—which was saying something, because Casper had studied forty-one of them, six of which were officially listed as extinct, and two of which were officially listed as never having existed at all.

The syllables arrived most nights now. They didn't mean anything, as far as he could tell. They were just *there*, patient as weather, waiting behind his teeth until he woke and let them dissolve. He kept a recorder by his bunk for the first year and captured maybe thirty seconds of himself murmuring in the dark. He played it back once, alone in the galley of the *Perihelion* while the stars wheeled past indifferent and slow, and heard a voice he almost didn't recognize—soft, rhythmic, grieving somehow, though grief wasn't a thing a sound could carry on its own.

He deleted the file. He told himself it was nothing. A mind full of dead languages composting itself in its sleep, turning over old phonemes like a gardener turning soil. His academy instructors would have had theories. His academy instructors had all had theories about Casper Ashgrove, most of them unflattering, all of them wrong in ways he couldn't quite articulate.

He poured the coffee and carried it to the cockpit, where the Long Route unspooled ahead of him in the long amber smear of Thessaly's shipping lanes.

The Route was dying. Everyone said so, and everyone said it the way you'd remark on the weather—as though it were a fact of nature rather than a choice someone had made. When Casper's grandfather's generation had signed on, the circuit had run nine colonies and a dozen way-stations, fed by monthly packets from Earth, thick with news and letters and the small gossip of a species trying to stay one species across impossible distances. Now there were five colonies left on the official manifest, the Earth transmissions came thinner every cycle, and half the relay buoys between Meridian Station and Cold Harbor had gone dark for want of anyone willing to service them. The Concord administration called it "rationalization." The route-dwellers called it what it was: being slowly, politely forgotten.

Casper didn't call it anything. He flew the mail.

It was not a heroic job. It was barely a job at all, some cycles—the *Perihelion*'s hold was more than half empty this run, just four hundred kilos of sealed correspondence, a crate of medical supplies for Cold Harbor's clinic, and one pallet of seed stock that had been sitting in Thessaly Reach's warehouse so long the manifest had yellowed. But mail mattered differently out here than it did in the inner worlds. Out here, a letter might be the only proof, months running, that the person who wrote it was still alive. Out here, people read envelopes the way pilgrims read scripture.

Casper checked the seals, logged his position, and settled in for the last leg to Cold Harbor. Eleven hours. He spent the first three reviewing the delivery manifest, because that was the kind of man he was—the kind who checked things twice, who filed reports nobody read, who maintained a translation implant suite tuned to languages no living person spoke because somebody ought to remember they had existed.

The fourth hour, he slept. And the syllables came again.

This time there was a new one. He woke with it whole in his mouth, a sound like water finding a crack in stone—two soft consonants wrapped around a vowel that seemed to fall downward even as it was spoken. He sat up in the dark of his cabin and said it aloud before he could stop himself, and the cabin took it and gave nothing back, and he lay there listening to his own heartbeat and telling himself, as he always told himself, that it meant nothing.

It was getting harder to believe that. The syllables weren't random anymore. He'd noticed it over the past few months: they repeated. Not exactly—there was variation, drift, the way a melody varies when different voices sing it—but the underlying pattern held, night after night, voyage after voyage. Something in him was circling the same ground. Something in him was looking for something.

He got up, made himself finish the coffee, and went back to the manifest.

* * *

Cold Harbor came up on the scopes as a bruise against the black—a lumpy moonlet, half-mined, wrapped in the orange glow of its pressure domes and the thin silver lattice of its orbital tethers. It had been a mining concern once, then a garrison town, then neither, and now it was simply a place where eleven hundred people had decided to keep existing, which out here counted as an act of quiet defiance.

The harbor master was a woman named Dree Okafor, broad-shouldered and gray-braided, who waved the *Perihelion* into berth seven with the economy of someone who'd done it ten thousand times. Her twin brother ran the independent relay up on the ridge; between them, the Okafors constituted roughly half of Cold Harbor's connection to the rest of human space, and both of them treated the job with the seriousness of priests.

"Ashgrove." She clasped his forearm when he came down the ramp, which was as close to affection as harbor protocol allowed. "You look terrible."

"I always look terrible."

"True. Consistency is a virtue." She handed him a chit and a look. "Four hundred twelve items inbound. Two hundred ninety outbound, and one of them's flagged priority-personal, so don't lose it."

"I've never lost anything."

"You lost my brother's wrench."

"That was returned. Eventually. By a third party." He signed the chit. "How's the relay?"

Dree's face did something complicated. "Holding. Barely. Earth's downlink dropped to quarter bandwidth last month. My brother says at this rate we'll be listening to them through a straw by winter." She shrugged, and the shrug contained an entire philosophy of endurance. "You know how it goes. The Route forgets us a little more every year."

"Somebody remembers," Casper said. It was the closest thing he had to a creed, and he delivered it flatly, without conviction, the way you recite something you've said so often it's worn smooth.

Dree looked at him a moment longer than the joke required. "Somebody," she agreed, and turned to the cargo.

* * *

The letter was the reason he'd come down to the surface personally instead of letting the dock drones handle the transfer. Priority-personal, hand-delivery requested, sender deceased.

That last line was why the request existed at all. The sender had been a woman named Odile Ferrant, resident of Thessaly Reach, and she had died fourteen months ago—Casper knew because her final letter had traveled with him on three separate runs while her estate was settled, and somewhere in that bureaucratic limbo a clerk had finally stamped the envelope FORWARD PER INSTRUCTIONS ENCLOSED. The instructions enclosed directed the letter to one Marta Voss, Cold Harbor, Dome Three, Well Street. The date on the letter was twenty-two years old.

Twenty-two years. Casper had checked twice, thinking he'd misread. But Odile Ferrant had written the letter in a season of grief or love or whatever it had been, addressed it, and then—for reasons known only to her—not sent it. Kept it in a drawer for two decades. And then, dying, had decided it should go anyway.

Well Street in Dome Three was a corridor of hydroponic terraces and aging residential modules, warm and green-smelling, loud with water cycling through pipes older than anyone living. Marta Voss's unit was at the far end, its door painted a defiant blue that had faded toward violet. Casper stood in front of it for a long time, the letter in his jacket pocket, feeling the particular dread that was the worst part of his job.

He knocked.

The woman who answered was perhaps seventy, small and straight-backed, with white hair cut short and eyes the pale gray of Cold Harbor's sky. She looked at his courier's tabard and her hands came together in front of her chest, fingers interlacing, tightening.

"Mail," she said. Not a question. On the Route, everyone knew what a personal delivery from a courier's own hands meant, and it was never good.

"Mrs. Voss? Marta Voss?"

"Yes."

"My name is Casper Ashgrove. I fly the Thessaly Reach circuit." He drew out the letter. "I have a delivery for you. It's—" He stopped. Every script he'd prepared on the flight down abandoned him at once.

Marta Voss looked at the envelope, and Casper watched her recognize the handwriting from a meter away. Watched her understand, in the same instant, everything the envelope implied. Her fingers stayed interlaced. Her knuckles went white.

"Odile," she said.

"There are forwarding instructions," Casper said gently. "The letter was found among her effects. She wanted you to have it."

"When?" The word cracked. "When did she write it?"

Casper could have lied. He considered lying. He was, in that moment, genuinely uncertain whether kindness and truth were the same thing, or whether they had ever been.

"Mrs. Voss, I—"

"It's old," Marta said. She wasn't asking him. She was reading the paper itself, the style of the envelope, the stamp. Twenty-two years of postal history written in pulp and adhesive. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it's old. She wrote this when—" And then she pressed the envelope to her sternum with both hands and stood very still in her doorway, in the green light of Well Street, while the water pipes sang their old song, and she did not cry, or if she did, she did it somewhere deeper than her face.

"Would you like me to wait?" Casper asked. "There's no regulation. I can stay."

"No." She shook her head. "No, thank you. Thank you for bringing it. Twenty-two years." She laughed, a small broken sound. "She always was late. Forty years I knew that woman and she never once arrived anywhere on time." She looked up at him, and her gray eyes were fierce and wet. "Did she say anything? At the end?"

"I never met her," Casper said. "I only carried her mail."

Marta Voss nodded slowly, absorbing this, and something in her expression shifted—from grief toward a strange, wondering gratitude, as though the letter were a message in a bottle and Casper merely the sea that had carried it. "Well," she said. "You carried it true. That's the whole job, isn't it? Carrying it true."

"Yes," Casper said. "That's the whole job."

She closed the door, gently, and Casper stood alone in the corridor for a while, listening to the water move through the walls, feeling the ache settle into him the way it always did after deliveries like this—the ache of a man who ferried other people's connections because he had none of his own. No one wrote to Casper Ashgrove. No one waited for his letters. He was a bridge that no one crossed in either direction, and he had made his peace with it, or something adjacent to peace, or at least a workable truce.

He walked back toward the docks through Dome Three's evening shift-change, past market stalls closing and children chasing each other between the terrace rows, and told himself the familiar lie: *this is enough. The work is enough.*

* * *

He lifted from Cold Harbor two days later, outbound along the Meridian leg of the circuit, and it was on that leg—bored, restless, unable to sleep, monitoring frequencies he had no professional reason to monitor—that everything changed.

But that came later. First there was the transit itself: the long silence between colonies, the *Perihelion* threading the dark with her half-empty hold, Casper in the cockpit running scans he didn't need to run, listening to static he didn't need to listen to. It was a habit he'd never examined closely. Dead bands, decommissioned channels, the frequencies of relays that had gone silent decades ago. He surfed them the way other men thumbed worry-beads, without purpose, without hope, just keeping his ears in the water.

Meridian Station answered his handshake on schedule, its automated beacon crisp and cold. Beyond it, the deep relay to Earth hung in its distant orbit like a closed door. Casper checked the transmission schedule out of ritual: next packet from the inner worlds, six days. Content: routine. Bandwidth: degraded, again.

"The Route forgets us," Dree had said.

"Somebody remembers," he'd answered, and hadn't believed it, and had said it anyway, because it was the only prayer he knew.

He was sweeping the band between Cold Harbor and Meridian—dead spectrum, officially unallocated since before he was born—when his implants woke up.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a sleeper wakes when their name is spoken. The translation suite, tuned to forty-one languages and six extinct ones, lit up across his neural interface like a struck bell, and Casper Ashgrove sat forward in his chair with his coffee going cold in his hand and stared at a waveform that had shape. Structure. Rhythm. Repetition.

Static didn't repeat. Noise didn't have grammar.

And buried under the hiss of a channel no one had used in fifty years, something was saying the same thing over and over, patiently, the way you'd call into a canyon—except this canyon was two centuries wide, and the voice inside it was speaking a language that Casper knew, bone-deep, from a single semester of forbidden study and a lifetime of dreams:

Vessari.

His hands were shaking. He told himself it was nothing. An echo, a ghost in the relay, a corrupted buoy looping garbage.

He recorded it anyway.
