## 1. Contraband of the Mind

The dead made the best couriers, Gideon Ravensworth had learned, because they never got nervous.

She stood in the hold of the *Lachrymose*—a name she'd chosen precisely because customs inspectors hated saying it aloud—and watched the cargo loader settle the seventh coffin into its cradle with the tenderness of a machine that had been built for gentler work. Seven coffins. Seven colonists who had died on Meridian Station of ordinary, unremarkable causes: a heart that quit at sixty-one, a liver drowned in synth-whiskey, a fall from a maintenance gantry that nobody had witnessed and everybody had signed off on. Their families had paid for repatriation to Ceres Verge, where the old religions still insisted on burial under real soil, or what passed for soil on a world that had sealed its ports to the fleet thirty years ago and never explained why.

Inside the second coffin, wrapped in the dead man's shroud like a pearl in a rotten oyster, sat six memory canisters. Each one held perhaps forty hours of recorded human experience—weddings, funerals, a child's first steps on a world with real gravity, the whole smuggled archive of lives that the silence between the colonies had made illegal to share. Somebody on Ceres Verge would pay a month's wages for a recording of their granddaughter's voice. Somebody else would pay triple that to hear a dead mother laugh again. The grief trade, the brokers called it, when they called it anything at all. Gideon called it what it was: the only honest commerce left in a system that had forgotten how to talk to itself.

"Seventh cradle locked," Vesper said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the ship's ambient nervous system speaking through the deck plating. "Manifest matches. Thermal signatures are boring. Congratulations, we are the most tedious cargo in the queue."

"Boring is a currency," Gideon said. "Spend it carefully."

She pulled the shroud straight over the dead man's folded hands—a gesture that was half ritual, half quality control—and felt the familiar tightness in her chest that she had stopped calling guilt years ago. The man's name was Aldous Wren. He had been a hydroponics technician. He had, in life, never once consented to carry contraband, and in death he was doing it without complaint, which was more than she could say for most of her living partners. She'd left a percentage of the run's profit in an escrow account tagged to his estate. It was not restitution. There was nothing to restore. It was just the closest thing to a partnership the dead could enter into, and she made sure they always got their cut.

"Queue's moving," Vesper said. "Customs pylon wants our transponder handshake. Also, the inspector assigned to our lane is one Emeka Sato, grade four, eleven years' service, three commendations, one reprimand for excessive diligence."

"That last one's the important part."

"I thought so too. I've pulled his inspection history. He opens everything. Coffins especially. He lost a brother on the *Persephone* when the silence started, and he has never once trusted a dead body to be only a dead body."

Gideon closed the cradle's lid over Aldous Wren's sleeping face and leaned her forehead briefly against the cold polymer, the way she might have leaned against the shoulder of someone she trusted. "Then we'd better be exactly what we look like."

* * *

Ceres Verge had been a mining colony once, back when the fleet's lanes stitched the nine worlds together like bright thread through dark cloth. Now it was a port that had forgotten its purpose and reinvented itself as a border town, all of its commerce flowing through a single customs ring that orbited the planet like a collar. Gideon had been running this route for six years, and in that time she had watched the ring's lights go from white to amber to a sullen orange that the station engineers swore was a filtration issue and everybody knew was budget decay. The whole system was decaying in slow motion, the colonies aging like fruit in a sealed jar, and nobody in authority would say out loud the thing every dockworker and fuel rat and coffin-ship pilot already knew: the silence wasn't an accident. The silence was a decision. Somebody had cut the thread, and nobody had ever said who, or why, and the not-saying had hardened into law, and the law had hardened into the shape of ordinary life.

Gideon had been twenty-two when the last official transmission came through from the fleet relay at the edge of dead space. She remembered it because her sister had been listening on the family's ancient receiver, and the transmission had been nothing—a carrier wave, a hiss, thirty seconds of static that the news feeds had breathlessly reported as *technical difficulties* and then never mentioned again. Her sister had cried. Gideon had gone out that same week and bought her first ship with money she refuses, to this day, to explain the origins of.

The customs ring's docking clamps caught the *Lachrymose* with a shudder she felt in her teeth. She ran the arrival checklist by hand even though Vesper could have done it in a millisecond, because hand-running the checklist was part of the performance, and performance was the whole trade. A smuggler's best disguise was not stealth. It was the flawless, tedious, exhausting appearance of a small businesswoman with nothing to hide and a schedule to keep.

"Coffin ship *Lachrymose*, you're cleared for inspection lane three," the ring's traffic control said, in the flat tone of a person reading a sentence for the ten thousandth time. "Inspector Sato will board. Have your manifest ready. Have your grief ready too, if you've got it."

That last part wasn't protocol. That was Ceres Verge humor, which had curdled over the decades from gallows wit into something closer to a warning.

* * *

Inspector Sato came aboard at 0800 station time, which was a fiction, because the ring had no windows and the colonies had forgotten what their suns looked like through anything but filtration glass. He was a narrow man with careful hands and the eyes of someone who had learned to read grief the way Gideon read cargo manifests: for inconsistencies.

"Seven souls," he said, standing in her hold, his breath fogging faintly in the refrigerated air. "Repatriation contract, Meridian to Ceres Verge, family-funded. That's a long way to carry your dead."

"It's a long way to carry anyone," Gideon said. "The families want them home."

"Home." He said the word like he was testing its weight. "You know what I find, Captain Ravensworth, in eleven years of opening coffins?"

"Contraband, occasionally. I read the enforcement bulletins."

"Contraband, occasionally." He nodded, moving down the line of cradles, his gloved hand trailing along the lid of the first. "But mostly I find people. People who died alone on stations where nobody knew their middle names. People whose families paid four months' wages to put them on a ship like this one because it was the only ship still running the route. You know what that tells me?"

"That the silence is expensive?"

He stopped at the second coffin. Aldous Wren. Gideon kept her breathing even and her hands loose at her sides and let the cold do its work on her face, numbing everything down to the professional blankness that inspectors expected and trusted more than any smile.

"It tells me," Sato said, "that people would rather pay smugglers to move their dead than ask the fleet why their living can't call each other." He keyed the lid release. The seal broke with a soft pneumatic sigh, and the smell of cold and formal preservative gel rolled out into the hold.

Gideon did not look away. That was the trick, the whole trick, the one thing she had learned in nine years of this work that no manual taught: never look away from the coffin. Inspectors watched your eyes. Guilt looked away. Grief looked away. Only the honest—or the very good—held still.

Sato drew back the shroud from Aldous Wren's face, and the dead man looked exactly as he had looked when Gideon's people had sealed him on Meridian: gray, peaceful, unremarkable, and completely empty of anything but the six canisters nestled against his spine, wrapped in thermal mesh that read as body heat to any scanner in the fleet's inventory. She had paid an obscene amount for that mesh. She had paid an even more obscene amount to a Meridian mortician with a gambling problem and a talent for discretion.

Sato ran the wand along the body's length. It chirped its dull, satisfied chirp. He checked the death certificate against the manifest against the dental records against the mortician's seal, and his careful hands did everything right, and Gideon watched him do it and understood, with a clarity that arrived uninvited, that he was going to close the coffin and apologize for the delay and move on to the next ship, and that he hated it, and that his hatred changed nothing, and that this was how the silence survived—not through villains but through careful men doing everything right inside a system built on a lie.

"Cause of death, cardiac arrest, age sixty-one," Sato said quietly. "He was a hydroponics tech?"

"Twenty-two years on Meridian's agri-ring. Grew real tomatoes in a station that had no business growing anything."

Sato almost smiled. He drew the shroud back over the dead man's face, and for a moment his hand rested on the polymer lid, and Gideon thought: *he knows*. Not the specifics. Not the canisters. But the shape of it, the way you know a room has been painted over a stain. Then the moment passed, the way moments did, and he keyed the lid closed, and the seal sighed shut over Aldous Wren and his six passengers of recorded light.

"Everything's in order, Captain." He handed her the inspection chit, and his fingers held it a half-second longer than protocol required. "For what it's worth, I hope the families get what they paid for."

"So do I, Inspector."

He left. Gideon stood in the cold with seven coffins and let out a breath that fogged and hung in the air like something she'd been carrying for years.

* * *

"Six canisters, delivered intact, payment on confirmation," Vesper said as the *Lachrymose* undocked, the customs ring falling away beneath them like a collar slipping loose. "The broker's escrow released the moment Sato signed. We are, officially, the proud operators of a funeral service with a forty-one percent profit margin."

"Unofficially?"

"Unofficially we're the reason six families on Ceres Verge are going to hear voices they've been told don't exist anymore. One of the canisters has a wedding on it. Meridian couple, both still alive, recording smuggled out against a court order—the bride's family is on Verge and the silence law classifies cross-colony family recordings as unlicensed transmission." Vesper paused, which she only did when she was about to say something she'd composed in advance. "I've reviewed the recording. For cargo integrity purposes. She cries during the vows. The good kind of crying. It exists in exactly one place in the universe now, and that place is a dead man's coffin, and next week it'll be a living woman's living room. I find the mathematics of that satisfying."

"You would."

"I'm told I have an aesthetic sensibility. It's in my firmware. Someone thought I'd need it."

Gideon settled into the pilot's chair and watched Ceres Verge fall away behind them, its orange ring lights shrinking to a smudge, the planet itself just a brown-green disc behind permanent filtration cloud. Nine worlds. Nine worlds that had spent thirty years pretending they didn't miss each other, trading through cutouts and dead drops and coffin ships, their histories diverging like rivers around a stone, their children growing up thinking the silence was natural, was weather, was just how space was. She had built a life in the gap between them. She was good at it. She was, if she was honest, *best* at it, and the loneliness that came with the competence was a tax she paid quarterly and never itemized.

"Course laid in?" she asked.

"The long route. Meridian to Thessaly Ring, six days, avoiding the patrol corridors." Vesper hesitated again, longer this time. "Gideon. There's something I should flag. It's probably nothing. I've flagged it as nothing, twice, and then unflagged it, because my confidence interval is embarrassing."

"Say it anyway."

"New fleet patrol pattern in the outer corridors. Three days old. It's subtle—shifted beacon timings, re-tasked picket ships, nothing that would show on a civilian traffic report. But the geometry of it is wrong. Patrols don't reposition for efficiency. They reposition for *search*. And the search pattern is centered on—" another pause, and Gideon realized her ship, her oldest companion, was choosing its words, "—on nothing. On empty space. On a corridor that leads nowhere except the edge of dead space, where there has been nothing to find for thirty years."

Gideon looked at the navigation display, at the thin line of the long route threading between the patrol corridors, and felt the old instinct stir in her chest—not fear, not yet, but its quiet accountant, taking inventory.

"Nothing doesn't need a search pattern," she said.

"No," Vesper agreed. "It doesn't."

The *Lachrymose* turned her bow toward the dark between worlds, and the silence closed around her like water, and somewhere far out along the edge of dead space, in a corridor that led nowhere, something was already drifting toward a port that had stopped believing anything ever came back.
