## 1. The Last Tenant

**Archive Log — Meridian Station, Cycle 4,112. Day 61 of the local year.**

My name is Cyrus Crane, and I am the archivist of Meridian Station. There is no one else to introduce me, so I will have to do it myself.

I begin every log this way. It is not vanity. It is procedure. An archive without a named keeper is just a pile of data, and data rots faster than memory if no one claims it. So: my name, my station, my duty. The words have worn a groove in me by now, the way water wears a groove in stone — not because the water is strong but because it never stops.

Meridian Station hangs at the edge of the Kestrel Drift like a coat someone forgot on a hook. That is not a metaphor I invented; it was written in the original survey report two hundred and eleven years ago, and I have kept it because it remains true in every particular. We are forgotten clothing. We are what the fleet left behind when it moved on to warmer rooms.

The population stands at forty-three as of this morning's census. Yesterday it stood at forty-three as well, which sounds like stability until you understand that yesterday's forty-three included Old Sefa Okonkwo, and this morning's forty-three includes a birth in the Vashti family line — a girl, named Bright, seven hours old and already louder than the reactor hum. One out, one in. The ledger balances. The ledger always balances. That is the cruelest thing an archive teaches you: how neatly loss can be made to look like bookkeeping.

I recorded both events today. A death certificate for Sefa, ninety-one years old, cause listed as *cardiac cessation, natural*. A birth registration for Bright Vashti, weight 3.1 kilograms, mother doing well. Two entries, forty seconds apart, filed under the same cycle. If you read only the index, Meridian Station looks immortal.

* * *

The station itself is dying in the slow way that large things die — not with failures but with omissions. The hydroponics bays on rings three through five went dark eleven years ago, sealed rather than repaired, because we could no longer spare the parts or the hands. Ring six flooded during a coolant rupture when I was nine — when *the original* was nine, I should say, though the distinction matters less every year — and nobody ever pumped it out. It sits down there still, a drowned deck of corridors, its windows green-black with algae bloom, home now to things I have never seen and cannot catalogue because I cannot go there.

The lights work. The air works. The gravity plating works, mostly, though ring two has developed a stutter that makes dropped pens fall in small hesitant arcs, as if even physics has grown uncertain of us. Forty-three people live in the working third of a station built for twelve thousand. You can walk an entire day through Meridian's corridors and meet no one. You can walk an entire day and hear nothing but your own boots and the ventilation breathing its long mechanical breath, in and out, in and out, patient as something waiting for us all to leave so it can finally rest.

I do that walk sometimes. Officially it is infrastructure inspection. Unofficially — well. An archivist is permitted her unofficial reasons. I walk the dead rings and I touch the nameplates on doors that haven't opened in decades. *Okonkwo. Reyes-Lin. Adeyemi-Voss.* Whole family lines that once filled these halls with noise, gone outward or gone under, and their names still bolted to the metal because removing them would require paperwork, and paperwork requires a reason, and no one has ever had a reason except me.

I am the reason. I am always the reason. That is the whole of my employment.

* * *

**Archive Log — Supplemental. Same cycle, evening.**

Let me tell you about the file, since I have put it off for six paragraphs now, and an honest log does not permit evasion.

There is a case in my archive that I did not collect. I inherited it the way you inherit a house with something buried in the yard — you don't get to choose whether it comes with the property. It is catalogued under CRANE, C. (ORIGINAL) — HOMICIDE, UNSOLVED, and it occupies exactly one drawer in the records vault, a thin drawer, because murder investigations on Meridian Station are rare enough that no one built shelving for them.

The original Crane — my original, my template, the woman whose face I wear and whose name I carry like a borrowed coat — was killed fourteen years ago in the sub-corridors beneath the old administrative wing. She was struck from behind with a blunt instrument that matched nothing in the fleet's weapon registries, nothing in the station's tool inventories, nothing anywhere. She died between one log entry and the next. Her final entry was routine: atmospheric readings, grain yields, a note about a flickering light in corridor 9-C. Then nothing. Then me.

They grew me eight months later. This is standard practice for certified archivists — continuity of record-keeping, the charter calls it, as though a person were merely a position to be refilled. I woke in the medical bay with fourteen years of her memories decanted into me up to a point eighteen months before her death, and everything after that point was silence. They never transfer the final memories of a murdered original. Some mercy, or some policy. I have never decided which.

So I know her childhood. I know her mother's voice, the smell of the seed-vaults, the way the station looked before ring six drowned. I know how to hold a stylus the way she held it. And I know that somewhere past the edge of my inherited memory, she walked into those sub-corridors carrying something worth killing for, and I will never know what it was unless I find it myself.

For the first few years I did not try. I told myself the file was closed, unsolved but archived, and that reopening it would be vanity — the clone playing detective with her original's ghost. I told myself my duty was forward-facing: births, deaths, harvests, the slow ledger of a dying colony. Duty is a very comfortable place to hide. I recommend it. It has room for everything you'd rather not feel.

But files do not stay quiet. That is the thing no one warns you about archives. Everything in them waits. Every unopened drawer is a held breath. And some nights, when the ventilation sighs and the gravity stutters and I am the only consciousness awake on a station of forty-three sleeping people, I stand in front of that thin drawer and I can feel it waiting the way the drowned decks wait below us — patient, certain, in no hurry at all, because it knows I will come down eventually.

Tonight I opened it. Just opened it. Read the first page. Photographs of a corridor I have walked a hundred times, lit wrong, angles unfamiliar. A report in a clerk's handwriting that trails off mid-sentence, as if whoever wrote it lost interest in justice around the same time everyone else did. Fourteen years, and no one has added a single page.

I closed the drawer. I logged this entry instead. See how dutiful I am.

* * *

**Archive Log — Cycle 4,112. Day 63.**

Two days of ordinary entries to report, and I will report them, because ordinary is the substance of an archive and I refuse to be an archivist who only records the dramatic.

Day 62: The Vashti matriarch brought me a jar of preserved plums from the last orchard tree on ring four, the one growing wild now through a crack in the ceiling panel, untended and improbably fertile. She set it on my desk without a word, looked at the murder file's drawer — it doesn't close flush anymore, the seal has warped, a hairline of darkness shows along its edge — and looked at me, and said, "You should let old things sleep, girl." Then she left. I have entered the plums in the inventory under *gifts, miscellaneous* and I have entered her sentence nowhere at all, though it has lodged in me like a splinter. How did she know what the drawer was? Everyone on this station knows what the drawer is. Fourteen years is long enough for a murder to become furniture.

Day 63: Repaired the environmental sensor array on ring two. Logged a minor pressure fluctuation in the water reclamation system. Interviewed Devi Okonkwo — Sefa's granddaughter, nineteen, sharp-eyed — for the oral history project I started three years ago, the one where I record the memories of the dying so the station keeps more than names. Devi talked for two hours about her grandmother's stories of the Before, the years when the fleet still came, when supply ships docked monthly and children learned fleet-standard navigation in school. Then the visits thinned. Then the curriculum changed. Then, twenty-two years ago, the last scheduled ship arrived carrying half its promised cargo and no explanation, and no ship has come since.

"Why did they stop coming?" I asked her, because it is the question at the center of every history I record, and no one has ever answered it.

Devi shrugged the shrug of someone raised inside a mystery. "Grandmother said the war took everyone's attention. But the war ended, didn't it? Grandmother said the station was written off as nonviable. But we're viable. We're right here." She turned her tea mug in her hands. "She said once — this was near the end — she said the fleet didn't forget us, Cyrus. Forgetting is passive. She said somebody *decided*."

I entered it all in the oral history file. I marked the segment *speculative* the way I'm supposed to. Speculation is not evidence. I am very good at telling myself things like that.

* * *

**Archive Log — Cycle 4,112. Day 65.**

Forty-three people. I keep returning to the number the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. When I was decanted, the population was sixty-one. The original knew a station of nearly two hundred. Somewhere in the vault there is a census from the golden years listing eleven thousand, four hundred and six souls, each one a row in a spreadsheet, each row once a whole human weather-system of wants and griefs. Now forty-three. The arithmetic of abandonment is simple and relentless: no new blood coming in, the young leaving on the rare unscheduled freighter that passes within hailing distance, the old going down into the ground of whatever passes for ground aboard a station — the recycling vats, actually, though we call it the Garden, and I have recorded enough funerary rites to know the euphemism comforts no one.

Sometimes I think about who will file my death entry. Because there will be one — the archive demands symmetry — and the answer is that no one will. If I die, the entry goes unfiled until someone finds my body, and by then the archive will have been dark for however long that takes, and a dark archive is just a tomb with better organization.

Unless they grow another me. Continuity of record-keeping. There are still templates in cold storage, still certification protocols, still a charter that treats a person as a post to be staffed. Sometimes I catch myself wondering whether the original felt this — this vertigo of being a function wearing a face — and whether it was worse for her, being the first draft, or whether it is worse for me, being the revision. Neither of us chose it. That is the one thing we share completely, she and I: neither of us applied for this life. It was issued to us, like a uniform, like a drawer in a vault.

And yet. Here is the confession I will bury in the middle of a routine log, where no one reading the index will ever think to look: I do not want to be a continuation. I want to be a person. I want my handwriting to drift from hers. I want opinions she wouldn't have had, tastes she never acquired, a life that would confuse anyone comparing our files. Every deviation I allow myself feels like theft and freedom at once, and I have begun, quietly, without announcing it to any oversight committee, to steal.

Small things. I take my tea bitter, where she took it sweet — I found that in her early logs and reversed it deliberately, the first act of rebellion in either of our lives. I have grown my hair longer than she ever wore it. I have started ending my logs with a personal line, which violates protocol section 12, subsection 4, which states that archive entries shall contain no editorial content. Consider this paragraph a violation, then. Consider this whole voice a violation, accumulating slowly, entry by entry, into someone.

* * *

**Archive Log — Cycle 4,112. Day 66. Evening.**

Today's entries: one equipment maintenance report, one ration inventory update, one addendum to Sefa Okonkwo's death certificate correcting her age from ninety-one to ninety-one years and four months, per family records. The station breathes. The ledger balances. All normal.

All normal, except that tonight I went back down to the vault, and this time I did not stop at opening the drawer.

I laid the file out on the reading table under the good lamp, all of it — forty-one pages, six photographs, one evidence bag containing a fragment of corroded metal that the original investigators tagged *unknown origin, non-fleet alloy* and then apparently never thought about again. Non-fleet alloy. On a station built entirely of fleet alloy, maintained exclusively with fleet parts, where every nut and rivet has a serial number traceable to a shipyard two centuries dead — and the weapon that killed the archivist was made of *nothing we have.*

Fourteen years ago someone stood in the sub-corridors holding a piece of metal that did not belong to our world, and swung it, and the entire apparatus of Meridian Station — the council, the clerks, the charter, the inertia of a dying colony too tired to ask questions — agreed to file it under *unsolved* and let the drawer close.

I sat with the photographs for a long time. In the background of the third one, half-blurred, there is a door. Not a station door. The frame is wrong, the material is wrong, and there is a marking beside it that I do not recognize — angular, deliberate, older-looking than anything else in the image. The investigators circled it in red ink and wrote one word in the margin: *unrelated.*

Unrelated. In a murder file, the word *unrelated* is not a conclusion. It is a door held shut.

I photographed the photograph. I logged the anomaly — flagged, cross-referenced, dated, my own hand on the entry, my own judgment behind it. First investigative action taken on this case in fourteen years, and it took me less than an hour, which means it took everyone else fourteen years of deciding not to.

Old Sefa's granddaughter said her grandmother believed somebody *decided* the fleet would forget us. The matriarch said to let old things sleep. The margin of a dead clerk's report says *unrelated.* Everyone on this station keeps telling me, in one dialect or another, the same sentence: *do not open that door.*

Tomorrow I am going to start looking for it anyway. Not because I am brave. Because I am the last tenant of this place, and the rent I pay is the truth, and I am beginning to suspect the truth has been in arrears for a very long time.

End of log. Cyrus Crane, archivist, Meridian Station. Population forty-three. Open questions: one.
