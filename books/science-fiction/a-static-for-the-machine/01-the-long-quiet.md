## 1. The Long Quiet

The colonies had stopped talking to each other the way a body stops talking to a limb that's been cut off — not all at once, but in stages, each severance a little quieter than the last, until the silence was total and you forgot there had ever been a sound.

Wren Montrose kept a list.

It wasn't an official list. The Commonwealth Bureau of Intercolonial Logistics kept its own, of course, all cargo manifests and tonnage quotas and the dry arithmetic of survival, but Wren's list was different. It lived in a battered slate he'd carried for eleven years, and it recorded not tonnage but voices. *Kestrel's Landing, last transmission: 4 years, 2 months. New Halvard, last transmission: 6 years, 11 months. The Meridian Reach colonies, all channels: 9 years.* He updated it every run, though there was almost never anything to update. The list only ever got quieter.

Tonight, with the *Cormorant* riding the long dark between folds, he read it the way other men read letters from home.

He was thirty-nine years old and he had been the Commonwealth's finest xenolinguist, which was a sentence people used to say to him at conferences, in the years when there were conferences, in the years when the Commonwealth still pretended the war was about territory. Then the Concord had cut the diplomatic channels — or the Commonwealth had, depending on which history you'd been issued — and a xenolinguist became a man with a beautiful instrument and nowhere to play it. The Bureau had reclassified him. *Civilian logistics contractor, priority routing.* He carried medicine to colonies that had stopped thanking anyone for it. He carried seed stock, fusion cells, the small unglamorous freight of people staying alive.

He did not carry messages, because there were no messages. That was the whole of the war now, in the Reach: not fighting, not out here, but silence. Two empires shouting at each other across a border neither would name, and the colonies in between learning to live like the deaf.

Wren had once catalogued forty-one distinct dialects of the Concord's trade-pidgin. He had written the paper that cracked the Concord's honorific system, the one still cited in every first-contact curriculum in the Commonwealth. He had spent six years of his life learning to think in a grammar that organized time as concentric circles rather than a line, and he had loved it — loved it the way you love a city you've lived in, with the specific ache of knowing you'll never quite belong.

Now the only Concord voices he heard were in his dreams.

* * *

He slept in the pilot's chair, strapped in, because the *Cormorant*'s bunk had become a place he avoided the way you avoid a room where someone died. In the chair, the dreams came anyway. They always came.

This one had the shape of water that remembers being a wave.

There were no words in it — that was the thing no one believed when he tried to explain, back when there had been people to explain things to. The language in his dreams had no words the way a river has no individual drops you could point to. It had *movements*. A meaning would arrive whole, structured, inflected, carrying tense and obligation and a quality he could only think of as *distance-from-truth* — the grammar marked how far a statement was from the speaker's certainty, in graded steps, like heat. And it would arrive not as a sentence but as a weather system, a pressure front of meaning moving through him, and he would understand it completely and be unable, on waking, to render a single syllable of it in any human tongue.

The dream-language had started four years ago, after the incident he did not let himself think about — the survey wreck, the Concord artifact field, the eleven days he'd spent alone with his suit radio dead and something vast and patient humming through the debris. The Commonwealth doctors had scanned him twice and found nothing. *Stress response,* they said. *Isolation artifact. The mind invents patterns.* They had never asked him what the patterns were *about*, because he could not have told them, and because asking would have required the Commonwealth to admit its finest xenolinguist had come back from a derelict speaking a language that appeared in no intercept, no archive, no captured Concord lexicon anywhere.

He had told himself it would fade. Languages you don't use fade. That was linguistics 101; he'd taught the module himself.

It was not fading. It was growing.

He knew this the way a farmer knows rain is coming — not from any single sign but from the whole pressure of the air. Three years ago the dream-language had been a vocabulary of perhaps two hundred movements. Now it was a weather system with seasons. Last month, for the first time, he had dreamed a *question*. He had woken with his heart hammering, because questions require an addressee, and a question in the dream-grammar was structured — he could feel the structure even now, in the daylight, like a stone in his shoe — as a *request for the other to complete the circle*. The language assumed a listener. The language assumed, with the serene confidence of a thing that had never been alone, that communication was the natural state of the universe and silence was the aberration to be explained.

Wren Montrose had spent nine years professionally cataloguing silence, and the language in his head considered silence a grammatical error.

He woke in the pilot's chair with salt on his face and did not remember crying, which was its own small horror. Grief without a memory of grieving. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand and pulled up the navigation display and did not think about the dream, and thought about nothing else for the next hour.

* * *

The *Cormorant* dropped out of fold at the edge of the Meridian-adjacent cluster on a Tuesday, if Tuesdays still meant anything out here. Six colonies, once a thriving little constellation — fishing the gas giants' upper decks, refining helium-3, trading, quarreling, marrying across stations the way small communities do. He made the rounds the way a doctor makes rounds.

Kestrel's Landing answered his hail on the third try, a woman's voice, tired, suspicious until he identified the cargo. Medicine. She softened by degrees. They talked for eleven minutes, mostly about the shipment, a little about the weather in the way spacers talk about weather — radiation fronts, harvest yields, the birth rate. At the end she said, "You're the first voice from outside in eight months," and then, before cutting the channel, so quietly he almost missed it: "Does anyone out there remember us?"

He said yes. It was the official answer. He did not know if it was true.

New Halvard didn't answer at all. He sat in orbit for six hours running hail after hail, watching the colony's lights cycle in their normal patterns — they were alive down there, or the automation was — and the comm channels gave back nothing but carrier static. He logged it. He added a line to the list: *New Halvard, last transmission: 7 years, 0 months.* The roundness of the number bothered him more than he could say. Seven years exactly. As if someone had drawn a line under it.

The third colony, Meridian-adjacent Station Voss, answered with a recorded message: *This channel is monitored. State your cargo, registry, and clearance. Do not attempt personal communication.* He complied. The cargo transfer was handled entirely by machine, dock clamps and drones and a cargo-master AI that spoke in the flattened tones of something designed to avoid the liability of personality. Wren had been a linguist long enough to hear the dialect of fear in it. Someone on Voss had decided that words were dangerous. That was what nine years of war did to a language community: not new words, but fewer. A grammar of suspicion, where every sentence was a customs declaration.

He was two days out of Voss, running the empty dark toward the last three colonies on his route, when the feeling started.

He had no better word for it than *the feeling*, though the dream-language had a movement for it — a slow convergence, a meaning approaching from a great distance the way a ship approaches, its signature growing. He'd felt it maybe four times in four years, always in the hours before something happened: the survey wreck, once, and a fold-anomaly near Kestrel that had nearly killed him, and twice for no reason he ever discovered. The language in his head was not decorative. It was not a scar. It was, he was increasingly unable to deny, a *sensory organ* — and it was telling him that something was coming.

He ran the diagnostics twice. Fold-drive clean. Reactor clean. Comms clean, which was almost funny — clean meaning empty, meaning the same beautiful terrible nothing the whole Reach had given him for nine years.

On the fourth watch he fell asleep in the chair again, and the dream came, and this time it was different, and the difference frightened him down to the marrow.

The dream-language had always been ambient — weather, pressure, the hum of a system he could not parse. This time it was *addressed*. A meaning arrived with his name in it — not *Wren*, the dream-language had no proper nouns, but the movement that meant *the-one-who-listens*, inflected with the concentric-circle tense that marked *now, and also nine years ago, and also not yet*. And beneath it, threaded through it like a wire through wax, was a second structure. Human-made. Encrypted. Old.

He could not read it. He could feel it, the way you can feel a letter through an envelope — its shape, its urgency, its weight of sealed intention — without being able to open it. Something out in the dark was carrying a message in a human code, wrapped in a grammar that only he had ever learned, and the language in his head had turned toward it the way a flower turns toward light.

He woke at 0347 ship-time with the name of a coordinate cluster already forming on his lips, numbers he had no reason to know, a patch of empty space on the charts between New Halvard and the fold-lanes — a region the Commonwealth had marked, in the bland euphemism of military cartography, as *inactive transit zone*.

He pulled up the charts. He stared at the coordinates. He checked them against his memory of the dream, and they matched, and his hands were shaking, and he was a rational man, a scientist, a man who had spent his life insisting that language was a human technology and meaning a human act.

The comm board pinged.

Not a hail. Not a distress call, not yet — he would tell himself later that it wasn't, at first, though the distinction was one only a linguist would have made. It was a signal without structure, a carrier wave with something riding it, and it came from the inactive transit zone, from the coordinates, from the place where nothing had been for nine years.

And it was repeating. And the repetition had a cadence.

Wren had spent his career listening to how beings spoke when they were afraid. Adults spoke in compressed bursts. Machines spoke in perfect intervals. But children — children who had learned to call for help in the dark, children who had not yet learned that no one might come — children spoke in a cadence that rose at the end, that left itself open, that asked the universe a question it could not answer.

The signal rose at the end of every cycle.

Wren sat in the dark of the *Cormorant*'s cockpit with his list of silenced colonies glowing on the slate beside him, and listened to a dead zone call out in a child's voice, and understood that the long quiet was over.

He set a course for the coordinates, and told himself it was because the signal might be a trap, and knew, in the grammar of his own heart, that it was because something out there had finally asked him a question in a language he could not refuse.
