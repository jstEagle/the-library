## 3. Halcyon's Quiet Ledger

The archive beneath the Council Hall had no windows, and Maren Sol had long ago stopped missing them.

She had her lamps instead — six of them, tuned to a warm amber that made the paper records look the way paper was supposed to look, honeyed and patient, as if the words on them had all the time in the world. Which was, she supposed, the point of an archive. Everything else in Halcyon hurried. The greenhouses ran their cycles, the air scrubbers counted their revolutions, the children grew and forgot and grew again, but down here the founding generation sat in its boxes and binders and sealed canisters, waiting to be read by someone with clearance and courage, and there were very few of either.

Maren had both. She was not sure which one she regretted more.

She ran her thumb along the spine of the day-log for Founding Year Two and did not open it. She knew what was inside it. She had read it once, at thirty-one, alone, on the night her predecessor handed her the keys — actual keys, brass, absurdly analog for a colony that ran on fusion and fiber — and told her, *Some of this you will never show anyone, and that is not a burden, it is the job.* He had said it kindly. That was the worst part. Kindness made it stick.

Forty years of days since then, and she had kept the ledger quiet. Not locked — locks invited questions — just quiet. Shelved correctly. Cross-referenced nowhere. The kind of silence that looked like order from the outside and felt like holding your breath from within.

Her terminal chimed. A request form, routed up from the visitor wing.

*Eriksen, R. — claims investigator, Vesper Line. Requesting: environmental allocation records, Halcyon district four, past ninety days.*

Maren read it twice. Then she set down her tea, which had gone cold anyway, and pulled up the access log for the public records tier, because Rafael Eriksen would not have filed a request without first trying the front door, and the front door's hinges always told her where people had been.

He had been busy. Three days on-world, and he had already touched the greenhouse failure report — of course, that was his claim, the dead crop worth a fleet pension, the reason the Vesper Line had put him ashore at all — but after the greenhouse report he had gone sideways, the way good investigators go sideways. Power grid load histories. Maintenance rotation schedules for district four. And this morning, before the sun cleared the ridge, he had stood in the public reading room for two hours with the *Colony Charter, Annotated Edition*, the one printed for schoolchildren, and she only knew because the reading room's attendance log was public too, and Maren Sol read logs the way other people read letters from home.

Annotated Edition. The children's version. The one where the founders' disagreement was described, in language approved by three successive councils, as *a philosophical difference, resolved amicably before landfall.*

Rafael Eriksen was checking the children's version against something. Against the power draw under district four, most likely, or against whatever the children had been saying about their dream — because yes, she had heard about the dream, everyone had heard about the dream, ten-year-olds did not keep secrets so much as broadcast them, and a shared nightmare of a burning orbital was exactly the kind of thing that traveled through a colony of eleven thousand like smoke through cloth.

Maren closed the access log and sat very still in her amber light.

*Not yet,* she thought. *You are pulling threads, Mr. Eriksen, and threads are all you have found so far. But I know what is woven into this fabric, and if you pull much harder, you will find it, and then neither of us gets to decide anymore.*

She drafted her reply to his request with the care of a woman defusing something.

*Investigator Eriksen — Environmental allocation records for district four are held at the restricted tier, pending Council review per Statute 12.4 (records touching critical infrastructure). Your request has been forwarded. In the interim, I'd be glad to assist you personally. The archive keeps regular hours, and tea is available. — M. Sol, Keeper of Records.*

Statute 12.4 was real. She had checked it forty years ago, when she first learned how useful real statutes could be. It gave the Council seven days to review any request touching critical infrastructure, and critical infrastructure was defined broadly enough to include, if a keeper of records wished it to include, the ground under everyone's feet.

She sent it, and then she went upstairs to meet him, because courtesy delayed nothing and a face-to-face warning delayed everything.

* * *

The Council Hall's atrium caught the morning light and scattered it across polished basalt, and Rafael Eriksen stood in the middle of it looking exactly like what he was: a man between ships. Travel coat still on. Case bag slung crosswise, the way people carry things they expect to defend. He had the particular stillness of someone who has spent eleven days in transit and learned to be comfortable nowhere, and Maren recognized it because she had married one of his kind once, briefly, before Halcyon's gravity and Halcyon's silences wore him back to Earth.

"Keeper Sol." He offered his hand. His grip was dry and brief. "I appreciate the fast reply."

"I appreciate investigators who file requests instead of picking locks." She smiled. "Though I'm told your reputation includes both."

"My reputation," Eriksen said, "is mostly paperwork."

"So is mine. Perhaps we'll understand each other." She led him toward the small consultation room off the atrium, the one with the table and the recorder that everyone knew was decorative, and poured tea from the pot a page had left warming, and sat across from him with her hands folded, and began the conversation she had rehearsed on the stairs.

"You want district four's allocation records."

"The greenhouse failed," he said. "A crop worth a fleet pension died in nine hours. The environmental systems logged nominal operation the entire time. Either the sensors are lying or the water went somewhere else. I'd like to know which."

"And your professional instinct says?"

He looked at her. He had gray coming in at his temples and eyes that did arithmetic while they watched you, and Maren thought: *this is the man they send when the numbers don't close.* "My professional instinct," he said, "says the water went somewhere else. District four sits above a section of the old foundation grid that draws more current than its floor plan explains. I pulled the public utility summaries last night. There's a load profile down there that doesn't correspond to anything on any map I've been given."

"Old grids accumulate ghosts," Maren said pleasantly. "Sealed conduits, decommissioned relays, metering errors. Halcyon is two generations of improvisation built on top of itself. Half our history is literally buried, Investigator. We dig foundations and find the founders' plumbing."

"Mm." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. "That's actually what I wanted to ask you about. The founders. I read the charter yesterday. The annotated edition."

"The schools use it."

"It says the founding schism was a philosophical difference, resolved amicably before landfall."

"That is correct."

"It's also," Eriksen said, in the mild tone of a man reading a shipping manifest, "vague enough to mean anything. Resolved how? Amicably by whom? I've adjusted claims on three colonies now, Keeper Sol, and every one of them had a founding story, and every founding story had a seam in it where something got stitched over. Usually money. Sometimes bodies." He met her eyes. "Your seam isn't money. I can tell that much already, because if it were money, your council would have sold the story by now instead of sealing it."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the Hall's clock ticked through the quarter hour, and Maren let it finish ticking before she answered, because a pause, properly deployed, was the oldest instrument in the archivist's kit.

"Investigator," she said, "do you know why Halcyon licenses exactly one relay ship?"

"Monopoly economics. Single-route efficiency."

"Sentiment," Maren said. "The founders wanted a colony that could hear Earth without being shouted at by it. One ship, making the long route, carrying mail and medicine and — occasionally — men like you, who come to count our losses for us. The Vesper Line is not a supply chain, Mr. Eriksen. It is a volume knob. And we keep it low." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, palms down, a gesture she knew read as openness and was in fact the opposite. "We are a quiet colony. We earned our quiet. Whatever the founders disagreed about, they settled it, and they settled it privately, because some arguments are more dangerous than the thing they're about. Forty years of peace is the proof. You are here to price a dead crop. Take my advice as a colleague in the records trade: price the crop, file your claim, and let the dead keep their privacy. Ghosts in the grid are cheaper than ghosts in the family, and every colony needs a few of both."

Eriksen listened to all of it without blinking. When she finished, he nodded slowly, as if she had said something reasonable, and Maren felt the specific dread of watching a careful man file her words away as evidence rather than instruction.

"That's a very good speech," he said. "I mean that. I may quote it in my report — 'the colony values its quiet.' Adjusters love context." He stood, buttoned his coat. "One more thing, since we're colleagues. The children who play near the district-four greenhouse. They've been describing a shared dream. Burning orbital, a voice counting down. Has anyone brought that to the Council?"

"We're aware of it," Maren said. "Children share dreams the way they share colds. Proximity and suggestion."

"Sure." He slung his case bag. "Except the countdown number changes, Keeper Sol. Night to night, child to child, it counts *down*. Suggestion doesn't do arithmetic." At the door he paused, half-turned. "Thanks for the tea. I'll be around another few days. I try not to pick locks."

And then he was gone into the atrium light, and Maren sat alone with two cooling cups and understood that she had just done the worst possible thing, which was nothing at all. She had warned him, and warning was information, and information was fuel. A man like that didn't stop when you told him the fire was private. He started wondering what the fire was *for*.

* * *

That evening she took the lift down past the archive, past the sub-basement, to the landing where the corridor ended in a door that appeared on no schematic and opened to a key she wore on a chain under her clothes, next to the locket with her mother's picture, because her father had insisted the two should weigh the same.

She did not go in. She never went in anymore; going in was a debt she couldn't afford to keep servicing. She stood with her palm flat against the cold metal and listened.

Through the door, beneath the concrete, beneath forty years of the Council's careful quiet, came the sound she had heard twice in her life and never forgotten: a hum. Deep, steady, patient. The sound of machinery drawing megawatts in the dark, month after month, year after year, fed by rerouted lines and falsified allocations and a dead greenhouse's worth of stolen water, and doing nothing anyone could see.

Two hundred sleepers. Two banks of a hundred pods each, keyed to codes from two worlds that had stopped speaking, frozen mid-argument by a founder who couldn't choose a side and so chose to postpone the choosing indefinitely. Her father had helped seal that door. Her father had taught her the statutes that kept it sealed. Her father had died respected, and Maren had spent her whole career standing guard over the respect.

*Seven days,* she thought, leaning her forehead against the metal, feeling the hum come up through her skull like a second heartbeat. *Statute 12.4 buys you seven days, Mr. Eriksen. Seven days to decide whether a dead crop is worth more than a living lie.*

Somewhere above her, in a guest room in the administrative wing, she imagined the investigator spreading his papers under a lamp, turning his quarter-turns, doing his arithmetic. And somewhere below her, deeper than her hand could reach, the pods drew their stolen power and dreamed their slow, burning dream, counting down toward something no one on the Council had ever had the courage to name.

Maren took her palm off the door.

Then she went back up to her office, unlocked the restricted tier herself, and pulled the district four allocation records — not to give them to him, but to see, before he did, exactly how much of the truth the numbers would tell without her help.

They told him plenty. She could see it from where she stood.

She began, with hands that were almost steady, to rewrite the ledger.
