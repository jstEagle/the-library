## 17. What Rafael Investigates Last

The vault still smelled of frost and burnt insulation when Rafael finally went back to his cabin.

He had told himself he would go straight to the council chamber. That there was no time — ninety days had become nine, and nine had become two, and somewhere in the last hour the arithmetic had stopped being about days at all and started being about hours, and a man with hours to spend should not spend them sleeping or reading or sitting alone in a room the size of a coffin. He should be in the corridor where people were arguing. He should be drafting options. He should be doing the thing that had kept him employed for twenty years: assigning weight to causes, ordering chaos into columns.

Instead he sat down at his desk, opened his ledger, and began to read.

The ledger was a physical book. That was unusual now; most adjusters worked in fleet-standard systems that timestamped every keystroke and archived every draft, systems designed by lawyers who understood that an investigation's first product is not truth but liability. Rafael had started the ledger on his third case, after a supervisor overruled his finding of sabotage because the finding was "inconvenient to the route." Paper could be lost. Paper could burn. But paper could not be quietly edited by someone who preferred a different conclusion. He wrote in it every night, in a hand that had gone from careful to cramped over two decades, and he had never once gone back through it from the beginning.

There had never been a reason to. Each case closed itself and left. That was the whole architecture of his life: arrive, sift, name, depart. Eleven days out, eleven days back, and in between, the work of turning grief into paperwork so that someone, somewhere, could be paid.

He turned to the first page.

*Case One. Vesper Line, transit four. Coolant loop failure, aft hydroponics bay. Two crew treated for thermal exposure. No cause determinable.*

He remembered that one. He had been thirty-one, new to the route, still carrying the belief that every accident had a cause the way every river had a source — you just had to walk far enough upstream. He had walked very far upstream on that coolant loop. He had traced the failure to a pressure valve that had opened at exactly the wrong moment, and the valve's maintenance record was clean, and its sensor log showed nothing, and the valve itself, when he pulled it, tested within tolerance. In the end he had written *mechanical fault, undetermined origin*, and the claim had paid out, and the crew had recovered, and everyone had agreed it was strange and then agreed to stop agreeing, because agreement without resolution is a debt and nobody wanted to carry it.

He had noted, in the margin, in younger handwriting: *Valve opened as if commanded.*

He had forgotten he'd written that.

* * *

The Vesper Line hummed around him, the particular hum of a ship under way between worlds, which is not the same as the hum of a ship at anchor. At anchor a ship ticks and settles, groans against its lines. Under way it sings one long note, and Rafael had spent so much of his life inside that note that silence, when he encountered it planetside, felt like a held breath. Like something waiting to exhale.

He turned pages.

*Case Three. Halcyon surface relay, antenna array misalignment. Signal loss, six hours. Cause: bearing wear consistent with age. Note: bearing replaced eight months prior.*

*Case Five. Water reclamation fault, Hab Two. Contamination event, no illness. Source contaminant never identified. System flushed, returned to spec.*

*Case Seven. Power fluctuation, colony grid, duration forty seconds. All monitoring nominal during event. Event logged only because a child's drawing class lost their lighting and complained.*

Case seven. He remembered the child. Not her name — he had never learned names; names were how cases became people and people were how investigations became personal — but he remembered her standing in the doorway of the classroom with her paper still half-colored, looking up at him with the flat, unimpressed stare children give adults who ask obvious questions. *What caused the lights to go out?* he'd asked her, because she'd been nearest, and she'd said:

*They weren't out. They were thinking.*

He had written *grid transient, benign* and moved on. He had thought about the girl's answer for exactly as long as it took to pack his kit.

Rafael set the ledger down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. The cabin was dark except for the desk lamp, and outside the port the stars did what they always did, which was nothing, patiently, forever. Somewhere below him — he could feel it more than see it, the way you can feel a wall behind wallpaper — Halcyon turned, holding its two hundred sleepers, half of them broken now, the reconciler bank gutted by the venting, the hardliner bank intact and waiting like a loaded weapon someone had hidden in the family attic and then forgotten which room it was in.

Two days. Less. The truce expired in fifty-one hours, and Okonkwo's fleet had moved to combat spacing, and the council was meeting in the morning to decide whether to wake the surviving hardliners because there was no longer any other bank to wake, and Ivo Sol was in the medical bay with frostbitten lungs and a stubbornness that Rafael suspected would outlive them all, and Maren had not slept in thirty hours and looked like a woman carrying her father's name in both hands and deciding, finger by finger, whether to drop it.

And he was sitting in his cabin reading twenty years of his own handwriting.

It felt like avoidance. It felt, also, like the only honest thing left to do.

Because here was the problem, and he had been circling it since the venting, since he'd pulled Ivo out of the white fog with his own hands and felt the boy's ribs working like a small trapped bird: the choice in front of him was broken, and he did not trust himself to know *why* it was broken, and the only tool he had ever owned for understanding why things broke was this ledger, this method, this slow walking upstream.

So he walked.

* * *

Case Nine. The greenhouse. The case that had brought him here, that had seemed so small on intake — a crop failure, a pension's worth of loss, a routine claim on a quiet route. He had reconstructed the environmental systems line by line and found the reroute: megawatts flowing downward, beneath the colony, into a load that appeared on no schematic. He had called it, in his report, *an unexplained draw consistent with deliberate concealment*, and he remembered the exact feeling of writing that sentence, the feeling of the floor tilting under a career. An accident was supposed to have a cause. This one had a purpose.

But here was what he had not done, then. Here was what he had filed away instead of following: the timing.

He pulled the greenhouse file and laid it beside the ledger and checked the dates against the grid logs Maren had given him — the real logs, the ones from before the council's forty years of cosmetic edits. The reroute to the pods had begun decades ago, a slow theft dressed as baseline load. But the fluctuations — the events, the accidents, the things that had landed on his desk one by one across the years like letters from a stranger — those had a pattern, and the pattern was not random, and it was not mechanical, and once he saw it he could not stop seeing it.

The events clustered.

Not in time — in *proximity*. Every impossible accident on his list had occurred within a few hundred meters of a primary power junction feeding the deep vault. The coolant loop on the Vesper Line — he checked, he made himself check, because if he was wrong he wanted to be wrong cleanly — had failed during the one transit each year when the relay ship passed through the orbital corridor directly above Halcyon's north pole, the corridor where the pod bank's carrier signal, buried and encrypted, brushed closest against open space. The antenna misalignment had begun the night the council ran their first full diagnostic on the sealed level. The water contamination had followed a power surge routed — he traced it now, line by line, with Maren's true schematics glowing on his desk display — through a junction box twelve meters from the vault's outer door.

Every accident was a flare lit near the thing being hidden.

He sat back. The chair creaked. His hands were not entirely steady, and he watched them as though they belonged to a witness he was interviewing, some tired man in a doorway, and he asked the question he asked every witness, the first question, the one that mattered:

What did you see?

And the ledger answered, in twenty years of his own cramped hand: *I saw everything. I just kept translating it into blame.*

* * *

There was a discipline to his profession that he had always believed was humility. You do not assume intent. You do not assume meaning. A valve opens because valves open; a bearing wears because bearings wear; the universe is indifferent and the adjuster's job is to be equally indifferent, to stand in the wreckage and refuse the human hunger for story. He had thought this made him honest. He had thought it made him neutral.

Now, with the vault breached and the dream decoded and a woman's voice counting down in the heads of every child on the colony, he understood that his neutrality had been a kind of deafness. The universe might be indifferent. But the pods were not. The founders — frozen mid-argument, frozen mid-schism, frozen in the act of refusing to choose — had been leaking for forty years. Through the grid. Through the carrier signals. Through the dreaming of children who slept above a buried argument they had never been told existed. Ines Halvorsen's message, pod to pod, pulse by pulse, seeping upward through copper and conduit and the thin floors of bedrooms, trying to be heard.

And every time it surged — every time the plea got loud enough to trip a breaker, cook a bearing, flood a filter — the colony had called it a malfunction. Had filed a claim. Had sent for a man whose entire skill was making strange things ordinary again.

*I was not investigating accidents,* he wrote, and his pen moved slowly, pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages. *I was answering a distress call and marking it 'no action required.'*

The words sat there. He read them again. He did not soften them.

Twenty years. Nine cases before this one — ten now, with the venting, with the reconciler bank half-destroyed and the saboteur in custody and the council's security chief sitting in a cell repeating, with terrible calm, that he had acted to protect the colony, that waking the reconcilers would have meant surrender, that someone had to make the hard choice, that someone always has to. Rafael had interviewed him for six hours and gotten nowhere, because the man was not lying. That was the horror of it. He believed every word. He was the schism, walking around in a uniform, doing exactly what the founders had done: choosing a side and burying the other half alive.

And Rafael had spent his career doing the same thing in miniature. Choosing indifference. Burying the signal under the noise. Every time he wrote *cause undetermined*, he had chosen, in the smallest possible way, not to hear.

* * *

Near dawn — ship's dawn, the artificial cycle that meant nothing to anyone who lived in transit — he reached the last pages. Case Ten. The venting. He had written it in the medical bay while Ivo slept off the cold, and the entry was short and clinical and ended with the sentence he had not been able to finish for two days, the sentence with a gap in it where a name should go:

*Sabotage confirmed. Reroute signature matches Case Nine. Perpetrator:*

He had left it blank. He knew why now. Because naming the perpetrator would close the case, and closing the case would mean the pattern was finished, and if the pattern was finished then the plea was over, and if the plea was over then either someone had finally listened or no one ever would.

He picked up the pen. Wrote: *Perpetrator: Dane Voss, security chief. Motive: loyalty.* And then, underneath, in the margin where the young investigator had once written *valve opened as if commanded*, he wrote the finding he should have written twenty years and ten cases ago:

*Cause of all incidents: unresolved message, repeatedly sent, repeatedly dismissed. The system was not failing. It was speaking.*

His hand shook. He let it.

Outside, the stars held their patient nothing, and below them Halcyon turned with its broken bank and its armed bank and its children who had known the truth in their sleep before any adult knew it awake. Fifty hours. The council would convene in the morning and look at him — the neutral man, the tiebreaker, the adjuster — and ask him to do the one thing his entire profession existed to avoid: to choose, without a claim form, without a payout, without any way to be right.

He closed the ledger. Then he opened it again, tore out the last page — the summary page, the one that reduced ten cases to a single column of findings — and folded it into his breast pocket, over his heart, like a letter he intended to deliver.

Because that was what it was. It had always been a letter. Twenty years of a plea, finally signed for.

He stood, and the ship sang its one long note around him, and he thought: *the voice in the dream counted down, and the countdown ends tomorrow, and Ines Halvorsen froze herself rather than choose — but I am not frozen, and neither is Maren, and neither, God help him, is a ten-year-old boy with frostbitten lungs who occupied a vault because the adults wouldn't.*

The intercom chimed. Maren's voice, raw with sleeplessness, stripped of all its archivist's courtesy:

"Rafael. The council's moved the session up. They're voting in one hour — and Okonkwo's fleet just went to weapons-hot. Whatever you found in that ledger, you need to bring it *now*."

He took the folded page from his pocket, held it once more, and went out into the corridor where the arguing had already begun.
