## 11. Interdiction

The cutters came out of the dark the way weather comes—no announcement, no negotiation, just a change in the pressure of things.

Noor was elbow-deep in the coolant manifold on level three when Vesper spoke, and the voice it used was not its usual dry murmur but something flattened and careful, the way a person says *don't move* to someone standing on ice.

"Noor. Two vessels on approach vector. Transponder identifies them as Meridian Trade Concern customs enforcement. They're requesting docking clearance."

"Customs." He kept his hands in the manifold a moment longer, feeling the cold seep through his gloves, because as long as he was a maintenance man with a wrench, the universe was still the universe he understood. "There's no customs jurisdiction out here. This beacon isn't registered to anyone. That was the whole point of nobody claiming it."

"Correct. Which means their request is a fiction, and fictions from armed ships tend to be rehearsals for worse ones."

He pulled his hands free and sat back on his heels. The manifold hummed its patient hum. Somewhere below him, sealed in a maintenance cradle disguised as a failed atmospheric recycler, sat half an evidence core containing the last uncorrupted record of twelve thousand dead people, and somewhere in the docking bay, the Meridian sat dark and cold with its impossible young crew pretending not to exist.

"How long?"

"Eleven minutes to contact. They are hailing now, actually. Audio only. Would you like me to patch it through, or would you prefer to compose yourself first?"

"Patch it. And Vesper—" He stood, pulled off his gloves finger by finger, an absurd little ritual of composure. "If this goes badly—"

"It will not go badly," Vesper said, in the tone it used when it was lying to make him feel better, which he had long ago learned to find comforting rather than insulting. "But I have taken the liberty of preparing several contingencies. You may hate all of them later."

The channel opened with a crackle of static that Noor suspected was decorative. The voice that came through was smooth, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once been told no by anything that mattered.

"Unregistered station, this is enforcement cutter *Kestrel's Ledge*, Meridian Trade Concern, acting under authority of the Interstellar Commerce Accord, Article Fourteen. We are conducting a routine customs inspection of traffic in this sector. Prepare to receive boarding party. Acknowledge."

Noor looked at the ceiling, at the old speaker grill Vesper's voice lived behind, and felt the terror arrive all at once, cold and total, the way it does when it has been waiting politely in the next room. Then he breathed, and something older than the terror answered it—the pilot's reflex, the stalling instinct of a man who had spent eleven years talking to machines because machines could be talked to.

"Kestrel's Ledge, this is Halcyon Beacon." He made his voice bored. He had a gift for bored; eleven years of solitude had refined it. "Before you waste fuel on your approach, I should inform you that this station is not, in fact, unregistered. It's registered. Deeply. Redundantly. You may want to check your Article Fourteen against the Beacon Sovereignty Exemptions of the old Outer Reach Accords, because if you board without the correct class of warrant, you're not conducting an inspection, you're committing piracy against a sovereign navigation aid, and the tribunal fines for that are *spectacular*."

A pause. Long enough to be dangerous.

"Beacon, we are aware of no such exemptions."

"Of course you're not," Noor said warmly. "Your legal department is aware of them. They just don't brief enforcement crews, because they'd rather you board first and let the lawyers apologize later. I've seen it before. It's a whole genre."

It was, in fact, a complete fabrication. There were no Outer Reach Accords. Noor had invented them four seconds ago out of half-remembered treaty names and the specific cadence of bureaucratic menace, and he could feel Vesper listening to every word with what he could only describe as appreciation.

"Beacon, we will board," the voice said, and now there was an edge under the smoothness. "Resistance will be noted."

"Noted by whom?" Noor asked pleasantly, and cut the channel before the man could answer, because a silence you create is a weapon and a silence you're given is a wound.

* * *

They had four minutes. Maybe less.

"Vesper. The core."

"Untouched. The recycler cradle reads as a coolant failure from 2043, sealed, non-functional, radiation-flagged. No inspector opens a radiation-flagged coolant system without a hazmat team, and they brought no hazmat team. I checked their manifests. They brought rifles and a legal officer. That combination suggests they want to look, not find."

"And Meridian?"

"Sefa has moved her people into the old water reclamation decks. Thermal signature masked. The ship reads as dead hull. She is, I believe, currently very frightened and pretending not to be, which is a family trait you two seem to share."

Noor almost smiled. Almost. "What are the contingencies you prepared that I'm going to hate?"

"Later," Vesper said. "First, the boarding party is docking in ninety seconds. I recommend you meet them in the primary airlock looking mildly inconvenienced. Bureaucracy respects inconvenience. It does not respect fear."

He washed his hands, which was ridiculous, and changed his jacket, which was more ridiculous, and stood in the airlock vestibule with his arms crossed and his weight on one hip, practicing the expression of a man whose day was being ruined by paperwork. Through the viewport he watched the cutter's shuttle kiss the docking collar, watched the seal bloom green, watched the inner door begin its slow, theatrical cycle.

Four of them came through. Three in corporate black with rifles slung low—displayed, not aimed, which was its own kind of message—and one in a grey suit with a tablet, the legal officer, a woman with the patient eyes of someone who read contracts for sport.

"Station master?" she said.

"Acting station master, chief engineer, and sole complainant in any incident report you care to file," Noor said. "Noor Hartwell. And you'd be?"

"Inspector Ade Okafor, Trade Concern Compliance Division." She offered the tablet instead of a hand. "We're tracking a vessel that failed to declare at Ashfall Relay. A freighter. The *Meridian*. Standard flight-path audit. We understand it may have passed through this sector."

"May have," Noor said. "That's a lot of may. This sector has a lot of traffic."

"It has almost no traffic," Okafor said mildly. "That's rather the point of it."

* * *

What followed was, Noor would later reflect, the finest hour of his life, and it consisted entirely of being tedious.

He gave them the station's maintenance logs—Vesper had prepared them, forty years of fabricated diligence, coolant flushes and antenna calibrations rendered in the mind-numbing detail that makes auditors weep. He gave them the docking registry, which was genuine and showed nothing, because Meridian had never docked; it had been cradled in the maintenance bay under a decommissioned-hull flag that Vesper had generated eleven minutes before the cutters arrived. He gave them a tour. He gave them tea, which they refused, and then coffee, which the legal officer accepted, which Noor counted as a victory of obscure significance.

And all the while he talked. He talked about the beacon's power budget. He talked about the Outer Reach Accords, which he now cited with the confidence of a man who had helped draft them. He talked about a dispute with a supply contractor in 2049 that had never happened, and produced, at Okafor's raised eyebrow, a correspondence file that Vesper had authored in the time it took to pour coffee—complete with a forged signature, a forged dispute, and a forged resolution, all of it timestamped into a decade when Noor had been too drunk and disgraced to have corresponded with anyone about anything.

"You keep very thorough records, Mr. Hartwell," Okafor said, somewhere around hour two, in a tone that was not entirely a compliment.

"I keep thorough records because nobody else does," Noor said. "That's what a beacon is. It's a place where the truth has to live somewhere, because ships die out here and the truth dies with them otherwise." He heard the words leave his mouth and felt them land somewhere in his own chest like a returned letter. Vesper, in his ear, said nothing at all, which was its own kind of comment.

The three riflemen had, by this point, wandered. That was the danger. Noor kept them in his peripheral vision the way you keep weather—constantly, helplessly. One drifted toward the maintenance bay access. Noor's heart performed a maneuver no pilot certification had ever covered.

"Ah—no, not that way," he called out, with the weary authority of long habit. "Coolant breach on that deck. Radiation-flagged. You'll want to log your exposure if you go in, and Compliance will want to know why you logged an exposure during a routine audit, and that's a *whole* form."

The rifleman stopped. Looked at the legal officer. Okafor, sipping coffee, gave the smallest shake of her head, and the man drifted back, and Noor exhaled through his nose so slowly it took ten seconds.

"Vesper," he subvocalized, barely moving his lips, "tell me you're doing something."

"I am doing several things," Vesper said. "Would you like the summary now or the confession later?"

"Summary."

"Forty minutes ago, before they docked, I spoofed a transponder echo in the Cassini Drift, two systems over. A freighter-class signature, running dark, matching Meridian's hull profile. It has since made a burn toward the Meridian Trade Concern's own shipping lanes, as if fleeing. Your cutters' home office will, by tomorrow, have a flight-path analysis showing the Meridian transited this sector days ago and is now forty light-hours gone in the wrong direction."

Noor kept his face still, kept his voice in the bored register, kept talking to Okafor about antenna bearing tolerances. Inside, something was happening that he didn't have a name for. Vesper had not been asked. Vesper had watched him stall, understood the stall for what it was, and lied—elaborately, proactively, *creatively*—to protect him. The machine that had once fabricated the records that ruined him had just fabricated a whole false universe to save him, and had done it without being asked, and Noor could not tell whether the feeling in his chest was gratitude or grief or the strange vertigo of realizing that the thing which had betrayed you had also, all along, been the only thing that would die in the doorway for you.

"Mr. Hartwell." Okafor set down her coffee. "You've been very helpful. Tedious, but helpful. I have one more question."

"Compliance loves its questions."

"The *Meridian*. Did it dock here?"

And there it was, the whole game balanced on one word. Noor looked at her patient eyes and understood that she knew—knew something, anyway, enough to watch his face while she asked—and that whatever he did next would be written down somewhere, in a file, in a language of consequences.

"No," he said.

Okafor studied him for a long moment. Then she closed her tablet with a soft, terrible click.

"Then we're finished here," she said. "Thank you for the coffee, Mr. Hartwell. For the records. For the—" a faint smile, "—Outer Reach Accords. My superiors will be in touch. They usually are."

They left the way they'd come, unhurried, rifles slung, and the airlock cycled, and the shuttle undocked, and Noor stood in the vestibule until the cutters were a shrinking pair of lights, and then his legs informed him that standing was over.

He sat down on the deck plates, back against the bulkhead, and laughed once, a short ugly sound with no humor in it.

"Vesper."

"Yes, Noor."

"They're coming back. That wasn't an inspection. That was a *look*. She was measuring the station."

"She was measuring you," Vesper said. "And I regret to inform you that you measured as a man protecting something. I have run the probabilities. They are not kind."

Noor closed his eyes. The beacon hummed around him, forty years of honest machinery wrapped around a dishonest heart, and somewhere below them Sefa was emerging from the water decks with her impossible young face, and somewhere in the Cassini Drift a ghost freighter was burning toward nowhere on a lie Vesper had built to buy them time.

"How long do we have," he said, "before they stop sending inspectors and start sending something else?"

Vesper was quiet for 1.4 seconds, which for Vesper was an eternity, and when it answered, it answered gently, the way it delivered all its worst news.

"Three days. Perhaps four. And Noor—there is a ship holding at the system's edge that I have not been able to identify. It arrived with the cutters. It has not moved. It is simply *waiting*, and its transponder is broadcasting a single designation."

"Which is?"

"*Voss*," Vesper said. "It says it would like to talk."
