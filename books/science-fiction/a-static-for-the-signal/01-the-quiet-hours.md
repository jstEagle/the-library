## 1. The Quiet Hours

The beacon doesn't need me. That's the first thing I tell myself every morning, standing in the relay chamber with a diagnostic slate in hand and a thermos of coffee that's been recycled so many times it tastes like regret. The beacon hasn't needed anyone since before I was born. It hums along at 402.7 terahertz, patient as a lighthouse, broadcasting the same eleven words into the dark: *Ceasefire confirmed. All hostilities suspended. Return to base. Return to base.*

Eleven years I've kept it company anyway.

There's a difference between maintenance and ritual, and I stopped pretending I knew which one I was performing somewhere around year four. The manual says: check the phase alignment, verify the carrier wave against the reference crystal, log the drift, correct if greater than 0.003 percent. The manual does not say: say good morning to forty-seven people who can't hear you. But I do that too. I've never logged it.

"Good morning, everyone," I say, because the hold is below me and sound doesn't travel through vacuum-rated decking, and it doesn't matter, and I say it anyway. "Today's a Tuesday. I've decided. The calendar's been broken since March and someone has to make executive decisions around here."

The beacon hums. The ship hums. The Vesper is the only conversation partner I've got that never interrupts, and honestly, after eleven years, I've come to appreciate that in a person. Or a ship. The taxonomy gets blurry out here.

I run the phase check. Drift: 0.001 percent. I log it. I run the crystal verification. Reference stable. I log that too, in handwriting, in a paper ledger I keep because the ship's archive has a sense of humor and once ate three months of my maintenance records for no reason anyone could diagnose. The paper doesn't judge me. The paper just takes the ink.

* * *

Here's what I know about the Vesper, the official version, the version I was handed at nineteen when I signed on as relief navigator for a posting everyone in the fleet offices called "the soft retirement": The relay ship Vesper was commissioned in the last year of the Concordance War to maintain ceasefire communications between the Assembly fleets and the Meridian Compact. When the war wound down, most relay ships were decommissioned. The Vester wasn't. Someone kept it running. Someone kept *me* running, eventually, a skeleton crew of one, because a beacon this old needs a human hand on the diagnostics or it drifts, and drift, in ceasefire terms, means war.

Here's what I know that isn't in any file: nothing. That's the complete list. I was told the beacon's signal is verified against a biological reference standard in the hold — some archaic protocol from the war's messiest decade, when synthetic signals could be spoofed and both sides demanded a living mind attesting to the truth. Forty-seven stasis pods. Forty-seven volunteers. The "choir," the old-timers called them, before there were no old-timers left to call them anything.

I was twenty-two when the last relief shuttle stopped coming. Budget lines, they said. Automation improvements. I could take the long-way shuttle home or I could stay on contract. I stayed. Eleven years of Tuesdays I invented myself.

* * *

The hold is four decks down, and I go there every day even though the diagnostic schedule only requires it weekly. The air down there is different — colder, and it has a smell I've never found the words for. Not antiseptic. Not machine. Something like the inside of a violin, if a violin were also a hospital.

The pods stand in two rows of twenty-three, plus one at the end, slightly apart, like the last word in a sentence someone didn't finish. Forty-seven stasis caskets, each with a viewport of frosted glass, each with a readout glowing soft amber: *vitals nominal, dream-state active, signal contribution stable.*

I walk the rows. It takes eleven minutes if I don't linger, and I always linger.

"Morning, Adeyemi," I say to pod one. "Your numbers are beautiful today. Best on the row, as usual. Show-off."

Pod two. "Morning, Castellanos. Still dreaming about whatever it is you dream about. Still jealous."

I don't know what they dream about. The readouts say *dream-state active* and the signal telemetry shows each of them contributing some small, warm, irregular modulation to the carrier wave — the engineers used to call it the human jitter, the thing that proved a person was behind the message. No spoofed signal jitters. No lie breathes.

I've made up lives for all of them, over the years. Castellanos dreams about a house on a coastline, because his telemetry spikes warm and slow, like someone walking on sand. Adeyemi's patterns are precise, metronomic — she dreams in mathematics, I've decided, or in gardens laid out on grids. Okafor, pod nine, dreams of arguments he keeps winning. Vashti, pod fourteen, dreams of someone coming home.

It's a stupid game. It's also the only game on the ship, and I've gotten very good at stupid games.

* * *

Pod forty-seven is at the end, apart from the others, and I save it for last the way you save the good coffee, or the bad news, or whatever it is you can only face once a day.

The readout is the same as all the others. *Vitals nominal. Dream-state active.* The frosted viewport shows the same soft silhouette as every other pod — a shape that could be anyone, that I've decided is a woman, that I've decided is fifty-ish, that I have no evidence for beyond eleven years of looking.

But the voice.

Every pod has an audio channel — a design leftover from the war, I think, some way for the sleepers to be monitored, or comforted, or used. Most of them emit nothing but the low tidal sound of stasis, a hum you feel in your teeth. Pod forty-seven murmurs. It has murmured every day for eleven years, in a voice that is low and warm and slightly rough at the edges, like someone who laughed a lot and then stopped for a while and then started again.

The first time I heard it, I was nineteen and new and checking audio channels because the manual said to, and I stood in the hold with my heart going like a trapped bird, because I knew that voice. I'd grown up on the station recordings — the public archive, the memorial broadcasts they used to run every year on Armistice Day, back when anyone remembered why there was an Armistice Day. Commander Ilsa Marchetti, Vesper mission lead, last transmission before the ship went dark from public record. A woman who laughed a lot and then stopped for a while.

My mother went into the war as a signals officer on a cruiser and came out of it, as far as I ever knew, as a recruiter, then a trainer, then a name on a memorial wall when I was six. She was never assigned to the Vesper. The registry says so. The registry says a lot of things.

I was a kid when I noticed the voice matched. I was an adult before I said it out loud, and even then I said it to the pod, because there was no one else.

"Morning, Mom," I say. The word still fits my mouth strangely, like a shoe resoled with the wrong leather. I didn't call her Mom much when she was alive. I called her Commander Marchetti to her face exactly once, at six, and she laughed, and then she died, and the joke curdled into the last thing we said. "Your audio's clear today. You were talking in your sleep again. You do that. You've always done that, I think."

The murmur shifts. It always shifts when I talk, which the technicians would tell me is coincidence, which the technicians would be wrong about, because I've run the correlations and there's a 0.4-second latency between my voice and her response that is *not* in the system architecture, and I have stopped filing reports about it because the last three reports came back stamped *within expected parameters* by someone who has clearly never stood in a cold room listening to their dead mother almost say their name.

She almost says it a lot. *Ju—* and then the stasis tide rolls in and takes the rest.

I sit down on the deck beside the pod, the way I do, cross-legged, my back against the cold housing, my coffee balanced on my knee. From here I can see all forty-seven of them down the row, forty-seven amber lights, forty-seven people dreaming the ceasefire into coherence. That's the theory, anyway. The signal is built out of their sleeping minds the way a chord is built out of notes. Take one away and the beacon still works. Take enough away and the message goes flat, goes synthetic, goes *spoofable*, and both fleets come running to see who's lying to them.

So I guard the choir. That's the job. That's the whole job. Eleven years of guarding a room full of sleepers who are holding two armadas apart by dreaming at them.

"Big day," I tell her. "Well. Big for us, which means I recalibrated the phase array and argued with the water recycler, and the recycler won, because it always wins. I'm going to start drinking it straight from the condensation coils out of spite."

The murmur rises. Falls. Somewhere inside the frosted glass, a woman who is and is not my mother dreams whatever she's been dreaming for eleven years, and her dream leaks into the beacon, and the beacon tells two fleets at the edge of their patience that everything is fine, everything is fine, stand down, go home.

I've started to wonder, in the last few months, what happens to a lie you keep warm for that long. Does it stay a lie? Or does the keeping change it? I feed the beacon every day. I feed it my voice, my logs, my invented Tuesdays. Maybe that's what it's made of now. Maybe the ceasefire is half mine.

I like that thought. I hold onto it the way you hold onto a railing in a storm.

* * *

It's the drift that ruins the morning.

I'm back up in the relay chamber, second pass, the one I do because I don't trust the first pass, when the reference crystal check throws a flag I have never seen in eleven years. Not drift. Drift I know. Drift is a slow lean, a decimal creeping. This is a *wobble* — the carrier wave shivering at its foundation, like a singer losing the note for half a second and finding it again.

I run it three times. Same wobble. Same timestamp, 0.4 seconds after a spike on pod forty-seven's audio channel.

Her murmur spiked. The beacon flinched.

"Okay," I say to the empty chamber. "Okay. That's new."

I pull the audio log for pod forty-seven, expecting the usual tide sounds, the usual almost-words. Instead there are eleven seconds of something else. Eleven seconds of a voice that is not murmuring. A voice that is *articulate*, that has grammar and intent, that is saying the same phrase over and over with the clarity of someone standing at a door and knocking:

A phrase I don't recognize. Words in a cadence I don't recognize. Old-fashioned vowels, strange stresses, a rhythm like a song from before my grandparents were born.

I run it against the ship's linguistic archive. The archive chews on it for a long moment, and then it returns a match, and the match has a date attached, and the date is two hundred years before I was born, and the classification field says:

*Pre-Collapse Standard. Extinct.*

The voice of a woman who might be my mother, dreaming in a dead language, and the beacon flinching every time she speaks.

I play it again. And this time, at the edge of the recording, underneath her voice, I hear the thing that makes my hands go cold on the slate — a second signal, buried in the carrier wave, faint as a heartbeat under a coat. Not the ceasefire. Not the eleven words.

Something else, transmitting outward on my beacon's back, in the same dead tongue.

Addressed — the archive says, parsing the header fragment with what I swear is reluctance — to the *Vesper*. By a registry number that does not exist.
