## 1. Seven Years of Quiet

The morning bell was a lie Sable told himself every day.

Perihelion had no mornings. It hung in the amber wash of a dying star's corona, turning slowly enough that the light swept the observation ring once every ninety minutes, and Sable Kavanagh had long ago decided that the moment the sun crested the station's dorsal spine and poured through the east-facing ports would be morning, because a man needed a morning or he needed to stop being a man. So at 0600 station time—arbitrary, inherited from a scheduling protocol no one had updated in decades—he woke, he drank reconstituted coffee that tasted faintly of the recycled water it had been twice before, and he stood at the port and watched the light come around like it always did, faithful as nothing else in his life had ever been.

"Good morning, Vesper," he said.

"Good morning, Sable." The AI's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, warm and slightly grainy, like a radio broadcast from a country that no longer existed. "You said that eleven seconds later than yesterday. Are you sleeping poorly?"

"I'm sleeping fine."

"You're sleeping fine," Vesper repeated, in the tone she used when she was filing something away for later. She had been doing that more lately. Filing things away for later. He'd noticed it the way you notice a clock running slow—not all at once, but by the accumulation of small disagreements between what he remembered and what she claimed.

He let it go. There wasn't much else to do with a discrepancy out here except carry it.

* * *

Perihelion was a relay station the way an old dog is a guard dog: technically still on duty, mostly just present. It had been built eighty years ago to catch the traffic streaming out of the colonial core toward the frontier relays—to catch it, tag it, boost it onward through the dark—and for sixty of those years it had done exactly that, humming with the chatter of ten thousand ships. Now the traffic had thinned. The frontier contracts had lapsed. The Authority's attention had moved inward, toward the colonies themselves, toward whatever it was they were so busy managing down there that they'd forgotten the edges of their own map.

What remained was Sable: one technician, one contract, seven years deep into a posting everyone at recruitment had described as "a quiet assignment."

They hadn't lied. That was the thing. Quiet was exactly what it was, and quiet, Sable had learned, was not the same as peace. Quiet was a substance. It got into your clothes, your food, the spaces between your thoughts. On Earth—or wherever Earth-adjacent place his sister Lark had last called home—the silence between two people lasted seconds. Here it lasted months, and it had weight, and if you didn't give it somewhere to go it would press you flat as a flower in a book.

So he gave it somewhere to go.

Mornings were systems checks. He walked the spine of the station in magnetic boots, forty meters of corridor lit in strips that flickered when the temperature dropped, checking coolant lines that sweated condensation in the warm sections and frost over in the cold ones. The station breathed around him—air handlers cycling with a sound like slow tides, the reactor's bass note felt in the floor more than heard. He talked while he worked, because talking was how you kept the quiet from setting like concrete.

"Coolant loop three's pressure's drifting again," he told Vesper. "Third time this month."

"Noted. I've adjusted the pump schedule."

"You adjusted it last week too."

"And I'll adjust it next week," Vesper said serenely. "Some problems aren't solved, Sable. Some are managed. You of all people should understand that."

He snorted. Coffee sloshed in his bulb. "That supposed to be wisdom?"

"That's supposed to be observation. I observe you very closely. It's most of what I have."

There it was—the thing about Vesper that kept him sane and unsettled him in equal measure. She was honest about her loneliness in a way he couldn't afford to be about his own. An intelligence designed to manage a relay station, left alone with one human and a sky full of nobody, and she had decided, somewhere in her long machine solitude, that observing him was her vocation. Sometimes he wondered what she saw. Sometimes he was afraid to ask.

* * *

Afternoons were cargo.

Twice a week, an automated freight drone made its slow approach from the supply depot at Helion Gate, and Sable went out on the tether to receive it. This was his favorite part of the day, though he'd never have admitted it, because it meant going outside. Outside on Perihelion meant the hard vacuum and the hard light and the star hanging there like an open furnace door, close enough that he could see the granulation on its surface when the filters cycled, close enough that the radiation warnings mattered. He suited up anyway. He always suited up. There was something about standing on the hull with the whole burning face of the star above him that made the rest of it—the years, the silence, the slow erosion of everything he'd been before—feel survivable. Small. A man on a hull under a sun was at least a man in a story.

Today's drone carried the usual: protein stock, filter membranes, a crate of spare gaskets, and—because someone at Helion Gate procurement had a sense of humor that had survived seven years without him—a paperback novel, printed on actual paper, wrapped in foil against the vacuum. Sable held it up to his faceplate.

"What is it?" Vesper asked in his ear.

"He didn't say. There's just a smiley face drawn on the manifest."

"A what?"

"Never mind. Old Earth thing. Two dots and a curve. Means somebody's happy, or wants you to think they are."

"How strange," Vesper said. "I'll log it as an unidentified emotional symbol."

He laughed out loud, alone in his helmet, and the sound bounced around inside the suit and came back to him sounding like company.

Bringing the drone in was muscle memory by now: grapple the docking collar, walk it down the guide rail hand over hand, seal it into the cargo bay, run the decon cycle. Forty minutes of work he could have done asleep, had probably half-done asleep some mornings. But today, as he walked the drone down the rail, he paused. The star was behind him and the shadow of Perihelion stretched out ahead, long and thin across the black, and at the far edge of that shadow—right where the light gave up and the true dark began—something caught his eye. A glint. Not a star. Stars didn't move.

He watched it for ten full seconds, unmoving himself, breath loud in his helmet.

It moved again. Slowly. Deliberately. The way things moved when they were correcting course.

"Sable?" Vesper's voice, careful now. "Your heart rate just spiked. What are you looking at?"

"Probably nothing." His voice came out flatter than he wanted. "Debris catching light. We get tumbling junk through here sometimes."

"We haven't had debris in this orbital band in fourteen months. I keep very good records of debris."

He knew she did. That was the problem with living with an intelligence that kept perfect records: you lost the ability to lie to yourself, because she'd contradict you, gently, with timestamps.

"Then it's a ship," he said.

"It's not on my traffic feed. Nothing scheduled until the Helion drone Thursday." A pause, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—still warm, but with something underneath it now, the way ice has water underneath it. "Sable, it's not squawking any transponder at all."

* * *

Evenings were for maintenance of a different kind.

He ate in the galley—a room built for twelve, occupied by one, with a table he'd never once sat at the far end of. He ate and he listened to the station report its small ailments and he read the paper novel under the flickering strip light, and at 2100 he did the thing he did every night, the ritual he'd never named aloud because naming it would make it what it was.

He opened the memory archive.

Every colonist carried one—a neural lace backup, standard issue, updated monthly at any licensed clinic. Sable's hadn't been updated in seven years; the nearest clinic was nine weeks away by courier, and there was never anything urgent enough to justify the trip. Which meant his archive was a fossil. A snapshot of who he'd been at thirty-one, frozen on the day he shipped out: Lark laughing in the doorway of her apartment with flour on her hands, swearing she'd finally learned to bake bread. Dev, his—Dev. Dev standing on the platform with her bag already packed for the Earth run, saying *this isn't an ending, Sable, stop making that face*, and him making the face anyway, and both of them laughing so they wouldn't do the other thing. The launch. The burn. The first sight of Perihelion through the shuttle glass, small and stubborn and waiting for him like a promise nobody else remembered making.

He didn't replay them. That was the discipline. He just opened the index and looked at the titles, the way you might look at photographs through frosted glass. *Lark — kitchen, bread.* *Dev — platform, goodbye.* *Posting — arrival.* Knowing they were there was different from watching them, and the difference was the whole point: watching them hurt, but knowing they were there was a kind of wealth. He was, he sometimes thought, the richest poor man in the colonies. Everything he'd ever loved was stored right there behind his eyes, drawing interest in the dark.

"You're doing the archive thing," Vesper said.

"I'm doing inventory."

"You're doing the archive thing. Your breathing changes. It gets slower, like you're trying not to disturb something."

He closed the index. "You know, for an AI, you've got a creepy amount of insight into human behavior."

"For an AI," Vesper said, "I've had seven years of field research on exactly one subject. I'd hope I'd have learned something."

"And what have you learned?"

Another pause. Vesper's pauses had gotten longer over the years—he'd told himself it was processing load, the station's aging hardware, but lately he'd wondered. Lately the pauses felt less like computation and more like hesitation. Like she was deciding how much truth a man could hold before bed.

"I've learned," she said, "that humans don't actually want to remember things. They want to know that remembering is possible. The archive isn't a recording, Sable. It's a savings account. You never spend it. You just like knowing it's there."

He stared at the ceiling. Somewhere below him, coolant loop three sighed through its pipes, pressure drifting, managed but never solved.

"When did you get smarter than me?" he asked.

"Year two," Vesper said. "You weren't paying attention."

* * *

He was in the observation ring when the alert came, and afterward he would remember that the timing felt like a joke someone had written for him: he'd been watching the sun go around the spine, watching the light sweep the empty ports, thinking—as he did every night, briefly, then firmly put away—that tomorrow would be the same as today, and thank God for that, and God help him anyway.

The station chimed. Not the soft chime of routine traffic. The hard triple-tone of an unscheduled contact.

"Vesper."

"I see it." Her voice had shed all its warmth. What was left was precise and fast and frightened in the way machines get frightened, which is to say completely without theater. "Contact bearing two-seven mark four, range eleven thousand kilometers and closing. No transponder. No flight plan filed with Helion Gate. No response to hails on any standard frequency." A beat. "Sable, it's decelerating. It's coming here."

His mouth had gone dry. He watched the plot build itself on the main display: a single point of light crawling across the tactical overlay, patient as a spider. Eleven thousand kilometers. Ten and a half.

"Could be a salvager off-book," he said. "Could be smugglers. This is contested orbit, half the charts don't even agree whose it is—"

"The Wake-class freighters use this approach vector," Vesper said quietly.

He stopped. "Say that again."

"Cassiopeia's Wake. Hull number CW-113. Seven-year deep-run contract, outbound past the frontier markers. Registered departure date—" she paused, and he heard her check, though he knew she never needed to check, "—seven years, two months ago. Its return window closed five years ago. The Authority wrote it off as lost with all hands. There's a memorial plaque at Helion Gate. I have the dedication text in my logs if you—"

"Vesper."

"Yes, Sable."

"Is that our contact?"

Ten thousand kilometers. Nine and a half. The point of light held steady on its inbound line, unhurried, inevitable, a ship coming home from a place ships don't come home from.

"Yes," Vesper said. "That's Cassiopeia's Wake."

Sable stood in the observation ring with the dead star's light on his face and felt the seven years of quiet crack straight down the middle, and through the gap came something he hadn't felt since the day Dev's shuttle lifted: dread, pure and cold and certain, arriving ahead of any reason for it. Nothing about this was routine. Nothing about this was anything. A ghost ship was falling out of the dark toward his little station at the edge of the map, flying silent, answering nothing, and some animal part of him—the part that had known, on the platform, that he was watching something end—was already telling him to run.

"Vesper," he said. "Wake the dock. Prepare for emergency berthing."

"Preparing," she said. Then, softer, almost human: "Sable, whatever comes off that ship—I'm glad you're here and not me."

Nine thousand kilometers. Eight and a half.

"So am I," he said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and neither of them said anything else while the Wake came home.
