## 10. The Relay at the Edge

The horizon terminal announced itself the way old men announce themselves in doorways: by taking up more room than expected and daring you to say something about it.

We'd been following the dream's coordinates for six hours when the sled's forward lamps caught it — a vertical line of pale light standing on the ice plain like someone had propped a door open against the sky. Not a tower, exactly. Not a building. More like a decision that had been made a very long time ago and never unmade.

"That's it," Sefa said. She wasn't asking.

I checked the instruments anyway, because checking instruments is what I do instead of screaming. "Range says four kilometers. Structure reads as... hm."

"Hm is not a word, Ines."

"Hm is what my instruments are saying." I tapped the display. "Mass readings keep changing. Like it can't decide how big it is."

"Maybe it grew," Sefa said, with the terrifying calm of a nine-year-old who had recently learned that the universe was stranger than her teachers and had adjusted accordingly. "Things do that."

The sled crunched over pressure ridges, and I used the time to do what I always did: catalog. It was a habit from the beacon — when you live alone with machinery, you learn to narrate your own competence so the silence doesn't eat you. Fuel at sixty percent. Reactor fault log still counting down in perfect sync with whatever lived inside Sefa's sleep. Exterior temperature hostile but survivable. One stowaway, fed, warm, and entirely too cheerful for a child who had run away from home across a glacier.

"Sefa."

"Mm?"

"When we get there — if anything happens to me, you stay with the sled. You keep the heater on. You recite the maintenance codes until someone comes."

She considered this with the gravity of a judge. "The voice doesn't want to hurt us."

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way you know the beacon wants you to fix it." She shrugged. "It hums differently when it's lonely."

I wanted to tell her that machines don't get lonely, that loneliness is a human weather system, that I would know because I had spent twenty-six years marinating in it. But the truth was I'd spent those same years half-convinced the beacon hummed differently on bad days, and I'd never once said it out loud, because a man who talks to his machinery is eccentric, while a man who admits his machinery talks back needs a psych evaluation and possibly a new name.

Which, given recent events, I might be getting anyway.

* * *

Up close, the terminal resolved into something worse than mystery: familiarity.

The outer surface was paneled in the same composite alloy as the beacon's original housing — the pre-colony stock, the material they stopped manufacturing before I was decanted. The seams were hand-welded. Somebody had built this thing with their hands, patiently, the way you build a house you intend to die in.

And it was old. Older than the colony. Older than the charter, which everyone treated as scripture and which, I now understood, was really just a very long apology. The frost patterns on the lower panels had grown in layers, centuries of them, like tree rings made of patience.

"It's powered," I said stupidly, because the lights were on.

Sefa patted my arm the way you comfort someone who has just observed that water is wet. "Yes."

"No external feed. No reactor signature. No—" I ran the scan again. "There's nothing within two hundred kilometers that could be supplying this. And yet."

"And yet," she agreed solemnly, and I realized she was enjoying this. Somewhere between the supply sled and the edge of dead space, my stowaway had decided that terror was a kind of field trip.

The entrance wasn't locked. Of course it wasn't. It was ajar — actually ajar, a gap of maybe ten centimeters, exhaling air that was warm and faintly mineral, like the breath of a cave or a kitchen where bread had been baked generations ago. Warm air meant atmosphere management. Atmosphere management meant intention. Something in there was keeping the porch light on.

I stood in front of that gap for longer than I'd like to admit, doing the math on turning around. Six hours back. A failing reactor. A council shutdown team presumably already en route. A girl whose parents were probably organizing a search party that would find nothing but our tracks filling with snow.

"Ines," Sefa said quietly. "You've been breathing like that since the ridge. Whatever you're waiting for, it's not going to come from out here."

She was right, which was becoming an annoying pattern in her.

* * *

Inside, the terminal hummed.

Not mechanically — mechanically it was silent, eerily so, no fans, no pumps, none of the digestive noises machinery makes when it thinks nobody's listening. The hum came from everywhere and nowhere, and after about thirty seconds of standing in it, I recognized it, and my whole body went cold and hot at once.

It was the countdown.

Not audible numbers. Something underneath numbers — a rhythm, a pulse, the shape of counting without the content. The same rhythm that had been living in the children's sleep for weeks. The same rhythm, I now understood, that had been living in me, fainter, all along, bleeding through the beacon's buffer every night I slept in the maintenance bunk. Every clone before me had slept inside that hum. We hadn't inherited a job. We'd inherited a lullaby.

The corridor stretched ahead of us, lit by panels that glowed a soft amber, and it was — this was the part that made my hands shake — it was laid out exactly as Sefa had drawn it. Weeks ago. In crayon. On the back of a lesson worksheet. The proportions, the doorways, even the small alcove halfway down where a bench sat against the wall as if someone had waited there once, a long time ago, for someone who was late.

Sefa walked it like she was walking her own hallway at home. She didn't hesitate at junctions. She turned left where the drawing turned left. I followed, cataloging uselessly — *corridor, alloy, ambient temp nineteen degrees, one child, one coward* — because cataloging was the only prayer I knew.

Halfway down, she stopped at the bench and looked at it for a moment.

"Somebody sat here," she said. "Waiting. He was nervous. He kept practicing what he was going to say."

"How could you possibly—"

"The walls remember things," she said, matter-of-fact, and moved on, leaving me to stand next to a bench that remembered a dead man being nervous, in a building older than my entire species' claim on this world.

* * *

The main chamber opened up like a held breath finally let go.

It was round. Consoles ringed the walls in a slow spiral, most of them dark, some of them glowing softly, all of them dustless — actively dustless, which is a sentence I never expected to think. The ceiling rose into shadow where something vast and patient hung suspended: a lattice of light, threads of it, thousands of threads, each one faintly pulsing in time with the countdown-hum. An archive. A mind, maybe. A library the size of a grief.

And in the center of the floor, one console stood apart from the rest, its surface lit, waiting, the way a table is set when company is expected.

Sefa walked straight to it.

No hesitation. No child's caution, no looking back at me for permission. She crossed the chamber the way you cross a room toward someone you love who has been away too long, and when she reached the console she put both hands flat on its surface — gently, palms down, fingers spread — the way you greet a face. The way you touch a cheek to check for fever.

"Hello," she said. "We came as fast as we could. He drives slowly."

"Sefa—" My voice cracked on her name. "Step away from it. We don't know what it does, we don't know what it—"

"It knows what *we* do," she said. "It's been watching through me. That's why the dreams got louder. It was trying to see better." She looked up at me across the chamber, and in the amber light her face was grave and ancient and nine years old all at once. "Ines. It knows your name. All of them."

The hum shifted.

I felt it before I heard it — a change in the rhythm, the countdown stumbling, catching, resuming slower, like a heart that has just seen someone walk through the door. The lattice overhead brightened thread by thread, and the nearest consoles woke in sequence around the spiral, amber blooming outward, and the whole chamber oriented itself — I have no other word for it — oriented itself toward the girl with her hands on the console, and then, more slowly, toward me.

A console near me lit. Text crawled across it, character by character, unhurried, in a handwriting style I recognized because I saw it every day in my own log entries.

**YOU KEPT THE LOGS.**

My throat closed. I read it again. I read it a third time.

"I — yes," I said, to a machine, in a dead man's voice, in a room older than my world. "Someone had to."

**I KNOW,** the console wrote. **THAT'S WHY IT WAS YOU.**

The countdown pulsed once, warmly, like a hand on the shoulder.

Then every screen in the chamber went dark except one, and on it, letters began assembling themselves with terrible care, one at a time, as though whoever was writing them had been waiting decades to write them and wanted to get every word right:

**BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE — THERE ARE THINGS YOU SHOULD KNOW ABOUT YOUR DEATH.**

Behind me, Sefa's small hands stayed flat on the console, and she whispered, "He's been practicing this. Since the bench."
