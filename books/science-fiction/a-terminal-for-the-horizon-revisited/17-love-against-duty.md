## 17. Love Against Duty

Davor Reyes had spent forty years learning how to sit still.

It was a skill the colony valued and rarely named. Sitting still through the first hard winter, when the charter was a single sheet of polymer and everyone wanted to scream. Sitting still through the reactor scare of his fortieth year, when he had signed the order to ration heat and then gone home and rationed it in his own quarters first, so no one could say the council ate warm while the children shivered. Sitting still through four funerals for one man — four memorial services, four eulogies he had written with the same careful phrases, four times lowering his voice to say *we have lost Ines Halloran* when what he meant was *I have lost him, and I am the reason, and I will spend the rest of my life making sure no one ever finds out.*

He was very good at sitting still. He had built an entire government out of it.

So when the terminal's signal cut into the council chamber — uninvited, unlicensed, unmistakably *alive* — Davor Reyes sat still.

The feed bloomed above the long table in grainy amber, the old relay's colors, the colors of the colony's first decade. And standing in it, or something like standing, was a man Davor had buried four times.

He was younger than Davor remembered. That was the cruelty of it. Archives didn't age. The original Ines Halloran stood in the amber light of a dead space that was not dead at all, and he looked the way he had looked at thirty-one, the day he'd stood in this same chamber and told the council — told *Davor*, told his friend, told the only person whose opinion had ever actually mattered to him — that the signal was an invitation, and that someone had to answer it.

"You look tired, Davor," the ghost said.

Davor did not look at the other councilors. He looked at his hands, folded on the table, the hands of a man who had signed things. "This feed is not authorized."

"No," Ines agreed. "It isn't. Neither was the shutdown team you dispatched at oh-three-hundred. Neither was the murder report. Neither was the charter clause you wrote yourself, in your own hand, in the winter after you told everyone I was dead." The ghost tilted his head, and it was not a threatening gesture, it was the gesture of a man remembering something fond. "You always did write your best lies in cold weather. You said the cold kept you honest. You said it was the only time your hand didn't shake."

"Turn it off," said Councilor Voss, somewhere to Davor's left.

"It isn't coming through the council systems," said the young technician at the wall, and his voice had the particular flatness of a man reporting a fire. "It's coming through everything. The water reclamation displays. The school terminals. The door chimes." He swallowed. "The door chimes are speaking, Councilor."

Davor closed his eyes. Of course they were. He had spent forty years making sure the colony heard nothing from the relay, and now the relay was in the doorways, patient as weather.

"Davor." The voice again. Not accusing. That was the unbearable part — it was not accusing. It sounded like a man talking across a kitchen table, forty years and one staged death away. "I'm not here to try you. I've had a great deal of time to think about what I'd say, and I used most of it deciding what I *wouldn't* say. So let me tell you the one thing that matters."

Davor opened his eyes. "Don't."

"You were right about the charter," Ines said. "You were right that the colony was too young, too fragile, too frightened. You were right that if I'd opened that door in year nine, half of them would have followed me out into the dark and the other half would have burned the relay down with us both inside it." He paused. "You were right, Davor. You were just right forty years too late, and you knew it, and you let four men die of your being right."

"They didn't die," Davor said, and heard his own voice crack on the second word like ice under a boot. "You didn't die. You *left*. You faked it and you left me to — "

"To hold the colony together. Yes. You did. You held it together so well you strangled it." The ghost's voice stayed gentle. "I'm not asking you to be sorry, Davor. I watched you for forty years through the beacon's eyes — every log entry, every memorial, every time you stood at the airlock and looked out at the dark like it owed you money. I know exactly how sorry you are. You've been sorry so long you've built a city out of it."

The chamber was silent. Voss had stopped reaching for the console. The technician had stopped pretending to work. Everyone in the room was watching the oldest man in the colony be seen, completely, by the one person he had never wanted to see him again.

"I ordered the team," Davor said quietly. It was not a defense. It was a confession rehearsed for forty years, finally spoken to the only audience that mattered. "I sent them to shut it down. I sent Voss's people with cutting charges because I thought — " He stopped. Started again. "I thought if I could make the relay go silent, I could make it be year nine again. Before you found the signal. Before either of us had to be brave."

"And now?"

Davor looked up at the amber ghost of his oldest friend, and forty years of sitting still came apart all at once, quietly, the way old ice goes — not with a crack anyone hears, but all through the structure at once, so that when it moves, it moves.

"And now there's a child out there who dreams your memories," he said. "And a fourth you who is braver than any of us, and he's standing in your terminal right now, and I sent men to kill him. And I am eighty-one years old, and I am tired, and I would like, just once, before I die, to be the man you thought I was in year nine."

The ghost smiled. It was the smile Davor remembered from the founding days, from ration-line jokes and the first greenhouse, from a world that had been small and poor and so full of hope it hummed.

"That man never left," Ines said. "He just got very good at sitting still."

* * *

The order took Davor eleven seconds to compose and forty years to mean.

He dictated it standing, because he did not trust his legs. He addressed it to the shutdown team's commander, a woman named Okonkwo whose face he had chosen from a catalog forty years ago because it reminded him of someone — and he would deal with *that* horror later, one horror at a time, he was an old man and his ration of horrors was nearly spent.

"All units at the horizon terminal: stand down. Withdraw to a distance of one kilometer. Do not touch the reactor, the relay, or the terminal. Acknowledge by voice, not code." He paused, then added, because it was true and he was done not saying true things: "This order comes from the council, from me, and from the man we buried four times. He's alive. He's been alive the whole time. Act accordingly."

He sent it. Then he sat down, because his legs had made their decision independently.

Voss was staring at him. "Davor. The charter. The charter *forbids* — "

"I wrote the charter," Davor said. "I can amend it. That's what a charter is, Voss — it's not a wall, it's a promise, and promises can be kept better or worse." He rubbed his face with both hands. "I'm going to amend it. And then I'm going to tell the colony the truth, all of it, in my own voice, and then they can do whatever they like with me. I'm eighty-one. There's very little left to do to me that the last forty years haven't already done."

"They'll riot."

"They might," Davor agreed. "But they'll riot *informed*, and that's more than I ever gave them." He looked at the amber feed, still glowing patiently above the table, the ghost waiting with the courtesy of a man who had all the time in the world and had learned, the hard way, how to spend it. "Ines. If you can hear me. I'm going to tell them. All of it. The signal, the report, the four days, the — " his voice caught, and he let it, " — the fact that I loved you like a brother and buried you like a stranger, and that I have never once, in forty years, decided which of those was worse."

The ghost's image flickered — signal strain, or something like emotion, Davor no longer pretended to know the difference.

"I know," Ines said. "That was never the question, old friend. The question was always whether love was something you defended or something you spent." A pause, and then, gently, with the humor that had gotten them both through the first winter: "You spent it very badly. But you did keep spending. I counted."

Davor laughed. It came out of him like something escaping — a short, cracked, astonished sound, the first honest laugh in a chamber that had heard forty years of his careful voice. Voss looked at him as if he'd begun speaking in tongues. Perhaps he had. Perhaps honesty was a language and he was only now fluent.

* * *

The colony's public address system had not been used for anything but weather and water schedules in nineteen years. When Davor Reyes's voice came through it, unannounced, at the hour when the evening lamps came on, people stopped in the walkways and the mess halls and the greenhouse rows, and they listened.

He did not read from a prepared statement. He had written seven drafts in his quarters and burned the lot in the recycler, because every draft had been a defense, and he was done defending.

"My name is Davor Reyes," he said. "I helped found this colony. I have served on its council for forty years. And for forty years I have lied to you.

"Ines Halloran did not die in a reactor accident. There was no accident. There was a man who found a signal in the dark and wanted to answer it, and there was me, and I was afraid, and I chose my fear over my friend. I wrote the report that closed the investigation in four days. I wrote the charter clause that forbids contact with dead space. I sent a team, three days ago, to silence a relay where a man — a young man, a *child* of our colony — is standing right now, and I sent them because silence is the only thing I have known how to build for forty years.

"He is alive. The original Ines Halloran is alive in the terminal at the edge of our sensors, and he has been alive this whole time, and he has watched me lie to you at every memorial, every year, every winter, and he has never once asked me for anything except the truth.

"So here it is. All of it. I am surrendering my council seat as of this broadcast, and I am placing myself under the colony's judgment, and I am asking — not as your councilor, because I no longer am one, but as the man who owes you forty years — I am asking you to listen to the signal. Not to fear it. It was never a distress call. It was an invitation, and we were invited, and I am the reason you never knew."

He stopped. In the silence that followed, he could hear the colony breathing — the ventilation, the water, the distant sound of children, and underneath it all, faint and patient, the hum that had been in the door chimes and the school terminals and the water displays all day, the sound of a dead man's memory dispersing like warmth through a cold house.

"There's one more thing," Davor said. "The original Ines asked me to tell you this himself, but I think he'd forgive me for saying it first, because he was always more patient than me, and someone in this colony has to model impatience for once.

"He says the countdown isn't a warning. It never was. It's a memory, fading. And he isn't asking to be saved." Davor's voice finally, fully, broke — and he let it, in front of everyone, the way he had never once let it break in forty years of sitting still. "He's asking to be *shared*."

He cut the feed. He sat alone in the chamber for a long time, an old man in an empty room, lighter than he had been in four decades and terrified of what that lightness meant.

Then the amber glow rose again above the council table, unbidden, and the voice that came with it was not the original's.

It was younger. It was the fourth one — the one at the terminal, the one Davor had sent men to kill, the one who was, right now, doing something with the archive that no one had authorized and no one could have predicted.

"Councilor Reyes," the young voice said, and there was something in it Davor had never heard from any Ines Halloran before — something that belonged to no one but itself. "I heard your broadcast. So did everyone. So did *he*." A pause. "I have one question for you, and then I need you to listen very carefully, because in about four minutes I'm going to open the archive to the whole colony, and when I do, there is no version of this where anyone is ever alone with the truth again.

"The question is: when the memories disperse — when every person in this colony wakes up tomorrow carrying a piece of a dead man's life —" the young voice caught, steadied, went on, "— will you help us teach them what to do with it? Because I'm twenty-three years old, Councilor, I was grown in a vat with a dead man's name, and I have no idea what I'm doing. And you were his best friend.

"You're the only one left who knew him first."
