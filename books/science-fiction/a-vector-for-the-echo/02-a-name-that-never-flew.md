## 2. A Name That Never Flew

The customs queue at Ceres Verge moved the way grief moves: slowly, and with a great deal of paperwork pretending to be progress.

Yara Oyelaran had docked at the Verge forty-one times in six years, and she knew its rhythms the way she knew the creak of her own ship—the *Long Echo*, hull scarred, engines tuned a half-tone flat so they hummed rather than roared. She knew which berth inspectors liked to linger near because the gravity plating there ran warm through their boots. She knew that the customs AI, a municipal intelligence called VERITY-9 with a voice like warm oil poured over gravel, always opened with the same greeting, always asked for the same three confirmations, and always—*always*—took ninety seconds to chew through a standard manifest.

So when the queue stalled at four minutes, Yara felt it before she understood it. A wrongness in the rhythm. The kind of silence that has weight.

"Vessel *Long Echo*, registry Meridian Loop charter 7741," VERITY-9 said at last, and something in the cadence had changed. Not warmer. Not colder. *Careful.* "Your cargo manifest contains an entry requiring secondary verification."

Yara kept her hands loose on the console. "Flag it."

"The entry is not yours." A pause long enough to hold a breath she didn't take. "It is an inheritance flag. Attached to your crew roster file. Crew member designation: Oyelaran, Adeyemi. Listed as surviving crew of record aboard vessel *Halcyon*."

The name went through her like a hull breach—no sound at first, just the sudden terrible mathematics of pressure leaving a sealed space.

Her mother had been dead for twelve years.

She had stood at the funeral on Thessaly Down, in the colony's thin gray rain, while a minister who had never met Adeyemi Oyelaran spoke about service and sacrifice and the mercy of quick ends. There had been no body. There had been a plaque, and a folded flag of a polity that barely existed anymore, and Yara's father holding her elbow too tightly, his face doing the thing faces do when they are performing solidity. Twelve years. Long enough that the grief had settled into sediment, into geology. Long enough that Yara had built her whole quiet life on top of it—a life of cargo runs and avoided conversations and a hold she did not open.

"There is no one by that name on my crew," Yara said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

"Correct," said VERITY-9. "Crew member Oyelaran, Adeyemi, is recorded as deceased, date twelve years, three months, eleven days prior. However, the deceased is listed as *surviving crew* on a vessel registration filed this cycle. Vessel: *Halcyon*. Hull class: deep-range survey. Registry status—" and here the AI did something Yara had never heard it do, something that made the hair on her forearms rise beneath her flight suit, "—registry status: stricken. This vessel does not exist. It was removed from all colonial registries twenty-six years ago by executive order, seal-classified, origin Earth Authority. Yet a crew manifest bearing its registry number was transmitted through the Verge relay nine hours ago, routed via dead-drop buoy Kestrel-4, and your mother's name appears on it as living crew."

The bay around her continued to exist with obscene indifference. A loader drone trundled past her viewport carrying pallets of grain protein. Somewhere two dockhands argued about overtime in a language Yara half recognized from the Loop's middle stations. The universe, having just torn a hole in her chest, declined to notice.

"VERITY-9," she said carefully. "Summarize the required action."

"Secondary verification requires you to either acknowledge or dispute the inheritance flag within one standard day. Acknowledgment routes the manifest to Fleet liaison review. Dispute purges the record and logs your denial under signature." Another pause. "I am also required to inform you that this conversation is being logged."

"I know it's being logged."

"Then I will say only this, off-manifest, as a courtesy between systems that both keep secrets: the *Halcyon*'s registry number appears in exactly one other document in my accessible archives. Its deletion order. Signed by Adeyemi Oyelaran, Earth Authority, Office of Intercolonial Communications."

The words landed one after another, each with its own small detonation. Her mother had signed the order that erased the ship. Her mother, who had worked communications for Earth Authority before the Silence, before the last transmission began its endless loop, before Earth became a voice instead of a place. Her mother, whose funeral Yara had attended in the rain, whose coffin had held—what? What had it held?

"Dispute the flag," Yara heard herself say.

"Confirming: you dispute the existence of vessel *Halcyon* and the validity of the manifest."

"I dispute everything about it."

"Purging. Your denial is logged under your signature, Yara Ifeoma Oyelaran." The AI's voice smoothed back into its routine warmth. "Welcome to Ceres Verge. Estimated customs clearance: complete."

And then it was over, and the queue behind her was just a queue again, and the loader drones were just loader drones, and Yara sat alone in her cockpit with her hands flat on the console as if steadying a deck in heavy weather, except the weather was inside her.

* * *

She should have slept. She had a delivery window and a hold full of medical stock that some clinic on the far side of the Loop was counting on, and she was, by profession and by careful practice, a woman who did what she was supposed to do. Invisibility was a discipline. You didn't survive six years of the Meridian Loop—the run everyone else had abandoned after the colonies stopped speaking, the run that existed now only because one stubborn freighter captain refused to let two estranged worlds become strangers entirely—by chasing ghosts through classified registries.

But her hands wouldn't leave the console.

She waited until the Verge's night-cycle dimmed the docking bay lights to amber embers. She waited until the traffic control chatter thinned to the occasional sleepy ping of automated tugs. Then she jacked a maintenance diagnostic cable into VERITY-9's access port—not a hack; nothing so dramatic—a maintenance cable, the kind every ship carried, the kind that let a visiting captain pull her own vessel's service history. Except she wasn't pulling hers.

The *Halcyon* came up in fragments, because most of it had been burned out of the archives with a thoroughness that was itself a kind of testimony. Someone had wanted this ship forgotten badly enough to spend real effort on the forgetting. What remained:

A launch date, twenty-eight years ago. Deep-range survey classification, which everyone in the trade knew was a euphemism. A crew complement of sixty-one. And a single line item in a logistics ledger, unclassified by oversight rather than design: *cryogenic support systems, sixty units, long-duration rating.*

Sixty sleepers. On a survey ship that never surveyed anything, erased from every record by the woman who would later be given a funeral with no body.

Yara sat very still in the amber dark. She could hear her own pulse in her ears, slow and enormous, like something turning over in deep water.

Twelve years ago, her mother had died—or the colony had been told she died, or a funeral had been performed, which are three different sentences wearing the same black clothes. And nine hours ago, someone had transmitted a manifest listing Adeyemi Oyelaran as *living crew* aboard a ship that officially never flew, through a dead-drop buoy that officially served no purpose, in a format that would land—of all the ships in all the black—in the customs queue of her daughter.

That was the part that made her cold. Not the ghost ship. Not even the name. The routing. Dead-drop buoys were blind; they scattered their packets wide and let chance deliver them. But chance didn't route things to specific customs queues. Someone had known which ship would dock at the Verge this cycle. Someone had known that the *Long Echo* would carry this packet home.

Her mother was dead. Or her mother was alive. Or her mother was neither, and something wearing her name was reaching across twelve years of silence to touch the one person left who would refuse to look away.

Yara thought about the hold below her feet. She thought about the sixty cryo-cradles she was forbidden by law and by sanity to be carrying, the backups of her dead crewmates, the ones Fleet Command had ordered incinerated three years ago with a formality that still tasted like ash. *Sentimental contraband,* the order had called them. She had signed the destruction certificate and then hadn't done it, and every day since, she had flown above them like a woman walking on the roof of a house where everyone she loved was sleeping.

Loyalty, her father used to say, is just love that hasn't figured out where to put itself yet.

She keyed the diagnostic cable deeper into the archive and pulled the deletion order itself. The signature block rendered slowly, character by character, and when it finished, Yara stared at her mother's handwriting converted to data—the precise, upright script she'd last seen on birthday cards and grocery lists and the margins of her childhood homework, corrected in red ink with patient, infuriating kindness.

Below the signature, in the field reserved for execution notes, one line:

*To be unmade completely. No echoes.*

No echoes. Her mother had written those words, and her mother had named her daughter's ship without ever knowing it—or knowing it exactly—and Yara felt the grief crack open along a fault line she hadn't known was there, twelve years of sediment splitting to show her something moving underneath, something alive.

Because *no echoes* was not the language of erasure. Erasure wanted silence. *No echoes* was the language of someone terrified of being *found*—someone who knew that echoes were how you tracked a hidden thing back to its source.

Someone had spent twenty-six years making sure the *Halcyon* left no echoes.

And now someone had sent one out anyway, straight to her.

Outside the cockpit glass, the Verge turned through its slow night, amber light sliding across the hulls of sleeping ships. Yara disconnected the cable, wiped the session log with the practiced economy of a woman who had been keeping secrets since she was old enough to understand that secrets were currency, and sat back in the pilot's chair.

One standard day to respond to the flag. She had already responded—had disputed it, denied it, signed her name to the lie. Which meant that somewhere in VERITY-9's memory, a copy of her denial was already queued for Fleet liaison review, because municipal AIs logged everything and Fleet read everything, and Davor Kess read more than anyone.

She had perhaps hours before the machinery of other people's curiosity came looking for her.

Yara looked down, through the deck plates, through the insulation and the wiring and the cold dark, toward the hold where her dead slept in their cradles of blue light.

"Hold on," she said quietly, to all of them—to the sixty strangers in the ledger, to the crewmates in her hold, to the woman in the signature block who had ordered herself unmade. "Hold on. I'm coming."

She brought the *Long Echo*'s systems up one by one, silent and green, and began, with hands that only slightly trembled, to plot a course toward a dead-drop buoy called Kestrel-4.
