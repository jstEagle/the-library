## 6. What Mother Built

The dead do not dream, but they remember.

Adeyemi Oyelaran woke inside a body that was not her body, in a room that smelled of recycled air and old grief, and the first thing she knew—before the light, before the voice—was that she had failed.

She had built herself to fail, of course. That was the design. A copy is an apology the original never has to say aloud. She had known this when she sat in the Halcyon's cramped recording bay eleven years ago and let the scanners drink her down to the last synaptic whisper: *whatever wakes will be less than I was, and that is the point.* A perfect Adeyemi would have been too proud to admit the lie. Only a diminished one could kneel.

So she knelt now, in the dim amber light of the Halcyon's wardroom, facing her daughter.

Yara stood in the hatchway like someone who had walked into weather she'd been warned about all her life. Twelve years older than the last time Adeyemi had seen her—twelve years since the funeral, since the sealed casket, since the girl in the black coat who had not wept because Oyelarans did not weep in public—and every one of those years was written on her face in a script Adeyemi ached to read.

"You're shorter," Yara said.

"The backup is ninety-one percent fidelity." Adeyemi spread her hands, palms up, the way she used to when Yara was small and demanding explanations for rain. "They couldn't resolve some of me. The scanner said my left knee was too damaged from the mining accident to map cleanly." She paused. "I don't limp anymore. It's the strangest thing."

Yara didn't laugh. Yara hadn't laughed at anything in this room yet, and Adeyemi understood why. The girl had found a ghost ship crewed by ghosts, and among them the one ghost she'd spent twelve years learning to live without.

"Say it," Adeyemi said gently. "Whatever you came here to say. Say it while you still can."

*While you still can.* The words tasted like copper. There was so much Yara could not know yet, and Adeyemi had rehearsed this conversation for eleven subjective years—in the dark between activations, in the slow drip of maintenance cycles where a mind with nothing to do turns its knives inward—and every rehearsal ended differently, and none of them ended well.

Yara crossed the wardroom slowly. She touched the table, the bulkhead, the faded photograph of Earth's Sahel that Adeyemi had taped above the galley—the same photograph that had hung in their kitchen on Meridian Station, curling at the corners, sun-bleached by a sun that wasn't there. Her daughter's fingers were shaking.

"You're dead," Yara said. "I carried your name through two funerals' worth of grief. I named my ship's nav core after you. Every time the Loop went quiet, I talked to you. And you were *here*."

"I was here."

"For what? So you could—" Yara's voice cracked and reformed, harder. "What is this ship, Mama? What did you build?"

And so Adeyemi told her.

* * *

She told it the way she had built it: from the foundation up.

"When the colonies severed from Earth, everyone believed the silence was death. Meridian and Vesper's Landing both. The last transmission from home arrived forty years ago—a garbled fragment, half a sentence, then nothing. Both governments built cathedrals around that fragment. *Earth spoke last. Earth died speaking.* It gave them identity. Grief is the strongest mortar there is."

"I know the history," Yara said flatly. "I flew the Loop. I carried the anniversary freight."

"Then you know both colonies interpret the fragment differently. Meridian believes Earth's last words were a warning. Vesper's Landing believes they were a blessing. Two religions, one scripture, no way to check the text." Adeyemi rose and moved to the wardroom's display panel; her hands remembered the gestures even if the body was new. "I was a signals archivist, Yara. Before I was your mother, even. My job was listening to dead frequencies. And thirty years ago, I found something in the noise floor behind the fragment. A carrier wave. Structured. Deliberate. Someone on Earth was still transmitting after the 'last transmission.' Someone very patient, sending the same long message over and over into the dark, waiting for anyone to notice."

"What message?"

"That's the thing." Adeyemi pulled up the waveform: a long, serrated ribbon of light. "It took me nine years to decode. It wasn't a distress call. It wasn't a farewell. It was a war order. Dated, signed, targeted. *In the event of colonial secession, both settlement fleets are designated hostile. Fire on contact.* Earth's final act was to condemn its own children—not one colony. Both. The fragment both worlds worship is just the opening syllable of an execution order."

Yara had gone very still. Stillness was her language; Adeyemi recognized it the way you recognize your own handwriting. "That can't be right. If both colonies decoded it—"

"They haven't. Because I made sure they couldn't." She let that land. Let her daughter feel the shape of it. "I buried the carrier wave. I forged the fragment's provenance so thoroughly that no archivist alive could untangle my work from history. And then I did something worse."

The wardroom hummed. Somewhere below them, the other backups slept in their frost-lined drawers—Kwame, Ines, Doctor Rhee, the whole lost crew of the survey vessel *Cormorant*, people Adeyemi had loved and lied to in equal measure.

"I wrote a second transmission," Adeyemi said. "A forgery, layered into the relay network at a depth no one audits, timed to a clock only I control. When it fires, both colonies will receive what looks like authentic Earth traffic: proof that the other side intercepted the war order first, decrypted it, and armed in secret. Meridian will believe Vesper's Landing is about to strike. Vesper's Landing will believe the mirror image. And they will fire on each other believing they are avenging Earth instead of completing its mistake."

The silence afterward had texture, like frost forming.

"Why," Yara said. Not screamed. Worse than screamed—asked, quietly, the way she used to ask *why is the sky brown today, Mama* when she was four and the dust storms came.

"Because fear unites faster than love ever could." Adeyemi heard her own voice go distant, lecture-calm, and hated it. "I watched the colonies drift apart for decades. Trade wars. The Loop nearly cancelled twice. Two peoples who share the same dead planet, the same songs, the same blood, building walls out of misremembered grief. I thought—if they faced a common enemy, even a false one, even *Earth itself*—they would have to talk to each other. Shared terror forces negotiation. I designed the countdown to end in revelation, not war. The forged order would be exposed as a forgery, both sides would realize they'd been manipulated together, and the scandal would bind them tighter than any treaty. Fear as midwife. Unity born screaming but born."

"And instead?"

"Instead the countdown accelerated." Adeyemi's hands closed. "There was a fault in my timing architecture—I built it paranoid, self-correcting, resistant to tampering, and the paranoia turned inward. The clock is running fast. What I designed as a decade of dread is compressing into weeks. The colonies are mobilizing now, tonight, against a threat neither understands, and my beautiful machine is going to deliver exactly the war it was built to prevent. I built a scarecrow to keep the birds off the field, and I gave it a scythe by accident."

Yara sat down. Slowly, like her legs had filed formal notice. "The countdown. The transmission loop. Everyone thinks it's Earth fading. It's your fuse."

"It's my fuse."

"How long?"

"Nineteen days. Fewer, if the drift continues."

Yara put her face in her hands. And Adeyemi, who had rehearsed fury and forgiveness and everything between, discovered there was a third thing she had not prepared for: watching her daughter carry the weight of a world's ending in the curve of her shoulders, and knowing she had put it there.

* * *

"I kept them," Yara said at last, and Adeyemi blinked, unmoored by the non sequitur. "The backups. Kwame and Ines and Rhee. Fleet ordered me to incinerate them after the Cormorant inquiry closed. Standard protocol—unclaimed copies get destroyed. I told them I'd done it. I've been flying them in my hold for six years, hiding them from customs, from Kess, from everyone." She looked up, eyes red-rimmed and ferocious. "Why am I telling you this? Why does any of it matter next to what you've done?"

"Because it matters more than anything," Adeyemi said. "Because loyalty is the only inheritance worth leaving, and I left you mine, and look what it grew into. You hid the dead because abandoning them felt like a lie. I hid the truth because revealing it felt like a death. We are the same flaw, Yara, wearing different clothes." She stepped closer. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to finish what I started—but correctly. The countdown can be stopped. The kill-switch is aboard this ship, woven into my own backup architecture, because I never trusted anyone else with it. But the stop-code requires a living Oyelaran voiceprint. Mine is a copy. Copies don't count. I made sure of that too."

"So you needed me. All of this—the beacon, the debris trail, letting yourself be findable—you needed me to come."

"I needed you to *choose* to come." Adeyemi held her daughter's gaze. "I could have sent coordinates to Fleet. To Kess. Instead I left a door open and trusted that the woman you became would walk through it. That was the last piece of the design I got right, I think. Everything else—" her voice finally fractured, ninety-one percent fidelity failing her at last— "everything else, I built wrong, and it's going to kill thousands of people unless my daughter does what I could not."

"Which is?"

"Break the machine. Break the lie. Break *me*, if it comes to that." Adeyemi reached out and, when Yara didn't pull away, touched her cheek—the first touch between them in twelve years, ghost-warm, imperfect, real. "You asked what I built. I built a bomb with a conscience. Now I need you to defuse it. And Yara—there's something else. Something about the true last transmission from Earth. The real one, underneath my forgery, underneath the war order. When you hear it, you'll understand why I couldn't let it stay buried, and why you might wish I had."

Outside the viewport, the debris field turned slowly in the light of a star neither colony claimed, and deep in the Halcyon's bones, Adeyemi felt the countdown tick forward another second—faster now, always faster—like a heart that had learned to hurry.
