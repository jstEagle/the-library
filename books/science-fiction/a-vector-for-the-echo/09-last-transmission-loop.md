## 9. Last Transmission Loop

The countdown had a sound.

Yara had never noticed it before—twelve years of listening to Earth's last transmission and she had never once heard the seam where the loop closed and began again. But now, with the timer stripped bare in the corner of her display, she could hear it: a fractional silence between iterations, thinner than breath, where the signal reset itself like a heart between beats. *Fourteen hours, eleven minutes.* The gap widened as the transmission degraded, the way a dying man's pauses lengthen between sentences. Soon there would be nothing but the pause. Then the fleets would fire.

She sat in the cockpit of the *Meridian's Grace* with her hands hovering over the console, not touching it, the way you hover over a sleeping child you're afraid to wake. Below her feet, in the hold, forty-one backups had voted to die honest. She could still hear the echo of their voices in the ship's intercom—the flat, sleep-roughened timbre of people who had been dead for years and had just agreed to be dead again, on purpose, for the truth.

Her mother's voice among them. Adeyemi Oyelaran, who had said, *Do it, Yara. Say my name out loud so they know I lied.*

Yara pressed her palms flat against the console instead of her face, because if she covered her face she would come apart, and there was no time to come apart. There were fourteen hours and eleven minutes, minus however long it took her hands to stop shaking.

*Stop shaking,* she told them. *You've smuggled ghosts across two colonies for twelve years. This is just one more cargo run.*

It wasn't. She knew it wasn't. But the lie steadied her hands, and she had learned from the best that a well-made lie can hold a world together—at least long enough to take it apart properly.

* * *

The relay network was a spine of ancient buoys strung along the Meridian Loop like vertebrae, built before the Silence, maintained by no one, trusted by everyone. Both colonial fleets routed their targeting telemetry through it because it was the only infrastructure old enough to predate the schism—shared bones, inherited from a body neither side admitted belonged to them both. That was the irony Yara intended to weaponize: the two armadas closing on each other through the dark were, at this moment, speaking through the same throat.

She jacked into the relay buoy at Meridian Deep with a cable older than she was, its insulation cracked and taped, humming against her wrist like a held note. The buoy's security was a museum piece—Kess's fleet engineers had hardened the endpoints, the colony-side gates, but nobody had touched the spine itself in decades. Why would they? No one hijacked the loop. The loop was sacred. The loop was how Earth's last words came down every day, faithful as rain.

*That's exactly why,* Yara thought, and began to climb inside it.

The intrusion felt physical. The relay's architecture unfolded around her awareness like a cathedral of light and latency—packet streams falling past her in slow golden curtains, checksums ringing like bells, and beneath everything, the Earth transmission itself, vast and patient, playing its twelve-year vigil. She moved through it the way she'd once moved through her mother's house at night, knowing which boards creaked, which doors stuck, which silences were safe.

Then she found the forgery.

It lived underneath the lullaby like a stone under moss. Adeyemi had buried it deep—a second signal, phase-locked to the first, invisible unless you knew to listen for the interference pattern. And there it was in the decoded layers: the war order. *Launch authorization confirmed. First strike detected. Response mandatory.* Stitched from authentic Earth command codes, timestamped to look decades old, timed to surface when the loop finally failed so that both colonies would read the same dying order and each believe the other had sent it.

Her mother's handwriting was all over it. Not literally—but in the elegance, the economy, the terrible care. Yara recognized it the way you recognize a parent's stitching inside a coat. She had spent her whole life learning to make herself invisible using techniques her mother had invented, and here was the masterwork: an invisibility so complete it had nearly killed two worlds.

*I admired you,* Yara thought, her fingers moving through the code. *I hated you. I don't know which one kept me alive.*

She didn't delete the forgery. That had been the argument in the hold—Kess's voice still ringing in her memory from the comm burst three hours ago: *Let it run, Oyelaran. Fear is holding the trigger fingers steady. Truth means both fleets fire blind.* He'd sounded almost gentle. That was the worst part about Kess; he was never cruel when he was wrong.

No. She wouldn't delete it. She would *show* it. Dragged up into the light beside the thing it had hidden behind, timestamped, annotated with the Halcyon's logs—every decision, every desperate calculation, Adeyemi's own voice explaining what she'd done and why and how sorry she was. You couldn't kill a lie by burying it deeper. You killed it by making everyone watch it being born.

* * *

The countdown crossed ten hours as she worked, and the fleets crossed the meridian.

She felt them before her instruments showed them—two pressures in the relay traffic, like weather fronts colliding. The Vesta Compact fleet, forty-one ships burning hard out of the inner system. The Halcyon Reach flotilla, thirty-eight, coming around the far beacon in formation so tight it looked like fear made visible. Between them, the loop's silence gathering like a held breath.

Targeting telemetry bloomed across the shared spine. She watched lock signatures propagate through the network she was standing inside—red threads weaving themselves through the golden curtain, dozens of them, then hundreds, each one a promise someone else was making about her future. Her mouth went dry. Her hands, traitorously steady now, kept working.

*Terror and clarity,* she thought. *They can live in the same body. They're living in mine.*

A hail punched through on fleet priority. KESS, DAVOR — LIAISON, MERIDIAN AUTHORITY. She let it ring twice, then opened audio only.

"Yara." His voice was stripped of its portside warmth. "My sensors show you welded into Relay Nine. Whatever you think you're doing—"

"You know what I'm doing."

"The countdown fails in nine hours and fifty-one minutes. When it does, both sides receive launch confirmation. If you broadcast what I think you're broadcasting, you remove the ambiguity that might—" he caught himself, recalibrated, tried honesty, which was somehow worse, "—that might make them hesitate. Right now half the gunnery officers on both sides are praying the other guy blinks. Doubt is armor, Yara. Don't strip it off them."

"Doubt is also why they've pointed guns at each other for twelve years," she said. "Doubt is the disease, Davor. I'm done prescribing it."

"They will fire. Both fleets. I've seen the readiness boards."

"Then they'll fire at each other over a lie their own grandmother wrote," Yara said, "while her voice tells them she's sorry. Let them choose after they know. That's all I'm asking. That's all anyone gets."

Silence on the line. In it, faintly, she heard his breathing, and beneath that—she was inside the relay, she could hear everything the relay carried—the lullaby, still looping, still faithful, singing its counterfeit peace over the gathering dark.

"If this fails," Kess said quietly, "it's not just you who burns."

"I know," Yara said. "Wake the dead, or burn with the living. We voted."

She cut the channel before he could answer, because his answer didn't matter anymore, and because if he'd said something kind she might have wept, and her hands needed another hour of being dry.

* * *

Eight hours. Six. The red threads thickened. Somewhere below her, in the hold, the backups slept their last ordinary sleep, having chosen to spend their second lives as evidence. She thought of Tomas's backup saying, *We were always going to be cargo, Yara. At least let us be true cargo.* She thought of her mother's face on the Halcyon's cracked screen, older than memory, younger than grief.

At four hours, she finished.

Everything was staged: the forgery, exposed and annotated. The Halcyon's full logs. The crew manifest—including the names of the dead, including her own mother's name, including the word BACKUP beside each one, because the truth has no room for softening. And beneath it all, queued and waiting, the raw transmission. Earth's actual final words, uncorrupted, the layer under the layer under the layer.

Not a war order. A woman's voice, recorded in the last functioning studio on a dying world, singing a lullaby in Yoruba and English both, the same song Adeyemi had sung to a small girl in a habitat with failing lights. *Sun re o, sun re o / Sleep, my love, the stars are watching too...* Twelve years the colonies had heard it without hearing it, their instruments filtering it down to carrier noise while they listened for orders in the static.

There had never been any orders. There had only ever been a mother singing, and a daughter who finally understood that the song was the message.

Yara looked at the fire key. It wasn't shaped like anything dramatic—it was a prompt, blinking, patient. BROADCAST ALL CHANNELS: CONFIRM?

Her heart was slamming so hard she could feel it in her teeth. Two armadas hung in the dark outside, minutes from their triggers, and she was one person in a freight hauler with taped cables and stolen bandwidth, and there was no version of this where she survived unchanged.

*Mama,* she thought. *Watch me do it better than you did.*

She pressed confirm.

* * *

The relay lit up like a struck bell.

Every channel, every band, every frequency the loop had ever carried—the forgery first, naked and undeniable, its forged timestamps peeling away under public scrutiny in real time; then the Halcyon's logs unspooling across every screen on two colonies, Adeyemi's voice filling briefing rooms and galleys and gunnery stations: *I did this. I thought fear would hold you together. I was wrong, and I am sorry, and here is the proof of everything;* then the manifest, the names, the word BACKUP repeated forty-one times like a tolling bell.

And then, under all of it, rising, the song.

Yara watched the red threads falter. Watched lock signatures dissolve—not all at once, but in stuttering clusters, like a crowd forgetting why it was angry. On the tactical overlay, two fleets hung motionless in the dark, weapons hot, and on every bridge of both armadas the same voice was singing the same lullaby, and somewhere in the Vesta Compact flagship a gunnery officer was calling up the authentication records with shaking hands, and somewhere in the Reach flotilla a captain was reading Adeyemi Oyelaran's confession aloud to a silent crew.

The countdown reached zero.

The loop went silent—and in the silence, for the first time in twelve years, both colonies heard the same thing at the same moment: nothing. No order. No enemy. Just the space where the lie had been, and the song still hanging in it, fading, *sun re o, sun re o*, and two hundred thousand people holding their breath across the black.

On the far edge of the tactical display, one ship broke formation. Then another. Not retreating. Turning. Slowing. Waiting.

Yara slumped back in the pilot's chair, trembling so violently the harness rattled, laughing or crying or both, and keyed the all-ship channel with the last of her steadiness.

"This is Yara Oyelaran, aboard the *Meridian's Grace*. The loop is open. Come talk."

The reply, when it came, was not from either fleet.

It was from the hold.
