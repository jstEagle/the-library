## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The trouble with gravity, Bram Eastwick had long ago decided, was that you only ever noticed it when it left.

They were thinking about this at 0347 ship's time, halfway through a haul of atmospheric condensers and seed-stock crates from Halcyon Ring to the terraforming stations at Vess, because the coffee had gone cold again and their knees ached in that particular way that meant the Kestrel's Wake's grav-plate in the mess section was running four percent under spec. Four percent. Nobody else noticed. Nobody else ever noticed. That was the whole architecture of Bram's life: the person who noticed the four percent, who reseeded the greenhouse trays nobody else remembered to water, who filed the maintenance requests in the tidy bureaucratic language that made Combine orbital authority skim past them like water off waxed canvas.

Invisible. That was the job. That was, if Bram was honest, the entire personality they'd built over eleven years of hauling freight through contested orbits: competent, unremarkable, a gardener with a freighter's license and no history worth auditing.

The coffee was cold because they'd been staring at the cargo manifest for twenty minutes without reading a word of it, and the manifest was boring, and boring was good, and Bram still couldn't stop looking at the little amber tick in the corner of their retinal display where the ship's systems interface lived. The tick had been there for six days. A diagnostic flag. *Implant firmware variance detected. Recalibration recommended.*

Six days ago, Bram had recalibrated. Twice. The tick stayed.

"Eastwick." Sefa's voice came through the mess hatch along with the smell of burnt protein and the particular clatter of a woman who believed every object in a galley should be handled like it owed her money. "You're up. You're never up at this hour unless the greenhouse timer broke again."

"Timer's fine."

"Then you're brooding, which is worse, because brooding spreads." Sefa dropped into the opposite seat with a bowl of something gray and steaming. She was the ship's engineer, sixty-two years old, built like a reactor housing, and she had once told Bram — drunk, on the anniversary of some disaster she'd never named — that the Kestrel's Wake was the only family she had left and she'd space anyone who made her get another one. Bram had always taken this as affection.

"I'm not brooding," Bram said. "I'm reviewing."

"Reviewing what?"

"The manifest."

Sefa looked at them over the rim of the bowl. "You've reviewed that manifest three times. I watched you do it yesterday. You read condenser serial numbers the way other people read poetry, and I want you to know I've never once judged you for it, but this is different and we both know it."

Bram opened their mouth to lie, which was a skill they'd gotten genuinely good at, and that was when gravity left.

It didn't fade. There was no warning hum, no flicker in the lights, no polite countdown. One moment Bram was sitting in a chair with cold coffee, and the next moment the chair was a suggestion and the coffee was a slow brown sphere rising out of its cup and the entire contents of the mess — two bowls, a spoon, Sefa, Bram, a dish towel, a jar of protein powder Sefa had been hoarding since the last port — was adrift in the cabin like the world's saddest aquarium.

"What in the—" Sefa got one hand on the table's edge and held on, gray hair floating around her head like a halo that had given up. "That's not the mess plate. That's *all* the plates. That's the whole ship."

She was right. Bram could feel it in the way their inner ear screamed, in the readouts scrolling up their retinal display: *grav-plate array: NO RESPONSE. Deck 1 through Deck 4. Cargo hold. Greenhouse module.* All of it. Every gram of artificial gravity aboard the Kestrel's Wake, gone at once, mid-haul, in contested orbits where a ship drifting dark and tumbling was a thing that got boarded or salvaged, and usually both.

"Sefa—"

"I see it. Hold something."

Bram held the table. Their stomach performed a slow revolt. The coffee sphere drifted past their nose and they watched it go with the detached interest of a person whose body had decided fear was a problem for later.

And underneath the fear, underneath the lurching stomach, something colder was happening in the back of Bram's skull, in the place where the implant lived — the standard colonial-issue neural lace every licensed hauler carried, the one that tracked vitals and cargo and biometrics for the Combine's shipping authority. It was logging. It always logged. But it was logging *fast*, a data spike spooling through Bram's visual field in columns they couldn't read, a torrent of something dense and encrypted, and the tick in the corner of their display — the little amber diagnostic flag that had sat there for six days like a splinter — flared bright orange and then white.

*Genomic key match,* the interface said, in the flat, pleasant voice the manufacturer had chosen, the voice of a helpful assistant who had never once been helpful. *Identity confirmed. Inheritance protocol initiated.*

Bram's mouth went dry. "Sefa. How long has the gravity been out?"

"Four seconds. Why, are you counting?"

"Yes."

"Why are you *counting*—"

"Five. Six." Bram's heart was doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional. "Seven."

"Eastwick, you're scaring me, and I want it noted that I don't scare."

"Eight." The coffee sphere had reached the ceiling vent. The spoon was orbiting the light fixture with a kind of serene indifference to the end of the world. "Nine."

Gravity came back.

It didn't ease in. It arrived — a full, hard-handed return, and everything that had been floating came down at once. The spoon hit the deck. The bowls hit the table. Sefa hit her chair with a grunt. Bram, who had been holding the table edge with both hands and therefore came down mostly upright, felt the impact in their teeth and their knees and somewhere behind their sternum, in the place where dread kept its office.

The mess was silent except for the tick of the cooling unit and the soft, wet slap of the coffee sphere meeting the ceiling vent and dying there.

"Nine seconds," Bram said.

"Nine seconds," Sefa agreed. She was already pulling up the engineering console on her sleeve display, fingers moving. "Full array failure. Not a brownout, not a plate fault — a *command* failure. Something told the whole system to stop, and then told it to start again. Nine seconds, on the dot." She looked up. "You knew. You counted it before it finished. How did you know it would be nine?"

"I didn't know."

"You counted like a person who knew."

Bram's display was still glowing. The white flare had settled back down to something new: not amber, not orange, but a thin, patient line of gold across the top of their vision, like a horizon. Beneath it, text was assembling itself, character by character, in a font Bram didn't recognize and in a cadence that felt less like a system message and more like a letter.

*Fragment 1 of [unknown]. Decryption available at [unknown].*

*Hello, seedling.*

Bram read it twice. Three times. The words didn't change. The word *seedling* sat there in their vision like a hand pressed flat against the inside of their skull.

"Eastwick." Sefa's voice had gone quiet, which was worse than shouting. "Your eyes just did something. Some kind of display bleed. I can see it from here, gold light, right across — what is that? What are you looking at?"

"Nothing." The lie came out smooth and instant, eleven years of practiced invisibility doing its work, and Bram hated how good it felt and how much it cost. "Display artifact. Implant hiccup. I'll run diagnostics."

"Gravity just died for nine seconds across the entire ship and your implant is having a *hiccup*."

"The two things might be related," Bram admitted. "Or they might not. I'll check."

Sefa looked at them for a long moment. She had a way of looking at people that made them feel like a faulty valve she was deciding whether to replace or bypass. "Ilo felt the stutter up in the nav blister. She's already re-running our drift solution. Vash is awake — you know how he gets, he sleeps like a cat and wakes up like one too. Everyone's going to have questions, and the liaison office at Vess is going to have questions, because a nine-second full-array failure gets *reported*, Eastwick, it gets reported and logged and someone from Combine orbital authority reads the log over their morning ration paste, and if there's anything on this ship you don't want that particular someone reading—"

"There isn't," Bram said.

"Right." Sefa stood, collected her bowl, and paused at the hatch. "Eleven years, you've been aboard. You're the only person on this crew who waters plants nobody's contracted to eat for a hundred years. I've never asked you a single question about where you came from, because everybody on this boat is running from something and the running is what makes us a crew." She tapped the doorframe twice, an old habit, a good-luck thing. "Whatever that gold light is, whatever you counted to nine for — you tell me when you're ready. Not the Combine. Me."

The hatch closed behind her.

Bram sat alone in the mess, in the smell of spilled coffee and burnt protein, and let the practiced calm drain out of their body one muscle at a time. Their hands, when they looked at them, were shaking. Their display was still gold at the top of their vision, patient as a held breath.

*Hello, seedling.*

Nobody called them that. Nobody alive. There was exactly one person in the universe who had ever used that word for Bram Eastwick, and that person had walked out of a rented room on Meridian Station twenty-nine years ago and never come back, and the official record — the record Bram had pulled, at nineteen, with forged credentials they were still quietly ashamed of — said *deceased, no next of kin, remains unclaimed.*

The record was wrong. The record had been wrong for twenty-nine years, and something with their parent's voice in its vocabulary had just reached across dead space and knocked on the inside of Bram's skull.

*Fragment 1 of [unknown],* the gold line said. *Say yes to begin.*

Bram sat in the mess of the Kestrel's Wake, in the contested orbits where the Combine's flags flew on every station and every patrol and every ledger line of every haul, and understood — with the specific, vertiginous clarity of a person watching their own reflection blink out of sync — that the invisible life they had built, the four-percent grav plate and the greenhouse timers and the tidy boring manifests, had just cracked straight down the middle.

They thought about saying no.

They thought about it for nine seconds. It seemed like the right unit of measurement.

Then Bram Eastwick, gardener, freight pilot, ghost, looked at the gold horizon across their vision and said, out loud, to nobody, in the empty mess of a ship full of people who trusted them:

"Yes."

The display bloomed. And the first thing the fragment showed them was a face.

* * *

Up in the nav blister, Ilo watched the drift solution re-run itself for the third time and frowned at a number that shouldn't exist. The stutter hadn't just touched the Kestrel's Wake. Her long-range passive array had caught the same nine-second signature echoing off three other hulls in the contested band — freighters, tugs, a Combine patrol cutter — every artificial gravity field in a two-hundred-thousand-kilometer radius, dropping and restoring in perfect synchrony.

Not a malfunction. A broadcast.

She was still staring at it when Bram's voice came over the intercom, carefully light, carefully ordinary.

"Ilo? Plot us a correction back to the haul lane. And, uh — when you get a chance, I need to talk to you about the greenhouse."

Ilo looked at the impossible number on her display one more time, and keyed the comm.

"Sure, Eastwick," she said. "Come on up. Bring the manifest."

She didn't know why she said that last part. It just felt like the kind of night where everybody was carrying something.
