## 1. The Manifest of a Ship That Never Flew

The fire at Berth Nine had destroyed nothing, which was, in Rosalind Coldharbour's professional opinion, the most suspicious thing about it.

She stood on the dock in the grey half-light of Terminal's morning — a morning that arrived late and apologetic, the way most things did on a colony Earth had stopped returning calls to forty years ago — and surveyed the scene. The Halcyon Vane sat in its slip like a patient who has fainted dramatically but is, upon examination, entirely fine. Scorch marks laced her hull in elegant black whorls. Her paint had bubbled in places. And not one component, not one rivet, not one crate of salted kelp protein in her hold had suffered so much as a scorch.

"Insurance fraud usually involves *something* being damaged," said Dockmaster Ivo Pell, who had followed her out onto the planking with the resigned air of a man who knew that once Rosalind started asking questions, his morning was forfeit. "That's generally the point."

"That's what makes it interesting." Rosalind crouched by the mooring cleat, where the fire had licked up the piling and then, apparently, thought better of it. The burn pattern stopped in a clean line, like a signature. "Fires are honest, Ivo. They eat whatever's flammable and they don't apologize. This one ate the *paint*. It dined decoratively and left the kitchen untouched."

"You always talk like that?"

"Only when I'm about to ruin someone's week." She straightened, brushing soot from her gloves. "Who filed the claim?"

"The ship itself. Automated filing, priority flag, Earth-standard encryption." Ivo scratched under his cap. "Which is odd, because the Halcyon Vane hasn't flown in eleven years. She's been sitting here since before you took the job. Before your predecessor took the job. She's basically furniture with an engine bolted on."

"A ship that never flew files a claim for a fire that never happened." Rosalind smiled the smile she reserved for impossible claims — the one that made junior adjusters nervous and made her, privately, feel more alive than anything else on this damp little world. "Ivo, this is the best thing that's happened to me all year, and I want you to appreciate that my year has included a man who claimed his greenhouse was destroyed by hail during a drought."

"I remember. You made him replant it."

"I made him *show me where he'd replanted it*, which is different, and educational." She patted his arm and headed for the gangway. "I'm going aboard. If anyone official-looking arrives, I'm inspecting ballast."

"There's nobody official-looking on Terminal. That's rather the point of Terminal."

"One lives in hope."

* * *

Inside, the Halcyon Vane smelled of cold metal and old air-recycler lavender, a combination that suggested the ship had been mothballed by someone with sentimental tendencies. Rosalind moved through the corridors with her lamp held high, cataloguing: no heat damage past the outer skin, no smoke residue in the vents, no water from any suppression system because no suppression system had activated. The fire had occurred entirely on the outside, like a rumor.

The claim documentation terminal in the cargo office still had power — drawing, when she checked, from a source that wasn't the dock grid. That was the second impossibility of the morning, and Rosalind wrote it down in her notebook with the small pleasure of a collector.

The manifest came up on the third try, after two corrupted files and one screen of what appeared to be someone's grocery list from a decade ago (flour, yeast, batteries, *more flour*). The file header read:

**HALCYON VANE — OUTBOUND MANIFEST — FINAL REVISION**

Outbound. A ship that hadn't flown in eleven years, filed for departure.

Rosalind scrolled the cargo lines first: standard colony freight, sealed containers, weights that balanced to four decimal places, which no real manifest ever did. Real manifests were messy. This one had been *composed*.

Then the passenger list.

One passenger.

**COLDHARBOUR, E.**

She read it twice. Three times. The dock sounds faded — the gulls arguing over the fish processing plant, the distant clang of the tide bells — until there was only the hum of the terminal and her own pulse, which had picked up the way it did before a difficult negotiation.

Coldharbour was not a common name on Terminal. It was, in fact, a family of exactly two, and one of them was standing in a dead ship reading a manifest.

Her mother had been dead for nineteen years. Rosalind had processed the claim herself, at twenty-two, fresh-licensed and shaking, because that was how things worked here: the colony's only adjuster handled her own griefs, stamped them, filed them, and went home to an empty flat. **Cause of loss: respiratory failure. Claim status: settled. Payout: applied against outstanding colonial debt.** Nineteen years of interest on a debt Earth insisted the whole colony owed it, deducted from every wage, attached to every birth certificate like a barnacle.

And E. Coldharbour had not been born on Terminal. Nobody over fifty had been. But the manifest didn't just give a name. It gave a birthdate.

Rosalind checked it against the founding charter date in her head, then against the copy pinned inside her notebook cover, because she was thorough even when her hands weren't steady. The date preceded the colony's founding by six years. Preceded, in fact, the *announcement* of the colony by three.

A passenger born before the place existed, listed on a ship that never flew, claiming damages from a fire that never burned.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty office, because saying things aloud kept the dread at a manageable volume. "Someone's idea of a joke."

But jokes were sloppy. Jokes got the details wrong. And the detail this one got wrong — or right — was the name.

Her mother's name was Edith. Everyone on Terminal had spelled it that way, in the school registers, in the clinic logs, in the death filing Rosalind had signed with a pen that skipped. But Rosalind, at nine years old, learning letters, had decided that *Edith* was insufficiently interesting and had spelled it **Eadhyth** on every drawing, every card, every school form she was allowed near. Her mother had found it hilarious and adopted it as a private joke between them, signing birthday notes to her *Eadhyth, obviously*.

Nobody else had ever used that spelling. Rosalind had never written it anywhere as an adult. It existed nowhere in any record, any archive, any database on Terminal or off it.

Except now, glowing softly on a dead ship's manifest:

**COLDHARBOUR, EADHYTH.**

The professional thrill — the lovely, clean, soluble thrill of an impossible claim — curdled in her chest and turned into something older and heavier. Dread, maybe. Or recognition. The feeling of hearing your own name called in a house you thought was empty.

She sat down slowly on a cargo crate that smelled of nothing at all, because even the crates on this ship were pretending.

Below the name, the manifest offered a single additional field. Not a destination. Not a seat assignment. Just a line of coordinates and a flag:

**DELIVER TO: COLONIAL ARCHIVE, SUBLEVEL 4. STATUS: UNDELIVERED. WINDOW CLOSING.**

"Window closing," Rosalind repeated. "What window?"

The terminal, having delivered its impossibility, went dark — not crashed, she noted, but *closed*, politely, like a door shut by a hand.

She sat in the lavender-and-cold-metal dark for a long moment, listening to the tide bells, doing the arithmetic of her own heartbeat. Then she opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in her careful adjuster's hand:

*Claim 4471-HV. Fire, none. Ship, grounded. Passenger, deceased 19 yrs. Birthdate, impossible. Spelling, mine.*

Underneath, after some consideration, she added:

*Mother?*

Then she closed the notebook, climbed down out of a ship that had never flown, and set off across the docks toward the sealed archives, walking quickly, the way people walk when they are trying to outrun a feeling and already know they won't.
