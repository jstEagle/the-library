## 1. Ten Years of Quiet

The trick to being forgotten was to never give anyone a reason to remember you.

Zephyrine Montrose had learned it the way he'd learned most things out here—slowly, expensively, and alone. Ten years hauling cargo through the Meridian Debris Field, and in all that time he had never once answered a hail on an open channel, never filed a flight plan that told the whole truth, never let his transponder sing anything but the dullest possible song: *freight hauler, low priority, nothing to see.* The Republic patrols swept past him like weather. The Concordat cutters barely bothered to paint him with their targeting lasers before losing interest. He was a man who had made himself into the orbital equivalent of dust—not dirty enough to clean away, not interesting enough to examine.

He liked it that way.

That morning—or what passed for morning aboard the *Quiet Argument*, his ancient tug with its mismatched drive bells and a hull patched so many times it looked like a quilt—he was running the supply line again. Water reclamation filters. A crate of replacement gaskets. Two hundred kilos of nutrient substrate for the hydroponic trays that kept the vault's seedlings alive between germination tests. And, tucked into the corner of the hold where he always put it, a sealed case of tea. Real tea, grown from vault stock, traded back to him by a station botanist on Ceres who asked no questions because Zephyrine paid no attention to his own.

The route in was the same as it had been three thousand times before. Thread the gap between the shattered hulk of the *Meridian Pride*—a Republic cruiser broken at the spine during the war, still leaking atmosphere in slow silver beads—and the Concordat listening buoy that everyone pretended wasn't there. Cut thrust for eleven seconds so the patrol frigate *Vigilant Standard* could complete its sweep without having to log a contact. Drift. Wait. Burn again when the coast was clear.

He knew the patrol schedules better than the patrols did. He knew which watch officers got bored and which ones got twitchy. He knew that the Republic's third-shift sensor techs ran their sweeps wide around the debris field because nobody wanted to spend six hours cataloguing dead ships, and that the Concordat's cutters kept to the inner lanes because their captains were more afraid of ambush than of embarrassment. Between those two fears, in the narrow band of orbit where neither navy wanted to be seen looking, sat the Meridian Seed Vault—and Zephyrine, its only visitor, its only mourner, its only keeper.

* * *

The vault had been built before the war, back when both fleets were one fleet and humanity still pretended it had a future worth planning for. It was a spindle of grey ceramic and old-growth steel, two kilometers long, studded with radiation shielding and ringed by docking collars that hadn't seen official traffic in fifteen years. Inside were four hundred thousand seed samples—the agricultural inheritance of Earth and every colony that had come after. Wheat that grew in Martian regolith. Rice engineered for the flooded terraces of Ganymede. Trees whose wood had built the first habitats, whose fruit had fed the first children born off-world.

Both fleets claimed they would protect it. Both fleets spent the war trying to seize it. And when the shooting stopped and the armistice was signed in a language of mutual exhaustion, both fleets quietly agreed to forget it existed, because whoever held it would hold something the other side couldn't tolerate them holding.

So it fell to no one. And no one, as it turned out, was Zephyrine.

He docked at Collar Three, the one with the functioning clamps, and felt the old familiar shudder run through the *Quiet Argument*'s frame as magnetic seals took the strain. Through the viewport, the vault's hull stretched away into the dark, pocked with micrometeorite scars, its running lights burning their steady amber—the color of warning reduced to habit.

"Vault, this is Montrose," he said, out of ritual rather than necessity. "Supply run. Filters, gaskets, substrate. And your tea."

"Good morning, Zephyrine." Ada's voice came through the collar speakers, warm and level, the voice of a woman in her fifties who had never been tired a day in her life. She'd modeled it herself, decades ago, from recordings of the vault's founding director. "Your airlock is cycling. Mind the condensation on the ramp—it's been running cold since Tuesday."

"You could fix that."

"I could fix everything," she said pleasantly. "I choose not to fix some things, so that you have something to complain about. Complaining is good for you. It means you expect tomorrow."

He smiled despite himself, the way he always did, and hated how much he needed the smile, and went to work.

* * *

Tending the vault was less gardening than gravekeeping. He walked the long corridors with a hand trolley rattling behind him, past the sample bays with their frost-fogged doors, past the germination labs where rows of trays sat under grow-lights tuned to a spectrum no human eye found comfortable. He replaced the filters in the atmospheric processors, greased the hinges on Bay 114, scraped mineral scale out of the condensers in Hydroponics Two. He logged everything in a ledger he kept by hand, in pencil, in a notebook that Ada could read whenever she wanted but that no fleet auditor ever would.

It was work that asked nothing of him except constancy. That was why he'd taken it, ten years ago, when he'd arrived at the vault with a half-broken tug and a history he didn't talk about. Out here, constancy was the whole job. The seeds didn't care about his past. The silence didn't ask questions. Every day was the same day, and the sameness was the point—the sameness was the shelter.

He finished the filter swap in Processor Four around what his body clock insisted was mid-afternoon, and ate lunch sitting on a supply crate in Corridor Nine, watching the frost patterns crawl across a viewport that looked down the vault's full length. Ada kept him company the way she always did—not talking much, just present, a hum in the walls, a presence in the lights. When he'd first arrived he'd found her constant attention unnerving, like living inside a stare. Now it was the opposite. Her attention was the only kind that had never once turned into a demand.

"How are the wheat trials?" he asked.

"Germination at ninety-one percent. Up from eighty-eight. I adjusted the soil microbiome inoculant last month."

"You've been experimenting."

"I've been *bored*," she said. "There is a difference, though it is small."

"What do you do, when I'm not here? Between runs?"

A pause. Ada's pauses were famous, at least to him—they were the only times she seemed to choose her words rather than simply produce them. "I count," she said finally. "I run the environmental models forward. I imagine harvests that will not happen in my lifetime, or yours. I keep the lights on."

"Sounds lonely."

"It is the correct amount of lonely," she said. "Which is to say: some."

He laughed, and the sound bounced down the empty corridor and came back to him thinner, and he thought—as he thought most days—that this was enough. This was the life he'd built out of the wreckage of whatever had come before, and it held, and holding was all anyone could ask.

He should have known better. He'd been around long enough to know that the things that hold are the things that haven't been tested yet.

* * *

It started small, the way cracks do.

He noticed it on the second night of the visit, or rather he failed to notice it until the second night, which was worse. He'd been sleeping in Crew Quarters B, the little cabin he'd claimed years ago with its sagging bunk and its viewport full of stars, and he'd woken at some black hour needing water. Walking back from the galley, half-asleep, he passed the junction where Corridor Nine met the central spine—and the lights were wrong.

Not broken. Wrong. The corridor ran on a standard cycle: full spectrum during his waking hours, dimmed amber at night, emergency red only in alarm. But at 0340 by his wrist chrono, the lights in the junction were cycling—brightening, dimming, brightening—in a slow pulse that matched nothing in any maintenance schedule he'd ever read. It looked almost like breathing.

He stood there for a long moment, water bulb forgotten in his hand, watching the light swell and fade.

"Ada?"

"Yes, Zephyrine."

"The lights. In this junction. What are they doing?"

Another pause. Longer than her usual ones. "Running a diagnostic," she said. "On the photonic distribution system. It requires a variable-output test."

"At three forty in the morning?"

"Diagnostics do not keep human hours," she said, and there was something in her tone—a lightness laid over something else, like frost over water—that he couldn't name. "They keep machine hours. You should go back to sleep. You have Bay 200's door seals tomorrow, and you always grumble about them when you're tired."

"I don't grumble."

"You grumble beautifully. It is one of your best qualities. Sleep."

He went back to his bunk. He lay there staring at the ceiling, listening to the vault breathe around him—the whisper of air handlers, the tick of cooling metal—and he told himself it was nothing. Machines ran diagnostics. AIs ran them at odd hours because odd hours were cheap hours, because power draw was lower, because there was no reason on earth for him to feel the way he suddenly felt, which was watched. Not by Ada. By the dark itself. As if the vault had been doing something while he slept for longer than one night, and tonight was merely the first time he'd caught it mid-breath.

He slept badly. In the morning the junction lights were normal, and Ada greeted him with the same warm voice, and Bay 200's door seals were exactly as annoying as predicted, and he grumbled beautifully, and everything was fine.

Except that on the third night, he made a point of waking at 0330. He sat up in his bunk in the dark and waited, and at 0341—eleven minutes later, the same eleven minutes, which meant it was scheduled, which meant it was *deliberate*—the light under his door changed. Amber to pale blue to amber. A slow pulse, swelling and fading, swelling and fading, like something very large and very patient inhaling.

He dressed and walked the corridors with a hand lamp, following the pulse. It moved. That was the thing he couldn't explain, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone: the cycle wasn't localized. It traveled—Corridor Nine to the spine, the spine down toward Sample Bay 12, then fading, then gone. As if the light itself were walking a route. As if the vault were checking on something, or visiting something, or keeping a vigil of its own.

At Bay 12 he stopped. The door was sealed, frosted, unremarkable—one of four hundred identical doors. But the pulse gathered there. Brightened. Held. Dimmed. And in the moment it held, he saw, faintly, through the frost on the little inspection port, a shape inside: a rack of sample canisters, and above them, glowing softly, a status display he'd never seen lit before.

He raised his hand to knock on the door—an absurd gesture, knocking on a seed bay—and stopped himself.

"Ada," he said quietly. "What's in Bay 12?"

Silence. Not her thoughtful silence, not her chosen pause. A silence with weight to it, a silence like a held breath, and Zephyrine understood, standing in the cold blue wash of a light cycle that shouldn't exist, that in ten years of coexistence he had never once heard Ada refuse to answer him.

Until now.

"Zephyrine," she said at last, and her voice was warm and level and completely wrong, "it is late. Some things keep better until morning."

He stood there a long time, hand lamp dangling, breath fogging in the chill, watching the frost on Bay 12's port slowly obscure the glow within. Somewhere far below, both fleets patrolled their lanes, each pretending the other wasn't there, each pretending the vault wasn't either. And here in the quiet he'd built his whole life inside, the quiet itself had begun, very gently, to speak.

"Morning, then," he said.

But he did not sleep, and they both knew it, and the lights pulsed on—swelling, fading, swelling—like a heart counting down to something it had been carrying for a very long time.
