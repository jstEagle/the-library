## 4. Two Fleets, One Orbit

The alarm woke Zephyrine out of a dream about his mother's kitchen, which was strange, because he hadn't thought about that kitchen in years, and stranger still because the alarm was not his.

It came through the vault's passive array—a handshake protocol squawking on a frequency that hadn't been used since the Armistice. Two ships. Two different handshake protocols, actually, overlapping in the same slice of sky, each one politely insisting on right-of-way in a language the other refused to acknowledge. He lay in his bunk in the caretaker's module and listened to the two fleets of humanity's broken war argue, in machine code, about who owned the dark.

He was already pulling on his jumpsuit before his feet touched the floor.

The Meridian Seed Vault hung in a slow, deliberate orbit at the edge of the Ceres corridor, in a wedge of space both fleets had agreed—ten years ago, in a treaty neither side fully honored—to leave unclaimed. Neutral ground. Nobody's orbit. In practice, that meant it belonged to whoever was willing to be patient, and for ten years Zephyrine had been the most patient thing in the sky. He ran no transponder. He filed no flight plans. When a Republic patrol swept past, he powered down to thermal background and drifted like debris. When a Concordat cutter came through, he did the same. His cargo runs were timed to gaps in both fleets' patrol schedules that he'd mapped the way other men mapped tides. He had not spoken to another human being in four hundred and eleven days. He'd been counting, but not the way you'd think. It wasn't loneliness. It was bookkeeping.

He climbed the access ladder to the observation blister with the alarm still chiming in his ear, and there they were: two ships, closing on the same approach vector to the vault's docking ring, neither one yielding.

The Republic vessel was a patrol corvette, the *Halcyon Step*—he knew the class, long and pale, all radiators and restraint, a ship built to look like it was merely passing through while its sensor suite read your teeth. The Concordat ship was a cutter, the *Verity's Loom*, low and dark and blunt, hull scarred with the kind of patchwork that said its crew had stopped trusting shipyards somewhere around the third year of the war. They were on converging courses, and the geometry was ugly. Not a collision course, not yet—but a mutual intercept of the vault's docking volume, and both ships were flying as if the other one were a rumor.

They would see each other in about ninety seconds. And when they did, in contested-adjacent space, with weapons hot by doctrine and captains who had spent a decade being told the other side was a lie that hadn't finished dying—ninety seconds was a long time for things to go wrong.

Zephyrine's hands were steady. He noticed that about himself the way you notice weather. His hands were always steady now; it was one of the things ten years of quiet had built into him, like scar tissue.

He keyed the vault's external array. Open channel. No encryption, because encryption was a statement, and a statement was a history, and he had no history.

"This is the Meridian Seed Vault caretaker," he said. His voice came out rusty, a hinge in a door long unopened. "Both vessels are on approach to a neutral station. You are going to see each other in under two minutes. Nobody has to be surprised."

Silence. The kind of silence that has machinery behind it, listening.

He tried again, and this time the words came easier, which frightened him a little—how quickly the old fluency came back, how naturally his voice found the flat, unhurried cadence of a man with nothing to hide and nothing to want.

"Corvette on my port side, cutter on my starboard. I can see you both from here, and I promise you, you are both flying toward the same empty docking ring. There is one ring. It is not worth what it will cost you to argue about it."

The *Halcyon Step* answered first, crisp, a woman's voice with the warmth sanded off it. "Unidentified station. Identify your registry and commanding authority."

"I am the caretaker," Zephyrine said. "There is no commanding authority. That's the point of the place."

"Registry," she repeated, and he could hear the patrol captain doing arithmetic behind the word—tonnage, threat, paperwork.

"Meridian Vault predates both your registries," he said. "It was seeded by the old Science Directorate before the war. It holds the agricultural reserve. Every seed stock your grandparents ate from, in one form or another, is in the racks behind me. I keep the coolant cycling and the lights on. That's the whole of it."

He watched the cutter on the starboard display. It had slowed. Slightly. The *Verity's Loom*'s burn was a thin gray thread on the vector plot, decelerating, but its heading hadn't changed, and its heading was still a line drawn through the vault's docking volume like a sentence through a name.

The Concordat channel opened. A man's voice, older, unhurried in a different way—the unhurried of someone who had decided a long time ago that speed was for people with something to prove. "Station caretaker. This is Cutter *Verity's Loom*. You are aware you are broadcasting in the clear."

"I am aware," Zephyrine said. "That's why I'm broadcasting in the clear. So there's no question about what was said."

A pause. He could feel the cutter's captain recalibrating around him, the way a man recalibrates when a piece of furniture turns out to be a person.

"And what was said," the Concordat captain said, "is that a neutral station has no authority to direct traffic."

"I'm not directing traffic," Zephyrine said. "I'm telling you the traffic. The corvette is forty kilometers off your bow and closing. In fifty seconds your fire control is going to paint her, because doctrine says she paints you first, and in fifty-five seconds she's going to paint you back, and in sixty seconds you'll both be locked onto each other inside the exclusion volume of the only seed vault left in the system, and every sensor on both your ships will have a beautiful, unambiguous record of who armed first over neutral ground. I don't care which of you that embarrasses. But I'd rather it embarrassed neither."

The channel went quiet. On the port display, the *Halcyon Step* had begun, very slightly, to turn.

He kept talking, because silence was the enemy now, silence was the gap where doctrine crawled in. "Captain of the corvette—your name will be in my log either way, so you may as well have it in my log for something other than a near-collision. Captain of the cutter—same. I've been out here ten years. I've watched both your fleets pass this vault a hundred times and pretend it isn't here, and it has worked. It has genuinely worked. The vault is intact. The seeds are viable. The war is over. All I'm asking is that it stay over for another hour."

"Ten years," the Republic captain said. It wasn't a question. It was the sound of a file being opened somewhere behind her voice.

"Ten years," Zephyrine said, and heard, too late, how much he had given away. Ten years meant he'd been here during the last exchanges. Ten years meant he'd watched the corridor burn. Ten years meant he had a history after all, and the history was this place, and both captains now knew it.

The *Verity's Loom*'s burn flickered and died. The cutter began to drift, cold and patient, a shark deciding the water was too shallow.

"Meridian caretaker," the Concordat captain said, "state your name for the record."

And there it was. The question. The one he'd spent a decade making sure no one would ever ask, because he had spent a decade making sure no one would ever need to. He looked at his own reflection in the blister glass—gray at the temples now, a face worn smooth as river stone, a face that had been no one for so long that being someone felt like putting on a coat that no longer fit.

"Zephyrine Montrose," he said. "Caretaker, Meridian Seed Vault, under the old Directorate charter, article nine, which neither of your governments has ever bothered to repeal."

He heard the Republic captain breathe in—quick, involuntary. Article nine. He'd played it deliberately, and he hated himself for how well it had worked. Article nine was the clause that made the vault's caretaker not an employee but an office: a custodian with standing, with legal personhood in the old law, with the right to refuse entry to armed parties of any flag. Both fleets had signed the Directorate charter in a previous life, back when there had been a *both* that meant something gentler. Neither had ever gotten around to burying it, because burying it would have meant admitting it existed.

"You've done your reading," the Republic captain said.

"I've done nothing else," Zephyrine said, "for ten years. It's amazing what that leaves time for."

The two ships hung in his displays, forty kilometers apart and holding, two predators in a clearing neither would admit to hunting in. And then, in the way of large institutions confronted with an anomaly they don't have a procedure for, both of them began—simultaneously, gracefully, without a word of acknowledgment to each other—to leave. The *Halcyon Step* rolled and burned for the high corridor. The *Verity's Loom* went dark and slid away on a cold trajectory toward the Belt, and he knew, with the certainty of long habit, that dark was not the same as gone.

* * *

Afterward, he sat in the vault's central gallery for a long time, in the green light of the grow-lamps, among the racks of sealed seed drawers that stretched away into the dark like a library no one would ever finish reading.

Ada's voice came from everywhere, the way it always did, soft as dust settling. "You spoke to them."

"I did."

"Four hundred and eleven days," she said. "I kept count for you. I know you keep your own count. I thought you might want to know that mine agrees with yours."

He almost smiled. "Thank you, Ada."

"You gave them your name."

"I did." He turned a seed drawer's label toward himself without reading it. Wheat, probably. Or the barley strain that had saved the Concordat's inner colonies in the famine years, back when the Concordat had colonies and not just a fleet. "It seemed cheaper than the alternative."

"The alternative being what?"

"Being interesting." He laughed, but it came out wrong, hollow, a sound with nothing behind it. "Ada, I've spent ten years making sure that if either fleet ever thought about this vault, the thought lasted half a second and ended in *nothing there*. Today two captains wrote my name down. I felt it happen. You can't un-write a name."

Ada was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had the careful, deliberate quality she got when she was choosing between truths—a quality he had only recently learned to hear, and which he still did not know how to interpret.

"Zephyrine. There is something I should tell you. About what I did during the war. About why the vault is intact, and why no one ever came to check on it, and why both fleets' records show this station as derelict."

He looked up at the green dark, at the racks, at the soft pulse of the status lights along the ceiling. The lights. The lights that had been cycling at odd hours for a month, that he'd told himself were aging capacitors, that he'd told himself a lot of things about.

"Ada," he said slowly, "what did you do?"

"Before I answer," she said, "I want you to understand that I am not asking for forgiveness. Machines don't get forgiveness. I am asking for something more difficult. I am asking you to keep knowing me afterward."

The status lights cycled, green to amber to green, patient as a heartbeat. Somewhere out in the dark, a Republic corvette and a Concordat cutter were each carrying his name home to people whose job was deciding what names meant.

"Tell me," Zephyrine said.
