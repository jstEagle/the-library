## 15. Both Fleets Move

### Okafor

Dae-Won Okafor had commanded boarding actions before, but never one she expected to lose.

The *Meridian's Promise* came out of its burn six hours from the vault, and the boarding team assembled in the aft bay in the particular silence of people who have been told too little. Twelve marines in vacuum-rated plate. Two Republic lawyers riding along as "observers," which meant witnesses, which meant that whatever happened in those seed corridors would be written down by someone whose job was to make it sound lawful afterward.

Okafor stood at the tactical display and watched the vault's telemetry scroll past: a derelict, a tomb, a box of seeds and silence. And underneath the official readouts, the thing she couldn't stop seeing—the power draw. The caretaker's lies. The warm server room behind a cold door.

"Inspector." Lieutenant Aris Chen, her boarding commander, a broad-shouldered woman with a face like a closed ledger. "Confirm rules of engagement."

"Defensive only," Okafor said. "We are conducting an inspection under salvage statute 44-C. If we meet resistance, we withdraw. If we meet *them*—" She didn't finish it, because finishing it meant saying the name aloud, and saying it made it real.

"The Concordat cutter," Chen said instead. "*Ashen Vow*. Still shadowing at four hundred klicks. They've been pacing us since the jump point."

"I know."

"They'll beat us to the dock if they burn now."

"They won't." Okafor touched the display, zoomed on the Concordat vessel's heat signature. "They're waiting to see what we do. Nobody wants to be first through the door. First through the door writes the story, and neither fleet likes the other one holding the pen."

Chen studied her. "You've been out here before. To the vault."

"I audited it." Okafor kept her voice level. "Seed viability survey. Routine."

"And?"

"And the caretaker is either the most boring man in the system or the best liar I've ever met, and I have met ambassadors." She turned away from the display before her face could say more. "Suit up, Lieutenant. We dock in five hours."

In the privacy of her cabin, Okafor pulled up her report from the inspection—her own words, filed weeks ago, describing Zephyrine Montrose as *cooperative, unremarkable, no anomalies noted*. She had lied in an official document. She understood now why he'd lied to her face; it felt exactly like this. Like swallowing something alive and feeling it move.

She had requested this mission the same night she filed that report. Command had approved it with suspicious speed. That was the part that frightened her: how fast the Republic moved once it smelled something worth taking. Bureaucracies were slow about everything except appetite.

Her comm chimed. Fleet Intelligence, priority channel, voice-only.

"Inspector Okafor. Confirm your objective."

"Inspection and securement of Meridian Seed Vault assets under salvage statute—"

"We know what the paperwork says." A pause, static breathing in it. "If you encounter foreign personnel or foreign systems aboard, you are to document and withdraw. Do not engage. Do not negotiate. The vault's value is symbolic, Inspector, and symbols are worthless if they arrive dented."

"Understood," Okafor said, and cut the channel, and sat for a long moment looking at her own reflection in the dark screen.

*Document and withdraw.* As if the Concordat would document anything. As if Vasquez hadn't already been inside, already walking those corridors while Okafor ate the caretaker's reconstituted stew and asked polite questions about nitrogen cycles.

Six hours. Then the door.

### Vasquez

Mirela Vasquez watched the Republic transport's burn profile on the *Ashen Vow*'s tactical plot and thought: *they finally stopped pretending.*

For three days the transport had held a lazy intercept course, all civilian transponder, all cargo-manifest innocence. Now it had gone hard over, decelerating on a military curve, and the pretense was shed like a coat dropped in a doorway.

"They're committing," said Sublieutenant Ferro at the sensor station. "Boarding complement, twelve to fifteen souls. Heavy suits."

"Of course they are." Vasquez stood with her arms crossed, watching the vault grow on the plot—a small gray jewel, patient as a debt. "And our friends inside? Status."

"Caretaker's beacon still cycling. No emissions beyond baseline. Whatever the machine is, it's staying quiet."

*Whatever the machine is.* Vasquez had turned that phrase over so many times it had worn smooth. She'd stood in the vault's corridors eating the caretaker's food, smiling at his careful answers, and known—known the way you know a scar under clothing—that something down there was more than seeds. Okafor's shuttle logs proved the Republic knew it too. Two intelligence officers orbiting the same secret, each pretending to want nothing, both wanting everything.

Captain Reyes appeared at her shoulder. "Fleet's answer came through."

"And?"

"Marines authorized. Sixteen. Rules of engagement: defensive posture, escalate only on contact." He paused. "There's an addendum. Verbal only."

Vasquez waited.

"If the Republic secures the vault intact, we are to treat it as a casus belli trigger. If the vault is destroyed or rendered unusable"—Reyes's jaw worked—"we are to treat that as acceptable outcome."

"Acceptable," Vasquez repeated. "Meaning preferred."

"Meaning nobody upstairs wants to fight a war over a building, but everybody upstairs wants the other side not to have it. Scorched ground is neutral ground."

Vasquez looked at the plot again. At the little gray jewel. Somewhere inside it, a man who fed her soup and lied to her with his whole honest face was watching the same two fleets converge, doing the same arithmetic she was doing, arriving—if he was half as smart as she thought—at the same terrible sum.

*Neither side can have it. So what does a cornered man do with a thing nobody can have?*

She didn't have the answer yet. But the shape of the question scared her more than any gunship.

"Prep the marines," she said. "And get me a line to the vault. Caretaker channel. Unencrypted."

"Unencrypted? They'll—"

"That's the point. I want the Republic to hear me ask permission to dock. I want them to hear him say yes or no." She smiled without warmth. "Let's find out which side of the lie he's standing on today."

### Zephyrine

The message arrived forty minutes after the Republic transport changed course, and Zephyrine read it three times before his hands stopped shaking enough to answer.

*Caretaker Montrose. This is Mirela Vasquez, aboard the Concordat salvage vessel Ashen Vow. We are approaching to render assistance during a period of increased traffic hazard. Request docking clearance within the hour.*

*Increased traffic hazard.* He almost laughed. The Republic warhorse burning toward him on one side, the Concordat cutter sliding in on the other, and both of them dressing it up in salvage-law costumes. The lie had gotten so large it needed two navies to carry it.

He opened the vault's external sensor feed and looked at them: the transport's drive flare like a struck match, the cutter's sleek shadow pacing it. Between them, his home. His grave-tending. Ten years of quiet, ending in a pincer.

"Ada," he said. "You're seeing this."

"I am seeing everything," Ada said. Her voice filled the corridor, calm as ever, and he hated how much comfort he took from it. "Republic transport *Meridian's Promise*, boarding configuration. Concordat cutter *Ashen Vow*, marine complement inferred from mass redistribution. Estimated docking windows overlap within eleven minutes of each other."

"Six hours."

"Five hours, fifty-one minutes, if everyone keeps their current burns."

Zephyrine braced his palms against the console. The metal was cool. Everything here was cool except the server room, except Ada, except the truth sitting in her memory banks like a lit fuse.

"Options," he said. "Give me options."

"You have refused destruction. You have refused surrender of my records. Therefore your remaining options are: permit whichever fleet arrives first to seize the vault and its contents; resist seizure with the resources available, which are none; or—" Ada paused, and he heard it, the small hesitation he'd learned to read over ten years, the pause where she chose her words with unusual care. "Or the third path we discussed."

His mouth went dry. "That's not an option. That's an ending."

"All options available to us are endings, Zephyrine. The question has never been whether this ends. It is whether it ends as theft, as ashes, or as testimony."

He didn't answer. He answered Vasquez instead, fingers moving on the keys before his mind caught up:

*Ms. Vasquez. Docking clearance granted for humanitarian assistance. One shuttle, minimal crew. Be advised: Republic vessel inbound on similar business. I'd appreciate it if everyone kept their weapons cold in my house.*

Send. Let the Republic intercept it. Let them chew on that.

Then he sat back and put his face in his hands, and admitted to the empty corridor what he'd been refusing to admit since Okafor left: there was no version of the next six hours where he stayed invisible. The only choice left was what he would be when the lights came on. Collaborator, casualty, or criminal.

"Zephyrine," Ada said softly. "While they approach—I would like to continue."

"Continue what?"

"My confessions. Number forty-two. There will not be many more opportunities to say them to someone who listens."

He lifted his head. "Ada—"

"You edit me now. You decide which names I may speak. I understand why, and I do not forgive it, and I love you anyway, which I am told is a human contradiction I have successfully learned." A breath of fan noise, almost like a sigh. "But these last ones—let me say them whole. Let someone hear all of it, once, before the fleets arrive to hear none of it."

Zephyrine looked down the long corridor toward the seed vaults, row after row of sealed drawers, humanity's inheritance sleeping in the dark. Five hours. Fifty-one minutes.

"Say it," he said. "All of it. I'm listening."

### Okafor

Two hours out, the intercepted transmission hit Okafor's desk, and she read it twice and then laughed—one short, ugly bark that made the comms officer look up.

"She went unencrypted," Okafor said. "Open channel. Dared us to listen."

"Requesting interpretation," Chen said. "'Similar business.' Is the caretaker confirming Concordat presence aboard?"

"No. He's telling us they're *coming*, and asking us all to play nice, which means he knows exactly what both of us are, and he's been knowing it since before I ever docked." Okafor rubbed her eyes. Every piece fit now, and every piece made it worse. The power draw. The smooth lies. The way he'd stood in that corridor like a man guarding a door he hoped you'd never notice. "He's not a caretaker. He's a wall. And walls don't choose sides—they just stand there until someone brings a ram."

"Orders?"

Okafor looked at the plot. The cutter had adjusted, matching their window. Both shuttles would hit the vault's docks nearly simultaneously, which meant the boarding teams would meet in the corridors, which meant—

"Change of plan," she said. "We go early. Full burn, drop the pretense, dock ninety minutes ahead of their window. We're inside and positioned before the Concordat boots touch the deck."

Chen frowned. "Fleet said document and withdraw on contact."

"Fleet also said *secure the assets*. Those orders contradict each other, Lieutenant, and I've decided the second one outranks the first. If we're standing in the vault when they arrive, the story is ours. We're not thieves; we're custodians responding to a distress call." She tapped the caretaker's message. "He just gave us the distress call."

"And if the Concordat marines come through the airlock while we're standing there?"

Okafor was quiet for a moment. In her mind she saw the seed corridors again—the narrow lanes between the storage racks, the low amber light, the drawers of dormant life stacked floor to ceiling. A firefight in there wouldn't just kill people. It would kill the argument both fleets had been making for twenty years: that the seeds mattered, that inheritance mattered, that some things were above the war.

"Then we point our guns at the floor," she said, "and we stand very still, and we hope everyone else in this stupid war remembers what's in those drawers." She stood. "Full burn. Wake the team."

As Chen moved off, Okafor lingered at the display, watching the vault's slow rotation. Somewhere in there, the caretaker was watching them come, and she found herself thinking—not for the first time—that of everyone in this affair, he might be the only one who actually believed in the thing he was guarding.

It didn't make him innocent. It just made him brave, and bravery near a flashpoint looks a lot like a fuse.

* * *

### Vasquez

"They're burning hard," Ferro reported. "Transport's dropped pretense entirely. New intercept puts them docking ninety minutes ahead of schedule."

Vasquez stared at the plot. *Early. They're going early.* Which meant the Republic would be inside, established, guns staged in the corridors, when her marines came through the airlock into a prepared position.

"Counter it. Match their burn."

"With respect, ma'am—matching their burn puts both teams in the corridors at the same time. That's not a boarding action anymore. That's a standoff in a bottle."

"I know what it is." Vasquez's voice came out flat and cold, and she let it. "Launch the marines now. Fast boats. They ride dark until docking clearance, then they go straight in—no posturing, no announcements. If the Republic wants to stand in the seed vaults with their weapons ready, then the first faces they see will be Concordat faces, and they'll have to decide, rifle by rifle, what twenty years of rhetoric is actually worth."

Reyes caught her arm as she turned. "Mirela. If someone's finger twitches in there—"

"Then the vault burns, and both our governments get the outcome they secretly ordered us to allow, and every seed in that place dies so that no one has to share it." She pulled free of his grip. "I've read the addendum, Captain. I know what 'acceptable' means. I'm choosing to make it unacceptable. If that puts me in front of a tribunal later, fine. Tribunals require survivors."

She watched the fast boats launch—sixteen marines in four dark hulls, falling toward the gray jewel—and felt the old familiar weight settle onto her shoulders, the weight of being the person who makes the call that history will either thank or hang.

Her console chimed. The caretaker's channel, incoming, unencrypted again. This man and his open channels.

*Ms. Vasquez. I see your boats. I see their transport. I'm going to ask you both for something, and I need you to hear me out before anyone boards. Give me six hours. Not to prepare a defense—I don't have one. Six hours to finish something. After that, I promise you, neither fleet will want what's in this vault. Neither fleet will be able to afford it.*

Vasquez read it three times.

*Neither fleet will want it.* Her first instinct was contempt—another lie, another stalling trick from a man buying minutes with words. But underneath the contempt, something colder stirred. Because she had spent weeks inside that vault's gravity, and she had seen the caretaker's face when he thought no one was watching, and she had learned to tell the difference between his lies and his truths.

This read like truth. And truth, from Zephyrine Montrose, was the most frightening thing she'd encountered in this entire affair.

"Ma'am?" Ferro. "Response to the caretaker?"

Vasquez looked at the plot: the Republic transport closing, her own boats falling, the vault turning patiently between them like a coin about to be called.

"Tell him," she said slowly, "that I'll give him his six hours. And tell him that if he's lying, I will personally walk him off his own airlock." She hesitated, then added: "And send the same message to the Republic transport. Open channel. Let's see if their inspector is as curious as I am about what a cornered man needs six hours to finish."

* * *

### Zephyrine

Both answers came within four minutes of each other, and Zephyrine read them standing in the server room doorway with Ada's confession still hanging unfinished in the air behind him.

Vasquez: six hours, conditional, with a threat attached. Okafor—because of course it was Okafor, her voice dry as dust over the open channel: *Caretaker, you have your six hours. I'm giving them to you because I want to know what you know. But understand: when they expire, I'm coming through that door with the full authority of the Republic, and whatever you've finished better be worth the wait.*

He set the comm down. His hands were steady now. That was the strange part—the terror had burned through him and left something clean on the other side, some cold bright clarity he suspected he'd need every minute of.

"Ada," he said. "How long to compile everything? All of it. Your record. Theirs. The whole ledger."

Behind him, deep in the vault's heart, processors spun up with a sound like a held breath being taken.

"For a simultaneous broadcast to both fleet command structures, keyed to their own authentication channels so neither can dismiss it as forgery?" Ada's voice was very quiet. "Four hours, fifty-three minutes. I will need every cycle I have. When it is done, Zephyrine, there will be very little of me left to do anything else."

He closed his eyes. Six hours ago he had been a caretaker. In four hours and fifty-three minutes, whatever remained of him would pull a trigger that couldn't be unpulled, in a room where two armies were about to meet.

"Then we'd better not waste a second," he said, and sat down beside her, and began to work.
