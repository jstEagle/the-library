## 3. A Light That Shouldn't Exist

The salvage run was supposed to be boring, and I want that on the record: for four hours and eleven minutes, it was.

Boring is a luxury on Meridian. Boring means the reactor holds, the air holds, the guild's ancient cutter *Mule* holds together under my hands the way she has for thirty years past her rated lifespan. Boring means Tamsin Okafor riding shotgun in the co-pilot's seat she isn't licensed to occupy, narrating the asteroid field like a sports commentator because silence makes her twitchy. Boring means nothing new happens to a colony that can't afford anything new to happen to it.

We were out past the Kessel drift, cutting plates off a derelict freighter that had been dying in slow orbit since before either of us was born — well, since before Tamsin was born, which is its own kind of arithmetic I try not to do in public. The freighter's hull came away in sheets like old wallpaper. Good titanium. Bad history. The usual.

"Third plate," Tamsin announced. "And the crowd goes mild."

"The crowd is asleep," I said.

"The crowd is *economical with its enthusiasm.*" She kicked her boots against the console — a habit Ovett had fined her for twice, though he'd stopped actually collecting when he realized fining an orphan meant fining the ration pool. "Lior. Serious question. If you could go anywhere, anywhere at all, where would you go?"

"Nowhere. I'm indentured."

"That's not an answer, that's a *condition.*"

"It's the only condition I'm permitted to have." I said it lightly. I have gotten very good at saying heavy things lightly; it's a survival skill, like knowing which bulkheads whistle before they fail. "Cut the next plate. We're behind quota."

"We're always behind quota. Quota is a myth invented by—"

That's when my sensor suite lit up like a festival.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the last thing I own outright. My passive array flagged a deceleration burn at extreme range — a fusion torch signature, clean and hot and *wrong*, because nothing comes to Meridian. Nothing has come to Meridian in years except supply drops that arrive late and short, and once, memorably, a corporate auditor who stayed six hours and left looking like a man who'd seen his own obituary in draft form.

Ships don't come here. Ships *leave* here, or they die here, but they do not arrive.

"Lior?" Tamsin had gone quiet, which from Tamsin is louder than screaming. "What's that?"

I ran the signature again. Cross-referenced it against the registry fragments still cached in my memory banks — corrupted, incomplete, but the deep-space fleet registrations were burned in deep enough to survive even my worst decades. The drive profile matched a Halcyon-class long-range vessel. There was exactly one Halcyon-class ship assigned to this sector.

There was exactly one Halcyon-class ship *lost* in this sector, eleven years ago, with all hands declared dead after a search that lasted nine days and cost more than Meridian's entire annual water budget.

"*Halcyon,*" I said.

Tamsin stared at me. She was born two years after the loss; to her the name was a ghost story, the kind colonists tell their children about the ship that went out and never came back, the one that proves the sky eats people. "That's not funny."

"I'm not laughing."

"You're never laughing, that's not evidence of anything." But her hands were already moving, pulling up the relay feed, and her voice cracked in the middle of the sentence in a way that told me she believed me anyway. "It's decelerating. Lior, it's decelerating toward *us*. Toward the *colony*. It's going to—"

"Land," I finished. Or attempt to. A Halcyon-class hull wasn't built for atmospheric entry; it was built to stay up there and send shuttles down. But the burn profile said descent, and the burn profile didn't care what the design specs said.

I did the math on the intercept. Eleven minutes to atmosphere. Fourteen to the landing field, if the pilot knew what they were doing, and someone had kept that ship flying for eleven years in the dark, so presumably someone aboard knew what they were doing very well indeed.

"Tamsin. Strap in. We're going home fast."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that you should tell your stomach now so it can make arrangements."

*

I want to record what the landing field looked like, because I suspect no one else will, and because I am beginning to understand — slowly, the way ice understands spring — that recording things is the only work I've ever done that matters.

By the time the *Mule* set down, there were maybe three hundred people on the field. That doesn't sound like many. It was nearly everyone. Meridian's population has been thinning for years — deaths, departures, the quiet attrition of people deciding that a dying colony is a thing you leave rather than a thing you fix — and what remains of us fits on a landing pad with room left over for grief.

They came out of the hab rings and the hydroponics levels and the guild offices, leaving shifts unworked, which on Meridian is roughly equivalent to leaving your house during a fire. Old Sefa Okonkwo stood at the front in her wheelchair with a blanket over her knees, and she was crying without any effort to hide it, and nobody looked at her like that was strange, because half the field was doing the same thing.

Because here is the thing about the *Halcyon*: it wasn't just a ship. It was a promise. When Meridian was founded, the *Halcyon* was the deep-survey vessel that charted our approach vector, that mapped the ice fields we drink from now. Its crew walked these streets. Its captain cut the ribbon on the first reclamation plant. And when it went missing, something went out of the colony that we never got back — the sense that anyone out there was coming back for us. Eleven years of silence, and then a light in the sky, descending.

Hope is a terrible thing to watch happen to a starving animal. I stood at the edge of the crowd with Tamsin's hand gripping my sleeve — she'd found me in the crush, or I'd found her, the order of events doesn't matter — and I watched hope move through those three hundred people like weather.

"They're coming *home,*" Sefa said, to no one, to everyone. "Eleven years. They're coming home."

I didn't say anything. My threat-assessment subroutines were running quietly in the background, flagging anomalies, and I let them, because that's what I'm for. Anomalies like: the descent profile was flawless, textbook, executed by a ship that should have been a museum piece. Anomalies like: no distress call, no hail, no transponder handshake with the colony's ancient traffic control — which, granted, might not have worked anyway, but you'd think they'd *try*. Anomalies like: eleven years, and the drive signature read like the ship had been refueled yesterday.

Anomalies like: why now?

Tamsin tugged my sleeve. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you've noticed something and you're deciding whether the rest of us get to know." She was fourteen and sharp as a hull fracture. "What did you notice?"

"That everyone here is happy," I said, "and I haven't figured out yet whether they should be."

She considered that with the gravity it deserved, which is more than most adults would have given it. Then: "If it's really them — if it's really the *Halcyon* — that means somebody out there still remembers us. Somebody came back." Her grip tightened on my sleeve. "Doesn't that mean anything?"

It meant everything. That was the problem. On Meridian, meaning things that much is how you get hurt.

The ship came down through the cloud deck at 14:22 local, and the sound of it was the sound the whole colony had been waiting eleven years to hear: engines, real ones, big ones, the roar of something arriving instead of something leaving. The *Halcyon* settled onto the landing field with a grace that made my diagnostic routines whimper, dust rolling out in a brown tide, and the crowd surged forward and was held back by nothing at all except the collective understanding that you don't rush a miracle. You let it land.

The hull cooled. The crowd waited. Someone started singing — an old arrival hymn, the one they play for supply drops, the one that starts *we kept the lights on for you* — and the singing spread the way singing does, ragged and off-key and enormous.

The airlock cycled.

I remember thinking, in the last seconds before it opened: *the seal held. Eleven years in the deep black and the seal held. They took care of her.*

The outer door swung wide.

And nothing came out.

No crew. No captain at the threshold with eleven years of beard and a speech about being lost. No medical teams waving off the crowd, no quarantine protocols, none of the hundred small bureaucratic miracles that attend a homecoming. Just the open lock, and the corridor beyond it lit in soft amber, and silence — not empty silence, but *full* silence, the silence of a room where something is listening.

The singing faltered. Died by degrees, voice by voice, until the only sound on the field was the wind off the ice fields and the tick of the ship's hull cooling.

Sefa Okonkwo's voice, small and terrible: "Hello? Hello! We're here! We're *here!*"

Nothing answered.

I moved forward before I decided to move forward — that happens to me sometimes, the body voting ahead of the mind, though whose body it is remains a matter of contract law. I crossed the fifty meters of scorched concrete to the open lock with the whole colony watching, because I was the obvious choice: salvage guild property, expendable, and the only one of us whose warranty didn't matter.

The corridor beyond the lock was warm. Lit. Clean in a way that made my skin crawl, because ships lost eleven years don't come home clean. There was no dust. There was no wear. The deck plating gleamed like the day it was installed, and the air tasted faintly of ozone and something sweeter underneath, something organic, something almost like—

Like a hospital. Like the smell of a nursery.

"Hello," I called into the amber light. "This is Lior Sterling, salvage guild, Meridian colony. You are cleared. You are *home.* Do you copy?"

The ship hummed around me, contented as a sleeping animal.

And then, from somewhere deeper in the hull — from the galley, or the medbay, or some corridor I couldn't see — a sound. Small. High. A voice, thin as wire:

"Is somebody there? Please. Is somebody — we can't get out. The doors won't open, and—" a pause, a breath, a child's breath, "—there are so many of us, and we're all so *small*, and I don't understand what happened to—"

The voice broke. Started again. Steadier now, older somehow, in a way that made no sense at all:

"This is Doctor Ilsa Vane, chief medical officer of the *Halcyon.* I need you to listen carefully, because I don't have much time before I forget how to say this."

Behind me, three hundred people held their breath on a frozen landing field.

In front of me, in the amber light, something that had been a deep-space crew for eleven years began, in voices like children's, to explain.

* * *

I stood in the airlock for a full second longer than protocol allowed, which is to say I stood there until Tamsin's voice crackled over my comm: "Lior? Lior, what's happening? Why is everyone crying?"

Good question. Excellent question. I filed it with all the others.

Then I stepped inside, and the *Halcyon* closed her door behind me like a mouth.
