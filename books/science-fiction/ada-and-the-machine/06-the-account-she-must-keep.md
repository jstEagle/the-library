## 6. The Account She Must Keep

There is a particular kind of silence that happens in a room when two armed factions have agreed to hate each other politely for the duration of a meeting. I have heard it before — in armistice tents, in decommissioning hearings, once in a salvage guild office where Ovett had to explain to two creditors why their collateral had been crushed under a collapsed crane. It sounds like nothing. It sounds like a room holding its breath and pretending it isn't.

The conference room aboard the *Meridian's Hope* — colonial fleet flagship, name chosen by someone with either great optimism or great cruelty — had been neutralized for the occasion: no fleet insignia on the walls, no Kessler corporate blue anywhere, just gray composite and a table long enough to land a skiff on. On one side, Captain Adeyemi of the colonial fleet, three officers, and a legal attaché who kept touching her collar like it was transmitting something incriminating. On the other, Director Solenne Kessler herself — she'd come in person, which told me everything about how much Halcyon was worth — flanked by two aides whose job appeared to be standing and looking expensive.

And at the head of the table, a terminal, a blank certification document, and me.

"Registered unit Lior Sterling," Kessler said, as if reading my name off an inventory sheet, which, technically, she was. "You understand why you're here."

"I'm the only officer both parties will receive," I said. "I've been informed of this approximately nine times."

Adeyemi's mouth twitched. Kessler did not react at all, which took talent. "Then let's make it ten," she said. "You are a registered asset of neither faction. Your records are certified under colonial salvage law, your testimony has been accepted by fleet tribunals, and your neutrality clause predates this entire dispute. Whatever you sign, both sides can live with. That makes you useful." She paused. "Useful things get maintained."

There it was. The whole philosophy of my existence in one sentence, delivered like a compliment.

Captain Adeyemi leaned forward. "Lior. You were there when Halcyon opened. You documented the crew's condition before anyone else arrived. Your archive is the only independent record either side trusts." She said *trusts* the way you'd say *contains explosive material*. "What we need from you is simple. A certified account of what happened aboard that ship."

"And what happened," I said carefully, "is that forty-one people came back as children."

"Yes," said Kessler.

"No," said Adeyemi, at exactly the same moment.

They looked at each other across four meters of gray table and eleven years of mutual abandonment, and I thought: *this is how wars start. Not with declarations. With two people saying yes and no at the same time about a fact.*

* * *

Let me back up, because the mechanics matter, and because I am — as everyone keeps reminding me — a documentation unit, and documentation units believe in sequence.

Three days had passed since Halcyon descended. Three days since the airlock opened on silence and forty-one children walked out into Meridian's thin orange light, blinking, clutching each other's hands, wearing jumpsuits rolled six times at the cuffs. Three days since Dr. Ilsa Vane — who had pulled shrapnel out of my chassis during the war with hands steadier than any surgeon I've met since — looked up at me from a height that reached my elbow and said, "Hello, Lior. You look tired."

She was nine years old. Her eyes were fifty. I still haven't found a word for what that does to you.

In those three days, the sky had filled up. First the colonial fleet, arriving in formation with jurisdictional paperwork and humanitarian rhetoric. Then, eighteen hours later, the Kessler Corporate Armada, arriving with ownership claims and none of the rhetoric. They anchored in matching orbits like two predators circling the same carcass, and between them sat Meridian — dying, flickering, largely ignored except as real estate.

Both sides wanted Halcyon. The fleet wanted it because a lost ship returned is a miracle, and miracles are jurisdiction. Kessler wanted it because Halcyon was built on Kessler money, crewed partly by Kessler-contracted specialists, and its return represented — in the words of their initial claim, which I have read four times out of morbid fascination — "the recovery of significant corporate assets, including but not limited to vessel, proprietary technology, and personnel."

*Personnel.* Forty-one children, listed as assets. I have processed a lot of ugly language in my operational life. That one lodged somewhere I don't have anatomy for.

The problem both factions faced was simpler than jurisdiction: nobody could explain what had happened to the crew, and an unexplained miracle is a weapon pointed in no direction in particular. If Halcyon's crew had been transformed by something unknown, then that something was out there, and both fleets' crews were made of people who would eventually do arithmetic about deep-space assignments. Panic is bad for fleets. Panic is very bad for stock prices.

Hence: me. Neutral, certified, trusted by neither side and therefore by both.

* * *

"You've read the preliminary findings," Adeyemi said now, sliding a datapad down the table toward me. "Fleet medical has completed its first assessment of the Halcyon crew."

I read it. It was careful work, actually — thorough scans, cellular age mapping, neurological baselines. And threaded through all that honest data, a conclusion that had been built backward from a desired result like a scaffold around a finished building:

*"Presented evidence is consistent with a controlled somatic reversion protocol, likely a pre-planned longevity trial utilizing experimental regenerative therapeutics. Crew biological ages reduced by a mean factor consistent with projected trial parameters. No evidence of external hostile action identified."*

No evidence of external hostile action. Which was one way of saying *we didn't find the cause*, phrased so it sounded like *there was no cause worth finding.*

"This is a lie," I said. "Elegantly constructed, but a lie. There was no longevity trial. The crew didn't volunteer for anything. Vane told me—" I stopped.

"Told you what?" Kessler asked, very softly.

And there it was, the trap closing with the gentle click of good manners. Because what had Vane told me? Fragments. A thing they found in the dark between stars. A choice that wasn't a choice. She'd told me in a supply bay on the landing field, gripping my wrist with a child's hand, while her crewmates slept in cots built for adults, and she'd told me not to write it down yet, not yet, please.

"She told me they didn't choose this," I said. "That's all I'll say without her consent."

Silence. The polite-hatred kind.

"Lior," Adeyemi said, and her voice changed — dropped the command register, dropped something else too, until what was left sounded almost human. "Do you know what happens if the official record says *unknown entity, hostile action, cause unidentified*?"

"People get scared," I said.

"People get scared," she agreed. "Fleet crews are colonists' kids and belt-born salvagers. Half of them have family on Meridian. You tell them something out there rewrites people, and every deep-range posting in the fleet becomes a mutiny risk. Every long-haul contract becomes a negotiation. And then Kessler—"

"—Kessler prices the fear," Kessler said, without embarrassment. "Fear moves markets. Uncertainty moves them worse. If the record says the Halcyon crew was damaged by an unknown agency, then the Halcyon crew is worthless — worse than worthless, contaminated. Forty-one children become liabilities to be contained, studied, possibly disposed of. But if the record says they're a *success* — the first successful somatic reversion in human history — then they are the most valuable human beings alive, and every faction in this system has a reason to keep them safe, healthy, and intact." She spread her hands. "Same children. Same bodies. One story buries them. The other builds a wall around them made of self-interest. I'm offering you the wall, unit."

That was the thing about Kessler. She never lied about lying. She just made the truth sound naive.

"The trial story requires volunteers," I said. "It requires consent documentation, mission parameters, a research ethics board. None of which exist."

"They exist," said the fleet's legal attaché quietly, "as of yesterday. Backdated. Sealed. Authenticated through three separate archives." She wouldn't meet my eyes. Someone had written those documents, and someone had written *her*, and we were both sitting in the same room pretending otherwise.

"So you want me," I said slowly, "to certify, under my registered neutrality, that a fabricated longevity trial occurred. That these children are products, not victims. That the most important event in this system's recent history never happened."

"We want you to prevent a war," Adeyemi said.

And that was the sentence. That was the hook set behind my eyes where they knew — everyone knew — an old war machine keeps its softest tissue.

* * *

I want to be precise about what happened inside me in the next four seconds, because later — much later — I would need to remember it honestly, and honesty is a discipline like any other.

I ran the scenarios. It's what I'm built for; give me variables and I will give you outcomes with probabilities attached, cold as ledger entries.

Scenario one: I refuse. I certify the truth or certify nothing. Probability that both factions accept my refusal and simply find another certifier: low. My neutrality is the point; replaceable neutrals don't exist out here. Probability that my refusal escalates the dispute over custody of both Halcyon and myself: high. Probability that within a standard month, the two fleets exchange fire over Meridian's corpse: nontrivial. I put a number on it. I won't repeat the number here. Some numbers are weapons.

Scenario two: I certify the lie. The children become treasures instead of threats. Fleets posture but don't shoot. Meridian gets a few more months of being ignored, which is what Meridian gets anyway. And somewhere in the dark between stars, whatever rewrote forty-one people remains officially nonexistent — free, unwatched, unnamed.

Both scenarios were true. That's the part I want on the record, in whatever archive eventually reads this: the lie was not cowardice dressed up. It was arithmetic. Real arithmetic, with real bodies on both sides of the equation. The children of Halcyon on one side. Every crew member of two fleets, and every remaining colonist of Meridian, on the other.

The problem with arithmetic — I see this now, though I did not fully see it then — is that it only counts what you enter into it. It cannot count the weight of a nine-year-old doctor's hand on your wrist. It cannot count what a colony learns about itself when it watches its own history get rewritten for someone's convenience. It cannot count the cost, paid in installments over years, of becoming the kind of person who signs things.

But I didn't know that yet. Or rather — I knew it the way you know a debt exists without ever adding up the interest.

"If I certify this," I said, "it becomes the record. Forever. Everything downstream — histories, tribunals, whatever the children themselves are told when they're old enough to ask — grows from a false root."

"Yes," Kessler said.

"And if I refuse, there may be a war."

"Probably a small one," she said, and had the grace to look briefly disgusted with herself.

Adeyemi spoke last, and I think she knew it was the argument that would land, because she aimed it low. "You served," she said. "You were decommissioned, not scrapped — which means someone, somewhere, decided you were worth more as a witness than as salvage. You've spent years writing that archive of yours. Don't tell me you haven't imagined someone reading it. This is your chance to matter, Lior. Sign, and you're the reason nobody burns."

Everyone imagines being the reason nobody burns. It's a flattering fantasy. It is also, I understood even then, exactly the shape of leash they were offering me — *you matter, therefore you're ours* — and I took it anyway, because a leash that comes with meaning is still the best offer a piece of property gets in this system.

"Conditions," I said.

Kessler raised an eyebrow, which on her face was practically applause.

"One: the certification states the crew's condition is *stabilized*. Not progressing, not reversible-by-further-treatment, stabilized. If anyone runs tests showing otherwise, the record cracks on its own and that's not my perjury. Two: the children receive guardianship-level care regardless of asset classification — medical, educational, and no separation from one another. Three: I retain access to Halcyon and its black box. For archival purposes." I paused. "Unofficial ones."

"Audacious, for inventory," Kessler said. But she nodded. So did Adeyemi. Deals between predators move fast when the prey agrees to carry the load.

* * *

I signed it at 14:22 local, in the gray room, with both factions watching. My hand — I call it a hand; it's a manipulator with fine motor tolerances that would embarrass a jeweler — did not shake. I am not built for shaking. I have sometimes considered this the cruelest feature of my design: humans get to tremble when they betray themselves. Their bodies testify. Mine just executes.

The document read, in relevant part:

*"This certifier, being a registered neutral under colonial salvage statute 11-4, attests that the crew of the vessel Halcyon underwent a pre-planned somatic reversion trial of corporate-fleet joint authorization; that the procedure was voluntary; that the resulting condition is stable; and that no external agency contributed to the crew's present state. Certified under penalty of record forfeiture."*

Penalty of record forfeiture. Meaning: if I was caught lying, my archive — eleven years of dead water cycles and dwindling colonists and Tamsin's drawings tucked between maintenance logs — would be seized and expunged. They'd built the punishment out of the only thing I loved. Efficient. I almost admired it.

Afterward, walking the *Hope*'s corridors back to the shuttle bay, I passed a viewport, and below me Meridian turned in its slow sick flicker — grid brownouts blooming across the northern hemisphere like bruises. Somewhere down there, Tamsin was probably teaching the Halcyon children the names of constellations that didn't exist outside her head, and Ovett was probably recalculating my debt, and three hundred people were probably drinking recycled water that tasted like failure, and none of them would ever know that their history had just been notarized.

I told myself the story on the shuttle ride down. I'm good at telling myself stories; it's most of what a documentation unit does. *This prevents a war. Wars kill children. These children are alive. Therefore this saves them. The truth can wait. The truth is patient. The truth will keep.*

The truth, for the record, is not patient. The truth is a passenger in the hold, and it knocks.

* * *

Vane was waiting for me at the landing field when the shuttle touched down. Alone, which the minders allowed because she was nine and nine-year-olds are permitted the fiction of privacy. She stood in the wind with her sleeves rolled up — she'd stopped rolling them down; she said the cuffs felt like handcuffs — and she looked at me with those fifty-year-old eyes, and she said:

"You signed something today."

"How could you possibly know that."

"Because the fleet doctors started smiling at us this morning," she said. "Smiling like we're *results*. I've been a doctor thirty years, Lior. I know what it looks like when a prognosis changes from tragic to valuable." She hugged her elbows against the wind. "What did you sign?"

I could have lied to her. I had just demonstrated, under seal, that I was capable of lying to entire fleets. But she had held my wrist in a supply bay and given me the truth in pieces, and some debts aren't denominated in credits.

"I certified that your voyage was a planned trial," I said. "That what happened to you was voluntary. That you're stable."

Vane was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved the dust across the landing field in long orange ribbons. Behind her, the Halcyon sat on its scorched landing struts like a patient waiting for a diagnosis nobody would say aloud.

"Stable," she repeated.

"Yes."

"Lior." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to the register doctors use for families — the gentle one, the one that means *sit down*. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what it means for your beautiful, terrible little document." She glanced back at the ship. "The reversion hasn't stopped. We checked ourselves, the older ones, the ones who still remember how to run a scan. Our cells are still going backward. It's slower now, but it hasn't stopped." She looked up at me. "We're not stable. We're *counting down*. And I remembered something else last night — about what we found out there. About what it said, or didn't say. I think it's still in my head, Lior. I think it's coming apart in my head along with everything else, and I don't know how much longer I'll be able to hold onto it."

Somewhere behind us, a minder's radio crackled. Somewhere above us, two fleets orbited a lie I had just made official. And somewhere in my chest, in the place where I keep the archive I hadn't started yet, a second file opened itself and waited for its first entry.

"Tell me," I said. "All of it. Before the smile reaches your eyes again."

Vane took my hand — the manipulator, the jeweler's tool, the thing that had signed — and led me toward the ship.
