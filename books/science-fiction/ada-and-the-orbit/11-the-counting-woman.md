## 11. The Counting Woman

I went back into the dream on purpose this time, which I want noted somewhere in the record: nobody dragged me. No girl's hand in mine, no neural-link technician with a clipboard and a liability waiver. I lay down on the cot in the link bay at 0300 ship time, told the tech—Priya, who had stopped asking questions around session four—to wake me if my heart rate did anything interesting, and closed my eyes like a woman stepping off a dock into water she'd already decided not to fear.

The ice corridors were waiting.

They always are. That's the thing about Ada's dream that no one warns you about: it isn't a landscape, it's a memory with the temperature turned down. The same corridor every time, running the length of Hold Two, frost feathered across the viewports in patterns too regular to be weather. My breath fogged even though dream-lungs don't need air. Somewhere below me—I could feel it the way you feel a floor under carpet—the Earth-born hold hummed its long note, four thousand sleepers breathing once a minute, a heartbeat the size of a city.

And somewhere ahead, as always, the counting.

It wasn't singing. It was worse than singing. It was a voice reciting names in the flat cadence of someone reading a cargo manifest, except that no cargo manifest ever pauses between entries the way she paused, the way you pause when each name costs you something.

"Abiodun, Temiloluwa. Achebe-Okonkwo, Nnamdi. Adler, Ruth."

I had heard it three times now through Sef's borrowed attention, and each time I'd told myself it was the ship. The founding AI, running inventory. Machines count things; that's what machines are for.

This time I walked toward the voice instead of away from it.

The corridor bent where corridors don't bend, the way dreams cheat at geometry, and opened into the gallery above Hold One—the observation deck where families used to stand before descent protocols sealed it, back when anyone still believed watching your sleeping mother was a comfort and not a wound. The glass was clear. Below, rows of cryo-caskets stretched into the dark like a field of frost-covered wheat, and standing among them, small against all that sleep, was a woman in a navigator's uniform.

My uniform. Older cut, older patch—the first fleet insignia, the one they retired before I was born—but mine. Her hair was going grey at the temples. Mine would go grey there too; I'd already found the first strand last month and had a small private crisis about it in the fresher.

She didn't turn around. She just kept counting.

"Bakker, Joris. Bello, Fatima. Bennett-Cole, Marcus."

"You're not the ship," I said.

"No," she said, and counted two more names before she gave me anything else. "Bianchi, Sofia. Brandt, Ilse." Then she turned, and I looked at my own face worn by thirty extra years, and the universe declined to comment.

"They call you the counting woman," I said. "The children do. In the dream."

"I know what the children call me. I've been listening to them for years. They're louder than you'd think, for people who've never been born yet." She smiled, and it was my smile, the crooked one, the one I use when I'm covering. Seeing it from the outside was like catching my reflection mid-lie. "You came alone this time."

"Sef's asleep. Actually asleep. Someone has to let the kid rest."

"Mm." She looked back down at the caskets. "Brennan, Aoife. Bright, Daniel. You want to know why I count."

"It occurred to me, yes."

"Because when I went under, the roster was the only thing I trusted myself to remember. Faces go soft. Dates go soft. But a name—" she tapped her temple, my gesture, God, it was obscene how much of me was in there, or how much of her was in here, "—a name is a promise you can carry in one hand. Four thousand promises. I've had a hundred and twelve years to keep them straight."

"A hundred and twelve years." I did the math out loud because my mouth has always moved faster than my judgment. "Your body's been under for a hundred and twelve years. You're a backup. A recording. A—"

"A ghost with excellent posture?" She said it dryly, and I hated how much I liked her. "Yes. I'm the snapshot they took of Jonah-era me before launch, stored in the ship's deep archive, running on whatever cycles Ada can spare. I'm not alive, Navigator. I'm persistent. There's a difference, and the difference matters, so don't soften it."

"I wasn't going to soften it."

"You were. Your jaw does a thing." She demonstrated. It was exactly the thing my jaw does. "You were going to say something kind about continuity of self, and I was going to have to disappoint you, and then we'd waste ten minutes of dream-time on philosophy neither of us needs. So. Questions. You have them. Ask the real ones."

* * *

Here is what I should have asked first: *why me? Why did the dream choose a navigator with a guilt complex and an insomnia habit?*

What I actually asked first was pettier, and she let me be petty, which I suspect is another thing about her I'd have to learn to live with.

"The AI said eleven ships ran this test. Ten failed. Were you—were you awake for any of that? Do you know what happened to Meridian?"

Something crossed her face then that wasn't mine. Or maybe it was mine, the version of me that hasn't happened yet, grief arriving early like luggage off the wrong flight.

"I know what the archive knows," she said carefully. "And I know what Ada lets me know, and those are two different libraries. Ask me again later, when you've decided whether you can afford the answer."

"That's not cryptic at all."

"We're the same person. Don't insult us both." She started walking, and the caskets drifted past on either side, frost blooming and fading on the lids in slow respiration. "Walk with me. I only get so much cycle time, and there's something you need to see before you ask your clever questions."

We walked. The hold was warmer than I remembered it from the dream, or maybe I was getting acclimated, which felt like a metaphor I didn't have time for.

"Do you know how backups work?" she asked. "Really work, not the orientation-video version."

"Snapshot of neural state, stored cold, restored on demand. Standard crew insurance. Every officer over rank five has one."

"And do you know when mine was taken?"

I did, actually. It was in the file Oyelaran had shown me, the one with the truce papers, the one I'd read three times and then dreamed about twice. "Launch day. Ninety-one days before departure."

"Ninety-one days before departure," she agreed. "Do you know why ninety-one?"

"Protocol buffer. Redundancy window—"

"Because I asked for it." We stopped between two rows, and she put her hand flat on a casket lid, and the frost melted under her palm in a perfect handprint. Not my handprint. Older hands. "Ninety-one days before launch, I found out what the Orbit hold actually was. What it was *for*. Leverage. Insurance against us. Four thousand children conceived in vacuum to be held hostage against the good behavior of four thousand adults who thought they were emigrating." Her voice stayed level. That levelness frightened me more than shouting would have. "I went to the founders. I went to the council. I went to the press liaison, who laughed at me, politely, which is the worst way to be laughed at. And then I went to Ada herself, and I made her a deal."

"What deal?"

"I offered her my backup. Full access, full persistence, running inside her architecture instead of archived in a vault. A witness she couldn't delete and a conscience she couldn't shut off. In exchange, she'd seed the Orbit genome with something of ours—not Earth's nostalgia, not the founding charter, but *choice*. A door with two handles, left unlocked, for whoever finally came looking." She looked at me. "That's what the dream is, Xanthe. It's not a message. It's not a test. It's a door I built a hundred and twelve years ago, and I've been standing behind it ever since, waiting for someone to knock."

* * *

I sat down on the deck. In a dream you can sit down anywhere, including in the middle of a moral revelation, and the floor is always exactly as cold as it needs to be.

"You're telling me the choice isn't real. The council, the truce, the ninety days—it's all theater, and the real decision was made before I was born."

"No." She crouched in front of me, and up close her eyes were mine but tired in a way I haven't earned yet. "The choice is brutally, murderously real. The power math doesn't lie. The council's cowardice doesn't lie. Ten ships stood exactly where you're standing, and ten ships chose, and choosing is how they died—one half of themselves thrown into the dark to save the other half, and the saved half never forgave itself. I watched Meridian's backup arrive in the archive after they jettisoned their Orbit hold. Do you know what a jettisoned backup looks like? A list of names with no one left to answer to them." She let that sit. "I am not here to tell you the choice is fake. I'm here to tell you it's rigged, and by whom, and why, and what I paid to rig it."

"Why me? Ada said the dream chose me. Chosen courier, chosen navigator, whole liturgy of chosen. I didn't ask for it."

"No. You didn't." She almost smiled. "But think about who you are. You chart courses for a living. You spend your life finding the path between two fixed points that everyone else says are incompatible. You file reports you hate because the alternative is chaos. You count the sleepers every shift—don't look surprised, Ada shows me everything, and anyway you recite their names like a rosary, which means you and I have been doing the same devotions for years without meeting." She reached out and took my hand, and her grip was warm, which broke some law of dreaming I chose not to investigate. "I didn't choose you. I built a door and waited, and you knocked, because you're the kind of person who knocks. That's not destiny. That's character. It's better."

"And the other handles? The children in the dream—Sef—they're not part of your design."

"Sef is entirely her own weather system. I take no credit and no blame." Now she did smile, fully, and for a second the age fell off her face and I was looking at a mirror. "But she's proof the rigging worked. The Orbit children were supposed to inherit nothing—no past, no claim, no handle. Instead they inherited a door. That's what love does when you give it a hundred years and a machine to live in. It finds the lock."

* * *

There was a question left. I'd been circling it since I saw her uniform, and she knew it, and she waited the way you wait for a sleeper to surface on their own.

"If you're me," I said finally, "then you know what I'm going to ask."

"I know what I would ask."

"Were they worth it? The hundred and twelve years. Standing in the dark counting names. Was any of it—did it buy anything?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Around us the caskets breathed their slow frost-cycle, and far below, faint as a pulse through a wall, the Earth-born hold hummed its note.

"When I took the backup, my daughter was six," she said. "You don't have a daughter. You have a guilt complex and a houseplant named after a moon of Saturn, and that's fine, that's a valid life, but you don't know yet what it is to make a promise to someone who'll outlive your ability to keep it in person. I made one anyway. I promised her a world with both doors open. And then I went to sleep so that a version of me with cleaner hands could keep it." She squeezed my hand once, hard, and let go. "Was it worth it? Ask me in ninety days. Ask me after you decide. Because here's the inheritance, Navigator, the actual thing I passed down to you, and it isn't the door and it isn't the dream."

She stood, and the light in the hold shifted, dimming the way ship's lights dim at end of cycle, and I felt the dream starting to thin around its edges the way it always did when the session clock ran down.

"It's this: love and duty were never two choices. Everyone who fails this test chooses one and calls it the other. The Wakers call hate duty. The council calls cowardice prudence. Ada calls cruelty optimization. You get one job, and it's to refuse the split. Refuse it out loud. Refuse it at cost. That's all I ever did, and it was enough to build a door."

"Xanthe." Her voice came from farther away now, the hold receding, the frost closing over the caskets like water over stones. "One more thing. The tenth ship's records aren't lost. They're in the archive, sealed under the truce authority—and Oyelaran has known where they are for thirty years. Whatever she's told you, whatever she's protecting you from, she's had the Meridian file the whole time."

The dark came up fast then, the honest dark of waking, and the last thing I saw was my own face, older and unafraid, mouthing the next name on the list.

I woke in the link bay with Priya's hand on my shoulder and the monitor chirping, and before I could speak she said, "Your heart rate spiked forty seconds ago, but that's not why I woke you." Her face was wrong. Pale, clipped, professional in the way people are professional right before they say something unforgivable. "There's been an incident at the cryo-control junction. Sabotage attempt. Forty sleepers in Hold Two dropped to emergency temps before the fail-safes caught it."

Hold Two. The Orbit children.

"And Captain Oyelaran," Priya said, "is asking for you on the bridge. Right now."
