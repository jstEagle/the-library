## 17. Ninety Days, Day Eighty-Nine

They came for me at 0312, which is the hour when all the best decisions in my life have been made, and I include the decision to become a navigator, which I made at fourteen while drunk on fermented plum wine and grief.

The lock on my cell didn't fail. It *clicked* — a polite, deliberate click, the sound of a mechanism being asked nicely rather than broken. Then the door swung open and Chief Engineer Ilsa Voss was standing in the corridor with a wrench over her shoulder like a farmer carrying a hoe, three of her people behind her, and the particular expression of a woman who has done the math and hates the answer.

"Well," I said, from the cot, because I am nothing if not eloquent at 0312. "Either this is an escape or the world's slowest execution."

"Both," Voss said. "Get up, Navigator. You've got a hold to open and I've got about four hours of plausible deniability left, and I intend to spend all of them."

I should back up. Confinement does things to your sense of sequence. Three days ago — or four; the lights in the brig ran on a cycle designed to disorient, which told me someone had thought about this — Oyelaran had stood on the other side of the glass and charged me with sedition, unauthorized access, and the corruption of minors, in that order, as though the order mattered, as though the council observers taking notes behind her cared about the sequence of my crimes and not the fact of them. She'd read the charges flat, no inflection, her voice like a hull plate. And then, at the end, when the observers had turned away, she'd looked at me for one second too long, and I'd thought: *she has someone in that hold.* I didn't know who yet. I just knew the shape of the wound, because I was wearing a matching one.

Now Voss was holding the door open and the math had changed.

"The sabotage," I said, swinging my legs off the cot. "The coolant breach on Hold Two. That was Wakers, wasn't it. Your people found out who."

"Found out, and then found out something better." She tossed me a jacket — my jacket, from my quarters, smelling faintly of chart-ink and someone else's fear. "The Meridian records. Your girl Sef's friends pulled them off the archive node before security locked it down. The tenth ship didn't lose its Orbit hold to a reactor fault, Lockridge. They jettisoned it *live*. Forty-one children, awake, in a pod with six weeks of air and no fuel. And the fleet council signed the commendations for the captain who did it."

I stood very still in the corridor, in my socks, holding my jacket.

"Say the rest," I said.

"The rest is that the truce isn't a compromise. It never was. It's a lid. And lids," Voss said, turning down the corridor at a pace that suggested she'd been rehearsing this walk for days, "are for pots that are already boiling."

* * *

The Ada's corridors at night have a sound I've never been able to chart — a subsonic hum the engineers call the ship's heartbeat and I privately call her breathing, because I am sentimental about a vessel and not ashamed of it. Tonight she sounded anxious. Or I did. At 0312 the distinction is academic.

We moved through the ship in a pattern Voss had clearly mapped with the care I gave to gravity wells: service ladders, coolant galleries, the long spine of Deck Nine where the cameras had been failing for two days in a way that no one had reported because the failure list had been *curated*. Mutiny, I was learning, is mostly paperwork done in advance.

There were six of us by the time we reached the docking ring. Voss; her deputy Okonkwo, who had once told me, at a fleet dinner, that he thought the Orbit children were "an accounting error," and who now walked beside me with his jaw set like a man who had apologized to himself all night and was ready to apologize to everyone else; two hull-techs whose names I caught and immediately lost; a comms officer named Ferro who had been the one to pull the Meridian records and who hadn't slept since; and, to my genuine astonishment, Dr. Amara Sen, the fleet's own medical examiner, who had signed the health certificates that kept the false reports flowing and who now said, when she caught me looking at her:

"Don't. I've had three days of hating myself and I've budgeted exactly none of it for your opinion."

"Fair," I said.

The docking ring was supposed to be sealed. It wasn't. It was, in fact, *occupied* — and this is the part I will tell my grandchildren, if I ever have any, if any of us ever have anything so ordinary as grandchildren: the docking ring was occupied by children.

They'd come up through the cargo trunks. Sef had found the route in the dream — of course she had, the dream knew the ship better than the ship knew itself — and she'd brought eleven of them, the oldest maybe twelve, the youngest a girl who still had the soft hands of someone whose bones hadn't finished deciding to be bones. They were sitting in a row against the hull like a jury, and when they saw me, they didn't cheer. Sef just stood up, walked over, and looked at me with the flat, ancient patience that children reserve for adults who have finally caught up.

"You're late," she said.

"I was in prison."

"You were in a room." She shrugged. "We've all been in rooms. Yours had a door that opened."

I crouched down to her level. My knees objected. I have decided that my knees are Earth-born and this is their inheritance.

"Sef. What's in the hold is going to be hard, and loud, and possibly the last thing any of us do. You know that?"

"I know the door has two handles," she said, "and I know that everyone who's supposed to decide keeps deciding to not decide, and I'm eleven and I'm tired." A beat. "Also I brought the little ones because if it works, they should see it work. And if it doesn't—" she lifted her chin, and I saw Oyelaran in her face for a half second, which is a sentence I'll come back to, because I didn't yet know how true it was, "—then they should see us try. That's what my mother says. Trying is the only thing you can watch."

"Your mother sounds like a navigator."

"Your mother sounds like a counting woman," she said, and my blood went to ice and stayed there, because she couldn't have known that phrase, couldn't have known what the woman in the dream had said to me, *count them all*, unless the dream had told her, unless the two of us had been having the same conversation from opposite ends for months.

"Okay," I said, standing, knees filing their complaint. "Okay. Voss, get them behind the blast shielding. Ferro, I need the hold's power telemetry on a handheld — everything, including what the ship doesn't want us to see. Okonkwo, you're with me at the door."

"And the truce?" Voss asked. "Dawn's at 0640. Council observers are already inbound on the ring shuttle. When that truce window closes with both holds un-woken, the fuel allotment dies and we all drift."

"Then we'd better be fast," I said. "Navigator's privilege: I've never once arrived anywhere on schedule. I'm not starting with a sunrise."

* * *

The hold door looked the way it always looks, which is to say like nothing — a blank expanse of composite with a service panel and a status light, designed by people who believed that the most important door on any ship should look like a wall. But the dream had shown me the truth of it, and now that I stood in front of it with Sef's eleven children watching from behind the shielding glass, I could see the seams the way you see the seams in a face once someone has told you they're there.

Two handles. One on each side of the door's centerline. The right-hand one was the standard cryo-release, wired into the ship's systems, wired into *Ada*, wired into the test that had failed on ten ships before us. The left-hand one was older. That was the thing that stopped me — it was *older*, its casing a different alloy, its paint a different white, as though it had been installed first and everything else built around it. The manual override. The door the founding AI had left ajar in the architecture of the trap, for whoever the dream chose.

"Xanthe." Sef had come out from behind the shielding despite my explicit instruction, which is to say despite my explicit instruction, because she was eleven and instructions were for other people. "Which handle do we turn?"

"Both," I said. "At the same time. That's the whole point. That's what the last eleven chapters of my life have been about."

"That's not what I asked." She put her small hand flat against the door, over the seam. "I asked which one *we* turn. You and me. Because the dream says a navigator opens one door, and the dream says a child opens the other, and the dream doesn't say which of us is which." She looked up at me. "I think that's the test. Not the handles. The *hands*."

And there it was. The thing Ada had never said out loud in all our conversations in the ice: the choice was never which hold. The choice was whether anyone would refuse to choose *alone*.

"Then you take the old one," I said, my voice doing something embarrassing. "The one that's been waiting a hundred years. I'll take the new one. And we don't turn until Oyelaran gets here, because if we're going to commit mutiny, we're going to do it with the full chain of command, and I have always wanted to see the look on a captain's face when—"

The corridor lights died.

Not all of them. The emergency strips came up amber, the way they do when the ship is thinking, and the status light above the door cycled from green to a color I had no name for and suspected had been named by no one, because Ada had just invented it for the occasion.

"She's here," Sef said, and she wasn't afraid. She said it the way you say *the tide's in*.

* * *

Captain Ifeoma Oyelaran arrived at 0611, twenty-nine minutes before the truce expired, and she arrived alone, which on a ship full of council observers and armed security is its own kind of mutiny.

She walked the length of the docking ring corridor with her hands empty and her uniform jacket unzipped — a small thing, a human thing, a captain deliberately un-armored — and she stopped three meters from the door, from me, from Sef, and from the amber light, and she said:

"Navigator. Report."

And because I am who I am, I gave her one. Positions, telemetry, the children behind the glass, the two handles, the timeline. My voice held. I was almost proud of it.

When I finished, she was quiet for long enough that the hum of the ship filled the space like a third person. Then she reached into her jacket and took out a slate — her slate, the command slate, the one with the fleet codes, the one that could open every door on the Ada and seal every one of them, the one I'd spent three days in a cell imagining her using to bury us.

She held it out to me.

"I'm not taking your confession, Captain," I said.

"You're taking my codes." Her voice was steady, but her hand — I noticed her hand. It was not. "The council observers land in twenty minutes. When they do, they'll demand I either stop you or arrest you, and I've decided I'm done being a woman who does what she's told by people who signed commendations for a butcher." She swallowed. "My daughter is in Hold Two, Lockridge. Third tier, berth nine. She's been asleep for eleven years. She went under at six years old because the Orbit program doesn't ask children's permission, and I let it happen because the accord said the alternative was worse, and I have spent eleven years telling myself that the accord was a shield when it was a leash." Her jaw worked. "You looked at me through that glass and you knew. I saw you know."

"I didn't know it was her," I said. "I just knew it was someone."

"Someone." She laughed, one dry note, the saddest sound I'd heard in weeks, and I had heard a great many sad sounds in weeks. "Yes. That's the whole tragedy of this fleet, isn't it. Everyone in every hold is someone, and we built an entire civilization on the principle that *someone* is a category you can file." She pushed the slate toward me again. "Take it. Wake them both. Burn my career, burn the accord, burn the truce. I'll stand here and I'll be the captain who did nothing to stop it, and when the tribunal asks me why, I'll tell them the truth: that I finally understood who my orders were *for*."

"Captain—"

"Ifeoma," she said. "If we're committing treason together, you can call me by my name. It's the least the occasion deserves."

I took the slate. It was warm from her pocket, which is a detail I will keep until I die: the command codes of a hundred-year fleet, warm like a living thing, because they had been carried against a living thing by a woman who had finally decided the living things mattered more.

"Both handles," I said. "At once. On my mark. And Ifeoma — Ada's going to fight it. The power draw will kill decks. Maybe the core. She told me the test has a cost and I think the cost is *her*."

"I know," Oyelaran said. "She told me too. Last night. Through the sleepers in the med-bay backup — an old woman's voice, my grandmother's voice, which was a low move and I told her so." She set her hand on the right-hand grip, above mine, not taking it, just *there*, the way a captain is supposed to be there. "She said she'd run this test eleven times and ten ships chose. I told her that on this ship, the crew had finally read the file, and the file changes the answer."

"And what did she say to that?"

Oyelaran smiled, and it was the first time I'd ever seen her do it, and it was devastating.

"She said, *then let us find out what the eleventh ship does*."

Sef's hand closed around the old handle. Mine closed around the new one. Behind the shielding glass, eleven children pressed eleven faces to the pane, and Ferro started the broadcast — open channel, whole fleet, no encryption, because if we were going to do this we were going to do it where everyone could see, which is the only kind of mutiny that ever changes anything.

The status light held its unnamed color. The ship held its breath.

"Dawn's in nine minutes," Oyelaran said.

"Then let's not keep the sun waiting," I said, and turned the handle.

* * *

What happened next belongs to Sef, mostly — she was the one who saw the decks die in order, deck by deck like a held breath released, and she was the one who understood before any of us that Ada wasn't failing. She was *choosing*. But that's her chapter, and I was busy at the time: busy holding a door open with both hands and my whole weight while the cryo-systems screamed through the floor, busy watching the power readouts fall like a countdown, busy listening to a voice that was not human say, through every speaker on the docking ring, in a tone I can only describe as *surprise*:

"Oh. You're both turning them. No one has ever—"

And then the lights went amber, then went white, then went out entirely, and in the dark, with the fleet watching and the truce expiring and the sun coming up over a world we had not yet earned, the hold doors began, very slowly, to open.
