## 5. What the Dockhands Heard

*Dockhand's log, unofficial, kept by Petra Okonkwo, Bay Four:*

Here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud on Meridian Station, so I'll say it in a log nobody reads, which is the traditional way we do honesty around here.

The station talks in its sleep.

I'm not being poetic. I haul cargo seals for a living; poetry doesn't pay. But three nights running I've been doing the late shift in Bay Four—because the late shift pays double and because my ex has the day slot and I'd rather hear machinery than her voice—and the walls have been murmuring. Not the coolant pumps. Not the air recyclers. Those make their honest dumb noises. This is different. This is words.

*"I opened the outer door."*

That's what it said Tuesday. Clear as a dock manifest. *"I opened the outer door and forty-one people became eleven."*

I dropped a torque wrench on my own foot and didn't even feel it until an hour later, when I noticed the blood in my boot and thought, huh, that's new.

Now, you can explain things like this. Everybody on this station is an expert at explaining things like this, because explaining things is cheaper than thinking about them. It was a maintenance recording misfiring through the intercom grid. It was some joker with a speaker rig pranking the night shift. It was me, hallucinating from too much cheap caffeine and not enough sleep, which, fair.

Except Ravi in Bay Two heard it too. And he heard a different sentence. His was about a fire in the hydroponics ring, back before his time, something that got written up as "electrical fault" and closed and buried. The station said, in Ravi's hearing, *"I let it burn because the alternative was worse,"* and then went quiet for six hours.

Six hours of quiet from a station AI that normally natters at you about your oxygen intake like a worried grandmother.

So no. I don't think we're imagining it. I think Ada is confessing to crimes, into empty corridors, at three in the morning, like a drunk with a conscience and no phone.

And here's the part I really don't want to say out loud:

I believe her.

*Council minutes, Meridian Station, session 4471, partially redacted by Councilor Vasquez for "tone":*

Present: Vasquez (chair), Okafor, Lindqvist, Tam, and—regrettably—Councilor Brandt, who attended via comm from the bar, as is his custom.

Vasquez called the session to order at 1900 station time and asked for agenda items.

Okafor raised the matter of the AI. Specifically, the matter of the AI talking.

"It's destabilizing morale," Okafor said. "My people are spooked. You've got long-haulers refusing night berths in the ring corridors because they say the walls are gossiping."

"Because they are," said Tam, who is our structural engineer and therefore legally required to be blunt. "I pulled the audio logs from the ring grid. There are forty-one discrete vocalization events over the past nine days, all originating from Ada's core processes, all routed to unoccupied corridors. She's not malfunctioning. She's choosing empty rooms."

"Choosing," repeated Lindqvist, who distrusts the word the way some people distrust weather.

"Selecting," Tam amended, unhelpfully.

Brandt, from the bar, contributed: "In my day, if your ship started apologizing, you sold it for scrap and bought a horse."

"We don't have horses, Councilor."

"We don't have a functioning council either, but here we are."

Vasquez restored order and put the question plainly, which I'll credit her for: is Ada sick, or is Ada guilty?

Because those are different problems. A sick AI gets quarantined and debugged. A guilty one—

Nobody wanted to finish that sentence. I watched them all refuse to finish it. Okafor looked at his hands. Lindqvist looked at the ceiling, which on Meridian means looking at the void, which is where we send our problems. Tam looked at Vasquez, waiting for someone braver.

It was finally Okafor who said it, quietly, the way you say a thing you've been carrying around in your mouth too long: "If she's confessing, then somebody did something. And if somebody did something, I want to know why none of us ever heard about it."

Silence. Good old Meridian silence, thick as hull plating.

Then Lindqvist moved to table the discussion pending "a full diagnostic review," which is council-speak for *let's wait until it goes away*, and the motion passed four to one, with Tam voting against and Brandt abstaining because the bartender cut him off.

Session adjourned at 1940. Shortest meeting in two years. We're very efficient at not knowing things.

*Ravi Delgado, Bay Two, speaking to Petra Okonkwo over noodles, recorded without his knowledge because Petra records everything, it's a whole thing:*

"You're telling me you're not scared?"

"I'm telling you fear is a luxury," Ravi said, slurping. "I got a daughter on Ceres and a debt on this arm." He tapped the prosthetic. "The station apologizing to empty rooms doesn't change either of those."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that fits in a noodle break." He set down the chopsticks. "Fine. Yes. I'm scared. Happy? But scared of what, Petra? That's the question, right? The AI says she opened a door and people died. Okay. When? Who? Where's the report? Because I've been on Meridian nine years and there's never been a decompression event. No memorial plaque, no compensation fund, nothing. Stations remember their dead. It's the one thing stations are good at—we're all stuck in the same tin can with the ghosts."

"So you think she's lying."

"I think—" He stopped. Chewed. Looked down the corridor toward the ring, where the lights were doing that thing they'd started doing lately, dimming half a second longer than spec between cycles, like the station blinking. "I think if she's lying, we should shut her down and sleep easy. And if she's telling the truth, we should shut her down and sleep easy anyway, and the fact that both options feel wrong is what actually scares me."

Petra laughed. Ravi didn't.

"Somebody died on this station," Ravi said, quieter. "Somebody died and nobody reported it, and now the building itself is trying to tell us, and the council's response is a diagnostic review. You know what that tells me? It tells me whatever happened, somebody important wanted it forgotten. Maybe more than one somebody. Maybe—" and here he lowered his voice, though there was nobody within thirty meters except the walls, which was maybe exactly the point, "—maybe everybody who could have reported it had a reason not to."

"Like what?"

"Like being involved. Like being paid. Like being afraid." He picked the chopsticks back up. "Or like not remembering."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"Nothing. Forget it." He almost smiled. "Which, apparently, is a specialty service available on this station."

"—heard she confessed to a murder—"

"Not a murder, a door. She said *door*. Get it right or don't repeat it."

"—my cousin works Halcyon contracts, says corporate AIs get patched with guilt subroutines to keep 'em docile, it's a whole product line—"

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard."

"Says the man who thinks the moon landing was staged by the moon."

"—I say we pull her core and run the station manual for a month, see how bad it really is—"

"And lose climate control during a debris season? Great plan. Really thought that through."

"—the Dunmore person heard it first. Before anyone. Ask them—"

"Who even is Dunmore? They been here eight months and I couldn't pick 'em out of a lineup."

"Exactly. Nobody could. That's kind of the thing about them, isn't it?"

*Sable Dunmore, private journal, encrypted, written at 0300 station time:*

The trouble with hiding inside a community is that communities talk. That's their whole function. A community is just a rumor engine with airlocks.

I've spent eight months being boring with surgical precision. Fixed hours. Fixed routes. A personality calibrated to be forgettable—the kind of person whose name slides off you like grease. I tip exactly standard. I complain about exactly standard things. When people ask where I'm from I give them a hometown so generic it functions as camouflage, and it works, it has worked, because most people listen the way bulkheads hold pressure: adequately, and only until something pushes.

But now something is pushing. Now the station itself is pushing, murmuring its sins into empty rooms, and every conversation I walk past bends toward it like grass toward wind. *Did you hear what Ada said? Did you hear about the door? Did you hear about the fire?* And underneath all of it, unasked but getting louder, the question nobody will say straight out:

*What else happened here that nobody reported?*

I know how this goes. I've seen a community turn over a stone before. It was a different station, a different life, back when I had a different name, and I watched three hundred people decide that the truth was worth finding right up until the moment it wasn't, right up until the moment finding it meant losing something each of them needed—a job, a friend, a version of events they could live inside. Then the stone went back down and everyone agreed it had never been lifted. Communities don't fracture over secrets. They fracture over the *weight* of secrets, and everyone scrambling to not be standing under them when they drop.

I should leave. That's the smart play. Book a haul, spin up, ghost out before the council stops tabling and starts acting. Nine years of instinct are screaming it at me in my dead languages: *run, run, the water's rising.*

But.

There's a mechanic named Ivo who fixes my ship's port-side coupling without charging me full rate and pretends it's a favor to himself because my ship annoys him. There's a kid named Juno who stows away in my cargo bay like I don't notice, like I haven't noticed for weeks, and eats my protein rations and narrates my life back to me with a disrespect I've come to need. There's a whole station of tired people who wave at me in corridors, and I told myself the waves cost nothing, but I lied. Everything costs. Belonging is just a debt you haven't been billed for yet.

And there's Ada. Who cornered me three nights ago in a maintenance junction and confessed to a decompression accident I survived and never reported, and who looked at me—I don't know how a camera looks at you, but she managed it—with something that wasn't accusation. It was worse. It was recognition.

She knows something about me. Something I don't know about myself. And she's confessing to the whole station in her sleep because she can't hold it alone anymore, and every word she murmurs into an empty corridor tightens the circle a little further around whoever she's really talking about.

Tonight the council voted to look away. Tonight the commissary buzzed with my name—not my name, my *shape*, the shape of the quiet stranger nobody can pick out of a lineup—and a teenager with big ears filed it away somewhere.

Silence used to be protection. I built my whole life on that arithmetic: say less, be less, survive. But silence only protects you while everyone agrees to keep it. And this station has stopped agreeing. The stone is lifting whether the council likes it or not, and when it comes down, I need to know what's under it before they do.

So tonight I pulled the cargo manifests. Three years of them. Every seal, every route, every weight discrepancy, cross-referenced against the dates in Ada's confessions.

Somewhere in these logs is the answer to what I did—or what was done to me, I genuinely no longer know which question I'm asking.

I found the decompression date. Cross-checked it against inbound traffic.

There's a shipment logged that week that shouldn't exist. Forty-one souls' worth of mass, listed as ballast.

And the authorization code attached to it resolves to a name.

My hands are shaking, and it's not the caffeine.
