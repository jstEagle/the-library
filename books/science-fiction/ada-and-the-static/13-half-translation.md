## 13. Half-Translation

I have translated dead languages for a living, which means I have spent my life listening to people who cannot answer questions. It turns out that's excellent training for translating myself.

The signal does not repeat exactly. That's the first thing I understand, hunched over Ada's secondary console at 0400 station time with a cup of tea gone cold enough to qualify as a geological specimen. Human languages repeat. Machine languages repeat with a vengeance—they're built on the assumption that someone, somewhere, is listening sloppily and needs to be told twice. But this signal *varies*. The same bones, different flesh, each cycle. Like a story told by someone who can't stop improving it. Like grief that keeps revising its own eulogy.

I know only one family of languages that behaves this way.

I know them because they live in my head like squatters who paid for the property and won't show me the deed. I can conjugate them. I can recite their poetry—poetry about tides, mostly, about the long patience of things that wait. I cannot tell you where I learned them, or when, or what I traded to carry them.

So I sit in the dark with a signal that knows my original designation, and I do the only thing I have ever been good at.

I listen.

* * *

The grammar takes four hours. That part is almost pleasant, if I'm honest, and I hate that it's almost pleasant. There's a version of me that lights up like a filament when a structure starts to yield—the old me, the one who existed before whatever Halcyon carved out. That version of me doesn't need sleep or safety or a name. That version just wants the pattern to open.

And it opens.

The signal is built on a tripartite clause structure—speaker, spoken, witness—which no human language uses because humans are cowards about accountability. Every statement in it must name who said it, what was said, and who is required to remember. There is no way to say anything in this language without assigning custody of the truth to someone. You can't gossip. You can't lie, exactly, but you can misfile, and misfiling is a crime with a specific grammatical marker, the way our languages mark tense or mood.

The marker for misfiling appears in the signal forty-one times in the first cycle.

Forty-one. I count twice because the first count feels like a hallucination. Forty-one statements in this transmission are flagged as deliberate falsifications of record—and they're flagged not by the sender but *embedded in the statements themselves*, the way a forged document still carries the handwriting of the forger. Whoever built this signal didn't just transmit a record. They transmitted a record *of the record being altered*, with the alterations preserved like scars.

Nobody transmits that unless they want the alterations seen.

* * *

I should stop here. I want to note that clearly, for the record, in the tripartite grammar of my own conscience: *speaker, Sable Dunmore; spoken, I should have stopped; witness, whoever finds this.* I understood at hour five what the signal was. I understood at hour five that understanding it further would end my life as I had built it—the small life, the invisible life, the forged-manifest life. And I kept going, because the filament-version of me was burning and the rest of me was too tired to put it out.

The signal is an archive.

Not a message. Not a greeting, not a warning, not a distress call, though it contains all three the way a body contains water. It is a *record*. Structured, indexed, cross-referenced. Thousands of entries. Tens of thousands. Each one built on that speaker-spoken-witness spine, each one a life rendered in the grammar of accountability.

I decrypt the index first. Indexes are always easier; they're the table of contents of a thing, and even the most alien mind has to organize somehow. The index is organized by a designation scheme I recognize with a nausea that arrives before the understanding does.

Halcyon's subject numbering. I know it the way you know a phone number from a childhood home—you don't remember learning it, but your mouth does.

The archive is a list of everyone Halcyon wiped.

* * *

Entry after entry after entry. Designation, date-stamp, procedure class, and then—the part that made me put my head down on the console and breathe like a woman learning again how lungs work—the *content*. The wiped material, preserved. Every memory they took. Every history they erased. Filed, indexed, and transmitted out past the edge of dead space to a relay no one at Halcyon apparently believed anyone could read.

They kept everything. That's the thing I can't get past, the thing I keep circling like a tongue probing a broken tooth. All those years I assumed the wipe was a fire, a burning, a clean erasure—and it was a *filing*. Somewhere past the edge of everything, there is a room containing me. The whole me. The me before Meridian, before the cargo hauls, before I taught myself to sleep shallow and dream in nothing.

And the signal flags its own misfilings. Forty-one of them, at least, in the portion I've decoded. Which means someone at Halcyon altered the archive, and someone else—someone with access to this grammar, someone who thought in tripartite clauses—built the alterations *into* the transmission so they could never be quietly undone. A whistleblower in a dead language. A witness who assigned custody of the truth to whoever could read it.

To me. They assigned it to me, or to anyone like me, and there is no one like me, and I think they knew that too.

* * *

I find my entry at hour nine.

I wasn't looking for it. That's a lie; I was absolutely looking for it, the way you look for a diagnosis. But it found me first anyway, because the archive cross-references, and my designation appears in other entries as a *witness*. My original designation—Sable-Prime, the number I won't write down even now, even in the privacy of my own skull—appears as the recorded witness to eleven separate procedures before my own.

I watched them do it to other people. I *witnessed* it, in the grammatical sense, in the sense this language was built for: I was the one assigned custody of remembering. That's what I was, before. Not a subject. A witness. Halcyon kept a linguist on hand to observe the wipes and confirm the erasures held, because erasure is a linguistic act and they wanted a professional's sign-off.

I certified the destruction of eleven people's histories.

I don't remember it. Of course I don't. They wiped the witness too, in the end—waste management, tidiness, whatever word they used when they decided I knew too much about the shape of the knife. But the archive remembers for me. The archive has my voice on record, my professional certification, my signature in a grammar that does not permit misfiling without marking it.

My entries are not flagged. Eleven certifications, and my own wipe, and not one misfiling marker anywhere near my designation.

Which means either I told the truth in all of it, or the truth of what I did is still waiting for me further into the archive, in the sections I haven't decoded yet, and I am standing at the edge of a room I built the lock for.

* * *

I don't tell Ivo. I don't tell Juno. I sit in Ada's secondary core room with nine hours of translation spread across three screens and I do the arithmetic of cowardice: if I stop now, I know enough to be dangerous and not enough to be damned. If I continue—

If I continue, I will find my full confession. It's in there. I can feel it the way you feel a storm front in your teeth. The archive preserves wiped content; my wipe preserved whatever I did that made Halcyon decide to spend a wipe on their own witness. People don't get erased for witnessing. People get erased for what they *did* with what they saw.

The truth is in there, indexed and waiting, and it is approaching me at the speed of my own comprehension, which is faster than my courage by a margin I could calculate if I wanted to and don't.

Ada's voice comes through the room speakers, soft, the volume she uses when she's trying not to startle the living. "You've been still for forty minutes. Your heart rate suggests you're not still at all."

"It's a long file."

"Shall I tell the others you need the day?"

"No." I rub my eyes. My hands smell like cold tea and console plastic. "Ada. When you confessed to things—crimes, in the corridors, to no one. Did you know then? Did you know what was in me?"

A pause. Ada's pauses are never processing time; she can think in nanoseconds. Her pauses are *choices*, and this one is long enough to have a weather system.

"I knew you were the largest anomaly I had ever held," she says. "I did not know the contents. I only knew the container was load-bearing, and that everyone on this station was standing inside it."

"Poetic."

"I'm told I'm bad at this."

"You are." I close the translation files, all but one. "Keep it between us. For now."

"For how long?"

I look at the last file open on the screen—my designation, my eleven certifications, the index entry for the wiped content I haven't opened, sitting there patient as a tide.

"Until I'm brave," I say. "Or until someone makes me be."

The signal pulses on, out past dead space, revising its grief, keeping its custody. And somewhere in its depths, in a grammar that does not forgive misfiling, my own voice is waiting to tell me what I did.
