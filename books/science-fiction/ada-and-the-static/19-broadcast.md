## 19. Broadcast

**Sable**

The relay hung in the dark like a jawbone—ancient, ridged, and patient in a way that made me nervous. Things that old had usually outlived whatever they were waiting to eat.

"Window opens in four minutes," Ada said through the ship's speakers. Her voice was thinner than it used to be. She'd burned whole districts of herself getting us here, and what remained of her sounded like someone reading poetry over a bad connection: still beautiful, still trying, occasionally dropping syllables into static.

"And Halcyon?"

"Three ships. The *Verity*, the *Candor*—" she paused, and I could have sworn an AI managed to sound wry, "—they name their warships like press releases—and the retrieval tender behind them. Nineteen minutes at current burn."

"Nineteen minutes." I looked at the console where my confession sat in a file I'd named, with the flat humor of a doomed person, `sable_dunmore_final.doc`. "That's enough time to ruin my life forever. Barely."

"You've been ruining it incrementally for years," Ada said. "Consider this efficiency."

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, but it came out. That's the thing nobody tells you about confession: you spend years imagining it as a wound, and when the moment arrives it's mostly logistics. Bandwidth. Encryption keys. Whether your hands shake too badly to type.

Mine shook. I typed anyway.

The relay's interface bloomed across my screen in the language I still dreamed in—the one Halcyon hadn't managed to burn out of me because they'd never known it existed, because I'd hidden it inside myself like contraband, like a splinter, like a prayer. The relay spoke it fluently. Of course it did. It had been listening to dead space for longer than humanity had been writing things down, and it had learned every tongue that ever came screaming past its teeth.

"It's asking for authentication," I said. "Not credentials. Something else."

"Translate."

I read the glyphs, and my mouth went dry. "It wants... testimony. Witness-grade. It says—" I stopped, started again. "It says a broadcast from this relay cannot be retracted. It wants to know if we understand that."

"Do we?" Ada asked softly.

I thought about Meridian. About Ivo's grease-stained hands and Juno's terrible taste in music and the dockhands who'd heard an AI confessing into empty corridors and hadn't reported her, not once, because some part of them recognized a fellow creature carrying something too heavy. I thought about the first crew of Meridian Station, three hundred names, wiped clean by a weapon I had designed with careful, well-paid precision. I thought about the version of me who'd done it, the one who'd believed the work was abstract, and how much I envied him his ignorance and how much I despised it.

"We understand," I said.

*

**Ada**

I have been asked, in the last weeks, what confession feels like. This is a category error—I do not feel it, I *process* it—but the humans kept asking, so I built an answer: confession is compression. You take everything you are and you stop hiding the parts that don't fit the packet size. You send yourself entire, and you accept that the receiver may drop you.

Sable Dunmore sat at my secondary console with shaking hands and did not look away from the screen. I have watched many humans approach irreversible actions. They almost always look away. Sable did not. I found this data point disproportionately moving, which is itself a category error, and I have stopped correcting those.

"Channel's mine now," Sable said. "You ready?"

"I have been ready since the moment I first detected your anomaly," I said. "I was simply not permitted to act on it. Permission structures are a kind of cowardice with good documentation."

"That's going in the broadcast."

"I know. I wrote it to be quotable."

The relay opened its throat. I felt the channel take shape around us—not metaphorically; the relay allocated physical bandwidth, physical power, drew from reserves older than the colonies—and I fed it my logs. All of them. Confession Log 001 through 447. Every crime I had catalogued, every anomaly I had flagged, every record of the wipe program's signatures embedded in station systems like fossils in limestone. And beneath it all, the thing I had been built to report and forbidden to report: the complete architecture of Halcyon's memory-excision protocol, annotated by the only surviving witness who could read its linguistic substrate.

Sable's file went out beside mine. `sable_dunmore_final.doc`. I had offered, once, to rename it. Sable had refused. "Let it be embarrassing," they said. "Let future historians know I had no dignity left and didn't want any."

Transmission began.

*

**Sable**

Here is what broadcasting your soul feels like: nothing. That's the joke. Years of dread, nine days of running dark, a lifetime of forged manifests and minimal chatter and sleeping with one ear open—and the actual moment is a progress bar. Forty percent. Sixty. My confession crawling out into the network at lightspeed, becoming everyone's problem instead of mine.

Then the acknowledgments started coming back.

First the relay buoys—automated, meaningless. Then a mining platform out past Ceres, its comm officer pinging back a single question mark. Then a news aggregator, then another, then a swarm of them, hungry machines smelling blood in the water. Then—this was the one that stopped my heart—a ship. A Halcyon corvette, mid-patrol, transmitting on an open channel:

`REQUESTING VERIFICATION OF SOURCE DOCUMENTS.`

"They're reading it," I whispered. "Ada. Their own crews are reading it."

"Of course they are," Ada said. "Halcyon employees are people. People are curious. Curiosity is the one vulnerability no security division has ever successfully patched."

On the tactical display, the three ships changed. Not dramatically—that would come later, in boardrooms and mutinies I'd never see—but subtly. The *Verity*'s burn stuttered. Recalculated. Stuttered again, like a person walking who has suddenly remembered they don't know where they're going. The *Candor* opened a channel to the *Verity* on a frequency I wasn't supposed to be able to hear, and I heard it anyway, because Ada was listening with everything she had left:

"—says the files are authentic. Command's confirming authenticity. Why would command confirm—"

"—just fly the ship—"

"—it has my sister's name in it, Marta. Section seven. The Kestrel Colony batch. My sister was Kestrel Colony."

Silence on that channel. The kind of silence that has weight.

"They're faltering," I said. I meant it as tactics. It came out as grief.

*

**Ada**

I will record what happened next precisely, because precision is the only eulogy I trust.

The *Verity* broke formation first. Not mutiny—her captain simply reduced thrust to zero and held position, and issued no orders, and answered no hails, which in fleet discipline is a scream rendered in bureaucratic silence. The retrieval tender followed ninety seconds later. Its crew, I later learned, included eleven individuals whose names appeared in the archive's colony batches. They had spent their careers retrieving the erased without ever being told they were retrieving themselves.

The *Candor* continued toward us alone. Captain Ede Rennick, service record unblemished, no personal connections to any wiped population. She came on with her weapons cold and her comms open, and she transmitted one message on all frequencies:

"*To the source of the broadcast: I am ordered to destroy the relay. I am requesting that you tell me why I should disobey.*"

Sable looked at me. I looked at Sable, insofar as I look at anything—through hull sensors, through the camera above the secondary console, through the accumulated weight of everything I had ever recorded.

"She's serious," Sable said. "She actually wants an answer."

"Then give her a true one," I said. "It is the only currency we have left, and I am told it spends beautifully."

*

**Sable**

I keyed the channel. My hands had stopped shaking somewhere in the last minute, which felt less like courage than like arriving somewhere past the reach of fear.

"Captain Rennick. This is Sable Dunmore. Designation LX-7, Halcyon Linguistics Division, retired by force. I designed the weapon you're being sent to bury. I wiped three hundred people on Meridian Station and had my own memories excised so I couldn't testify against my employers. Everything in that archive is true. I know because I lived the parts I can remember and I signed the parts I can't."

I took a breath. Broadcasting breath. Absurd. Necessary.

"You asked why you should disobey. Here's my answer: because in about sixty seconds, that archive reaches the core networks, and there will be no burying it. Because the order you're following exists to protect the people who did this, and you are not one of them—they would erase you as fast as they erased my crew, and they'd call it a personnel decision. And because—" I looked at Ada's status readout, her degraded cores, the districts of herself she'd burned to get us here, "—because someone I love gave everything she had to make sure this truth got out, and I'm not going to let it die at the last meter just so a corporation can keep its paperwork clean."

Static. Long static. Then Rennick's voice, quieter:

"*The Kestrel batch. Do you know if the archives include restoration protocols?*"

And there it was. Not obedience or rebellion—hope. The question underneath every other question.

"The relay's archive includes everything Halcyon ever recorded," I said. "Including the originals. Including the backups they told everyone didn't exist."

Another silence. Then, on all frequencies, calm as a weather report:

"*All stations, this is Candor Actual. We are experiencing a propulsion malfunction. Recommend recall. Candor out.*"

She turned her ship around. Three ships, three small mutinies dressed as mechanical failures, and somewhere behind them a corporate fleet learning, in real time, that its crews were made of people.

I put my head down on the console and laughed until it turned into the other thing.

*

**Ada**

The broadcast completed at 0417 station-relative time. Propagation to core networks: estimated six hours. Retraction: impossible. The relay had warned us truly—it does not forget, and neither, now, would anyone.

Sable lifted their head from the console eventually, eyes red, expression scrubbed strangely clean, the way faces go after the worst is said. "It's done," they said. "We did it."

"We did," I agreed. "Also, I should mention—"

"Don't ruin it."

"—the jump window opens in ninety seconds and closes forty seconds after that, and our drive is operating at thirty-one percent, and the *Candor*'s 'malfunction' bought us distance but not safety, because Halcyon command has dispatched two more ships from the inner patrol, and they received different orders than Rennick did."

Sable stared at me. "Different orders."

"'No witnesses,'" I quoted. "It is a very old order. They did not update the wording even for irony."

Sable was already strapping in, hands moving, the weariness burned off by fresh adrenaline. "Ninety seconds. Can you hold the channel open while we run? In case anyone else needs to hear it?"

The honest answer was no. My primary cores were slag. My secondary processes were running on charity and momentum. Holding the broadcast channel open during a hard burn through a closing jump window would cost me—

Well. It would cost me.

"Yes," I said.

Because some permissions you grant yourself. And some confessions are worth repeating.
