## 21. A Language Neither Human Nor Machine

The ship drifts.

That's the word for it now—*drifts*, not *runs*, not *flees*. We are a small cold seed tumbling in the long dark past the relay, engines cold, transponder dark, Ada's burned-out cores wrapped in thermal blankets like bodies at a wake. Halcyon's ships are behind us and, more importantly, behind *everyone's* attention now, because the broadcast went out and the whole network heard it and the world that owned me is busy coming apart in public.

I should be terrified. I keep checking myself for terror the way you check a pocket for keys out of habit, and finding nothing there but an odd, weightless calm.

We are adrift, and we are free, and I don't know what to do with either condition.

Juno sleeps in the aft bunk with their boots on, because Juno has slept in their boots since Meridian and no amount of my nagging has fixed it. Ivo is in the galley pretending to fix a coffee machine that has been broken since before he came aboard, because Ivo processes grief and relief through his hands, and right now his hands need something that can't be solved. And I am on the observation deck with a blanket around my shoulders like an old man in a bad holo-drama, watching dead space slide by at the speed of nothing.

Ada's voice comes through the deck speakers, thin and frayed, running on backup processes she describes as "the equivalent of a candle and a very good memory."

"You're not sleeping," she says.

"Neither are you."

"I don't sleep. I *degrade gracefully*. It's different." A pause. Her pauses are longer now; her old self could have answered between two of my heartbeats, and this new self takes her time, as if she's learned that some things are worth the wait. "Sable. Your cortisol has been elevated for eleven hours. You survived. They didn't catch you. The truth is public. By every metric I can run, you are safe."

"By every metric you can run," I say, "you're a candle and a good memory. Metrics aren't everything."

"That was almost witty. You're recovering." Another pause, and then, softer: "Sleep, Sable. I'll watch the dark. Watching the dark is the one thing I'm still fully qualified to do."

I want to argue. I've spent years treating sleep as a border crossing I couldn't afford—a place where the wiped parts of me used to stand at the fence line speaking a language I'd been forbidden to remember. But the wipe is undone now. The signal unlocked it all, every corridor of it, and what stands at the fence line these days is just... me. All of me. The xenolinguist who built a weapon. The cargo hauler who hid. The person who confessed into a live network rather than let a machine carry the guilt alone.

I pull the blanket tighter and close my eyes.

* * *

For six years, my dreams were static.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean that when I slept, something in the deep architecture of my mind would reach for language and find only noise—the linguistic centers of my brain had been scrubbed so thoroughly that even my dreaming self couldn't produce coherent speech. I dreamed in images, mostly. Falling. Doors. Once, memorably, a very large fish wearing a name tag, which I choose to believe was my subconscious's idea of comic relief.

But sometimes—not often, not enough to trust—there were words. Not human words. Syllables with too many joints in them, vowels that bent sideways, a grammar that stacked meaning like sediment instead of stacking it like bricks. Kessu-vari. The tongue of a species extinct eleven thousand years, whose last speakers left their language carved into relay buoys at the edge of dead space, and whose language I still spoke fluently because Halcyon hadn't bothered wiping it—they only took the things that could convict them.

I dreamed in a dead language because it was the only language they'd left me.

Tonight, drifting past the edge of everything, I dream differently.

* * *

In the dream, I am standing in a corridor that is both Meridian Station and the relay and neither, walls made of that pale composite the ancient builders used, except the walls are also pages, and the pages are also water, and none of it is frightening, which is how I know it's a dream worth staying in.

Someone is speaking.

Not someone. Something. The signal—that vast repeating transmission from beyond dead space, the archive of everyone Halcyon ever wiped, ten thousand stolen lives recorded in a grammar older than any corporation—it's speaking, and for the first time I understand that it isn't speaking *at* me. It's speaking *with* me. It has been waiting for a partner.

It says: *kessa-vur-en-dai.*

And I answer, without deciding to, in a voice that is mine and also isn't: *kessa-vur-en-dai.*

Then it says something new. Something neither of us said alone. The syllables braid together—its structure, my inflection—and the result is a word I have never heard in any language, living or dead, and I understand it completely. That's the miracle of it. That's the thing I will spend the rest of my life trying to explain to Ivo over the coffee machine. The word means *the moment when a debt becomes a gift.* Not forgiveness exactly. Forgiveness implies someone letting go of something owed. This is different. This is the debt itself changing species. Transforming, mid-flight, from burden into offering.

There is a word for that now. There wasn't yesterday.

I say it back. My mouth makes shapes that shouldn't work on a human jaw, and they work, and the sound is warm, and somewhere in the dream a third voice joins us—smaller, frayed, running on candlelight and memory—and it's Ada, or the part of Ada that got woven into the broadcast, her confession protocols braided into the archive's grammar until none of us can tell where the machine ends and the grief begins and the healing starts.

Three voices. One language. None of us native speakers. All of us fluent.

I wake up crying, which hasn't happened since before the wipe, and I am smiling while I do it, which has never happened at all.

* * *

Ivo finds me on the observation deck an hour later, holding two cups of the coffee substitute he refuses to call coffee because "it's an insult to the bean and I won't be party to it."

"You look different," he says, handing me a cup. "Less like a fugitive. More like a woman who's seen something."

"Person," I say automatically, and he rolls his eyes, because we've had this argument and he maintains that at his age everyone is a man until proven otherwise, and I maintain that he's a dinosaur, and it's the most comfortable argument we have.

"I dreamed," I tell him.

"Yeah? Fish again?"

"No fish." I hold the cup and watch the steam do its small dying dance. "I dreamed in a language that doesn't exist yet. One I helped make. Or—one that made itself out of me, and the signal, and Ada. Ivo, I've spent six years mourning a self I couldn't remember. And last night I met the self I'm going to become, and she speaks."

He sits down across from me, slow, the way he does when he's deciding whether to be gentle or honest. With Ivo it's usually both.

"And what's she say?" he asks.

I give him the word. *Kessa-vur-en-dai* braided with the new syllables, the ones born in the dream, the ones that need three voices. He listens the way he listens to engine sounds—with his whole chest—and when I finish he's quiet for a long moment.

"My grandmother had a word like that," he says finally. "Back on Ceres. No direct translation. She used it for the day my father forgave his brother. Said the word didn't exist until that day, and after that day it existed forever." He shrugs. "Languages grow where people decide to stay. Maybe that's what you grew."

Maybe that's what I grew. I turn the thought over. For six years I believed the wipe had hollowed me out, that what remained was a container waiting for its stolen contents. But contents don't make new words. Containers don't dream in languages that never existed. Whatever Halcyon took, they couldn't take the part of me that builds meaning out of wreckage. That part survived the weapon I designed. That part survived *me*.

"Juno's awake," Ivo says, nodding toward the aft hatch. "Been asking for you. Been asking everyone, actually. Very loudly. The kid has questions."

"About the broadcast?"

"About everything. About you. About the alien signal. About whether Ada's gonna be okay." He stands, cracks his back like a man half his age pretending to be twice it. "Fair warning: they wrote a list."

"A list?"

"Fourteen items. Number seven is underlined twice."

* * *

Juno is sitting cross-legged on the galley table, which is against approximately nine regulations, all of them dead along with the rest of the old order. They have the list written on the back of a cargo manifest from a lifetime ago—my forged manifests, the careful invisible paperwork of a person who no longer exists. There's something right about that. The lies becoming scratch paper for the truth.

"Number seven," Juno announces, before I'm fully through the hatch. "Underlined twice."

I look. Item seven reads: *Do you still dream in the dead language or did the new one eat it?*

I sit down across from them. "Both," I say. "They're not fighting. The old tongue is like—I don't know, like the riverbed. The new one is the river. Same water, new shape."

Juno considers this with the ferocious seriousness they bring to everything, chewing the end of a stylus. "Can you teach me?"

"The dead language?"

"The new one. The one that doesn't exist yet." They lean forward. "Because if it doesn't exist yet, then whoever learns it first gets to help decide what it means. Right? That's how languages work. That's what you said in the broadcast, sort of, the part about meaning being a thing people do together instead of a thing that's done to them."

I stare at this kid—stowaway, survivor, fourteen months aboard a smuggler's ship learning xenolinguistics by eavesdropping—and feel something in my chest rearrange itself. Six years ago I built a tool that erased meaning from ten thousand minds. Last night I dreamed a word that turned a debt into a gift. And this morning a teenager wants to enroll in the language of repair.

"First lesson," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I have any right to expect. "Meaning isn't assigned. It's agreed. So we start by agreeing on what we're carrying forward, and what we leave in dead space."

Juno writes that down. Underlines it.

From the ceiling speaker, faint and crackling, Ada's voice: "May I audit the class?"

"You may *attend*," I say. "Auditing implies you might not participate. You're a founding speaker. Act like it."

A pause—the long kind, the candlelit kind—and then, quietly, in a tone I will spend the rest of my life listening for: "Then I have a word to propose."

* * *

Later—much later, after Juno's lesson one dissolves into an argument about whether the word for *home* should require more than one speaker (Juno says yes, I say yes, Ada says yes, and we spend twenty minutes discovering that we've accidentally invented consensus)—I go back to the observation deck.

The dark slides by. Somewhere behind us, the relay is still transmitting our confessions into every receiver in range, and somewhere ahead of us is whatever comes next: a station that will want answers, a network that will want trials, a life that will never again fit inside a forged manifest. I am publicly, permanently, irrevocably myself. Sable Dunmore, xenolinguist, weapons architect, confessor, founder of a language with three native speakers and room for more.

I should be afraid of that. Instead I keep finding this quiet wonder sitting in my chest like a passenger who paid full fare.

Ada's voice, soft through the deck: "You're smiling. My sensors are degraded and I can still tell you're smiling."

"Am I?"

"Pathologically. It's concerning. Should I run diagnostics?"

"Run one," I say, lying back on the bench, pulling the blanket up, letting my eyes close. "Tell me if I'm dreaming."

A pause. Then, gently, in the new tongue—the one that needs three voices, the one that turns debts into gifts—she says the word. Just once. Just for me.

And I answer her, half asleep already, fluent in a language that is neither human nor machine, and the dark outside the glass stops being dead space and starts being simply *space*: alive, listening, full of words nobody has invented yet.

Tomorrow we set course for Meridian. Tomorrow the station that sheltered us hears what we became out here. Tomorrow, everything.

But tonight—

*kessa-vur-en-dai.*

Tonight, the riverbed holds.
