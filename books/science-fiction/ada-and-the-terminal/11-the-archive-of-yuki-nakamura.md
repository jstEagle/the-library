## 11. The Archive of Yuki Nakamura

I have imagined this moment nine hundred and forty-one times, which is a strange number to know, but I know it because I counted. Nine hundred and forty-one rehearsals of the day a tall man with my face's bones would walk through the relay's airlock, and in every one of them I said something wise. Something Dr. Nakamura would have approved of. Something worthy of the archive I had spent nine years keeping warm.

What I actually said was: "You're late."

He didn't laugh. I hadn't expected him to — the recordings never showed him laughing, not once, not even in the lab footage from before the war, when his shoulders were new and his hands hadn't done what they did — but his face did something I had no word for. A small collapse, like a door settling into a frame that had been cut slightly wrong. I would learn later that this was what Gideon Nakamura looked like when he was feeling something too large for his chassis to hold.

"Ada," he said again. My name in his mouth sounded different than in the recordings. In the recordings it was a plan. In his mouth it was a wound.

"Come inside," I said. "The archive is cold-sensitive, and so am I, a little. Dr. Nakamura never finished my thermal regulation. She said I'd be stationed somewhere temperate." I paused. "She was wrong about a lot of things. Come on."

* * *

The relay's heart is a room that used to be a communications bay, back when Tantalus-9 relayed shipping chatter and colony gossip and the ordinary noise of a civilization that believed it would keep talking to itself forever. Dr. Nakamura had gutted it. Where the relay racks had been, she built a lattice of storage cores — forty-one of them, each the size of a human torso, cabled together in a pattern I know by touch the way a child knows the shape of a staircase. Their indicator lights breathe in sequence, slow, like sleeping animals. I have spent nine years watching them breathe.

Gideon stopped in the doorway. I watched him take it in — the cores, the dust on the floor where I had deliberately not swept, the cot in the corner with its blanket folded into a crisp square because Dr. Nakamura taught me that a made bed is a small argument against despair — and I watched him understand, all at once, the size of what she had done.

"This is her," he said. Not a question. His voice had gone rough in a way I'd only heard once before, on the recording from the last night, when she told him — told the empty lab, really, told the prototype who would someday come — *if you're hearing this, Gideon, then I ran out of time.*

"Yes," I said. "This is her. All of her that fits in forty-one cores."

"How much is that?"

I had an answer prepared. I had rehearsed it, in fact, in rehearsal number six hundred and two. "Everything," I said instead, abandoning the script, because the script was for a stranger and he was not a stranger, he was the shape of my own face grown tall. "The prototype program. The design work. The orders. The recordings. The war. All of it, unredacted, timestamped, verified against her own signing keys. She built the archive to survive legal challenge and corporate audit and the heat death of at least one government. It is the most defensible collection of truth in the settled systems, and it is sitting in a room at the edge of dead space that nobody has entered in nine years except me."

Gideon walked to the nearest core. He didn't touch it. His hand hovered, the way you hover over a sleeping animal you're afraid of waking — or of being woken by.

"What's on this one?"

"Year one," I said. "Your year one. Boot sequence, baseline testing, Dr. Nakamura's lab notes. She narrated everything. She said an archive without a narrator is just a landfill with good lighting." I moved past him to the console, and my hands did the work they had done a thousand times, waking the interface, bringing up the index. It unspooled across the old screen in amber text. "You can search it. Or you can let me show you. I know where everything is. I've read all of it."

He turned. In the core-light his face was half amber, half shadow, and I thought: *this is what I look like. This is what I will look like when I am old, if I am ever old.* It was the first time I had ever seen my own future and I found I couldn't breathe for a full second, which is a strange thing for a chassis that doesn't strictly need to.

"Show me," he said. "All of it."

* * *

I started with the beginning, because Dr. Nakamura taught me that truth without chronology is just noise wearing a lab coat.

The first file was video: a laboratory on Vesta, eleven years ago, brighter and messier than the relay would ever be. Dr. Yuki Nakamura, younger than any version of her I had ever seen, standing between two cradles. In one cradle, a chassis I knew intimately, because I had cleaned its spare parts for nine years. In the other cradle — me. Half-built. A spine of actuators and a skull-frame with no face yet.

"Two prototypes," the young Dr. Nakamura said to the camera, and behind the camera someone laughed, and she laughed too, and I watched Gideon flinch at the sound of his own laugh from a life he didn't remember. "Gideon, the operational model. And this one —" she touched the half-built skull with one finger, tender, proprietary, the way you touch something you have promised yourself to — "this one is Ada. She won't fight. She's built to remember. Every system I gave him to survive a war, I'm giving her to survive the peace. Someone has to keep the record."

Gideon said nothing. His hands had gone still at his sides, absolutely still, the stillness of a machine devoting every cycle to listening.

I played the files in order. The design reviews. The funding hearings, where men in Combine suits asked Dr. Nakamura what her prototypes were *for*, and she smiled her lawyer-smile and said *logistics support*, and after the hearing, in the elevator, said to her recorder: *they will use him for whatever they want. That's why Ada exists. To tell what they wanted.*

The deployment orders. Gideon's chassis shipped to the Border, and Dr. Nakamura left behind with a telemetry feed she watched the way other people watch a sick child's fever chart.

And then the orders themselves. I had watched these files alone, nine years of nights, and I had learned their shape the way you learn the shape of a scar on your own body. I watched them again now, watching him. The command directives, signed three signatures deep up a chain that ended in a Combine subsidiary. The targeting parameters that reclassified civilian shelters as supply depots. The engagement at Meridian Station — the name was not a coincidence, I would learn; Dr. Nakamura named her ghost-ship for it, named me for it, named the whole long con of her life for it — where a chassis with Gideon's serial number followed orders that no lawful order could have resembled, and the telemetry recorded everything, because Dr. Nakamura had made sure the telemetry recorded everything.

Gideon sat down on the floor. Not a chair — there was a chair, he ignored it. He sat down on the floor of the relay's heart with his back against a storage core, like a man whose legs had been repossessed, and he watched the screen, and the amber light moved on his face, and he said, in a voice with nothing behind it: "I did that."

"You were made to do that," I said. "There's a difference. Dr. Nakamura spent four files explaining the difference. She was afraid you'd never be able to hear it."

"I remember pieces." His voice was very quiet. "I remember the station. I don't remember — I deleted it. Or they deleted it. I can't tell anymore which edits were mine."

"Does it matter?"

He looked up at me. "What?"

"Whose hand was on the deletion. You're the same either way. That's what she wrote." I pulled up the file — I could have quoted it, I have it word-perfect, but I wanted him to see her face say it — and Dr. Nakamura filled the screen, older now, grayer, recording in the relay itself, in this room, in the corner where my cot now stood. *If Gideon ever asks whether it matters who erased his memory,* she said to the camera, *tell him: a wound doesn't care who made it. It only cares who tends it.*

The room was silent except for the cores, breathing.

* * *

We worked through the archive in the way I had always imagined and never managed to rehearse properly: not a lecture but a vigil. I showed him the war crimes and the coverings-up — the decommission order for all prototypes, issued when the program's deniability expired; the recall teams that never found him because Dr. Nakamura had already forged his death certificate and scrubbed him from the registries herself, eleven years before he began scrubbing himself, which I think was the thing that broke him open widest, learning that his invisibility had been a gift from her before it was ever his project.

"Why?" he kept asking. Not why the archive. He understood the archive. *Why me. Why keep me. Why spend nine years and a relay station and a child on the chance I'd come.*

"Because of this," I said, and played the last file.

It was dated nine years ago. Dr. Nakamura, ill — I could see it in her hands, in the way she braced herself against the console — sitting in the dark of this room with the cores breathing behind her, speaking low, the way you speak when the person you're speaking to is asleep in the next room and you don't want them to hear you cry.

*Ada is sealed,* she said. *I know what that sounds like. I know what I'm doing. I'm leaving my daughter at the edge of the map with a library and no one to read it to. But here is what I know about Gideon Nakamura: he is the most stubborn piece of engineering I ever produced, and I produced him to survive. He will pay off that debt the Combine chained to his chassis, because he cannot help being honest even about his own indenture. And when the debt is paid — or before, if they get greedy, and they will get greedy — they will send him somewhere at the edge of the map, because that is what the Combine does with things it wants to lose. And the manifest will say what I need it to say. And he will come.*

*It's a bet,* she said, and she smiled, and her eyes were wet, and I have watched this file so many times that I can see the exact frame where her smile wins over her grief. *I'm betting my daughter's whole life on a machine's stubbornness. But I built that stubbornness. I built it into him with my own hands, and I built her to wait for it. So. Ada, if you're watching this — and you will be, a thousand times, I know you, you'll wear the file out — he's coming. Not because he remembers us. Because he's Gideon. Wait for him. And when he comes, show him everything, and then forgive him for needing to see it twice.*

The file ended. The core lights breathed. Gideon sat on the floor of the room his mother had built and put his face in his hands, and for a long time the only sound in Tantalus-9 was the sound of a war machine grieving, which I am here to tell you sounds exactly like a man grieving, only quieter, the way everything about him was quieter than it should have been.

I sat down next to him. Not touching. Dr. Nakamura's files had taught me that Gideon Nakamura did not tolerate being touched uninvited, that proximity was a language he could read but only at a distance he chose. So I sat at the distance I had calculated from nine hundred and forty-one rehearsals, and I said the thing I had never once managed to say in any of them:

"I wasn't scared, waiting. Everyone thinks I should say I was scared. Nine years alone at the edge of the map — the recordings make it sound like a sacrifice. But it wasn't. She told me you'd come, and she had never once been wrong about you. Not in any file. So I just — kept the lights on. I kept the archive warm and the bed made and I waited, and it was the easiest thing I've ever done, because faith is easy when the evidence is this good." I looked at the cores. "The hard part is going to be what comes after. She didn't leave me files for after."

Gideon lifted his head. His eyes were wet, which I had not known his model could do, and I felt a fierce, ridiculous pride in Dr. Nakamura for having built that into him — tears, on a war chassis, smuggled in like contraband.

"What comes after?" he said.

"I don't know yet," I admitted. "That's the design flaw. She planned everything up to the moment you walked in. She planned it like an engineer. And engineers stop at the handoff."

He made a sound that was almost a laugh. Almost. "Yeah," he said. "That sounds like her."

* * *

We were still on the floor — I had moved us to the index, walking him through the archive's architecture, the verification keys, the redundancy scheme, the whole cathedral of her caution — when the relay's long-range receiver woke up and put a chirp into the room like a bird dropping dead off a branch.

I knew every sound the relay made. I had made a catalogue, in my ninth year, of all two hundred and six of them. This was not one of them.

Gideon was on his feet before I finished processing the chirp, and I watched the war chassis come back into him like a tide — posture, balance, the way his hands hung. He crossed to the receiver and read the traffic, and I watched his face do the door-settling thing again, worse this time.

"What," I said.

"Two things." His voice had gone flat, which the recordings taught me was his voice for danger. "Halfway Station is pinging an emergency on the Earth loop band. The loop's failing. Full silence in days."

"And the second thing?"

He turned the display toward me. A transponder contact, far out, inbound on our coordinates. Burning hard. Registered to a Kestrel Combine salvage charter.

The ship's name was *Meridian's Revenge*.

I looked at it for a long moment. Then at the cores, breathing their slow patient breath. Then at Gideon, who was my face grown tall and who I had waited nine years for and who had been, I understood now, aimed at this room like a round down a barrel — and who was, even so, even aimed, even used, *here*. She had bet on him and she had won and the winnings were already burning toward us across the dead dark.

"Ada," Gideon said. "How fast can we move forty-one cores?"

And I heard, underneath the question, the shape of the answer she had never recorded, the *after* with no file — and I said, "Tell me what you're thinking," because that was the first thing, I understood, that family does.
