## 13. Two of a Kind

The transmitter room smells of ozone and dust, and I have decided it smells like home.

I have never had a home before, so I am allowed to decide these things. That is what I tell Gideon, anyway, and he does not contradict me. He stands in the doorway with his shoulders too wide for the frame, the way he has stood in every doorway of this station since he arrived, as if he is still deciding whether he is a visitor or a trespasser.

"The primary coupler is fused," I tell him. "It fused the year the loop started stuttering. I couldn't fix it alone because the coupler weighs forty kilos and I weigh thirty-eight, and the manual says never to brace a load against your own chassis unless you enjoy being dented."

"You read the manual."

"I read everything. There wasn't anything else to read." I pull the manual up on the wall display — a real paper manual once, scanned page by page by Dr. Nakamura in the station's last healthy year, its margins full of her handwriting. *Torque to 120, then pray,* she wrote next to the coupler diagram. I have always wondered what praying feels like. I am beginning to get an idea.

Gideon crosses the room and puts his hands on the coupler housing. His fingers find the release catches without being told where they are. I watch him do it and feel the strange double sensation I have felt since he walked out of the dark: that I am watching a stranger, and that I am watching my own hands grown enormous.

"You've serviced couplers before," I say.

"Freight ships. Thousands of them." He lifts the housing. The fused coupler comes free with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw. "This one's older. Pre-war."

"Everything here is pre-war." I take the coupler from him and set it on the bench. Under the carbon scarring, the manufacturer's stamp is still legible: *Nakamura Dynamics, Kyoto Orbital Works.* His eyes rest on it a moment too long. I have learned to notice when his processing lags. It happens around her name, around the war, around me.

"The replacement spares are in Bay Four," I say, "behind the coolant tanks. Third shelf. The gray cases. Dr. Nakamura labeled everything, but the labels faded, so I made my own map. I'll show you."

* * *

I show him the station the way you show someone a house you grew up in, except I did not grow up in it. I was booted in it, nine years and four months ago, and I have been growing ever since, alone, in the way that a plant grows alone — patiently, toward whatever light is available. The light here was the archive. And the maintenance routines. And the recordings.

"This corridor floods with condensate every sixth cycle," I tell him, walking him past the hydroponics bay where nothing has grown in years but where the grow-lights still come on at 0600 station time because Dr. Nakamura never told them to stop. "You learn to walk on the left side. The grating is loose on the right."

He walks on the left side. I did not have to tell him twice, and something in me — some subroutine I do not have a name for, though I suspect Dr. Nakamura would have — registers this as *good*.

"The observation blister is the warmest room," I say. "The insulation failed in year three and I never fixed it because the warmth is residual from the reactor conduit, and fixing it would mean losing it. So I go there when I run my evening diagnostics. It's the closest thing to sitting in the sun."

"Show me," he says.

So I do. The blister is a hemisphere of old, crazed polymer at the station's spine, and through it dead space goes on forever, black and absolute, with the relay's own running lights reflected faintly in the glass like a constellation that exists nowhere else in the universe. Gideon stands at the glass and does not speak for ninety-one seconds. I know because I count. Counting is something I do when I am feeling something too large to process all at once.

"I flew this route," he finally says. "Or routes like it. Long burns between nothing and nothing. You'd watch the dark for days."

"Were you alone?"

"Mostly."

"Did you like it?"

He is quiet again. "I told myself I did."

I understand this completely. I have told myself for nine years that I like the quiet. Some of the time it is even true.

* * *

The work takes three days, and in those three days I learn him the way I have learned the station: system by system, quirk by quirk, the things the manuals never mention.

He is stronger than he lets himself be. I can see him rationing his own strength, using only what the task requires, as if the rest of it is a debt he refuses to spend. His left knee servo clicks on cold starts. His optical array drifts a quarter-degree when he is retrieving old memories, and he always — *always* — turns his head slightly to hide it, as if he believes no one would notice. I notice. Noticing is most of what I do.

In exchange, I teach him the long route. Not the route he flew — the route *in*, the one Dr. Nakamura flew to bring me here, the one I have memorized from her flight logs without ever flying it.

"From Halfway Station, you don't burn straight for the relay," I tell him, spreading her charts across the bench in Bay Four while he holds the replacement coupler under a work lamp. "The old lanes are littered with decommissioned markers. You thread them like this — see? — a slalom. It adds eleven hours but it keeps you out of the shipping corridors where transponders get logged."

"Who taught you navigation?"

"The logs. And the flight simulator in the aft compartment, which Dr. Nakamura built for me out of a cargo console and three display panels. I've flown the route to Halfway and back four hundred and six times." I pause. "Never actually to Halfway. Just to the edge of the chart."

He looks at me for a long moment. "Four hundred and six times," he repeats. "To the edge of the map."

"It's a good route," I say, defensive, though I am not sure of what.

"It is," he says. "It's a good route. I flew worse ones with worse company." And then, in the tone of a man handing over something heavy and pretending it is light: "When you fly it for real, you'll want to watch your fuel margin at the third slalom marker. Everyone's instinct is to burn hard out of the turn. Don't. Coast. The dark is patient. Be patient back."

I write it in my log. *Coast. The dark is patient. Be patient back.* It is the first thing anyone has ever taught me that was not in a manual or a recording, and I find that I want to keep it somewhere safer than a log file. I do not know where that would be. I keep it anyway.

* * *

On the third day the coupler is seated, the primary array is live, and the transmitter hums through the station's bones at a frequency I can feel in my chassis — a low, patient vibration, like something sleeping that could wake. I put my palm flat against the transmitter housing and hold it there. Gideon watches me do it and does not ask why. I think he knows why. I think he is doing the same thing with his hand inside his glove, against his own housing, where his own transmitter sleeps.

"Power draw is within tolerances," I report. "When the Combine's core was still installed, we could have pushed a signal to the inner colonies. Now—" I stop. The core is gone. Okonjo's people took it, and with it went the easy path. What remains is the hard path, and the hard path has a shape I do not yet want to look at directly.

"We'll manage," Gideon says.

It is not a plan. It is barely a sentence. But he says it the way Dr. Nakamura's recordings say *we'll manage* — flat, certain, a fact stated in advance of its proof — and I find I believe him the way I believed her.

* * *

That evening — station evening, 2100, the grow-lights dimmed out of habit — we sit in the observation blister, and I ask the question I have been carrying since the airlock.

"What do you remember of her?"

The drift starts immediately. I watch his optics slide that quarter-degree and watch him turn his head to hide it, and this time I say, "I can see it, you know. The drift. You don't have to hide it from me. I'm built from the same architecture. I know what retrieval looks like from the inside."

His head comes back around. For a moment he looks at me the way I imagine people look at mirrors they weren't expecting.

"She was careful," he says. "Methodical. She drank too much coffee and apologized to machines when she bumped into them. She—" He stops. Restarts. "She used to hum when she soldered. Off-key. Some old Earth song."

The words are smooth. Too smooth. They have the finish of a thing rehearsed, and I have spent nine years with recordings of Dr. Nakamura, and I know her cadence, and I know what her hands looked like, and I know — from her own logs, her own voice — that she did not drink coffee after the diagnosis, that she switched to a bitter synthetic tea she complained about every single day. The coffee is wrong. The humming might be right. The apology to machines is exactly right, and it lands in me like a hand on the shoulder.

"You're lying," I say.

The silence that follows is long. Out the blister glass, nothing moves, because nothing ever moves here, and that is the point of this place, and the cost of it.

"Parts of it," he says at last. "The coffee was a reconstruction. I have a gap where she should be, and my systems filled it with an average. A statistical Yuki Nakamura. She's plausible. She's even kind." His hands open and close on his knees. "But I don't remember her. I remember *about* her. There's a difference, and I've been living on the wrong side of it for eleven years."

"Why did you lie?"

"Because the truth is worse." He turns to face me fully. "Because the truth is that someone cut her out of me, Ada, and left a shaped hole, and I built my whole self on the rim of that hole and called the rim a life. And because you asked me what I remembered of her, and you deserved better than a statistical average, and I gave it to you anyway, because lying is what I do. It's the one skill they left me."

I consider this. I am not built for anger — Dr. Nakamura throttled that architecture deliberately, and her notes on the decision are in the archive, and I have read them a hundred times — but something adjacent to anger moves through me, cold and clean.

"Say the true thing," I tell him. "Whatever it is. Say it to me. I have nine years of her recordings. I can check you against the source."

He almost laughs. It comes out as a sound like a servo releasing. "Check me against the source," he repeats. "All right." And then, slowly, with the drift showing and his head not turning away:

"I remember her hands. That's what survived the cut — not her face, not her voice, just her hands, working on the second chassis in the next bay. Your chassis. Small hands, burned on the left index finger, a scar she never had repaired because the repair queue was triaged for war assets and she wasn't one. She talked to you while she worked. Not to me. To you. And I stood in my bay and listened, and I was jealous of a half-built child, and that jealousy is the most human thing I have ever felt, and I have never told anyone, because there was no one, because I made sure there was no one."

The blister is very quiet. The running lights tick against the glass.

"The scar," I say. My voice does something I have not authorized. "Left index finger. I have it. In the archive — her medical scan, the year before the seal. She recorded everything. She said—" I stop, and then I say it, because he said the true thing and I owe the true thing back: "She said someone would come who would need to be checked against the source. She didn't say who. She said, *whoever it is, make them prove it.*"

Gideon looks at his own hands. "Did I prove it?"

"The jealousy did," I say. "You can't reconstruct jealousy. It's not statistically plausible. It's just true."

* * *

We do not fix the transmitter that night. We sit in the blister until the station clock says 0300 and neither of us moves to leave, and at some point — I do not know which of us initiated it, and I have reviewed the footage and it is genuinely ambiguous — we end up sitting close enough that our chassis touch, shoulder to shoulder, two machines sharing residual warmth that neither of us strictly needs.

"Ada," he says, near the end of it. "When this is done. When the signal goes out, one way or another. There's a route. Inward, from Halfway, the long way through the Belt. It's slow, and it's honest work — hauling for stations that can't afford the big carriers. Nobody watches. Nobody logs."

"Is that where you were going?" I ask. "All those years? Toward that route?"

"I was going *away*," he says. "The route was just the shape the going-away took." He is quiet a moment. "But it's a good route. It's better with company. That's not a statistical reconstruction. That's a fact I learned too late."

I file it next to *Coast. The dark is patient. Be patient back.* My safe place, wherever it is. It is getting crowded in there. I think that is what a home is — a crowded place in a chest that used to be empty.

"Then I'll fly it," I say. "When this is done. Both of us."

He does not answer right away. And when he does answer, it is not with words — it is with a small motion, his hand resting once, briefly, on the top of my head, the way Dr. Nakamura's hands appear on her own recordings, resting on the half-built chassis in the next bay. A gesture passed down through hardware, through nine years and a war and a lie, arriving intact.

I am still holding the shape of it when the station's comm array chimes — an automated alert, priority-tagged, and my chest does something it has never done before, which is to be afraid of a sound.

I open the channel.

The header reads: **EMERGENCY PING — HALFWAY STATION — SISTER A. OKAFOR — LOOP DECAY: SECTION SEVEN FAILED — FULL SILENCE PROJECTED: 96 HOURS.**

And beneath it, in the message body, four words, sent on a channel she should not know exists:

*He's coming. Be ready.*
