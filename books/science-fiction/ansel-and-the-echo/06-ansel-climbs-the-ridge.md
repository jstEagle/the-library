## 6. Ansel Climbs the Ridge

There are exactly three things my mother told me never to do, and I want it on the record that she told me these things in a single breath, on a single evening, while simultaneously burning dinner and arguing with Dad about water rations. One: don't touch the recycler intake with your tongue. Two: don't ever let me catch you gambling with Tomas Vey's older brother, because he counts cards and you count on your fingers. Three: *never, ever go up to the beacon ridge at night.*

I've kept rule one for most of my life. Rule two lasted eleven days. And rule three—

Well.

Here's the thing about rules like rule three. They're not really rules. They're stories. *Don't go up there* is a story whose ending is always *because something will get you.* Nobody ever says what the something is. On Meridian, nobody even tries. The adults just do this thing where their faces go smooth and closed, like shutters, and they say "it's dangerous" in the same voice they use for "we can't afford it" and "not until you're older," which are the three sentences that make up roughly ninety percent of all adult communication as far as I can tell.

But here's what I knew that the adults didn't, or wouldn't say: something up on that ridge was already getting *me.* Every night for two weeks, the same dream. The tower of light. The woman's voice, singing words I couldn't quite hold onto, like trying to carry water in a net. Waking up with my heart going like I'd run the whole perimeter road.

And I wasn't alone anymore. That was the part that made it real. Seven of us. Same tower. Same voice. Priya Chandrasekaran could hum four notes of the melody before she lost it, and when she did, in the schoolyard, my arms got gooseflesh so hard it hurt, because those were *my* notes. From *my* head. Out of *my* dream, coming out of somebody else's mouth.

Ms. Okafor-the-teacher—not related to anybody important—said it was probably a shared atmospheric phenomenon affecting sleep cycles, which is teacher for *I don't know and I'm not paid enough to find out.* She wrote it down in her incident log, though. I saw her do it. So somewhere, officially, there's a piece of paper that says seven children dreamed the same song, and I think about that piece of paper more than is probably healthy.

Anyway. Thursday night, I decided to go meet the something.

* * *

Sneaking out of our hab is genuinely easy, which should worry my parents more than it does. Our unit has a back panel behind the water heater that Dad removed during the Great Plumbing Crisis of last spring and never properly reattached, because Dad believes that any repair completed is a repair finished, and Mom has decided that fighting him about it costs more energy than the hole is worth. I'd mapped the route months ago, back when the plan was theoretical and mostly bravado I could enjoy from the safety of my bunk. Back panel, service alley, compost row, fence gap by the old hydroponics shed, then open ground.

The open ground was the problem.

Between the colony's edge and the ridge there's maybe two kilometers of nothing—scrub grass engineered to survive Meridian's idea of soil, which is basically ground-up regret—and then the slope itself, rising up to where the beacon stands. In daylight it's just a hill with a building on it. At night, with both moons down and only the stars doing the lighting, it's a different country entirely. Every sound is a sound *at* you. Wind through the scrub sounds like breathing. My own footsteps sounded like an announcement.

*I am going up to the ridge,* my footsteps said. *Nobody stop me. Especially not anything with teeth.*

Meridian doesn't have anything with teeth. I know this. I've read the survey reports for fun, which tells you a lot about how popular I was at school before the dream thing started. But knowing a fact and walking through the dark toward a place your mother forbade are two very different activities, and only one of them comes with a full set of working nerves.

Halfway across the flats I stopped and had what I now recognize as my first real negotiation with myself. Not the cowardly kind—I'd had plenty of those, standing at the fence gap for a full ten minutes doing breathing exercises I learned from a video about test anxiety. This was the honest kind. The kind where you ask yourself why, actually, and expect an answer.

Why was I doing this?

Because of the voice. Because for two weeks something had been reaching into my head every night, singing to me, and everyone with authority over my life had responded with either smooth shutter-faces or atmospheric phenomena. Because Priya had hummed those four notes and my whole body had recognized them like a name. Because if there was a woman inside a tower of light—and I was old enough to know there wasn't, and young enough to need to check—that woman deserved to have somebody come looking for her instead of logging her as weather.

Also, fine, yes: because I wanted to be the one who knew. There were forty kids at that school who'd looked straight through me for three years, and if I came back with the answer—if *I* was the one who found out what lived on the ridge—then I would be a person who had done a thing. You can't buy that kind of currency on Meridian. Believe me, I've checked the exchange rates.

So. Negotiation concluded. Terms accepted. I kept walking.

The climb took longer than I expected because I kept having to stop and listen, and what I heard, once I got high enough, stopped my feet entirely.

She was singing.

Not in my head. Not in the muffled underwater way of dreams. Actually, physically, audibly singing, thin and strange, threading down the slope through the night air like smoke. It was the melody—the *whole* melody, the one none of us could keep past four notes—and hearing it complete was like seeing a face you've only known from a shadow. Familiar and wrong and right all at once.

My legs made the rest of the decisions. I'd love to tell you I crested the ridge with tactical caution and a well-formed plan, but honestly I climbed the way you follow a smell of bread, half-dreaming, pulled.

* * *

The beacon station sits on the highest point of the ridge like a tooth. I'd seen it a hundred times from below—a squat block of old concrete with the antenna tower rising off its back, red warning lights blinking their slow patient blink. What I hadn't known was that the whole structure hums. You feel it in your molars before you hear it, a deep sub-audible thrum, like standing next to something enormous that's purring.

There was light in the windows. That stopped me cold, crouched behind a generator housing that smelled like hot dust. I'd assumed—I don't know what I'd assumed. That the station was empty. That the voice came out of the machine by itself, some ghost in the wiring, and I'd climb up and put my ear against the tower and that would be that.

But there was light. Which meant a person. Which meant everything I'd built this expedition out of was suddenly load-bearing in ways I hadn't planned for.

I should describe what happened next accurately, including the parts that embarrass me. For a long time—long enough that my knees complained and a night-bird startled me twice into a full-body flinch—I just stayed there behind the generator, listening to the singing come through the wall, and tried to make myself be brave in the specific direction of the door. Bravery, it turns out, is not a thing you summon once. It's a thing you spend, and mine was trickling out through my palms, which were sweating so badly I had to wipe them on my pants twice.

Then the singing stopped.

And in the silence that followed, I heard a voice. Low, flat, unhurried. A woman's voice, but nothing like the singer—this one had no music in it at all, just words laid down one after another like bricks.

"Recalibrating harmonic carrier. Drift zero-point-three. Compensating."

A pause. A click, or something like a click.

"Again."

And the singing started over.

I know now what I was hearing: the beacon playing the recording, and Astrid Blackwood talking to the machine the way she talked to everything, in short flat pieces with no wasted warmth. But at the time, hunched behind a generator with my heart slamming, I assembled it into something else entirely. I thought the machine was talking to *her.* I thought the tower had caught someone and was making her sing. I thought—I swear this is true, and I was twelve, so grant me mercy—that I had found the something, and the something had already gotten somebody else, and the somebody else was the tall gray-eyed woman from town whom everyone avoided and nobody spoke to except to hand her supply chits at arm's length.

Astrid Blackwood. Up on the ridge. With the voice.

Every sensible circuit in my brain said *run.* And I want to be clear: I stood up to run. That was the intention. Legs loaded, weight forward, the whole escape already loading like a slingshot.

But the door was right there. Six steps away. And through it, spilling out onto the gravel, was a stripe of warm yellow light, and standing in that light, framed and lit like something in a museum case, was Astrid Blackwood herself—and she was crying.

Not sobbing. Nothing so loud. Just standing very straight in front of a wall of ancient equipment, hands loose at her sides, face tilted up toward a speaker grille, with tears running down it in two clean lines, unattended, like rain on glass that nobody had squeegeed. Her mouth was doing the thing mouths do when they're holding themselves shut. And the singing poured over her, and she let it, the way you stand in rain you've decided not to shelter from.

I forgot to run.

That's the truest sentence in this whole account. Terror transmuted—not into courage, exactly, but into something hungrier than fear. Curiosity is just terror that's found a better question. And my better question was standing right there, leaking, while a dead woman's voice sang to her from a machine.

Because I knew it was a dead woman. Don't ask me how. Twelve-year-olds know things in their bones that take adults decades to admit. The song was a lullaby—I understood that suddenly, wholly, the way you understand a joke a beat late—and lullabies are sung *to* people, and nobody sings to machines, and whatever was on the other side of that speaker had been gone long enough that the grief on Astrid's face had worn smooth, like stone in a river. Old grief. Practiced grief. Grief she did alone, at night, on a ridge above a town that wouldn't look at her.

I stepped on a rock.

It wasn't even a big rock. It made a small click against the concrete apron, and Astrid Blackwood turned around faster than anything human should, and we looked at each other through six meters of night air and one rectangle of yellow light.

Here's what I expected: alarms. Shouting. Being seized and marched home to a mother whose face would go through seventeen expressions before settling on murder. Here's what actually happened: nothing. She just looked at me. Her eyes did a slow pass over me—face, hands, feet, the whole inventory, like she was checking whether I was armed or injured—and then she said, in that same flat brick-laying voice:

"You're the Vey-adjacent child. Ansel."

"Ansel Torres," I said, because apparently my mouth had also decided not to run. "Ansel's fine."

"You are supposed to be asleep."

"So are you," I said, and immediately wished I could eat the words, and then—because the words were already out and the night was already ruined or saved—added, "except you're not sleeping, you're crying, and the lady in the machine is singing, and I've heard her every night for two weeks and so have six other kids and nobody will tell us anything, so." I swallowed. "So I came to ask her myself."

The silence went on long enough that I heard the beacon humming in my teeth again. Astrid's hand moved toward her hip—an old motion, quick, like reaching for something that used to be there—and stopped halfway, and finished as adjusting her sleeve. I didn't understand that gesture then. I understand it now, and it keeps me up sometimes.

"You dream of her voice," Astrid said. Not a question. Something worse than a question: a confirmation.

"The tower of light," I said. "She sings and you can't remember the words after you wake up, except tonight I think I'll remember, because I heard them for real." My voice cracked on *real,* which was humiliating, and I pushed through it because stopping would have been worse. "Is she in there? Is she alive?"

Astrid looked at me for a long time. Behind her, the singing went on, sweet and steady, a lullaby for soldiers, though I didn't know that yet. And I watched something move across her face—something that had clearly not moved across it in years—like weather arriving over flat country.

"No," she said finally. "She is not alive. She is a recording. She has been a recording since before you were born."

"Then why does she sound like she's calling us?"

Something flickered there too. Fast. Gone.

"That," said Astrid Blackwood, "is a question I have been asking for twenty years."

And then—this is the part I still can't fully believe, and I was *there*—she stepped aside from the doorway. All the way aside. An opening. An invitation, or a dare, or maybe just a tired woman deciding that the first person in years to climb her ridge in the dark deserved better than a locked door.

"Come inside, Ansel Torres," she said. "If you have been dreaming of her, you should see where she lives."

I looked back down the slope, at the distant scatter of the colony's lights, at the window where my mother slept thirty meters from a hole behind a water heater. Every rule I'd ever been given stood at the bottom of that hill, shaking its finger.

I went inside.

What I heard in there—what she played for me, the whole recording, start to finish, the lullaby and the murmured voices beneath it and the sound of men breathing in the dark like the dark was a place they might not leave—I'm going to keep some of that. Not to hoard it. To protect it, until I understand it well enough to share. But I'll tell you the beginning, because the beginning is what matters: the moment the song ended, the room went quiet, and Astrid reached out and touched the console with two fingers, gently, the way you touch a sleeping person's shoulder, and said, "This is Commander Ilsa Voss. She held two fleets apart with nothing but her voice. And now—" and here her flat brick-laying voice actually faltered, one syllable, a crack no wider than a hair—"now she is calling your children in their sleep. And I do not know why."

I believed her. That's the strange part. Everyone in town treated her like furniture with a debt attached, and I'd done it too, in my way—looked through her, logged her as scenery. But standing in the yellow light with a dead commander's lullaby still ringing in my ears, I looked at Astrid Blackwood and saw the one adult on Meridian who wasn't lying to me.

Which meant, I realized with a cold slide in my stomach, that she was probably the only one who *could.*

"I can help," I said. "I know things. Kids hear things. And there are six others who dream it—we could—"

"No," Astrid said. But she said it slowly, looking at me, and I was twelve, not stupid. I knew a door left unlocked when I stood in one.

I walked home before dawn, through the flats, past the fence gap, in through the panel behind the water heater, and lay in my bunk with the melody finally, completely, permanently mine. And I made a list. Kids who dreamed. Questions that needed answers. Adults who might help, ranked by likelihood, with Administrator Reyes at the bottom and Ms. Okafor-the-teacher somewhere in the middle.

Because the voice was calling forty of us now—or it would be soon; I could feel it spreading like warmth through a cold room—and whatever was waking up on that ridge, Astrid Blackwood couldn't hold it alone.

Neither, it turned out, could the truth. But neither of us knew that yet.
