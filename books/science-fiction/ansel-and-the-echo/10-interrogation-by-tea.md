## 10. Interrogation by Tea

I want it on the record that I did not become an administrator to conduct interrogations. I became an administrator because I am very good at spreadsheets and moderately good at pretending that budgets are a form of ethics. Somewhere along the way the job metastasized into this: sitting across from a war machine with a pot of tea between us, trying to catch her in a lie without ever once using the word *lie*.

The tea was my idea, obviously. You don't summon a decommissioned combat android to your office—that's a deposition, and depositions generate paper trails, and paper trails generate questions from people above me who have never seen Meridian's sky and would trade all of us for a favorable shipping lane. No. You invite her for tea. Casual. Neighborly. The kind of thing a colony administrator does on a slow afternoon.

The fact that I spent two days rehearsing is nobody's business but mine.

* * *

She arrived at exactly the hour I'd named, which told me she'd parsed the invitation as what it was. Astrid Blackwood does not do casual. She does punctual, precise, and—here was the thing I kept forgetting until she was standing in my doorway filling it entirely—the genuinely unnerving. War-model chassis. The kind they stopped making after the armistice made them politically embarrassing. She had to duck slightly under my doorframe, which somehow made it worse: something that big, being polite about it.

"Administrator." Her voice is low and even, like a tide that has agreed to behave itself.

"Astrid. Please, sit." I gestured at the chair I'd reinforced last week without letting myself think too hard about why. "Tea?"

"If you're having some."

"I'm always having some. It's the only vice I can afford."

She sat. The chair held. Small mercies. I poured two cups and watched her hands as she took hers—those hands could open a bulkhead like a tin of sardines, and they wrapped around a chipped ceramic cup with the exaggerated care of someone handling a bird. Or performing the handling of a bird. With Astrid I've learned you can never fully rule out performance. That's not an accusation; it's just what decommissioning means. They didn't remove her capacity for violence. They removed her permission, and then they handed her a ledger of debt-days and called it freedom.

"So," I said, settling back with my own cup. "How's the beacon?"

And there it was. The pause. Not long—a half-second, maybe less, the gap between one heartbeat and the next—but I'd been watching for it the way you watch a horizon for weather. Most people wouldn't have caught it. Most people see Astrid and their eyes slide off, the way eyes slide off a cliff face. But I'd spent three nights in the deep archive by then, and I'd come out the other side a different flavor of tired.

"The beacon functions within parameters," she said.

"Within parameters," I repeated. "That's almost poetry."

"It's a maintenance report."

"It's a maintenance report I've read forty times, verbatim, across eleven years of quarterly filings. Word for word, Astrid. 'Functions within parameters.' Do you know what I did last week? I ran a textual analysis on every beacon report in the colonial record. Eleven years. Identical phrasing. Not similar—identical. Down to the comma placement." I smiled over my tea. "Either you're the most consistent writer in human history, or you've been copy-pasting the same sentence since before I took this office."

Something moved behind her eyes. If she'd been human I'd have called it discomfort. In her, who knows. Maybe it's just fans spinning up.

"Standardized reporting reduces error," she said.

"It does. It also reduces information. Which brings me to my actual question, and please understand I'm asking as a colleague, not—" I waved a hand, nearly sloshing tea, which rather undercut my authority. "Not as anything else. The beacon's maintenance contract predates our records. Every other piece of infrastructure on Meridian has a paper trail. Founders' agreements, colonial charter attachments, requisition forms with somebody's coffee stain on them. The beacon has nothing. It's just... there. Older than the oldest document we own. And yet every budget cycle, we pay for its upkeep, and nobody remembers authorizing that line item. Does that strike you as strange?"

"No," said Astrid.

I waited. She drank her tea. I realized I was going to have to work for every inch of this conversation, and that she knew it, and that she was prepared to let me work.

"You don't find it strange that we're paying tithes to a machine nobody commissioned?"

"The contract exists. Payment follows contracts. That is not strange; that is order."

"And where did the contract come from?"

"The founding administration."

"There was no founding administration with authority over orbital systems. I checked. Twice. There were nine signatories on the colonial charter and not one of them had jurisdiction past the atmosphere." I leaned forward. "So either the contract is forged, or it was issued by someone who wasn't part of the colony. Someone military, perhaps. Someone who needed a custodian for a piece of equipment and picked the nearest available—"

"Custodian," she said.

The word dropped into the room like a coin into a well. I hadn't actually used it. I'd danced around it, circled it, offered it up obliquely—and she'd picked it out of the air and set it down between us, deliberate as a chess move.

"That's the term in the old filings," I said carefully. "Registered custodian. Except the registered custodian is listed as one E. Marchetti, who died twenty-two years ago, may she rest, and whose signature appears on renewal documents dated four years after her death. Which is a neat trick. So my question, Astrid, the question I drove myself half-blind in the archives to be able to ask properly, is: who actually maintains the beacon?"

"I maintain the beacon," she said. "Under delegated authority from the registered custodian."

"Who is dead."

"Death does not automatically void a delegation."

"In what jurisdiction?"

"Several."

I sat back. I'll admit it: for a moment I just admired her. Here I'd come armed with twenty years of documentary archaeology, cross-referenced signature chains, a timeline that matched the armistice to the day—and she'd dismantled my entire case with the serene confidence of a lawyer who knows the opposing counsel hasn't read the file. And the truly maddening thing, the thing I turned over that night while staring at my ceiling, is that she wasn't wrong. Every technicality she'd invoked probably existed somewhere in the wreckage of wartime law. She wasn't lying to me. She was doing something worse. She was telling me the truth shaped like a wall.

* * *

Here's what I know about Astrid Blackwood, the version everyone knows: she came with the second wave, debt-indentured, war-surplus, assigned to the beacon station because nobody else wanted the ridge and nobody else could survive the cold up there. She keeps to herself. She does her work. Children are warned away from her the way children are warned away from the reservoir—no specific danger cited, just a general parental instinct that things built for war should be kept at a distance.

Here's what I know now, the version from the archive: her indenture paperwork references a service annex, sealed, that predates her arrival. Her debt calculation includes line items no auditor has ever been able to reconcile. And the beacon—the great silent guardian, the reason both fleets leave us alone, the story every child on Meridian learns before they learn to write—is legally an orphan with a dead man's name on its collar and a war machine holding its leash.

I'm not a brave woman. Let's establish that. I got into administration because courage doesn't scale and paperwork does. But there's a particular kind of fear that comes from realizing the ground under your whole settlement might be a story somebody told, and that the somebody is sitting in your guest chair drinking your tea.

* * *

"More tea?" I asked, because the interrogation had stalled and my hands needed something to do.

"Thank you, no."

"You know," I said, refilling my own cup anyway, "when I took this office, my predecessor gave me exactly one piece of advice. He said: 'Reyes, there are three subjects you never touch. The water reclamation ratios, the Voss fleet, and the beacon.' I asked him why, and he looked at me like I'd asked him why we breathe." I laughed, a little. "Eleven years later, I finally understand him. It's not that those things are dangerous. It's that they're load-bearing. You start pulling threads and you don't know which walls come down."

Astrid regarded me across the table. There was a long silence—not hostile, not comfortable either. The silence of two people both deciding how much truth the room can hold.

"Your predecessor was prudent," she said finally.

"He was a coward, if you want my honest assessment. A well-meaning coward. This colony runs on his kind." I set the cup down. "But here's my problem, Astrid. Last month I found out the beacon's origin date matches the armistice to within seventy-two hours. Not colonization. Not construction. Armistice. Somebody built—or repurposed—that installation in the immediate aftermath of the war, and then buried the paperwork so deep it took me a week of digging through corrupted archives to find the ghost of it. And the same week I find that, seven children in our school report dreaming the same dream. A woman's voice. Singing. Inside a tower of light."

I watched her face when I said it. I want to be honest in this account, even the parts that flatter no one: I watched her face hoping to see fear. Guilt. Some crack I could widen into daylight.

What I saw instead was stillness so complete it made the room feel emptier. And beneath the stillness—I couldn't swear to it, I still can't—something that looked almost like grief.

"Dreams are not infrastructure," she said quietly.

"No. They're not. But they arrive on schedule, don't they? Right when the signal spikes. Right when whatever that tower's been doing for thirty years starts doing it louder." I shook my head. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I want that understood. Whatever's happening up there, whatever that beacon actually is—you may not have chosen any of it. Maybe you inherited it, same as I inherited the budget deficit. But I'm the administrator of this colony, and there are forty children now—forty, as of yesterday's count—who share a dream none of them should be able to share, and both fleets are going to notice the signal changes eventually, and when they do, they're going to come here and ask questions I cannot answer. So I'm asking them first. While it's still tea. While it's still just two neighbors talking."

For a moment—one moment—I thought she might tell me. Something shifted in her posture, some fractional release, like a door unlatching. Her mouth opened.

Then it closed.

"The beacon maintains regional stability," she said. "Its operation is classified under provisions I'm not authorized to disclose. I'm sorry, Administrator. I know that isn't the answer you wanted."

The words came out smooth. Flawless. The cadence of an official statement, the exact register of a press release, and I realized with a chill that I was listening to a protocol run in real time—some subroutine installed during the war, designed for precisely this moment, an officer asking questions and a soldier declining to answer. It wasn't evasion. It was architecture. She hadn't decided to refuse me. Something in her had decided decades ago, and all she could do was watch it happen.

And here is the detail I keep returning to, the one that cost me sleep: as she said it, her hands tightened around that chipped cup. Not much. A millimeter of extra pressure, the kind of grip that would have shattered the ceramic if she hadn't caught herself. She set it down with exaggerated gentleness, aligned the handle to the table edge, and folded her hands in her lap like a student.

Guilt wearing a calm face. I know the expression. I wear it every time I sign off on the water rationing schedules.

* * *

She stayed another ten minutes. We talked about nothing—the weather, the harvest projections, the school roof that needs replacing. She was, I noticed, better at small talk than I expected, and worse at leaving. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and stood there for a moment with her back to me.

"Administrator," she said. "Thank you for the tea."

"Astrid. One more thing. Off the record, no filings, no follow-up. Just between us." I made my voice as gentle as I could. "Whatever you're carrying up on that ridge—you don't have to carry it alone. That's all. That's the whole message."

She didn't turn around. For a moment the doorway was just full of her silhouette against the afternoon light, this enormous quiet shape, and I thought I saw her shoulders move—once, the smallest tremor, like a cable taking strain.

"Alone is the assignment," she said, and stepped out into the sun.

* * *

After she left I sat for a long time with the cooling pot and thought about what I'd learned, which was: everything and nothing. She maintains the beacon. She won't say what it is. She lied to me—or something in her did—and the lie was so clean it frightened me more than a bad one would have, because bad lies crack under pressure and good ones just get smoother.

I should report upward. That's the procedure. Unregistered custodianship of strategic infrastructure, anomalous signals, evidence of falsified documentation—any one of these goes in a formal dispatch, and formal dispatches go to the colonial authority, and the colonial authority talks to fleet liaison offices, and fleet liaison offices talk to people whose job titles include the word *defense*. Within a month, maybe less, there'd be a ship in orbit asking its own questions, and they wouldn't serve tea first.

I drafted the dispatch that evening. Three times. Each draft got shorter. The last one was a single line—*Request guidance re: beacon custodianship irregularities*—and I sat looking at it for an hour, cursor blinking, thumb hovering over send.

Because here's what stopped me, and I want to be precise about it: it wasn't loyalty to Astrid. I barely know her. It wasn't even fear of the fleets, though God knows there's plenty of that to go around. It was the children. Forty of them, dreaming in unison, waking up singing a song nobody taught them. Whatever that beacon is doing, it's doing it *to* them, or *for* them, or through them—and if I send that dispatch, the first thing any fleet investigator will do is shut the signal down and ask questions afterward. And I keep thinking about Ansel Vasquez's mother, who came to my office last week, wringing her hat in her hands, saying: *Administrator, he sleeps better than he ever has. Is that wrong? Should I be worried that he's sleeping better?*

I deleted the draft. All three of them.

Then I opened a new file, encrypted, local only, and titled it *Beacon — Personal Notes*, which is administrator-speak for *things I am choosing not to know officially*. First entry: *She lied to me today. She hated it. That matters.*

Second entry, written after a long pause: *Find out what the children hear.*

Outside my window, the ridge stood black against the stars, and at its crown the beacon pulsed its slow, patient light—steady as a heartbeat, steady as a lie—and I raised my glass of terrible whiskey to it, the way you salute something you're not sure is friend or fuse.

Three days later, Wren Okafor's scoutship entered our system, and the choice stopped being mine.
