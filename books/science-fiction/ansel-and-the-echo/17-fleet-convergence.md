## 17. Fleet Convergence

The signal died at 0347, ship's time, and I knew before the console told me.

That's the thing they don't put in the recruitment holos about being a scout: you spend so many years listening to a frequency that it stops being sound and becomes weather. The Voss deterrent carrier had been in my ears—filtered, compressed, buried under forty other channels—for six years. A low harmonic drone, steady as a heartbeat, with that strange buried texture underneath that the analysts called "carrier artifact" and everyone else called nothing at all. You stopped hearing it. Then one night you'd lie in your bunk and realize its absence was louder than anything else on the ship.

At 0347 the absence arrived, and I was already sitting up.

"Confirm," I said to the duty board, though there was no one to confirm to. Night watch on the *Kestrel's Lament* is a crew of one and a coffee dispenser with opinions. "Confirm loss of Meridian beacon."

The board confirmed. Of course it confirmed. The board has never once disagreed with me, which is either a testament to my competence or an indictment of my imagination.

I ran the diagnostics twice anyway. Antenna fault? No—the passive arrays were clean, and we had three independent receivers on that band. Propagation anomaly? Meridian's star was quiet; no flare activity in nine days. Jamming? Nothing on the spectrum but the usual hash of colonial traffic, domestic and dull as porridge.

The beacon wasn't being jammed. It wasn't being masked. It was simply *gone*, the way a voice goes gone when the singer stops mid-note.

I sat with that for a long moment, watching the light of Meridian's system crawl across my display—eleven days out at scout burn, close enough to listen, too far to touch—and I felt something I hadn't felt since the armistice.

Fear. But not the fear I'd been trained into. Something underneath it. Something that felt horribly like relief.

* * *

By 0600 I had a report drafted, redrafted, and redrafted again, which is what happens when the truth you're reporting is a shape you don't want to look at directly.

Standard protocol: *Deterrent signal from Meridian station lost at 0347. Cause unknown. Recommend immediate threat assessment.* Twelve words of procedure wrapped around a scream.

Captain Adeyemi read it in her ready room while I stood at parade rest and watched her face do very little. That's the thing about fleet officers above a certain rank—they treat their faces like classified documents.

"Lost," she said. "Not degraded. Not intermittent."

"Lost. Clean cutoff, mid-transmission cycle. No debris field, no weapons signatures in the system, no distress traffic from the colony." I paused, and then—because I'm apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone—"No evidence anyone attacked it, Captain. If someone had fired on that station, every listening post in the sector would have heard the shot."

"And if no one fired on it?"

"Then it stopped on its own."

She set down the pad. Looked out the viewport at the long dark, where Meridian was a smudge of light you could cover with a thumbnail.

"Do you know what the beacon is, Scout Okafor?" she asked.

"A deterrent, ma'am. Post-armistice. Assured retaliation against whichever party violates the cease-fire first."

"That's what the briefing says." Her voice was flat, careful. "I've been reading the briefings for twenty years. Do you know how many times anyone has verified the claim?"

I didn't answer, because the answer was *no*, and because the question was doing something to the floor beneath my feet.

"The armistice held for two decades," she went on, "on the strength of a signal nobody has ever examined at the source. We assumed the colonials built a doomsday weapon and pointed it at us. They assumed we did the same. And now the signal is gone, and both sides are going to spend the next eleven days deciding what the silence means." She turned back to me. "You're the closest asset. You'll go ahead. Full burn. Get to Meridian, get eyes on that station, and find out what we're actually looking at before the main body arrives."

"And if the main body arrives to find an answer it doesn't like?"

Adeyemi looked at me for a long moment, and behind the classified-document face I saw something tired move.

"Then eleven days will have been wasted on a question we should have asked twenty years ago," she said. "Go get dressed, Scout. You leave in an hour."

* * *

The *Lark*—my scout pinnace, all engine and eyelid-thin margins—came off the *Kestrel's Lament* like a splinter leaving skin, and I pointed her nose at Meridian and lit the drive.

Full burn means four days alone in a can the size of a storage closet, which sounds like punishment until you remember that some of us chose this job precisely because of the closet. I set the autopilot, strapped into the couch, and let myself think the thoughts I'd been not-thinking since 0347.

Here's what I know about the war, having fought in its last year and spent every year since inside its shadow: nobody I served with could tell me what it was for. Not the veterans, not the instructors, not the chaplains. There were territories, and then there were claims on territories, and then there were fleets built to enforce the claims, and somewhere in the accretion the original argument became vestigial—an appendix of history that hurt only when it inflamed.

And here's what I know about the armistice: it didn't hold because anyone made peace. It held because everyone got scared at the same time and stayed scared in the same direction. The Meridian beacon was the drum we all agreed to march to. *Fire first and die second.* Nobody ever asked what the dying mechanism actually was, because asking felt like testing it, and testing it felt like firing it.

Twenty years of peace resting on a noise neither side had ever opened up.

I'd told myself for years that the discomfort I felt around the beacon was superstition. Sailors' lore. But lying in the *Lark*'s couch with the stars sliding past, I finally admitted what had been scratching at me since the signal cut: when the silence came, I didn't feel dread.

I felt quiet. Like a headache stopping.

Which raised a question I didn't want to hold up to the light: if the end of the deterrent felt like relief instead of terror, what exactly had the deterrent been protecting me from?

* * *

Day two of the burn, the traffic started.

First it was routine—colonial freighter chatter, the administrative burble of a frontier system. Then the channel texture changed. More voices. Tighter intervals. Military cadence underneath civilian noise.

I pulled the intercepts and ran them through the linguistics stack, and the stack came back with its polite machine equivalent of raised eyebrows: colonial defense flotilla, three hulls, sortie posture. They were coming out of Meridian orbit and forming up on the approach vector—not toward the beacon station, but along the lane the Voss fleet would naturally use.

They'd heard the silence too.

Of course they had. The beacon sang to both sides equally; that was the whole architecture of the thing. The colonials had spent twenty years believing their own deterrent protected them from us, and now their protective noise had stopped, and from inside their fear it could only mean one thing: *the Voss did something.*

And back home, the mirror image. By now the fleet council would be looking at my report and drawing the same conclusion in reverse: *the colonials silenced their own beacon—which means they're preparing a strike—which means the armistice is already broken.* Two fleets, each staring at the same silence, each certain the other had made it.

I ran the geometry. Main Voss body, eleven days out when the signal died, now ten. Colonial flotilla, already moving, maybe six days from the station. My pinnace, four days out, arriving first by a comfortable margin—which meant that when the shooting started, if it started, the first witness on scene would be me.

Wonderful. I'd always wanted to be history's footnote.

I sent a flash message to Adeyemi: *Colonial flotilla underway, defensive posture. Recommend we broadcast open-channel assurance of investigative intent. Recommend hard.* The recommendation went into the queue and sat there, because recommendations from scouts sit in queues, and captains sleep on things, and fleets are slow the way glaciers are slow right up until they aren't.

Then I did the thing I probably shouldn't have done. I opened the raw archive of the beacon's last transmission cycle—the full-spectrum capture my receivers had been hoarding for years like a dragon of data—and I listened to the final minutes at maximum resolution, stripped of filters.

Underneath the deterrent drone, faint as breath under a door, there was something else. Something the analysts had filed under *carrier artifact* for twenty years.

It had melody.

I played it over and over until the drive clock dragged me back to watch the fuel margin, and every time, the same thought tightened another notch: *that's not a weapon. Weapons don't sing.*

Whatever had been holding two fleets apart for twenty years, it wasn't a doomsday device. I was starting to be able to see its outline now—a bluff, maybe, or a promise, or a grief someone had wired into a transmitter and dared the universe to call. And the terrible arithmetic of it was this: the lie, if it was a lie, had worked. Twenty years of children born who never saw a burn ship in the sky. Twenty years of farms on contested soil. All of it standing on a sound that had just stopped.

And now two fleets were converging on the silence, each one carrying the certainty that the other had broken faith, and the only person positioned to stand between them was a scout in a tin can with a recommendation stuck in a queue.

I keyed the log recorder, because someone should say these things out loud somewhere, even if it's just into a cabin the size of a coffin.

"Wren Okafor, day two, inbound Meridian. Personal log. I think the beacon was never a weapon. I think it might have been a lullaby that we all agreed to be afraid of. And I think in about four days, two exhausted fleets are going to arrive at a small farming colony ready to kill each other over the death of a song." I paused. "Recommendation follows: somebody should tell them what it actually was. Recommendation status: nobody to recommend it to. End log."

The stars kept sliding. The flotilla kept closing. Somewhere ahead, on a ridge above a town that had done nothing wrong except believe in its own safety, a tower stood silent for the first time in twenty years.

Four days. I pushed the throttle up anyway, past the line, into the red.

Let them court-martial me after.
