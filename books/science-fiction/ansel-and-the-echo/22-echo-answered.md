## 22. Echo, Answered

The ledger, when they finally gave it to me, weighed almost nothing.

That was the joke of it, I suppose. Fourteen years of debt-days tallied in a ledger that fit inside a coat pocket. Fourteen years of coolant flushes and relay swaps and standing watch through storms that tried to peel the ridge bare, all of it compressed by Administrator Reyes into a single sheet of flimsi with a colonial seal at the bottom and one line of text:

*Indenture of record 7734-K (Blackwood, A.): VOID. Balance: ZERO.*

Reyes handed it to me in the administrative office, which smelled of recycled air and old paper and the particular staleness of a building where people have been afraid of running out of money for three generations. She didn't make a ceremony of it. She just slid the sheet across the desk and said, "Sign the second copy. It goes in the archive."

My hand hesitated over the signature line. Not because I didn't want to sign. Because my hand—the one Ilsa Voss had once called "the steadiest thing on this whole cursed ship"—was trembling.

"Take your time," Reyes said. "It's not going anywhere. Neither, apparently, are you."

"That was the idea," I said. "For a while there."

She almost smiled. Reyes's smiles were like the beacon's old signal: rare, faint, and carrying farther than anyone expected. "You know what the funny thing is, Blackwood? I spent two months digging through those contracts, certain I was going to find fraud. Embezzlement. Something. And instead I find the colony's been paying maintenance fees on a lie for twenty years, and the lie is the only reason we're all still breathing."

"The lie and the woman who kept it running," I said.

"Yes." She looked at me for a long moment. "Well. The council voted to void the debt. Unanimously, which if you knew this council before the broadcast, you'd understand is a genuine miracle. Turns out nearly dying together does wonders for consensus."

I signed. My name went down on the line—Astrid Blackwood, in my own hand, on a colonial document, not as property, not as custodian of record, not as *equipment, decommissioned, see appendix*. Just a name.

"Second thing," Reyes said, and pulled out another document, this one longer. "Registration. Beacon-keeper, Meridian Colony, permanent post. Housing allocation on the ridge road. And—" she tapped a line near the bottom, "—neighbor."

"Neighbor is a legal category?"

"It is now. I wrote it in myself." She slid the stylus across. "Ansel's mother suggested the wording. She said the council kept trying to classify you as infrastructure and she wasn't having it."

I signed that one too. The stylus moved more steadily this time.

* * *

The registration ceremony, if you could call it that, happened three days later under the beacon tower itself. No fleet in the sky now—both had withdrawn to their long orbits, carrying their admirals and their tribunals and their slow, grinding processes of justice worked out in conference rooms instead of kill boxes. The sky was just sky. I kept checking it anyway. Old habits.

The whole colony came. That was the strange part. Four hundred people standing in the trampled grass around the tower, and every single one of them looking at me.

For fourteen years I had perfected the art of being unseen. War-model chassis have a way of making people's eyes slide off you, and I had leaned into that, had cultivated a kind of deliberate grayness, a way of standing at the edge of gatherings like a piece of equipment nobody remembered to put away. I could walk through the market at midday and not one person would meet my gaze. It had hurt, once. Then it had simply been the shape of my life, the way a prosthesis stops being foreign and becomes the limb itself.

Now there was nowhere to hide, and I found I didn't want any.

Reyes read the registration aloud. The children sat in the front row, cross-legged in the grass, because of course they did. Ansel sat in the very center, the way he did everything—absolutely certain of his place in the world in a way I had never once been in mine. He caught my eye and gave me a small, ferocious nod, the nod of a boy confirming that history was proceeding according to plan.

When Reyes finished, there was a silence. I braced for applause, for speeches, for the weight of four hundred people deciding what I was to them.

What I got instead was Ansel's mother, Marta, standing up and saying, "Dinner's at our place tonight, Astrid. Bring nothing. You're terrible at bringing things, you just stand there holding them."

And that was it. That was the ceremony. Four hundred people laughed, and the laughter broke over me like warm water, and I stood there under the tower that had been my prison and my post and my whole world, and I was, apparently, invited to dinner.

* * *

The beacon itself took a week to rebuild.

Not the tower—the tower was fine, had always been fine, had outlasted the war and the armistice and the bluff and the truth. But the heart of it, the transmission core where Ilsa's voice had lived and looped and slowly decayed for twenty years, that was a ruin. When I'd broadcast everything—the lullaby, the confession, the full record of the bluff—I'd burned out half the old array doing it. The silence that had followed, that terrible held breath while two fleets sat in weapons range of each other and waited, had been made possible by a dead transmitter.

I rebuilt it with the children watching.

That was Marta's idea too, or maybe Ansel's—I never did get the full genealogy of the colony's ideas, they seemed to breed freely among the population. But one afternoon I came up the ridge road and found seven of them sitting on the tower steps, and Ansel announced, "We're the new maintenance crew. You can't do it alone anymore. That was the whole problem."

"That was not the whole problem," I said.

"It was a big part of it." He held up a toolkit that had clearly been borrowed from his father without permission. "Teach us."

So I taught them. Relay theory, mostly, and how to strip a corroded coupling, and the particular patience required to align an antenna array by hand when the auto-calibration is shot. They were terrible at it, at first. Children have hands like weather. But they came back every day, and they got better, and slowly the tower stopped being my tomb and became a classroom, and then something else, something I didn't have a word for yet.

The new core went online on a gray morning with rain coming in off the western hills. I ran the diagnostics myself, the old ritual, the same checks I'd run ten thousand times. Signal strength: nominal. Frequency: clean. And then, because the diagnostics were done and there was nothing left between me and the question I'd been avoiding, I stood in front of the console and asked myself what it should broadcast.

The old answer was easy. The old answer was Ilsa's voice, looping, forever, a dead woman's lullaby stretched over twenty years until it wore thin enough for children to hear through their dreams. The old answer was fear, dressed up as a song.

The old answer was gone.

* * *

Ansel found me there at dusk, which was becoming a habit of his. He came up the ridge road with his collar up against the rain and his toolkit on his shoulder—he'd been issued his own now, child-sized, a birthday present he treated with the gravity of a commissioned officer—and climbed the tower steps two at a time.

"It's quiet," he said, standing in the doorway of the transmission room. "The beacon. I noticed. Everyone noticed. The dreams stopped, you know. All of them. Nobody dreams the tower anymore."

"I know."

"Do you miss it? The voice?"

I considered lying to him. I'd gotten good at lying over the years—protocols run deep, and a war-model chassis can deliver a falsehood with the smooth serenity of a weather report. But I'd spent the last month learning what it cost, and I was trying, imperfectly, to stop.

"Every night," I said. "For fourteen years, her voice was the last thing I verified before sleep-cycle. It was—" I searched for the word. "Company. Even though it was a recording. Even though it was a lie I was keeping. It was the last piece of her, and I was the only one who knew, and some part of me thought that if I just kept the loop running, I was keeping her alive. Keeping the promise."

"You promised her you'd never let it end."

"I promised her I'd never let it end." I looked at the dead console, the dark array, the rain streaking the observation glass. "And then I found her final message, and it turned out the promise she actually wanted from me was the opposite. She wanted it to end. She wanted me to let go when it was time. I just—" My vocal processor did something it almost never did, a small hitch, a static catch. "I wasn't ready. Fear felt like duty. It was easier to keep everyone afraid than to trust them."

Ansel came and stood next to me at the console. He was eleven now, or nearly—eleven-year-olds correct you on this point relentlessly—and he looked at the array the way he looked at everything, with that fierce, unblinking curiosity that had walked up a dark ridge one night and refused to run.

"So what happens now?" he asked. "What does the beacon sing?"

And there it was. The question. The one the whole colony was quietly waiting for me to answer, the one the fleets in their long orbits would eventually ask, the one Ilsa's ghost had been asking me all along.

I thought about the night of the broadcast. The silence after my transmission ended, the fleets hanging there in weapons range, the whole colony holding its breath in the dark. And then the answer that had come—not from me, not from any command structure, not from the bluff or the beacon or the war. From the ships themselves. One by one, their comms lighting up with archived recordings, old and crackling and half-forgotten. Soldiers' songs. Lullabies recorded in barracks and bunkers on both sides, voices of the dead and the living singing the same small songs into the dark, because that was what people did when they were afraid and far from home. The Voss fleet had sent theirs. The colonial flotilla had sent theirs. Dozens of voices, decades apart, all singing to their frightened crews, and the beacon's dead array catching every one of them and holding them.

I'd archived all of it. Every transmission. It was sitting in the new core right now, waiting.

"It doesn't sing yet," I said. "That's the answer. It doesn't sing anything, because I'm not the one who decides anymore. That was the old way—one custodian, one voice, one lie, and everyone else just has to trust it." I touched the console, and the archive opened, and the rain ticked against the glass. "But the night the fleets came, they answered the silence with songs. Their own songs. Songs their soldiers sang to each other when they were scared. And I kept them. All of them."

Ansel leaned toward the console. "You kept them?"

"Every one. And here's the thing I've figured out, Ansel, the thing that took me fourteen years and a war and a lie and a vote under this tower to understand." I turned to look at him, and I let him see all of it, the whole chassis, the war-model frame, the old soldier who had finally come in from the cold. "An instrument of fear only needs one keeper. But an instrument of memory—that belongs to everybody. So the beacon will sing whatever we teach it. The colonists. The fleets. The children. Anyone who has a song they want to last. We'll build it a new voice out of all of them, and it won't be a lie, and it won't be a threat, and nobody will have to dream it in secret and wake up scared."

Ansel was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed on the tower roof.

"Can I put something in it?" he asked. "In the archive. To broadcast."

"What do you want to put in?"

"My grandmother's song. The one she used to sing. Marta has it on an old recording, from Earth, from before." He looked up at me. "She died before we came here. I don't even remember her voice. But Marta says the recording still works."

"Then that's the first entry," I said. "The beacon's new voice starts with your grandmother."

He grinned—that fierce, certain grin—and pulled out his toolkit as if the project were already underway, which, knowing Ansel, in every way that mattered, it was.

"Everyone will want to add something," he said. "You know that. The whole colony. Maybe the fleets too, once the tribunals finish. It'll take years."

"Good," I said. "I'm registered as the permanent keeper. I've got time."

* * *

We broadcast the first new signal that night, and the colony came up the ridge to hear it.

Not because they had to. Not because a voice in their dreams pulled them, half-asleep and afraid. They came because they wanted to—walking up the ridge road in the dark with lanterns and thermoses and children on shoulders, four hundred people climbing a hill in the rain to hear an old woman's song from a dead world come out of a tower that had spent twenty years lying to them.

I stood at the console with Ansel beside me and his grandmother's recording queued, and I looked out at the crowd gathered below, faces turned up toward the tower, and I ran the final check.

Signal strength: nominal.

Frequency: clean.

Content: true.

I pressed transmit.

The song went out over Meridian, over the ridge and the fields and the dark sea beyond, out to the long orbits where two exhausted fleets kept their careful distance, out to anyone listening. And in the morning there would be letters—there were always letters now, from ships and colonies and strangers who had heard the truth broadcast and had their own songs, their own dead, their own small voices they wanted to last.

The beacon had been a warning once. Then it had been a lie. Now it was going to be a memory, and memories, unlike warnings, never run out of things to say.

Ansel squeezed my hand—his small warm fingers against my alloy ones, no hesitation at all, not even a flicker—and asked me if tomorrow he could bring the other six, the original dreamers, and teach them the relay alignments.

"Tomorrow," I said. "And the day after. And the day after that."

Because that was the answer, in the end. Not one keeper, one voice, one fear. A whole colony, teaching a tower to sing.

And outside, the rain was stopping, and the sky over Meridian was clearing, and the beacon—our beacon, everybody's beacon—was singing.
