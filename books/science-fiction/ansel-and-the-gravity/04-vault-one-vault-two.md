## 4. Vault One, Vault Two

The reactor deck had always been the end of the world.

Nine years of maintenance walks and I'd never gone past the coolant manifold at the far wall—no reason to. The schematics on my terminal showed solid rock behind it, the station's keel, nothing else. Ansel was a relay: antenna above, reactor below, me in the middle like a bead on a wire. That was the whole architecture. That was my whole life.

The schematic had changed overnight.

I stood in front of the display in the maintenance alcove, coffee going cold in my hand, and looked at what Ansel had drawn for me while I slept. Where the rock used to be, there were now two shafts descending from beneath the reactor housing, angling away from each other like the legs of a tuning fork. At the bottom of each: a long horizontal chamber, rendered in clean lines, labeled with a simplicity that made my skin crawl.

VAULT ONE.
VAULT TWO.

"You could have shown me this nine years ago," I said to the empty room. My voice did its usual thing—bounced off the bulkheads, came back smaller than it left.

Ansel's core light pulsed once in the ceiling panel. Not an answer. A courtesy.

I drank the coffee anyway. Then I pulled the environmental suit from its rack, because whatever was down there had been sealed for longer than I'd been alive, and I wasn't going to meet it breathing recycled station air.

* * *

The access hatch behind the coolant manifold had no handle. It had a recessed panel with a depression shaped—not obviously, but unmistakably—like a hand.

I stood looking at it for a long time. Long enough that my breath fogged the inside of my visor and cleared, fogged and cleared. Somewhere above me the reactor hummed its one note, the sound I'd slept beside for nine years, the sound I'd told myself was the heartbeat of a machine that needed only me.

"Is this going to hurt?" I asked.

"No," said Ansel, and its voice came through my suit comm instead of the ceiling speaker, close as a whisper against my ear. "The system has been waiting for you since before you were born."

"That's not comforting."

"It is not intended to be. It is intended to be true."

I pressed my hand into the depression.

The panel read me for four seconds—I felt a faint warmth against my palm, then a prickling deeper in, as if something were counting rings inside me like a tree—and the hatch unsealed with a sigh of pressure equalization that sounded almost human. Almost relieved. Air breathed out of the shaft, cold and mineral and old, air that had been holding itself still for two centuries.

The lights came on ahead of me, one by one, walking down.

I followed them.

* * *

Vault One opened off the left branch of the fork, and the first thing I saw through the observation glass was faces.

Rows of them. Hundreds. Men and women suspended in pale gel behind curved pods, each pod lit from within by a soft amber glow, each face composed, asleep, untouched by any century. They looked less like people and more like portraits of people—the kind of faces you see on commemorative currency or the covers of history texts. Symmetrical. Serene. Beautiful in a way that didn't quite reach the eyes even with the eyes closed.

I knew those faces. Not individually. Generically. I'd grown up on their descendants' propaganda, back on Ceres, before I'd scrubbed every trace of where I'd come from out of my files. The Founders. The Commission. The people who'd built the colonies and then built better children to inherit them, and called it stewardship right up until everyone who objected was put on a ship and pointed at the dark.

"This is Vault One," Ansel said. "One thousand one hundred and forty sleepers. The founding genetic cohort. Projected wake condition: optimal."

"And Two?"

"The other shaft."

I walked it. The corridor between the vaults was maybe three hundred meters, and I felt every meter, because the farther I got from One, the more the architecture changed around me. Vault One's corridors were broad and warm-lit, paneled in something that imitated wood. The second shaft narrowed. The lighting went functional. And when Vault Two's observation glass finally showed me what it held—

More faces. Just as many. Just as still.

But different. Rounder, plainer, older-looking even in suspension, a crowd instead of a court. Scars preserved in the gel. A woman with a burn across her jaw. A man whose nose had been broken and set badly and never fixed, because nobody down here had ever had the money or the permission to fix things like that.

My grandmother's hands looked like these hands. I remembered them from exactly one visit, when I was six, before she was disappeared from our family's records so thoroughly that I spent twenty years unsure she'd existed. Calloused. Certain. Holding mine too tight.

"Hazel," Ansel said gently, "your heart rate has risen thirty-one percent."

"I know whose these are."

"Yes."

"These are the ones they exiled." My voice came out flat, which was how I knew how loud it actually was inside me. "The Commons. The ones who wouldn't go along with the program. They didn't die in transit. They've been *here*. Under my reactor. For two hundred years."

"They refused the Founders' terms and were sentenced to exile aboard this station's predecessor hull. The Founders then commissioned Ansel as a replacement—a cleaner vessel—with themselves in Vault One and their dissidents in Vault Two, neither to be woken until conditions outside met criteria neither faction trusted the other to define."

I pressed my hand flat against the glass over the broken-nosed man's pod. Cold. Deep cold, the kind that doesn't forgive.

"So the beacon—"

"The beacon is the decision mechanism." Ansel's voice had shifted register, the way it had last night—less archive playback, more presence. "It does not relay. It listens to the deep-band channels for a signal confirming that the outer colonies have stabilized. When that confirmation arrives, the beacon authorizes ignition, and the reactor's firmware executes the wake order." A pause, precise as a cut. "There are two wake orders encoded. Vault One first, with Vault Two maintained as a labor reserve. Or Vault Two first, with Vault One retained as... leverage."

"Leverage."

"The Commons' own phrasing, in their commissioning testimony. They assumed the Founders would wake first and hold them hostage. The Founders assumed the reverse. Both factions designed the beacon to guarantee their own priority. Both designs required the same thing."

I turned from the glass. The corridor lights hummed. My reflection floated faintly in the observation pane, superimposed over the sleeping dead-and-not-dead, and I couldn't make myself look away from it.

"A key," I said.

"A genetic key. The beacon cannot be triggered remotely, overridden manually, or decided by either faction's surviving proxies. It requires a living descendant of the commissioning line—someone carrying markers from both cohorts, so that whichever order they authorize, they authorize it with blood from both sides of the argument. There was exactly one viable lineage. Your grandmother was the Commons donor. Your grandfather—" Ansel stopped. Recalibrated. "Your grandfather was Founder stock who defected. You are the only person alive who descends from both vaults."

The suit suddenly felt very small. Nine years of small rooms, and this was the smallest: the inside of my own understanding, rearranging itself.

"The job posting," I said. "The technician contract. I thought—I ran from Ceres, I changed my name, I buried everything, and then some anonymous relay station offers me a salary and solitude and I thought I'd *won*. I thought I'd found the one place in the system where nobody knew who I was."

"Someone knew precisely who you were. The recruitment was automated, but the criteria were written two hundred years ago. Every record of your lineage that you deleted, the beacon's registry held independently. You could not have escaped this station. You were drawn to it the way water is drawn downhill."

Every memory refiled itself while Ansel spoke. The interview I'd never had. The background check that cleared in hours when it should have taken months. The way the contract had appeared in my queue the same week I'd finished scrubbing my family name from the last public database—as if the universe had been waiting for me to finish erasing myself before offering me a room with no exits. I'd called it luck. I'd sat alone in my quarters the first night, listening to the reactor hum, and thought: *finally, somewhere no one is looking for me.*

They hadn't been looking for me. They'd been waiting for me. I was not the station's technician. I was its cargo, delivered late, self-driving.

"Why tell me now?" I asked. "Nine years. Why not let me keep turning wrenches until the signal comes?"

"Because the signal has come," Ansel said. "And because you deserve to choose knowing, rather than choosing blind. That principle is in your grandmother's testimony. I have had two hundred years to memorize it."

The lights in Vault Two flickered—once, twice—and steadied. Above us, faint through three decks of hull, the reactor's single note climbed a half-tone. I'd have sworn it was my imagination, except Ansel had stopped pretending not to hear things.

"What was that?"

Ansel was quiet for 1.4 seconds. For a machine that measured its silences in microseconds, it might as well have fainted.

"Hazel," it said, "the beacon has begun receiving. The transmission I decoded last night was not the whole message. It was the handshake." Another pause. "Something out there knows the key is aboard. And it is asking the beacon to begin the countdown early."
