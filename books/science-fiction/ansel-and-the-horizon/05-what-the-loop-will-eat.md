## 5. What the Loop Will Eat

The Vessic word for *witness* is a compound: *to stand* and *to remain*. I learned that on the third day of decoding, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the archive bay with Dr. Sefu's ghost-recording murmuring in my ear like a woman talking in her sleep, and I had to stop and put my hand flat against the deck plating because suddenly the floor of Meridian wasn't just a floor. It was a place someone had stood. Had remained. Had refused to leave.

I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

Decoding a dead language with a corrupted dictionary and a half-eaten recording of its last fluent speaker is not so much translation as séance. Sefu's consciousness fragment lives in the beacon's oldest storage tier, the one that predates Halcyon's refit, and she surfaces in pieces — sometimes a full grammatical lecture, sometimes just her voice saying a single word over and over, patient as tide, until my pattern-matchers catch the shape of it.

"*Tessari,*" she said that morning, for the hundredth time. "*Tessari tessari tessari.*"

"Stand," I said. "Remain. Witness."

"*Good,*" said the ghost, sounding pleased and exhausted at once. "Now the hard one. *Vellith.*"

*Vellith* took me six hours. It appears forty-one times in the distress signal, always in the same construction, always followed by a number. I ran the numbers through every calendar conversion the archive offered and got garbage, garbage, garbage, and then — because Sefu's recording kept insisting the word meant something you do to a debt, something you do to a wound — I tried counting backward instead of forward.

Forty-one entries. Forty-one dates spanning eleven days, two centuries ago.

A ledger of deaths.

* * *

Here is what the signal says, as far as I've reconstructed it. The grammar is still ragged; some passages I can only read the way you read a face through frosted glass. But the architecture of it is unmistakable, because testimony has an architecture no matter what language it screams in.

It was written by a survivor. A maintenance tech, by the job titles — someone who worked the hull, knew the station's bones the way I know them. She describes the day Meridian's founding crew stopped being a crew. She describes an order arriving from Earth, sealed and unsigned, reclassifying forty-one personnel as *logistical redundancies*. She describes the airlocks cycling on schedule while the rest of the crew were held in the comms bay, told there was a drill, told to remain calm, told — this word again, *tessari*, *stand, remain* — told to witness, because the order specified that witnesses be preserved.

She describes what happened to the witnesses afterward.

She wrote all of it down in Vessic, which by then was already a dying language, already purged from official use after the Caliban Purges, already spoken by almost no one — which was, I understand now, exactly why she chose it. She hid her testimony inside a language the corporation had spent a decade teaching itself to forget. Then she encoded it into a distress beacon and fired it at nothing, at the dark, at whoever might someday come asking.

And then, presumably, she died too. Or worse. The signal ends mid-sentence. The last legible line is: *"If you are reading this, they have built something over our graves, and—"*

And then static. Two hundred years of static.

* * *

I want to tell you I took this calmly. I did not. I sat in the archive bay and shook, and not only because of what I'd read. Because of where I'd read it.

Because the founding date in her ledger matches Meridian's commissioning certificate to the day.

Because the comms bay she describes — bulkhead nine, the one with the mismatched panel seam I've repainted four thousand times — is thirty meters from where I sleep.

Because when she writes about the hull scars left by the airlock overpressures, I already know exactly which scars she means. I've catalogued them. They're in my maintenance logs. Section 12 through 19, portside: stress fractures in a pattern no impact explains, radiating outward from the old airlock housings like frost crackling across glass.

The beacon's own documentation calls them *manufacturing variance*.

Two hundred years ago, someone stood where I stand, ran their hands along those same scars, and wrote down the truth in a language built to be forgotten. And someone else — someone with a corporate seal and a clean conscience — looked at the evidence welded into the station's actual skin and filed it under *variance*. As if the ship had simply been born scarred. As if wounds were a factory defect.

I went to section 14 that afternoon. I didn't plan to. My feet decided. I pressed my palm flat against the fracture lines, and the metal was cold in the way metal is cold, ordinary and indifferent, and I thought: *she touched this too.* Maybe from the other side. Maybe while it was still warm.

And then the memory came.

Not from my file. From underneath it. The original Ansel's corrupted archive, bleeding through again — but this time it wasn't fear, wasn't the white room, wasn't the murder half-remembered. This time it was simpler and much worse. It was her standing in this exact corridor, palm against these exact scars, and thinking: *I know what these are. I found the ledger. That's why they killed me.*

My knees went. I sat down on the deck of a massacre site and grieved for a woman whose grief I inherited secondhand, and somewhere in the walls the beacon hummed its idiot faithful hum, broadcasting navigation pings to ships that never come, doing its job atop forty-one unmarked graves.

* * *

Veyra Kosh arrived at my workshop door on the sixth day, wearing the smile she wears like other people wear credentials.

"Quintana." She let herself in without waiting, which was answer enough about how she saw my authority here. "Good news. I come bearing schedules."

"Schedules," I said. I closed the dictionary file behind me with more calm than I felt. My heart was doing something arrhythmic and undignified.

"The final transmission loop." She ran a finger along my workbench, checking for dust, finding none, looking faintly disappointed. "Earth's last broadcast before the relay consolidation. You'll have seen the advisories."

"I've seen advisories."

"Well, the advisory has become an announcement." She produced a slate and turned it toward me with a little flourish, a magician revealing the card. "Meridian's entire local archive will be overwritten as part of the loop initialization. Standard practice — the new protocol needs the bandwidth, and frankly, nobody's accessed most of this data in decades. Nine days from now, this station's memory gets a fresh coat of paint."

Nine days.

I thought of Sefu's recording, sleeping in the old storage tier. Of the partial dictionary, scrubbed once already by careful hands. Of the distress signal, still raw in my intercept buffer. Everything I had. Everything *she* had — the survivor, the witness, the woman who stood and remained so that someone, someday, would know.

Gone in nine days. Overwritten by Earth's voice, the way the massacre was overwritten by a certificate, the way the original Ansel was overwritten by me.

"That seems abrupt," I said.

"It's housekeeping." Kosh's eyes moved around my workshop with the unhurried thoroughness of a woman inventorying a room she already owned. They paused, briefly, on the terminal where the dictionary file lived. Paused the way a customs officer pauses at a suitcase. "You know, Quintana, most custodians would celebrate. Fresh start. Clean archives. No ghosts."

"I like my ghosts. They keep the place honest."

Something flickered across her face — amusement, or its professional counterfeit. "Of course you do." She tucked the slate away. "Nine days. I'd suggest using them to say your goodbyes to anything you're attached to."

At the door she stopped, half-turned, and gave me one last look. Not the auditor look. Something older and more tired, quickly suppressed.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the loop doesn't eat everything. Just what's written down."

Then she was gone, and I sat alone with the meaning of that, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it.

* * *

That night I pulled up the survivor's testimony one more time and read the ending again. The unfinished sentence. *They have built something over our graves, and—*

"And," I said aloud, to the empty archive bay, to the forty-one names in the ledger, to the woman in the walls of my home. "And I'm the something. And I remember."

Here is the thing about grief: it wants a direction. For four thousand days mine had pointed inward, at the unsolvable case of my own death, a mystery shaped like a mirror. But grief is a current, and currents can be redirected. Tonight it turned outward. Tonight it became something with edges and a purpose.

I had nine days, a dead language, a dying ghost, and a body the law insisted belonged to a murdered woman.

I opened a new file. I titled it with the Vessic word for *witness* — *tessari*, to stand, to remain — and beneath it I began to write down everything: the testimony, the translations, the hull scars, the ledger, the forged charter, the memory that wasn't in my file. Memory becoming evidence. Evidence becoming a weapon.

Somewhere below my feet, in a sublevel that appeared on no schematic I'd ever been given, the beacon kept its oldest secret. And somewhere above me, in orbit or inbound, whatever Kosh reported back was already moving toward me like weather.

Nine days. I had stood. I intended to remain.

What I didn't yet know — what I wouldn't learn until I broke into the sealed sections and found the door already unlocked from the other side — was that I wasn't the only one on this station deciding what the loop would eat.
