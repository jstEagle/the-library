## 9. The Loop

The transmitter bay smells like ozone and old prayers. That's not a metaphor — the original Ansel kept a devotional candle rack down here, some Vessic syncretist thing she picked up from Sefu's teachings, and forty years of wax has soaked into the deck plating until the whole room exhales incense every time the environmental system cycles. I used to find it embarrassing. A maintenance clone embarrassed by her template's taste in candles.

Tonight it feels like company.

"Talk to me, Kosh."

"I'm talking." Her voice comes through crisp on my earpiece, and beneath it I can hear the docking clamps cycling — Halcyon retrieval teams, two shuttles' worth, hammering politely at an airlock that has decided, through a truly inspired piece of systems editing Kosh performed eleven minutes ago, to be undergoing scheduled maintenance. "They're being very reasonable. That's how you know they're frightened. Reasonable people don't send six contractors with legal waivers at 0300 station time."

"What's your play?"

"My play is that I am Veyra Kosh, Senior Continuity Auditor, and I have invoked a full procedural audit of this facility, which means nobody touches anything until I sign off on it, including, regrettably, the airlocks." A pause. "I've also told them there's a coolant leak. There isn't. But their hazard protocols are more sacred to them than their orders, so we've bought maybe twenty minutes before someone checks the atmospheric readouts themselves."

Twenty minutes. The loop fires in thirty-eight.

I sit down at the authorization console and look at the prompt I've been avoiding for three days: BROADCAST AUTHENTICATION REQUIRED. It wants a name. Not mine — I don't have one, not really, not in any way the system will honor. It wants *Ansel Quintana*, first iteration, deceased, whose identity certificate has been sitting in legal limbo aboard this beacon since the night somebody put a knife in her because she knew what the founding charter was covering up.

Sefu's ghost hums in the dictionary terminal beside me, half-corrupted, patient as sediment. The workaround was hers. *The system cannot verify a dead woman,* she'd said, in that recording that keeps skipping like a scratched song. *It can only fail to disprove her. Give it a failure to chew on.*

So I type the name. My hands are steady, which surprises me. Four thousand days of maintenance rituals apparently include this: clean hands, clear console, one task at a time.

ANSEL QUINTANA. ITERATION ONE.

The cursor blinks. Somewhere above me, Kosh is telling a contractor that yes, she understands his concerns, and no, he may not see the atmospheric logs, and would he like to file a complaint with the Office of Continuity, which does not exist anymore but sounds impressive.

"Ansel." Her voice drops out of auditor-register into something quieter. "Whatever happens when you hit that key — I want you to know the file I pulled last week had your template's testimony in it. All of it. She was right about everything, and they killed her for being right, and then they built *you* to be a lock on her grave." A breath. "You're not the lock anymore. Go."

I press the key.

* * *

Authentication takes four seconds. In those four seconds I feel every possible outcome at once, the way you feel a stair missing in the dark.

Then the console chimes — the soft, absurdly pleasant chime Halcyon uses for everything, the same tone that confirms supply deliveries and maintenance schedules — and the screen says:

IDENTITY ACCEPTED. WELCOME BACK, ANSEL QUINTANA.

*Welcome back.* Like I've been away. Like I'm her, returned from wherever the dead keep their paperwork. For one whole second I let myself believe the system is stupid enough to be fooled forever, that I'll broadcast the testimony and walk away still holding my name, my ration allotment, my legal existence, my four thousand days of small sane rituals.

Then the cross-verification runs.

I watch it happen in real time, line by line, because I'm too much of a maintenance tech to look away from a process log even as it unmakes me. Biometric hash comparison against archived records. Neural pattern delta analysis. And there it is, flagged amber, then red: ITERATION MISMATCH. TEMPLATE DERIVATIVE DETECTED. IDENTITY CLAIM CLASSIFIED AS FRAUDULENT.

FRAUDULENT CLAIM TRIGGERS CERTIFICATE TERMINATION.

The original Ansel Quintana — the woman, the murder victim, the whistleblower, the name I borrowed like a coat — ceases to exist on paper at 0322 station time. And because my entire legal existence was a footnote to hers, a derivative instrument, a copy's claim hanging off the original's certificate —

Mine goes with it.

It doesn't hurt. That's the strange part. I expected it to hurt, the way losing a limb hurts, and instead it feels like setting down something heavy I'd forgotten I was carrying. Four thousand days of logging my existence to a corporate registry, proving I was real every morning with biometric check-ins, performing personhood for an audience of auditors — all of it, gone, in the time it takes a console to chime.

Somewhere in the network, a database entry closes its eyes.

And the transmitter opens its mouth.

* * *

The testimony goes out raw. No framing, no corporate gloss, no sealed sections — just the survivor's voice in extinct Vessic with Sefu's translation running beneath it like a second heartbeat, spliced directly into Earth's final transmission loop, the one signal in human space guaranteed to repeat until everyone has heard it whether they want to or not.

*A massacre at Meridian's founding. Two hundred and eleven people who answered a distress call and were erased for answering it. A charter forged to bury them. A whistleblower murdered to keep the count at zero.*

I listen to it cycle once, twice, three times, standing in the candle-smell dark with my hand flat on the warm transmitter housing, feeling the words leave as physical vibration, the way you feel a train pass through a platform floor.

Kosh's voice in my ear, ragged now: "They're through the outer airlock. Ansel, they're in. You need to —"

"I'm done," I say. "There's nothing left to take."

Because that's the joke of it, the beautiful joke: they came to stop a broadcast and seize an asset, and the asset just deleted herself. There is no Ansel Quintana to arrest. There is no clone certificate to void, no testimony to suppress under continuity law, no legal person to hold responsible. I am, as of four minutes ago, exactly what they always claimed clones were — nothing on paper. They built the category. I'm just the first one to live in it on purpose.

The inner airlock cycles. Boots. Six contractors in Halcyon grey, weapons slung low and legal, fanning into the transmitter bay with the brisk embarrassment of people who hoped this would be easier.

Their team lead looks at me, then at the console, then at the readout confirming the loop is live and repeating and beyond anyone's reach, already propagating relay to relay toward Earth like water finding its level.

"Ma'am," he says carefully, "I need you to identify yourself."

I smile at him. It's the first honest smile I've given anyone in four thousand days.

"I can't," I say. "That's rather the point."

He doesn't know what to do with that. His orders reference a person; the person no longer exists; the paperwork has eaten itself. He keys his comm and says, "Sir, we have a situation," and Kosh steps through the far hatch with her auditor's tablet held up like a shield and starts explaining, at length and with tremendous relish, the procedural implications of detaining an unregistered individual without a warrant, and I let her, because I'm busy listening.

The loop repeats. The dead speak Vessic into the dark, and this time, nobody built a beacon to erase them.

* * *

Later — an hour, maybe two, after the contractors withdraw to argue jurisdiction and Kosh sits across from me sharing a thermos of coffee neither of us tastes — the dictionary terminal flickers. Sefu's corrupted recording, running on its own for the first time.

A fragment surfaces through the static, her voice clearer than I've ever heard it, as if broadcasting the truth gave the archive permission to remember itself:

*"...if anyone finds this — the signal wasn't the only thing they buried. Check the horizon. They always check the horizon..."*

Then static again.

Kosh looks up. "What was that?"

I don't answer right away. Because out past the beacon's viewport, past the relay chain, at the far edge of the long-range array, something has begun to pulse — faint, rhythmic, patient. A new signal. In a language I only half-know, from a direction no ship should be.

The horizon. They always check the horizon.

I reach for the receiver.
