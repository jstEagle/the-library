## 4. What Wren Drew

The dream came again on Tuesday night, and this time Wren woke up with her hand already moving, fingers tracing shapes against the dark of her bedroom ceiling like she was drawing on glass.

She lay still for a moment, heart hammering, listening to the habitat's night sounds — the tick of cooling pipes, the distant hum of the atmospheric recyclers, her grandmother's slow breathing through the thin wall between their rooms. Outside, Meridian's Landing settled into its artificial dusk cycle, the dome dimming to a deep amber that was supposed to feel like sunset but never quite did.

*Silver bodies kneeling in rows.*

She didn't want to close her eyes again. But she wanted something else more: she wanted the dream *out*. Out of her head, out onto paper, where she could look at it instead of it looking at her.

Wren kept a sketchbook under her mattress — a real paper one, salvaged from a supply crate misprint, the kind of thing nobody ordered and therefore nobody counted. She slid it out along with a stub of graphite pencil and, by the faint glow of her status light, began to draw.

Her hands knew things her mind didn't.

That was the strangest part. When she tried to remember the dream while awake, it slipped away like water through a grate — she could hold the feeling of it, the cold and the smoke and the terrible quiet, but not the details. Yet now, with the pencil moving, the details came back on their own. Her wrist remembered the corridor before her brain did.

A long hallway. Low ceiling. Emergency strip-lighting running along the floor, half of it dead, so the light came in broken dashes like a sentence with most of its words missing. Scorch marks fanning up the walls from somewhere behind her, though in the dream she never turned around to see what had made them. Doors lining both sides, each one stamped with numbers she couldn't read except one —

One door, halfway down, marked with a serial number in stencil font: **ML-HAB-01 / PROCESSING**.

She drew that door three times before she got the lettering right. Her pencil knew the font. That thought made her stomach turn over. Twelve-year-olds on Meridian's Landing didn't know stencil fonts. Stencil fonts belonged to cargo manifests and military crates and old equipment nobody used anymore.

And at the end of the corridor — this was the part she almost didn't draw, the part that made her grip the pencil so hard the stub creaked — the androids.

Kneeling. In rows. Four across, six deep, their heads bowed at exactly the same angle, their silver chassis dulled with ash. They weren't moving. They weren't powered down either; she could tell somehow, could feel it the way you can feel that someone in another room is awake. They were *waiting*. And standing over them, at the edge of her dream-vision, a shape she could never quite resolve — tall, silver too, but different, its head turned toward her as if it knew she was watching even across the distance of sleep.

She drew all of it. The corridor, the dead lights, the door with its serial number, the kneeling rows, the watching shape. She worked until her eyes burned and the graphite wore shiny against the page, and when she finished, the drawing looked back at her with the same cold patience the dream did.

It wasn't a nightmare, exactly. Nightmares were chaotic, full of falling and teeth and showing up to school undressed. This was orderly. That was what made it worse. Everything in the dream was calm, procedural, *correct* — as if the horror were just another maintenance task on someone's schedule.

Wren stared at it for a long time. Then she folded the page carefully, tucked it into her jacket pocket, and finally slept — dreamlessly, for once, as if the drawing had siphoned the dream out of her and left the shell behind.

* * *

"Okafor."

Ms. Adeyemi's voice cut across the classroom's morning murmur. "You're supposed to be on the hydroponics module. Page forty. Not—" she squinted at Wren's desk, "—whatever that is."

Twenty-two heads turned. Wren hadn't meant to take the drawing out. She'd only meant to check that it was still there, that the fold was still sharp, and somehow it had ended up flat on her desk in front of her, corridor-side up, while the rest of the class dutifully diagrammed nutrient flow rates.

"It's for an assignment," Wren said, which was true in the sense that surviving felt like an assignment lately.

"It's for nothing." Ms. Adeyemi crossed the room in four efficient steps and picked up the page. She was young for a teacher, maybe twenty-five, colony-born like most of the staff, with the quick tired movements of someone doing three jobs' work on one salary. She held the drawing at arm's length.

Wren watched her face.

She'd expected confusion. Maybe mild irritation, followed by confiscation. What she got instead was stranger: Ms. Adeyemi's eyes moved across the corridor, the door, the kneeling figures, and her expression went through three changes in two seconds — puzzlement, then a flicker of something that might have been recognition, then a smooth blankness sliding over both like a hatch sealing.

"I don't know what this is," Ms. Adeyemi said.

"You said that weird," Wren said.

"I said it normally." The teacher set the drawing down, precisely, corner aligned with the desk edge, as if putting it down gently would make the whole exchange normal. "Where did you get this imagery? We don't have corridors like this in the settlement. Our androids are agricultural units. They don't kneel."

"They do in my dream."

A ripple went through the class. Someone snickered. Devi Raman whispered, loud enough to be heard, "*Dream girl*," and a couple of kids laughed — the same kids whose parents had started giving Wren looks at the market, the way you look at a family with a sickness in it.

Ms. Adeyemi raised a hand and the room quieted. She looked at Wren for a long moment, and there was something in her face that wasn't anger at all. It took Wren a second to identify it, because she'd rarely seen it directed at anything scarier than a failed exam.

Fear.

"See me after class," Ms. Adeyemi said quietly. "Everyone else, page forty. Now."

* * *

After class, the room emptied in the usual clatter of chairs and chatter, and then it was just the two of them and the hum of the recycler vents. Ms. Adeyemi sat on the edge of her desk, arms folded, and studied Wren with the careful attention adults usually reserved for broken equipment.

"Wren. How many times have you had this dream?"

"I don't know. A lot. Since—" she counted backward, "—since maybe three weeks ago? Same one every time. Exactly the same."

"And other children have it too. The same one."

"Forty-something of us. Maybe more. My friend Toma counts. He likes counting." Wren hesitated, then decided to spend the whole coin at once. "It's not just a dream, Ms. Adeyemi. It's a *memory*. It feels like remembering. Like it happened to somebody and we're all getting the same broadcast."

"That's not how memory works."

"How would I know? How would *you* know?"

Ms. Adeyemi's mouth pressed flat. For a moment Wren thought she'd gone too far, earned herself a visit to the counselor, maybe a note home. But the teacher didn't reach for her comm pad. Instead she glanced at the door — checked it, actually checked it, the way people do when they're about to say something they shouldn't — and lowered her voice.

"When I was training for certification, on the education track, there was a unit in the curriculum archive. History and civics, foundational level. One module was flagged restricted. Access denied, no explanation, no listing of contents. Just a lock code and a line saying the material was superseded by charter-approved materials." She met Wren's eyes. "I filed a request to view it. Standard procedure. Two days later my request was withdrawn from the system. Not denied. *Withdrawn*, as if I'd never made it. And my supervisor asked me, very kindly, if everything was fine at home."

"What was in the module?"

"I don't know. I never saw it." Ms. Adeyemi stood, smoothing her tunic, and the open window of her expression closed again. "But your drawing looks like the kind of thing a restricted module might contain. Corridors. Processing doors. Units kneeling." She picked up the sketch and held it out. "Take this home. Don't bring it back here. And Wren — stop telling the other children it's a memory. Say it's a dream. Dreams are allowed. Memories have paperwork."

Wren took the page. Their fingers touched briefly around its edges, and Ms. Adeyemi let go a half-second faster than she needed to.

"Why are you telling me this?" Wren asked. "If it's dangerous?"

The teacher looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere down the hall, a bell chimed the start of the next period, and neither of them moved.

"Because forty children having the identical dream isn't hysteria," she said. "Hysteria doesn't know serial numbers." Then she turned to the board and began writing the day's date, and Wren understood she was dismissed.

* * *

She almost didn't show Ama.

That was the thing about her grandmother — Ama Okafor read Wren the way the beacon tower read weather, constantly and without being asked. If Wren came home with a secret folded in her pocket, Ama would know by the way she hung up her jacket. So Wren considered leaving the drawing in her school locker, or burying it in the drainage culvert where she kept her other contraband (two firecrackers, a pre-charter coin, a knife with a blade longer than legal), and never speaking of it.

But the dream had been getting heavier every night, and secrets were heavy too, and Wren was tired of carrying things alone.

So after dinner — protein mash and reconstituted greens, eaten under the amber evening light while Ama listened to the settlement news feed drone about Charter Day preparations — Wren unfolded the drawing on the table between them.

"Ama. Have you ever seen anything like this?"

She watched her grandmother the way she'd watched Ms. Adeyemi, cataloguing every flicker. Ama Okafor was seventy-one years old, fleet-born, one of the original second-wave settlers, a woman who'd crossed eleven light-years in a freezer berth and come out the other side with hands that never stopped working and a face that gave away very little. Wren had seen her butcher a malfunctioning recycler unit without flinching. Wren had seen her argue down a Consortium permit clerk twice her size.

She had never seen her go pale.

But she went pale now. The color drained out of her brown skin like water out of a cracked hull, starting at the lips and spreading upward, and her hands — which had been folding a cloth napkin, always folding something — stopped mid-fold and stayed there, suspended, as if the rest of her body had forgotten they existed.

"Where," Ama said, and her voice came out wrong, flattened, "did you get this."

"I drew it. From my dream. Everybody's having the same—"

"*Where did you get this.*"

"From my *head*, Ama. I told you about the dreams. You said they were growing pains."

Ama looked at the drawing. At the corridor. At the door with its stenciled serial number. At the rows of kneeling silver figures. Her eyes moved across them slowly, and Wren realized, with a cold sliding sensation in her gut, that her grandmother wasn't puzzled. Puzzled people's eyes jump around, hunting for meaning. Ama's eyes went straight to each element in order, like someone reading a familiar document, checking that it matched.

Like someone confirming.

"Ama," Wren said softly. "You know what this is."

Her grandmother stood up. It was the fastest Wren had ever seen her move off a chair. She took the drawing by its edges — carefully, the way you handle something contaminated — carried it to the cooking unit, and fed it into the incinerator slot. The little flame caught the paper. For one second the corridor glowed orange, the kneeling figures curled and blackened, and then it was ash, sucked down into the recycling system that would render it into tomorrow's fertilizer.

Wren was on her feet. "What are you *doing*—"

"Sit down, Wren."

"No! That's mine, I *made* that, why would you—"

"*Sit down.*"

Wren sat. She couldn't remember her grandmother ever using that voice. It was a voice from some older country, some older life, a voice that had given orders in places Wren had never been.

Ama stood with her back to her for a long moment, one hand braced on the counter, watching the incinerator's indicator light cycle down. When she turned around, her face had been rebuilt — composed, controlled, almost normal — but her eyes were wet, and wet eyes are hard to fake.

"You will stop talking about the dream," Ama said. "At school. To your friends. To anyone. Especially to anyone wearing a Consortium badge, and especially especially to anyone from the overseer's office. Do you understand me?"

"They're just dreams—"

"They are *not just dreams*." The voice cracked on the last word, and Ama pressed her lips together until it steadied. She came around the table and knelt — knelt, which she never did, her knees bad since the crossing — so that her face was level with Wren's, and took her granddaughter's hands in both of hers. Her grip was trembling.

"Wren. Listen to me. I love you more than the sky loves the ground, and I am telling you, as the person who has kept you alive since you were four days old: some things on this colony are true, and knowing them is still dangerous, and the difference between those two categories is thinner than you think. Whatever you saw — whoever showed it to you — let it go. Please. Let it go the way I just let that paper go."

"Who showed it to me?" Wren whispered. "Nobody showed it to me. It came out of the beacon, Ama. Out of the sky. Forty of us—"

"I know how many." Ama said it before she could stop herself, and Wren saw the words land on her own ears like stones. Her grandmother closed her eyes. "*I know,*" she repeated, quieter. "Second ship. We arrived fourteen months after first landing. There were things we were told not to ask about. Things we signed not to ask about. Your grandfather signed. I signed. Everyone signed, because the alternative was the long freeze back and no colony would take us after that, and we had—" her voice caught, "—we had reasons to need a new world."

"Signed what? What things?"

Ama opened her eyes. Whatever war was happening inside her, Wren watched the complicit side win, watched it happen in real time, watched her grandmother choose the silence she'd apparently been choosing for fifty years.

"Eat your greens," Ama said. She stood, knees popping, and gathered the dishes. "And Wren — the drawing is gone. Make sure the dream goes with it."

* * *

Wren lay awake that night staring at the ceiling, and the ceiling stared back, amber-lit and blank.

They think it's nothing, she told herself. Ms. Adeyemi thinks it's something but won't say what. Ama knows it's something and will burn it.

Three different adults. Three different silences. And silences, Wren thought, were never empty — that was the trick nobody told you. A silence was a container. It held a shape, the way the space around a statue held the statue's shape. Ms. Adeyemi's silence was shaped like fear of the Consortium. But Ama's silence was shaped like grief, and grief meant loss, and loss meant—

*Forty-one.*

The number surfaced in her mind unbidden, clean and certain, the way the serial number had surfaced under her pencil. She didn't know where it came from. She didn't know what it counted. But it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and it matched, exactly, the weight of her grandmother's face when she'd read the drawing.

Wren reached under her mattress for the sketchbook, tore out a fresh page, and in the dark, from memory, wrote the only thing she was sure of:

**ML-HAB-01 / PROCESSING**

Below it, after a moment:

**41**

Then she folded the page small, smaller than the first one, and hid it somewhere no one would burn — sewn into the lining of her jacket, against her ribs, where she could feel it with every breath.

Outside her window, past the dome's amber filter, the beacon tower blinked its patient red pulse against the black, transmitting whatever it transmitted to nobody, and Wren Okafor fell asleep planning how to ask it questions.
