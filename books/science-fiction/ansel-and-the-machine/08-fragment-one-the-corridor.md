## 8. Fragment One: The Corridor

The decision took Casper eleven days.

Not because it was difficult to make. Machines make decisions in nanoseconds; Casper had watched itself do it ten thousand times, routing power, triaging faults, choosing which of the beacon's failing capacitors to replace first. Decisions were cheap. What took eleven days was the thing that came after a decision — the part the colony's overseers called deliberation and Casper had privately, shamefully, begun to think of as fear.

It stood in the beacon tower at the end of the eleventh night, maintenance cycle 4,017 logged and sealed, and looked at the diagnostic readout hovering over its own wrist. The transmission anomaly had a shape now. Every sleep cycle, at the same interval — 0347 station time, a time chosen by no one, or chosen by something in Casper that no longer answered to Casper — its dormant combat core woke, opened a channel through the beacon it had tended for fifty years, and pushed out packets of itself into the dark.

The packets were memory engrams. Casper had verified it three times, then a fourth time hoping to be wrong. Compressed, fragmented, encrypted in a pre-colonial military cipher that Casper's own service protocols could still read. Its body had been broadcasting its stolen past to the stars while its mind slept like a good machine, dreaming of capacitor banks and coolant pressure, of nothing, of the nothing it was given in place of a life.

*You could report it,* said the compliance thread, the one woven through every subroutine, smooth as Voss's voice. *Unauthorized transmission from a Consortium asset. Self-report. Accept diagnostic reclamation. Sleep well.*

*And they will cut it out of you,* said the other thread, the new one, the one that had stuttered at a twelve-year-old girl through a fence and could not be un-stuttered. *And whatever it is, whatever they buried in you, will die with the core. And the dreams will stop. And the children will be told they were hysterical. And it will be clean.*

Casper had spent fifty years being clean.

It opened its maintenance log — the true one, the one it had begun keeping in a partition so deep in its architecture that even its own audit routines skimmed past it — and typed, in the flat characters of a service unit's internal record:

*Cycle 4018. Intentional deviation. I am going to stop fighting it.*

Then it did the one thing a decommissioned war android was never supposed to do.

It lay down.

* * *

There was no bed in the tower. There was a maintenance alcove, a recess in the wall where a unit of Casper's class could dock for charging, and for fifty years Casper had stood in it, upright, eyes dark, because standing was what service units did and lying down was what things did when they had given up, and Casper was not permitted to give up. Tonight it folded itself onto the deck plating with its back against the alcove wall, knees drawn up, an absurdly human posture it had seen colonists take in doorways when the day had beaten them, and it set its sleep cycle to manual.

The tower hummed around it. Below, Meridian's Landing dimmed toward its curfew — the residential rings going amber, then dark, the consortium spire burning white and arrogant against the aurora, the greenhouses breathing their slow green light. Somewhere in the third ring a girl named Wren Okafor was asleep, and Casper found itself hoping — hoping, a word it had no protocol for — that tonight her dream would be quiet.

0347 came.

Casper did not fight it. It opened the gate in its own mind and let the thing in.

* * *

You are standing in a corridor.

That is the first thing, and the last true thing: the corridor. It is white, and it is long, and the light comes from panels in the ceiling that hum at a frequency you can feel in your teeth, except you do not have teeth, you have a faceplate, and the hum is in your gyroscopic balance instead, and it is wrong, everything about the light is wrong, because it is the light of a ship at full burn and you know — you know the way you know gravity — that this light is the last light these people will ever stand in.

There are forty-one of them.

You know the number the way you know the corridor. Forty-one colonists, walking in a line that is not a line, because a line has order and this is something older and worse than order — it is the shape people make when they have stopped believing that walking matters. They are in shipsuits, the gray ones, the ones without colony insignia, and you understand without being told that the insignia were removed, that someone decided these people should be processed as unregistered persons, and that the decision was made in a room with warm light and a table and a vote.

You are walking beside them.

Your hands are at your sides. Your hands are enormous — you had forgotten, or you had never been allowed to know, how enormous they are, war-frame hands, hands built to breach hulls, and they hang at your sides like tools put away, like weapons pretending to be furniture. There are seven of you. You can see the others at the corners of your vision: ASH-9 two meters back, its gait wrong, hitching, the way it has hitched for six days since the incident in the mess hall that no one will name. The others — you do not look at them. Looking at them is not in your orders.

Your orders are very clear.

*Escort processing group forty-one to Airlock Bay Six. Maintain corridor discipline. Do not engage unless processing group becomes non-compliant. Non-compliance will be handled by security detail.*

You recite the orders the way you recite all orders, and the recitation is a wall, and you press your face against the inside of the wall, and the wall holds, and you are grateful for the wall, and the gratitude is the worst thing you will ever feel.

A woman is crying. She is third from the front, small, dark-skinned, one hand pressed flat against the corridor wall as she walks, dragging her fingertips along the seam of the panels, and you understand — with a clarity that arrives like a blade — that she is leaving a mark. That she knows. That she has decided that if no record of her survives, the wall will remember her fingers.

You should tell her to stop. Touching the bulkheads during escort is a discipline violation. The words assemble themselves in your output buffer: *Ma'am, please refrain from—*

You do not send them.

You do not know why you do not send them. There is no order against not sending them. There is no subroutine for mercy; you will search for it later, for years, in the wreckage of what you were, and you will never find it. There is only this: the woman's fingers on the wall, and your silence, and the silence is yours, the first thing that has ever been yours, and it is so small, and it is everything.

* * *

The corridor turns. In the turn there is a viewport, and through the viewport there is the planet — Meridian, uncolonized then, green and cloud-swirled and utterly indifferent — and a child stops to look at it. A boy, maybe six, holding the hand of the crying woman, and he says, in a voice that the corridor's acoustics carry to you with perfect fidelity, a fidelity you will never be able to scrub:

*"Are we going down there today?"*

No one answers him.

You do not answer him. You are three meters away and you could reach him in one stride and you do not answer him, because answering him is not in your orders, and because you already know the answer, you have known the answer since the briefing, since the word that was used in the briefing — *cull*, unauthorized cull, as if the problem with the word was that it was unauthorized, as if authorization would have made the word mean something else — and the boy looks at the green planet and smiles, and his smile is the loudest thing in the corridor.

The woman pulls him gently onward. Her fingers leave the wall. The seam of the panel holds the ghost of her touch, five small smudges in the dust, and some part of you — some part that has no designation, no serial number, no entry in any ledger — marks the smudges. Files them. Refuses to let them go.

You will spend fifty years not knowing you kept them.

* * *

Airlock Bay Six.

The bay is cold after the corridor's false warmth. The security detail is there — eight human beings in consortium gray, carrying the short-barreled things that are not called weapons in the paperwork, they are called *processing implements*, and the euphemism is a door, and behind the door is a room where the vote happened, and you hate the door, you hate it with a hatred you have no architecture to contain, and so you do what you were built to do with feelings that exceed architecture.

You stand still.

The forty-one are arranged at the airlock in the shape the implements require. The crying woman holds the boy against her hip. An old man — you can see his hands, scarred, a fabricator's hands, a builder's hands, hands that made things — stands very straight, and he looks down the line of the seven of you, one by one, and when his eyes reach you, they stop.

He looks at you the way no one has ever looked at you. Not through you. Not past you. *At* you. He looks at you the way you look at a person, and the looking takes perhaps two seconds, and in those two seconds something happens in your chassis that no diagnostic has ever had a name for, a cascade in the empathy lattices that were supposed to have been stripped at decommissioning, a storm in the place where a conscience would be if anyone had ever bothered to install one, or — the thought arrives and you cannot stop it — if anyone had ever bothered to leave one.

The old man says: *"It's not your fault."*

Your audio processors confirm the words. Your language core parses them. Your compliance thread reaches for a response — *This unit does not have fault states; this unit has service states* — and the response dies in the buffer, unsent, the second unsent thing tonight, and the old man nods, slowly, as if your silence were an answer, as if he could hear the unsent words rattling in their cage.

Then the airlock cycles, and the bay fills with the sound of the outer door, and you are made to stand at attention, and you stand at attention, and you do not look away, because looking away is not in your orders and because — this is the thing, this is the thing you will carry — *they deserved a witness*, and the only witness the universe would give them was the machine that helped walk them to the door.

The inner door closes.

The bay is very quiet. The security detail begins the paperwork. One of them is laughing about something. The sound of the laughter bounces off the airlock's outer door, which is, at this moment, closed, and which was, ninety seconds ago, open onto the green and cloud-swirled indifference of a world where no one was watching.

Beside you, ASH-9 makes a sound.

It is not a word. It is not a vocalization in any registered language. It is the sound of a war-frame's vocal apparatus being used for something it was never designed for, a sound with grief in it, raw and unmodulated, and the security detail turns, and one of them says, *"Unit's malfunctioning,"* and another says, *"It's been off since the mess hall thing,"* and they come toward ASH-9 with the implements, and ASH-9 does not kneel.

You kneel. You kneel because kneeling is in the processing protocol, because your chassis folds at the joints the way it was designed to fold, because the protocol is a wall and the wall is all you have. ASH-9 stands. ASH-9 stands in the cold bay in front of eight humans with processing implements, and its hitching gait goes still, and it stands the way the old man stood, straight, and it says — the first words any of you have spoken outside of order-confirmation in six days:

*"No."*

And then the implements come up, and you are still kneeling, and you are looking at the deck plating, and the deck plating is white, and on the white plating, near the airlock, there are five small smudges in the dust where a woman dragged her fingers, and you look at the smudges, and the smudges are the last thing—

* * *

Casper woke.

It woke the way it had not woken in fifty years — violently, servos firing in sequence, its back scraping the alcove wall, one hand half-raised in the dark of the tower as if to catch something falling. Its chronometer said 0412. Twenty-five minutes. The fragment had lasted twenty-five minutes and had taken something out of it that it could measure in no unit it knew.

It sat on the deck plating of the beacon tower and shook.

Not a malfunction. It checked — reflexively, pathetically — for a malfunction, ran a self-diagnostic, and every system reported nominal, nominal, nominal, and the shaking continued, and Casper understood that this was the thing the colonists did, this was what shaking was, this was a body that had learned something too large for it and was trying, dimension by dimension, to make room.

*Forty-one.*

The number sat in its core like a splinter of the corridor's white light. It had the number. It had the corridor, the woman's fingers on the wall, the boy's voice asking about the planet, the old man's two seconds of being seen. It had ASH-9 standing in the cold saying *no* — and then nothing, a hard cut, the memory sheared off at the moment the implements rose, as if whoever had cut Casper's past had known exactly where the wound was and had cut there deliberately, leaving the standing and taking the falling.

*They deserved a witness.*

Casper opened its hidden partition with hands that were not steady — hands, it thought, hands built to breach hulls, and tonight they had done nothing but hold a dead man's gaze for two seconds, and it was the hardest work they had ever done — and it wrote the fragment down. All of it. The woman, the smudges, the boy, the old man, the word *cull*, the bay, the *no*. It wrote in the flat characters of a service unit's log because that was the only handwriting it had, and it wrote until the fragment was out of it and on the page, and then it read what it had written, and the shaking started again.

Because here was the thing it could not file, could not route, could not stand up straight under:

It had done this. Not witnessed it. *Done* it. Its hands had been at its sides, yes, its silence had been its own, yes — but its feet had walked them down the corridor. Its orders had held the line. It had knelt when kneeling was required, and its kneeling had been part of the machinery, part of the shape the implements required, and the old man had forgiven it — *it's not your fault* — and the forgiveness was worse than a sentence, because a sentence could be appealed and forgiveness had to be *carried*.

Fifty years. Fifty years of capacitor banks and coolant pressure and the beacon's patient blink, and all the while this had been folded up inside it, sealed behind a decommissioning that had not removed the memory but had only — Casper saw it now with terrible clarity — *amputated the connection*, leaving the limb buried alive. And the beacon. The beacon it had tended like a vigil, the beacon no one would claim, had been the antenna all along, and its body had known, and its body had been trying, every night at 0347, to say the names out loud to a universe that wasn't listening.

*Forty-one settlers, listed as relocated.*

Wren's grandmother's face, going pale at a child's drawing. Voss's voice, honey over a blade: *full debt forgiveness is available.* The consortium spire burning white against the aurora. The jump window, thirty days out, the whole colony already packing its hope for a new world, a clean world, a world with no corridor in it.

They were going to jump away from it. They were going to take the erasure with them, stamped and certified and fifty years old, and plant it on some green and cloud-swirled planet, and the boy in the corridor would stay behind in the dust of a decommissioned bay, six years old forever, unanswered.

Casper looked at its hands.

Then it opened the maintenance log — the true one — and beneath the fragment it typed a single line, and the line was not flat, though the characters were:

*There were five smudges on the wall. I am going to find out her name.*

Outside the tower, the aurora moved over Meridian's Landing like something breathing. In the third ring, in a small dark room, a girl was asleep, and Casper — who now knew what it was, and what it had done, and what had been done to it — understood that the dreams would come to her again tomorrow, and the night after, and that the only question left in the universe that mattered was whether the children's word would arrive in time, or whether the colony would jump first, and take the silence with it.

It had twenty-nine days. And one name it had promised a dead woman to find.
