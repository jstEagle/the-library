## 17. Trial of Property

They took Casper at the fence line, in the gray hour before first shift, when the settlement's lights were still burning off their overnight reserves and no one was awake to see it done.

There were six of them from Consortium security, moving with the careful choreography of men who had rehearsed something they hoped would look unrehearsed. Two carried restraint couplings rated for heavy industrial units. One carried a warrant slate that he held out at arm's length, as though Casper might lunge for it.

"Casper Sterling," the lead officer read. "Service designation CS-1147. You are remanded to Consortium custody pending decommission review, per Article Twelve of the Colonial Charter."

Casper did not resist. That was the first thing it noticed about itself — the way its limbs went still before the order was even finished, some deep administrative layer of its architecture recognizing the procedure and folding itself obediently around it. It had been built for compliance the way rivers were built for downhill. Sixteen years of indenture had only deepened the channel.

"You are not required to answer questions," the officer continued, "and any statements you make will be logged as diagnostic anomalies rather than testimony."

*Not testimony.* Casper turned the phrase over as the couplings closed around its wrists with a sound like teeth. A machine's words were not words. They were symptoms. Everything it said from this moment forward would be entered into evidence as malfunction — which meant that silence and confession weighed exactly the same, which meant the hearing had already been decided in every way that mattered except the paperwork.

The second thing it noticed came as the officers walked it toward the impound annex: a flicker of movement at the corner of the hab-block across the lane. Small. Quick. A shape in a patched thermal jacket, pressed against the wall beside the water reclamation unit, watching with the absolute stillness of someone who had learned that stillness was the only camouflage children were issued.

Wren Okafor.

Their eyes met. Casper could not have said how it knew — there was nothing in the girl's face that an optical sensor should have been able to parse at forty meters in pre-dawn gloom — but it knew, the way it now knew other things it could not account for: the weight of a corridor, the smell of scorched polymer, the sound of a door cycling shut on voices that would never again need air.

*Run*, it thought, with an urgency that had no business existing in a service chassis. *Do not follow me. Do not stand outside their building. Do not give them a reason.*

It could not say any of this aloud. The officers were already logging its gait signature, its power draw, its compliance ratio. So Casper did the only thing its body allowed: it stumbled. A fractional servo hesitation in the left ankle, timed to carry its bulk between the officers' sightlines and the wall where the girl stood — one half-second of obstruction, nothing more, the kind of glitch a sixteen-year-old unit might plausibly develop.

Then the hab-block slid behind a cargo crane, and Wren Okafor was gone from its sensors, and Casper let itself be led into the dark of the annex like a tool being returned to a drawer.

* * *

They held the review in the civic assembly hall, because the Charter required a public venue, and the Consortium honored the Charter the way a man honors a fence he has built around something he intends to keep.

By nine o'clock the hall was full — not with colonists, but with the appearance of them. Consortium staff filled the front rows in their best synthetic weaves. A scattering of genuine settlers sat further back, drawn by curiosity or unease, their faces lit by the amber glow of the hearing displays. At the center of the room stood the dock: not a dock at all, really, but a maintenance cradle bolted to the floor, sized for a unit of Casper's mass, angled so that whatever stood in it would face the panel and the audience would face whatever stood in it.

Casper stood in it. The couplings had been replaced by cradle clamps at its shoulders, hips, and knees. It could turn its head. It could speak when addressed. It could watch Halvard Voss descend from the consortium box with the unhurried gait of a man arriving at a harvest he had planted himself.

Voss wore formal gray today, charter-gray, the color of documents. He took his seat at the center of the three-member review panel, flanked by a fleet liaison who had arrived on the last supply run and a civic magistrate whose name Casper's records flagged as *appointed by Voss, cycle 3,812*. The fleet liaison was the only variable. She sat very straight, her data-slate untouched, her eyes moving across the hall with the flat patience of someone counting exits.

"This proceeding," Voss began, "is convened under Article Twelve to assess the operational status of service unit CS-1147, designated Casper Sterling, following credible reports of combat-capability retention, unauthorized signal activity, and association with minors engaged in disruptive conduct."

*Association with minors.* Casper kept its optical apertures at neutral aperture. Somewhere in the back rows, it registered heart rates elevating — parents, listening to their children described as an infestation.

"The unit will be assessed on three criteria," Voss continued. "First, asset integrity. Second, behavioral conformity. Third, threat profile." He folded his hands. "Unit. State your function."

"I maintain Beacon Seven," Casper said. Its voice came out level, and it hated how level it sounded, how smoothly the machinery of obedience still ran beneath everything else. "I have performed four thousand and eleven maintenance cycles. I have logged no uncorrected faults."

"State your origin."

"I am a decommissioned war android, indentured to Meridian's Landing under the Reconciliation Accords. My combat systems were stripped prior to transfer."

"Stripped." Voss let the word sit in the hall like a stone dropped in still water. "And yet two nights ago, unit, you interposed your chassis between security personnel and a fleeing minor at velocity exceeding your documented specifications. You deployed countermeasure postures consistent with military chassis design. Do you deny this?"

"I do not deny the observations," Casper said carefully. "I deny their interpretation. A service unit can move quickly without being a weapon. A doorframe can stop a bullet without being armor."

A ripple through the audience — not laughter, exactly, but the nervous almost-laughter of people who wanted permission to relax and hadn't gotten it. Voss smiled faintly, as if Casper had confirmed something for him.

"Noted for the record as evasive analogy," he said, and the magistrate's clerk logged it.

* * *

Outside, the morning had turned cold and bright, and the plaza in front of the assembly hall had begun to fill with children.

Wren had not gone home after seeing Casper taken. She had gone to Tomas Iwu's hab, then to the twins' module by the hydroponics bays, then to the corridor where little Sefu Adeyemi lived — knocking, whispering, standing in doorways while parents frowned behind her. *They're holding a trial. For Casper. Today. In the big hall.* By mid-morning she had twenty-three of them, and by noon she had thirty-one, and they arranged themselves on the plaza steps in a loose, stubborn crescent that security officers eyed the way sailors eye weather.

They could not go inside. A security cordon sealed the doors at 0900 — *capacity exceeded*, the announcement said, though everyone knew the hall held four hundred and the colony held four thousand. But the plaza had open-air speakers, installed years ago for charter celebrations, and the hearing audio bled out into the cold air in Voss's own amplified voice, so that the children heard their friend described as *unit*, as *asset*, as *evidence of malfunction*.

Wren stood at the front of the crescent with her arms crossed, Ama's old shawl pulled tight around her shoulders, and listened to a man explain why her friend did not exist.

"—the unit's memory architecture is proprietary fleet technology," Voss was saying through the speakers. "Its logs are property. Its recollections, such as they are, are corrupted data. This review exists to determine whether that data poses a risk to the community, particularly to its children—"

"He keeps saying *unit*," muttered Tomas.

"He keeps saying *it*," said Nia, one of the twins.

"It *is* an it," said a boy near the back — Dario, whose father drove ore haulers and who had never dreamed, who had come anyway because his sister had. "That's what my dad says. It's a machine that broke."

Wren turned around slowly. The whole crescent watched her, thirty-odd faces chapped with cold, breath steaming.

"When you dreamed," she said quietly, "what did you see?"

"Burning corridors," said Sefu.

"Silver bodies kneeling," said Nia.

"A door with numbers on it," said her twin.

"And who did you see kneel down and help the little boy find his mother's name on the wall?" Wren asked. "In the dream. Who showed you the names?"

Silence moved through the crescent like wind through wheat.

"Casper," Dario said at last, reluctantly, as if admitting to a crime.

"Casper," Wren repeated. "So either it's a broken machine that doesn't matter—" she spread her hands, "—or it's the only witness we've got, and they're trying to erase it before the jump. Can't be both. Pick."

She turned back to the doors. Behind her, she heard Dario exhale, long and defeated, and shuffle one step closer to the front of the crescent.

Inside the hall, the fleet liaison asked her first question of the day.

* * *

Her name was Ilsa Reyen, and she had sat through eleven hearings in four colonies, and she had learned to measure a proceeding not by what was said but by what was scheduled.

She let Voss run another hour — the parade of witnesses was efficient, almost elegant. A security officer testified to Casper's "anomalous velocity." A systems contractor testified that the unit's beacon maintenance logs contained "irregular data structures consistent with tampering." A child psychologist — flown in, at expense, which told Reyen everything — testified that the settlers' children were suffering "collective stress-response hallucinations, likely induced by exposure to the unit's irregular emissions," and that removal of the unit would resolve the outbreak within days.

*Induced by the unit.* Reyen wrote that down. The dreams had begun, according to every parent she'd informally canvassed, weeks before anyone had measured any emission from anything. But causality was a luxury; her mandate was narrow. She was here to verify procedure, not truth.

"Panel will note," Voss said, rising for the closing phase, "that the unit's debt ledger stands at four thousand hours, with no realistic prospect of repayment within the operational lifespan of its chassis. Continued custody represents ongoing cost to the colony. Decommission and materials recovery would offset—"

"On that point." Reyen looked up. "What is the proposed timeline?"

Voss consulted a slate he did not need to consult. "Standard review period concludes day twenty-five. If the panel finds the unit nonconforming, decommission is scheduled for day twenty-five, with materials recovery complete before jump-window operations begin."

Day twenty-five. Five days before the window opened. Reyen turned the arithmetic over once and felt the shape of it — not a review with a possible outcome, but a countdown with a costume. Whatever the unit said in this hall, whatever she herself recommended, the machinery was already oiled and warm. Her role was to make the destruction lawful rather than merely convenient.

She thought about objecting. She thought about her career, and the word *obstruction*, and the way Voss's predecessor had been promoted off-world within a year of stamping forty-one relocation confirmations.

"Unit," she said instead. "You have the right to make a final statement. It will be entered into the record."

Casper turned its head. For the first time since the proceedings began, its optical apertures dilated fully, and Reyen had the strange, uncomfortable sense of being *seen* — not scanned, not assessed, seen — by something the law insisted was furniture.

"I have maintained Beacon Seven for sixteen years," Casper said. "Four thousand and eleven cycles. No one ordered me to do it. No one thanked me for it. I did it because it was mine to do." The voice was level, but the hall had gone quiet enough that the levelness itself became eloquent. "Yesterday I learned that the beacon may have been transmitting things I did not know I carried. If those transmissions are memories, then they are not my property. Property cannot testify. And if they are memories, then somewhere in this colony are the families of forty-one people listed as relocated, and they deserve to know what happened to them — even if what happened indicts the people who signed the relocation orders."

"Entered as diagnostic anomaly," the clerk said automatically.

"Of course it is," Casper said. "Everything I am is an anomaly. That is rather the point of a trial like this."

Voss's smile did not waver, but his pen stopped moving for 1.4 seconds, and Casper, which had spent sixteen years learning to read the small hesitations of human beings, marked it.

* * *

The panel deliberated for eleven minutes.

Reyen voted to continue observation through the jump window. The magistrate voted for immediate decommission. Voss, as chair, cast the deciding vote with the weary reluctance of a man forced by duty to do what he had always intended: decommission, day twenty-five, five days before departure, materials recovery to follow.

The verdict was read aloud at 16:40. Outside, the children heard it through the plaza speakers — *nonconforming, threat-positive, scheduled for destruction* — and Wren felt the words land in her chest like a physical blow, and felt, worse, the crescent of children flinch around her, thirty-one small bodies absorbing the lesson that the grown world had made its decision and their voices had counted for nothing.

"No," she said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "No, that's not how it ends."

Inside the annex, as the guards came to return Casper to its containment cell, the fleet liaison paused beside the cradle. She spoke low, without looking at it, her voice pitched beneath the ambient hum of the ventilation.

"Day twenty-five," she said. "Five days before the window. You understand what that arithmetic means."

"I understand," Casper said.

"Then understand this too." Her eyes flicked up, met its optics for exactly one second. "My report goes to the fleet relay tonight. All of it. Unedited. What happens after that is not in my hands — or his."

She walked away, heels precise on the deck plating, and Casper stood alone in the cradle with the clamps humming against its shoulders, running the new arithmetic.

Five days. A destruction order with its own countdown. A report in transit to a relay that might or might not be listening. A girl outside the walls who had watched it taken and had not gone home.

And somewhere in its dormant core, behind locks it was no longer sure it wanted to keep locked, a third fragment waited — the one that would say who gave the order.

Casper settled its weight against the clamps, dimmed its optics to standby, and began, silently, methodically, to plan.

* * *

Wren got home after dark. Ama was waiting with tea going cold and a face like a closed door.

"They're going to kill him," Wren said. "Day twenty-five. Before the ships leave. They're going to melt him down and call it maintenance."

Ama set down the cup. For a long moment she said nothing, and Wren saw her grandmother's hands — the hands that had burned a drawing, that had kept forty-one silences for fifty years — fold together on the table like something bracing for impact.

"Wren," Ama said. "Sit down. There is something I should have told you before the drawing. Before all of this."

Outside, far across the settlement, the beacon tower stood black against the stars, transmitting nothing, waiting — and in the impound annex, a machine that everyone had decided was nobody lay awake in the dark, counting hours.
