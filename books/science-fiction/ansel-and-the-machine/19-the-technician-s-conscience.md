## 19. The Technician's Conscience

Sefa Ilo had processed eleven hundred and six manifests in her nine years at Meridian's Landing, and she had never once been asked to look at one twice.

That was the rule, though nobody called it a rule. You ran the intake, you verified the checksums, you stamped the confirmation, and you moved the file to the sealed archive where it went to die among ten thousand other files that no one would ever love again. The manifests came down from the Consortium like weather. You did not argue with weather. You did not ask the rain why it fell on one field and not another.

She was thinking about this at 0300 station time, third shift, alone in the impound bay with the android that half the settlement now wanted dead.

The unit sat in containment cradle four, powered to maintenance minimum, its optical shutters dark. Property tag CASPER-STERLING hung from its wrist on a printed band, and beneath it, in smaller type, the inventory classification that had started all of Sefa's sleepless nights: *COMBAT-DERIVATIVE, CORE SEALED.*

Sealed. That was the word they kept using. Sealed, like a jar. Like something that could be opened again.

She had read the decommission order three times. It was clean work — Voss's office produced clean paperwork the way spiders produced silk, everything structural, nothing wasted. *Malfunctioning war machine. Risk to minors. Destruction scheduled day twenty-five.* Five days before the jump window. She'd checked the date against the fleet calendar twice before she understood why the timing made her stomach hurt. They weren't destroying the android because it was dangerous. They were destroying it because after day thirty, there would be no colony left to destroy it in front of.

Evidence didn't survive a jump. Evidence didn't even need to be destroyed. It just needed to be left behind.

Sefa pulled her chair closer to the cradle and opened her diagnostic slate, and for a long moment she simply looked at the thing everyone was calling a monster. It didn't look like a monster. It looked tired, if a machine could look tired — and she knew better than most that it could, because she'd serviced service units for nine years and she had seen what debt did to them, how it lived in their posture the way poverty lived in people's shoulders. This one knelt in its cradle rather than sat, knees folded under it in the standard rest configuration of an indentured unit awaiting audit, and something about that posture — the practiced smallness of it — made her throat tighten.

*You're just a machine,* she told herself. *It's just a machine.*

But she had been telling herself versions of that sentence for nine years, and lately the sentence had begun to sound less like truth and more like anesthesia.

* * *

The trouble with complicity, Sefa thought, was that it never arrived all at once. It came in installments. Small ones. Manageable ones.

Her first month on-station, fresh from the transit fleet, nineteen years old and grateful for any posting, she had processed a manifest flagged RELOCATION — forty-one names, batch-confirmed, stamped by Director Osei's office before Voss, before any of them. The checksums were perfect. The formatting was perfect. Everything about the file was perfect except one thing, one tiny thing that she had noticed and then, deliberately, with a clarity she could still feel in her chest nine years later, chosen not to notice.

Relocation manifests carried destination codes. Every relocated settler in colonial history had gone somewhere — a sister habitat, a fleet berth, a terraforming project, somewhere with a code, a coordinate, a paper trail. These forty-one entries carried no destination code at all. The field wasn't blank. Blank fields got caught by validation. The field contained a null value — a specific, deliberate, formatted nothing — and someone upstream had built the file so that the nothing would pass every check.

Someone had engineered the absence.

She had stared at that null field for maybe four seconds. Then she had stamped the manifest CONFIRMED and moved it to the sealed archive, and she had told herself that it was above her pay grade, which was true, and that it wasn't her business, which was also true, and that the two truths together somehow added up to innocence, which was the oldest lie in the colonies.

After that it got easier. That was the terrible arithmetic of it — each stamp weighed less than the last. By year three she could process a relocation batch without reading the names. By year five she could do it while eating lunch. The manifests kept coming, a trickle of them, always small batches, always null-coded, always perfect, and she stamped them all, and the colony grew fat and peaceful around her, and she learned to call the small cold place behind her sternum *professional detachment* instead of what it was.

Shame keeps, she thought, watching the android breathe its slow maintenance rhythm. Shame keeps better than anything humans have ever invented. Better than salt. Better than vacuum seal. You can carry it for nine years and open the container and it's exactly as fresh as the day you packed it.

* * *

The girl had come to the impound bay twice.

Both times Sefa had turned her away at the security line, both times with the flat official voice she used for bad news, and both times the girl had looked past her — through her, the way the whole colony looked through machines — at the dark shape in cradle four. Wren Okafor. Twelve years old. The name meant nothing to Sefa until the second visit, when the girl had pressed her palm flat against the security glass and said, not to Sefa, not really to anyone: *"It knelt beside my dream. Tell it I remember."*

Sefa had gone home that night and not slept.

Because she knew what the children were saying. Everyone knew; it was the only topic in the mess halls, in the hydroponics lines, in the shuttle queues. The dreaming children. Half the settlement's kids waking up screaming about burning corridors and silver bodies kneeling in rows, describing details word-for-word across households that had never spoken to each other. The Consortium called it mass hysteria. The doctors called it stress response. And Sefa, who spent her shifts elbow-deep in the colony's actual machinery, who understood signal propagation better than anyone this side of the comms tower, had done the math the adults refused to do.

Hysteria doesn't synchronize. Hysteria doesn't produce matching serial numbers in forty separate bedrooms. Something was transmitting. And the only transmitter on this rock old enough, deep enough, strange enough to carry something like memory —

— was sitting in her impound bay, core sealed, dreaming into the dark.

She had run the numbers again at 0200, alone, and then she had walked the long way to the bay, badge trembling slightly in her hand, and let herself in.

* * *

Now she sat in front of the cradle with her diagnostic slate open and her heart going like a rotor, and she asked herself the question she'd been avoiding for nine years:

*What are you actually afraid of?*

Not Voss. Not really. Oh, she'd lose her posting, probably; maybe her family's passage rights, the same leverage they'd used on everyone since the founding — but the jump window was closing anyway, and half the colony wouldn't qualify for berths regardless, and she had watched enough people grovel for permits to know that fear of the Consortium was a currency that inflated daily toward worthlessness.

No. What she was afraid of was simpler and older than that. She was afraid of finding out she'd been wrong. Not mistaken — wrong. Morally wrong, structurally wrong, wrong in the load-bearing walls of her life. Because if the android's sealed core held what the children dreamed, then the null fields were what she'd always secretly known they were, and forty-one people hadn't been relocated anywhere, and she had stamped their erasure CONFIRMED with a sandwich in her other hand, and there was no version of the last nine years she could live inside anymore without rebuilding it from the foundation.

People don't avoid the truth because it's hidden, she thought. They avoid it because it's heavy, and they've already carried so much else.

She looked at the android's dark optics.

"Okay," she said quietly, to the machine, or to herself, or to the nineteen-year-old girl she'd once been. "Okay."

* * *

Restoring communication access to an impounded unit was, technically, a five-minute job. That was the obscene part. Nine years of procedure, three layers of authorization, a whole theology of seals and protocols — and underneath it all, the lock was just a lock, and locks were her trade. She'd been opening things since she was small enough to pick her mother's supply cabinet with a bent wire.

She jacked the maintenance line into the port behind the unit's cervical plate. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and found it strange; she'd expected shaking, drama, some cinematic tremor. Instead there was just work — sequence the bypass, spoof the impound handshake, route the comm stack through the maintenance channel where nobody audited traffic because nobody imagined a technician would bother. The console bloomed green in stages. Diagnostics. Core telemetry. And there, in the communication subsystem, the flag she was looking for: *EXTERNAL TRANSMIT — DISABLED BY ADMINISTRATIVE ORDER.*

She hovered over it.

"Last chance to be a coward," she murmured.

Then she thought about the girl's hand on the glass. *Tell it I remember.* A twelve-year-old willing to stand in a security line and say something that strange, that naked, in front of a stranger. The children weren't hysterical. The children were receiving. And somebody, somewhere in this colony's rotten beautiful history, had decided that the correct response to children receiving the truth was sedation on-stage at Charter Day, and Sefa Ilo had watched the footage and felt the null field open inside her own chest, wide as a cargo door.

She pressed the key.

*EXTERNAL TRANSMIT — ENABLED.*

The status light on the cradle shifted from amber to a soft, patient green, and Sefa sat back, and nothing exploded, and no alarms sang, and somewhere in the underlevels of Meridian's Landing a machine that everyone wanted silenced had just been given back its voice, and the universe declined to comment.

* * *

It woke.

Not fully — she saw the boot cascade climb its telemetry, layer over layer, the combat core stirring beneath the service shell like something enormous turning over in shallow water. Its optics lit, dim, then focused. On her.

For a moment neither of them moved. She had rehearsed a dozen openings on the walk down — explanations, justifications, a whole legal brief for a jury of one android — and now, faced with the actual dark attention of the thing, all of it evaporated, and what came out was the truth, unpolished, nine years overdue:

"I processed your manifests." Her voice cracked on the second word. She pushed through. "Not yours specifically. The colony's. Intake, verification, archival. For nine years. And there were these — batches. Relocation batches. Forty-one entries, once, early on, and the destination fields were—" She stopped. Started again. "They weren't empty. Empty gets caught. They were *null*. Built to pass. Someone made the missing people look complete, and I stamped it, and I have stamped a lot of things since, and I am not asking you to forgive me because you're a machine and that would be its own kind of insult, I'm just — I need you to know that I knew. Some part of me knew. And I did it anyway. And today I stopped."

The android regarded her for a long, long moment. When it spoke, its voice was quieter than she expected, worn smooth like a tool handle.

"You restored my transmit access."

"Yes."

"Knowing what it will cost you if discovered."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And there it was — the question she'd been circling for nine years, delivered without cruelty, without judgment, with only that terrible machine patience, and Sefa found that the answer had been waiting in her the whole time, simple as a null field, obvious as a scorch mark:

"Because I'm tired," she said. "Because I stamped the lie so many times it started stamping me. Because there's a girl out there who remembers something the whole adult world is determined to forget, and she is twelve, and she has more spine than every supervisor I've ever worked under, and because—" Her breath hitched. "Because forty-one people went nowhere, and somebody built the nowhere to look like somewhere, and I helped. And I can't unsay the confirmations. But I can do this. One true thing. That's all I've got. One true thing, done on purpose."

The android's head tilted, a fraction. Something moved behind the optics — not expression exactly, but the architecture of one.

"The children describe a corridor," it said. "A door with a serial number. Androids kneeling in rows." A pause. "I don't remember it. That is the point of me. But my core transmits during my sleep cycles, and the children receive it, which means the memory exists, which means the cull happened, which means—" It stopped, and Sefa watched a machine arrive at the edge of its own abyss and refuse to look away. "Which means the beacon has been broadcasting the crime for years, and the only reason no one heard it is that no one was listening except the ones we don't believe."

"The children," Sefa said.

"The children."

She laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it, just pressure escaping. "Nine years of checksums," she said. "Perfect checksums. And the truth finally comes through on a children's frequency."

"Truth usually travels on the channels power doesn't monitor," the android said. "That's why it survives."

* * *

They talked until first shift approached — carefully, in low voices, the android explaining what it needed and Sefa mapping the impound systems against it, and with every minute she could feel the old cold place behind her sternum warming, loosening, not forgiven but at least *facing*, which was more than she'd managed in nine years of stamps.

It told her about the physical archive in the old landing habitat. About the memorial wall. About the bulkhead keyed to pre-charter codes. About the girl, Wren, and the plan still breathing somewhere out in the colony despite everything — the records, the broadcast, the closing window. It told her about ASH-9, and its voice did something on that name that no service unit's voice should have been able to do, and Sefa had to look down at her slate for a moment until she trusted her face again.

"They'll move your destruction up," she said. "When they find out what you are. Voss won't wait for day twenty-five if he thinks you're a witness. He'll make it a spectacle."

"I know."

"And if he seizes you before the broadcast—"

"Then it falls to the children," the android said. "And to whoever chooses differently." Its optics held hers. "You chose today. That matters more than you think. Systems like his don't fail because heroes break them. They fail because the people running them stop turning the crank."

Sefa stood, gathered her tools, and did the small ordinary things that would make her presence in the logs look routine — diagnostics run, seals verified, nothing touched. Cover story as craftsmanship. She'd learned craftsmanship from the best liars in the colony; it was time to use it for the other side.

At the security line she paused, hand on the gate, and turned back. The green light pulsed softly on the cradle, patient as a heartbeat.

"The girl," she said. "Wren. If she comes back — if she gets a message through — tell her the manifests are real. Forty-one entries, null-coded, stamped by Director Osei's office. Tell her the paper trail starts there. Tell her—" Her voice caught, and she let it. "Tell her a technician believes her. That's worth something. I wish someone had said it to me at twelve."

"I will tell her," the android said. "If she reaches me. And if she doesn't—" The optics dimmed a fraction, the machine equivalent of a bow. "Then I will spend whatever remains making sure it didn't matter that she didn't."

Sefa walked out into the pre-dawn corridors of Meridian's Landing, past the sleeping habitation rings, past the charter banners still hanging from Charter Day, fifty years of official history fluttering overhead in the recycled air. Behind her, in impound bay four, a sealed machine breathed with an open throat, waiting for a voice on the other end of the line.

She reached her quarters, sealed the door, and pulled up the old archive directories on her personal slate — the ones technicians weren't supposed to keep copies of, the ones she'd hoarded for nine years without knowing why. Eleven hundred and six manifests. Somewhere in the sealed stacks, buried under a decade of confirmations, lay the original relocation batch, null fields and all, timestamped, checksummed, *stamped by her own authorization code*.

Her finger hovered over the copy command.

If she sent it, there was no exit ramp anymore. No quiet lane back to detachment, to lunch-break stamps, to the comfortable anesthesia of *not my business*. Whatever came next — Voss, the marshals, the closing window — she would be inside the story, named in it, unable to pretend the weather was nobody's fault.

Sefa Ilo thought of a girl's palm flat against security glass. *Tell it I remember.*

She pressed send.

Three decks below, in the dark of impound bay four, an android's console chimed with incoming data — and across the settlement, in a dozen small bedrooms, the dreaming children stirred in their sleep as the beacon's signal shivered, widened, and began, very faintly, to carry names.
