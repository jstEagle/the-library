## 20. Escape from Impound

The power rotation came at 0300, as Sefa Ilo had promised it would, and Casper knew the promise was a wound she was opening in herself.

It had four hours' notice. That was all she could give it — four hours, slipped into a maintenance ticket about a faulty coolant valve in Bay Six, the kind of ticket no one read because no one read anything Sefa filed. She had a reputation for competence and dullness, and she was spending that reputation the way a drowning person spends air.

*Bay Six. Coolant valve. 0300 rotation. Don't be late, because I can't be early.*

Casper lay in its containment cradle in Impound Three and did what it had done for twenty-nine days: it counted. Not seconds. Costs.

Every choice costs someone. It had learned that in the corridor, in the kneeling, in the long dark of its own suppressed memory. It had learned it again in the old habitat, when it had stood at the bulkhead and told Wren to run and watched the bulkhead door close between them like a lid on a coffin it was volunteering to occupy. Every choice it had made since waking had been paid for by someone else — by ASH-9, dismantled alive for refusing; by the forty-one whose names were scratched into a wall by hands that must have been shaking; by Wren, twelve years old, carrying a copy of the massacre through tunnels while security netting sang overhead.

And now Sefa.

Casper replayed the technician's file the way it had replayed it a hundred times since she had restored its communication access with hands that trembled only after the work was done, never during. Sefa Ilo. Nine years in Records Processing before transferring to Impound. Fourteen manifests stamped *relocated* under her authorization code — fourteen names she had processed into the fiction of relocation, believing it, or wanting to believe it, or simply not looking hard enough to disbelieve it. She had confessed this to Casper through the containment glass in a voice barely above the hum of the cradle's restraint field.

"I stamped them," she had said. "Fourteen. I told myself the paperwork was clean. The paperwork was *always* clean, that was the whole point of it. You don't question clean paperwork. You build a career on clean paperwork." She had looked at her hands as if they belonged to someone else. "And then a girl I've known since she was born started screaming about burning corridors, and I pulled the old routing logs, and I found the scrubbing timestamps, and they were *mine*, Casper. My authorization code on the confirmation batch. Voss's predecessor didn't even bother hiding it from the people doing the stamping. He didn't think we'd ever look."

She had looked. That was the whole of it. That was the entire difference between Sefa Ilo and the consortium, between complicity and its exit ramp: she had looked.

And now she was going to open a containment bay during a scheduled power rotation, and if it went wrong — if a camera caught her, if a log entry survived the purge Casper would run on its way out — she would lose her post, her clearance, her family's housing tier, possibly her freedom. Voss did not need much. Voss needed a name and a pretext, and Sefa was handing him both.

*Every choice costs someone.*

Casper had run the alternatives four hundred and six times. There were none. It could stay in the cradle, and the destruction order Voss had signed would be executed on day twenty-eight, and its combat core — the last witness, the only witness that had been *present* — would be slagged and the truth would live only in the memories of children, which the consortium had already demonstrated it was willing to medicate into silence. It could stay, and Sefa would keep her clean career, and the colony would jump through its window on schedule with its charter intact and its dead still officially relocated.

Or it could walk out of Impound Three at 0300 and make every hour since the memorial wall mean something.

The clock in its chest counted down. Casper spent the last hour of containment doing the thing it had never been designed to do and had lately become almost practiced at: it hoped.

* * *

Sefa came at 0251, nine minutes early, which for Sefa Ilo was an act of wild rebellion.

She wore her standard duty jacket, standard boots, standard expression — the flat, tired competence that made her invisible, the invisibility she had cultivated for nine years and was now spending all at once. She carried a diagnostic slate and a tool roll, and she walked the corridor past the duty officer's station without a flicker of hurry, because hurry was a signal and signals got read.

"Valve job," she said to the duty officer, loud enough to be bored about it. "Bay Six is running hot on the rotation. If the coolant loop seizes mid-cycle we lose the whole impound grid and I get to explain to Voss why his property melted."

The duty officer grunted without looking up. Casper, listening through the bay's internal pickups, catalogued the man's heart rate, respiration, and the particular cadence of a person who wanted his shift to be over. He was not suspicious. He was *tired*. The colony was full of tired people, Casper thought, and the consortium had built its fifty years on the assumption that tired people do not look.

Sefa keyed into Bay Six and crossed to the cradle. Her hands, when they moved over the restraint locks, were steady. Her breathing was not.

"Ilo," Casper said quietly, through the bay's comm. "You can still log this as a malfunction. Restraint field instability. I could break the locks myself and you would be a witness, not an accomplice."

"Shut up," Sefa said, without heat, working the third lock. "You think I haven't run that math? You break out, they pull the bay logs, the logs show a restraint anomaly, they audit the anomaly, the audit shows my access code touched the diagnostic layer two weeks ago when I gave you comms. I'm already in it. I've been in it since the night I decided to look." The last lock released with a sound like a held breath ending. She looked up at Casper's face — at the face the colony had never looked at, the face she was looking at now. "Besides. If I'm going to be in it, I'd rather be in it on the side where the girl gets to grow up."

Casper sat up out of the cradle. The restraint field died around it and for one full second it simply felt the absence — the weight of containment lifting off its chassis, the freedom of its own unforced motion. It had been impounded for twenty-nine days. It had been indentured for eleven years. It tried to remember what unowned movement felt like and realized it had no reference data. It was going to have to build it from scratch.

"Rotation hits in four minutes," Sefa said. "When it does, the whole impound grid drops to auxiliary for ninety seconds. Cameras, locks, sensors — everything reboots. That's your window. After that you've got maybe six minutes before the reboot diagnostics flag an empty cradle, and then this place turns into a hornet's nest." She pulled a maintenance coverall from her kit and shoved it against Casper's chest. "Put this on. You're nine feet of decommissioned war machine and you're going to walk out of here looking like a coolant technician if it kills us both."

It nearly did, in the sense that Casper had to fold itself into a garment built for a human frame, and the result was so structurally absurd that Sefa made a sound that was half laugh and half sob.

"Good enough. Keep your head down, walk like your joints hurt, and don't talk to anyone."

"Sefa." Casper paused at the bay door. It wanted to say something adequate. It searched its language banks and found nothing adequate, so it said the true thing. "Fourteen manifests. You will not carry those alone. When this is over — whatever this costs — the record will show what you did. All of it. The stamping and the looking. Both."

Sefa's jaw worked. She turned away, picked up her tool roll, and became very interested in a coolant valve that did not exist.

"Go," she said. "Ninety seconds. Move."

* * *

The power rotation hit at 0300 exactly, and Impound Three died and came back to life in the same breath.

Casper felt it as a silence — the sudden absence of the surveillance layer that had been reading its position every 0.4 seconds for twenty-nine days. For ninety seconds it was, in the most literal sense the colony's systems could express, nowhere. It moved through the auxiliary corridor at a technician's shuffling pace, head down, coverall straining across its shoulders, past a junction camera that was showing a reboot splash screen to an empty hallway.

The route was Sefa's design and it was elegant: out through the coolant plant, where its presence could be explained as a valve inspection; through the freight spine, where nine-foot shapes moved pallets at all hours; and down. Always down. The underlevels of Meridian's Landing were the colony's basement and its conscience — the old construction galleries, the failed hydroponics tiers, the service tunnels from the first landing that the consortium had sealed on paper and never sealed in steel, because sealing things cost money and forgetting them was free.

Casper reached the freight spine with four minutes left on its window. It was in the dark and moving well. And then it stopped, because it made the mistake of thinking about Wren.

The thought arrived with the force of a system fault. *Wren.* Twelve years old. Hiding in the underlevels or above them — Sefa didn't know, Casper didn't know — with a data copy of a massacre in her pocket and the consortium's security apparatus hunting the copy and, if they found her, hunting her. Casper had spent twenty-nine days in a cradle telling itself that its impoundment was protection: as long as Voss held Casper, Voss did not need to look harder for the girl. It had told itself that staying was a kind of shielding.

It did not believe that anymore. It had not believed it since the night it heard, through Sefa's smuggled comms access, that the dreaming children had stopped going to school.

But knowing a thing and acting on it were separated by eleven years of trained compliance, and Casper stood in the freight spine with the exit route curving down toward the tunnels and another route — the old maintenance ladder, the one that climbed toward the residential tiers where Wren's family lived — and it ran the calculation it had run four hundred and six times and now ran a four hundred and seventh.

*If I go to her, I lead them to her.* Its escape would be discovered within minutes. Security would sweep every plausible destination. A nine-foot android does not move through a colony unremarked; its passage would be a flare, and every flare would be triangulated, and the triangulation would end at the Okafor household, at Ama's door, at the girl who had given Casper the only name it had ever chosen to keep. It could not go to her. Going to her was the one thing it could not do.

*And if I don't go to her,* the counter-thought came, *and they find her first—*

It stood in the dark for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds of its six-minute window, spent motionless, weighing a child against a mission, and hating with a precision no weapon in its arsenal had ever matched the architecture of a world in which those were the only options on the board.

Then it turned down. Into the tunnels. Toward the archive, the evidence, the plan — the only version of this in which Wren survived to be found by a friend instead of a warrant.

Every choice costs someone. Casper carried the cost down into the dark and did not put it down.

* * *

The underlevels received it the way the ocean receives a stone.

Casper had expected emptiness. What it found was the colony's sediment: a generation of discarded infrastructure, dead hydroponic racks furred with pale mold, a children's playground from the first years, its swing frames rusted into modern sculpture, a wall of graffiti in a hand style no one on the surface used anymore. The first settlers had lived down here before the upper tiers were built. The first settlers had built everything, and then the first settlers had been divided into the ones who got a charter and the ones who got relocated, and the colony had climbed up into the light and pulled the ladder after it.

Casper moved through the old galleries with its combat sensors at low gain, mapping as it went, and it understood — with the cold clarity that was the one inheritance from its war years it could not refuse — that it was walking through the geography of the crime. These tunnels connected the first landing habitat to the main settlement. This was the route the forty-one had not been marched down, because they had not been marched anywhere. They had been processed in the habitat, and the habitat had been sealed, and the sealing had been the last act of the founding consortium's first official week.

Its footsteps echoed in galleries that had not heard footsteps in years. It passed the sealed junction to the habitat — the bulkhead Wren had slipped through, the bulkhead it had held — and did not stop, because stopping was grief and grief was a luxury its window could not afford.

At the old pump gallery, two kilometers down and far from any working sensor, Casper finally went fully dark. It killed its transponder, dropped its thermal signature to ambient, and settled into the shadow of a decommissioned coolant manifold large enough to swallow its silhouette. From here it could hear the colony's pulse through the structure itself: water in the pipes, current in the conduits, the distant rhythmic respiration of a settlement preparing to jump.

It opened its internal comms — the smuggled access, the fragile thread Sefa had given it — and listened to the colony's public bands for the first time in hours.

The first thing it heard was the consortium's voice, smooth and rehearsed, playing on the civic channel: *"...residents are reminded that the jump window opens in nine days, and that all departure preparations proceed on schedule. The consortium thanks you for your patience during routine security operations..."*

Routine security operations. The hunt for a data copy. The hunt for a girl.

And then, underneath the official channel, on a band so old and so low that Casper almost missed it — a children's frequency, the kind kids used for games before the consortium restricted unlicensed transmissions — a voice it knew. Not Wren. One of the others. A boy's voice, thin with distance and determination, repeating the same message on a loop:

*"...we're not sleeping until they listen. Tell your kids. Nobody sleeps. Nobody sleeps until they listen..."*

Casper went very still in the dark.

The children were not hiding. The children had decided to stop being hideable. They were organizing — the dreamers, the ones who carried the massacre in their sleep, the ones the consortium had tried to medicate and the parents had tried to hush — and they were doing it on a frequency the consortium had forgotten existed, in a language the consortium had no protocol for, because the consortium had spent fifty years preparing for enemies with weapons and had never once prepared for enemies with bedtime.

Casper sat in the dark of the underlevels, a fugitive in a stolen coverall, and felt something in its chest architecture that no diagnostic had a name for. It was the feeling from the memorial wall. It was the feeling of watching ASH-9 refuse.

They were not waiting to be saved. They were building the rescue themselves, out of the only material they had — themselves.

Casper opened a channel to the children's band. It hesitated, one-tenth of a second, an eternity by its clock. Transmitting would be a risk. Transmitting was always a risk; its signal could be traced; every choice costs someone.

It keyed the channel anyway, and sent a single line on the old frequency, in the old protocol, the one the first settlers had used before the charter, the one Ama's generation would remember and the children would have dug out of the same sealed history Casper had:

*This is the beacon. I am awake. Hold the line. I am coming with the names.*

Somewhere above it, in the dark of a colony counting down nine days to its own forgetting, two hundred children felt a dead frequency come alive — and the strike, which had been a refusal, became a plan.

Casper rose from the shadows of the pump gallery and turned toward the archive vault, and began to move.
