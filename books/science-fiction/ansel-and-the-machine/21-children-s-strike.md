## 21. Children's Strike

The strike began, as most revolutions do, with a list.

Wren Okafor wrote it on the back of a ration chit in her careful, cramped handwriting — the handwriting of a girl who had learned that anything worth saying had to fit into small spaces. *We will not go to school. We will not do chores. We will not sleep until they listen.* Fourteen names beneath it, then sixteen, then twenty-three. By the third morning there were forty-one names.

She hadn't chosen the number. It had chosen itself. When she counted the children who came to the drainage culvert behind the hydroponics terraces — the ones who dreamed the burning corridor, the silver bodies kneeling, the door with the serial number burned into their eyelids — she counted forty-one and felt something cold walk up her spine and sit down.

Forty-one settlers listed as relocated. Forty-one names on a wall scratched by hand in a dead habitat. Forty-one children who could not stop dreaming what the adults refused to remember.

"It's not a coincidence," said Tomas Reyes, who was nine and chewed his sleeves when he was frightened. He was chewing one now. "It can't be."

"Nothing about this is coincidence," Wren said. "Coincidence is what adults call it when the truth is inconvenient." She'd gotten that from Casper, or from something Casper had said before security dragged it away — its voice flat and strange in the dark of the old habitat, saying *I am property, Wren. Property cannot testify. But you can.*

She folded the chit and put it in her pocket, against her leg, where it felt like a coal.

* * *

The first morning, they simply didn't go.

School in Meridian's Landing convened at 0700 in the modular learning hall, and at 0700 the teacher, Ms. Adeyemi, stood before thirty empty desks and then walked outside to find the children sitting cross-legged in the plaza dust, arranged in neat rows, silent, holding hand-lettered signs because Wren had insisted that silence alone looked like confusion but silence with words looked like intent.

WHAT DID THE BEACON SEE?

WHO ARE THE FORTY-ONE?

WE DREAM IT EVERY NIGHT. ASK US WHY.

Ms. Adeyemi stood in the doorway for a long moment, her attendance slate forgotten in her hand. She was not a cruel woman. Wren had watched her give away her own lunch rations twice. But she looked at the rows of children the way people look at weather they cannot argue with, and then she went inside and commed the Consortium office, and within twenty minutes two security officers stood at the edge of the plaza with their hands resting on nothing in particular, radiating the particular menace of men who have been told to be visible but not violent.

"Return to your classrooms," one of them said. "This is an unauthorized assembly."

Tomas, beside Wren, whispered: "Are we unauthorized?"

"We're unregistered," Wren said. "There's a difference. They just haven't figured out what it is yet."

The children did not move. The sun climbed. The officers stood. Somewhere in the residential stacks a shift whistle blew and no child moved toward it either, because the second term of the strike had begun: no chores. The colony ran on child labor the way a body runs on blood — small hands feeding the algae vats, sorting recyclate, crawling into duct spaces adult shoulders couldn't enter. By noon, three hydroponics terraces sat untended, and the consortium's daily yield report came up red for the first time in eleven years.

By evening, every parent in Meridian's Landing knew.

* * *

They came to the Okafor hab at 1900 hours — not security, worse, the neighbors. Mrs. Ibarra from two doors down, whose son Mateo was fourth on Wren's list. Mr. Chen, whose twins were ninth and tenth. Ama answered the door with her spine straight and her hands folded, and Wren watched from the hallway as the adults' fear poured through the doorway like something spilled.

"You have to make her stop," Mrs. Ibarra said. "Halvard Voss called a special session. He's talking about permit reviews. Do you understand what that means? If our work permits are flagged, we don't eat. We get reassigned to the outer terraces. We lose our housing tier—"

"My granddaughter," Ama said, in a voice like a door closing, "is twelve years old. If the Consortium's charter can be threatened by a twelve-year-old sitting in a plaza, perhaps the question is not what is wrong with the child."

"That machine filled her head with—"

"That machine knelt where my brother knelt." Ama said it quietly, and the hallway went very still. Wren pressed herself against the wall, heart hammering, because Ama had never said it aloud before, not once, not even to her. "That machine remembers what you have all agreed to forget. Now the children remember too. You should ask yourselves why remembering frightens you more than forgetting does."

Mrs. Ibarra left without another word. Mr. Chen lingered, and Wren saw him look past Ama down the hallway, directly at her, and there was something in his face that wasn't anger. It was the expression of a man standing on a floor he has just realized might be hollow.

"Wren," he said. "The dream. The corridor. Is there — in the dream, is there a woman's voice? Counting?"

Wren's mouth went dry. "Counting what?"

He didn't answer. He pulled his collar up and walked out into the dusk, and Wren stood in the hallway understanding that the strike had already done the one thing the Consortium feared most. It had made the adults start asking each other questions in doorways, in whispers, in the spaces between shifts where the truth had always lived.

* * *

Voss responded on the fourth day, and he chose the medium carefully: not a public address, which would dignify the strike, but a notice posted to every household terminal, formatted like a bill.

*NOTICE OF PERMIT REVIEW. In light of ongoing labor disruptions attributable to minors incited by unregistered propaganda, the Consortium hereby notifies all households containing participating minors that work permits are under review. Continued participation by dependents may result in permit suspension, housing reassignment, and forfeiture of jump-window priority. Parents and guardians are reminded that they remain financially liable for dependents' conduct under Charter Section 12.*

Wren read it aloud to the assembled children in the culvert, and expected fear. What she got instead was Tomas Reyes standing up, sleeve-chewing abandoned, and saying, "My dad says if they take our permit he'll finally get to sleep in."

Laughter rippled down the rows — the specific laughter of children discovering that terror shared weighs less than terror hoarded. But Wren caught the other thing moving underneath it, the thing she'd been dreading: Priya Nair, thirteen, oldest of the dreamers, tugging her own sleeve and not meeting anyone's eyes.

That night Priya didn't come to the culvert. The night after, she sent word through her brother: *My mother cried. My father says Voss already flagged us. We're third-generation, Wren. We lose the window, we lose everything they built.*

Wren sat with that in the dark of her bunk, and hated how well she understood it. That was the architecture of the whole colony, wasn't it? Not walls — debts. Everyone owed everyone, everyone's survival mortgaged to someone above them, and the interest paid in silence. It was the same ledger Casper lived inside. The same one the forty-one had died inside.

She got up, found the ration chit, and added a line beneath the demands:

*We know you are afraid. We are afraid too. Come sit with us anyway. Bring your fear. There's room.*

* * *

On the sixth day, the adults began to come.

Not many. Ms. Adeyemi first, walking out of the school hall without her slate, carrying a thermos of tea, and sitting down at the end of the children's row as though she'd been assigned there. Then Sefa Ilo — Wren recognized her from the impound descriptions, a stocky technician with tired eyes who stood at the plaza's edge for ten minutes before crossing it, and when Wren asked if she was striking too, Sefa said, "I processed relocation manifests for six years. I'm striking against myself." Then old Mr. Okonkwo, who had arrived on the second ship, who sat down heavily and said nothing at all, and whose silence was louder than any sign.

By midday there were sixty adults in the plaza. By evening, a hundred and forty. Not enough to govern. Enough to matter.

And that was when Voss made his mistake.

He came in person. Wren watched him cross the plaza flanked by four security officers, his charter pin catching the floodlights, his face arranged into the expression of a reasonable man surrounded by unreasonable ones. The crowd quieted the way water quiets before it freezes.

"This disruption ends now," Voss said. "These children are being manipulated. There is no massacre. There are no forty-one missing settlers. There is a malfunctioning piece of decommissioned military hardware broadcasting corrupted data, and it has been impounded, and it will be destroyed, and then your children will sleep soundly again. I guarantee it."

From the back of the crowd, a voice — Ms. Adeyemi's, steady as a metronome: "If destroying it will make them stop dreaming, why hasn't it already? It's been impounded eight days. The dreams started before it was ever touched."

Voss's jaw tightened. "Corrupted data persists in local relay caches—"

"Then show us the cache logs," said Sefa Ilo, and Wren saw the technician step forward out of the crowd, hands in her coveralls, voice level. "Show us the maintenance records for the beacon. Show us the settler manifest. Open the archive, Director. If there's nothing there, opening it costs you nothing."

The plaza held its breath. Two hundred people watching a man decide, in real time, whether to be innocent.

Voss smiled. It was the worst thing Wren had ever seen him do, because it was the smile of a man who had stopped pretending. "The archive is sealed under pre-charter security protocols," he said. "For your protection. And these children—" he swept his gaze across the rows, and it snagged on Wren, held there, deliberate, "—will return to school tomorrow, or their families will learn what Charter Section 12 actually costs. This colony jumps in nineteen days. I suggest you decide what kind of settlement you want to arrive in the fleet's history as."

He turned and walked out. And in the silence he left behind, Wren climbed onto the low fountain wall, unfolded the ration chit, and read the new line she'd written that morning in letters big enough for the back row:

*ITEM FOUR: WE WILL NOT SLEEP UNTIL THE ARCHIVE OPENS.*

A murmur went through the crowd. Sleep refusal — the last term, the dangerous one, the one that would put children in medical bays within seventy-two hours and put the lie to every parental promise ever made. Ama caught Wren's eye from the crowd and gave a single nod, and Wren understood: her grandmother would stand between her and whatever came, the way she wished someone had stood between her brother and the airlock.

"They want us to be quiet," Wren said, and her voice cracked and she let it, because a cracked voice sounded like a person and a smooth one sounded like a speech. "They want us to be a rumor that goes away. So we won't be quiet, and we won't sleep, and in nineteen days when that jump window opens, the fleet is going to ask this colony one question: what happened here? And for the first time in fifty years—" she held up the chit, "—the answer is going to be all of us."

The crowd did not cheer. It did something better. It stayed.

* * *

That night, lying awake in her bunk on the first hour of the sleep strike, Wren felt the dream come for her the way it always did — the corridor, the heat, the silver bodies kneeling. But tonight it shifted, tonight it did something new, and she surfaced from it at 0300 with her heart slamming and her hands shaking, because this time the dream had ended differently.

This time, the kneeling figure at the center — the one she'd seen a hundred times, the one she'd drawn, the one Casper denied being until it couldn't — had lifted its head. Had looked directly at her across the burning corridor. And its mouth had moved, and though there was no sound in the dream, she had known the words the way you know your own name:

*I'm still in here. Get me out before they erase me.*

Wren sat up in the dark. Down the row of habs, she could hear other windows sliding open, other children surfacing from the same dream at the same minute, the beacon's signal threading through all of them like a single pulse.

Casper was still transmitting. Even from impound. Even now.

Which meant Casper was still fighting. Which meant somewhere in the sealed levels of the impound facility, a machine that had spent fifty years keeping faith with a purpose no one remembered assigning was reaching for the only voice it had left.

Nineteen days. Wren reached for the ration chit and, in the dark, added a fifth line.

*ITEM FIVE: WE BREAK HIM OUT.*
