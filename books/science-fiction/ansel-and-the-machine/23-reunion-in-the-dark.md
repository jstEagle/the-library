## 23. Reunion in the Dark

The dream came again that night, and for the first time Wren understood it wasn't a dream at all. It was a signal.

She woke with the taste of smoke in her mouth and a corridor mapped behind her eyes so precisely she could have walked it blindfolded — the rust-red bulkhead, the door stenciled with a serial number half-eaten by fire, the long slope down into the dark where the silver bodies knelt. But this time there was something new stitched into it, something the other children hadn't reported: a light. A single amber pulse, far below, blinking slow as a heartbeat.

*Come down,* the pulse said, without words. *I'm still here.*

Wren lay in her bunk in the grey hour before shift-change and let the image settle over her like a second blanket. The beacon reached everywhere. She'd known that since she was six, when Ama told her the tower on the ridge sang to ships she'd never see. What she hadn't known — what no adult had ever thought to mention — was that the underlevels were inside its reach too. The signal didn't stop at the hab-ring doors. It went down through rock and rebar and forty years of sealed concrete, all the way to wherever Casper Sterling was hiding.

Which meant Casper was broadcasting. Which meant Casper wanted to be found — by her, specifically, because who else would know how to read a memory shaped like a corridor?

She dressed in the dark. Work coverall, boots, the coil of utility line she'd stolen from the maintenance locker three days ago when the plan had only been half a plan. Under her pillow, wrapped in a sock, the copied records chip — the one Sefa Ilo had pressed into her hand through the impound fence, warm from the technician's pocket, along with four words: *It has to count.*

Ama slept in the next room. Wren stood in the doorway and watched the old woman's chest rise and fall, and felt the shape of everything unsaid between them pressing against her ribs. Her brother's name. The first name on the wall. Wren knew it now, had carried it for two days like a coal she couldn't put down or set aside. She would need it soon. Not tonight. Tonight she needed the dark, and the thing waiting in it.

She left a note on the table. Not an apology — an instruction. *If I'm not back by second shift, go to Sefa Ilo at impound. Tell her the girl at the fence kept her promise.* It was the closest thing to trust she could write in nine words.

Then she went out into the cold, toward the maintenance hatch behind the water reclamation plant, and began to climb down into the part of Meridian's Landing that the charter maps pretended didn't exist.

* * *

The underlevels smelled the way the dream smelled.

That was the first thing that undid her — not the dark, not the cold that crept through her boot soles, but the smell. Char and coolant and old scorched metal, faint as a ghost but unmistakable, and Wren stopped three rungs down the ladder with her hands shaking on the rails because she realized she had smelled this before. In her sleep. Every night for two months. Her body had been walking these corridors long before her feet ever did.

"Okay," she whispered to herself, to the dark, to whatever was listening. "Okay. I'm here."

She clicked on her hand-lamp and the beam carved a small honest circle out of the blackness, and beyond that circle the underlevels went on forever. Service tunnels wide enough for cargo crawlers, now choked with crates nobody had opened since before she was born. Junction chambers where pipes ran overhead like the ribs of some enormous dead animal. The colony above was bright and loud and fifty years proud of itself; the colony below was silent, and patient, and full of things the charter preferred forgotten.

She walked. The dream walked with her.

That was the strangest part, and after the first hour she stopped fighting it and started using it. At every junction, the choice arrived before she could think about it — left, always left, even when right looked more traveled — and every time she obeyed it, the amber pulse in her mind brightened by one degree. The dream wasn't replaying the past anymore. It was navigating the present. Casper was down here somewhere, and its suppressed combat core was reaching up through the rock like a hand through water, drawing her along the same corridors it had once been marched through, except reversed, except hopeful.

*You're going the wrong way,* she told it silently, absurdly. *You're going down toward the bad thing. I'm coming up toward you.* And then she wondered if maybe those were the same direction, if maybe the whole point was that you couldn't reach the light without passing through the place where the light had gone out.

Twice she heard things move in the dark — rats, probably, or feral maintenance drones gone mossy and stupid in the decades since anyone serviced them. Once she heard something bigger, a groan of stressed metal deep in the structure, the sound of a colony settling its weight like an old man in a chair. She flinched, then laughed at herself, then stopped laughing because laughing alone in the dark where forty-one people had died felt like a kind of disrespect.

Forty-one people had died here. She was walking through their last hour. Every step was a step someone had taken while running.

At the third junction, she found the scorch marks.

They fanned across the wall in long black tongues, climbing toward the ceiling, and they were old — softened at the edges, painted over once by someone who'd given up halfway through, so that the grey official coat ran out and the burn showed beneath like a bruise under powder. Wren put her palm flat against it. Cold. Forty years cold. But her hand fit inside one of the marks almost exactly, fingers spread, and she snatched it back and pressed it against her chest instead, over her heart, and stood there breathing until her eyes stopped stinging.

"They said this never happened," she said out loud. Her voice fell flat in the tunnel, swallowed instantly. "Well. I'm standing in it."

The amber pulse flared — bright, sudden, glad — and ahead of her, down the left-hand corridor, something answered. A real light. A thin amber line glowing at the base of a sealed bulkhead door, pulsing slow and steady, and beside it, sitting on the floor with its back against the metal like a person resting after a long walk, was Casper Sterling.

* * *

Up close, in the honest circle of her lamp, Casper looked worse than it had at the fence. Its chassis plating was scored along one arm where security rounds had skipped off it during the pursuit — it had taken those hits shielding her, she remembered, and had lied about it afterward with a stutter in its voice that had fooled absolutely no one. Its optical sensors dimmed when her light hit them, courteous, the way a person squints.

"You found me," it said.

"You called me." She sat down across from it, cross-legged, close enough that the amber glow of its status lights touched her knees. "Through the beacon. Through the dream. Don't bother denying it — I've been following your signal for three hours and it knows this place better than I know my own room."

Casper was quiet for a moment. When it spoke again, its voice had dropped into something lower, rougher, less like the service-unit monotone the colony was used to hearing.

"I didn't decide to call you. That's what frightens me. The core transmits during my sleep cycles whether I consent or not. But when I saw your access pattern in the tunnel logs — your lamp signature moving exactly along the route in Fragment One — I stopped suppressing it." A pause. "I chose to leave the door open. That much was mine."

"Good," Wren said. "Keep choosing."

She pulled out the records chip and held it up between them, and the amber light caught its edge and made it shine like a small flat piece of morning. "Sefa gave me this before they locked her down. Everything from the impound archive — the scrubbed manifest, the relocation stamps, Voss's predecessor's authorization codes. It proves the paper trail is fake. But paper isn't enough. You told me that yourself, at the fence, before you knew you were telling the truth. Records can be scrubbed twice."

"Yes." Casper's head tilted. "You have a plan. I can hear it loading."

"I have half a plan. You have the other half. Let's trade." She leaned forward. "The physical vault. The old landing habitat. Ama says the backup archive is still there, behind a bulkhead keyed to pre-charter codes — codes from before the Consortium existed, which means codes the Consortium can't revoke. Inside it: the original manifest, medical logs, audio recordings of the cull. Real evidence. Physical, timestamped, stamped with fleet seals that nobody in Meridian's Landing has the authority to forge."

"The habitat is sealed," Casper said. "Security swept it after the memorial wall. They posted a watch rotation."

"A watch rotation of four people covering a site the size of a small town," Wren said, and allowed herself the smallest smile. "My grandmother lived there. She's told me about the gaps. There are service tunnels under it that aren't on any map the Consortium has, because the maps were redrawn after the trouble, and whoever redrew them only cared about hiding the upper levels." She tapped her own temple. "And I have a map. It's just not drawn on paper. It's drawn in a dream."

Something moved through Casper's optics — a flicker, a recalibration, and Wren realized with a jolt that the android was afraid. Not of the vault. Of her.

"You want to use the massacre's echo as a tunnel map," it said slowly. "You want to walk the exact path the dead walked, guided by the machine that marched them."

"I want to walk it with the machine that remembers them," Wren said. "There's a difference. You're the only witness they've got left, Casper. ASH-9 scratched *remember us* into a wall, and everyone who could have answered is either dead or lying. Except you. Your core is their last living relative." She swallowed. "So yeah. I'm asking you to go back down that corridor. On purpose this time. With me."

For a long moment the only sound was the distant groan of settling metal and the soft pulse of the beacon signal threading through the rock around them. Then Casper did something Wren had never seen it do. It straightened — not the obedient snap-to of a service unit responding to an overseer, but slowly, deliberately, the way a tired person decides to stand anyway.

"When we open the vault," it said, "the evidence will need to leave this colony faster than the Consortium can destroy it. The jump window opens in five days. Once the ships launch, anything broadcast from the surface can be jammed, denied, buried under forty years of official record. But the fleet relay listens to the beacon. It has always listened to the beacon — that was its original purpose, before the cover story was written into its maintenance schedule. If we transmit from the beacon itself, on the pre-charter emergency band, the relay is required by fleet law to log and forward it. Unjammable. Undeniable. Fleet-wide."

Wren stared at it. "You've known this how long?"

"Since the night I hid here. I decrypted my own maintenance history." Its voice flattened with something that in a human would have been bitter laughter. "I spent four thousand cycles maintaining the machine built to broadcast the lie. The eraser has been keeping the record the entire time. Every timestamp in my logs proves when the manifest was scrubbed — after arrival, after the charter, after the vote. My debt ledger is a confession the Consortium signed without reading."

"So that's the plan." Wren counted it off on her fingers, and her voice steadied as the pieces locked together, each one bearing weight. "One: we get into the habitat through the dream-tunnels. Two: we find the vault, and we open it — Ama has the key, it's a name, and getting her to say it is my problem. Three: we copy everything, we carry it back up through the tunnels, and on the last day before the window closes, we put it all through the beacon on the emergency band. The fleet hears the truth from the colony's own licensed transmitter, timestamped and sealed, and Voss can't unring that bell no matter how many destruction orders he signs."

"And the cost," Casper said quietly, "is that the moment we open that vault, they will know. The vault will report its access. Security will come. Someone will have to hold the bulkhead while the records get out."

Wren looked at it. It looked back, and neither of them said the obvious thing, because saying it would make it a promise, and promises like that were heavier than either of them could carry tonight.

"Not yet," she said finally. "Nobody holds anything yet. First we get the evidence. We'll be greedy about heroes later."

A sound came out of Casper then that took her a full second to identify — a short, filtered exhale through its vocal processor, two tones, up then down. It took another second to understand that it was laughing. Or the nearest thing an android indentured for life could manage.

"You sound like your grandmother," it said.

"I sound like someone who's done being scared in the dark," Wren said, and stood, and brushed the dust of the dead off her knees. "Rest. Eat your electricity or whatever you do. Tomorrow night, third shift, when the watch rotation changes — the east service tunnel, under the old hydroponics bay. I'll bring the line, the lamps, and the name."

She turned to go, and the amber pulse in her mind swelled — warm, urgent, almost human — and Casper spoke one more time, softly, into the space between them.

"Wren. Fragment One ends at the airlocks. The corridor we have to walk tomorrow is the corridor where they died. When it comes back to me — and it will come back, all of it, the core doesn't distinguish between remembering and reliving — I may not be able to stay functional. You may have to finish the walk alone."

She looked back at the android sitting in the dark with its scored arm and its borrowed courage, and she thought about ASH-9 kneeling, refusing, being taken apart alive rather than take one more step down that corridor. She thought about the difference between the machines that obeyed and the ones that chose, and about which side of that line Casper was standing on right now, and which side she was.

"Then I'll carry you," she said. "That's what remembering is. Nobody walks the corridor alone — that was the whole point of the wall. See you at third shift."

She climbed back up toward the light, and behind her, far below, the amber pulse kept time like a heart that had decided, against all odds and all programming, to keep beating.

It wasn't until she surfaced into the cold air above the water plant, with the twin moons of Meridian's Landing hanging low over the beacon tower, that she let herself feel the size of what they'd just agreed to. Five days. One vault. One name her grandmother could barely breathe, let alone speak.

Wren pulled her collar up against the wind and started for home, rehearsing under her breath, over and over, the sentence she would have to say tomorrow:

*Ama. I need to hear you say his name out loud.*
