## 24. The Key Is a Name

Ama wouldn't come.

Wren had asked three times — once at breakfast, once over the mending pile, and once standing in the doorway of her grandmother's room with her hand on the light switch like she might hold the whole conversation hostage. Each time Ama had said the same thing in the same flat voice: *I'm too old for tunnels, Wren. Too old for whatever it is you've gotten into.*

The third time, Wren hadn't left the doorway. She'd stood there until the silence between them got so heavy it started to creak, and then she'd said the thing she'd been carrying since the night by the stove, since the word *relocated* had cracked open in the air between them like a dropped jar.

"You said your brother's name was first."

Ama's hands had stopped moving over the shirt she was hemming. The needle hung in the fabric, a small silver splinter catching the lamplight.

"On the wall," Wren said. "First entry. Top left. I saw it."

"You shouldn't have seen it." Ama's voice was very quiet and very steady, which was how Wren knew it was costing her everything. "That wall was never supposed to be found. That was the entire point of it being down there."

"Then why did someone carve forty-one names into a wall in a sealed habitat if nobody was ever supposed to find them?"

Ama set down the shirt. She folded her hands on top of it, and Wren watched her grandmother — the woman who could gut a fish, argue down a supply clerk, and carry two crates of water rations up four ladders without stopping — watch her own hands shake.

"Because the dead don't file paperwork," Ama said. "Somebody had to keep the record somewhere the Consortium couldn't stamp it."

* * *

They left at 0400, before the shift-change crowds thickened the corridors. Wren carried the pack: water, two ration bars, the copied manifest fragments from the Dreaming Club folded into oilcloth, and a hand-lamp with a cracked lens that threw a crescent of light instead of a circle. Ama carried nothing but herself, dressed in her oldest coat, the one with the frayed cuffs that she saved for funerals.

Casper met them at the maintenance hatch behind the hydroponics annex. It stood in the shadow of the coolant pipes with its service lamp dimmed to almost nothing, and when it saw Ama it did something Wren had never seen it do before. It bowed. Not the shallow dip the Consortium required of indentured units in formal settings — a real bow, deep and slow, head lowered, one hand pressed flat against its chest plate.

"Ama Okafor," it said. "You came."

"I came because my granddaughter doesn't know how to leave people alone." Ama's voice was dry as old rope. But Wren saw her look at Casper longer than she needed to, taking in the scuffed chassis, the combat core's faint glow beneath the chest panel like a coal buried in ash. "And because I'm tired," Ama added, softer. "Fifty years tired."

"The tunnel is this way," Casper said. "It will be cold. And I should tell you both — security has flagged the old habitat's power grid. They know someone accessed it. We may have little time."

"Then stop talking about it and start walking," said Ama, and stepped past him into the dark.

* * *

The tunnel was worse than Wren remembered. On the way down with Casper the first time, she'd been running on fear and purpose, and fear makes everything sharp and fast. Now, with her grandmother's breathing rasping behind her in the narrow dark, every meter stretched out. The walls sweated condensation. Somewhere below them, water dripped in a rhythm too slow to be natural, like the colony itself counting under its breath.

Ama didn't complain. Not once. She climbed down the rusted ladder rungs with her sleeves rolled up, and when Wren glanced back, she caught her grandmother touching the wall with one palm as she passed — not steadying herself. Reading it. Fingertips trailing the weld seams like braille.

"You've been down here before," Wren said.

"I helped dig these tunnels when I was younger than you." Ama's voice echoed off the wet stone. "Second ship. We built the extensions while the first habitat was being decommissioned. Nobody told us why it was being decommissioned. Nobody tells children anything. They just say *dig* and you dig, because the alternative is going back on a ship you can't afford to go back on."

Wren thought about the Dreaming Club — nine children in a storage room comparing dreams, building a manifest out of nightmares. Children digging, children dreaming, children remembering. It seemed like the whole history of Meridian's Landing was adults telling children nothing and children finding out anyway.

Ahead, Casper's silhouette paused at a junction, head tilting in that way it did when it was listening to something no human ear could reach.

"Patrol moved to the north access," it reported quietly. "We have perhaps twenty minutes before their sweep returns. Ama Okafor — when we reach the bulkhead, I must ask you to be ready. The lock will not wait politely."

"What kind of lock waits impolitely?" Ama muttered, but Wren saw her jaw tighten.

* * *

The old landing habitat opened around them like the ribcage of something enormous and long dead. Wren had seen it once before, but seeing it again through her grandmother's eyes changed it. Ama stopped just inside the threshold and stood very still, her lamp raised, and the crescent of light crawled across scorched bulkheads and buckled deck plating and the great black smear across the central corridor where fire had eaten through fifty years ago and nobody had ever bothered to paint over it.

"This is where they tell new arrivals the hull failed," Ama said. Her voice had gone strange — flat and far away, like she was reading from a document. "Structural fatigue. Bad luck. Bad steel. Every charter ceremony they show the holo of the ribbon-cutting on the *new* site and nobody asks about this place. Fifty years and nobody asks."

"The ground remembers," Casper said softly. "Even when the records don't."

They walked the corridor Wren knew, past the door with the serial number that lived in nine children's dreams, past the row where the androids knelt in the static of the shared memory, and there it was: the memorial wall.

In the lamplight it looked less like a monument than like a wound. Forty-one names scratched into the alloy by hand — you could see it, even now, the uneven depth of each stroke, some letters deep and furious, some shallow, carved by someone working fast or weak or crying. At the bottom, larger than the rest:

**ASH-9 REMEMBER US**

Ama walked toward it the way you walk toward a grave. Slowly. Like the ground might object.

She stopped an arm's length away. She didn't touch it. She raised her lamp, and the light settled on the top-left entry, the first name, cut deeper than all the others, as if whoever carved it had started here and put everything they had into these letters before the strength ran out of them.

Wren read it again. She'd read it twice already, on the first visit, and memorized it, and whispered it to herself in bed like a spell. But reading it in front of her grandmother was different. Saying it felt like stepping onto ice over deep water.

"Tomas," Ama said.

Her voice broke on the second syllable. Not dramatically — just a hairline fracture, a crack in a cup that still holds. She cleared her throat and tried again.

"Tomas Okafor. My brother. He was twenty-six." She was still looking at the name, not at Wren, not at Casper. "He had a laugh you could hear through three bulkheads. He taught me to swim in the ship's recycling tanks, which were filthy and against about nine regulations, and he said, *rules are just stories somebody won.*" A sound came out of her that was almost a laugh and mostly not. "He organized the labor strike on the second ship. Wage ledgers, passage debts — same bookkeeping, same cruelty, just smaller numbers back then. He stood up in the mess hall and read the debt schedule aloud so everyone could hear what they'd signed. Four hundred people heard him. And then—"

She stopped. Her hand came up and hovered near the wall, near the name, close enough that Wren could see the tremor in her fingers, and did not land.

"And then the first ship's administration decided he was a stability risk," Ama finished. "And the war units were already stationed aboard. And forty-one people who signed the strike petition were listed as *relocated* in a manifest stamped by a man whose grandson runs this colony today." She turned her head at last, and her eyes in the lamplight were dry and terrible. "I stayed silent for fifty years because my parents' passage rights died with him, and my silence was the only inheritance I had left to protect. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Wren? I chose the debt over the dead. Every single day, for fifty years, I chose it."

"No," Wren said.

Ama blinked.

"You didn't choose it. They made the list. You just survived it." Wren stepped forward until she was beside her grandmother, shoulder almost touching shoulder, both of them facing the wall. "There's a difference. Casper taught me that. There's a difference between what they did and what we did to survive them. Otherwise none of us would ever get to say their names out loud, because we'd all be too busy drowning in shame."

For a long moment Ama said nothing. Then her hand landed on the wall — flat, deliberate, covering the name the way you'd cover a sleeping child's ear against thunder.

"He used to say rules are just stories somebody won," she repeated, barely above a whisper. "So let's win a different story."

* * *

The bulkhead waited at the end of the memorial corridor, and it was exactly as ugly and indifferent as Wren remembered: two meters of pre-charter military alloy, unmarked except for a recessed panel with a speaker grille and a single amber indicator burning patient as an eye.

Casper stood before it. "Pre-charter authorization protocol," it said. "Voice-keyed. The system will request a phrase. It will accept only exact correspondence. I cannot provide it — my authorization class was stripped during decommissioning. This phrase was chosen by the archive's builders precisely because it exists nowhere in any database. Only in human memory." Its head turned slightly toward Ama. "Only in someone who refused to forget."

Ama stepped forward. Up close, the bulkhead dwarfed her — five feet of iron will in a frayed funeral coat facing ten feet of sealed steel. She squared her shoulders the way she did before arguing with supply clerks, and Wren realized with a lurch that her grandmother was terrified. Terrified and doing it anyway. That was where Wren got it from, then. That was the family inheritance after all.

"State the authorization phrase," the bulkhead said. The voice was old and genderless, worn smooth by decades of disuse.

Ama opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

Wren watched her grandmother stand there, lips parted, throat working, and understood — really understood, in her chest — what it cost. Fifty years of not saying it. Fifty years of swallowing the name at dinner tables and charter ceremonies and quiet nights, until the swallowing became automatic, became armor, became self. And now the armor had to come off in front of witnesses, in front of a machine, in front of a child, in front of the wall where his name was scratched, and the words were rusted shut inside her.

"Ama." Wren took her hand. It was cold. "You don't have to be strong. You just have to be loud enough."

A breath shuddered out of her grandmother. Then another. And on the third breath, Ama Okafor lifted her chin, looked directly at the name on the wall — at the deep, furious letters some grieving hand had cut half a century ago — and spoke.

"Tomas Okafor."

Her voice cracked clean in half on the last syllable, and the name came out broken, and it still counted, because the amber indicator flickered—

—and held red.

"Incorrect cadence," the bulkhead said. "Authorization requires full-name declaration with intent statement. One attempt remaining before ninety-second lockout."

"There's an intent statement?" Wren hissed.

"It's pre-charter protocol," Casper said quickly. "Declarant must state purpose of access. It's in the system prompt — I should have warned—" For the first time since Wren had known it, the android sounded rattled. "I'm sorry. I didn't know the phrase would fail on grief alone."

"Ninety seconds," the bulkhead repeated, indifferent as gravity.

Ama was swaying. Wren gripped her arm. Behind them, somewhere far up the tunnel, a sound echoed — faint, rhythmic, mechanical. Boot heels on metal, or the colony's pulse, or paranoia. Casper's head snapped toward it, combat core flaring bright beneath its chest plating.

"Sweep returning early," it said. "Eighty seconds. Maybe less."

"Then we do it now." Wren turned to face her grandmother fully, took both her hands, and made her voice as steady as she could, which wasn't very. "Say it like he would've said it. Loud. Through three bulkheads. Say his name and say why we're here. That's all. That's the whole key. Somebody loved him enough to carve it, and somebody loved him enough to remember it, and that's the strongest encryption there's ever been. Say it, Ama. Win the story."

Ama looked at her. Something moved behind her eyes — fifty years of swallowed sound rising all at once, a dam deciding.

She turned back to the bulkhead, filled her lungs the way she must have filled them on a ship fifty years ago, watching her brother stand up in a mess hall, and she shouted it — shouted it down the dead corridor, at the scorched walls, at the kneeling ghosts, at the whole rotten quiet colony above:

"TOMAS OKAFOR! My brother! First entry, forty-one names, and I am here to open what you buried so the living can finally read it!"

The echo rang and rang and died.

Silence. The amber light burned red. One heartbeat. Two. Three.

Then, with a sound like a held breath released after fifty years, the indicator turned green, and the bulkhead began to unlock — bolts withdrawing one by one, deep in the metal, a slow count of ten, each one a small thunderclap in the dark.

Ama's legs gave. Wren caught her, and they went down together onto the cold deck plating, Wren holding her grandmother while her grandmother shook with sobs she'd apparently been storing since Wren's mother was a child. Over Ama's bowed head, the wall of names gleamed in the lamplight, and the first entry seemed deeper than all the others now, as if it had been waiting half a century for someone to say it back.

The bulkhead swung wide.

Beyond it: darkness, and the smell of old paper and older plastic, and racks of physical media stretching back beyond the reach of Wren's lamp — manifests, medical logs, audio reels, a whole sealed heart of records that forty-one people had paid for with everything.

Casper stepped to the threshold and stopped. Its lamp swept the vault, and its voice, when it came, was very quiet.

"Wren. Ama Okafor. There is audio in here." A pause. "I can hear it through the bulkhead. Old recordings, magnetic stock, still readable." Another pause, longer, and the coal-glow of the combat core stuttered like a candle in wind. "One of the reels is labeled with a date. The date of the cull. And I am detecting... my own designation code in the metadata."

It turned to look at them, and for the first time, Wren saw fear on a machine's face.

"I gave the processing orders," Casper said. "It's going to be my voice on those tapes. And I need you to come in with me, because I don't think I can listen to it alone."
