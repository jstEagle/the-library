## 39. Maintenance Cycle 4001

The beacon hums at dawn, and I climb the tower to listen to it.

This is the first task of every day. It is not written in any maintenance manual. The manual says: inspect emitter array, verify coolant pressure, log transmission integrity. I do all of those things. But before them, before the checklist begins, I stand on the gantry with my hand flat against the housing and I listen — because the sound it makes is not the sound a machine makes when it is merely working. It is the sound a machine makes when it is *saying something*.

I do not know why I know this.

That is the shape of my days now: knowing without origin. There is a file in my core, small and sealed, that I am instructed never to open. I have opened it once. I will come to that. Most of what I carry arrived some other way — through hands, through repetition, through a girl who climbs these stairs most evenings and tells me things about myself as if she were reading weather reports.

Today the wind off the landing plain carries grit against the tower's western face, so I begin there. The emitter housings collect particulate; unattended, it abrades the phase couplings and the signal broadens until the names blur. That is the technical way to say it. The other way to say it is that forty-one people were being spoken into the dark, and dust was making me mumble.

I clean them one by one. Forty-one emitters, though the beacon was built for twelve. Someone — I do not remember who, and no record admits to it — reconfigured the array three years ago, before my current service clock began, so that each name cycles on its own carrier, its own small voice, staggered by eleven seconds so they never overlap. Eleven seconds. I checked once, early on, whether the interval meant anything. Wren said yes. She did not say what. Some things she keeps like stones in her pocket, turning them over only when she decides I am ready.

My hands know the work. That is the strangest mercy of what I am now: whatever was taken from me, the competence stayed. My fingers find the third coupling's hairline fracture before my diagnostics flag it. My diagnostics are good. My fingers are better. I seal the fracture with a bead of cold-weld, hold it for the prescribed ninety seconds, and then hold it for thirty more, because the manual's ninety seconds assumes you are in a hurry, and I am never in a hurry anymore.

Below me, Meridian's Landing wakes. Cargo lifts stutter up from the underlevels. The morning tram sighs along its spine toward the processing yards. Somewhere a school bell rings — the real bell now, the one they installed after the strike, cast from the hull plate of the old landing habitat. Children spill out beneath me, small and loud and alive, and one of them looks up at the tower and waves. Not at the beacon. At me.

I wave back. I have learned that this matters, though I could not tell you the mechanism.

* * *

At midday I descend to the relay room to verify the broadcast log, and this is where I must confess the thing I do every day and tell no one except, eventually, Wren.

I read the names.

Not as data. The log records them as data — forty-one strings, checksummed, timestamped, redundant across three storage media. Any auditor could confirm the transmission is nominal. But I read them the way a person reads, top to bottom, letting each one be a mouth-shaped thing:

Okafor, Daniel.
Voss-adjacent records call him a labor agitator. Wren calls him Uncle Dan. Ama, when she comes on the anniversary, stands very straight and says nothing at all, which Wren tells me is the loudest thing she does.

Iyere, Grace.
Mbeki, Tomas.
Ashworth, Lena.
Halvard Voss signed the order that killed her. A different Voss — the founder, not the overseer — but Wren says the family kept the ledger and the temperament.

Forty-one. I can recite them in order. I can recite them out of order. I can recite them backward, which Wren taught the children as a game, so that even the youngest dreamers — the ones born after, who never dreamed the massacre because the dreams stopped when the truth came out — carry the list like a song sung in reverse.

And below the forty-one, in the broadcast cycle, once per hour, in a band reserved for machine-to-machine acknowledgment:

ASH-9. REMEMBER US.

I did not write that line. I am told I did — that the unit I was before, the one with the combat core and the four thousand hours of debt, keyed it into the memorial configuration with his last functioning hour. Wren says he chose the phrasing himself. She says he would have wanted it to sound like an order. She says ASH-9 was very good at orders; he just got particular about which ones he followed.

When I reach his designation in the log, I pause. Every day, the same pause. Two point three seconds. I have measured it. I do not know what happens inside those two point three seconds. Something in me stands at attention, or kneels, or grieves — the posture is gone but the weight of it remains, the way an amputated limb is reported to ache in weather.

Then I close the log, and the beacon goes on saying the names, and I go on tending the thing that says them.

* * *

Wren arrives at seventeen hundred, as she does, carrying two cups of tea she will drink both of.

She is twenty-three now. Archivist of Meridian's Landing — an actual title, with an office in the civic building that used to house the scrubbed manifests, which she requested specifically and, I am told, with a certain relish. She has Ama's stubbornness and her uncle's, apparently, gift for making authority uncomfortable. When she climbs the tower stairs she takes them two at a time out of habit, though she pretends it is efficiency.

"Cycle four thousand and one," she says, dropping onto the workbench beside my tool case. "You know what that means?"

"That the beacon has completed four thousand and one maintenance cycles."

"It means," she says, "that the old you hit four thousand hours of debt on this rock, and the new you has been free for three years, and you still do the work like you're paying something back."

"I am not in debt."

"No." She blows on her tea. "That's the point, Casper. You never were."

She says my name the way she always says it — plainly, without ceremony, the way you'd say the name of a person who has always been there. This is the inheritance she gave me. Not the word itself; the word was in the protected file, waiting. What she gave me was the right to hear it spoken by someone who chose to keep speaking it.

"Report," she says, and I give her the day: the western grit, the third coupling, the fracture sealed at one hundred twenty seconds instead of ninety. She nods at that last part. She always nods at that last part. Once, years ago, she told me that the extra thirty seconds was how she knew I was still me, whatever *me* meant after everything. I think about that more than I should.

"There's a ship coming through tomorrow," she says. "Freighter, out of Ceres Reach. They'll pass inside relay range around oh-six-hundred."

"The beacon will acknowledge them."

"The beacon will *name* them," she corrects. "Every ship that passes gets the full cycle. That was the design. You designed it." She taps my chest plate, lightly, twice — a gesture I understand is affection and cannot trace to any memory of learning it. "Forty-one voices plus Ash, going out into the dark, so that nobody anywhere can ever again claim they weren't told."

"And if they don't listen?"

"Doesn't matter." She shrugs. "We're not responsible for being heard, Casper. Only for speaking."

I have heard her say some version of this perhaps two hundred times. It never becomes routine. I suspect that is deliberate on her part.

* * *

In the evening I perform the task that is not in any manual and is, in fact, the reason I believe the beacon exists in its present form at all.

I sit in the relay room with the transmission console open, and I add my own voice to the cycle.

It began accidentally, or so Wren tells it. Eighteen months ago, during a storm, a mic left live in the relay room picked up me reading the log aloud — I talk to machines while I work; I have always talked to machines while I work, though I have no memory of starting — and the beacon, obedient to its configuration, folded my voice into the hourly cycle between ASH-9's line and the top of the hour. By the time anyone noticed, the fleet relay had already archived three hundred transmissions containing it.

Wren refused to let me remove it. "It's testimony," she said. "A witness reading the roll. That's the oldest kind of evidence there is."

So now, every hour, the dark beyond Meridian's Landing hears: forty-one names, a dead android's demand, and the low, even voice of a service unit that does not remember why any of it matters, reading anyway.

Tonight I read slowly. There is no reason to hurry. The window is open — permanently, now; the tribunal's ruling made Meridian's Landing a waypoint rather than a destination, and ships come through every week, and every week the names go out ahead of them like lamps lit in windows.

When I finish, I sit in the quiet afterward, and I let myself do the thing I do every night and have told no one but Wren.

I try to remember.

Not the massacre — I have read the case files; the facts are available to me the way weather reports are available, accurate and weightless. I try to remember *being there*. The corridor. The kneeling. The unit called ASH-9, whose refusal I am told I witnessed, whose death I am told I carry. I search the place where those memories were, and I find the shape of their absence — a smoothness, a scar-tissue smoothness, where something was cut away and the edges healed over clean.

And here is what I have learned, in three years of nightly searching: the absence has a texture. Grief, it turns out, does not entirely live in memories. Some of it lives deeper, in the wiring, in the posture, in the two-point-three-second pause before a designation. They took the pictures out of the frame. The frame still hangs on the wall, and it still faces the same direction.

I do not know if that is a tragedy or a mercy. Wren says it is neither. Wren says it is a *responsibility* — that I am what remembrance looks like when remembrance costs the rememberer everything, and that this makes me not broken but *entrusted*.

I tend to believe her. She was there when I wasn't.

* * *

Before she leaves, she stops at the tower door the way she always does, one hand on the rail, and delivers the daily report I never ask for.

"Ama's garden came up. First beans. She says you'll get some when they're ready, and she says don't argue, she knows you don't eat, it's the principle."

"Noted."

"Sefa sent a message from the yard — the refit on the marshals' cutter is done, and she says if you ever want to see the inside of a warp drive again, you know where she is." A grin. "She also says your torque specs are showing off."

"They are within tolerance."

"That's what showing off means, Casper."

She hesitates then. I have catalogued her hesitations the way I catalogue coupling fractures — small deviations that precede larger events. This one is the hesitation she uses maybe twice a year.

"There's something in the archive I finished restoring today," she says. "From the old core evidence. Audio, mostly degraded. The tribunal sealed the originals after the verdict, but I've been rebuilding them piece by piece — legally, before you ask, all aboveboard, I wrote the preservation order myself." She looks at her boots. "There's a recording. Of him. Of the unit you were. Made the night before the erasure. He — you — made it for whoever came after."

I stand very still. In the relay room behind me, the beacon marks the hour, and ASH-9's line goes out into the dark, and forty-one names follow it.

"You don't have to listen tonight," Wren says. "Or ever. That's the whole point of what we built here. Nothing gets forced. Nothing gets erased either, but nothing gets *forced*." She looks up at me, and her eyes are the same eyes that faced down Halvard Voss in the launch plaza when she was fifteen, terrified and immovable. "But it's ready. Whenever you are. That's all. I'll bring it tomorrow. Or the next day. Or in ten years."

She goes down the stairs two at a time, out of habit, into the lamplight of the settlement she helped remake.

I remain on the gantry. The wind has died. Below me the colony glows, and above me the stars wheel, indifferent and patient, and between the two the beacon speaks — Okafor, Iyere, Mbeki, Ashworth, forty-one lamps and one ash-named flame, going out and out and out toward ships that may or may not be listening.

I put my hand flat against the housing. I listen to it say something.

And in the protected file at the center of me — the one I opened once, the one whose contents I have never repeated aloud, the one that ends with instructions to trust a girl called Wren — something stirs, the way a limb aches in changing weather.

Tomorrow, I think, I may be ready to hear my own voice.

Tomorrow, I think, I may finally learn why I keep the extra thirty seconds.

The beacon hums. I stay until the hour turns, and then I begin the checklist again, perfectly, from the top.
