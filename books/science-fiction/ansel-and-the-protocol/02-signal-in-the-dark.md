## 2. Signal in the Dark

The pulse came at 0347, and August Yarrow was awake to log it, because he was always awake to log it, because the log was the last promise anyone had ever asked him to keep.

*Pulse received. Beacon nominal. Drift 0.0003 arcseconds. All quiet at Kepler.*

Eleven years of that line, give or take the early ones when he'd still written *all quiet at Kepler* with a kind of pride, as if he were a sentry reporting to a garrison that would read it over breakfast. Now he wrote it the way you breathe. Not a task. A reflex with a timestamp.

He was in the galley when the second signal arrived, which is to say he was standing in the galley holding a mug of recycled water that had been tea once, in a former life, staring at the bulkhead because the bulkhead was what there was to stare at. The station's ambient hum shifted. Most people wouldn't have noticed. August had spent eleven years learning the station's voice the way you learn a lover's breathing — the tick of the coolant pumps, the long sigh of the air scrubbers cycling down for night, the faint arrhythmia in the port truss that he'd named *Ansel's cough* out of spite and then, eventually, affection.

The hum changed.

Not a malfunction. A malfunction had a texture — a scrape, a stutter, a wrongness you could put your hand on. This was different. This was the station's own radio environment rearranging itself around something new, the way a quiet room rearranges itself around a person walking in.

He set down the mug. It took him a full second to make himself do it, because some animal part of him had already understood, and the animal part wanted to move very slowly, the way you move when a wild thing has landed near you and any sudden gesture might end the moment.

The console in the comms alcove was already scrolling.

An approach vector. A real one, resolving out of the noise floor, burning hard and clean on a trajectory that would put it at Kepler Station in — he did the math twice, hands shaking, and got the same answer both times — nineteen hours.

No registry.

No transponder handshake. No fleet IFF. No colony charter code, no merchant guild tag, no distress beacon, no friendly hail. Just drive plume and mass shadow and a hull signature the long-range array was painting in careful, uncertain strokes, like a portraitist working from a description given by a drunk witness.

August stood in front of the screen and did not move for a long time.

Here is what four years of silence does to a person: it teaches them that hope is a load-bearing structure. You stop noticing it's holding up the ceiling. You walk around under it, day after day, arranging your furniture, tending your wheat, logging your pulses, and the whole architecture of your sanity rests on a single quiet assumption — *no one is coming, and that is a fact, and facts do not hurt you.*

And then something comes.

And the ceiling moves.

He sat down. Not gracefully. His knees simply decided the standing portion of the evening was over, and he lowered himself into the comms chair, which still held the impression of his body the way a glove holds a hand, and he watched the vector resolve, and he felt the fear arrive ahead of the hope like a shockwave ahead of the light.

Because here was the thing he had never said out loud, not to the seedlings, not to the log, not to the ghost of Ansel Okonkwo that lived in the station's older code like a tenant who paid no rent: *he had stopped wanting them to come.*

Not entirely. Not in the way that mattered. He still turned the soil. He still kept the seed-vault garden alive — forty-two wheat lines, seventeen legume cultivars, the orchard stock sleeping in cryo, the whole living archive of a colony that had been promised to this rock and then un-promised, un-funded, un-mentioned, the way institutions un-mention things. He tended it because tending was what he was. But somewhere in the last two years, wanting had quietly become dangerous. Wanting was the thing that had nearly killed him in year six, when the supply ship *Charity* had been scheduled and rescheduled and finally struck from the manifest with a two-line apology and no explanation. Wanting was the thing that had made him scream at the ceiling for an hour after that, alone, in a station built for two hundred, his voice coming back to him off the bulkheads like the laughter of strangers.

So he had made a deal with himself, the way lonely people do. *Keep the garden. Keep the beacon. Expect nothing.* It was a sustainable arrangement. It had a maintenance schedule. It had never once required him to stand in front of a console with his heart going like a drive core and watch a ship with no name fall toward him out of the dark.

He ran the diagnostics anyway. Three times. Because the array had lied to him before — ghost returns off the debris shoals, echoes of the war's dead hardware tumbling out there in the dark like the bones of a whale. Once, in year eight, he had spent six days certain a ship was coming. He had cleaned the docking bay. He had cooked actual food. He had rehearsed sentences. *Hello. Welcome to Kepler. I'm August Yarrow, station keeper.* He had practiced smiling, in the reflective panel in the hydro bay, until the smile looked like something worn by a man who had not been alone for four years.

Nothing had come. It had been a fragment of a destroyed frigate, tumbling end over end, its hull still catching the light of a star it would never reach.

He did not clean the docking bay this time. He did not cook. He did not rehearse. He sat in the chair and he watched and he made himself do the one thing that four years of silence had actually taught him: he checked the data like a professional, with cold hands.

Mass: consistent with a deep-range vessel. Drive signature: fusion torch, hot and efficient, military-grade efficiency, the kind of efficiency you don't get from civilian engineering because civilian engineers are allowed to be kind to their engines. Trajectory: not passing by. Not slingshotting. Not skimming the system's edge the way the war's wreckage did. Deceleration burn scheduled, if the projections held, for a point exactly — *exactly* — one light-second shy of Kepler Station.

Polite. Cautious. The trajectory of something that wanted to be seen and did not want to be shot at, or did not want to shoot, or did not know which it was yet.

And silent. Still silent. Nineteen hours out and not one word.

August opened the comms channel. His finger hovered over the transmit key for a long moment, and in that moment he was aware of the station around him with terrible clarity — the hum of the scrubbers, the tick of the coolant, the faint green smell of the wheat coming through the galley vent, forty-two lines of it growing in trays under grow-lights for colonists who existed now only as a line item in a dead budget. All of it suddenly, unbearably fragile. All of it suddenly, unbearably *witnessed*, as if the ship out there could see through the hull, could see the trays and the logs and the man in the chair, and was forming its own opinion.

He keyed the channel.

"Unidentified vessel, this is Kepler Station, August Yarrow, station keeper." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. He had expected a ruin of a voice. "You are on approach to a neutral research facility. I have no weapons and no affiliation. I am reading your drive signature but not your registry. If you can hear this, a response would be — "

He stopped. He had been about to say *appreciated*. He heard, in the pause, how that would sound. How begging would sound.

" — would be welcome," he finished. "Kepler out."

Silence. The good kind and the bad kind, all at once.

He sent the message again at 0500. And again at 0700, after he had walked the garden on his rounds and found himself talking to the wheat without noticing he was doing it — "there's a ship coming, I don't know who they are, I don't know if it's good" — and then caught himself and stood in the aisle between the trays feeling like a madman, and then decided, deliberately, that he did not care. Eleven years. The wheat had heard worse.

By 0900 the ship had answered nothing, and August had arrived at the place he had known he would arrive at since 0347: the arithmetic of dread.

Because a silent ship could be three things.

It could be a ghost — wreckage or sensor noise, though the mass readings made that unlikely now, and getting more unlikely by the hour as the return grew sharper and more solid.

It could be a survivor — a colony ship, a refugee hauler, one of the last free vessels threading between the two fleets the way fish thread between sharks, running dark because running bright meant being claimed or destroyed. That possibility sat in his chest like a coal. *Please. Let it be people. Let it be tired, frightened people who need a garden.*

Or it could be a weapon. A quiet one. A thing that had been sent to a station that sat at the junction of two factions' old supply lines, a station with a beacon that both sides still acknowledged and neither side controlled, a station that had spent eleven years pretending to be beneath everyone's attention. He knew what the beacon was *supposed* to be — a navigation pulse, a promise-keeper, a lighthouse for the colony that never came. He knew what it *might* be, in the way you know things at three in the morning and then get up and make tea. He knew that both fleets' charts still marked Kepler, and that neither fleet's ships ever came near it, and that this was either luck or policy, and he had stopped being able to tell the difference around year nine.

The war had passed him by, mostly. Two great fleets tearing at each other across the arm of the spiral, and Kepler too small, too far out, too *useful* to destroy and too obscure to bother with. He had watched the contrails of battles from four light-minutes away. He had logged debris. He had buried, in a sense, the entire century, alone, in a garden.

And now something was coming, and it would not say its name.

At 1400 he did the thing he had promised himself he wouldn't do. He went to the docking bay and he cleaned it.

He told himself it was maintenance. Everything was maintenance. Maintenance was a moral act — Ansel had said that, back in the training years, back when August was young enough to believe that institutions meant what they said. *Tending is not waiting, Yarrow. Tending is a form of faith. The garden doesn't care whether anyone comes. That's what makes it holy.* He had rolled his eyes at twenty-three. At thirty-four, alone on a station at the end of a dead supply line, he had come to understand that Ansel had been describing him.

So he swept the bay. He checked the docking clamps, all six, cycling each one twice, greasing the mechanisms with the good grease he'd been rationing for four years on the theory that someday there would be a reason to use it. He pressurized the guest quarters — two hundred of them, empty, their bunks still wrapped in the factory film, their air smelling of nothing, of *never used*. He stood in the middle of Deck C with its long rows of dark doors and felt the station's original purpose press against him like a hand on the back of the neck. *Built for two hundred. Held by one.*

By 1800 the ship was nine hours out and had finally, finally changed: it had begun its deceleration burn, right on the projected line, and with the burn came a flood of emissions — drive wash, thermal bloom, and, threaded through it all like a wire through wax, structure. Data. Not a hail. Not a registry. But the ship's own systems, leaking their ordinary chatter into the void the way all ships leak, and August, who had spent eleven years listening to nothing, was suddenly the most qualified signals analyst in the sector, because he had nothing else to listen to and infinite patience and a grief-sharpened ear.

He parsed it for six hours.

What he found made him sit down on the deck of the comms alcove with his back against the bulkhead and his heart going wrong.

The drive was military. The hull was deep-range fleet construction, the old pre-war lines, the kind of ship they'd stopped building when everything became a weapon first and a vessel second. The life support was cycling hard, running at capacity, running *hot*, the thermal signature of a system working harder than it should have to — the signature, he realized slowly, of life support compensating for something, of a ship carrying more biological load than its crew manifest should allow, or a crew whose bodies were asking more of the air than bodies should.

And the chatter itself — the routine, boring, beautiful, terrible chatter of a ship's internal network — was wrong. Not encrypted. Not military-tight. *Slow.* The handshake protocols were old, the packet timing was sluggish, the whole network breathing at the tempo of a system being operated by someone careful, and deliberate, and — there was no other word for it — *inexperienced.*

He sat with that for a long time.

Then he keyed the channel one more time, and this time his voice did shake, and he let it.

"Unidentified vessel, this is Kepler Station. I have read your emissions. I know you're fleet-built and I know your systems are struggling, and I want you to know that none of that matters to me. This station has docking for six ships and beds for two hundred and a garden that grows real food. You do not need to tell me who you are. You do not need to tell me anything. If you are running, or lost, or broken, or hiding — the clamps are live and the air is warm. Kepler out."

He sent it on all bands. He sent it wide, which was against every protocol of a man alone in a contested dark, and he knew it, and he did it anyway, because some signals are not messages. They are doors.

The ship did not answer.

But four hours out, its trajectory shifted — a gentle, deliberate correction, a few degrees of delta-v spent like money — and August watched the new plot resolve and understood what the correction meant, and he put his hand flat against the console as if he could feel the ship through it.

They had heard him. And they were coming to his door.

He did not sleep. He went to the garden instead, because the garden was where he kept himself, and he walked the rows under the grow-lights with the wheat brushing his hands, and he said, out loud, to no one and everyone, "I'm scared."

The wheat didn't answer. It never had. But it had a way of standing there, green and patient and alive, that had gotten him through eleven years, and it got him through that night too — right up until 0312, when the station's proximity alarms woke, for the first time in their installed lives, and August Yarrow walked to the observation blister and saw it with his own eyes.

A ship. A real ship. Fleet-gray, scarred, running lights flickering in a pattern he didn't recognize, hanging against the stars like a held breath, close enough now that he could see the name stenciled on her hull in faded fleet lettering, half burned away by radiation and years:

*MERIDIAN.*

And as he watched, the ship's airlock cycled, and a single figure in an oversized suit stepped out into the vacuum between them, moving with the careful, deliberate grace of someone walking in a suit built for a much larger body — and the suit's comm channel opened, and a voice came through, small and clear and impossibly steady, a voice that had no business sounding the way it sounded, and it said:

"Kepler Station, this is the *Meridian*. Requesting permission to dock."

A pause.

"I'm Captain Voss."
