## 9. Two Flags

The fluctuation lasted four seconds.

August caught it because he caught everything — four years of watching the beacon's pulse the way other men watched their own heartbeat. The signal stuttered, a half-tone drop in the carrier wave, and then steadied again as if nothing had happened. But the logs didn't lie. At 0347 station time, Beacon Kepler-9 had hiccupped for the first time in eleven years.

He sat in the dark of the comms room with his coffee going cold and did the math he didn't want to do. The beacon's signal propagated at lightspeed. The Concordat picket at the Verge was nine light-hours out. The Assembly listening post at Thessaly was eleven. Which meant that somewhere out there, right now, two rooms full of people were looking at the same four-second stutter and reaching for the same red phones.

"Both of them," he said aloud, to no one. "They'll both come."

Ilsa was asleep in the medbay cot, or pretending to be. The children — the crew, he kept correcting himself, the *crew* — had claimed the galley and the observation deck and were sleeping in shifts, small bodies curled in oversized bunks like seeds in furrows too deep for them. He'd learned their rhythms in six days. Chief Engineer Okafor woke at 0400 sharp, no matter what, because some part of her forty-year-old mind still kept the watch. Doctor Reyes slept with the lights on. Ilsa slept like a soldier, which was to say instantly and lightly, and woke like one too.

He heard her footsteps on the ladder before he heard her voice.

"You felt it." She dropped into the chair beside him, feet not quite reaching the floor, and pulled up the waveform without being shown. Twelve years old. Reading a signal analysis like a morning paper.

"Four-second dropout. Self-corrected. It shouldn't have been possible — the beacon doesn't stutter. It's not built to."

"It's not built to," Ilsa agreed. "But it's been running for eleven years on a reactor you told me is held together with prayer and spite. Something degraded. And something out there noticed." She tapped the edge of the display, where August had already plotted the two contacts. Two drives igniting, nine and eleven light-hours out, both burning hard toward Kepler. "Concordat corvette, Assembly frigate. They'll be here in — what, thirty hours? Thirty-six?"

"Thirty-one and thirty-four. They'll arrive four hours apart."

"Four hours." Ilsa laughed, and it was an old woman's laugh coming out of a child's mouth, and August had to look away from it. "Enough time for the first one to demand we swear allegiance before the second one arrives to shoot us for it."

"That's the shape of it, yes."

She was quiet for a moment. In the porthole, the stars did what stars do, which is nothing, which is everything. "August. Which one do we hail?"

And there it was. The question he'd been turning over since 0347, the one with no clean face on any side of it.

* * *

He laid it out for the crew in the galley at 0600, because they deserved to hear it from him and not from a display. Eleven of them now — eleven children in a room built for forty adults, sitting on stacked crates so they could reach the table. Okafor at the head, because Ilsa had given her the head and because Okafor at six years old had the gravity of a woman who'd once held a fusion torch steady through a hull breach.

"Two ships inbound," August said. "One from each side. They've seen the beacon fluctuate, and they're going to assume the worst — that the other side is moving on the station, or that we're compromised, or that this is the opening move of something. When they get here, they're going to want answers. And they're going to want them in a way that proves whose side we're on."

"So we don't pick," said Reyes, the doctor, who was nine now and had to stand on a crate to see the tactical plot.

"There's no such thing as not picking." August pulled up the map. "If I hail the Concordat first, the Assembly reads it as Kepler defecting. If I hail the Assembly, same thing in reverse. If I hail neither, they both assume I'm hiding something — which, to be fair, I am. If I hail both simultaneously on an open channel, they each assume the other got a private message first. There is no configuration of radio waves that says *I am neutral* to people who have spent eleven years teaching themselves that neutrality is a weapon."

"Because it is," Okafor said. "Neutral ground is just ground nobody's fortified yet." She said it flatly, the way children say terrible things they remember being true.

"So what do we do?" That was Vashti, the youngest, five years old, comms officer, who still sucked her thumb when she thought no one was looking and who could decrypt a fleet cipher in her sleep.

Ilsa stood. She had to stand on her chair to be seen over the crates, and she did it without a flicker of self-consciousness, and August watched eleven children straighten like plants toward light.

"We do the thing nobody expects," she said. "We tell the truth. Both of them, at once, on open channel — not a hail, a *broadcast*. The beacon fluctuation, the reactor status, the fact that we have a crew that fleet command would very much like to disappear. We make ourselves boring. We make ourselves a maintenance problem, not a strategic one. Nobody wants to fire the first shot of the next war at a station full of children filing reactor reports."

"And if they don't want boring?" Okafor asked.

Ilsa's jaw set. "Then we'll know exactly who we're dealing with. And so will everyone else, because it'll all be on the open channel."

It was a good plan. August had spent the walk down from the comms room trying to build a better one and failing. It was also, he understood, a plan that only worked if the truth was still small enough to be boring — if all they broadcast was a stutter and a reactor report and not the thing Ilsa carried in her skull, the thing that would turn two corvettes into an armada.

He didn't say that part. Neither did she. But he caught her eye across the galley, and the look they exchanged was the look of two people carrying the same secret up a staircase, each pretending the stairs weren't creaking.

* * *

The broadcast went out at 0900. August wrote it himself, three drafts, each one stripped barer than the last, until it read like what it was: a station log. *Kepler-9 beacon experienced a four-second carrier fluctuation at 0347. Cause under investigation. Reactor operating at reduced margin. Station crew: twelve. Requesting no assistance at this time. Beacon pulse remains nominal.*

He signed it with his name and his old fleet designation and the word *civilian* after it, like a man holding up empty hands.

For six hours, nothing. The children went about the work of keeping the station alive — Okafor down in the reactor gallery with August's annotated manuals, Reyes restocking a medbay whose shelves she could no longer reach, the others running the small endless maintenance of a habitat that had only ever needed one pair of hands and now had eleven small ones. August taught them how the air scrubbers cycled, how the water reclamation loop sang when it was happy, how to read the garden's humidity sensors. Vashti followed him into the greenhouse and asked why he talked to the wheat.

"Because everything alive does better when it's spoken to," he said, and then heard himself, and heard Ansel in it, thirty years downstream, and had to turn and check a gauge that didn't need checking.

At 1512, the Concordat answered.

The message was polite the way a drawn blade is polite. *Station Kepler-9, this is Concordat patrol vessel Stern Watch. Your beacon fluctuation has been logged. Maintain current posture. A boarding and inspection team will arrive with our vessel. Cooperation is expected. Acknowledge.*

"Acknowledge," August read aloud. "Not *requested*. Expected."

"They're going to board you," Ilsa said. She'd been standing at his shoulder for the whole exchange, and he could feel the tension coming off her like heat off the reactor housing. "Inspect. And find a twelve-year-old captain and a crew of children who don't exist in any registry, and a beacon that just stuttered for the first time in a decade. They'll have questions we can't answer without —"

"Without the truth."

"Without the truth." She said it like a weight being set down. "August. When they board, I can't be here. If the Concordat sees me — if they run my biometrics — Ilsa Voss, captain of the Meridian, listed missing with all hands eleven years ago, suddenly twelve years old? That's not a question anymore. That's a *prize*. They won't ask what happened to us. They'll ask how to make it happen to *their* people. And then the Assembly finds out we're here, and —"

"Then we hide you."

"Hide eleven of us?"

"Hide the fact that you're *who you are*. The station has old maintenance tunnels, cold storage, the greenhouse infrastructure. I've spent eleven years learning every meter of this station. I can make twelve people vanish into its bones for four hours."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. "You've thought about hiding people before."

He hadn't. But Ansel had — Ansel had built hiding places into Kepler's bones the way he'd built everything else, redundancies inside redundancies, and August had never known why until this week. "The station's designer was a paranoid man," he said. "Turns out he had reason."

At 1903, the Assembly answered too.

Theirs was not polite. *Station Kepler-9, this is Assembly vessel Vigilant Truth. We are aware a Concordat vessel is inbound to your position. Any cooperation with Concordat forces will be construed as hostile alignment. Shut down all nonessential emissions and prepare for Assembly inspection. This is your final notice before escalation protocols apply.*

"Escalation protocols," August repeated. The words tasted like copper. "Eleven years I kept this beacon alive for both of them. Eleven years, and the first two messages I ever get are *expected* and *final notice*."

"They're scared," Ilsa said. "Scared people don't write polite."

"Scared people with warships write *final notice*."

* * *

The Stern Watch came in first, at 0330 the next morning, sliding into docking range with the ponderous grace of a ship that had never once been in a hurry. August stood alone in the docking bay to meet it. That had been the plan, argued out over two days: he would meet them alone, a lone civilian keeper, no crew to explain, no children to disappear. The others would be in the tunnels — Ilsa had resisted, had argued that a captain didn't hide from inspections, and August had argued back that a captain's first duty was to her crew, and had watched the argument land on her like a blow, because it was her own doctrine thrown back at her in his older voice.

The airlock cycled. Four figures came through in Concordat gray, rifles slung but hands near them, and at their head a lieutenant with a face like a closed ledger.

"August Yarrow," the lieutenant said, reading from a slate. "Keeper, Kepler Station. Fleet designation 4471-C. Eleven years unrelieved." Her eyes came up. They were the eyes of someone cataloguing. "You've been busy for a man alone."

"Station keeps itself, mostly. I keep the beacon."

"Mm." She walked the bay slowly, and August walked with her, and behind her eyes he could feel the inspection happening — the extra boots in the corridor logs he'd doctored, the galley rations drawn for twelve, the small warm human signatures that a station built for forty couldn't fully hide. "Your beacon stuttered."

"It did. It won't again. I've compensated."

"You've *compensated*." She stopped at the bay's observation port, where Kepler's sunward face showed the greenhouse dome, and August's stomach dropped as he remembered — the greenhouse, visible from orbit, its environmental draw doubled these past six days. "Your life support draw is up forty percent, Keeper Yarrow. Your water reclamation is cycling for triple its rated load. Your galley has consumed rations for twelve." She turned. "You are alone. Where are the other eleven?"

The bay was very quiet. August heard his own heartbeat, and beneath it, faint through the deck plating, the beacon's pulse — steady, steady, the metronome he'd kept for eleven years, keeping him.

"Refugees," he said, and it was the truth, or the shape of it. "Derelict survivors. I found them six days ago. I haven't reported them yet because I don't know which side to report them *to*."

The lieutenant's expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts before it cracks. "And which side would that be, Keeper?"

"That's the problem, Lieutenant. They're not either side. They're — "

The comms panel on the wall chirped. Twice. The two-tone alert he'd set for one thing only: the Assembly frigate, burning in four hours ahead of schedule, its transponder now broadcasting the one phrase every spacer in the system knew and dreaded.

*Escalation protocols engaged.*

The lieutenant heard it too. Her hand moved to her rifle, not toward August — toward the threat she'd been trained her whole life to expect, arriving exactly as trained. "Keeper," she said, and her voice had changed, the ledger opening, "I'm going to ask you one more time, and then this stops being an inspection."

Out in the dark, the Vigilant Truth's drive flare grew brighter. And in the walls of the station, in cold tunnels Ansel Okonkwo had built for a war he'd seen coming, eleven children waited, and August Yarrow stood between two flags with nothing in his hands, and understood at last that the beacon had never been the only thing on this station wired to go off.

"I'm listening," he said.
