## 10. Ansel

The dream always started with the smell of solder.

August stood in the training bay at Corvus Station, twenty-two years old and certain he already knew everything, and the air smelled like flux and hot metal and, underneath it, the green tang of the hydroponics annex that shared a wall with the electronics lab. Eleven years on Kepler had scrubbed most things out of his memory. That smell had stayed. He suspected it would outlive him.

"Yarrow. You're soldering like a man who hates the joint."

Ansel Okonkwo stood behind him, hands clasped at the small of his back, watching August's iron wobble over a relay board. He was a broad, slow-moving man with grey at his temples and reading glasses he only wore when he wanted you to think he wasn't paying attention. Fleet uniform, but worn wrong — sleeves rolled, collar open, the rank tab on his shoulder so faded it might have been decorative.

"It's holding," August said.

"It's holding *now*. Solder isn't about now. Nothing you maintain is ever about now." Ansel leaned in, took the iron from August's hand with the unhurried certainty of a surgeon, and reflowed the joint in one motion. "A joint either fails in ten years or it doesn't fail. There's no in-between. You're not fixing a relay, Yarrow. You're making a promise to a person you will never meet, who will open this panel at three in the morning, in the dark, scared, and they will find your work either sound or not sound. That's the whole job."

August had heard the speech before. Everyone in the maintenance track had heard the speech. It was a running joke in the barracks — *make a promise to a person you'll never meet* — and they'd turned it into a drinking toast, glasses raised to the invisible three-a.m. stranger.

He wasn't laughing now, in the dream. He wasn't laughing then, either, though he'd pretended to.

"Say it back," Ansel said.

"Maintenance is a moral act."

"Maintenance is *the* moral act. Everything else is commentary. Anyone can be brave for a day. Show me a person who keeps a seal greased for thirty years and I'll show you a saint." Ansel set the iron down and looked at him over the glasses, and for a moment the lazy humor drained out of his face and left something older and much more tired. "The fleet will tell you your job is beneath the war. The pilots get the parades. The admirals get the statues. You will spend your whole life keeping things running so other people can break them. Don't let them make you bitter about it. Bitterness is how a maintainer starts cutting corners."

"And if the thing I'm keeping running is wrong?"

The question came out of August before he could stop it — the way questions do when you're twenty-two and the air smells like solder and you have just enough knowledge to be dangerous. Ansel didn't flinch. He took off the glasses, folded them, and put them in his breast pocket, and August would remember later, much later, that he'd done it the way a man puts away a weapon.

"Then you have a much harder job," Ansel said. "And you'll know it when you have it, because you won't be able to sleep."

* * *

The dream moved the way dreams do, without transition, and August was in the archive annex two years later, pulling maintenance schematics for the beacon program, and Ansel was there, and it wasn't a dream anymore, it was memory, and memory had weight.

It was the last year of the war's first phase. The armistice talks were a rumor everyone pretended not to believe. August had been assigned to Kepler Station's prep crew — the beacon was his to commission, his first solo posting, and he'd been walking around for a week with the quiet, unbearable pride of a man trusted with something important.

Ansel had come to the annex uninvited. He'd pulled a chair up to August's terminal without asking, the way he did, and sat reading over his shoulder while August worked through the beacon's authentication stack.

"You wrote some of this," August said. It wasn't a question. Ansel's style was unmistakable — the same unhurried redundancy he brought to solder joints, every critical function built three times, no single point of failure, no single point of *truth*.

"I wrote some of it."

"Why does a beacon need a dead-man's switch?" August had pulled the layer up on his display, frowning. "It's a lighthouse. Lighthouses don't threaten people."

Ansel was quiet long enough that August turned around.

"Everything we build gets conscripted eventually," Ansel said. "You build a hammer, someone makes it a weapon. So the ones of us who build — we have a choice. We can pretend that's not going to happen, or we can build for the day it does." He tapped the display, on the switch's trigger condition, the silence clause. "This isn't a threat, Yarrow. It's a *test*. It's the fleet being forced to keep telling the truth, forever, out loud, or lose the thing they built. I made them marry the beacon. They wanted a tripwire; I gave them a conscience."

"You gave them a bomb."

"I gave them a mirror." Ansel's voice didn't rise. It never rose. That was the thing about him that command had never learned to handle — he could not be provoked, only *outlasted*. "If both sides know that silence means fire, then both sides have to keep the beacon fed, and to keep it fed they have to keep the station staffed, and to keep it staffed they have to admit it exists, and to admit it exists they have to admit what it's for. I hid the truth in the architecture. It's the only place they never look. Command reads the reports. They never read the code."

August stared at the display. At the bottom of the authentication stack, in a comment field that would never be read by anyone who mattered, was a string of characters: *AO-keeps-time*.

"Your initials are in my beacon."

"Your beacon." Ansel smiled, and it was the saddest thing August had seen in twenty-two years, though he didn't know yet why. "Good. Then it's in your hands now. Remember the three a.m. stranger."

He stood, pushed the chair back in — he always pushed the chair back in — and left the annex, and that was the last time August saw him for four months.

* * *

The disappearance was not called a disappearance. That was the first thing August learned about how institutions grieve: they don't. Ansel Okonkwo was *reassigned*. Then he was *on extended leave*. Then his name was gone from the duty rosters, and his quarters were struck, and his file in the training archive returned a polite access-denied, and the man who had taught half the maintenance corps everything they knew became, in the space of one administrative cycle, a rumor.

August asked questions. That was the mistake. He asked his posting officer, who looked at him with genuine pity. He asked a friend in signals, who told him to stop asking in writing. He asked, finally, the one person who might answer — Commander Reyes, who had served under Ansel during the Meridian shakedown, and who took him aside into a supply bay where the surveillance coverage had a documented gap, because of course Ansel had taught Reyes about the coverage gaps too.

"They asked him to sign off on the beacon's final certification," Reyes said. "He wouldn't. Not the way they wrote it. He wanted the trigger conditions changed — he wanted the silence clause to require a *witness*, some independent party, before retaliation armed. They said no. He said he'd built a failsafe into the code anyway and they could court-martial him or live with it. They chose the third option."

"Which is?"

"You're standing in it, Yarrow. The third option is that men like Ansel Okonkwo become a story we tell each other in supply bays." Reyes put a hand on his shoulder. "He questioned command. That's not a crime. But he questioned command *correctly*, with evidence, in writing, and that terrifies them more than mutiny ever could. Mutiny they can shoot. A man who's right, they have to erase."

August stood in the supply bay among the crates and the cable spools and felt the floor tilt under a certainty he hadn't earned yet: that one day, on some station, in some decade, he would be the three a.m. stranger. That the promise would come due.

He was twenty-four. He thought he had time.

* * *

The dream broke there, as it always did, and August came awake in his bunk on Kepler with the reactor's hum in the walls and Ilsa Voss's voice still hanging in his memory from the night before — *the code, August, the code, there's something wrong with the code* — and he lay in the dark and understood that the dream had not come to haunt him.

It had come to brief him.

He got up. He dressed. He walked the long corridor to the beacon room where the girl-captain sat cross-legged on the deck with a portable terminal balanced on her knees, surrounded by printouts because the station's displays were all committed to the countdown, and she looked up at him with a twelve-year-old's face and a forty-year-old's exhaustion.

"I found something," she said. "In the authentication stack. A comment field. It repeats — it's not random, it's a signature, and it's threaded through the whole dead-man's logic like a spine."

August sat down beside her. His hands were steady. He had spent eleven years making them steady, and now was what they'd been made for.

"Read it," he said.

Ilsa turned the terminal toward him. On the screen, in a comment field no one who mattered would ever read, the characters stood in a row:

*AO-keeps-time.*

The floor of the station hummed. Somewhere below them, the reactor counted down a number it hadn't finished choosing. And August Yarrow, keeper of a garden for no one, maintainer of a promise he'd only just now recognized as his own, heard a slow voice across eleven years and half a war: *You'll know it when you have it, because you won't be able to sleep.*

"Ansel Okonkwo," August said. "He built this. And they erased him for it."

Ilsa watched him, waiting. Outside the viewport, the dark of contested space was beginning, very faintly, to fill with the drive-flares of ships that had seen the beacon flicker.

"Tell me everything you remember about him," she said. "Because I think what he hid in here — I think it's the same thing I'm carrying. And I think we're going to need both."
