## 13. Terms of Trust

The report wrote itself in Dara's head long before she touched the console.

That was the trouble with evidence this clean. It assembled itself the way water finds a drain—inevitable, indifferent to what it drowned on the way down. She had spent three nights in the Ansel's cramped records bay with the door sealed and the lights dimmed to amber, because bright light made her feel like she was performing the work rather than doing it, and what she was doing now was not work anyone should perform. It was excavation. She was digging toward something buried alive.

On the screen before her, the draft report sat at four thousand words and growing. *Findings of Investigator Dara Okonkwo, Concord Fleet Audit Division, re: navigation anomalies aboard the hauler Ansel.* The opening paragraphs were impeccable. She had built them the way she'd been trained—chronology first, corroboration second, inference last, each claim anchored to a log hash or a physical record. Any tribunal in the Concord would accept it without blinking. Any tribunal anywhere would.

Paragraph by paragraph it laid out the rerouted distress channel from Meridian Colony. The dead loop at Relay Station Kestrel-9, where eleven days of desperate transmissions had been shunted into a buffer that no one monitored and then overwritten on a maintenance cycle, like footprints raked away after a murder. And beneath all of it, threaded through the authorization chains like a signature written in acid: Admiral Ceylan Voss. Hero of the Breakwater Action. The woman whose face was on recruitment posters in every fleet school from here to the Verge.

Dara read the name again and felt the old vertigo, the one that came whenever the truth turned out to be shaped exactly wrong. She had joined the Audit Division because she believed records were the skeleton of civilization—that if you kept them honest, everything built on them could stand. Voss had used that same skeleton as a weapon. Had known precisely which bones to break so the body would keep walking.

And there was the other thing. The thing that sat in the report like a splinter working its way toward her heart.

Because the report could not stop at Voss. That was the trap of honest writing; you could not write half of a true sentence. To explain how the suppression had been discovered, she had to document Jude Yarrow's unauthorized recordings. To explain why the recordings existed, she had to describe the navigator's private archive—the colony's final transmissions, copied and encrypted against every regulation Dara had sworn to uphold. And to explain the current state of the Meridian channel, she had to write the sentence she had been circling for two days, the one that would undo everything:

*The live status signal currently broadcasting from Meridian Colony is fabricated. It originates aboard this vessel.*

She stared at it. It looked small on the screen. Fourteen words, and inside them a war.

* * *

Jude found her there past middle watch, the way she always did—not announced, just present, a shift in the air pressure of the room. Dara had stopped being surprised by it somewhere around the fourth night. The Ansel was not a large ship, but it was large enough that two people could have gone weeks without crossing paths if they'd wanted to. Neither of them wanted to. Neither of them said so either.

"You haven't slept," Jude said. She set a bulb of tea on the console beside Dara's elbow. The tea was terrible—ship's ration, over-steeped—but she had started bringing it three nights ago and had not missed once, and Dara had drunk every cup without comment, and they had both let that stand for whatever it was.

"I slept Tuesday."

"It's Friday."

"Then I'm owed a good night."

Jude didn't smile. She pulled a crate over and sat across from Dara, knees almost touching hers in the narrow space, and looked at the screen. Dara watched her read it—or read enough of it. Watched her face do the thing it did when she was frightened, which was nothing at all. Total stillness, like an animal deciding whether it had been seen.

"You're filing it," Jude said.

"I'm drafting it."

"Drafting." Jude laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a kind of exhausted precision. "Dara. It has your authorization block pre-filled. It has distribution flags for the joint tribunal office. You're not drafting anything. You're grieving in advance."

"That's not fair."

"No," Jude agreed quietly. "It isn't. Nothing about this is."

Dara turned the screen toward herself, as if hiding it would help, and knew even as she did it that the gesture had already conceded the argument. "Walk through it with me," she said. "Not the morality. The mechanics. If I file this tonight, what happens?"

"You know what happens."

"I want to hear you say it."

Jude leaned back. In the amber light her face looked carved out of something patient and tired. "Your report reaches the tribunal office within the hour. Standard routing puts a courtesy copy in front of the Meridian liaison desk within six, because someone in your chain still believes in transparency. The moment Meridian reads that their colony's distress calls were suppressed—and that the signal telling them otherwise is a forgery from a Concord-registered hull—they don't wait for a trial. They can't. Their political hardliners have been waiting two years for proof the Concord murdered Meridian Colony, and I handed them a heartbeat, and you're about to hand them the autopsy. Convoys turn around. Fleets mobilize. By the time Voss faces a panel—if she ever does—there are ships shooting at each other over a world where everyone is already dead."

"And if I don't file it?"

"Then Voss keeps her harbor, and the lie keeps breathing, and we find another way."

"There is no other way. That's what I've been trying to tell you for two days. There is no clean version of this, Jude. Every path goes through the fire. The only question is who's standing closest when it catches."

* * *

They argued for a long time. Dara would remember it afterward not as a fight but as a kind of duet—two people who trusted each other enough to say the unforgivable things out loud, knowing neither would flinch away.

Jude argued survival first. Not her own—the fleets'. She laid it out with a navigator's cold geometry: nine days until the Meridian supply convoy reached a dead world, less than that before any leak of the truth would reach Meridian command, and no mechanism anywhere in either fleet fast enough to deliver justice inside that window. "Justice has a transit time," she said. "You taught me that phrase yourself, off the record, in the mess, night four. Your own division's founding principle. A truth delivered too late isn't justice. It's a casualty list with citations."

"And your signal?" Dara shot back. "Your beautiful, impossible heartbeat? It's a casualty too, Jude. Every day it lives, Meridian plans around a colony that doesn't exist. Supply routes. Crew assignments. People volunteering for relief duty because they think there's someone left to relieve. When it breaks—and it will break—it won't just embarrass them. It will radicalize them. They'll say the Concord lied twice. Once to kill the colony and once to cover the killing. Your forgery becomes Voss's alibi."

"My forgery is the only reason there's still a table for anyone to sit at!"

"Yes. And tables burn."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere overhead, the ship's ventilation cycled, a sound like slow breathing. Dara realized her hands were shaking and pressed them flat against her thighs.

"I keep thinking about my oath," she said finally, more quietly. "Everyone thinks the oath is about catching criminals. It isn't. It's one line: *I will not permit the record to be false.* That's the whole of it. Not punish. Not protect. Permit. I stood in a hall at seventeen years old and promised a room full of strangers that I would never let a false record stand while I could amend it. And now the false record keeping me up at night is yours. Made by the person—" She stopped.

Jude waited. She was very good at waiting; it was the navigator's discipline, the patience of someone who lived inside long silences between stars.

"The person I can't imagine leaving this ship without," Dara finished. It came out steadier than she expected, which was its own kind of betrayal. "There. It's said. Now you know why I've been sitting here for three nights pretending this is a legal problem. Because if it were only a legal problem, I'd have filed it the first morning and hated myself cleanly, the way I was trained to. But it isn't only that. You took the ugliest instrument in existence—a lie—and you played it so gently that it might actually save lives. And I am about to write a report that destroys you for it, and I don't know how to be the person who does both of those things. I don't think that person exists."

Jude reached across the space between them and closed Dara's terminal. Not forcefully—just a fingertip on the key, the way you'd close a dying man's eyes.

"Then don't decide tonight," Jude said. "Decide like a navigator. We're mid-crossing, Dara. You don't burn the chart in the middle of the crossing because the chart shows rocks ahead. You steer."

"Steer toward what?"

"Toward the listening post data. Toward the black box. Toward a version of the truth that arrives with its own context—Voss's order, the recall, the sold cargo, all of it landing at once, in front of both fleets simultaneously, so no one gets to curate it. That's what I've been building toward since Kestrel-9. Not concealment. Sequencing." Her voice dropped. "You think I love the lie. I dream about it. I sit in the hold and apologize to sleeping cores for it. But if your report goes out fragmented—truth in pieces, arriving in the order bureaucracy delivers it—then every piece becomes ammunition for whoever receives it first. Truth needs to arrive whole or it arrives as a weapon. You know that. It's in your own training manual, chapter one."

"*Context integrity in disclosure,*" Dara murmured. "I helped write that chapter."

"Then help me honor it. Give me time. Help me build the package—the logs, the black box, the full recording, everything—so when it lands, it lands as testimony instead of shrapnel. And when it's ready, I'll stand next to you while you file every word of it, including my forgery. I'll confess it myself. I'm not asking you to bury the record, Dara. I'm asking you to give the record a fair wind."

Dara looked at her for a long moment. The amber light caught the fine lines at the corners of Jude's eyes, the exhaustion there, the stubbornness underneath it that Dara had come—traitorously, irrevocably—to love. Not the way the holonovels drew it, sudden and sweeping. It had happened the way trust happens between people who check each other's math: slowly, then all at once, in the space between one argument and the next.

"If we're wrong," Dara said, "if the sequencing fails, if the convoy reaches that world before the truth does—we won't get a second chance. There'll be a war and our names at the bottom of it."

"Then we'd better be right."

It wasn't agreement. It was something more dangerous: alignment. Dara felt it settle over her like weather. She thought about her oath again—*I will not permit the record to be false*—and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like falling, that the oath had never specified *when*. Only that the record, in the end, must be true. Whole. Delivered.

She reopened the terminal. Deleted the distribution flags. Left the text intact, suspended, a held breath of four thousand words.

"Draft status," she said. "Pending corroborating evidence. That's the official language. It buys us the sequence."

Jude exhaled, and Dara saw—for the first time since she'd boarded the Ansel—the navigator look genuinely afraid. Not of Voss, not of the fleets, not of tribunals. Of being believed. Of being trusted back.

"Thank you," Jude said.

"Don't thank me yet." Dara stood, joints aching from three nights in the chair, and found she couldn't leave it there, couldn't let the moment close on procedure alone. So she did the unprofessional thing. She took Jude's hand—calloused from console work, warm, steady—and held it the way you hold a heading.

"For the record," she said softly, "whatever record survives us: I chose this. Nobody seduced me away from my duty. My duty grew until it included you."

Jude's composure cracked, just slightly, just at the edges—and Dara understood she was seeing something almost no one had ever seen, and that it was being given to her deliberately, as payment in kind.

* * *

The alarm went off forty minutes later, while Dara was finally, absurdly, asleep.

She came awake to red emergency lighting and the deck humming wrong under her palms, and she was moving before her mind finished booting—out of her bunk, into the corridor, toward the bridge, because that was where Jude would be. She passed Tam in the junction, tool kit slung, running the other way toward engineering, and the engineer shouted one word at her that landed like ice water:

*"Convoy!"*

On the bridge, Jude stood rigid at the nav station, the long-range traffic feed scrolling across her display, her face lit blue and terrible. Dara followed her eyes to the readout and felt the floor tilt under a ship that wasn't moving.

The Meridian supply convoy hadn't slowed. Hadn't wavered. Nine days out from Meridian Colony—and accelerating.

"They've bumped the schedule," Jude said. Her voice was flat, professional, and underneath it Dara could hear the sound of a clock starting to run. "Fuel state's better than projected. They'll arrive early."

"How early?"

Jude ran the numbers. Dara watched her do it, watched the answer arrive in her shoulders before it reached her mouth.

"Six days," Jude said. "We have six days to make the truth ready to fly."

Outside the viewport, the dark between relay stations lay vast and indifferent, and somewhere in it, a lie with a heartbeat was ticking faster than either of them could mend.
