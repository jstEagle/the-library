## 18. Broadcast

The array hummed like a struck bell that had not yet decided to stop.

Jude felt it through the deck plates, through the soles of her boots, through the bones of her hands where they rested on the navigation console. The Ansel's main transmitter had never been built for what Tam was making it do. It had been built to handshake with relay buoys, to file manifests, to whisper routine courtesies across the dark. Now its capacitors were being fed by every spare power cell on the ship, and the whole hull sang with a frequency just below hearing—the sound of something gathering itself.

"Thirty seconds to alignment," Tam said over the intercom, his voice stripped down to its essentials by the static between decks. "Both fleet channels are open. I'm reading Meridian command traffic and Concord high-band both keyed to receive. They're listening, Jude. Both of them. Right now."

Of course they were listening. She had made sure of that. For weeks she had woven her false signal into the fabric of both fleets' certainty—*Meridian Colony lives, Meridian Colony breathes, hold your fire, hold your grief*—and now the lie she had built was about to become the loudest thing either fleet had ever heard. Not because of what she would say. Because of what she would finally stop saying.

She looked at the file sitting in the queue. One file. She had carried it for three years in encrypted fragments, split across drives, disguised as calibration noise, hidden inside the ordinary machinery of her shifts the way grief hides inside professionalism. *Meridian_final_recording.wav.* Four hours and eleven minutes of distress calls. Of names. Of a woman's voice saying *this is Dr. Amara Yarrow, Meridian Colony, we have contamination in the water reclamation loop, we are requesting immediate relief, please acknowledge* — and then, forty minutes later, the same voice thinner: *please acknowledge* — and then, near the end, no protocol at all, just: *Jude. If this ever reaches you, I want you to know I wasn't afraid until the end, and even then mostly I was angry, which I think you'll appreciate.*

And beneath all of it, threaded through the silence where acknowledgment should have been, the black box telemetry from the Vesper. The ship that launched in secret. The ship that was recalled. The relief cargo sold at Ceres Station while her sister counted the days left in her water supply.

Jude's finger hovered over the transmit key.

"You don't have to narrate," Dara said quietly.

Dara stood at the comm station behind her, out of uniform for the first time since Jude had known her—her investigator's insignia folded in her pocket like something already buried. Her tribunal stall with Voss's officers had bought them six hours. Six hours was what Tam needed. Six hours was also, probably, all the time in the universe they were going to get.

"I know," Jude said. "I'm not going to."

She thought about all the versions of this moment she had rehearsed in the dark watches of the night shift. In some of them she gave a speech. In some of them she named Voss first, let the accusation lead, made sure the fleets understood whose hand had been on the channel when Meridian went quiet. In one version—she was not proud of it—she said nothing at all, just let the recording play and vanished into the anonymity she had spent her whole career cultivating, invisible as a navigator, unaccountable as static.

But the dead colony deserved better than a speech, and better than a ghost. It deserved to be heard whole. Unedited. With the silences left in.

"The silences stay in," she said aloud, to Tam, to Dara, to herself. "Don't compress anything. Don't clean up the dropouts. If there's four minutes of nothing between calls, they hear four minutes of nothing. That's the point."

"That's going to be hard to listen to," Tam said.

"It was hard to record."

She pressed the key.

* * *

For the first eleven seconds, nothing happened that anyone could see. The array discharged its gathered charge into the dark in a single wide-band pulse, and somewhere in the Ansel's guts a breaker tripped and reset, and the lights dipped brown and came back. On Jude's console, two delivery confirmations bloomed green almost simultaneously—one tagged CONCORD FLEETNET, one tagged MERIDIAN COMMAND RELAY—and then, within seconds, dozens more, as the broadcast propagated through relay after relay, buoy after buoy, the recording replicating itself across the volume of space where two fleets hung facing each other like drawn blades.

Then the acknowledgments stopped being automatic.

Jude watched the traffic flags change. Routine receipt codes gave way to priority flags. Priority flags gave way to direct voice requests—*Ansel, identify yourself, Ansel, cease transmission, Ansel, who authorized this*—arriving from both fleets in interleaved confusion, Concord and Meridian voices tangled together on frequencies that had not carried each other's words in fourteen years.

On the bridge speakers, because Tam routed everything to the bridge speakers, the recording began to play back through the ship itself.

*This is Dr. Amara Yarrow, Meridian Colony, we have contamination in the water reclamation loop—*

Jude closed her eyes. She had heard it a thousand times. She had never heard it in a room full of people. Tam had come up from engineering and stood at the aft bulkhead with his hands flat against the metal, head bowed, listening the way you listen at a funeral. Dara stood beside Jude's chair, close enough that their shoulders touched, and did not say anything, because there was nothing in any legal code or any language that fit this moment, and Dara Okonkwo had always known the difference between a gap in the law and a gap in the truth.

*Please acknowledge.*

Four minutes of nothing. The Ansel's engines ticked. Somewhere in the hold, ten thousand sleeping backup cores dreamed their slow dreams, unaware that their colony's last voice was moving through the walls around them at the speed of light.

*This is Meridian Colony. We are now rationing at survival minimums. I am recording the names of the dead so that someone, eventually, will know them.*

Names. Forty minutes of names. Jude had memorized them without meaning to, the way you memorize a wound. She listened anyway. Witnessing was not something you could do once and be finished with.

Then the black box telemetry began, and the tone of the channel changed.

She knew the moment it changed because the incoming traffic changed with it. The confused demands from both fleets cut off mid-word. For ninety seconds, the entire volume went silent except for the Vesper's own recorded voice: launch authorization, recall order, the cold administrative language of a cargo offload manifest stamped with a signature Jude had burned into her memory.

**CEYLAN VOSS, ADMIRAL, CONCORD FLEET COMMAND.**

The silence that followed the telemetry was not empty. It was the silence of thirty thousand people on two fleets hearing the same sentence at the same time, and Jude could feel it pressing against the hull like deep water.

"She'll come now," Dara said softly. "Not a tribunal. Not a summons. She'll come herself."

The proximity alert began to scream before Dara finished the sentence.

* * *

Voss's flagship came out of translation burn eight hundred kilometers off the Ansel's bow, and even at that distance it filled the forward viewport like an eclipse—a Concord dreadnought, *Harbor of Law*, her name a joke that had finally curdled. Her running lights blazed full, every gun port illuminated, which was not how warships announced themselves in peacetime. It was how they announced an execution.

"Incoming hail," Tam said. "Flagship, priority absolute. Audio only." He paused. "It's her. Voiceprint confirmed."

"Put her through," Jude said. "Leave the recording playing."

Voss's voice filled the bridge, warm and level and terrible, the voice of a woman who had given eulogies for heroes and meant every second syllable. "Ansel, this is Admiral Ceylan Voss. You are transmitting stolen and falsified materials on fleet-wide channels in violation of the Communications Security Act. Shut down your array, surrender your records, and prepare to be boarded. Do this, and I give you my word—" a pause, perfectly measured, "—that everyone aboard will be treated with the dignity their service deserves."

Jude keyed the response channel. Her hand was steady. She noticed that it was steady and found that strange, the way you notice small things in the last moments before a storm makes small things irrelevant.

"Admiral Voss," she said. "My name is Jude Yarrow. My sister was Dr. Amara Yarrow of Meridian Colony. You recalled the ship that was sent to save her, and you sold its cargo, and you silenced her voice for three years." She let the recording answer for her—in the background, faintly, Amara's voice was saying *please acknowledge* again, looping toward the end of the file, beginning again, because Tam had set it to repeat until manually stopped, and Jude had told him never to stop it. "I'm not shutting anything down. And neither are you. Because if you fire on this ship, every soul in both fleets will know exactly why, and exactly what you were trying to bury when you did it."

A long silence. When Voss spoke again, the warmth was gone, and what remained was worse: precision. "Navigator Yarrow. You are a civilian asset in possession of classified materials. This is a security matter, not a personal one. I take no pleasure in what happens next." A beat. "Weapons free. Target the transmitter array only. I want the ship intact."

"They're charging main batteries," Tam reported, dropping into the engineering seat, fingers flying. "I'm seeing targeting locks on the dorsal array—Jude, if they hit the array while it's hot, the capacitor discharge will cook every data core on this deck. Everything goes. The recording, the black box, the sleepers' life-support backups—all of it."

"Can you shunt it?"

"Not fast enough. Unless—" He stopped. Looked at the power distribution board. Looked at the manual override at the far end of the engineering bay, past the capacitor bank, past the conduit run that was already glowing faintly amber with load it was never rated to carry. "Unless somebody's standing at the junction when it blows, and pulls the physical shunt, and doesn't care what the arc does to them."

"No," Dara said.

"Somebody has to," Tam said. He was already unbuckling. He grinned at Jude, and it was the same grin he'd worn in the hold, the night he'd found her confessing to the sleeping cores and offered instead of reporting. "Hey. I've been keeping a lie alive for a month. Let me keep the truth alive for five minutes."

He was through the hatch before either of them could move.

* * *

What happened next, Jude experienced entirely through instruments and sound, and she would spend the rest of her life reconstructing it from those fragments.

Tam reached the junction. The shunt engaged—she saw it on the board, the array's lethal charge bleeding safely into the hull grid, the data cores dropping from critical to stable. And then the *Harbor of Law* fired.

The first shot missed. Deliberately or not, Jude never learned. It passed close enough that the Ansel's hull rang like a gong and every loose object on the bridge jumped, and the viewport filled for one instant with the blue-white smear of a plasma bolt traveling very fast through nothing.

The second shot found the dorsal antenna farm.

Even with the array shunted, the strike tore through the upper superstructure in a bloom of venting atmosphere and burning composite. Alarms cascaded. The deck lurched. And through it all, impossibly, the recording kept playing—because Tam had routed the playback through the lower arrays before he'd gone, because of course he had, because Tam did not build systems that died with him.

"Engineering, report!" Dara shouted into the comm. "Tam! Report!"

Static. Then, wetly, a laugh. "Array's down but the signal's out, Jude. It's everywhere. It's—" A cough. "Conduits got me. I'm gonna need that medic you never hired."

"He's alive," Dara breathed, and her hand found Jude's shoulder and gripped it, and for one second the investigator and the falsifier were simply two people holding onto the same rail in a storm.

Then the comm crackled with a new voice, and it was not Voss's.

"All vessels, all vessels, this is Captain Osei-Ra of the Concord cruiser *Verity*, acting under witness law, article nine. The vessel Ansel is transmitting evidence material currently under active review by this officer. Any strike against a transmitting witness vessel constitutes destruction of evidence and will be logged as such." A pause, and then, deliberately, in the old formal cadence that predated both fleets: "Furthermore, I am opening this channel to Meridian command. Admiral Voss, you are asked to stand down pending joint review. Meridian ships—I ask you to witness with us."

And the dark answered.

Jude watched the plot fill with contacts. Meridian destroyers, translating in from the convoy lane, weapons cold but present. Concord cruisers shifting station—not toward the Ansel, but between the *Harbor of Law* and everything else. The *Verity* herself, sliding her mass into the firing line with the unhurried confidence of a ship that had decided, all at once and irreversibly, whose side of history she stood on.

On the open channel, Voss's voice came one more time, quieter now, addressed to no one and everyone: "You are all fools. I saved the fleet."

No one answered her. The recording played on. *Please acknowledge. Please acknowledge.* And now, at last, across thirty thousand ships and two fleets and the grave-silence of a dead world, everyone was acknowledging. Jude watched the confirmation flags stack up until the console ran out of room to display them.

Dara sat down slowly in the chair beside her. Her insignia was still folded in her pocket. She took it out, looked at it for a long moment, and set it face-down on the console, like a card she was done playing.

"Well," she said. "You did it. Both fleets heard her."

Jude nodded. She found she couldn't speak. Amara's voice was still moving through the ship, through her, saying the names of the dead, and Jude said them silently along with her, every one, the way she'd promised herself she would.

Then the comm lit with a final flag, and she read it twice before her hands started shaking.

It was from the Meridian convoy flagship. Nine days out from Meridian Colony, course unchanged, but with new orders appended—orders issued jointly, countersigned by Concord command, the first document in fourteen years to bear both fleets' seals:

**PROCEED TO MERIDIAN COLONY. ALL SHIPS. TOGETHER.**

Beneath it, a single line of text, unsigned, from someone on that bridge who had been listening:

*We will be there when the recording ends. Tell us where to stand.*
