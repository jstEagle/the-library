## 20. Testimony

The inquiry chamber had been built by people who believed that architecture could make truth feel inevitable. It was a converted cargo auditorium aboard the Concord station *Halcyon Reach*, its vaulted ceiling stripped of freight cranes and hung instead with the twin banners of two fleets that had spent a decade pretending the other did not exist. The blue-and-silver of the Concord on one wall. The black-and-gold of Meridian on the other. Between them, a single table of gray composite where the joint tribunal sat—three officers from each fleet, six faces arranged in an arc like a half-formed thought.

Jude Yarrow stood at the center of the floor, alone, in a borrowed uniform jacket with her navigator's insignia freshly unpicked. The holes where the stitching had been were still visible if you looked closely. She looked closely. She made herself look.

The chamber was full. Not just with officers—with witnesses, clerks, fleet archivists, and, in the gallery behind a low rail, civilians: survivors of the convoy crews, families of the Meridian dead who had come to hear a name spoken aloud for the first time in four years. She could feel them the way she felt mass in the dark, without instruments, by pressure alone.

Dara sat at the second table, to Jude's left, in her investigator's grays, hands folded, face composed into something professional and unreadable. But when Jude glanced over—and she allowed herself exactly one glance—Dara's eyes held steady, and in them was not reassurance, exactly. Reassurance would have been a lie, and they had agreed, in the small hours of the last three nights, that there would be no more lies between them. What was in Dara's eyes was simpler and harder: *I am here. Whatever you say, I am here.*

Tam sat behind her, one arm still in a support sling from the array hit, radiating the particular stubborn calm of an engineer waiting to see whether someone else's wiring holds.

"Navigator Yarrow." The presiding officer was a Meridian commodore named Ilsa Reyes, broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, with a voice like a hull plate being set down carefully. "You have waived counsel. You have waived the right to submit a written statement in place of oral testimony. Do you confirm this?"

"I confirm it," Jude said. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "I want to say all of it. Out loud. Into the record."

"The record is open," Reyes said. "Proceed."

Jude had rehearsed this. That was the strange part—she had stood in her quarters on the *Ansel* for hours, saying the words to the dark, to the sleeping cores she could no longer visit, to a sister's voice she kept on a loop she no longer needed because she knew every syllable by heart. And now, standing in the light, the rehearsal fell away, and what came out was not the speech but the truth underneath it, which had been waiting patiently all along.

"Four years ago," she began, "I was assigned as relief navigator aboard the hauler *Corvid's Loom*, running the outer relay chain past the Meridian Verge. On the night of the eleventh of Thaw-Month, I picked up a distress transmission from Meridian Colony. Full-band, automated first, then human voices. Then nothing. I recorded all of it. Every second."

She heard the gallery shift. Somewhere, a woman drew a breath that did not come back out evenly.

"My ship's logs show no such transmission," Jude continued. "Because I never entered it. I recorded it privately, on my own equipment, encrypted, because—" and here her voice did waver, once, and she let it, "—because my sister was down there. Sefa Yarrow. Hydrologist, third rotation. And I was afraid that if I logged it officially, I would be ordered to surrender the recording, and then it would be gone, and she would be gone twice."

She looked up at the tribunal. Six faces. Three Concord, three Meridian. None of them looking away.

"For four years I carried that recording. I told myself it was grief. A private thing. Then, eleven months ago, auditing cargo records aboard the *Ansel*, I found my own name—misspelled—on the manifest of the *Vesper*. A relief ship listed as lost before launch. Except it wasn't lost before launch. It launched in secret, was recalled under Admiral Voss's authority, and its cargo was offloaded and sold while Meridian Colony died waiting for it. And I found that the colony's distress channel had been rerouted through Concord Relay Station Nine into a dead loop. Suppressed. By order. Signed."

She let the word sit in the chamber air. *Signed.* It had weight here; she could feel the whole room leaning against it.

"When Concord Investigator Okonkwo boarded the *Ansel* to audit navigation anomalies—including my unauthorized recordings—I understood that her audit would surface the suppression order. And I understood what would happen next. The Meridian fleets would learn their colony had called for help and been silenced by the Concord. There are forty thousand armed vessels in the Meridian line, Commodore. I grew up on those decks. I know what they do with grief that size."

"So you falsified a signal," said the senior Concord member, Vice-Admiral Ostrander, a thin man with a dry voice that gave away nothing.

"Yes, sir. I fabricated a live status telemetry from Meridian Colony and injected it into the relay chain so that both fleets' monitoring systems read the colony as alive. I maintained it for sixty-one days. I built it a heartbeat. Respiratory cycle, thermal bloom, comm handshakes—the whole body of a living settlement, grown out of code, because the real one was ash."

"You understand," Ostrander said, "that falsifying fleet telemetry is a capital-adjacent offense under Article Fourteen."

"I've read Article Fourteen," Jude said. "I can recite it. I'd like to recite what it doesn't cover."

Reyes raised a hand before Ostrander could answer. "Continue, Navigator."

"It doesn't cover the fact that the lie worked," Jude said. "It doesn't cover the fact that for sixty-one days, two fleets that have been one bad transmission away from open war held their fire, held their convoys, held their breath—because both sides believed there was still something left to save. My forgery bought time. Time enough for Investigator Okonkwo to trace the suppression order. Time enough for us to recover the *Vesper*'s black box. Time enough for Admiral Voss to offer me her silence-for-silence bargain, and for me to refuse it. Time enough for the truth to arrive with evidence attached instead of arriving as rumor, which is how wars start."

She turned, slowly, to face the gallery. She had promised herself she wouldn't—but the woman in the third row had Sefa's jawline, and Jude found she could not not look at her.

"I'm not asking you to forgive the forgery," she said. "I'm telling you what it cost. Every day the false signal ran, some family somewhere planned around a colony that didn't exist. Supply commitments were made against a fiction. When the Meridian convoy dispatched toward Meridian Colony believing it lived, they were nine days from finding nothing—and I knew it, and I let the clock run anyway, because I hadn't finished gathering proof. People will tell you my lie was noble. Don't believe them. It was a lie. I built a dead world a heartbeat and let strangers love it. That's not heroism. That's what desperation does when it's good at its job."

Her voice cracked on the last sentence, fully, and she stopped, and breathed, and the chamber waited with a patience she had not expected.

* * *

They broke for ninety minutes at midday. Dara found her in the side corridor off the witness antechamber, where the station's old plumbing ticked behind the panels like a patient clock. They stood a meter apart, which was as close as either of them dared in a building full of recorders.

"You're doing it," Dara said.

"I'm doing it."

"You don't have to carry all of it yourself. The signal design—some of it was mine. The cover story during the dark run. If they're weighing culpability, they should weigh mine too."

"They'll get to you," Jude said. "But this part has to be mine. I built the heartbeat. You kept it honest afterward—that's a different crime, and a smaller one, and we both know the tribunal knows it too."

Dara almost smiled. "A different crime. I'm going to have that engraved somewhere."

"Dara." Jude lowered her voice. "When they ask about the night I showed you the rerouted logs—you could have arrested me. Say it plainly. Don't soften it to protect me. If you soften anything, they'll wonder what else got softened, and then none of it lands on Voss."

For a moment Dara said nothing. Then she reached out—not quite touching Jude's sleeve, hovering a centimeter above it, the closest thing to contact the corridor allowed—and said, "I have spent nine years being believed because I never lied. Today you're going to be believed because you refused to stop. Those aren't the same thing, but today they weigh the same." She withdrew her hand. "Go finish it."

* * *

The afternoon session opened with the black box evidence, played into the record: the *Vesper*'s launch telemetry, the recall order bearing Ceylan Voss's authorization signature, the cargo manifests showing the colonist-relief supplies transferred and sold through three shell brokers in the Halcyon Reach freeport. Jude watched the tribunal watch the documents. She watched Vice-Admiral Ostrander's dry composure develop its first hairline fracture. She watched Commodore Reyes close her eyes for exactly four seconds when the audio played—the *Vesper*'s final internal log, a junior quartermaster's voice asking, again and again, why they were turning back, why they were leaving the colony's calls unanswered.

Then they turned back to her.

"Navigator Yarrow," Reyes said. "Describe the broadcast of the eleventh of this month. In your own words. Completely."

And Jude described it. The array Tam had rigged while Dara stalled Voss's tribunal with procedural challenges that would be studied, she suspected, in Concord law academies for a generation. The flagship's weapons lock. Tam taking the hit that cracked three ribs and nearly took the array with him. Dara invoking witness law—*a vessel transmitting verified historical record to multiple parties simultaneously constitutes protected testimony, and firing upon it constitutes destruction of evidence before a joint proceeding*—with her career in her mouth like a live coal. And then the transmission itself: the distress calls, the human voices, the silence, the black box, everything unedited, everything whole, poured out to both fleets at once on an open frequency.

"Why unedited?" asked the youngest Meridian member, a Captain Adeyemi whose pen had not stopped moving all day. "You could have excerpted. Curated. Made it more persuasive."

"Because editing is how this started," Jude said. "Someone edited the colony out of the record and called it necessity. Someone edited the *Vesper* out of its own flight history. If I edited even ten seconds—if I cut the parts where the voices fail, where the automated systems give up, where it stops being bearable—then I'd be doing Voss's work with better intentions. The dead don't need my curation. They need the whole recording. All of it. Even the parts that hurt to hear. *Especially* those."

Silence, then. The long kind. In the gallery, the woman with Sefa's jawline had her eyes closed, and her lips were moving, and Jude understood with a physical ache that she was mouthing along with words only she could hear—perhaps the same words Jude had carried for four years.

Ostrander leaned forward. "Let us be direct, Navigator. You committed serious crimes. Falsification of fleet telemetry. Unauthorized transmission. Evasion of a lawful intercept. By your own account, your motive was partly personal—your sister was among the dead. Why should this tribunal weigh your honesty as mitigation rather than simply as a late-arriving instinct for self-preservation?"

It was the question. The one she had dreaded and prepared for and dreaded again. Jude took her time.

"Because honesty after the fact is the only honesty anyone ever gets to verify, sir," she said. "You can't audit a person's soul in advance. All any of you can do—all any fleet can do—is watch what someone does when the lying stops being useful. My lie stopped being useful the moment the black box was recovered. From that moment forward, every hour I maintained the false signal was pure risk, for zero evidentiary gain. I kept it running anyway, because shutting it down abruptly would have signaled to the Meridian convoy that something was wrong, and I wanted the truth to reach them as truth, not as another silence. So yes—my honesty arrived late. Honesty usually does. The question isn't whether it's late. The question is whether, when it arrives, it tells the whole story or just the flattering part." She spread her hands. "This is the whole story. Weigh it however you must."

Reyes consulted her fellow members. Then: "The tribunal will recess to deliberate. Navigator Yarrow, you are remanded to station custody—not the brig," she added, before anyone else could speak, "the guest ward. Under the circumstances, the distinction matters to this chair."

* * *

They came back in ninety minutes, and the chamber had changed temperature. Jude felt it before Reyes spoke—the way a navigator feels a current shift before the instruments agree.

"Navigator Yarrow." Reyes's voice was careful, each word set down like glassware. "This tribunal finds as follows. One: that Admiral Ceylan Voss did knowingly suppress distress transmissions from Meridian Colony, resulting in the deaths of eleven thousand two hundred and six persons, and did further divert and sell relief supplies intended for those persons. She is referred for joint arrest and trial under the Concord-Meridian Provisional Accord, signed four days ago aboard this station. Two: that the suppression order constituted an act against both fleets, and that its exposure was necessary to prevent armed conflict between them. Three:"

Reyes paused. Looked directly at Jude.

"Three: that while Navigator Yarrow's falsification of fleet telemetry violated Article Fourteen and numerous subsidiary statutes, the tribunal finds that the forgery, in context, functioned as the sole mechanism by which both fleets were prevented from acting on incomplete information in a manner likely to produce catastrophic escalation. The tribunal further notes that the navigator's voluntary, complete, and unedited disclosure—both of the colony's final recording and of her own crimes—was material to this finding. Sentence is deferred pending the outcome of the Voss trial. Censure is entered formally into the record. Your navigator's certification is suspended. Whether it is restored, revoked, or transformed into something else entirely will depend on what this process teaches both fleets about the difference between a lie that kills and a lie that holds a door open."

Jude stood very still. Suspended, not revoked. Deferred, not delivered. It was not absolution—it was not meant to be—and she felt the weight of the censure settle onto her shoulders like ballast, proper and earned, and found that she could stand under it.

"Do you wish to add anything to the record before we adjourn?" Reyes asked.

Jude looked at the gallery. At the woman with her sister's jaw. At Tam, grinning despite the sling. At Dara, whose composure had finally, beautifully, cracked around the edges.

"Only this," Jude said. "Eleven thousand two hundred and six. When you enter them into the rolls—and you will enter them, both fleets, together—enter them as people who called for help and were answered too late, not as casualties of an accident. And enter the name of the ship that was supposed to save them. The *Vesper* flew. Let the record say so. That's all the dead ever asked for. To be flown to, and to be counted."

She stepped down from the floor, and the chamber rose—not in applause, fleets didn't applaud tribunals, but in that older, stranger motion of everyone standing at once because sitting had become impossible.

Outside, in the corridor, Dara was waiting, and beside her a Meridian liaison officer with a data slate already extended, already speaking: *"Commodore's compliments—the joint recovery charter drafts tonight, and they want a navigator's input on the route to the colony system. Someone who knows the relays. Someone both sides trust."*

Jude looked at the slate. At the route already sketched on it, running straight through the dark she had crossed once as a fugitive and would cross again as something else.

"Well," she said, taking it. "I know the way."
