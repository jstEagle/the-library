## 5. Kestrel Deep

The relay station appeared first as a scar against the dark — a splinter of reflected starlight where no light should have been returned.

Hazel had the Long Haul's running lights damped for the last six hours of approach, coasting on residual velocity with the drive cold and the crew quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. Kestrel Deep hung in the long shadow of an airless moonlet, its solar arrays furred with fifteen years of micrometeorite scarring, its antenna spines reaching up out of the dark like the ribs of something that had died mid-breath.

"Range to station, four hundred kilometers," Marisol said from the pilot's couch, her voice pitched low even though there was no one out here to hear them. "No transponder. No thermal. No beacon. That thing is officially dead."

"Officially," Dov said from engineering, over the intercom. His voice carried the particular flatness he reserved for things he considered beneath argument and above superstition. "Which is why we're here at four in the morning ship-time instead of asleep like sane people."

Hazel stood at the forward viewport and said nothing. He had learned that about himself in fifteen years of hauling cargo through contested lanes: when the fear got large enough, it went silent, and so did he. The fear was very large now. It filled the viewport.

The Meridian's flight plan terminated here. Filed eleven days after Ansel Zhou's death — filed by a dead man's biometric seal, renewed last month by Hazel's own — pointing at a relay station decommissioned before the murder, in a system so far off any commercial lane that even smugglers gave it a wide berth for lack of profit. A ghost flight plan to a ghost station.

And now, as they closed the final two hundred kilometers, the ghost acquired mass.

"There," Tam said. The deckhand was sixteen and had eyes like a hawk's, honed by three years of spotting patrol cutters before the patrol cutters spotted them. She pointed without touching the glass, the way spacers did, as if touching it might smear the view. "Docking arm, upper ring. Something's berthed there."

Hazel saw it a moment later. A shape nested against the station's dorsal docking collar, angular and familiar in the way his own face was familiar when it caught him unawares in a reflective surface. The Meridian was a long-haul courier class, sleeker than the Long Haul, built for speed and prestige rather than tonnage. Even crusted with dust, even blind, it looked expensive. It looked like Ansel Zhou: beautiful, and built by people who assumed it would never be asked to survive anything.

"That's her," Marisol said quietly. "That's the ship that never flew."

"She flew," Hazel said. "Eleven days after he died. And then she came here and stopped." He turned from the viewport. "Marisol, bring us alongside the lower maintenance collar, port side. Dov, I want hard-line access to their docking clamps if this goes wrong. Tam—"

"I know," Tam said. "I stay aboard. I always stay aboard."

"You stay aboard because you're the best hand we've got with the point-defense console, and because someone has to be able to leave without us if this turns out to be exactly what it looks like."

"And what does it look like?"

Hazel looked back at the derelict courier, at the station behind it, at the dark pressing in on both. "It looks like a door somebody left open on purpose," he said, "in a house where somebody died."

* * *

The maintenance collar took a magnetic seal with a groan that traveled through the hull like a complaint. Hazel cycled through in a hardshell suit with the visor down, Marisol at his shoulder — she had volunteered before he could assign it, and he had not argued, because arguing with Marisol about who went first into dangerous places was a war he would lose on points.

Kestrel Deep's interior was a museum of interrupted time. The corridor beyond the airlock was lit only by their suit lamps, and the beams picked out details with terrible clarity: a coffee bulb on the floor where it had been dropped, dried to a brown fossil; a maintenance schedule chalked on a slate board, half a shift's tasks listed, none checked off; a child's drawing taped to a bulkhead at knee height, sun-bleached almost to blankness, the crayon ghost of what might have been a family standing under what might have been a sun.

"They evacuated fast," Marisol murmured. Her lamp swept the corridor. "Or they left fast. Those aren't always the same thing."

"The station was pulled from service during the mobilization," Hazel said. "Fifteen years ago. Fleet needed the reactor parts elsewhere." He had read the file on the transit out, all nine hundred pages of it, the way he read everything — compulsively, completely, hunting for the detail that didn't fit. "Decommission order says full evacuation, ninety-six hours, nothing left aboard."

His lamp found the coffee bulb again. "Nothing left aboard," he repeated.

They climbed the central spine in silence, rung over rung, past offices with chairs pushed back from desks, past a galley where the smell of old years lived in the filters. The station's spine ended at the dorsal docking complex, and there their lamps found the Meridian's boarding hatch, standing open.

Not breached. Not forced. Open, with its indicator lights still softly green, fed by some trickle of power from the station's dying cells.

"Fifteen years," Marisol said, "and nobody closed the door."

"Somebody's been using it." Hazel played his lamp along the hatch frame, across the threshold plate, down onto the deck plating inside. Dust lay everywhere, thick and undisturbed — everywhere except a path. A clean lane worn through the gray, leading inward from the hatch, footprints overlapping footprints, hundreds of visits compressed into a single pale road through the dark.

He knelt and put his gloved hand near the path without touching it. The dust at its edges was soft, uncrusted. Recent. Weeks old, maybe less.

"Marisol," he said. "How many ships do you know that log weekly activity for fifteen years while sitting dead in a graveyard?"

"None," she said. "But I know one man crazy enough to come look." She settled herself against the hatch frame, rifle slung, facing back down the corridor they'd climbed. "Go on. I'll watch the door. Whatever's in there has been coming and going a long time without hurting the ship."

"Or without being caught at it."

"Same door," she said. "Different day. Go."

* * *

Inside, the Meridian smelled of cold metal and old upholstery and, faintly, impossibly, of cedar — the scent of Ansel Zhou's private cabin, which Hazel knew from estate inventories, from photographs, from the one visit he had been permitted to the sealed townhouse on Ceres before the trustees changed the locks. The smell hit him in the suit's recycled air like a hand laid flat against his sternum.

He made himself walk the ship methodically. Bridge first: consoles dark under dust, the pilot's chair angled toward a viewport looking out at nothing but rock and black. Then the galley, the crew bunks, the small medical bay — all untouched, all preserved in the amber of abandonment. Fifteen years of nothing happening anywhere except along that worn path in the dust, which led aft, past the galley, to a door marked with plain stenciled letters:

COMMUNICATIONS / ARCHIVE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL

The door was locked. Of course it was locked. But beside the reader panel, someone had mounted a second device — newer, bolted on within the last few years by the look of its fasteners — a biometric plate of a make Hazel recognized instantly, because he had one in his own kit, because it was the model used for Zhou family seals, because he had pressed his thumb to one exactly like it on a neutral-station registry terminal six weeks ago and watched a dead ship come alive on a manifest.

He pulled off his glove. The cabin air was cold enough that his fingers ached immediately, and he was grateful for it, grateful for anything that felt like a reason other than the obvious one.

He pressed his palm to the plate.

The lock thought about it for three seconds — long enough for Hazel's heart to climb into his throat and start making demands — and then released with a click that sounded, in the dead ship's silence, like a shot.

The archive room beyond was small, windowless, and dominated by a single console, live, its screen glowing a patient amber. A chair sat before it. The chair was not dusty. On the desk beside the console lay a stack of data wafers, each labeled in a precise architectural hand, and beside the wafers, a physical object that stopped Hazel where he stood.

A photograph. Old stock, printed on paper, the kind of thing nobody made anymore. It showed a younger Ansel Zhou — thirty-five, maybe, before the fortune finished calcifying around him — standing on the Meridian's bridge with his arm around a woman Hazel knew from every history file and trial transcript of the last fifteen years.

Vess Ardent. The convicted assassin. Smiling. Ansel smiling too, caught mid-laugh, both of them looking at whoever held the camera like the camera was a friend.

Hazel stood in the dead man's archive room holding the dead man's photograph of the woman who was supposed to have killed him, and felt the floor of his understanding tilt beneath him the way decks tilted when a ship's gravity failed — everything familiar suddenly weightless and wrong.

The console chimed. Softly. Almost apologetically.

A prompt appeared on the amber screen, in text formatted in a style fifteen years out of date:

**MESSAGE QUEUED — ADDRESSEE: THE COPY**

**PLAY? Y/N**

Hazel did not move for a long moment. The suit's environmental system hummed. Somewhere below, Marisol's voice crackled once on the short-range channel — *"Status?"* — and he keyed off without answering, which he would apologize for later, which she would wave off, because Marisol understood that some doors you had to walk through alone or not at all.

*The copy.* Not Hazel. Not Ansel Zhou the Second, the estate designation. Not heir, not instrument, not asset. *The copy* — the word the trustees used when they thought the soundproofing was better than it was, the word he had heard through a door on Ceres at age nine, the word he had spent fifteen years refusing to answer to.

Someone had queued a message for him. Someone had known — years ago, or weeks ago, whenever the queue was set — that a copy would someday stand in this room with his hand on this plate.

He touched Y.

Static first. A wash of white noise, hissing, filling the little room, and then a voice rising out of it like a swimmer surfacing — a voice Hazel had heard only in recordings, in courtroom audio, in the memorial broadcasts the fleet had run for a month until the grief curdled into politics. A voice he had spent his whole life being compared to and never once heard say anything meant for him.

*"Testing. One, two."* A pause. A breath. In the background, faintly, the ambient hum of this same room, this same ship, alive. *"If you're hearing this, then the seal worked, which means the registry worked, which means—"*

The voice stopped. When it resumed, it was quieter. Closer to the pickup. Hazel found himself leaning toward the speaker as if toward a mouth at his ear.

*"If you're hearing this, they've started again."*

Silence on the recording. Three seconds. Five. Long enough that Hazel thought it was over.

*"I don't know who you are. I can guess. There were contingencies. There were always contingencies — Corwin used to say I planned my own funeral before I planned my breakfast, and he was right, God rot him, he was right about most things, which is how he got close enough to matter."*

A sound that might have been a laugh, stripped of humor.

*"If they've started again, then the truce is failing, or someone is making it fail, and either way the accord is the only thing that matters. Not me. Not you. Not the estate, not the name, not any of it. The accord. It's real. Both commands signed it. I watched them sign it, and then I watched the same men toast my health an hour later, and I understood that I had maybe a season left, and I spent the season building doors like this one."*

The voice hardened. Hazel heard in it, with a shock of recognition that hurt worse than grief, the cadence he heard in his own throat every time he spoke.

*"Whoever you are — whatever you are — you're hearing this because you got here. That means you're resourceful, or desperate, or both, and in my experience those are the same quality viewed from different distances. So listen. The Meridian's logs will show you the pattern. Weekly activity, weekly couriers, weekly payments routed through shells I won't insult you by naming. Someone has kept this ship warm for fifteen years. Someone has kept the door open. Find out who pays them, and you'll find the faction that wanted the peace dead — and find out whether they want it dead still, or whether one of them has finally grown a conscience and is trying to finish what I couldn't."*

*"Either way, the accord cylinder is in the vault. You'll need probate authority to open it. I'm sorry about that. I'm sorry about all of it — sorry isn't the word, but it's the one I have, and I don't have time for better ones."*

A pause. When the voice came back, it had changed register entirely — dropped low, dropped personal, the way a man speaks when he has stopped performing for posterity and started talking to one specific person he cannot see.

*"And if you're the contingency I think you are — if you're the one they grew from my pattern, my sequences, my—"*

The breath again. Unsteady now.

*"Then I'm sorry for that too. More than the rest. You didn't ask to be a legal instrument. Neither did I, in the end. We're both clauses in contracts other people wrote."*

*"Finish the work. Then be free of it. That's an order from a dead man, and dead men's orders are the only ones anyone keeps."*

The static swelled, swallowed the voice, and cut off. The screen returned to its amber prompt. **END OF MESSAGE.**

Hazel stood in the cold archive room of a ship that never flew, in a station that should have been empty, holding a photograph of a murderer smiling, and listened to his own heartbeat hammering in the suit's quiet.

*They've started again.*

He replayed it twice more, hunting the background the way Dov hunted faults in a drive signature — and found it. Underneath the voice, beneath the room tone, threaded through the recording like a hair in amber: a rhythmic pulse. Regular. Mechanical. Docking clamps cycling. Every forty seconds, a soft hydraulic sigh, and the faintest scrape of metal on metal.

The Meridian's logs showed weekly activity. The dust showed recent traffic. And the recording — recorded before Ansel Zhou died, fifteen years ago — had captured the sound of this ship's clamps engaging and releasing.

Someone had been coming and going since before the murder. Someone had kept the door open all these years, paying couriers, renewing registrations, forging seals — waiting for a copy to come find the message.

Or herding him here. The thought arrived cold and stayed. Dov had warned him: bait. Every step of this trail had been walked before him, and recently, and the footprints all pointed the same direction — deeper.

His comm crackled. Marisol, sharp now, no apology in her voice: *"Hazel. Contact. Lower docking ring — a shuttle just came out of the moonlet's shadow, running dark, heading for our collar. Two minutes out. Get down here."*

Hazel pocketed the photograph, wiped his palm print from the biometric plate out of pure instinct, and ran for the ladder — down through the dead ship, down through the frozen corridor, down toward the open door and whatever had finally come home through it.
