## 2. Signal from Nowhere

The ping came at 0347 ship-time, which was to say it came at the hour Lena Eriksen had trained herself to be awake anyway.

She had learned, over nine years on the Long Quiet Route, that sleep was a thing you negotiated with rather than a thing you had. The *Kestrel's* cabin was small enough that her breath fogged the bulkhead in cold cycles, and she kept the lights at a dim amber because white light made her think of Concord inspection bays, and she did her best thinking in amber. So she was awake, tea going bitter in its bulb, when the transponder board chimed — a soft, apologetic sound, like a bird tapping glass.

She almost didn't look up. Derelict pings were common enough out here: dead satellites, orphaned buoys, the occasional coffin-ship still broadcasting its registry to no one. The route between Meridian and the severed worlds was a graveyard of signals, and a navigator who chased every ghost would never deliver so much as a crate of seed stock.

But this ping had a registry code attached.

Lena set down the tea. She pulled the code onto the main display and read it twice, then a third time, because the first two readings could not be trusted to anything but her own exhaustion.

*HLCN-114.*

Halcyon.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty cabin, in the voice of a woman correcting a child. "No, that's not possible."

The Halcyon had gone missing eleven years ago. Everyone on the route knew the story; it was one of the few stories left, in a corridor where news arrived like fossils. A survey vessel, state of the art, crewed by forty-one of the Concord's brightest — sent past the Fold Line to chart the deep dark beyond the silent worlds, and simply never heard from again. No distress call. No debris field. No wreckage for the grief-counselors to point at while they explained to the families that the Concord expressed its condolences and considered the matter closed. The Concord did not conduct searches. Searches cost fuel, and fuel was doctrine, and doctrine said the route ran forward or not at all.

Eleven years. And now here was its transponder, pinging from a coordinate in open space that, according to every chart Lena carried, contained nothing at all.

She ran the verification three times. The signal was clean — too clean, actually, crisp as a new-minted coin, none of the degradation you'd expect from an old hull's aging beacon. Either the transmitter had been recently maintained, or it wasn't transmitting from any distance worth mentioning.

Her hands were doing something without her permission. They were pulling up the navigation plot, laying in the intercept vector, calculating burn windows. She watched them work the console like they belonged to someone braver.

"Protocol," she reminded herself, and her own voice sounded thin. Protocol was very clear. Derelict contacts on the Long Quiet Route were to be logged, reported to the nearest Concord relay station within six hours, and ignored. Salvage rights belonged to the Concord. Rescue operations belonged to the Concord. Curiosity, specifically and explicitly, did not belong to her.

She thought about reporting it. She genuinely did. She sat there with her hand hovering over the comm panel and imagined the conversation: *Relay Station Kestrel-9, cargo hauler Eriksen, registry 7740-K, reporting derelict contact, coordinates following.* And then she imagined what happened next, which was that a Concord cutter would arrive in eleven days, or nineteen, or thirty, and whatever was out there broadcasting under the name Halcyon would either be gone by then or — worse — still there, waiting, having waited already eleven years, and the cutter's crew would find whatever remained and file it in a drawer and the drawer would close and that would be the whole of it.

That was how the Concord handled things. She knew. She had helped.

The thought arrived the way all her worst thoughts arrived — quietly, without drama, in the flat tone of a manifest being read aloud. She had helped. Nine years of running the Quiet Route meant nine years of carrying sealed orders, of relaying priority transmissions she wasn't cleared to read, of docking at worlds whose names appeared on no public chart and leaving before anyone could ask her what she'd seen. She told herself she was a courier, not a collaborator. She told herself the backups in her hold were cargo, not people. She told herself a great many things at four in the morning, and most mornings the telling held.

This morning it didn't.

Because the Halcyon had forty-one families on the inner worlds who had spent eleven years being politely informed that hope was inefficient. Because somewhere out in the nothing, something wearing that ship's name was calling home. And because if she reported it and let the Concord handle it, she would spend the rest of her life wondering what the drawer had contained.

Lena Eriksen was thirty-four years old and had never once disobeyed a direct protocol. It was not a record she was proud of. It was simply a fact about her, like her blood type, like the scar on her left knuckle from a cargo clamp. She was reliable. That was the word everyone used. *Reliable.* Reliable Lena, who delivered on time, who asked no questions, who kept her hold sealed and her mouth shut and her grief filed neatly in the same cabinet as everything else.

She looked at the vector her hands had plotted. Fourteen hours' burn, twelve hours' coast, arrival window inside the day.

"Curiosity is not a flight plan," she said, quoting her first instructor, old Marta Voss, who had beaten discipline into a generation of navigators and died poor and obedient.

Then she keyed the burn.

* * *

The *Kestrel* was a Meridian-class long-hauler, forty years old, ugly as a fist, and honest in the way only old machines are honest. She complained when she was pushed, and Lena pushed her now, riding the thrust at ninety-two percent rated output while the drive hummed a note low enough to feel in her teeth. She spent the burn hours doing what she always did when she couldn't sleep: maintenance rounds.

Which meant, eventually, the hold.

She didn't go there often. She went when the schedule demanded pod diagnostics, which was quarterly, and she performed them exactly as specified — external checks, power draw readings, coolant loop pressure — and she did not look longer than necessary at the seven pods bolted along the port side behind their gray shrouds. Seven pods. Seven frozen backups of seven crewmates from the *Aldrin's Wake*, lost nine years ago in the decompression event that the official report called an accident and that Lena, alone, knew had followed a relayed order she herself had transmitted. Priority directive, source encrypted, content sealed. *Abandon section four. Do not attempt recovery of personnel. Acknowledge.*

She had acknowledged. She had been twenty-five and trusted the seal, trusted the chain of command, trusted that whoever wrote those words knew something she didn't. By the time she understood that no one knew anything — that the order had come from nowhere and gone nowhere and killed six people and frozen their backup engrams mid-scream into pods she was forbidden by law to open — by then, obedience was the only thing she had left, and she clung to it the way drowning people cling to things that are also drowning them.

She checked the coolant pressures. All nominal. She did not lift the shrouds. She never lifted the shrouds.

"You'd tell me to go," she said to them, quietly, on her way out. "Any of you. You'd tell me to go see."

The pods, being frozen, said nothing. But she felt lighter walking back up the spine of the ship than she had in months, and she hated that she felt lighter, and she felt lighter anyway.

* * *

Contact at 1900 hours, eleven minutes ahead of window.

The Halcyon hung in the black like a question mark made of metal.

Lena brought the *Kestrel* alongside at half a kilometer and cut thrust, and for a long moment neither ship moved and neither ship spoke, and the silence between them was the oldest silence there is — the silence of space itself, which does not care what happens inside its darkness.

The survey ship looked wrong. That was the first thing. Not damaged — wrong. Her hull was clean. Not merely intact but *clean*, unweathered, the ablative coating unblemished, the registry numerals crisp, as though she'd launched last month rather than eleven years ago. Eleven years of micrometeorite sandblasting should have frosted every surface out here. Eleven years of thermal cycling should have cracked the paint. This hull looked like it had been through a week.

And her lights were on. Running lights, dock lights, the soft glow of illuminated viewports along the command ring. A derelict doesn't leave its lights on. A derelict doesn't have anything left to illuminate.

Lena hailed her on standard frequencies. Nothing. She hailed on survey-band, on emergency band, on the old pre-Concord handshake frequencies that half the route's smugglers still used. Nothing. The transponder kept pinging its clean, impossible signal, and the ship kept glowing its warm domestic yellow, and no voice answered her.

So she did the second disobedient thing of the day, which was easier than the first, because the first had already broken the seal on the container holding all her obedience, and once that spills you might as well use it.

She suited up.

The airlock cycle took four minutes and she spent all of them talking to herself, a habit she'd developed somewhere in year three of the Quiet Route and never managed to break. "Boarding an unresponsive vessel against protocol," she narrated, checking her tether, checking her oxygen. "Log entry follows. If I die out here, whoever finds this: I was curious. That's the whole story. Curiosity killed the navigator. Put it on the stone."

The airlock cycled. The black opened.

She crossed the gap on thrusters, slow, the way you move toward something you're afraid will flinch. Up close the Halcyon's wrongness got worse. There was no dust on her. Space dust settles on everything — it's the universe's way of keeping time — and there was none, not a film, not a fleck. And when Lena's glove touched the hull near the primary airlock, she felt vibration. Faint, rhythmic, alive. Machinery breathing somewhere inside.

The airlock accepted her override code on the third try. Standard Concord survey format — she knew it because every licensed navigator knew it, because the Concord wanted its ships accessible to its people and inaccessible to everyone else. The inner door opened onto atmosphere. Warm atmosphere. Breathable, pressurized, smelling faintly of recycled air and — she paused, helmet sensors sampling — something green. Something growing.

Plants. On a survey ship missing eleven years, someone was growing plants.

She cycled through, sealed her helmet open, and stood in the Halcyon's entry bay listening to the sound of a ship that was supposed to be dead going about the business of being alive. Air handlers. Water recirculation. Somewhere deeper, a faint chime, repeating, patient, like a timer nobody had ever reset.

"Hello?" Her voice fell into the corridor and came back smaller. "This is Navigator Eriksen, vessel *Kestrel*. Responding to your transponder. Is anyone—"

The lights along the corridor came up as she walked. Not motion-triggered — she tested that, stepping backward into shadow, and the lights stayed on. They were simply on, ahead of her, as if the ship had decided where she was going and prepared the way. The corridor walls bore the standard survey-vessel fittings: cable runs, storage alcoves, the little brass plaques the Concord liked, engraved with mottos about discovery. But the plaques were mounted slightly crooked, all of them, tilted a degree or two counterclockwise, uniformly, as if the entire ship had been rotated a fraction and set back down by careful hands.

She found the first of them in the mess hall.

Not a body. A person. Small, curled in the corner of a bench seat, knees drawn up, asleep — or something like asleep. A child, maybe eight years old, wearing a Halcyon survey uniform tailored for an adult and rolled at the cuffs, rolled at the waist, cinched with a length of cord, so that the fabric pooled around the small body like a tent. On the breast of the uniform, a name tape: *CMDR. OKONKWO.*

Lena stood very still in the doorway of the mess hall and felt the temperature of her fear change. It had been the ordinary fear of boarding derelicts — the fear of vacuum, of failure, of bodies. This was a different temperature entirely. This was the fear of standing in a place where the rules of the world had been quietly replaced with other rules, and no one had told her.

Because she knew that name. Every navigator on the route knew that name. Commander Adaeze Okonkwo, captain of the Halcyon, fifty-eight years old at launch, a woman famous enough that even the Concord's chokehold on communication hadn't fully smothered her — the woman who'd given the farewell address that got replayed in every dive bar from Meridian to the Fold Line. *We go out past the maps so that others may follow.* Lena had heard it a hundred times, usually drunk, sometimes weeping.

The child in the adult's clothes had Okonkwo's face. Older than eight, younger than fifty-eight — a face folded backward through decades, the strong jaw softened, the gray hair gone dark and fine. Asleep, she breathed with the particular controlled rhythm of a person who had taught herself to sleep in bad conditions and never unlearned it.

Lena's hand went to her comm. Stopped. Reporting this meant reporting her position, her diversion, her disobedience, all of it, in one transmission. Instead she knelt, slowly, and did the only reasonable thing, which was the unreasonable thing: she reached out and touched the child's shoulder.

The eyes opened. Dark, clear, adult eyes in a child's face — that was the worst part, that was the part Lena would carry for the rest of her life. The eyes were fifty-eight years old. They looked at Lena with recognition, with relief, with something close to joy, and the small mouth said, in a voice like a bell:

"You took your time."

Then the eyes rolled back, and the small body went slack, and somewhere deeper in the ship the patient chime stopped, and in the sudden silence Lena heard — distantly, from the direction of the command deck — footsteps. Many footsteps. Small ones.

Moving toward her.
