## 3. Everyone Younger

The Halcyon hung in the black like a held breath.

Lena brought the *Kestrel's Fortune* alongside on thrusters alone, no docking clamps, no hard seal — just her ship's belly kissing the survey vessel's airlock ring with a gentleness she usually reserved for landings on worlds that might still be alive. Through the viewport, the Halcyon's hull told its own story: eleven years of dust and micrometeor scarring, yes, but beneath it the clean lines of a Concord deep-survey ship, white composite gone bone-grey, the registry numerals half-eaten by radiation bloom. She had seen wrecks before. This was not a wreck. The running lights cycled in their slow patient pattern. The reactor sang at a frequency she could feel through her boots when she pressed her palm flat against the console. The ship was alive. It had simply stopped answering questions.

*Eleven years,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because the transponder had said something stranger than eleven years. The beacon's timestamp block — the part of a signal that ships forge last and least — claimed the Halcyon had been gone for three. Three years, four months, some odd days. As if the ship itself did not agree with the calendar about how long it had been away.

She suited up anyway. Protocol said quarantine, protocol said wait for Concord authorization, protocol said log the contact and move along your route, Eriksen, there's cargo rotting in silent holds on worlds that can't complain. She had spent nine years being the kind of navigator who followed protocol into the dark, who kept her voice level on comms, who filed her reports in clean grammar because clean grammar meant nothing was wrong. But she had already broken the first rule by diverting, and Lena had learned the particular physics of disobedience: it has momentum. Once you are falling out of formation, every correction costs more than the fall.

So she keyed the airlock herself.

* * *

The tunnel between ships was cold and smelled of ozone and old metal, that specific scent of a hull that has been breathing its own recycled air too long. Her helmet lamp swept across the Halcyon's inner airlock, and she saw the telltales green — the ship wanted her in. That was the first wrong thing. Derelicts do not want you in. Derelicts have dead doors and frozen seals and the particular silence of machines that have given up. This door opened for her with the smooth hydraulic sigh of a ship that had been waiting.

"Halcyon, this is Navigator Lena Eriksen, vessel Kestrel's Fortune, responding to your beacon." Her voice sounded strange inside the helmet, flattened, official. "I am boarding. Please acknowledge."

Silence, but not empty silence. Somewhere down the spine of the ship, something moved — a soft, arrhythmic sound, like small feet, like many small feet.

Her lamp found the corridor beyond the airlock, and the second wrong thing arrived all at once.

Clothes. Clothes everywhere, or rather clothes worn by bodies far too small for them. A woman's uniform jacket pooled around a child of perhaps six who sat against the bulkhead with the perfect, terrible patience of someone waiting to be told what happens next. The jacket's name tape read OKONKWO, T. The child wore it like a costume from a play about someone else's life. Further down the corridor, a man's boots lay upright and empty beside a boy of eight or nine who was walking toward her with those boots clutched to his chest as if they were the most important things aboard.

Lena stopped walking. Every instinct she owned — the ones that had kept her alive through debris fields and customs seizures and one memorable pirate interdiction off Thessaly Reach — screamed at her to turn around, seal the lock, burn away, file the report, let the Concord send people whose job this was.

But the children were looking at her. And they were not afraid.

That was the third wrong thing, and somehow the worst. Children who find a stranger in an armored suit in a dead ship should cry, should run, should cling to each other. These children looked at her the way crew look at relief. The little girl in Okonkwo's jacket stood up, the jacket dragging on the deck, and said, in a voice pitched high and clear:

"You came. We heard your engines two days ago."

Two days ago, Lena thought. I diverted six hours ago.

"I'm Lena," she said, and knelt, which made the suit awkward and made her feel enormous and ridiculous and frightened in a way that had nothing to do with danger. "What's your name?"

"Tomas," the girl said. Then, after a pause in which something flickered behind her eyes like a signal trying to resolve: "Tomas Okonkwo. I'm the... I was..." Her face crumpled and reformed, a child's quick weather. "I don't remember which."

* * *

She counted them in the galley, because the galley was where children go, and because counting was something her hands could do while her mind refused to hold still. Fourteen. Fourteen children in a ship built for a crew of thirty-one, wearing the uniforms of adults, moving through the corridors with the eerie competence of muscle memory — small hands finding door controls without looking, small feet taking the turns of familiar passages without hesitation. A girl of maybe seven sat at the nav console and ran a diagnostic with keystrokes too fluid for a child, her lips moving silently over numbers she could not possibly understand and clearly, somehow, did.

They were not feral. They were not traumatized, or not only. They were *crew*. They spoke of the ship in the shorthand of people who had lived in it for years — "the long corridor smells wrong past frame forty," "the port recycler sticks if you ask it nicely instead of firmly" — and then would stop mid-sentence, frown, touch their own faces as though checking whether they were still theirs.

"We got younger," the boy with the boots told her solemnly, when she finally asked the question sitting in her throat like a stone. His name was Devi, he said, and he was the quartermaster, and he was approximately nine years old, and he said all of this in the tone of a man reporting inventory discrepancies. "It happened during the fold. Everybody got younger except—" He stopped. His brow furrowed with genuine effort. "Except Ansel. Ansel stayed."

"Where is Ansel?"

"In the garden. He sits in the garden. He doesn't talk much." Devi considered. "He used to talk a lot. I remember him talking a lot. I remember him being tall." The boy looked up at her, and his eyes were old in a way that no nine-year-old's eyes should be, old the way a house is old, full of rooms nobody lives in anymore. "Did you come to take us home?"

And Lena, kneeling again in her ridiculous suit in the yellow-lit galley of a ship that had fallen out of time, heard herself say: "Yes."

She did not know if it was true. She said it anyway. Some promises you make because the alternative is to leave a child standing in the wreckage of a sentence.

* * *

The garden was real — a hydroponics bay converted, somewhere in the Halcyon's lost years, into something closer to a chapel. Rows of bean vines and leaf greens under grow-lights tuned warm, and in the middle of it, sitting on the deck with his back against the nutrient tank, a man.

Not a child. A man, whole and adult, mid-thirties maybe, dark-haired, hollow-cheeked with the leanness of someone who had not been eating properly, wearing a plain shipsuit with no name tape at all. He was watching the plants grow. Actually watching them, with the fixed attention of a man who has decided that if he looks away the growing might stop.

Lena stood in the bay entrance for a long moment before he noticed her, and in that moment she took inventory of everything wrong and right about him. Wrong: the stillness, the thousand-yard focus, the way the grow-light shadows pooled under his eyes like bruises. Right: he was breathing. He was intact. In a ship of reversed children, he was the single fixed point, the one body the fold had declined to touch.

When he finally turned and saw her, he did not startle. He looked at her the way the others had — recognition without memory, welcome without reason.

"You're the one who answered," he said. His voice was rough, underused. "We've been listening to traffic for weeks. Nobody answers on these routes anymore. You answered."

"The beacon wasn't yours," Lena said. "It was automated."

"I know." He almost smiled. "I hoped someone would answer it anyway."

She came closer, unhurried, the way you approach an animal that might bolt or might be the only sane creature left on the ship. Up close he was worse and better — worse because his hands trembled faintly, constantly, a fine motor tremor like a string plucked and never allowed to settle; better because his eyes, when they met hers, were present. Accounting for her. Accounting for himself.

"I'm Lena Eriksen. I run cargo on the quiet route."

"Ansel." He offered his hand, and hers met it, and his grip was careful, controlled, a man holding himself together at the seams. "I'd say welcome aboard, but I don't actually know what ship this is."

"It says Halcyon on the hull."

"Does it." He said it flatly, testing the word for weight and finding none. "There are fourteen children on this ship. They call me by name. They remember me. One of them — Devi, the quartermaster, he can't be more than nine — remembers my birthday, which I myself do not remember." His tremor worsened as he spoke, and he pressed his palms flat against the deck to still them. "I woke up in a medbay cot eleven days ago with no logs, no records, no memories that go back further than waking up. And everyone around me is a smaller version of who they're supposed to be. So either I'm insane, or—"

"Or the ship went somewhere that doesn't keep time the way we do," Lena said quietly.

He looked at her then, really looked, and something passed between them that she would spend a long time afterward trying to name. Not trust exactly — trust requires evidence, and neither of them had any. More like two instruments detecting the same signal and recognizing the frequency.

"You believe me," he said.

"I believe the children," she said. "Children are bad liars and worse conspirators. Whatever happened here, it happened to them, and they're living inside it." She settled onto the deck across from him, cross-legged, suit still sealed, feeling absurd and certain. "Tell me what you do remember."

"Not much. Fragments." He closed his eyes. "A planet. Green going gold at the edges, terraced fields, a city built up a hillside like an amphitheater. Someone shouting an order — not me, someone above me. Then noise. Then the fold, whatever a fold is, I only know the word because Devi uses it. And—" His breath caught. "A sound. A lot of voices, and then none. Like a door closing on a room full of people."

The grow-lights hummed. Somewhere in the vines, water dripped from a nutrient line with the regularity of a clock that didn't care what time it was.

"That's not nothing," Lena said. "That's a place. Terraced fields, hillside city. Does it have a name?"

He shook his head slowly. "It will. It's coming back. All of it is coming back, in pieces, like—" he searched, "—like sediment settling. Except the pieces hurt. I remember the voices stopping before I remember why, and I don't know which is worse."

Lena reached out and, before she could think better of it, put her hand over his where it pressed flat against the deck. He went very still. So did she. It was the first human touch either of them had described to themselves in longer than either could count, and it landed in the quiet of the garden like a struck bell.

"You don't have to remember it all tonight," she said. "Tonight we get the children fed and bedded down, and tomorrow we figure out how to move fourteen kids and one amnesiac on a route where every world went silent, and the day after that we figure out the rest."

"And when the Concord comes?" he asked. "Because they'll come. Ships like this don't just vanish; someone owns the vanishing."

Something cold walked down Lena's spine, because he was right, and because she had been trying very hard not to think it. The Concord did not lose ships. The Concord lost *reports*, and rewrote them, and buried the originals so deep that even the people who'd signed them forgot their signatures. Nine years on the quiet route had taught her that the silence between worlds was not natural decay. It was maintenance.

"Then we're ready before they come," she said.

* * *

She slept, or didn't, in the Halcyon's officer wardroom with the door open and the corridor light spilling in, listening to the sounds of fourteen children breathing in the dormitory down the passage — a sound like tide, like rain, like something the universe owed her and was finally paying back at interest. Twice in the night she rose and checked the backup pods' status feed on her own ship out of pure habit, that reflexive glance toward the hold where the frozen dead rode in silence, forbidden cargo, permanent guilt. The pods were green. They were always green. That was the problem with them.

Near morning — ship's morning, arbitrary, merciful — she found Ansel already awake, standing at the viewport outside the bridge, watching the stars with the expression of a man reading a letter addressed to someone else.

"Lena," he said, without turning. He knew her footsteps now; eleven days aboard with no one else adult, and he had learned to identify her by gait alone, which was either touching or alarming. "Devi remembered something else last night. He woke up crying and wouldn't say what about, but he drew this." He held out a child's drawing, crayon on the back of a supply manifest. A hillside city in amber and green. Above it, in a child's unsteady hand, letters that had clearly been copied from somewhere the boy could no longer see:

VESPER GATE.

Below it, smaller, pressed so hard the crayon had torn the paper, a single line that Devi had refused to explain:

*They told us to watch. We watched.*

Lena took the paper. Outside the viewport, the stars of the quiet route burned in their ancient indifference, and somewhere behind them, she knew, the Concord was already composing the report that would explain all of this — the children, the fold, the ship — in language designed to close doors.

"Ansel," she said slowly, "I think we just became the only witnesses to something."

He nodded, and his trembling hands folded the drawing with exaggerated care, as though it were already evidence.

"Then we'd better remember better than they erase."
