## 4. Ansel

He woke to the sound of someone breathing wrong.

Not the wet, labored rasp of a dying man — he knew that sound, though he could not say from where — but something smaller. Quicker. The breathing of a child who had run too far and was trying to hide it. Ansel opened his eyes and the light was wrong: too white, too even, the flat glare of emergency strips running on reserve power. It fell across a row of bunks in the Halcyon's mid-deck berthing compartment, and in the bunks lay his crew.

His crew.

He knew them. He did not know how he knew them, or why the knowing arrived without names attached, like tools stripped of their labels. The woman in the third bunk — he knew her hands, quick and sure on a nav console, knew the way she used to tuck her hair behind one ear before a burn — was nine years old at most. She slept with her fists curled under her chin, and even asleep she looked furious about something. In the bunk above her, a boy of perhaps eleven breathed that small hidden breath, and Ansel understood with a lurch that this was Chief Okonkwo, who had once talked for forty minutes about coolant tolerances while Ansel pretended to take notes. The chief's hands were soft and unscarred now. His forearms were smooth as wax.

Ansel sat up too fast and the compartment tilted. He gripped the edge of his bunk — his bunk, the frame still bearing the scratched tally marks he'd cut during some long transit he couldn't summon — and waited for the ship to stop moving sideways through his skull.

*Eleven years,* said a voice in him that was not quite memory and not quite thought. *You have been gone eleven years.*

The number meant nothing. The number meant everything. He held it in his mouth like a coin of unknown currency.

He became aware, then, of his right hand. It was clenched so tightly around something that his knuckles had gone bloodless, and when he forced his fingers open they protested like rusted hinges. In his palm lay a data shard: matte black, the size of a thumbnail, unmarked except for a hairline scratch across its face that might have been made by anything. It was warm. Not body-warm — warmer than that, as if it had recently been doing work, as if it had been running hot inside his fist all night, all decade, all whatever span of time had passed between the man he'd been and this one sitting upright in the dark.

He did not remember taking it.

He did not remember *not* taking it. That was the strange part, the part that made his stomach drop. Memory, he discovered, was not a wall with holes in it. It was a wound — an absence with edges, and the edges were sharp, and every time his mind brushed past one it bled a little. He remembered the smell of the Halcyon's galley: burnt coffee and recycled steam. He remembered that the captain hummed off-key and hated being told so. He remembered a corridor flooding with light the color of a struck match, and after that, nothing, nothing, nothing, and then this white-lit room full of children wearing the faces of his colleagues.

Between those points lay a country he had apparently traveled through alone.

* * *

He got up because staying down felt like drowning.

His legs worked, which surprised him. His body worked entirely, in fact — no weakness, no atrophy, none of the soft ruin eleven years should have written into muscle and bone. He stood in the aisle between bunks in his undershirt and trousers, both fitting perfectly, neither aged a day past what he remembered of himself, and that was its own kind of horror. Around him the reversed crew slept on, small chests rising and falling, and the ship around them ticked and sighed in the language ships use when they are running on fumes and pride.

He found the captain last, because he was avoiding finding the captain.

Captain Idris Voss occupied the end bunk, and he was perhaps fourteen years old. He slept on his back with military precision even now, hands folded at his sternum, and his face — Ansel had to crouch to see it properly, had to make himself look — was the captain's face scoured back six decades. The heavy brows remained. The stubborn jaw remained, softened by childhood but unmistakably itself. On the bulkhead beside the bunk hung the captain's dress uniform jacket, sized for a broad old man, hanging empty as a shed skin.

Ansel stood there for a long moment, holding the shard, feeling the edges of the wound-memory press against him from all sides.

*I know you,* he thought at the sleeping boy. *I signed your fitness reports. You promoted me twice. You told me once, over cards, that you'd buried a wife and a son and that the ship was the only family left to you, and you made me promise never to repeat it.* None of it surfaced as scene or sequence. It came as weight. As certainty without evidence. He knew these things the way you know your own name — not by remembering it but by being unable to be anyone else.

The boy stirred. Opened his eyes. They were dark and clear and sixty years too young, and they fixed on Ansel with an expression of profound, exhausted irritation.

"Voss," Ansel said, and his voice cracked on the name, on the absurdity of addressing a child by a rank and title that belonged to a dead man's shape.

"I know." The captain's voice was a boy's voice, thin and unbroken, delivering words in the cadence of command. "I know what I look like. Help me up."

Ansel helped him up. The captain weighed almost nothing. He sat on the edge of the bunk with his feet dangling short of the deck, and he looked at his own hands — small, unlined, the knuckles of a child — and turned them over once, twice, like a man checking a watch that has stopped.

"Report," he said.

"Sir, I don't—" Ansel's throat closed. "I don't have one. Sir. I don't remember."

Something moved behind the boy's eyes that was too old for them. Recognition, maybe. Pity. "Neither do I," Captain Voss said quietly. "None of us do. We're going to pretend that doesn't frighten us until we've counted heads. Count heads, Ansel."

He counted heads. Nine bunks, nine sleepers, nine children wearing the faces of adults he loved without being able to prove it. And himself. Ten souls, if he still counted as one. He said the number aloud because the captain asked him to, and the captain nodded once, slow, like a man confirming a loss.

"And the shard," the captain said. "Show me."

Ansel opened his hand. The shard sat there, still faintly warm, still scratched, still saying nothing.

"You don't remember taking it," the captain said. It wasn't a question.

"No, sir."

"Then someone gave it to you. Or you took it from someone who couldn't stop you." The boy-captain looked at the shard for a long time, and Ansel watched sixty years of judgment operate behind a child's face, watched experience outlive the body that earned it. "Keep it. Don't read it. Don't lose it. And don't tell the others it exists until we understand why our logs are empty and my bones are twelve years old." He swung his feet, which didn't reach the floor. "Dismissed, Mr. Ansel. And Ansel — sit down before you fall down. You're gray."

He was gray. He hadn't noticed. Fear, apparently, drained a man from the face inward.

* * *

She came aboard four hours later, or maybe six — time had gone soft, measured now in the captain's declining patience and the reserve batteries' slow drain — and Ansel heard her before he saw her: the clang of magnetic boots on the outer hull, three impacts, deliberate, spaced like a knock.

Later he would learn her name was Lena Eriksen, that she flew cargo on the silent route, that she had diverted against protocol and regulation and probably law to reach them. But the first thing he learned about her, the thing that arrived ahead of all facts, was that he trusted her.

It happened in the airlock vestibule, when the inner hatch cycled open and she stepped through into the Halcyon's stale air. She was tall, brown-skinned, close-cropped hair silvering at the temples, wearing a salvager's patched suit with the Concord crest half-scraped from the shoulder. Her eyes went around the vestibule the way a doctor's eyes go around a ward — cataloguing damage, triaging, refusing to flinch — and then they found him, standing there with a data shard in his fist and no explanation in his mouth, and something in his chest simply *settled*. Like a compass needle finding north. Like a bone setting itself.

He did not know why. That was the unbearable part. Trust, in his experience — in whatever experience remained to him — was built, plank by plank, out of shared hours and kept promises. This was not that. This was instantaneous, total, and completely unsupported by anything he could name. He trusted her the way the drowned trust a rope thrown into dark water: not because they've inspected the rope, but because letting go is not survivable.

"You're awake," she said. Not a question either. She was looking at him the way people look at impossible things — carefully, from slightly too far away, ready to revise.

"I'm awake." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Are you real?"

That startled a breath of laughter out of her, brief and unamused. "As much as anything is. You're the navigator's relief? You're not—"

"Intact," he said. "Yes. Everyone else is—" He gestured helplessly toward the berthing compartment, toward the small breathing shapes beyond. "Younger."

Her face did something complicated. He watched her decide, in real time, not to ask the obvious questions yet. He watched discipline win over wonder, and recognized the gesture, and trusted her more for it, which made no sense, which was becoming the theme of his existence.

"Eriksen," she said. "Cargo runner. I picked up your transponder pinging out of nowhere and made a bad decision." She glanced past him down the corridor. "How bad, exactly?"

"Bad enough that I'm glad you made it."

She looked at him again then, longer, and he felt the examination of it, felt her weighing him the way he was weighing her — two strangers in a derelict, each deciding whether the other was a rescue or a trap. Except it wasn't symmetrical. He had already decided. He had decided somewhere below the level of thought, in whatever flooded country lay between the match-colored light and this moment, and his decision had been *her*, specifically, out of all the possible people the universe might have sent.

"Why do you keep looking at me like that?" she asked.

"Like what?"

"Like you're waiting for me to say something."

He considered lying. He found he didn't want to. That, too, was new information about himself.

"I think I'm waiting for you to say something," he admitted. "I don't know what. Something you'll say eventually, maybe. Something I already heard once." He turned the shard over in his fingers. "My memory doesn't come back in order. It comes back as — recognition. I look at things and I know them without knowing why. I looked at you and I knew you."

"I've never seen you before today."

"I know."

"That's not comforting, Ansel."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." He heard himself, heard the flat certainty in his own voice, and marveled at it. Where was this man from? Who had taught him to speak like that — plainly, without apology, as if the truth were a tool he'd carried so long it had worn grooves into his hands?

Eriksen studied him for a long moment. Then she said, "Show me the others," and followed him into the berthing compartment, and stood very still among the bunks, among the small sleepers, and he watched her count them the way he had, watched her arrive at nine and go pale, and when she reached the captain's bunk and saw the empty uniform jacket hanging beside a fourteen-year-old boy, she put her hand flat against the bulkhead as if the ship herself needed steadying.

"What happened to you?" she whispered. Not to any of them. To the ship, maybe. To the whole vanished decade.

"We were somewhere," Ansel said. "For a long time. I can almost—" He pressed the shard against his sternum, unconsciously, the way a man presses a hand to an old scar. "There's a place my memory goes when I let it run. There's a corridor. Match-colored light. Someone screaming my name. And then—" He shook his head. "Then you."

"Match-colored light," she repeated slowly. "Screaming."

"The screaming stops," he said. "That's the worst part. I keep waiting for it to stop and it does, and then I wake up here, and everyone I love is nine years old, and I'm holding a piece of data I stole from God knows where, and I trust the first stranger who walks through the door." He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Tell me that's normal. Tell me trauma does that. Tell me there's a version of this where I'm not losing my mind."

Eriksen looked at him — really looked, the triage gaze again, but softer now, aimed at something beneath his skin — and said, "I've seen men hold onto stranger things than a shard. Myself included." She hesitated. Then, quietly, as if confessing to a crime: "I've got nine frozen backups in my hold that I was ordered to abandon. Dead crewmates. I service their pods every run. I've never opened one." Her jaw tightened. "So no. I don't think you're losing your mind. I think we're both carrying things we were ordered to carry and forbidden to look at."

The words landed in him strangely — not as comfort, exactly, but as kinship. A fellow carrier. He wanted to tell her everything, and had nothing to tell her with. He wanted to ask her everything, and suspected the answers would break him.

Instead he held out the shard.

She looked at it without touching it. "What is it?"

"I don't know. It's warm. It's been warm since before I can remember, which is a shorter sentence than it should be." He closed his fist around it again. "The captain says don't read it. Says we don't know what's watching."

"Your captain," she said, glancing at the boy asleep again in the end bunk, "is fourteen."

"My captain," Ansel said, "has thirty thousand flight hours and a dead wife, and he told me to keep it safe, and I am going to keep it safe, because keeping things safe is apparently the only thing I still know how to do."

Something shifted in her expression then — a crack in the professionalism, brief as a fault-line tremor — and she nodded once, and he understood that she had just decided something too, down in that wordless place where decisions get made before the mind is consulted. She had decided to believe him. She had decided to stay.

"Get your people's medical status together," she said, turning for the corridor. "Life support, food, power reserves. I've got supplies on my ship. And Ansel—" She stopped at the hatchway, silhouetted against the white emergency light, and looked back at him. "When your memory starts coming back — and it will, I've seen shock do strange arithmetic — you come to me first. Before the Concord. Before anyone. Whatever's on that shard, whoever erased you—" Her voice hardened. "They'll send someone official soon. Officials want things tidy. You're not tidy. Neither am I."

She left. He stood alone in the berthing compartment among the small breathing shapes of everyone he had ever cared about, and the shard pulsed its faint warmth against his palm like a second heartbeat, and he realized his hands were shaking — not from fear now, but from something adjacent to hope, which frightened him more.

He trusted her. He still didn't know why. He suspected he would find out, and that finding out would cost him everything he had left, and that he would walk into it anyway with his eyes open, because the alternative was standing still in a white-lit room forever, guarding a secret he couldn't read, surrounded by children who used to be his family, waiting for officials to come and make everything tidy.

Outside the viewport, the stars wheeled slow and indifferent. Somewhere out there, in the dark between silent worlds, a woman with scraped-off crests was carrying supplies toward him across the gulf, and his heart — his stupid, wounded, trusting heart — had already decided she was coming back.

He sat down on the deck beside the captain's bunk, put his back against the cold hull, and waited.

The shard stayed warm.

And far off, at the edge of the system, a transponder pinged — not theirs. Someone else's. Someone official, moving fast, running clean and bright and tidy toward the wreck of the Halcyon and everyone aboard her.
