## 6. Paper Ghosts

The Port Calloway records annex smelled of cold metal and older lies.

Sebastian had been coming here for eleven years—first as a junior adjuster learning which clerks could be bribed with coffee and which required something stronger, now as the man other adjusters came to when a loss refused to make sense. The annex occupied the lower levels of the Consortium Trade Authority building, six floors of server stacks and terminal carrels where the system's commercial memory went to be stored, indexed, and quietly forgotten. Nobody loved this place. That was why he trusted it more than most. People lied on purpose; neglect just told you what it actually didn't want to bother hiding.

He'd taken a carrel in the far corner of Sublevel Four, under a light panel that flickered at a frequency just shy of headache-inducing, and he'd been there for nine hours.

The tea beside his elbow had gone cold twice. He'd drunk it anyway both times, because habits were ballast, and tonight he needed all the ballast he could get.

On the screen: shipping manifests. Thousands of them. He'd pulled every cargo declaration filed along the Meridian corridor for the past thirty years, and he was reading them the way he read everything—not for what they said, but for what they repeated.

Trust nothing written. Trust patterns instead.

It was the first lesson anyone had ever taught him about investigation, back when he was young enough to still believe paperwork was a form of truth. His instructor—a gray woman named Okonkwo whose name appeared in none of the personnel files anymore, though that was a thought for another night—had put it differently. *Documents are testimony,* she'd said. *And everyone who testifies is testifying to something. Your job is to figure out what they're trying not to say.*

So he read the manifests the way you'd read a witness who kept glancing at the door.

* * *

The work itself was simple in the way that drowning is simple. You just kept going down.

Meridian Station—if it had ever existed—had supposedly been decommissioned thirty-one years ago, per the beacon still squawking its cheerful automated lie into the debris field where Sebastian had found it. Decommissioned stations don't order cargo. That was the first wall he hit, and the first crack he found in it: the corridor's traffic logs showed a steady stream of freighter arrivals at coordinates matching Meridian's registered position, right up until nine days before the gravitational flicker. Arrivals logged, yes—but the destination codes were scrambled. Not deleted. Scrambled, the way a clerk scrambles a code when the paperwork has to exist but the destination can't.

Somebody had wanted the shipments findable and the station invisible, and those two requirements had fought each other for thirty years, leaving fingerprints all over the record.

Sebastian pulled the arrival logs and matched them against the cargo declarations by tonnage and date. It took four hours. When the correlation matrix finished rendering, he sat very still and looked at it for a long time.

Ninety-four percent match confidence. A ghost with a pulse.

* * *

He started with water.

That sounded like a joke, even to him, alone in the flickering dark of Sublevel Four. But water was the honest cargo. Water was heavy, awkward, unprofitable to fake, and absolutely non-negotiable for any human habitat. Recycling systems lost mass no matter how good they were—seals wept, scrubbers clogged, people drank and exhaled and died and their bodies held water that eventually left the loop. A station of any size needed replenishment imports measured in hundreds of tons per year, forever, or it became a tomb.

The manifests showed water shipments. Hundreds of tons per year. Destination code scrambled, arrival coordinates consistent, receiving signature always the same string of characters: **MER-LOG-01**.

MER. He let himself feel the small cold thrill of it and then made himself keep working, because a coincidence was just a pattern you hadn't finished checking.

Then air. Gas cannister shipments—nitrogen-oxygen mix, industrial grade, the kind of thing nobody ships to a vacuum. Then protein stock. Then medical isotopes with half-lives too short for storage, meaning someone was using them within weeks of manufacture, meaning someone was *treating patients*. Then—this was the one that stopped him, that made him set down the cold tea and press his palms flat against the desk until the tremor in his hands settled—educational materials. School curricula. Children's readers, physical print runs, shipped quarterly for twenty-two consecutive years.

You don't send schoolbooks to a decommissioned station.

You don't send schoolbooks to a graveyard.

* * *

By hour fourteen he had stopped taking notes and started building. The annex terminals had modeling software meant for reconstructing accident scenarios—cargo shifts, hull failures, the physics of things going wrong. Sebastian repurposed it for the opposite: the physics of things going on.

He fed it thirty years of manifests. Water, air, food, medicine, fuel, spare parts, clothing, furniture, books. Every shipment tagged with its scrambled destination resolved to the same coordinates. The model asked him for a population estimate to balance consumption against supply, and he gave it a range, and it chewed for ninety seconds while the flickering light buzzed overhead like an insect thinking.

Then it drew him a station.

Not a guess—a reconstruction. Consumption rates implied a habitat of roughly eleven thousand permanent residents, growing slowly over three decades, with infrastructure scaled accordingly: hydroponics decks, a medical wing sized for a mid-sized colony, residential volume consistent with family units. The model rendered it as a wireframe ghost rotating slowly in the dark of his screen, and Sebastian understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he was looking at a portrait painted entirely out of groceries.

Eleven thousand tons of life-support imports. Per year. For thirty years.

He did the arithmetic by hand because he didn't trust the machine to break his heart correctly. Three hundred and thirty million tons of supplies, give or take, delivered to coordinates where the official record said there was nothing to deliver to. Eleven thousand people, give or take, eating and drinking and teaching their children to read at a station that had never existed.

The math proved people lived there.

The math also proved they'd died there, because the imports stopped nine days before the flicker—and nothing, in thirty years of meticulous logistical habit, had ever been shipped *out*. No relocation cargo. No evacuation manifest. No final inventory. Thirty years of steady flow into Meridian, and then a closed valve, and then silence.

You don't evacuate a station and leave its furniture behind. You especially don't leave behind thirty years of schoolbooks.

* * *

* * *

He must have dozed, because the light panel's flicker had synced itself into his dreams and he woke with the sensation of a strobe behind his eyes and Imogen Voss's voice somewhere in the back of his skull—*archives remember what people want forgotten*—though she hadn't said anything like that yet, wouldn't say anything like that for days, and the fact that he was already dreaming in her cadence told him something about how alone he'd been.

He checked the chronometer. 0340, station time. The annex was empty except for him and the servers, which hummed in their stacks like a congregation murmuring through a long service.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it the way you notice weather—distantly, impersonally. Nine hours ago this had been a claim. An anomaly, a nine-second flicker, a missing station, a denied report. Paperwork with teeth. Now it was eleven thousand people, and he knew exactly none of their names, and the not-knowing had become unbearable in a way he couldn't have explained to Director Hale or any of the polite machinery that had already told him once, in its courteous automated voice, that no such station existed.

They hadn't erased a station. They'd erased a *town*. Schools and clinics and someone's grandmother and someone's terrible amateur orchestra and kids who'd grown up knowing only the inside of a spinning habitat and the smell of recycled air—and then one day the universe had stuttered for nine seconds, and every one of them had become a debris field and a beacon lying about decommission dates.

Sebastian had spent his career quantifying loss. Dead cargo, dead crews, dead futures, priced to the decimal and filed. He had thought himself hardened to it. He understood now, sitting alone in the humming dark with a wireframe town rotating on his screen, that he had never once actually counted the loss. He'd counted the *paperwork* of loss. The difference between the two was eleven thousand people wide.

Quiet triumph curdled in his chest, slow and chemical. He had proven it. That was the triumph part—the manifests couldn't lie, the tonnage couldn't lie, the schoolbooks couldn't lie. Somewhere in the Consortium's own memory, in the boring unglamorous guts of its logistics apparatus, the truth about Meridian survived in duplicate and triplicate, filed by clerks who'd had no idea what they were witnessing. They could rewrite registries and beacons and news feeds. They apparently could not stop the water from being thirsty.

But if he could find it in fourteen hours, so could they. So could whoever had done the erasing in the first place—and an organization thorough enough to delete a station from reality would be thorough enough to notice a man pulling thirty years of corridor manifests at four in the morning.

Every query he'd run was logged. Of course it was logged. Everything was logged; that was the whole point of the place. He looked at the terminal, at the neat list of his own searches glowing on the screen, and felt the trap close around him with the softness of a form being stamped.

He copied everything anyway. Three physical data spindles, encrypted, one sewn into the lining of his coat, one taped beneath the tea-stained desk drawer of his rented room, one—he hesitated over this, then did it—mailed to himself via the port's slow courier, addressed to a locker under a false name he hadn't used since his second year of licensing. Redundancy was ballast too.

Then he wiped his session, logged out, and walked up through the sleeping annex with the coat heavy against his ribs like a bandaged wound.

* * *

The elevator opened onto the Trade Authority lobby, all marble and muted gold lighting, empty at this hour except for the night concierge and, standing beneath the great illuminated seal of the Consortium, a man in an expensive gray suit who was watching the elevator doors with the patient expression of someone who has been waiting exactly as long as it suited him to wait.

"Mr. Thackeray." The man smiled. It was a good smile, calibrated, the kind that had been tested on focus groups. "Forgive the hour. My name is Hale—Director Corwin Hale, Office of Commercial Integrity. I understand you've been doing some rather remarkable research."

The lobby was very quiet. Somewhere above them, the seal's light hummed.

"I wonder," Director Hale said pleasantly, "if you might have a few minutes to talk about your future."

Sebastian's hand, without consulting him, drifted to the lining of his coat, where eleven thousand tons of evidence rode against his heart.

"I have a few minutes," he said.
