## 21. Triage

The *Calloway's Grace* came out of jump with a shudder that Imogen felt in her teeth, and then the pain arrived properly, like a passenger who had been waiting politely in the corridor.

She was on the deck of the aft hold, and there was blood under her, hers, spreading in a slow black bloom across the plating where the relay station's shattered hull had opened her side. She could hear Sebastian moving somewhere above her—his boots on the ladder rungs, fast, uneven—and she thought, with a strange archival clarity, *this is what it sounds like when a record is being made. This moment will exist whether anyone writes it down or not.*

"Imogen." He dropped to his knees beside her, and his hands were already moving, peeling back her jacket, finding the wound below her ribs with a field medic's economy. "Talk to me. Stay with me."

"I'm not going anywhere," she said. "I can't. It hurts too much."

"That's the right answer." He had the medkit open. She watched him work—the antiseptic foam, the pressure seal, the coagulant injector pressed hard against her flank until she gasped. His face was very close, lit by the emergency strip above them, and she saw something in it she hadn't seen before: not fear for himself. Fear for her. It looked almost unfamiliar on him, like a word from a dead language.

"The hunters," she managed. "Did we lose them?"

"They broke off at the relay." His jaw tightened. "They didn't need to chase us. They got what they wanted."

"You mean me bleeding out on your floor."

"I mean they knew our vector before we jumped. Which means someone told them, or someone tracked us, and I've swept this ship twice." He said it flatly, without accusation, but the words sat between them anyway, heavy as ballast.

Imogen closed her eyes. The pain was a bright, insistent thing, and beneath it she felt the particular exhaustion of someone who has been carrying a secret through gunfire.

"Sebastian." She opened her eyes again. "There's something you need to know. And you're going to want to do it while your hands are busy, so keep working."

His hands did not stop. Good. She'd counted on that.

"Since Port Calloway," she said, "since the night you showed me the manifests and I showed you the symphony—I've been transmitting copies of everything. The broadcast fragment. Hale's ledger fragment. The black box data, as soon as we cracked its checksums. All of it. On a tight-beam burst, every time we docked, every time we passed within range of a comm buoy. To a dead-drop."

The silence lasted three seconds. She counted them, because counting was what she did when the world came apart—it was how she'd been raised, among archivists who believed that numbering the seconds of a catastrophe was a form of respect.

Sebastian sat back on his heels. The coagulant gun stayed in his hand. His face did something complicated—hurt first, she thought, actual hurt, before the investigator's machinery caught it and ground it into something harder.

"A dead-drop," he repeated. "You don't know who's on the other end."

"No."

"You transmitted evidence of a consortium weapons program—evidence they've killed a memory dealer for, shot down a relay crew, put a bounty on both of us—to an unknown recipient. Repeatedly." His voice was very quiet, which she had learned was worse than loud. "And you're telling me now. After Ambush number one."

"After we were ambushed," she corrected. "Yes. Because you deserved to know while you were deciding whether to keep patching me up or space me, and I wanted to watch your face while you decided."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. "That's a terrible reason."

"It's an honest one. I deal in honest records, even about myself." She winced as he taped down the pressure seal. "Especially about myself. You of all people should understand why. You spent four years not knowing what was in your own head. I refuse to be a gap in anyone's archive—including mine."

He worked in silence for a while. The ship hummed around them, running dark, coasting on a lazy drift toward nowhere in particular while they figured out where nowhere was safe. She felt him clean the wound's edges, felt the dermal binder pull skin together, and let herself catalog the sensations the way she catalogued everything: pain, seven out of ten; blood loss, moderate; prognosis, if he kept his hands steady, good.

"Who set up the drop?" he asked finally.

"My mother."

He looked up sharply. "Voss. Your mother was exiled before you were born. She died—"

"Eleven years ago. Yes. But exile doesn't stop correspondence; it just makes it slow. She maintained a network. Old archivists, scattered families, people who lost worlds in the Rewrites and never signed the amnesty. Before she died she gave me the routing keys and told me: *if you ever find something true and terrible, don't hold it alone. Hold it the way water is held—in many vessels, so breaking one changes nothing.*" Imogen breathed carefully around the binder. "So I've been pouring everything into that network since Calloway. I don't know who holds the vessels now. That was the design. Even I'm not supposed to know. If they take me, they take one node, and the record survives."

"And if one of those nodes is compromised? If one of your mother's faithful dead-droppers sold out?" He gestured vaguely in the direction they'd come, toward the relay where the ambush had bloomed out of vacuum like a trap springing shut. "Because that's what happened back there, Imogen. They were waiting at a relay we chose forty minutes before we jumped. Nobody followed us there. Nobody could have. Unless they read our route out of a transmission."

The thought landed in her stomach like cold iron, because she had already had it, alone, in the cockpit, while he prepped the jump. She had lain against the bulkhead doing arithmetic she hated: *the drop went out six hours before the ambush. Correlation is not causation. Correlation is also never nothing.*

"I've considered it," she said quietly.

"And?"

"And the math cuts both ways. My transmissions are tight-beam, encrypted on keys my mother built before the Rewrites—keys the consortium's cryptographers have publicly claimed are unbreakable, which either means they're lying or they're embarrassed. If the network were burned, they wouldn't have sent three hunters to a relay. They'd have come to Kestrel Deep's coordinates, which I have never transmitted anywhere, and burned the colony in its sleep." She held his gaze. "The ambush means they're still hunting *us*, Sebastian. Not the archive. Us. Two witnesses. That tells me the record itself is still safe."

"Or it tells you exactly what they want it to tell you."

"Yes," she admitted. "It might. I can't rule it out. That's the cost of distributed trust—you trade certainty for survival, and some days the exchange rate is terrible." She reached out, found his wrist, and held it—not to stop him, just to make him feel her pulse, steady under his fingers. "But here's what I know. When you found out your own memories were stolen, you didn't burn the black box. You carried it. When the archivists voted to publish, you gave them the one key that made it possible, knowing what it would cost you. You keep choosing the record over yourself. So does the network. We're the same kind of animal, you and I. I bet my life on that before I ever met you."

He didn't answer immediately. He finished the last of the binding, smoothed the seal along her ribs with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about him, and sat back against the bulkhead across from her, medkit closed, hands finally empty.

"When I was a technician," he said slowly, "I believed in single points of failure. One console. One operator. One deletion, and eleven thousand people stopped existing. Clean. Efficient. I built—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, "—I helped build a system where one man with credentials could unmake a world. Everything I've done since has been trying to become the opposite of that. Distributed. Redundant. Impossible to erase." He looked at her. "What you've been doing is the opposite of that. I should have been angry for ten seconds and then grateful. Give me the ten seconds; I already used them."

"You used them well. Very restrained. Most men would have made it a full minute."

"Don't push it." But the hurt was draining out of his face, replaced by something wearier and more durable. "One condition. From now on, no more solo transmissions. Whatever goes out, we compose together, we encrypt together, we send together. If the network is a vessel, fine—but I'm not going to be cargo in it without knowing I'm aboard."

"Agreed." She meant it. Then, because honesty was the whole point: "There's one more thing. The last transmission—the one six hours before the ambush—I didn't send the usual summary. I sent a request. A question."

His eyes narrowed. "What question?"

"I asked them whether anyone had ever heard of a project called Lattice operating before Meridian. Whether there were earlier tests. Earlier stations." She watched the color change in his face. "Because the black box proves what happened *at* Meridian. It doesn't prove Meridian was the first. And if we're going to stand in front of the system and say *this happened*, someone—Hale, the council, the courts—is going to ask why we believe a corrupted shard and a salvaged box over eleven thousand tons of official records saying nothing happened at all. We need precedent. We need pattern. Patterns are the only thing you trust, remember? Trust nothing written, trust patterns instead. Well. I went looking for the pattern."

"And?"

"And nothing yet. The drop is slow—weeks sometimes, months. Whoever answers answers on their own schedule, through relays I can't predict." She shifted against the bulkhead, testing the wound; it held, a deep hot ache instead of a scream. "But there's one more thing I haven't told you, and you're going to hate it."

"Imogen."

"The acknowledgment ping came back early. Twelve hours after the ambush, not weeks. Someone in the network moved fast for the first time in eleven years. And the message wasn't an answer." She pulled the thin flexi-slate from her jacket pocket—her jacket, ruined, blood-stiff, but the slate intact in its shielded lining—and held it out to him. "It was a warning. Three words."

He took it. Read it. She watched him go pale in the emergency light, watched his lips move silently around the words she already knew by heart:

***THEY KNOW KESTREL.***

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The ship drifted, dark and cold, its passive array drinking in a sky full of nothing.

"That's impossible," Sebastian said at last. "You just said you never transmitted the coordinates. The archivists scrubbed every approach log. The colony's existed for thirty years without—"

"Without being found. I know what I said." Her side throbbed in time with her heartbeat, and she made herself think past it, made herself do what she did best: order the fragments, find the shape in the noise. "So let's treat it like any other document. Verify provenance. Test for forgery. Who benefits from telling us the colony is blown?"

"Hale. If you panic, you lead us straight into a trap—or you scatter the archivists prematurely and they make mistakes."

"Agreed. Alternative: the network is genuine, the warning is genuine, and my mother's vessels have a leak none of us can see." She met his eyes. "Or third option, the one I like least: the warning is genuine, the network is genuine, and nobody leaked anything—because Hale never needed a leak. Because he's known about Kestrel Deep for years and left it alone deliberately, the way you leave a mouse hole alone until the day you need to flood the field."

Sebastian stared at her. "Why would he leave the last free archive in the system untouched?"

"Because archives are only dangerous when they move," she said. "A hidden colony of rememberers is a curiosity. A hidden colony that publishes? That's a declaration of war. He let them sleep because they were sleeping. And then we arrived carrying a black box and a vote." She laughed, short and bitter, and immediately regretted it as the binder pulled. "We didn't lead them to Kestrel, Sebastian. We *woke* it. There's a difference, and it matters legally, morally, and not at all to whoever's flying the strike fleet."

He was on his feet before she finished, moving toward the cockpit with the focused urgency she'd seen in him only twice before—at the debris field, and at the Spire console in her nightmares. "Then we warn them. Now. Full burn, direct beam, encrypted on the colony's own keys—"

"Which confirms to anyone listening that the transmission source knows Kestrel exists. If the warning is bait, we bite it and drag the fleet straight to the colony's throat." She struggled upright, hissing, one hand braced against the bulkhead, and planted herself between him and the cockpit door. "Think like an investigator, not a rescue party. What do we actually know?"

He stopped. Breathed. She watched the discipline descend over him like a visor—the weary competence she'd first seen on a freighter between silent worlds, the man who trusted patterns because patterns couldn't lie to him.

"What we know," he said slowly, "is that a twelve-hour response time from a network that historically takes months means someone prioritized us. Either a friend in high places or an enemy wearing a friend's coat. What we know is that the ambush at the relay used hunter signatures consistent with consortium black ops, which means Hale committed visible assets—which means he's running out of patience and starting to spend deniability. And what we know—" he paused, and something old and cold moved behind his eyes, "—is that I spent four years inside Project Lattice, and I never once saw a file on Kestrel Deep. Never. An operation that thorough, that paranoid, that obsessed with erasing every loose thread—they would have had a folder. They had folders on *memory dealers*. If Lattice never flagged the colony..."

"Then either the colony is genuinely invisible," Imogen finished, "or the person who ran the erasure wanted it invisible. Wanted a place where the forbidden records could survive." She felt the idea settle over her like snowfall, vast and quiet and terrifying. "Someone inside the consortium, all these years, keeping a sanctuary alive. Keeping vessels ready. Waiting for someone to pour the truth into them."

"Your mother's network."

"My mother's network." Her voice was steady. Her pulse, under the binder, was not. "Twelve-hour response. Knowledge of Kestrel. Access to consortium operational tempo. Mother, if you planned this from beyond the grave, I am going to find a way to be furious at you and grateful to you simultaneously, which I believe is called inheritance."

Sebastian crossed back to her, took the flexi-slate gently from her hand, and turned it over as if the answer might be printed on the back. "If there's a protector inside Hale's own house, then the warning isn't a trap. It's a clock. They're telling us the field is about to be flooded, mouse hole or no mouse hole, because they can't stop it anymore—only get the mice out first." He looked up. "How long do we have?"

The ship answered before she could. The passive array chimed—a soft, apologetic tone—and the traffic display bloomed across the cockpit doorway: drive signatures, distant, multiple, burning hard on a vector that intersected their drift and, four hours behind it, the empty stretch of black where a colony of rememberers had slept for thirty years believing itself forgotten.

Imogen counted the signatures. Seven. Then nine. Then the display refreshed and she stopped counting, because counting had stopped helping.

"Less than that," she said.
