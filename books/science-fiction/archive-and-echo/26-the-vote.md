## 26. The Vote

The chamber beneath Kestrel Deep had no name, which Imogen understood to be a kind of naming in itself. Everything here was deliberate. The room sat at the colony's oldest stratum, carved from the asteroid's nickel-iron heart before the Rewrites, before the consortium, before anyone had thought to fear a filing cabinet. Its walls were shelved floor to ceiling with physical media—crystal lattices, magnetic tape wound on spools gone brittle with age, paper. Actual paper, yellowed and foxed, sealed behind glass like the relics of a drowned civilization.

She had been on Kestrel Deep for six days, and she had spent four of them in this room, watching the archivists argue.

They were not what she had expected. She had imagined zealots—gaunt fanatics clutching forbidden truth like a torch. Instead they were accountants of the impossible: a heavyset woman named Odile Ferran who catalogued extinct languages; a thin, nervous man called Pell who had once been the consortium's own chief of archival integrity before he'd seen what integrity was used for; an ancient, half-blind technician everyone simply called Mother Quill, who had helped build the first archive spires and now devoted her remaining years to documenting how they lied. Eleven of them in all, plus the colony's elected speaker, a broad-shouldered ex-freighter pilot named Dagny Holt who spoke rarely and whose silences carried more weight than most people's arguments.

And Sebastian.

He stood at the edge of the chamber near the door, as he always did—not quite part of the circle, not quite outside it. Four days since the restoration. Four days since he had learned what he was, what he had done, and had not run. Imogen kept finding herself glancing at him the way one checks a wound dressing: frequently, compulsively, half expecting to find it torn open.

Tonight there would be no more arguing. Tonight they would vote.

* * *

Dagny Holt opened the session the way she opened everything, without ceremony. "You've heard the authentication findings. Mother Quill's protocols confirmed the black box data is genuine—unmanipulated, uncorrupted, timestamped within tolerance. You've heard Imogen Voss's analysis of Project Lattice. You've heard the testimony." Her eyes moved briefly to Sebastian, then away. "What you haven't heard is my opinion, because I don't have one yet. I want to hear yours. All of it. Then we vote."

Pell went first, because Pell always went first—he was a man who processed fear by speaking through it. "Publishing is suicide," he said, and his voice cracked on the word, and he pushed on anyway, which Imogen privately counted as courage. "I worked inside the Spire. I know what Hale commands. Three hunter-killers minimum, probably a full strike fleet under pirate-suppression authorization. They will come here. They will find us. And when they do, they won't just kill us—they'll erase us. Every record we hold, every name we've preserved, eleven centuries of forbidden history, gone in an afternoon. We are the last copy of things that exist nowhere else. You don't burn the last copy to make a point."

"We wouldn't be burning it," said Odile Ferran. "We'd be copying it. That's the entire difference."

"Copying it into a system that deletes copies."

"Into every terminal on every world at once," Odile said. "You can't delete what's already in ten million heads. That's the whole lesson of Meridian, Pell. The consortium didn't kill those people by shooting them. They killed them by making sure no one remembered them. Memory is the only territory that matters. Everything else is weather."

"And memory can be rewritten too!" Pell's composure finally slipped entirely. "You said so yourself—the Rewrites weren't perfect, but they were good enough. Good enough that I spent six years certifying lies and calling it integrity. If we broadcast and fail—if even one relay node scrubs the payload before propagation completes—we haven't published the truth. We've told the consortium exactly where we are, exactly what we have, and given them time to write a better lie over it. We lose everything, and the dead stay dead twice."

Silence followed, the heavy kind. In it, Imogen heard the hum of the colony's air scrubbers, the tick of old tape spools settling in their cases, and beneath it all the distant groan of rock—Kestrel Deep breathing around them.

Mother Quill spoke last among the elders, her voice papery but unhurried. "My girl," she said, and Imogen realized with a start she meant her, "how many records does this colony hold?"

"Four hundred thousand discrete items," Imogen said. "Approximately."

"And how many people have read them? Truly read them, start to finish, carried them in their minds?"

Imogen hesitated. "Dozens. Perhaps a hundred, over the years."

"A hundred people keeping four hundred thousand truths alive in the dark." The old woman nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis. "That is not preservation. That is embalming. A record no one living has touched isn't an archive. It's a grave we visit to feel virtuous." She turned her clouded eyes toward the center of the room. "I helped build the first Spire. I believed, then, that if you made remembering easy enough, cheap enough, universal enough, no one would ever dare erase anything again. I was wrong about human nature and right about the machinery. The machinery works beautifully. It is always the hands that fail." She folded her hands in her lap. "I say we test the hands again. Publish."

* * *

The debate turned after that, and Imogen listened the way she had been trained to listen—not to words but to structure, to the architecture beneath the argument. She noticed that no one asked whether the record was true. That question had died four days ago, in this same chamber, when the verification protocols came back clean and the eleven thousand names began scrolling up the wall display, one after another after another, until someone asked for it to stop and no one could say why.

What they were actually debating was arithmetic. The value of their survival against the value of the truth. Four hundred thousand items against eleven thousand names. A hundred keepers against ten million strangers who might, or might not, care.

It was Sebastian who broke the arithmetic.

He hadn't spoken all evening. When he finally moved forward into the lamplight, the whole room seemed to recalibrate around him, the way rooms did—she'd noticed it since the restoration, the way people couldn't quite look at him and couldn't quite stop. He looked thinner than he had a week ago. Wiped out from the inside.

"I need to say something," he said, "and then I need you to disregard everything about me except the thing itself, because I'm the worst possible messenger for it." His voice was steady. Too steady, the way a hull plate is steady right up until it isn't. "I helped build Lattice. I helped harvest Meridian. I erased myself afterward because I couldn't live with it, and erasure worked—it worked so well that I spent four years as an honest man investigating other people's losses while my own sat in my skull like shrapnel. So believe me when I tell you what erasure is. It is not peace. It is not mercy. It is the consortium's product. Forgetting is literally what they sell." He looked around the room, meeting eyes one by one, refusing to let anyone look away. "Hale offered me a scrubbed mind and a restored career three days ago in the Spire. Clean slate. No guilt. That's the competing offer, always, everywhere: safety in exchange for amnesia. This colony exists because some of you refused that trade. If you vote to hide, you're not choosing survival. You're choosing a slower version of the same deal."

"You don't get to—" Pell started.

"No," Sebastian agreed. "I don't. I'm not voting. I just needed the room to hear it from someone who took the other road and knows where it goes."

He stepped back to his place by the door. The silence that followed was different from the earlier ones—less like hesitation, more like held breath.

* * *

Dagny Holt called the vote at the second hour.

"Show of hands, and I'll count honestly, including abstentions. Publishing means the broadcast plan: simultaneous injection at the root archive node, full propagation, no partial releases. Hiding means sealing the black box back into the deep vaults and dispersing the couriers quietly. Neither option includes surrendering anyone to the consortium." She paused. "Vote."

Imogen raised her hand before she fully decided to, the way she'd once intercepted Sebastian at the memory market—her body committing before her caution caught up. Around the circle, hands rose. Odile Ferran. Mother Quill, her arm trembling with age but rising all the same. Two others. Three. Five.

Six against four, with one abstention.

Dagny Holt looked at the count for a long moment, and something in her broad face shifted—not triumph, nothing so simple. Resignation braided with relief, maybe. The look of a woman who had been waiting years for permission to stop running.

"Then we publish," she said. "Which brings us to the problem."

"The problem," Odile said dryly, "being that the root archive node sits at the top of the Archive Spire, behind nine layers of consortium security, guarded by the same fleet that's hunting us."

"Not quite the problem." Dagny shook her head. "The problem is that we don't have access. Root injection requires credentials at the administrator tier. Credentials that were revoked, purged, or buried when the Rewrites finished. In four hundred years, no exile has ever held root access to the Spire. That's rather the point of the Spire."

The room turned, slowly, the way water turns around a drain—and Imogen watched it happen, watched every gaze in the chamber arrive at the same destination, and felt her stomach drop before she understood why.

Sebastian had gone very still.

"I used to have them," he said quietly. "Administrator tier. Lattice program credentials. I used them to wipe myself out of the record." He laughed, a short, hollow sound with no humor in it at all. "Everyone assumed the purge killed the account. But I built the purge. I wrote it to be reversible. In case I ever—" He stopped. Swallowed. "In case I ever wanted to remember. I left myself a door."

Mother Quill leaned forward, blind eyes bright. "Does it still open?"

"I don't know," Sebastian said. "Four years is a long time. Hale knew about Lattice technicians; he may have closed every door I left. But if any path into the root node still exists anywhere in this system—" he exhaled, and Imogen saw his hands, flat and steady on the table, holding themselves still by force, "—it's mine. My sin. My key."

The chamber was silent again, but it was a different silence now—not the silence of a community weighing its survival, but the silence of a community realizing its survival had just acquired a price tag written in one man's name.

Dagny Holt straightened. "Then we have a vote and we have a door. What we don't have is time. The moment our authentication ping left this rock, the clock started. Hale will trace it—he'll already be tracing it. So we move tonight." She looked around the circle one final time. "Disperse the archives to the couriers by dawn. Skeleton team for the Spire infiltration. Volunteers only, and understand what volunteering means."

Imogen raised her hand a second time that night.

So did Sebastian.

Their eyes met across the chamber—across the shelves of dead languages and extinct histories, across four hundred thousand truths that had waited centuries in the dark for someone brave enough, or foolish enough, to carry them into the light.

Outside, far above them, the cold rock of Kestrel Deep turned silently in its hidden orbit, and somewhere beyond it, gathering like weather, the strike fleet was already moving.
