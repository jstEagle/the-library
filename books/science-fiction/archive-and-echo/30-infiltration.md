## 30. Infiltration

The Archive Spire rose out of the administrative district of Vantage Prime like a needle threaded through the planet's sky, two kilometers of white composite and glass, and every light on it was a lie.

I'd been looking at it for twenty minutes from the roof of a parking structure that smelled of ozone and cold rain, and the longer I looked, the more I understood what it actually was. Not a building. A throat. Everything the system knew about itself passed down through that spire—every birth, every debt, every death, every decommissioned station—and somewhere in its depths, on a floor I could not see from here, there was a room where a man had once sat at a console and deleted eleven thousand people with the same keystrokes he used to delete himself.

That man was me. And in forty minutes, I was going to walk back into his hands.

"You're doing the thing again," Imogen said beside me.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at a building like it owes you money." She was crouched at the parapet, her contractor's coveralls dull orange with reflective striping, her hair stuffed under a cap that made her look like every maintenance tech on Vantage Prime and no one at all. "We go in through the loading bay. We are two contractors from Halloway Facilities Services with valid work orders, valid badges, and a valid reason to be bored. That's the whole job until we reach the spine. Bored is armor."

"Bored," I repeated.

"It's the most convincing disguise there is. Nobody looks twice at bored." She stood, rolled her shoulders, and for a moment her hand found my sleeve—not a grip, just contact, the way you'd steady yourself on a railing. "Say it back to me. Who are we?"

"R. Calloway and T. Meric, Halloway Facilities Services, contracted for coolant-loop recalibration, sublevels nine through fourteen."

"And why do you know that so well?"

"Because I wrote the work order."

"Because you wrote the work order," she agreed. "Which means it's real. We're not forging our way in, Sebastian. We're filing our way in. There's a difference, and the difference is that paperwork gets believed."

That had been her insight, back on the courier that brought us to Vantage Prime, and it was the kind of insight that only an archivist would have. You don't break into an archive. Archives are built to resist breaking. You *enter* an archive, properly, with documentation, because an archive's deepest assumption—the assumption it cannot function without—is that records are true. Hale's people had rewritten history on a planetary scale, and the only reason it had worked was that the system trusted its own paper. So we gave it paper. Imogen had spent six days building Halloway Facilities Services out of nothing: a registration filed through three shell layers, a tax history, a service contract with the Spire's own facilities division, signed by a facilities manager who existed only as a signature and would never, in any universe, be asked a question in person.

The consortium had erased a station by writing it out of existence. Imogen was going to erase our way in by writing us *into* existence. I remember thinking, on the courier, that it was the most elegant act of revenge I had ever seen: using their own weapon against them, at the scale of two people instead of eleven thousand.

"Last chance," she said now. "We walk away, Kestrel Deep stays hidden, and we live long boring lives on a rock full of people who remember too much."

"You'd hate that."

"I'd *love* that," she said. "That's the problem. Come on."

* * *

The loading bay of the Archive Spire was a cathedral's back door: freight lifts, stacked pallets of storage media, and a smell of cold metal and machine oil that I knew the way you know a childhood house. Two guards at the checkpoint, bored themselves, scanning badges with the enthusiasm of men eight hours into a shift. My badge chirped green. Imogen's chirped green. The guard glanced up, glanced at our work order, glanced at the coolant diagnostic kit I was carrying, and waved us through with a jerk of his thumb.

That was all. That was the entire defense of the machine that owned the truth of eleven thousand dead. A badge, a signature, and the assumption that the record was the reality.

I kept my eyes down and my pace dull. Bored is armor. We crossed the freight floor, took the service lift marked SUBLEVELS 7–15, and stood shoulder to shoulder with two actual Halloway contractors—real ones, assigned to a different loop, chattering about a broken recycler on twelve—until the lift opened on nine and the world changed.

The spine of the Archive Spire was not a corridor. It was a vertical shaft, maybe forty meters across, and it went up and down beyond the reach of the lights. Around its circumference, ring after ring after ring, the server cathedrals opened like the galleries of some inverted colosseum: tiered banks of storage and processing cores, their status lights breathing in slow synchronized waves, blue to violet to blue, ten thousand pulses per ring, forty rings visible before the dark swallowed the rest. The sound was the thing no one warned you about. Not silence—the opposite of silence. A hum so deep and so total it stopped being sound and became pressure, became the feeling of standing inside a held breath. Every secret in the system was breathing around us.

"Sublevel nine," Imogen murmured, consulting the work order like she'd read it a hundred times. "Coolant loop C. We go down."

We went down. Service stairwells clanged under our boots; maintenance drones drifted past at head height, indifferent. On ten, we passed a sealed gallery behind blast glass where the cores were older—physical platters, the kind they'd stopped making decades ago—and I felt Imogen slow, just slightly, the way she always slowed near old records. Her hand brushed the glass as we went by. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. Somewhere in those dead platters, probably, was a copy of something the consortium had ordered destroyed, sitting unindexed and forgotten, and it was killing her not to stop.

"Later," I said quietly.

"I know." Her hand dropped. "Later."

On eleven, the first checkpoint.

It wasn't much—a second badge reader, a camera dome with a slow red sweep. But past it, the signage changed. MAINTENANCE AUTHORIZATION LEVEL 2. And past *that*, I knew from the schematics Imogen had reconstructed from a decade of public facility filings, the floors began to matter. Twelve held the index layer: the master catalog that decided what a record was and whether it existed. Thirteen held the write-once vaults. Fourteen—

Fourteen held the root node. The one machine in the system with authority over every other archive. And on fourteen, in a room I had walked into four years ago with a technician's clearance and a technician's empty calm, there was a console where I had once typed a station out of the world.

The badge reader took my credential and thought about it for one second too long.

I kept my face slack. Bored. Tired. A man thinking about his lunch. The light went green, and the checkpoint gate folded open, and I exhaled through my nose like a man who hadn't noticed anything at all.

"You held," Imogen said under the hum, as we climbed.

"It knew my name."

"Everything in this building knows your name. That's the point of it." She glanced at me, and under the cap brim her eyes were steady. "It just doesn't know it yet."

* * *

Sublevel twelve was where the plan stopped being a plan.

The coolant loop on twelve had a fault—a real one, we discovered, because reality is under no obligation to match your cover story. The loop's primary pump was down and a crew of three genuine technicians had the access corridor torn open, tools everywhere, a supervisor with a clipboard asking questions of everyone who came near.

Imogen didn't hesitate. She walked straight up to the supervisor, work order out, and said, "Halloway, sublevel nine through fourteen recalibration. Looks like you've got your hands full on twelve—want us to start deep and work up? Saves us crossing paths."

The supervisor looked at the order, looked at the corridor he'd rather not have two extra bodies crowding, and waved us toward the far stairwell. "Fourteen's sealed above the loop gallery. You'll need to route through thirteen. Badge'll open the interconnect."

"Thirteen's write-once," Imogen said, with exactly the right note of a contractor mildly annoyed at an inconvenience.

"Thirteen's write-once for data. You're carrying a wrench." He was already turning back to his pump. "Don't touch anything that hums."

We routed through thirteen.

I had expected the write-once vaults to feel like a tomb. They felt worse. The galleries on thirteen were darker—the cores here ran cold, their lights dimmed to preserve the media—and the hum dropped away into something closer to silence, a silence with weight, the acoustic equivalent of deep water. Row after row of sealed storage, each rack stenciled with a classification code and a date range, and the date ranges went back further than I could comfortably think. This was where the system kept what it never intended to change. Births. Treaties. The original charters of the trade routes I had flown my whole life. And somewhere in these cold dark rows, if Hale's ledger fragment was accurate, the operational records of Project Lattice—harvest manifests, yield reports, the logistics of turning human consciousness into nine seconds of gravitational violence—filed under codes no one would ever think to mourn.

Eleven thousand tons of life-support imports that went nowhere. I had found the math in manifests like these. The people themselves were filed here too, somewhere, as yield.

"Sebastian." Imogen's voice, very low. "Your hands."

I looked down. My fists were white-knuckled at my sides, and I hadn't decided to do it. I made myself open them, one finger at a time.

"I worked on this floor," I said. "Not this gallery. The processing annex, north side. Fourteen months." The memory was still a gap, mostly—the archivists at Kestrel Deep had restored the shape of it, the sequence of days, but not the texture; the texture was still gone, like a room described in detail by someone who'd never let you inside. "I can't feel it. You'd think—I don't know. You'd think standing here I'd feel something."

"Maybe the feeling is that there's nothing to feel," Imogen said. "Maybe that's what they took. Not the days. The weight of them." She touched my elbow. "We'll get the weight back. That's the whole point of tonight. Come on."

The interconnect to fourteen was a heavy door with a reader that wanted a Level 2 authorization, and my badge had it, because I had written it into my own work order six days ago, and the door opened onto a short service gallery and then—

Fourteen.

The root node's floor was different from everything below it. Quieter. Colder. The server banks here stood in perfect ranks with wide aisles between them, and the lighting was a flat, sourceless white that erased shadows and made everything look slightly unreal, like a memory of a room rather than the room itself. At the center, behind a curve of blast glass, the root node itself: a single black column, featureless, humming at a frequency I felt in my teeth rather than heard. Every archive in the system answered to it. Every terminal on every world, every registry, every beacon repeating its lie about Meridian Station—downstream of that column. What we seeded there tonight would flow into all of it.

And at the gallery's far end, past the node, a plain white door with a plain gray placard:

**LATTICE OPERATIONS — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL**

The room where I worked. Still there. Still staffed, probably, by someone doing what I had done, on whatever project came after Meridian.

"We're early," Imogen murmured. She checked the time against the maintenance window we'd engineered—forty minutes, a scheduled coolant purge during which the root node's external audit feeds would cycle and leave a gap, a gap that existed because the system trusted its own maintenance schedules the way it trusted everything else about itself. "Thirty-eight minutes. We stage in the loop gallery, wait for the purge cycle, then I seed the payload from the node's service interface while you—" She stopped.

"What."

She was looking past me, down the long white aisle, toward the Lattice Operations door. It was open. Just a crack. Just enough to show a wedge of warmer light and the edge of a desk.

"That door's supposed to be sealed," she said. "Level 3 clearance, biometric. It's on a separate air system. Why is it open at—" She checked the time again. "—three in the morning, local?"

"Maybe someone works late."

"Maybe." But her voice had changed, and I knew the change, because I'd heard it in her a hundred times over the last months: the archivist's instinct, the one that said *records don't lie but the people who keep them do.* "Sebastian. The purge cycle. Who schedules it?"

"Facilities. Automated."

"Who *approved* the work order that got us badges into this building?"

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the Spire's climate control. "The facilities division. Through three shell layers. You built them."

"I built them," she agreed quietly. "And six days is six days. If anyone pulled a thread—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. We had both spent the whole journey to Vantage Prime telling each other that the plan was elegant, that paperwork gets believed, that the system's deepest assumption was our deepest cover. Neither of us had said the other thing. That Hale had built this system's paper too. That he had erased a station and an investigator's entire history with keystrokes, and that a man like that does not leave his machine unguarded in the one week his enemies are known to be moving.

The Lattice Operations door drifted another centimeter open.

From inside it, unhurried, almost courteous, a voice I knew—the voice from a conference room in Port Calloway, the voice that had offered me a lucrative posting very far away—said:

"You can come in, Mr. Thackeray. Both of you. I've been looking forward to giving you the tour."
