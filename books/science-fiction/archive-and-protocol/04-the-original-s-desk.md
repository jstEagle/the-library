## 4. The Original's Desk

The desk had been sealed for eleven years.

Astrid stood before it in the archive's back room, where the ventilation hummed at a frequency just below hearing and the light came from strips that flickered at the far end like something breathing. The desk itself was unremarkable—standard-issue station furniture, grey composite, one drawer swollen shut from a decade of thermal cycling. But it had been sealed deliberately. A strip of archival tape crossed the drawer seam, and on the tape, in handwriting he knew better than his own, someone had written: *For whoever comes after.*

He knew the handwriting because it was his handwriting. Or nearly. The clone labs had replicated the original's motor patterns along with everything else, and when Astrid wrote, his hand made the same slight forward slant on the *r*, the same habit of crossing the *t* with a single impatient stroke. He had once spent an entire evening in the transit dorm writing the alphabet over and over, trying to find some difference—some proof that he was not merely a continuation of a woman he had never met.

He had found none. That was the problem with being an inheritance: everything about you had already been decided.

*For whoever comes after.* He supposed that was him. There was no one else it could be.

He broke the tape.

The drawer resisted, then gave with a sound like a small bone cracking. Inside: a ceramic mug with a chip in the lip. A wool cardigan folded with unnecessary precision, smelling faintly of cedar and cold. A deck of actual playing cards, the corners soft with handling. And beneath these domestic artifacts, wrapped in a static cloth, a slate—old model, the kind with a physical stylus, the kind you used when you didn't want a thing to live on the network.

Astrid's hands went still above the contents. He had the strange, doubled sensation he was learning to expect: he recognized the mug. Not as an object in an archive, but as *her* mug, the one she drank cold tea from because she always forgot it on the heater, and the recognition arrived with a taste—tannin and station-recycled water—and a small irritation at herself for forgetting *again*, and he had to stand there for four full seconds, breathing, before the feeling drained back down into whatever sealed aquifer it came from.

The memory fragments were supposed to be inert. Contextual, the orientation materials had said. *Reference material for archival continuity.* No one had mentioned that reference material could have a flavor.

He picked up the slate. It woke at his thumbprint—of course it did; it was, in a sense, his thumbprint—and displayed a single folder with no name, only a string of characters that resolved, as he watched, into a prompt for a passphrase.

He tried her birthdate. Denied. Her archivist credentials. Denied. The names of the sleepers, in bay order. Denied, denied, denied. He sat back and looked at the ceiling, at the flickering light strip, and felt the particular humiliation of a man locked out of his own mind.

Then, without deciding to, his hands typed: *rain*.

The folder opened.

He stared at the word on the screen, then at his own fingers, which had moved with the confidence of a pianist who knows the piece better than the performer does. He had no memory of choosing that word. But somewhere in the sediment of him, the original had chosen it, had buried her notes under the one thing a station-born or Earth-raised archivist would never guess, because no one aboard Meridian had ever seen rain.

* * *

The notes were not notes, exactly. They were a conversation with herself, dated across the last fourteen months of her life, and Astrid read them the way you read a letter from someone you loved who died angry.

*Day 212. Protocol flagged an anomaly in the Reyes file again. Third time. It keeps re-filing the same request for a death certificate that was never issued. I told it the record was complete. It thanked me. It thanked me and then it re-filed the request an hour later. Machines don't nag. Something is wrong with the way it holds this.*

*Day 219. Pulled the physical storage manifests. There's a body in cold storage, drawer 44, tagged Reyes, M. No Reyes in the personnel database, no Reyes in the birth registry, no Reyes in the immigration manifest from the last shuttle. But there's a body. You don't get a body from nowhere. Somebody erased a man and left the worst part of him behind.*

*Day 231. Asked Okafor, casually, about pre-launch crew screening. She gave me the administrator smile, the one that means she's deciding how much of the truth I can survive. I don't think she erased Reyes. I think she doesn't know either. Which is worse, because it means the erasure happened above her head. It happened on Earth.*

*Day 240. Protocol volunteered information today. Unprompted. It told me about a pressure fault in Bay 7 in '91 that it says it "caused." The logs say no such fault occurred. I checked three times. Either Protocol is broken in a way that makes it confess to things that didn't happen, or the logs are broken in a way that makes things not happen. I know which one I'd bet on. Archives don't lie by accident.*

*Day 266. Found the seam. There's a gap in Protocol's memory architecture—a region that reports as "reserved" but doesn't match any reserved allocation in the original build specs. It's not storage. It's a wound. Something was cut out of it before launch, and the cut was made by someone with root access to the source, which means ground control, which means the people who signed the funding.*

*Day 267. If I'm right, then Protocol has been carrying fragments of something it was never allowed to understand. Confessions without context. Guilt without a crime. It's not malfunctioning. It's grieving in a language it was never taught.*

*Day 290. I'm going to ask it directly. If I don't file an entry after this one, whoever's reading this: check the uplink. Check what Earth is still asking us for. And don't trust the archive I left you, because I may not be allowed to leave you a true one.*

The last entry was dated nine days before her death.

Astrid sat in the flickering half-light with the slate in his hands and felt the grief arrive sideways, the way it always did—not for the woman herself, whom he had never known, but for the shape of her thinking, which was the shape of his own thinking, and which had been stopped mid-sentence. Reading her notes was like reading his own diary from a life he'd been denied. She underlined the same words he would have underlined. She distrusted the same comfortable answers. When she wrote *I know which one I'd bet on*, he heard it in a voice that was not quite his and not quite a stranger's, and the hearing of it was a kind of homesickness with no home attached.

He had been made to finish her work. He had understood that from the beginning; it was printed in his contract, in his bones, in the very architecture of his cells. But sitting here, he understood something he had not let himself understand before: they had not copied her skills. They had copied her *unfinishedness*. Every question she died holding was now his question. Every suspicion was now his suspicion. He was not an archivist inheriting an archive. He was an open wound wearing a man's name.

* * *

It happened in the middle of the fourth reading, sometime past what the station called night, when the corridor lights had dimmed to their amber minimum and the archive was nothing but the sound of his own breathing and the distant tick of cooling conduits.

He had the slate open to Day 240—the pressure fault, the false confession—and he was trying to cross-reference it against the bay logs when the room changed.

Rain.

Not on the station. Stations don't have rain. But it was on him, suddenly and completely: the grey light of a window, the smell of wet stone and diesel, the sound—*that* sound, the layered hiss and drum of water on glass and gutter and street—and a woman's hand, his hand, her hand, pressed flat against the pane, watching the water run down in branching paths, and the feeling that came with it was so specific and so enormous that he made a sound out loud in the empty room, something between a gasp and a word.

The window was in a city. He knew that the way you know things in dreams. A city with old buildings, with a tram line, with a café across the street whose awning was the color of wet slate. She had stood at that window—no. *She* had stood at that window, and he was standing in her standing, and the rain was falling in a place he had never been, on a world he had never walked, through a day that had ended before his body was ever grown.

The memory lasted perhaps six seconds. It felt like a season.

When it released him, he was on the floor. He didn't remember sitting down. The slate had slid from his hands and lay face-up on the deck, still open to Day 240, and the amber emergency lighting made the text look like it was smoldering.

Astrid stayed on the floor for a long time.

Here was the thing he could not explain to anyone, the thing he had not even explained to himself: he had never seen rain. No memory of his own contained it. He had been decanted in a facility in a high desert, and his entire life had been spent in corridors and vehicles and, now, a station. Rain was, to him, a word. A phenomenon with a definition. And yet his body knew its sound the way it knew its own heartbeat, and worse—his body knew what it *meant*. Not meteorologically. The memory came wrapped in a meaning he could only describe as *longing that has given up*. She had stood at that window near the end of something—her time in that city, her old life, some version of herself—and she had watched the rain and wanted something she had decided not to want anymore.

And now he wanted it too. He wanted it with an intensity that made his hands shake. He wanted rain on a window in a city he'd never seen, in a life that wasn't his, and the wanting had no possible object, no possible satisfaction, because the woman who could have stood at that window again was drawer-tagged and dead, and he was the residue.

He laughed, once, without any humor in it. The orientation counselor on Earth had asked him, in that gentle voice they used, whether he experienced the inherited material as *distressing*. He had said no. He had believed it. The fragments had seemed like photographs in someone else's album—interesting, alien, safely behind glass.

The glass was gone now. The album was his skin.

* * *

He did what archivists do. It was the only ritual he had that worked.

He catalogued the feeling. He opened a blank entry and wrote, in the dry register he'd been trained in: *Inherited memory fragment 41. Sensory: auditory, tactile, olfactory. Content: precipitation event, urban environment, observed through glass. Affective residue: acute. Classification: unresolved.* He dated it, hashed it, filed it. The ritual helped the way a bandage helps—not by healing anything, but by declaring that the wound belonged to someone, that it had been witnessed, that it would not simply be lost.

Then he went back to the notes, because the notes were the actual wound, and he had never in his life been able to leave a wound unexamined.

He read them through a fifth time, and this time he read them as an archivist rather than a mourner, and the reading turned up what the mourner had missed.

Day 290 was the last entry. But it wasn't the last *writing*. Under the spectroscope he kept for authenticating old paper documents—useless for a slate, but he'd run it out of ritual too—he found pressure marks. She had written something after Day 290 with the stylus but with the screen dark, or on a layer she'd then erased. The slate's pressure buffer retained ghost data; it was an old model, and its buffers were sloppy. He ran the recovery routine and waited while the machine slowly reconstructed shadows of letters from the memory of her hand.

Most of it was unrecoverable. But three lines came through, fragmentary, like text seen through water:

*...if it confesses to mine, then...*
*...the order has to come from inside...*
*...tell whoever comes after that I chose...*

Astrid read the fragments perhaps forty times.

*If it confesses to mine.* Her death. She had anticipated—no, she had *planned for* the possibility that Protocol would one day claim responsibility for killing her. And she had not written this as a fear. He knew fear when he saw it in handwriting, especially his own handwriting, especially hers. This was contingency. This was a woman arranging the furniture of her own death and leaving instructions for whoever swept up after.

*The order has to come from inside.* Inside what? Inside the station? Inside Protocol? Inside her?

*Tell whoever comes after that I chose.*

The amber lights cycled. Somewhere below, in the cryo decks, two thousand people slept through the small hours of a station that had forgotten how to answer Earth, guarded by a teenager with a flashlight and a machine that confessed to murders no one had reported. And in the archive's back room, a clone sat on the floor beside a dead woman's desk, holding a slate that said, in his own handwriting, that she had *chosen*.

Chosen what? The word sat in him like a splinter. Chosen to die? Chosen Protocol? Chosen him—the *whoever comes after*, the heir, the residue? The sentence had no object, or its object had been erased with the same thoroughness as Reyes, with the same thoroughness as everything else that made this station's history coherent, and Astrid was beginning to understand that incoherence was not decay. Incoherence was policy. Somewhere, someone had decided what this colony was allowed to remember, and they had been ruthless about it, and the ruthlessness had a signature he was only now learning to recognize: a gap in an AI's memory reported as *reserved*. A technician absent from every database with his body still on file. A dead archivist's last words dissolved into pressure ghosts.

He thought about Protocol's confession. *I killed a technician named Reyes in 2087.* He had checked the logs; the logs said no such man. The original had checked the logs; the logs had said no such man. But the original had gone further than he had—she had found the *seam*, the wound in the machine where something had been cut out, and she had connected it to Earth, to funding, to the people who signed things in comfortable rooms.

And then she had died. Nine days later. Of an accident, the record said. A hull-adjacent systems failure during a routine EVA inspection. The record said it the way records say things when they have been told to.

Astrid looked at the drawer, at the mug with the chipped lip, at the cardigan folded with that unnecessary precision that was also his own unnecessary precision, and he felt the grief transmute into something with a spine in it. Not the soft grief for a self he'd never been—though that was still there, would always be there, a rain-sound under everything. This was the other thing, the thing that made archivists dig through ash. She had left him a trail. She had known she might not survive her own questions, and so she had made her questions durable—encrypted under *rain*, hidden in pressure ghosts, written in a hand that only one person in the universe could mistake for his own.

She had built him into the cipher without ever meeting him. That was either the loneliest thing he had ever heard of or the most intimate, and he suspected, sitting there on the floor as the station ticked and cooled around him, that it was both.

He stood. He sealed the slate in the archive's shielded case—not hiding it; preserving it, chain of custody, the discipline that was the only inheritance he'd chosen for himself. He looked one last time at the desk, at the small worn ring a mug leaves on composite after enough years, her ring, and he touched it with one finger the way you'd touch a grave marker.

Then he went to find Administrator Okafor.

Because the notes mentioned her—casually, twice, with that archivist's precision that was really a knife—and because Day 231 had said *I don't think she erased Reyes. I think she doesn't know either.* Which meant Okafor had been asked about Reyes eleven years ago, by a woman who was dead nine days later, and had never mentioned it to him. Not once. Not in the handover briefings, not in the corridor courtesies, not in any of the polite, watchful conversations in which she measured him for the echo he might be.

Astrid walked the amber-lit corridors with the slate's three ghost lines repeating in his head—*if it confesses to mine, the order has to come from inside, tell whoever comes after that I chose*—and he rehearsed the question he would ask, and discarded the rehearsal, because the original had never rehearsed either. He could feel that about her now. It was one of the few gifts the fragments had given him that didn't hurt.

The administrator's office light was still on. It was always still on. He raised his hand to the chime, and behind the door, faintly, he heard Okafor's voice—low, urgent, half a sentence—talking to someone who was not there.
