## 14. Imani's Question

The bleed came at 0347 station time, which meant it came at no time at all, in the hourless dark of a colony that had forgotten what dawn was for.

Astrid had learned to read the signs the way sailors read weather. First the pressure behind his eyes, like something rising through deep water. Then the wrongness of his own hands—he would look at them and see them younger, smaller, a woman's hands with a scar on the left knuckle from a cryo-valve that had bitten her in 2084. Then the voice. That was the worst part, the voice, because he didn't hear it from outside. He heard it from inside his own throat, already forming words in a cadence that wasn't his: clipped at the ends of sentences, a slight lift on the second-to-last syllable, a habit of pausing before names as if each one cost something to say.

He had taken to keeping a recorder running through the nights, and a towel folded on the desk, and a glass of water, and the lamp turned low because bright light made the fragments shatter faster and the shattering hurt.

At 0347 he was standing at the archive window—there were no windows in the archive, but she had stood at one, so he stood at the wall where a window would be, on a station that had no view worth the glass—and he was speaking.

"—and if Okafor won't sign the requisition, I'll route it through the medical override, she can't refuse a quarantine flag twice, she *can't*—" A pause. A breath that belonged to a dead woman's lungs. "Reyes. Reyes, listen to me. If I don't file it by the sixth, you file it yourself. Promise me. Promise me you'll—"

The name landed in the empty archive like a stone dropped down a well.

*Reyes.*

The technician who wasn't. The body in cold storage with no birth record, no service history, no death certificate—only a stasis drawer and an AI's confession. And here he was, in her mouth, in her last weeks, a man she had trusted with something worth filing.

Astrid's knees went. He caught himself on the archive table, scattering a tray of data chips, and the sound of them skittering across the deck was the sound of his own skull coming apart, and then the memory took him fully and he was not standing in the archive at all.

*Rain on a window.*

It was the fragment that had haunted him since the desk, since the encrypted notes, since the first night he'd understood that the original had carried something inside her that she'd chosen to leave behind. Rain—actual rain, falling water, which did not exist aboard Meridian Station and had not existed in the atmospheric processors for thirty years. She was looking through glass at it. The glass was old, warped, the rain streaking down in ropes. Beyond it: a gray field, a fence, a line of poplars bending in wind. Earth. She was on Earth, in the years before launch, and she was crying, and the rain was doing the crying for her, and someone behind her was saying something about *the protocol, Astrid, you have to understand what the protocol is for*—

The archive came back in pieces: the hum of the environmental scrubbers, the cold of the deck through his socks, the taste of copper. He was on the floor. He didn't remember getting there. His hands were shaking and his jaw ached and his throat was raw, and when he tried to say *I'm fine* to the empty room, it came out in her cadence, clipped at the end, lifting on the second-to-last syllable.

That was when the light in the doorway changed, and he knew someone was there.

He knew before he looked. Some part of him had learned the station's footfalls—the heavy tread of Okafor, the quick shuffle of the maintenance twins, the particular soft, deliberate step of the bay-tenders, who spent their shifts among sleeping people and had learned to move like the sleepers might wake.

"Astrid?" Imani's voice. Sixteen years old, station-born, with the flat vowels of a child raised on recordings of Earth English she'd never heard spoken live. "I saw the light under the door. You're supposed to be on sleep shift."

He tried to answer. What came out was: "*Don't*—don't come in. Give it a minute. It passes."

Her cadence. Her words, maybe—some memory of her refusing help, waving someone off from a doorway thirty years dead. He pressed his fist against his mouth.

Imani came in anyway.

She was in her tender's wrap, the gray coverall of the cryo bay crews with its faded blue piping, and her hair was braided tight for shift work, and she looked at Astrid Thackeray—clone, archivist, inheritor of a dead woman's murder—crouched on the floor of the archive in his socks, and she did not look afraid, and she did not look away.

"Okay," she said. "Give it a minute. I'll give it a minute right here."

She sat down on the floor beside him, cross-legged, her back against the table leg, close enough that he could have touched her and far enough that she wasn't touching him, and she picked up one of the scattered data chips and turned it over in her fingers like she was reading it.

"It passes," she said. Not a question. She'd seen one before. Of course she had—the bay-tenders worked the night shifts, and Astrid had been bleeding memories for weeks now, and the station was small and its walls were thin and its people watched everything because there was nothing else to watch.

"How much," he managed, in his own voice finally, rough and strange, "have you—how much do they talk about it? The tenders."

Imani set the chip down with great care, aligning its edge with the table leg, the way her people aligned everything, the way you learned to align things when you lived among two thousand sleeping bodies and the smallest misalignment felt like disrespect.

"Deni says you talk in your sleep on the night recorder feed. She runs the archive diagnostics, so she hears everything, she's not supposed to but she does." A shrug. "Kwame says you're haunted. He means it as a compliment. On the bays we think haunted things are just things that remember too hard."

"And you?" Astrid asked. "What do you say?"

Imani looked at him then, fully, and her eyes in the low lamplight were the brown of the station-grown coffee Okafor hoarded, and she said, "I say you should drink some water. You get dry when it happens. I've watched."

She got the glass from the desk and put it in his hands and waited until he drank, and the ordinariness of it—the sheer, unremarkable domesticity of a sixteen-year-old handing a grown man water in the middle of the night—undid him more thoroughly than the memory bleed had. His eyes stung. He blamed the copper taste.

* * *

They sat on the floor of the archive for a long time. Imani did not fill the silence the way station adults did, with talk of power draws and rotation schedules and the low constant anxiety that had become the colony's weather. She just sat, and occasionally turned a data chip over, and once she hummed four notes of something—a lullaby, he realized, one of the bay-tenders' songs, the ones they sang to the sleepers on the night rounds, though the sleepers could not hear and the singing was not for them. It was for the singers. It was so their voices would not atrophy in the silence of the bays. It was so that the children of the station would grow up knowing how to make a sound in a quiet room and not be afraid of it.

"Can I ask you something," she said eventually. "And you can not answer. Deni says you're allowed to not answer. She says it's in the archivist's charter or something."

"The charter says I can't be compelled to testify against the archive," Astrid said. "It's a very old clause. It's mostly about smuggling."

"Okay. Well." Imani turned the chip over again. "When it happens—when you go somewhere else, when you talk in her voice—do you know it's happening? While it's happening? Or is it like sleep, and you only find out after?"

Astrid considered the question with more care than he had given anything in weeks. It was, he realized, the first time anyone had asked him what the bleed was *like* rather than what it *meant*. Okafor asked what it compromised. Protocol asked what it revealed. Imani asked what it was like, which was the question you asked a person.

"Both," he said. "It's like—imagine you're standing in a room, and the room is yours, every wall is yours, and then the walls go transparent and there's another room behind them, and someone else is living in it, and for a while you can't tell which life is the one you're standing in. And then the walls come back. But they're thinner every time. I can feel how thin they're getting."

"Do you want them to stay up?" she asked. "The walls."

"I don't know." He said it before he could make it a lie. "That's the worst part. Some of what comes through is—I need it. She hid things in herself. Messages. Evidence. If the walls come all the way down, I'll have all of it, and I'll also have—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a version of this where I finish the bleed and there's nothing left over that's me. Just her, wearing a younger body, finishing her own work. And everyone would say that was fine. Everyone would say that was the design working as intended."

Imani was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "That's the dumbest thing I've ever heard."

Astrid laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, painful and good.

"No, I mean it." She had the fierce, flat certainty of the young, the certainty of someone who had not yet learned to hedge. "The tenders talk about this all the time, actually. Not about you specifically. About the sleepers. About what a person is. Because we grow up walking between the bays, right, and the founders are in there, and everyone says they're *the colony*, they're the real Meridian, they're the ones who get to decide everything when they wake. But I've stood at the glass my whole life and I've never met a single one of them. And meanwhile there's two hundred of us born up here, and we do all the work, and we sing to people who can't hear us, and nobody put *us* in an archive. Nobody thought we were worth the glass."

She picked up the chip again, held it up between two fingers.

"Deni says a person is whatever keeps being itself when you stop looking. That's her definition. Kwame says a person is whatever can be loyal. I think they're both wrong. I think a person is whoever shows up in the room." She gestured at the archive, at the floor, at the two of them sitting in it. "You showed up in the room. She didn't. She's in your walls. But you're the one on the floor, drinking water I gave you, in the middle of the night, in *this* room. So."

"So I'm a person because I'm sitting badly on a floor at 0400," Astrid said.

"You're a person because you're *here*," Imani said, with the patience of someone explaining gravity to an adult who should know better. "The rest is paperwork."

He wanted to tell her about the fifth name. He wanted to tell her that Protocol's confession log held a fifth entry now, that he'd found it three nights ago, that the AI had claimed responsibility for the original Astrid's death with a timestamp that could not be true, that someone had edited the archive itself, that the floor under everything he'd inherited was cracked straight through. He wanted to tell her that in a week or a month the reactor diagnostics would force the station to choose which half of its sleeping children lived, and that his compromised memory was going to be entered into evidence, and that everything she had just said about showing up in the room would be weighed against the fact that he might not be a who at all but a what—a delivery mechanism, a letter that had learned to walk.

He told her none of it. She was sixteen, and it was 0400, and some inheritances should not be passed down the way his had been: all at once, in the dark, to someone who hadn't asked.

Instead he said, "Why did you really come down here? The light under the door. That's not the whole answer."

Imani's braids clicked softly as she tilted her head. For a moment he thought she'd deflect—station kids learned to deflect early; it was a survival trait in a place where every adult was a superior officer. But then she shrugged, and the shrug had the whole weight of the night shift in it.

"Kwame's mom is in Bay Four. Founder cohort. She went under when he was a baby. He does the night rounds on Four even though he's not assigned there, and he sings to her, and last week he asked me—" Her voice did something small and complicated. "He asked me if she'd know him. When she wakes. If she'd know him at all, or if she'd just be someone from Earth standing in her friend's body."

The archive hummed. Somewhere above them, in the dark of the bays, two thousand people slept through the end of the world they'd been promised.

"And I couldn't answer him," Imani said. "Because I don't know. But you—" She pointed at him, and her finger did not waver. "You're the only person on this station who actually knows what it's like to be someone's second chance. You're living it. So I came to ask you, and you were on the floor talking to a dead woman, and I guess the question can wait."

"Ask it anyway," Astrid said. "I might have a better answer after."

"No." She shook her head, and something in her face was older than sixteen, older than the station, some inheritance of her own passed down through bay-glass and lullabies. "You don't answer questions like that while you're bleeding. That's rule one of the bays. You don't take a reading off a failing cell. You wait until it stabilizes, and then you take the reading, and then—" she paused, and the pause was the pause of someone quoting, someone passing down a protocol older than she was, "—and then you believe the reading. Whatever it says."

She stood, brushing off her wrap, and offered him a hand up. He took it. Her grip was strong from valve work, calloused in the pattern the tenders all shared, and it occurred to him, with a jolt like the memory bleed but warmer, that he knew that grip. He had catalogued it. In the original's notes, in her early files, there were pages of observation on the bay-tender crews—she had liked them, had trusted them, had written *if the colony survives it will be because of the children who sing to the sleepers, not the sleepers themselves*—and so even his fondness for this girl came to him secondhand, pre-loaded, a feeling installed at the factory.

Except it wasn't. That was the thing. The memory was hers, but the sting in his eyes at 0400 on the archive floor was his.

"The tenders have a roster," Imani said at the door, not turning around. "Night shifts, bay rotations, who brings whom tea. Your name's been on it for two weeks."

Astrid blinked. "I've never been assigned to a bay."

"You don't get assigned." She glanced back, and she was smiling, and the smile was an offer and a dare at once. "You get put on the roster. By us. Deni put you on. Kwame seconded it. I thirded. That's how it works—you're on the roster because the people on the roster say you're on the roster. Nobody above us gets a vote."

"I'm not—" He heard the protest forming and hated it, heard how much it sounded like her, the original, always deflecting belonging with procedure. "I'm not station-born. I'm not one of—"

"You're on the roster," Imani said, "because you showed up in the room. Bay Six, next sleep shift. Deni will teach you the song. You'll sing it wrong for a month. That's fine. Everybody sings it wrong for a month."

She left. Her footsteps went soft down the corridor, deliberate, the walk of someone who had learned to move among sleepers.

Astrid stood alone in the archive with the scattered chips and the empty glass and the ghost of rain on a window, and he found that his hands were shaking again, but not from the bleed.

Because she had offered it so freely. That was what terrified him. Duty he understood—duty had a shape, a clause, a signature; duty was the original's face looking at him out of the mirror every morning, asking if he'd finished her work. Duty could be inherited. But belonging couldn't. Belonging had to be *given*, and it had been given, casually, by a sixteen-year-old with valve-calloused hands, and there was no clause in the charter that told him what to do with it, no protocol for being wanted as himself rather than as the estate's executor.

He looked down at the recorder, still running on the desk, its little light steady. It had caught all of it—the bleed, the voice, the girl on the floor. Evidence, some cold part of him noted. The bleed is progressing faster than projected. The fifth name is corroborated by the original's own final weeks: she knew Reyes, she trusted him, she was filing something by the sixth. The timeline of her death is narrowing.

And beneath that cold note, another thought, warmer and more frightening: *Bay Six. Next sleep shift. He had a shift.*

He was still standing there, holding the empty glass, when the archive's console chimed—an incoming priority flag, Okafor's seal, the one she used for reactor business and nothing else. The subject line read: **Emergency session, full crew, 1200. Agenda: power allocation. Attendance mandatory.**

Astrid set down the glass, and reached for the recorder, and began, in his own voice, to prepare his testimony.
