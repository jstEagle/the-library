## 2. The Children's Chorus

The school auditorium smelled of recycled air and floor polish, the way every official gathering in Meridian Colony did. Elodie had slipped into the back row with her coat still on—her coat, with its secrets stitched into the lining like a second heartbeat—and watched forty children file onto the stage.

She'd come for cover, honestly. A public event was a smuggler's best friend: everyone watching the stage meant no one watching the woman by the door. Governor Okafor was giving some speech about civic renewal, and Elodie had planned to count the exits, memorize which inspectors were present, and leave before the applause.

Then the children began to speak.

It started as a recitation exercise. "Community Memory Day," the banner read, though someone had hung it slightly crooked, so it said *Communi ty Memory Day*, which felt accidentally honest. The children stood in rows, hands at their sides, and a teacher with tired eyes nodded them forward one by one.

The first child was small, maybe seven, with a cowlick that refused all governance. He stepped up to the microphone and said:

"I dreamed about the sky burning."

A polite murmur from the parents. Children say strange things; that was understood. The teacher smiled her encouragement smile.

"It wasn't scary," the boy continued. "It was just true. The sky was orange all the way down to the ground, and there was a door."

He paused. The microphone caught him swallowing.

"The door was sealed. And a lady was counting."

"Counting what, dear?" the teacher prompted gently.

"Down," he said. "She was counting down. From three hundred. She never got scared. She just kept going. Like she knew something we didn't."

Elodie's hand went still on the armrest.

The next child came up. A girl with braids, maybe nine. "I dreamed about the sky burning too," she said. "And the sealed door. And the lady counting down." She frowned, as if the words were arriving slightly ahead of her. "In my dream I knew her voice. Isn't that funny? You can't know someone's voice in a dream."

Another child. Another version—the same burning sky, the same door, the same countdown, each dream wearing different clothes but walking with identical steps. A boy described the smell of ozone and hot metal. A girl said the numbers were "warm, like someone reading you a bedtime story while the house is on fire." A small girl at the end of the row said, "I don't think it's a dream. I think it's a memory. But it isn't mine, and I've never been to Earth."

The murmuring stopped being polite.

Elodie sat very still and let the professional part of her mind do its work while the rest of her went cold. Because here was the thing—the thing she would carry home through three checkpoints and one bribed corridor, turning it over like contraband in her pocket:

She recognized the cadence.

Counting down from three hundred. Steady intervals. Not panicked, not rushed—a transmission rhythm. She knew that rhythm the way you know your own pulse, because she'd spent eleven years sitting beside it in the dark, calibrating against it, syncing her maintenance windows to it.

It was the beacon's handshake pattern. The preamble it sang out every ninety seconds before settling into its long, patient broadcast. Someone—or something—had set it to a human voice counting down from three hundred, and the children of Meridian Colony were dreaming it note for note.

*That's impossible,* said the sensible part of her, the part that filed false manifests and kept her face pleasantly forgettable.

But she'd been an invisible woman long enough to know that impossible things happened constantly; they just happened to people nobody bothered to watch.

* * *

Governor Okafor held a press conference two days later, because of course he did. Elodie attended in her capacity as Nobody In Particular, standing near the water reclamation display where officials never looked unless something was leaking.

"The so-called 'shared dreams' are a classic childhood phenomenon," Okafor said, with the warm confidence of a man who had rehearsed this in a mirror. "Children imitate one another. It's a fad, like the jump-rope rhymes last season, or the brief and regrettable enthusiasm for collecting insect husks. There is no cause for concern."

A reporter asked whether the dreams' content—Earth's destruction, specifically—was troubling given the colony's historical sensitivities.

"Our curriculum teaches Earth history responsibly," Okafor said. "Burning skies appear in every disaster holodrama ever produced. Children absorb imagery. They remix it. Last month they were all dreaming about the mascot of Ceres Fun-Zone, if you'll recall, and I didn't see anyone suggesting the purple wombat was transmitting."

Gentle laughter. Elodie didn't laugh. The purple wombat hadn't counted down from three hundred in a voice that matched, beat for beat, a signal she personally maintained.

She left early, as always. Invisible people make good exits.

* * *

Her district was the old one, the one the maps labeled "Legacy Infrastructure" as if that made it less real. She climbed the maintenance stairs two at a time out of habit, though nothing up here could hurt her except knowledge, and she already suspected what kind was waiting.

The beacon room was cold and hummed the way it always did—that low, patient tone beneath hearing, more felt than heard, like a sleeping animal's breath. Eleven years she'd come here. Oil, calibration, replacement of corroded contacts, the small devotions of keeping something alive that everyone else had forgotten. Her sanctuary. Her secret. The one fixed point in a life built on being forgettable.

She keyed the diagnostic console—an antique, older than the colony charter—and pulled the transmission log.

There it was. The preamble. Ninety-second intervals, three hundred pulses, descending.

She played it through the audio converter, and the machine rendered it in the only way it knew how: as sound. A woman's voice, synthesized, calm as still water.

*"Two hundred ninety-eight. Two hundred ninety-seven. Two hundred ninety-six."*

Elodie's skin prickled. She had heard this ten thousand times as data. She had never once heard it as a *voice*. Whoever had built the beacon's preamble had encoded it to resolve, under the right conversion, into speech—and she had spent eleven years dusting the machinery of a countdown without ever asking what it was counting toward.

Or who was listening.

Because beacons broadcast outward, yes. That was their purpose. But the cryovaults—the half-empty vaults beneath the settlement, holding the sleepers the founders had promised to wake "when the time was right"—were also receivers. Everyone knew that. It was in the charter. The vaults listened for the founders' authorized wake order.

Everyone knew the vaults only listened.

Elodie stared at the waveform, at the calm voice counting down toward a number she couldn't guess, and thought about forty children on a stage describing a sealed door none of them had ever seen.

The beacon wasn't talking to the stars.

It was talking *down*.

She pressed her palm flat against the console, feeling the vibration of the next preamble begin, and for the first time in eleven years, Elodie Hartwell was afraid of the one place in the universe that had never lied to her.

*"Two hundred eighty-one,"* said the voice, gentle as a lullaby. *"Two hundred eighty."*

The children weren't having a fad.

They were receiving.

And somewhere below her feet, behind sealed doors, the sleepers were beginning to stir.
