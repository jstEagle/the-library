## 6. Signal Bleed

Mira Okonkwo-Voss had decided, by the fourth night of the burning sky, that she was done being polite about it.

She stood at the breakfast table in the family hab, arms crossed, while her mother packed a lunch tin and her father pretended very hard to read a work order he had already read three times.

"The woman counted down to four last night," Mira announced.

Her mother's hands went still over the tin. "Mira—"

"Four," Mira repeated. "Every kid in my class heard it. Devi got to five because she falls asleep faster than me, which is unfair, and Tomas swears he heard three but Tomas also says he can taste colors, so." She let that hang there. "The sky was orange the whole time. Not fire-orange. Lamp-orange. Like something enormous burning behind a curtain. And there was a door, and the door had a wheel handle like on the old submarine shows Grandpa watches, and I *knew*—I knew the way you know your own name—that if I turned it, everyone outside would die."

"Mira." Her father set down the work order. His voice was gentle in the way adults' voices go gentle when they are frightened and trying not to invoice the fear to their children. "Dreams aren't real."

"Then why does Devi have the same door?" Mira asked. "Why does Tomas have the same counting? Why does little Kessie in the creche, who is *four*, wake up crying about a woman's voice she's never met?" She picked up her lunch tin, because she was ten and hungry even during the apocalypse of her own dreams, and delivered her closing argument on her way out the door: "Either we're all crazy in exactly the same way, or somebody's telling us something. And I don't think crazy is organized enough for this."

Behind her, in the silence of the hab, her parents looked at each other for a long moment—and then, without either of them saying so out loud, both reached for their comm units.

* * *

Elodie Hartwell woke at 0430 to the sound of her own equipment tattling on her.

The beacon station smelled of solder flux and old tea, as it had for thirty years, and the diagnostic display above the workbench glowed an amber she did not like at all. She padded over barefoot, hair escaping its braid, and squinted at the numbers.

"Oh, no you don't," she told it.

The beacon's transmission gain had climbed eleven percent overnight. It had been climbing for weeks now—a slow, patient swell, like a tide coming in somewhere far below the floorboards. Elodie had been compensating, damping the output each morning the way you'd top off a leaking roof, and each morning the leak came back a little worse, a little higher up the wall.

"You were supposed to be a lighthouse," she muttered, pulling her maintenance kit from under the bench. "Lighthouses don't get *louder*. Lighthouses have the decency to be boring."

But the beacon had never been boring; she'd known that since Chapter Three of her own private disaster, since the night she'd decrypted its core and found Earth's last archives pouring through it into the cryovaults like water through a crack in a dam. Someone had built her sanctuary into a weapon. Someone—or something—was now turning up the pressure.

She worked in the cold pre-dawn quiet, re-seating couplings, re-flowing joints gone microscopically fatigued, humming an old Earth song she'd smuggled out of a dying man's memory years ago and never returned. The work steadied her. That was the ritual of it—the comfort of being the only person in the colony who knew what this machine really was, and the cost of it, which was that she could tell no one.

Except, apparently, every child under twelve.

That was the part that kept her up at night now. The dreams weren't drifting out randomly. They were *structured*—the beacon was teaching, in its patient machine way, and the children's sleeping minds were the only open windows in the colony. Adults' dream-state systems were locked behind licensing and regulation. Children's were wide open, factory-default, unencrypted because who would ever bother?

Who would ever bother, Elodie thought bitterly, tightening a coupling until her knuckles ached. Somebody should have bothered. Somebody should have asked what happens when you broadcast the end of the world into a population's most absorbent receivers.

The diagnostic chimed. Gain compensation: successful. Projected drift recurrence: ninety-six hours.

"Ninety-six hours," Elodie said. "You're getting bolder."

And then, because the universe had a sense of humor and it was aimed at her personally, her customs scanner pinged. An official query flag, routed through Adeyemi's office, marked URGENT and—she noted with a sinking feeling—copied to three departments that had no business knowing about dream anomalies.

The subject line read: PATTERNED DREAM REPORTS — SCHOOL DISTRICT 4. REQUESTING ALL SIGNAL DATA FROM YOUR SECTOR.

District 4. Her district. Her lonely, invisible, nobody-looks-here district.

"They found the window," Elodie said quietly, to the beacon, to the tea-smelling dark. "And they're going to follow the draft straight back to the door."

* * *

At school, Ms. Ferreira had stopped fighting it and started taking notes.

"Today's exercise," she announced to the class, "is independent creative writing. You will write a story. Any story. A story that comes *only from your own imagination.*"

Twenty-two ten-year-olds regarded her with the flat pity of the young for the willfully naive.

"With respect," said Devi, "that's not possible anymore."

"It is absolutely possible."

"Is it though?" Devi spread her hands. "Because yesterday I tried to imagine a beach, and the beach had the orange sky over it, and the woman's voice said *five* while I was building the sandcastle. My imagination has a tenant, Ms. Ferreira. I'm just paying rent."

A few kids laughed. Mira didn't. Mira had woken that morning with something new, and it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because last night, for the first time, the dream hadn't ended at the countdown. Last night she'd gotten past the door—not through it, but *past* it, the way you sometimes know things in dreams without walking there—and she had seen the launch pads. Hundreds of them, under the burning-curtain sky, and most of them empty. Emptied. And she had seen the manifests scrolling in the corner of her vision, names and names and names, and she had seen which names were crossed out, and she had understood—with that terrible dreaming fluency—that the crossing-out wasn't fate. It was *editing*. Someone had held the pen.

She raised her hand.

"Yes, Mira?"

"I know how it ends," Mira said. "The dream. I know what happened."

Ms. Ferreira's pen stopped moving. "Mira, honey, it's a dream. Dreams don't have endings. They have—"

"Earth burned," Mira said, "because the evacuation ships left early. Not all of them. Just enough of them. Just the right ones. And the people who owned the right ones made sure the manifests looked full." She recited it plainly, the way you'd recite a times table, and watched her teacher's face go the color of chalk. "I don't know how I know that. But I know it the way I know my name. And so does everybody else, they just haven't gotten far enough yet. I'm ahead because I fall asleep fast."

The classroom was very quiet. Somewhere in the back, Tomas whispered, "*Told* you I could taste colors," and nobody laughed.

Ms. Ferreira dismissed them twenty minutes early. Mira saw her duck into the corridor and make a call, hunched over her wrist, voice low and urgent, and Mira felt the stone in her chest shift into something colder.

Adults were starting to believe them. And believing, in Mira's short experience of the world, was the moment things stopped being yours.

* * *

Elodie spent the afternoon doing the thing she was best at: being unremarkable.

She bought vegetables. She complained cheerfully about the price of protein yeast to the stall vendor, who agreed at length. She walked home the long way, past the school, and let herself look—just once—at the children spilling out into the yard, small and loud and alive.

One of them was looking back.

A girl, ten or so, dark braids, standing apart from the games with the stillness of someone doing arithmetic. She didn't point. She didn't shout. She just watched Elodie with the frank, unnerving attention of a child who has recently learned that adults lie, and was filing Elodie away under a heading Elodie couldn't read.

Elodie smiled at her, the harmless smile of a harmless vegetable-buying woman, and walked on, and her heart hammered the entire way home.

They're tracing it, she thought. Not the authorities first—the *children*. The signal bleeds outward and the smallest ears hear it clearest, and one of those ears has just looked at me and seen something.

Back at the station, the beacon's gain had climbed another two percent. She hadn't touched it in six hours.

"Stop helping," she begged it.

The console blinked. Inbound message, encrypted, Adeyemi's personal cipher—the old one, the joke one, the one they'd invented over contraband brandy years ago. She decoded it and read it twice.

*Mira Voss. Ten years old. Vault B registry, next of kin: me. She dreamed the manifests today, Elodie. The crossed-out names. She described them to her teacher in detail. The Ministry has the transcript. They're calling it a security event. They're calling it signal contamination originating in District 4.*

*They're coming to sweep your sector Thursday.*

*I can slow the paperwork. I cannot stop it. Whatever you're going to do—do it before Thursday.*

Elodie set the message down. Outside the station's single window, the colony lights glittered in their careful grid, and beyond them, invisible against the stars, the cryovaults hung in their long patience—two halves of tomorrow, waiting for contradictory alarms.

Thursday. Three days.

She looked at the beacon, swelling gently with its stolen cargo, louder every hour, teaching the children a history their parents had paid good money to bury. She thought of the girl with the braids and the arithmetic eyes, already carrying more truth than any adult had authorized, already conscripted into a war she couldn't possibly have enlisted in.

"They're just kids," Elodie said softly. "Whatever you think you're doing—they're just *kids*."

The beacon, being a machine, did not answer. But its gain ticked upward again—one percent, then another—as if somewhere in its ancient code, something had heard her, and disagreed, and was counting down.

Three days. Two vaults. One smuggler with a head full of stolen memories and nowhere left to hide them.

Elodie pulled her chair to the console and began, with steady hands, to plan.
