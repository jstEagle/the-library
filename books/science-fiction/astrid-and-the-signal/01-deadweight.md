## 1. Deadweight

There are three kinds of quiet on Ceres Station, and I've learned to tell them apart the way sailors used to read weather.

There's the quiet of a station running well, which hums. There's the quiet of a station running scared, which doesn't hum at all—you can hear the absence of the hum, like a tooth you keep tonguing. And there's the quiet of a station where something has already gone wrong and nobody's said it out loud yet, which is the worst one, because it sounds exactly like the first kind until you look closely.

Docking Bay Fourteen was humming when I came aboard the *Mule's Patience* at 0600 station time with my scanner case and my coffee that tasted like it had been filtered through a boot. Routine inspection. Manifest said agricultural seed stock, forty crates, inbound from Vesta, outbound to the Belt relay at Hygeia. Seed stock is the most boring cargo in the solar system, which is why I liked it. Boring cargo means boring paperwork means nobody looks too hard at the hauler hauling it.

I've been hauling cargo through contested orbits for seven years now. Before that, I did other things under another name. The name is in a file somewhere marked CLOSED, next to a death certificate I forged myself, which I'll admit was competent work. I checked it recently. It still holds up.

"Crane," said the bay master, a woman named Okafor with forearms like dock cable and the permanent expression of someone who has heard every excuse ever invented. "You're early."

"I'm always early. Early is how you find things before they've been tidied."

"That a professional philosophy or a personality disorder?"

"Yes," I said, and she almost smiled, which for Okafor was practically a parade.

The *Patience* sat in her cradle like something tired. Forty-year-old hauler, patched more times than a fisherman's coat, but her owner kept her honest—mostly. My job, as far as anyone on Ceres knew, was to inspect outbound cargo for the Meridian Combine's transit authority: weights, seals, manifests, radiation signatures, the whole liturgy. What the Combine thought they were buying was a plodding functionary. What they were actually getting was a plodding functionary who used to be very good at noticing when numbers lied.

I started with the manifest because everything starts with paper. Forty crates, seed stock, mass logged at 2,140 kilograms total. I walked the rows with the scanner, letting it chirp its little chirps, and the first thirty-eight crates were exactly what they claimed to be. Grain futures in biological form. You could set a metronome by them.

Crate thirty-nine was 1.3 kilograms heavy.

Now, 1.3 kilograms on a 2,140-kilogram manifest is nothing. It's rounding error. It's the kind of discrepancy that gets flagged by no system anywhere, because the systems are built to catch people stealing pallets, not people stealing a bag of flour's worth of mass. But here's the thing about 1.3 kilograms: it's deliberate. Accidents are sloppy. Accidents overshoot by eleven kilos or undershoot by forty because somebody miscounted a shipment in a hurry. One-point-three is a number chosen by someone who wanted the discrepancy to exist but never to be found—a signature hidden inside the noise floor, visible only if you went looking for it specifically, with instruments calibrated past spec, which I do, because paranoia is just attention span applied to survival.

I put my palm flat on the crate and stood there a moment, listening to the bay hum.

"Well," I said quietly, to nobody. "Hello again."

Because that was the second thing. The first thing was the weight. The second thing was that the crate's seal was perfect. Not intact—perfect. Seals get scuffed; seals have histories. This one had been broken and resealed with a bonding agent matched to the original manufacturer's lot, which is not something you buy at a station chandlery. That's something you order from a supplier who asks no questions and charges accordingly. I'd seen this exact technique once before, seven years ago, on a claims investigation into a hull breach on a Combine ore tender that everyone called an accident and I called, in a report that was later expunged along with the rest of my life, murder.

Someone out there still knew how Cyrus Crane thought. Or worse—someone out there had learned from what Cyrus Crane left behind.

* * *

I did what any sensible ghost would do. I finished the inspection.

That's the discipline. The moment you deviate from routine, you become interesting, and interesting is a word that gets people killed in contested orbits. So I scanned crate forty—it was clean—and I signed the manifest with my thumbprint and my little electronic signature, and I told Okafor the load looked fine, and I drank the rest of my terrible coffee, and I walked off the bay floor at a normal pace while every nerve in my body screamed at me to run.

Then I went back to my berth, locked the door, and pulled up the transit records.

Here's what I found over the next six hours, cross-referencing shipping lanes the way I used to cross-reference claims files in another life:

The *Mule's Patience* had made this run four times before, same route, same cargo class. Crates thirty-nine and forty hadn't existed on any previous run—the manifest had always been thirty-eight units. Two new crates appeared this cycle. Crate forty was clean. Crate thirty-nine was a message.

And the routing history on those two crates traced back through Vesta to a freight consolidator called Latch & Sons, which had been registered fourteen months ago, which had filed its incorporation papers with a template so generic it might as well have been signed "Anonymous," and which had one prior transaction on record: a single shipment of atmospheric scrubber filters to a research station that, when I checked, had been decommissioned nine years ago.

Somebody had built a company whose entire purpose was to move one heavy crate onto one hauler, through one inspector, at one station.

Me. I was the one inspector. This wasn't cargo fraud. Cargo fraud wants to avoid detection. This wanted to be detected—by me, specifically, by the only person on Ceres calibrated finely enough to catch 1.3 kilograms hiding in the noise floor. It was a knock on the door. A very expensive, very patient knock.

I sat in my berth with the lights down and let the fear come, because fighting it early just makes it arrive late and larger. Seven years ago I'd watched people die because someone powerful decided a problem needed solving, and I'd survived by becoming no one: no name, no history, no pattern. I'd been so careful. Different routes on different cycles. No debts. No lovers. No habits worth mapping. And now someone had spent fourteen months and a shell corporation to say *I see you* without saying anything at all.

Unless.

I got up and made tea I didn't drink and made myself consider the alternative, because the alternative was worse and I needed to look at it straight on. If someone wanted me dead, there were cheaper ways. Ghosts are easy to kill; nobody investigates. If someone wanted me arrested, they'd have sent the Combine's actual investigators, not a riddle in a seed crate. But if someone wanted to talk to me—if someone knew who I used to be, and knew I'd know what 1.3 kilograms meant, and knew I'd pull the thread—

Then it wasn't a threat. It was an invitation.

And the only people who knew enough about my old life to build an invitation like this were people who'd been there. People from Halcyon-9. People who were supposed to be dead.

I opened the old claims archives—the deep ones, the ones you need a friend in records to reach, and I am owed favors by precisely one friend in records, a woman on Pallas who thinks I'm a moderately corrupt insurance man named Ferro and charges me accordingly. I asked her for everything touching the Halcyon-9 incident. She came back in twenty minutes with a file so thin it insulted the dead: official cause, catastrophic reactor failure; official casualties, eleven; official survivors, zero. Closed, settled, paid. The Combine had cut checks to next of kin within ninety days, which in insurance terms is suspiciously fast. Grief takes longer than ninety days. Cover stories don't.

Eleven dead. Except I was one of the eleven, breathing and drinking bad tea in a berth on Ceres, which meant the count was wrong, which meant whoever compiled it either didn't check or didn't care, which meant—

Which meant there could be others. A twelfth name. Someone else who'd walked away from the fire and buried themselves the way I had, and who had now, after seven years, decided to stop burying and start digging.

I closed the archive. I sat with my cold tea. Outside the berth's little window, Ceres turned, and the traffic lights of the Belt crawled across the dark like slow constellations, and somewhere out there was a person who knew my real name and had just spent a small fortune to whisper it through a crate of seed stock.

I should have run. Every instinct I'd sharpened over seven years said run: new identity, new orbit, burn the berth, delete Ferro, start over. Running had kept me alive. Running was the only skill I trusted completely.

But I kept looking at that number. 1.3 kilograms. Chosen, placed, patient. Whoever built that crate hadn't wanted to hurt me. They'd wanted to be *found*, and they'd trusted me—the specific mechanics of my mind—to find them. After seven years of being no one, there was a person out there who remembered exactly who I was, down to how I read a seal.

You can hate someone for that. You can also, if you're honest, miss them terribly.

I pulled up the manifest one more time and looked at the consolidator's registration date. Fourteen months ago. I started doing arithmetic on what else had happened fourteen months ago—shipments, news, anything—and then I stopped, because my comm unit chimed with a work priority flag from the transit authority, and work flags at 1900 hours are never good news.

The message was short. A request, phrased with the particular courtesy that Combine bureaucracy uses when it isn't actually requesting anything: a senior investigator from Combine liaison would be arriving at Ceres within the week to conduct a review of inspection protocols, and Inspector Crane's recent certifications had been selected for audit. Please ensure availability. Please continue all duties as normal.

Please continue all duties as normal. Which meant: we may already be watching, and we want to see what you do next.

They'd flagged the same crate. Or they'd flagged me. With the Combine those were often the same thing. Somewhere between the seed crate and my cold tea, the game had acquired a second player, and this one wore an official badge and flew an official ship and had the power to peel my life open layer by layer until it found the corpse that wasn't there.

I looked at the message for a long time. Then I did the thing I'd sworn I'd never do again: I opened a channel to my friend in records on Pallas and typed a query I'd promised myself I'd never type.

*Pull the death certificate. Cyrus Crane. Tell me if anyone's touched the file.*

Her reply came back faster than I expected, which frightened me more than a slow reply would have.

*Touched? Honey, somebody filed against it last month. New claim. Active status.*

Someone had filed an insurance claim against my death. Using my birth name. Last month.

I set the comm down very carefully, the way you set down a thing that might be armed, and I looked out the window at the turning dark, and I laughed—one short, ugly syllable with no humor in it, the laugh of a man reading his own obituary and finding errors in the accounting.

Seven years of careful, and the past hadn't knocked after all.

It had filed paperwork.
