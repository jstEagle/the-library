## 2. A Name on the Manifest

The docking ledger updated itself at 0400 station time, the way it always did, a soft chime that Felix had long ago learned to sleep through. It was the Verdance's own small bureaucracy: cargo manifests, fuel requisitions, the quarterly atmospheric yield reports she filed to two fleets who never answered them but always, somehow, received them. She kept the records because records were what remained when everything else was taken away. Records were how you proved a thing had lived.

She was elbow-deep in the nutrient sump of Bay Three, coaxing a stubborn stand of rice-grass out of its sulking, when the chime sounded and did not stop.

Three tones, then silence, then three again. That was not the routine pulse. That was an anomaly flag — something the system almost never raised, because almost nothing happened here worth flagging. Felix pulled her arms from the green-slicked water, wiped them on her thighs, and crossed the catwalk with her heart already doing something foolish.

The terminal glowed amber in the dim of the control room. She keyed it awake and read the line twice, then a third time, because the first two readings could not be trusted to have happened.

**BERTH 9 — DEPARTURE LOGGED — VESSEL: MERIDIAN (REG. CANCELLED) — CREW MANIFEST: CRANE, T. / VEY, I.**

The rice-grass water dripped off her elbows onto the deck plates, one drop, then another, counting out seconds she wasn't using.

The Meridian had been lost eleven years ago. Lost was the word everyone used, the soft administrative word, as though the ship had wandered off like a dog and simply failed to come home. Felix knew the harder word. The Meridian had been on the relay run between the two fleet stations — her run, the route she flew now — when the war's first shots tore the system apart, and it had gone dark somewhere past the shoal fields with thirty-one souls aboard, her brother Tomas among them. There had been no wreckage recovered. No distress signal. Just an absence shaped like a ship, and a name on a memorial wall at each fleet station, spelled identically in both scripts, which was the only sentence the two fleets had ever agreed upon.

And now the ledger said the Meridian had departed Berth 9 last week.

Berth 9 was a derelict. It had been derelict since before Felix took the contract — a collapsed maintenance cradle at the far end of the ring, sealed behind blast doors, its atmosphere scrubbers dead for decades. Nothing departed from Berth 9. Nothing even arrived there. She had walked past its scarred doors four hundred times and never once heard anything on the other side but the wind of the moon's thin exhaled air moving through broken ductwork.

Her thumb hovered over the entry. The timestamp was precise: 0317, six days ago. Crew manifest: Crane, T. Vey, I.

Tomas. Her brother's name, typed into a departure log by some system, somewhere, that believed he existed.

Felix sat down slowly on the operator's stool, because her legs had informed her they were no longer reliable infrastructure. She thought, distantly, of the rice-grass waiting in its sump, and found that she could not care about it, which frightened her more than the ledger did. For eleven years the garden had been the only thing that could hold her whole attention — roots and yields and the slow patient chemistry of making air where there had been none. It was her devotion, her discipline, her answer to every night that asked *why are you still here*. And now a single line of text had reached into her chest and rearranged the furniture.

*Crane, T.*

It could be a clerical ghost. Old registries bled into new systems all the time; half the Verdance's own archive was corrupted entries and phantom ships. A cancelled registration resurrecting itself in a departure log was exactly the kind of nonsense software produced when nobody loved it anymore. She told herself this. She said it aloud, actually, to the empty control room, in her level gardener's voice: "It's a glitch."

The room did not agree with her. The room just hummed, and the amber light stayed lit, and the name stayed on the screen.

*Vey, I.* She didn't know the name. She turned it over like an unfamiliar seed — hard coat, unknown interior. Someone had boarded a dead ship with her dead brother and flown it out of a berth that couldn't hold air.

Felix looked at her hands. They were steady. That surprised her; she'd expected them to shake. But hands that had spent eleven years doing delicate work alone — grafting, calibrating, threading seedlings into hydroponic collars at the edge of a vacuum — knew how to be steady when the important thing arrived. And this was the important thing. This was the only important thing there had ever been, wearing the disguise of a database error.

* * *

Protocol said she filed an anomaly report. Protocol said she flagged the entry to both fleet stations, attached the raw log, and waited six weeks for a response that would either never come or arrive as a form letter thanking her for her vigilance. Protocol said the fleets handled their own ghosts.

But protocol also said the truce held because both sides respected the neutral channel, and Felix had spent enough years running supplies between two silent fortresses to know that respect was just fear wearing a clean uniform. Nobody would come. Nobody would look. If she reported it, the entry would vanish into someone's queue and Tomas's name would go back to being a wall engraving, and she would go back to the sump, and the rice-grass would grow, and nothing would ever crack open again.

She stood in front of the blast doors of Berth 9 at 0550, carrying a hand lamp, a pry bar, and a canister of breathing mix she'd told herself was for inspection purposes.

The doors were not locked. That was the first wrong thing. They were sealed — fused, in her memory, from some old decommission — but when she set her shoulder against the seam and pushed, they parted with a groan of reluctant hydraulics, smooth as if serviced yesterday. Warm air breathed out past her, carrying a smell that stopped her on the threshold.

Ozone. Machine oil. And underneath it, faint and impossible, the green smell of growing things.

Felix raised the lamp. The berth stretched away into shadow, and it was not the ruin she remembered. The cradle had been rebuilt — jury-rigged, scavenged, brilliant — scaffolding of salvaged strut-work climbing toward a patched hull. Power conduits ran along the floor in neat looms, tapped from somewhere, humming with stolen current. Tools hung on a pegboard in careful order. A cot sat against the far wall, blankets folded with a precision that made her chest ache, because Tomas folded his blankets exactly that way, corners squared, military-tight, a habit their mother had beaten into both of them and only one of them had kept.

Someone had been living here. Someone had spent months, maybe years, bringing a dead ship back to life in secret, inside the one place in the system nobody ever looked.

And the ship was still there. Half of it. The Meridian's hull filled the far end of the berth, scarred, patched, real — she knew those scars, she'd memorized the damage reports as a girl, read them so many times she could draw the breach patterns from memory. There was the micrometeorite pocking along the dorsal line. There was the scorch aft of the airlock that the official report attributed to debris impact and that had never quite matched any debris anyone described.

The airlock stood open. Beyond it, warm light.

Felix should have announced herself. Some part of her, the part that had survived eleven years of neutrality by being transparent as glass, knew that walking uninvited into a stranger's hideout aboard a ghost ship was the kind of decision that got people killed. She catalogued the risks the way she catalogued blight: methodically, calmly, and without any intention of stopping.

She stepped through the airlock.

Inside, the Meridian's corridor had been restored to something like life — lamps strung along the bulkheads, a garden rack bolted beside the galley holding seedlings in repurposed ration tins, and oh, that detail undid her, that someone out here grew things the way she did, that the dead ship had plants in it. Charts covered one wall: traffic patterns, fleet patrol windows, the supply route Felix herself flew, marked in two colors of ink with dates going back years.

Her route. Her schedule. Studied.

The lamp trembled now. She let it.

At the end of the corridor, a door stood half open on a cabin glowing with screen-light, and from inside came the sound she would remember for the rest of her life, whatever the rest of her life turned out to be: a voice, low and focused, reading aloud in the cadence of someone cross-referencing — numbers, coordinates, timestamps — and beneath the voice, faintly, a second sound. A man's voice, older recording, tinny with distance and damage, saying something she couldn't make out.

Saying something in a voice she knew.

Felix put her hand on the door. The woman inside hadn't heard her yet — headphones, maybe, or just the absorption of deep work — and Felix stood there in the doorway light with her lamp shaking and her grief cracked open like a geode, all these years of polished stone split down the middle to show the glittering impossible center.

Because the voice on the recording was Tomas's. Older than she remembered him, roughened, tired — but his. Her brother's voice, coming out of a speaker, aboard his lost ship, in a berth that shouldn't hold air, six days after the ledger said he'd sailed it away.

The woman at the desk turned, saw her, and went very still — hand sliding, unhurried and deliberate, toward something beneath the console.

Neither of them moved. The recording played on, filling the little cabin with the voice of a dead man, and Felix heard her own voice come out of her, quiet and cracked and absolutely certain:

"That's my brother. Where did you get my brother?"

The stranger's eyes — sharp, exhausted, and not entirely surprised — flicked from Felix's face to the lamp in her hand, and something in them shifted, the way weather shifts before a front comes through.

"You're Crane," the woman said. Not a question. "Felix Crane." She rose slowly, hands visible, deliberate. "I've been hoping you'd find me. I only wish you'd come a day later — or a year sooner."

Behind her, on the screen, the waveform of Tomas's voice scrolled past like a seismograph of everything Felix had buried. And below it, tagged and highlighted in the stranger's archival notation, was a string of data Felix didn't recognize — a signal signature, timestamped to the hour the war began.

"Ilsa Vey," the woman said. "We need to talk about what your brother heard."
