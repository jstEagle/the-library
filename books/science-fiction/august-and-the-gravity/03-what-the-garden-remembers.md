## 3. What the Garden Remembers

Ilsa Vey had spent eleven years learning to read silence, and the Verdance was the loudest silent place she had ever entered.

The dome breathed. That was the first thing—no archive had prepared her for it. The climate processors cycled with a slow tidal rhythm, and beneath that ran a hundred smaller sounds: water moving through nutrient lines, leaves shifting against one another in the ventilation current, the faint tick of expanding glass as the moon's long afternoon gave way to its longer evening. In the fleet archives everything was sealed, temperature-locked, dead on purpose. Here, everything was alive on purpose. It made her feel like an intruder at a vigil.

She had been hiding in the Verdance for two days.

Not hiding, exactly. Waiting was a kinder word, and she had learned to be precise with words; precision was the only discipline they hadn't been able to strip out of her when she walked away from the Meridian's records and the uniform that went with them. She waited among the third-tier planters, in the aisle of young birch hybrids where the humidity sensors were oldest and least reliable, curled into the gap between a water reclamation unit and the root mass of something Felix Crane's logs called *Betula cranesii*—named, Ilsa suspected, by the woman herself, because no committee would have permitted so small a vanity.

Felix Crane came to the garden every thirty-one hours. Ilsa knew this the way she knew everything now: from manifests, maintenance schedules, supply requisitions, the patient sediment of paperwork that settled over any life long enough. Thirty-one hours of running supplies between two fleets that did not speak, then back to the garden, where she would walk every row, touch certain leaves as if taking attendance, and stand for exactly four minutes at the eastern viewport looking at nothing—or at what Ilsa had come to believe was not nothing at all: the coordinates of empty space where a ship called the *Meridian* had died nine years ago.

Ilsa had watched her do it twice. On the second night, Felix had spoken aloud to the plants—a habit, apparently—and said, "Another week and the tomatoes will need staking," in a voice that made it sound like a prayer for someone specific.

That was when Ilsa understood she could not simply leave a message. She would have to be found, or find herself, and either way she would have to watch Felix Crane's face do whatever it did when the past stood up out of the soil.

* * *

It happened on the third evening, and it happened because of the birches.

Ilsa had miscalculated. The humidity sensors were old, yes, but Felix Crane was not a woman who trusted old things blindly; she calibrated them by hand, walking the aisles with a battered probe, and on this evening her route brought her down the third-tier aisle ten minutes early. Ilsa heard the footsteps stop. Heard the probe click off. Heard, in the sudden hush, the sound of a person deciding very carefully whether to be afraid.

"I know you're there," Felix said. Her voice was steady, which impressed Ilsa more than shouting would have. "The birch roots shifted two days ago. Something's been leaning against them. And my emergency blanket is missing from the locker, folded wrong."

Ilsa rose slowly from behind the planter, hands open and visible, the way you emerged from cover in a demilitarized zone. Up close, Felix Crane was younger than her file photographs and older than her voice, with soil worked permanently into the creases of her knuckles and eyes the flat gray-green of leaves grown under full-spectrum lamps. She held the probe like she'd forgotten she was holding it.

"You're Ilsa Vey," Felix said.

"Yes."

"The name on the manifest. With—" Her jaw tightened around the word. "With his name."

"With Elias Crane's name," Ilsa said. "Yes."

For a moment neither of them moved. The garden breathed around them, indifferent and enormous. Then Felix set the probe down on the planter's edge with exaggerated care, the gesture of someone putting down something heavier than a tool.

"Two days," Felix said. "You've been in my garden for two days."

"I needed to see how you tended it before I decided how much truth you could carry." It sounded cruel said aloud, and Ilsa flinched at her own sentence. "I'm sorry. That's archivist thinking. We weigh people like documents. It's a bad habit and I haven't unlearned it yet."

"And what did your weighing conclude?"

"That you'd already carried more than anyone should," Ilsa said, "and kept standing anyway."

Something moved behind Felix's eyes—not softening, not yet, but recognition, the look of one exhausted animal meeting another across a clearing. "Sit down," Felix said. "If we're going to do this, you'll do it while eating. There's soup. It's mostly chard. Don't make a face about the chard."

* * *

They sat in the potting shed, which was really half the maintenance bay, among seed trays and coiled hose and the sweet green smell of cut stems. Felix ladled soup without ceremony and watched Ilsa over the steam with the fixed attention of someone who had spent years alone and had not let the habit of observation rot.

"Start with the manifest," Felix said. "The *Meridian* has been listed as lost with all hands for nine years. Last week it logged a departure from Berth Fourteen. My brother's name was on that log. So was yours. Explain it to me slowly, because I've read that record forty times and it reads like a ghost signing a guest book."

"It reads exactly like that because that's what it is." Ilsa wrapped both hands around her bowl, needing the warmth. "The departure log is real. The ship is real—it's still sitting in Berth Fourteen, or it was until I left it. But no one departed. I logged the manifest myself, using names from the original crew roster, because I needed the record to surface somewhere it couldn't be quietly deleted."

"Why those names? Why him?"

"Because of who would notice." Ilsa met her eyes. "Elias Crane was the *Meridian*'s signals officer. When the ship was scuttled, his personal data core was never recovered—or rather, it was recovered, catalogued, and then flagged restricted by an authority above the archive directorate, which should not exist. I found the flag three months ago. I've spent eleven years in fleet archives, Felix. I know what a buried file looks like. This one wasn't buried deep. It was buried *deliberately shallowly*, the way you hide something you may need to dig up fast."

Felix put her spoon down. "Scuttled," she repeated. "You said scuttled. Not destroyed. Not lost."

"Scuttled. From inside." Ilsa set the bowl aside; she couldn't eat while saying the rest. "I pulled the engineering telemetry before the flag locked me out. The reactor didn't breach. It was vented—in a controlled sequence, from the bridge, with the crew alive aboard. Someone aboard the *Meridian* killed their own ship. And the crew knew why, because the last transmission fragment in the buffer—the piece that got flagged—references the first shot of the war. Not who fired it. Where it came from. And it isn't either fleet."

The shed was very quiet. Somewhere outside, the irrigation lines cycled, patient as breathing.

"You're telling me," Felix said carefully, "that my brother died knowing something about the war. And that someone in your fleet's command structure has been sitting on his last words for nine years."

"I'm telling you worse than that." Ilsa's voice came out lower than she intended. "I'm telling you that when I requested the file through proper channels, my access was revoked within the hour, and a transfer order appeared in my queue—reassignment to a station I'd have no archive access from. Standard containment. Except I'd seen the pattern before, in other files, other names, other people who asked questions and then stopped asking them. So I ran. I took the *Meridian* out of Berth Fourteen under a salvage pretext, flew it here, and put your brother's name on a departure log because you are the only person in this system with a documented reason to care about Elias Crane and no chain of command to answer to."

"A deserter," Felix said. Not accusing. Cataloguing.

"An archivist," Ilsa answered, "who finally read something worth defecting over."

* * *

Felix stood then, and walked to the shed's small window, where the first tier of the garden glowed under the evening grow-lights—rows of green going gold at the edges, the whole slow miracle of it. Ilsa watched her back and understood she was being measured now, weighed in return, and deserved it.

"My brother wrote to me," Felix said at last, still facing the glass. "Every run. Short letters, mostly complaints about the coffee and descriptions of clouds he'd seen from the observation deck. He loved weather. We grew up on a station where weather was a rumor, and he treated every atmosphere he ever passed through like a gift." She turned. "His last letter came eight days before the *Meridian* was lost. He said the crew had been briefed on something and he couldn't write about it, but that if anything happened to him, I shouldn't believe the official account. Those were almost his exact words. *Don't believe the official account.* I was twenty-three. I believed the official account, because believing it hurt less than wondering."

"I'm sorry," Ilsa said, and meant it, and hated how small the words were.

"Don't be sorry. Be precise." Felix came back to the table but didn't sit. "You sought me out by name. You used my brother's name as bait to get my attention. You hid in my garden for two days instead of knocking. Every one of those choices tells me you're afraid—not of me, but of whoever else might be looking for that file. So before I decide whether to trust you, I want the thing you're not saying. Why come to me at all? There are defectors, black-market brokers, a dozen people between these fleets with no love for either admiralty. Why does an archivist with a stolen warship seek out a gardener?"

Because I read your service record, Ilsa thought. Because you transmitted a distress signal nine years ago that no one can source and no one will explain. Because the forgery I found unraveling in the archives carries fingerprints, and every road I walk leads back to this dome.

But she looked at Felix Crane's face—wary, grieving, lit green under the grow-lamps—and understood that the truth offered all at once would land like a boarding party. Some doors had to be opened from the inside.

"Because the *Meridian*'s crew trusted someone enough to die for what they knew," Ilsa said, "and I think that kind of trust leaves a shape behind it. Your brother's letters are in your keeping. His data core is in mine. Between us, we might be able to reconstruct what he knew—and who buried it." She paused. "And because I checked, Felix. Before I came. You're the only person in this system who tends something instead of guarding it. I decided that mattered."

Felix studied her for a long moment. Outside the shed window, the garden's lights dimmed into their night cycle, tier by tier, the whole dome settling toward dark.

"All right," Felix said finally. "Here's my price. You tell me everything, in order, starting with what the *Meridian*'s crew actually saw. No weighing. No deciding what I can carry. I've carried nine years of an official account that my own brother told me was a lie—I'm done being protected from the truth by people who mean well."

Ilsa nodded slowly. "Then I should start further back than the *Meridian*," she said. "Because the file that got me exiled—the one flagged above the archive directorate—it isn't just about the first shot." She drew a breath. "It's about a distress signal. Nine years old. A signal that froze both fleets in place and bought this truce, and whose origin no one in either archive can account for."

Felix went very still. In the dimming light, Ilsa watched the color leave her face, and understood, with the cold clarity of a lifetime spent reading documents, that the gardener already knew exactly which signal she meant.

"When I show you what's in that file," Ilsa said quietly, "you're going to want to tell me something. And Felix—I need you to tell me all of it. Because I don't think either of us survives what's coming unless we stop carrying our secrets separately."
