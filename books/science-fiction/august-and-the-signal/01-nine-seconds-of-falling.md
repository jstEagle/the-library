## 1. Nine Seconds of Falling

The trouble with hauling cargo between the outer colonies, Rosalind Amberdale had long ago decided, was that the silence got into you. Not the good kind of silence — not the kind you chose, with tea and a paperback and the autopilot humming — but the kind that pressed against the hull like a hand. Six years since Earth stopped answering. Six years of flying the Long Route with nothing but the beacon chatter of colonies that had learned to talk to each other very carefully, very quietly, as if the dark were listening.

She was on her third cup of recaf and losing an argument with the coffee maker when it happened.

"You know," Idris said from the copilot's seat, feet up on the console in flagrant violation of about four regulations he'd personally written, "I've been thinking about retrofitting the galley. Proper espresso rig. Something with a pressure head."

"The last time you retrofitted something, we lost hot water for a month."

"That was a learning experience."

"That was a *crime*, Idris."

He grinned at her — that easy, crooked grin that had survived two years of shared watches and one genuinely catastrophic argument about thermostat law — and she was halfway to telling him exactly where he could install his pressure head when the burn light went amber.

Routine. Just a routine correctional burn, the kind they'd done ten thousand times. Rosalind reached for the throttle, and the universe reached for her.

Gravity died.

Not drifted. Not stuttered. *Died* — all at once, everywhere, like a switch thrown in some room she'd never been allowed to see. Her recaf lifted out of its cup in a brown spiral. Idris came up off his seat like a fish pulled on a line. And the ship — the ship kept burning, because the Long Route was an honest old freighter and nobody had told her engines that physics had just resigned.

The thrust pressed them both toward the aft bulkhead. Rosalind's hand found the throttle by muscle memory alone, cutting the burn, but momentum is a debt and gravity had just stopped collecting it. She tumbled. The cargo netting ahead of her rushed closer with terrible patience. Somewhere Idris was shouting her name, or maybe swearing, or maybe both — Idris could do both at once, it was a gift —

— and then gravity came back.

All of it. At once. Like the universe clearing its throat after an awkward pause.

Rosalind hit the deck flat, hard enough to knock the thought clean out of her. For a moment she just lay there, cheek against cold plating, listening to forty tons of secured cargo settle with a groan, listening to her own heart hammering out a rhythm that felt less like fear than accusation.

"Ros." Idris's voice, close, breathless. A hand on her shoulder. "Ros, talk to me."

"I'm fine," she said, which was true in the way that most things are true: technically, provisionally, pending further review. She rolled over. He was crouched above her, hair floating back down around his face, a smear of blood at his eyebrow where he'd caught the edge of the console. "You're bleeding."

"It's a small price for looking heroic." But his hands, checking her over — collarbone, ribs, the backs of her skull — weren't joking hands. They were careful and quick and they lingered half a second too long at each stop, and neither of them mentioned it, because they never did, because there was a whole architecture of things they never mentioned and it held up the roof just fine.

"Nine seconds," he said finally, staring at the chrono. "Give or take. The whole system, Ros. Beacon traffic is going insane. Meridian Station lost a whole dock crew off the gantry. Somebody's tug dropped a fuel skid through a greenhouse dome on Halcyon Row." He laughed, but it was the wrong kind of laugh. "Nine seconds. Everything fell."

Everything fell. She sat up slowly, and the phrase snagged somewhere in her chest, in a place below thought.

*Everything fell.*

*They fell for nine seconds and then the fire took the rest.*

Rosalind went still.

It wasn't a memory. That was the thing she understood first, with a strange clinical calm — because Rosalind Amberdale knew exactly what her memory contained, having spent three years cataloguing it the way you'd catalogue a house you inherited furnished by a stranger. She had her original's childhood on Ceres Station. Her flight certification. Her last cargo manifest. Her final transmission, cut off mid-word. What she did not have — what no clone ever got, what the licensing boards swore was impossible — was anything *after* the copy point.

And this was after. This was smoke and screaming and a gravity that failed for nine seconds while forty thousand people learned what freefall meant inside a burning habitat.

"Ros?" Idris again. His voice had changed. "You've gone white. Whiter. You're basically a moon."

"Kepler's Reach," she said.

His eyebrows went up. "What?"

"I don't—" She pressed her palm flat against the deck, as if she could feel the words coming up through the metal. "I don't know why I said that. There's no colony registered under that name. I checked the colonial registry last month, all of it, I was bored — there are eleven hundred registered settlements and none of them is called Kepler's Reach."

"Then where did the name come from?"

She looked at him. He was still crouched there, bleeding gently, waiting, and she loved him in the quiet unannounced way she loved him, which was to say constantly and without paperwork.

"I think it came from her," Rosalind said. "From my original. From whatever's left down in the deep layers they told us doesn't exist."

* * *

They spent the next six hours doing what freighter crews do best: making tea, running diagnostics, and refusing to panic in an organized fashion. The fleet-wide bulletins came in slow and contradictory. Gravitational anomaly, said the Colonial Authority. Natural oscillation event, said the Fleet Liaison Office, whose bulletins always sounded like a man reading someone else's apology. No casualties reported beyond structural incidents, said a third bulletin, issued four hours after the first reports of casualties.

"They're lying," Idris said, "or incompetent."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

"No, but usually you get to pick one."

Rosalind stood at the nav console and pulled up the star charts. She didn't know why, exactly, except that her hands seemed to have opinions her mind hadn't authorized. She ran the name through every database aboard — registries, manifests, insurance rolls, the ancient colonial gazetteer that predated the Silence. Nothing. Kepler's Reach did not exist, had never existed, was a phrase with no address.

Except.

In the deep nav logs — the ones the ship recorded automatically, the ones nobody read unless something went wrong — there was a single entry, timestamped fourteen months ago. Fourteen months ago was before her activation. Before Idris. It was the original's last run.

A course deviation. Small, deliberate, logged and then buried under a maintenance annotation so boring it could only have been written by someone who wanted it never to be read. Rosalind decoded the coordinates from the deviation vector and felt the hair rise on her arms.

The coordinates pointed into open space. Officially. Unregistered space, past the survey boundary, in the black between Halcyon Row and the Meridian relay — a stretch of chart so empty the cartographers hadn't bothered to name it.

But her original had gone there. Had spent nine hours of delta-v getting there, on a cargo run that couldn't afford nine hours of anything. And then come back, and filed nothing, and six weeks later was dead in a docking accident that the incident report described, in a phrase Rosalind had always hated, as *pilot error*.

Her original had flown like a metronome. Rosalind had her hands; she knew.

"Pilot error," she said aloud, tasting it.

Idris looked up from the damage report. "What about it?"

"Nothing." She closed the log. Opened it again. Closed it. "Idris, if I asked you to take a detour — a real detour, off-chart, unpaid, into a part of space that isn't supposed to have anything in it — what would you say?"

He considered her for a long moment. Outside the viewport, the stars hung in their usual indifferent arrangement, though she found she couldn't look at them now without wondering which of them had fallen, nine seconds' worth, and who had been underneath.

"I'd say," he said slowly, "that the gravity of the entire system just hiccuped for nine seconds, and the people who tell us what things are called are already lying about it. And then I'd say yes. Obviously. Because you asked."

She almost smiled. Then the fragment surfaced again — unbidden, unwelcome, smoke-colored: *nine seconds of falling, and then the fire.* Forty thousand voices she had no right to carry, pressing up through the floor of her mind like water through a cracked hull.

"Course laid in," she said, and her voice only shook a little. "Kepler's Reach."

"There's no such place," Idris said gently.

"I know," said Rosalind. "That's what frightens me. Someone made sure of it."
