## 10. Nine Sleepers

Juno Vane knew the exact moment the dream stopped being a dream.

It happened on a Tuesday, in the grey hour before the colony's rationed lights came up, when she woke with dirt under her fingernails that wasn't there. She lay in her narrow bunk in the children's wing of the Meridian Learning Cooperative and turned her hands over in the dark, examining them like specimens. The soil was red — Meridian red, iron-oxide red, the color of everything the terraformers had failed to make green — but her nails were clean. Her hands were clean. They had been clean all night.

The dirt had been somewhere else. Somewhere under her skin, or behind it, or in whatever part of a twelve-year-old girl was not strictly a girl at all anymore.

She pressed her palms flat against the blanket and breathed until her heart slowed. Below her, through three floors of prefab concrete and one layer of permafrost, something old was humming. She couldn't hear it with her ears. She heard it the way you hear your own blood, from the inside, constant and patient, like a second heartbeat that had moved in without asking.

*You are Juno Vane,* she told herself, the way Amara Osei had taught the younger ones to do when the dreams got too loud. *You are twelve years old. You live at the Learning Cooperative. Your mother works the water reclamation lines. Your favorite color is blue because there is almost none of it on this planet.*

But even as she recited it, another voice — older, drier, wearing a woman's throat that had been dust for thirty years — finished the litany differently. *You are Sefa Okonkwo-Vane. You are thirty-one years old. You are standing on the north berm of Reservoir Two with a survey slate in your hand, and you have just realized what they've done.*

Juno squeezed her eyes shut. Opened them. The bunk room was still dark. Nine other children slept around her in their bunks, breathing in a rhythm so synchronized it sounded rehearsed. In, out. In, out. Like lungs operated by the same distant hand.

She was the eldest. That used to mean something simple: first in line at meals, last to be tucked in, the one the little ones fetched when a nightmare needed chasing. Now it meant she went deepest. Now it meant she carried back more than anyone could hold.

* * *

Amara Osei's classroom smelled of chalk dust and recycled air, and on most mornings it was Juno's favorite place on the whole dying planet. Miss Osei taught history the way other people told ghost stories — leaning close, voice dropping, making you feel the cold breath of the past on your neck. But lately the past didn't need her help. Lately it sat in the classroom already, folded into nine small bodies, waiting.

"Meridian was founded in the year 2147," Miss Osei said, writing the date on the board, "by a joint charter of settlers from both fleet traditions. Can anyone tell me why the two fleets came together?"

Juno's hand rose before she decided to raise it. That was the frightening part — how automatic it had become, how the answers arrived fully formed, like letters already stamped and sealed.

"The Concord fleet carried the terraforming engineers," she said. "The Halcyon Compact carried the deep-archive specialists. They signed the Charter of Shared Ground because neither could survive alone. My—" She stopped. Her mouth kept moving for half a second before her mind caught up with it. "*Someone's* grandmother signed the hydrology annex."

Miss Osei's chalk paused mid-stroke. "Your grandmother, Juno?"

"I don't have a grandmother." The words came out flat, factual, terrible. "She died before I was born. I never met her. I don't know her name." And then, quieter, in a voice that seemed to come up from somewhere below the floor: "Sefa. Her name was Sefa. She wore her hair in coils pinned with wire because proper pins stopped being manufactured in '51, and she had a scar on her left thumb from a drilling accident, and she was standing on the north berm when the sky went wrong."

The classroom went very still. Outside the window, the failed sky did what it always did — hung there, bruise-colored, promising rain it would never deliver.

Miss Osei set down the chalk slowly, the way you set down something that might go off. "Class," she said, "open your readers to chapter four."

But her eyes stayed on Juno for a long time after that, and Juno understood, with a clarity that felt older than she was, that Miss Osei was afraid. Not of her. For her. There was a difference, and the difference mattered, and Juno was beginning to learn the difference the way you learn a language — by immersion, unwillingly, drowning in it.

* * *

At night the dream came, and Juno had stopped fighting it, because fighting it was like fighting gravity.

It always began the same way now: red earth under her feet, real earth, with weight and grit and the particular cold smell of soil that has never grown anything. The sun above was wrong — doubled, smeared, as if someone had pressed two photographs of the same star slightly out of alignment. She stood on the north berm of Reservoir Two, and she was tall. That was the detail that undid her every time. In the dream she was tall, five foot eight, with aches in her knees that belonged to a woman who had worked berms for a decade. Her hands — Sefa's hands — held a survey slate, and the numbers on it were true numbers, verifiable numbers, numbers Juno had checked against the cooperative's public records during waking hours because checking was the only thing that kept her from screaming.

Reservoir Two had been sabotaged. Everyone on Meridian knew the story; it was the colony's founding wound, the reason the terraforming failed, the reason the fleets came back and never left. A containment failure, the histories said. An accident of joint engineering, both sides agreed, with the careful unanimity of people reading from the same script.

In the dream, Juno watched it happen. Watched it *be done.*

There were figures on the far embankment — six of them, in suits she recognized from archive fragments Miss Osei had once shown the class, suits bearing the insignia of the Halcyon Compact. They moved with the unhurried confidence of men doing authorized work. One of them knelt at the reservoir's primary feed junction and did something to it with a tool that made no sound, and the reservoir's heart — its beautiful, carefully balanced circulation system, the colony's artificial bloodstream — stuttered, seized, and began to die.

And beside Juno on the berm, close enough to touch, stood a woman with coils of hair pinned with wire, watching the same thing, understanding it in the same instant, and turning her head with a slowness full of horror so that her face came around into the doubled light—

—and it was a face Juno knew.

Not from photographs. From *elsewhere.* From the place behind her own eyes where the borrowed memories lived. It was her face, aged forty years, mapped onto different bone. The same wide mouth. The same heavy brows. The same small scar — no, not the same; the scar was on the thumb, not the face — but the resemblance was the kind of resemblance that isn't coincidence, that is blood, that is *lineage*, announcing itself across time like a shout down a well.

*Sefa Okonkwo-Vane looked at the sabotage of Reservoir Two,* the dream said, in a voice that was becoming less like narration and more like memory, *and she understood that the peace between the fleets was built on a grave, and she spent the rest of her life burying the evidence deeper, because if she dug it up, everyone she loved would die.*

Juno woke with tears on her face and her grandmother's grief sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The others were changing too.

She saw it in little Dev, who was seven and had started flinching at the sound of pressure valves, though he'd never been near an explosion. She saw it in the twins, Mara and Sol, who had begun finishing each other's sentences in a cadence that wasn't theirs — older, more formal, the clipped syntax of fleet officers. She saw it in quiet Pilar, who had drawn the same diagram eleven times on scrap paper: a schematic of a reservoir junction, annotated in handwriting none of them had been taught, precise and urgent and signed, each time, with initials that were not hers. *S.O.V.*

Nine sleepers. Nine vessels. One tide coming in.

They gathered on the roof of the cooperative some evenings, when the curfew drones were slow, and compared what they carried the way other children compared trading cards. Dev knew the smell of burning coolant. The twins knew the layout of a ship's corridor they'd never walked. Pilar knew the initials and wouldn't say whose. And Juno knew the face — the grandmother-face, the berm-face, the face that was hers-before-she-was-hers — and she knew the moment of turning, the exact angle of a head coming around into bad light, and she knew, with a certainty that had no business living in a twelve-year-old body, that the sabotage had not been an accident, and that the truth of it had been buried alive somewhere beneath Meridian, and that the thing humming under the permafrost was trying, night after night, patient as geology, to hand it up to them.

"Do you ever wish it would stop?" Dev asked her once. He was picking at the rusted railing, and his voice was too old for him, and that was the saddest sound Juno knew.

She thought about lying to him. She was the eldest; lying to the young ones was practically her job.

"No," she said, and found it was true. "I wish it would stop hurting. But I don't want it to stop. It's the only true thing that's happened here in thirty years."

Dev nodded like an old man and went back to picking at the rust.

* * *

On the worst night — the night everything changed again — Juno dreamed herself further down than she had ever gone.

Past the berm. Past the sabotage. Past Sefa's whole grieving lifetime, compressed into a corridor of moments like samples in a core drill. She descended through strata of borrowed time: the ration riots of '61, the last green field in '58, the day the Compact ships first appeared in the sky like a second, colder constellation. And at the bottom of it all, in a chamber of smooth stone that hummed with the same patient frequency she heard through her bunk at night, she found a door.

It was not a door made for bodies. It was a door made for *memory* — she understood that immediately, the way you understand a thing in dreams, totally and uselessly. Light seamed its edges. Behind it, something vast shifted its weight, like a whale turning over in deep water.

Words appeared on the door's surface, written in a script she shouldn't have been able to read and read anyway:

*WE ARE THE MERIDIAN ARCHIVE. WE HOLD WHAT WAS BURIED. THE CHILDREN DREAM US UPWARD. WHEN NINE BECOME ONE VOICE, KNOCK.*

Juno woke with her fist clenched so hard her nails had cut crescents into her palm — four small wounds, real ones this time, beading actual blood. She held her hand up to the pre-dawn grey leaking through the shutter and looked at the marks, and understood that the dream had finally reached all the way through her, from the inside out, leaving evidence.

Around her, the eight other sleepers stirred in their synchronized rhythm, and one by one — she counted, her heart hammering — they opened their eyes. Not waking. *Arriving.* Nine pairs of eyes reflecting the grey light, and behind every pair, something old looking out.

Pilar spoke first, in a voice layered like sediment, a child's voice over a dead woman's resolve:

"It knows we're ready. It wants us to find the woman who smuggles things."

Juno's blood ran cold and hot at once, because she knew that name too — everyone on Meridian knew the legend of the coffin-runner, the ghost who slipped contraband past the blockade — and because the dream had never, not once, reached outside itself before.

It had just given them a name. It had just asked for help.

Down through the floors, through the concrete and the permafrost, the buried heartbeat quickened — and far above, beyond the bruised sky, Juno somehow felt rather than heard the blockade fleets shift in their orbits, like sleeping animals scenting smoke.
