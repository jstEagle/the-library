## 17. Evacuation of the Innocent

Amara Osei had spent eleven years teaching children to read the sky through a dome that filtered it brown, and in all that time she had never once broken a rule worth the name. She had filed her lesson plans on time. She had signed every visitor log. She had returned library chips unscratched.

Now she stood in the dark of the school's lower corridor with nine sleeping bags, nine thermoses of sweetened grain tea, and a manifest that said she was shipping agricultural cryo-stock to the orbital transfer station — and the manifest was a lie, and the lie had her name on it, and her hands were steadier than they had any right to be.

"Again," she whispered. "From the top."

Juno crouched beside her in the dim emergency lighting, twelve years old and older than that in ways Amara couldn't bear to measure. Behind Juno, eight smaller shapes waited in a line against the wall: Tam and the Okafor twins, little Sefa clutching her stuffed moth, the rest of them wrapped in thermal blankets because the school's heating had been rationed down again and no one had thought to complain. They had rehearsed this four times tonight. Quiet feet. No names spoken aloud. If anyone asked, they were cargo.

"Cargo doesn't cry," Sefa said, very seriously, wiping her nose with her sleeve.

"Cargo doesn't cry," Amara agreed, kneeling to tuck the blanket higher around the girl's shoulders. "And neither do teachers. We're both very good at our jobs."

It was meant as a joke. It landed somewhere else entirely, and she felt it land — felt the truth of it settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well. She thought of the Compact team's blood-draw kits laid out on her own classroom tables two days ago, the polite technician who had smiled at Juno and called her *a remarkable subject*, the word *subject* sitting in Amara's ears ever since like a splinter working its way toward the heart. She thought of the message Freya Sterling had sent through channels so convoluted it took three relays and a dead drop to arrive: **They've matched the dream signature to the bloodwork. The children are evidence now. Evidence gets archived or destroyed. I can get them out. Trust me one more time.**

She had trusted Freya once already, downwell, in the archive's cold antechamber, when everything sane in her had said *this stranger is danger*. That trust had cost her the comfortable shape of her life. It seemed only honest to let it cost the rest.

"Ms. Osei." Juno's voice, low. "The truck's here."

Amara heard it too — the electric whine of a freight hauler idling in the loading yard, muffled by two walls and thirty years of dust. She rose, knees cracking, and looked down the line of children one more time. Nine faces. She knew each of them the way she knew her own lesson plans: Sefa's fear of loud noises, Tam's habit of counting under his breath when frightened, the twins' silent language of glances. She had taught them their letters. She had taught them that the world outside Meridian's dome was mostly rumor.

Tonight she was going to teach them that it wasn't.

* * *

Idris Voss had worn his customs uniform for nineteen years, and he had never once been grateful for it until now.

The Concord inspection team moved through Docking Bay 14 like water through a cracked hull — thorough, inevitable, finding every gap. Two officers in slate-grey field gear walked the rows of outbound containers with scanner wands, checking manifests against mass signatures, while their supervisor stood at Idris's shoulder radiating the particular patience of a man who already knew what he would find and was simply waiting for the paperwork to admit it.

"The Halcyon Compact appreciates your cooperation," the supervisor said. His badge read OKONKWO, and he pronounced the colony's name — Meridian — with the flat distaste of someone reading an obituary. "Outbound traffic has been... irregular lately."

"Irregular," Idris repeated. He kept his hands folded behind his back, his weight even, his breathing bored. Nineteen years of practice. "We're a dying colony, Officer Okonkwo. Everything about us is irregular. Our air is irregular. Our harvests are irregular. Last month a man tried to ship himself offworld inside a fertilizer drum because he'd heard Mars takes anyone with functioning lungs."

Okonkwo almost smiled. Almost. "And did you let him?"

"I fined him for undeclared mass." Idris gestured toward the container row with the weary sweep of a man pointing at his own disappointment. "Bay 14 is all standard outbound this cycle. Cryo-agricultural stock, medical waste, three coffins." He tapped his tablet. "Coffin manifests are sealed under bereavement privacy statutes. You're welcome to verify mass and radiation profiles, but I'll need a Concord warrant to open them, and the families will need to be notified, and the families on this rock have nothing left to lose, which makes them litigious."

It was beautiful, really, how well grief worked as a lock. Okonkwo's scanner hummed over the first coffin-shaped cryo-unit — unit seven of nine, though the manifest listed five — and Idris watched the readout with the detached interest of a man watching weather. Inside that unit, curled around a coolant core designed for seed stock, was a boy named Tam, sedated to a depth Idris still found frightening, his vital signs threaded through a biosim loop that made him read as frozen biomass. Freya had built the spoof herself, in six hours, from schematics she claimed to remember rather than possess. *I used to hide memory chips in these,* she'd said, not looking up from the solder. *Coffins were always cleaner. Nobody audits grief.*

Nobody audits grief. And nobody, apparently, audits a customs officer standing in plain sight of the search, because suspicion is a creature of shadows and Idris Voss had spent nineteen years learning to stand in light.

"Mass profile's clean," one of the wand-officers reported. "Biomass consistent. Thermal decay curve matches long-storage agri-stock."

Okonkwo studied Idris for a moment longer than comfort allowed. "You've processed a lot of shipments, Officer Voss?"

"Eleven thousand, four hundred and six," Idris said. "I count. It passes the time."

Something flickered across the Compact man's face — not suspicion, exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a fellow functionary; or pity, for the same. "Your colony is scheduled for full data audit within the quarter," Okonkwo said, turning away. "If anything irregular surfaces before then, the Compact remembers cooperation."

"We're a dying colony," Idris said again, mildly. "Remembering us seems like a lot of effort."

When the team finally withdrew, taking their scanners and their patience with them, Idris stood alone in Bay 14 and discovered that his hands, once the inspection cleared, were shaking badly enough that he had to grip the tablet with both of them. Eleven thousand, four hundred and six shipments. He had counted every one honestly, until tonight.

He keyed the bay's internal channel. "Sterling. They're gone. Bring the truck in before I come to my senses."

* * *

The loading took eleven minutes, and Amara spent all of them memorizing.

Freya Sterling moved through the freight bay like a woman defusing something — quick, precise, silent — unlatching each cryo-unit and lifting the children out one by one into the hold of a battered short-haul shuttle whose transponder, Amara noticed, read as a Concord medical courier. Forged, obviously. Everything about Freya was forged, Amara thought, except her hands, which shook slightly as she lifted Sefa free of the coolant cradle and checked the girl's pulse with two fingers held there perhaps a second longer than necessary.

"They'll wake groggy," Freya said, not looking up. "Confused. Don't tell them where they are until they ask. Kids take fear better from questions than from answers."

"You've done this before," Amara said. It wasn't a question either.

"I've done the other thing before." Freya settled Sefa into a crash webbing and cinched it with practiced hands. "Moving things people want hidden. This is the first time I've moved something people want *found*. It's different. It's worse." She glanced up, and in the shuttle's amber work-lighting Amara saw the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the way her jaw was set like a door braced against wind. "They'll be safe at Transfer Station Kestrel. There's a Concord relief barge that services it — humanitarian corridor, both fleets honor it because neither wants to explain to the interstellar press why they shot at orphans. From Kestrel they scatter. New identities. Idris has the papers."

"And you?" Amara asked. "Where do you scatter to?"

Freya's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I have an appointment with an archive."

There it was again — that flatness, that finality, the sound of a woman who had already written her last chapter and was just filling in the middle pages. Amara had seen it before, years ago, on the face of a colleague who stayed behind during the quarantine year. She hated it. She hated recognizing it.

"Juno won't leave quietly," Amara said instead. "She told me. She says the dream isn't finished with her, and she doesn't think she should run from it."

Freya went very still. "The dream isn't finished with any of us," she said. "That's what makes it dangerous. But Juno is twelve, and the difference between a witness and a casualty is whether anyone bothered to move her first. Tell her—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Tell her the truth travels better alive."

* * *

Juno woke to the smell of coolant and old metal, and for three disorienting seconds she didn't know which memories were hers.

That was the worst part now, the part she'd stopped telling Ms. Osei: the seams had dissolved. She woke and her grandmother's kitchen was real — the yellow wall, the chipped bowl, the radio saying words Juno hadn't lived yet — and then Meridian's classroom was real, and then the sabotage was real, fire blooming silently across a fleet hull forty years before Juno was born, and all of it sat in her head side by side like books on the same shelf, and no librarian came to sort them.

She was strapped into a seat. Amber lights. A stranger's shuttle. Ms. Osei beside her, holding her hand, and eight smaller shapes asleep or half-asleep in webbing down the aisle, and — Juno turned her head — the smuggler woman, Freya, crouched by the hatch with a tablet, talking low with the customs officer from the stories, the one Ms. Osei called Idris, who looked like a man standing in a place he'd never expected his life to go.

"You're awake," Ms. Osei said softly. "How do you feel?"

"Like I'm carrying somebody else's luggage." Juno's voice came out rough. "All of it. Nobody else's."

Ms. Osei's hand tightened around hers. "We're getting you out. All nine of you. Off Meridian, past the fleets, somewhere they can't draw your blood whenever they're curious."

Juno looked down the aisle at Sefa, at the twins, at Tam still slack with sedation and dreaming, she supposed, whatever the dream dreamed when it was alone. Something in her chest pulled tight and strange. She had known these kids since she was six. She had taught Sefa to whistle. And now they were leaving together, packed like the seed stock they'd rehearsed being, and the strangest thing — the thing Juno turned over in her mind like a stone with a surprising underside — was that it didn't feel like escape.

It felt like a convoy. Like the founding ships must have felt, the ones in the dream, the ones her great-great-grandmother had boarded with nothing but a name and a bunk assignment. Leaving everything, but not alone, and not without a reason.

"The dream followed us onto the ships back then too," Juno said, before she could stop herself. "In the beginning. Everyone dreamed the same thing the first night out from Earth. My grandmother told— " She caught herself. Her grandmother hadn't told her. The grandmother in the dream had told the girl in the dream, and the dream had handed it to Juno like an inheritance nobody had signed for. "Someone told me," she finished.

Freya had stopped talking to Idris. She was looking at Juno across the aisle with an expression Juno couldn't file anywhere — grief and recognition and something almost like awe.

"What did they dream?" Freya asked. "That first night out?"

Juno closed her eyes and let the shelf open. "The same thing we dream now. The beginning. Earth going dark behind them. And a voice saying: *remember it correctly, whoever gets there after us.*"

The shuttle's engines spun up. Through the small viewport, Meridian's dome slid into view — brown-lit, dying, the only home any of them had ever had — and Juno pressed her palm flat against the cold glass, the way you'd press a hand to the flank of an animal you loved and couldn't save.

* * *

Idris rode the shuttle to the edge of customs jurisdiction because someone had to fly the approach vector that wouldn't trip the blockade's picket grid, and because — though he'd have denied it under oath — he wanted to see it done. He wanted to watch the nine units' worth of children become nine living children again, wanted the number to be real in his body and not just his ledger.

At the jurisdiction boundary, Freya took the controls back and brought the shuttle alongside his return skiff in the long silence of vacuum. Over the open channel, her voice came through stripped of everything but function.

"Kestrel's beacon codes are in the packet. The relief barge *Mercy of Thessaly* docks in thirty-one hours. Idris — the papers you forged are good. Better than good. When they ask how a customs officer produced nine clean identities overnight, don't tell them about me."

"Who would believe me?" Idris said. "I count shipments. It passes the time."

A pause. Then, quieter, in a register he had never heard her use: "Eleven thousand, four hundred and seven. That one counted."

The channel closed. The shuttle turned, a slow wheel of running lights against the brown curve of Meridian, and dropped toward the surface — toward the buried archive, toward the appointment she wouldn't name, toward whatever she had decided her last chapter said. Idris watched until the lights vanished, then set his course for the station, for the barge, for nine children who would wake up in a few hours as strangers with new names and one true story between them.

His tablet chimed. A priority flag from Compact command: **Audit accelerated. Full data seizure of Meridian orbital traffic commences 0400. Detain and question all personnel with archive-adjacent access.**

He looked at the timestamp. Four hours. Maybe less, if Okonkwo's team had already flagged the coffin manifests for a second look — and they would, because grief was a good lock but Okonkwo struck Idris as a man who carried bolt cutters.

He keyed the channel to the surface, to the school, to Amara, knowing even as he did it that he was burning the last of his careful, counted life.

"Amara. Listen carefully. They're moving early. Whatever Freya's doing down there — she has until dawn, and I don't think dawn's coming."
