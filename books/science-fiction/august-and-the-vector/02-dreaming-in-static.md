## 2. Dreaming in Static

The dream always began below hearing.

Not silence—Kettle's Reach did not know how to be silent—but a pressure, a slow tidal breathing that lived under the floor of sound the way roots live under a road. In the dream Lena stood ankle-deep in it. The frequency was somewhere around three hertz, though numbers were useless here; in the dream she didn't measure it, she *was* it, a long gray swell rolling through her chest cavity, rearranging her organs into instruments. Around her the salt flats stretched white to a horizon the color of old bone, and out of that horizon rose shapes—not mountains exactly, not buildings—more like the idea of shelter, half-finished, patient.

The planet spoke in the dream the way it spoke when she was awake with her equipment: not in words but in durations. A tremor held for eleven seconds meant something she had once translated, privately, in her own shorthand, as *the ground remembers rain*. A stutter of microquakes along the western fault line was either greeting or warning; after nine years she had stopped needing to decide which. Language, her mentor had told her a lifetime ago, is just agreement wearing a costume. Kettle's Reach had never agreed to anything. That was what made it honest.

In the dream tonight there was something new threaded through the swell—a phrase, if she could call it that, repeating at intervals too regular for geology and too gentle for machinery. It moved beneath the other patterns the way a second voice moves beneath a lullaby, harmonizing without ever surfacing. She leaned toward it the way you lean toward a conversation through a wall, catching cadence without content, and the leaning itself became the whole of the dream: her body tilting, tilting, the gray swell rising past her knees, past her waist, the unfinished shelters on the horizon seeming to lean with her—

She woke with her jaw clenched and her hands pressed flat against the mattress, as if she'd been bracing against a current only she could feel.

The station hummed its small human hum around her: air recyclers, coolant pumps, Idris's perpetually complaining reactor two decks down ticking over at its night-cycle pitch. Outside the viewport, Kettle's Reach turned through its long indigo dusk, the salt flats catching the light of the gas giant's reflected glow like a sea that had given up on water. Forty-one people slept inside this tin drum. Somewhere below all that rock and salt, the planet went on saying whatever it had been saying for four billion years.

Lena lay still and reached, as she did every morning, for her mother's voice.

It was gone.

She knew the shape of the loss better than the thing itself now—the way you know a missing tooth by the tongue's constant, surprised return to the gap. She could remember that her mother had had a voice. She could remember facts about it: low, a little hoarse in the mornings, prone to dropping into song mid-sentence, some old Baltic folk melody about a birch tree that Lena had hated as a child and would have given both hands to hear again. But the actual sound—the grain of it, the particular way her mother's vowels rounded off at the ends of words—had been sanded away year by year, and sometime in the last few months the last of it had gone under.

She tested herself anyway, lying in the dark, the way she did every morning. *Say something, Mama.* And memory produced not a voice but a description of a voice, a museum placard where the exhibit used to be. *Birch tree, white birch, standing at the water—*

Nothing. Static where the signal used to be.

She got up before the grief could settle into its daytime shape. There was tea to make, and a census to finish, and a planet outside that had been talking all night whether or not anyone listened.

* * *

The mess was warm and smelled of reconstituted oats and machine-lubricant, which was to say it smelled like home, if home were honest about itself. Sefa was already there, twelve years old and folded cross-legged onto a bench too high for her, eating with the ferocious efficiency of a child who had learned early that food was arithmetic. She looked up when Lena came in, and her face did the thing it always did—brightened first, then composed itself into the careful neutrality children of dying colonies learn instead of manners.

"You were talking again," Sefa said. "Last night. Through the wall."

"Was I."

"The new language." Sefa made a sound, low and sliding, an imitation of one of Lena's phonemes rendered through a child's throat—wrong in every particular and somehow recognizable anyway. "Idris says you're praying. Are you praying?"

"I'm practicing," Lena said, and took her tea to the window.

Practicing. That was the word she used, and it was even true, in the way that a map is true. What she practiced was a language with no other speakers, assembled across nine years from seismic harmonics and atmospheric pressure waves and the subsonic conversations of root-systems under the southern tundra. She had begun keeping a spoken version of it for herself because a language that lives only in spectrograms dies in them too, and because—and this was the part she did not examine in daylight—because speaking it aloud felt less like construction than like recall. As if the language had been waiting somewhere in her, the way a mother's voice waits, and all these years of listening were really a kind of remembering.

The Linguistics Corps would have called it contamination. Subject immersed beyond safe exposure thresholds; analyst's objectivity compromised; recommend rotation. She had signed away their right to rotate her. She had signed away quite a lot.

Sefa drifted over to the window beside her, oat-bowl cradled in both hands, and they watched the dawn line crawl across the salt together. Out past the eastern ridge, the ground steamed faintly where geothermal vents bled heat into the cold air, and Lena found herself reading the steam the way other people read weather—as text. *The vents are running rich today,* the plumes said, in the idiom she'd spent nearly a decade learning. *The deep water is moving.*

"Lena," Sefa said, in the tone of a question being smuggled in sideways. "What was Earth like?"

And there it was, the oldest trap on Meridian Station, baited fresh every few months by a new generation of children. Lena looked down at the girl—at the fine dark braids Idris's late wife had taught her to keep, at the collar of a jacket cut down from a man's coat because no supply ship had come with children's clothes in nineteen years—and she lied, gently, the way you set a broken bone: quickly, firmly, so the pain comes later and apart.

"Crowded," she said. "Loud. Full of voices in more languages than anyone could count, all of them talking at once, and everyone convinced their own was the important one." She smiled. "You'd have hated it for a week and missed it forever."

"Did you miss it? When you came here?"

"I hadn't left yet," Lena said, which was true, and also not the answer, and Sefa, who was sharp in the specific way of children raised by tired adults, let it go with the grace of someone banking a debt.

Across the mess, Idris looked up from his maintenance tablet, and for a moment his eyes rested on Lena with an expression she couldn't file—something between concern and the particular patience of a man watching weather he can't do anything about. Then he winked at Sefa, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

The recruitment had happened in a room with no windows, which she understood now was the Corps' idea of a joke, or maybe its idea of honesty.

She was twenty-six, freshly certified, top of her cohort in dead-language reconstruction, and she had been summoned—not invited, summoned—to a sublevel of the Corps annex in Nairobi where the walls were acoustic foam and the interviewer, a woman named Director Osei with a voice like dry paper, slid a file across the table.

"Kettle's Reach," Osei said. "Colony charter revoked forty years ago. Earth stopped answering their transmissions thirty-one years ago. Officially, the colony doesn't exist. Unofficially, forty-odd people are still alive down there, farming failing soil and maintaining equipment nobody will ever send parts for. And the planet itself—" Osei tapped the file, "—is producing structured non-random acoustic phenomena. Low frequency. Persistent. Possibly communicative."

"Possibly?"

"We don't fund certainty, Dr. Nystrom. We fund possibility." Osei leaned back. "Here is the arrangement. You go. Alone—you'll have station support, but no linguistic colleagues; we can't spare them and frankly the phenomenon doesn't warrant them. You listen. You catalog. You transmit your findings on the quarterly band. In exchange, the Corps keeps Meridian Station on the resupply registry. Food, filters, medical stock. Your work buys those people another decade. Maybe two."

Lena remembered turning the file over in her hands. Remembered the satellite imagery of the salt flats, white as a page nothing had yet been written on.

"And if I decline?"

"Then the station comes off the registry within the fiscal quarter. Colonies that produce nothing get nothing; that's not cruelty, it's accounting." Osei's face did not change. "I want to be clear about what we're offering, because most candidates hear it wrong. We are not offering you a career. This posting is a tombstone with a stipend. Nine years out, ten, and no one in this building will remember your name. What we're offering is a trade. Your life, for theirs. Do you understand?"

She had understood. That was the terrible thing, looking back—that she had understood perfectly, and said yes anyway, and the yes had not even required the full second it took her heart to catch up.

Because here was what she had not put in any application, what she had never told Osei or the certification board or anyone: she had wanted the exile. Her mother had died fourteen months earlier—quickly, a vascular event between one sentence and the next, mid-melody, the birch-tree song amputated at its third line—and Lena had discovered that grief, for her, did not want company. It wanted distance. It wanted a horizon with nothing on it. The Corps was offering her a wound dressed as a duty, and she had grabbed it with both hands and called it sacrifice, and perhaps it had been, and perhaps both things were true at once, the way a single signal can carry two messages on two bands and only the receiver decides which one is real.

Her last night on Earth, she had called her brother in Helsinki and let it ring until voicemail, and then stood in the transit lounge listening to the announcement systems cycle through six languages, trying to memorize the sound of human speech at scale—the roar of it, the sheer crowded tonnage of eight billion throats—because some part of her, the honest part, knew she was walking into a silence she would never fully walk back out of.

She had been right. She had been so completely right.

* * *

By afternoon the wind had come up off the flats, and Lena climbed the external stair to the listening post with her kit on her back and the wind worrying at her jacket seams like a dog that wanted playing with.

The listening post was hers alone: a sensor dome bolted to the rock above the station, a rack of aging analyzers, a chair with a broken armrest she'd never fixed because the break fit her elbow. From here, nine years of the planet's voice hung in ordered columns on her screens—seismic harmonics rendered as color, atmospheric infrasound as texture, the deep tidal groan of the mantle as a baseline that never varied and never quite repeated.

She settled in, jacked the hydrophones into the bedrock array, and let the day's recording begin.

And there it was again. The thing from the dream.

She sat forward. Ran it back. Ran it again.

Threaded beneath the familiar chord-structures of the planet's daily speech—the vent-song, the fault-hum, the slow mantle tide—was a new pattern. Not loud. Barely above the noise floor. But structured: a sequence of low pulses with internal repetition, recurring at intervals of roughly ninety minutes, each recurrence slightly modified from the last. Geology did not modify. Geology repeated until it eroded. This was doing something else. This was, she thought with a sensation like the floor of her understanding tilting a degree off true, developing.

She pulled up yesterday's archive. Nothing—or rather, nothing she could isolate; the pattern might have been there for days, weeks, buried under the planet's louder registers, waiting for the wind conditions and the array alignment to lift it into legibility. She tagged the segment, ran a preliminary structural analysis, and watched the readout bloom with figures that meant, in the cold grammar of the software, *non-random, non-periodic, hierarchical*.

Anomaly 400, she typed into the log. Possible structured signal, low-band, source undetermined. Recommend continued monitoring.

She looked at the recommendation she had just written—*recommend continued monitoring*, addressed to a Corps that had not answered a quarterly report in six years, filed in a log no one would ever open—and laughed, once, quietly, in the empty dome. The laugh came out sounding like static.

Outside, the sun was going down behind the gas giant, and the salt flats turned the color of a held breath, and far below her chair, below the rock and the permafrost and whatever waited in the deep places of Kettle's Reach, something patient kept saying its new phrase into the dark—over and over, each time a little changed, like a voice rehearsing, like someone very far underground practicing how to be heard.
