## 4. The Loop

Idris came to find her at the hour when the salt flats turned the color of old bone, when the wind off the Kettle dropped and the whole station seemed to hold its breath between one day and the next. She heard his boots on the gantry before she saw him—she always did; he walked like a man who had made peace with every machine he touched—and she closed the log she'd been writing in, the one with the anomaly in it, the phrase threaded through the planet's long seismic song like a silver wire through gray cloth.

"You're up on the roof a lot," Idris said, settling beside her on the cold plating. He handed her a mug of recaf that tasted, as always, faintly of the pipes it had traveled through. "Used to be you watched the sky. Now you watch the ground."

"The sky stopped saying anything interesting forty years ago," Lena said.

"That's what I wanted to talk to you about."

He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but she knew him well enough by now—nine years of shared air filters and shared silences—to hear the weight underneath. He was looking out at the flats, not at her, and his hands were wrapped around his own mug the way people wrap their hands around things when they need something solid.

"Come down to the comms room," he said. "I want to show you something. And I want Sefa there."

"Sefa?"

"She asked me a question last week I couldn't answer. I've been thinking about it ever since." He stood, joints cracking in the dry cold. "I think maybe you can answer it better than me. Or lie better. You're from off-world. You know how it's done."

* * *

The comms room at Meridian Station was the oldest room and the least used, a cathedral of dead equipment that Idris kept alive out of what Lena suspected was pure stubbornness. Racks of amplifiers older than either of them hummed at frequencies just below hearing—a sound Lena felt in her back teeth rather than heard, a sensation so familiar now that its absence would have been unbearable. The walls were lined with cable runs in colors that had faded to the same dusty anonymity. In the corner, under a sheet of salvaged polymer, sat the receiver that mattered: the long-band array that caught everything Earth chose to send them, which was, and had been for decades, exactly one thing.

Sefa was already there. Of course she was. Fifteen years old and built entirely of questions, she sat cross-legged on the floor beneath the main console, surrounded by the disassembled guts of a radio she'd been "fixing" for three months without any discernible progress or any discernible damage. She looked up when they came in, and her face did the thing it always did—the quick brightening, quickly suppressed, because she was fifteen and brightening was not yet cool.

"Took you long enough," she said.

"I'm old," Idris said. "Old men walk slow."

"You're not old. Amara's grandmother is old. You're just broken in."

Lena laughed despite herself, and Idris shot her a look of mock betrayal, and for a moment the room was just the three of them being what they were: the station family, small and warm inside its shell of steel, while outside the wind walked the flats and the planet sang its low song to no one.

Then Idris crossed to the console and woke the long-band receiver, and the moment changed.

"Show her," he said to Sefa. "You found it. You show her."

Sefa scrambled up, suddenly serious, and Lena felt something tighten in her chest—protective, preemptive, though she didn't yet know what she was protecting the girl from. Sefa's hands moved over the controls with an ease that would have shamed half the Corps trainees Lena had once taught. She'd learned on this equipment the way children learn language: completely, without knowing there was another way to be.

"Okay, so." Sefa pulled up the signal log on the cracked display. "You know how we get the Earth transmission, right? Every cycle. Forty seconds, then nothing for—" she checked, though she didn't need to check, "—six hours, eleven minutes, and change. It's been doing that since before I was born. Idris says since before *he* was born."

"Since before your grandmother was born," Idris said quietly.

"Right. So it's just... background. Like the wind. Nobody listens to it anymore because it never changes." Sefa glanced up at Lena, and there was something in her face that made Lena's stomach drop slowly, the way a lift drops—not fast, but all the way down. "Except it does change. A little. If you look at it over years instead of days."

She played the loop.

Lena had heard it before, of course. Everyone at Meridian had, at some point, usually once, usually young, standing in this same room while an adult explained what Earth was: the homeworld, the origin, the place that had sent their great-great-grandparents out into the dark with seeds and machines and promises. The transmission was a recording of a voice—a woman's voice, warm and slightly rough, the kind of voice that belonged to someone who talked for a living—and behind her, faintly, the sound of weather, real weather, rain on leaves, a sound Kettle's Reach had never made in the entire history of human habitation.

*"—to the settlers of the Reach, and to their children, and to their children's children: you are not forgotten. The work you do there matters. The world you are building matters. Hold fast, and know that we—"*

Forty seconds. Then silence, six hours long. Then again.

Lena had heard it a hundred times over nine years, mostly as texture, mostly as proof that the receiver still worked. But she heard it differently now, watching Sefa's face as the girl listened—watching the way Sefa's lips moved slightly along with words she must have memorized without meaning to, the way a child memorizes a prayer or a song from the next room.

"It changes," Sefa said, when the silence came back. She pulled up the spectral analysis, decades of it, compressed into a single waterfall of color. "Look. The carrier wave is degrading. Not the recording—the recording's fine, it's stored clean. But the transmitter itself, wherever it is, whatever's sending it—it's getting weaker. Every year, a little less power. Look at the edges here, see how they're softening? That's not the atmosphere. That's the machine dying."

"How long have you known?" Lena asked.

"A year? Maybe more. I didn't want to say anything until I was sure." Sefa's chin came up, defiant, daring them to have kept something from her that she hadn't kept from them. "I ran the decay curve. At current rates, the signal drops below noise floor in—" she paused, and for the first time her certainty flickered, "—eleven, twelve years. Maybe less if the degradation accelerates. Which it probably will. Machines don't die linearly."

The room hummed. Below hearing, below almost everything, the loop waited for its next scheduled repetition, patient as geology.

"So that's my question," Sefa said, turning to face them both, and Lena understood suddenly why Idris had brought her here, why he'd needed witnesses for this. "When it stops—when Earth goes actually silent, not pretend-silent like now—is anyone coming? Has anyone ever even *thought* about coming? Because I did the math on that too, and the last supply ship logged here was sixty-three years ago, and nobody has answered a single hails-and-requests packet since I've been alive, and Idris says—" her voice caught, just barely, and she pushed through it, "—Idris says they answered *his* grandfather's packets. So somewhere in there they stopped. And nobody told us why."

The question hung in the humming air. Rain on leaves, in a recording two centuries old. *You are not forgotten.*

Lena looked at Idris. He was watching her, and his face held no accusation, no demand—only the quiet, terrible patience of a man who had decided long ago that the truth was a machine he could keep running past its rated lifespan, and wanted to know if she agreed.

"Sit down, Sefa," Lena said gently. "Both of you. I'll tell you what I know."

It was, she would think later, the first lie of many. It was also, in its way, the kindest thing she did that year.

* * *

What she told them was this:

Earth was still there. Earth was vast and loud and full of people, billions of them, more people than Kettle's Reach had ever contained in its whole history, and most of them had never heard of the Reach, not because they didn't care but because the galaxy was large and attention was finite. The silence wasn't abandonment. It was bureaucracy—budget cycles, charter reviews, the slow grinding paperwork of a civilization that had spread too far to look closely at any single point of it. Somewhere in a records office, a file marked MERIDIAN STATION — STATUS: ACTIVE — REVIEW PENDING had been sitting in a queue for decades, and someday, eventually, someone would pull it, and ships would come, and everything would resume.

She told it well. She was a linguist; she knew how stories carried truth even when they were false, how the shape of a comforting sentence could hold a child upright the way a splint holds a broken bone. She gave them details she invented on the spot and delivered with the flat confidence of fact: the orbital elevators like threads of light, the weather controlled so thoroughly that rain happened only where it was invited, the universities where a girl from the Reach could study anything at all. Sefa listened with her whole body, leaning forward, eyes wide, drinking it in—and Lena watched her drink and felt each swallow like a stone going down her own throat.

Because the truth—the truth she had seen fragments of in Corps briefings, before the Corps stopped briefing anyone about the outer colonies, before the Corps itself went quiet in ways that had driven her, though she hadn't admitted it then, out to the edge of everything—was worse than silence. The truth had shapes in it she couldn't yet name, only feel, the way you feel a storm coming in the ache of old scars. There were reasons colonies went unanswered, and some of those reasons had protocols attached, and the protocols had names that sounded clean and meant fire.

But Sefa was fifteen. Sefa had never seen anything green that hadn't been grown under lamps. Sefa deserved ten more years of believing that the universe was merely distracted rather than deliberate.

"So they'll come," Sefa said. "Eventually. When the file gets pulled."

"Eventually," Lena said. "And when they do, they'll find us still here. Still holding fast." She smiled, and made the smile true, because a forgery believed by the forger is indistinguishable from honesty—though she wouldn't learn that lesson properly for years yet. "That's what the woman in the recording means, you know. *Hold fast.* It's not a slogan. It's an instruction. We're doing exactly what Earth asked. We're just... doing it very patiently."

Sefa laughed—a real laugh, relief-shaped—and Idris exhaled beside her, slow and careful, and Lena realized he had been holding his breath through her whole performance, waiting to see what she would choose.

Later, after Sefa had gone back to her radio autopsy, whistling, lighter by one enormous weight, Idris stayed behind in the humming dark.

"You're good at that," he said. Not admiring. Just noting it, the way he noted a pressure reading.

"I'm a linguist. Lying is ninety percent of translation."

"Mm." He looked at the receiver, at the little green light that meant the loop was mid-silence, six hours of nothing before the woman's voice rose again out of the rain-on-leaves past. "Twelve years, she says. Before the signal dies." He was quiet a moment. "You know what happens the day it dies, don't you? Officially. The charter says continuous contact with the originating authority. Signal loss triggers review. Review triggers—" He waved a hand. "Whatever triggers. Nothing good comes out of a trigger."

"I know what charters say."

"Do you?" He turned and looked at her then, fully, and there was something new in his face—not suspicion, not yet, but the first faint shadow of the shape suspicion would take. "Because sometimes I think you know a lot of things, Lena Nystrom. Things you brought with you from off-world. Things you keep in that log of yours that you close every time I walk up the gantry."

The hum filled the pause. Below hearing, below everything, the loop counted toward its next repetition.

"What I keep in my log," Lena said carefully, "is the planet's song. Seismic harmonics. Geology, Idris. Rocks being rocks."

"Rocks being rocks," he repeated, noncommittal, and let it go—but she saw him file it away, the way he filed everything, in that vast patient workshop of a mind where nothing was ever thrown out.

At the door he paused. "For what it's worth," he said, "the lie you told her was a good one. If the universe is kind, it stays true longer than twelve years." Then he was gone, boots fading down the gantry, and Lena stood alone in the comms room with the dead equipment and the living hum and the little green light.

She stayed until the loop fired—forty seconds of rain and warmth and *you are not forgotten*, spoken by a woman two centuries dead to a colony she'd never seen—and then she climbed back up to the roof, into the wind, and opened her log, and wrote:

*Anomaly 400 persists. Third recurrence tonight, same structure, same patience. Whatever is singing with the planet knows the song. And I am beginning to suspect the song is not the planet's at all.*

Below the salt flats, far beneath the reach of any receiver, something answered the next harmonic pulse on time, in grammar, and waited—as patient as a loop, as scheduled as a countdown—for her to understand.
