## 8. Forty-Two Souls

The salt flats began where the station's weather sensors gave up pretending. Beyond the last ridge of rust-colored rock, the ground went white—not snow-white, not bone-white, but a pale mineral glare that made the horizon dissolve into itself, so that walking across it felt like walking on the inside of an overexposed photograph. Lena had crossed the flats twice in nine years, both times for instrument maintenance she could have delegated. She had never crossed them alone, and never at night.

She crossed them now at night because the beacon said *do not tell the sky*, and the sky was the only witness the flats offered after dark.

Her skiff's running lights were off. She navigated by the low amber glow of her wrist display and by the planet itself—by the song, which was louder out here than anywhere on the surface, rising through the salt crust in long tectonic exhalations that she felt in her sternum before she heard them with her ears. Nine years of listening had taught her the song's grammar: the deep diurnal swell as Kettle's Reach's molten heart turned beneath its crust, the staccato chatter of thermal contraction along the eastern fault, the slow minor-key moan of the ice caps breathing. She knew this music the way she had once known her mother's humming—without effort, without translation, without any part of herself standing apart from it.

And threaded through it now, unmistakable even from thirty kilometers out, was the phrase.

*We are still here. Do not tell the sky.*

It repeated every forty-one minutes. She had timed it against the station's master clock three times before she'd allowed herself to believe it wasn't coincidence—the number of souls at Meridian Station, arriving like a signature, like someone below counting them the way she counted everyone else. The thought had kept her awake for two nights. Someone down there knew how many of them there were. Someone down there had been counting for a long time.

The bearing resolved under the flats, eleven kilometers past the old brine-processing ruins, at a place where the salt crust domed upward in a blister the size of a stadium. Her seismic maps showed what the surface didn't: a void beneath the dome, a chamber, and branching away from it, a lattice of galleries following the ancient brine channels like the root system of an inverted tree. She had catalogued these caverns years ago as anomaly clusters 112 through 118 and moved on, because the harmonics there had seemed like echo, like the planet talking to itself in an empty room.

They hadn't been empty. The room had been full of listeners, and some of them had been answering back.

* * *

She left the skiff two kilometers out and walked the rest, boots crunching on salt that broke like sugar glass under her weight. The cold up here was absolute—a dry, mineral cold that found the gap between her collar and her jaw and stayed there. Her breath came out in small ghosts that the wind took sideways. Above her, Kettle's Reach's sky performed its nightly ritual: the broken ring of the Ashfall Fleet's derelict relay, still visible after two centuries as a smudge of occluded stars, turning slowly, turning slowly, a wound in the constellations that every child at the station learned to point at and then stop seeing.

Lena had never stopped seeing it. Now she walked toward it as if it were a compass heading, though the beacon came from below.

The dome's edge announced itself as a change in the crunch—a softer give, salt laid down in thinner layers over hollow ground. She knelt and brushed the crust away with her gloved hand, and beneath it, exactly where her models said the chamber wall should be, there was metal.

Not wreckage. Not salvage. A door.

It was set flush into the rock, a meter and a half of dark composite shaped to follow the cavern's natural curve, and if you didn't know to look—if you hadn't spent nine years learning to read this planet's body—you would have taken it for geology. The seam was filled with pressed salt. Lichen the color of dried blood grew across its face in patterns too regular to be accidental, cultivated, she realized, or at least encouraged, a living camouflage renewed by hands. Someone tended this door. Someone had been tending it longer than Meridian Station had existed.

There was no handle. There was no lock. There was a shallow depression at chest height, and in the depression, worn smooth by use, a shape like a cupped palm.

Lena stood in the white glare of nothing with her hand hovering over the stone, and for a long moment she did not move. Every protocol she'd ever been taught, every ethics seminar in the Linguistics Corps, every line in the first-contact doctrine she'd memorized at twenty-three and half forgotten at forty—they all said the same thing: *you do not knock on the door of people who have hidden themselves. You wait to be found.* Contact initiated without consent was a violence. She had written a paper on it once. It had been well received by eleven people.

But the beacon had called her. That was the thing she kept returning to, the thing that let her breathe. *We are still here. Do not tell the sky.* A message sent outward is an invitation, however frightened. Whoever broadcast those words had chosen, after two hundred years of silence, to speak—and they had spoken in a language they must have known only a linguist could hear, threaded into frequencies no Earth survey would ever flag as anything but noise. They hadn't called for rescue. They had called for *her*. Or for whatever she represented: one pair of ears that had learned, slowly and by accident, to hear them coming.

She pressed her palm into the depression.

For ten seconds, nothing. Then the door hummed—a note she recognized, a note from the song, a harmonic she had filed years ago as anomaly 117-C, *resonant response, source unknown*—and the salt crusted across the seam shivered loose in a fine white cascade, and the door swung inward on darkness that breathed warm air into her face.

Warm air. After the flats' killing cold, it felt like being spoken to kindly.

* * *

The passage sloped down and widened, and the walls began to glow.

Not electric light—something older and stranger, veins of bioluminescent culture grown along the rock in long parallel lines, dim gold, pulsing very slightly, and Lena understood within a few steps that the pulses were language. The same harmonic architecture she'd been decoding for nine years, rendered visible, the planet's song written on the walls in living light. She stopped and put her bare hand against one vein and felt the vibration enter her bones, and the phrase it carried was simple, repeated, patient:

*Come in. Come in. Come in.*

The corridor opened into the chamber her seismographs had drawn for her years ago, and the reality of it took her breath the way the cold had, but differently. The dome-vaulted space rose sixty meters overhead, and every surface was inscribed—walls, ceiling, the great support pillars of fused salt and basalt—with the same luminous script, generations of it, layers over layers, a library grown rather than built. Walkways spiraled up the chamber's inner curve. Gardens hung in terraced shelves, lit by the same gold veins: pale tubers, fungal racks, something leafy and dark green that she had never seen growing anywhere on Kettle's Reach's poisoned surface. Water ran somewhere, a sound she hadn't heard outside the station's recyclers in nine years, clean and constant.

And there were people.

They stood at the edges of the chamber, maybe thirty of them where she first saw them, and more appearing along the walkways above, drawn by whatever signal her entry had sent through their walls. Adults, elders, children—children, actual children, a girl of perhaps six peering down between the rail posts with an expression Lena recognized from station mess halls, the frank unguarded stare of the young assessing a stranger. They wore clothing woven from fibers she couldn't name, dyed with salt-mineral colors, whites and ochres and a deep iron red. None of them spoke. The silence was total and deliberate and it was not empty; it was the silence of a room holding its breath.

Lena stood very still in the center of the floor with her hands visible at her sides, the way you stand before animals you don't want to startle, except these were not animals, these were people, these were—

She did the arithmetic before she could stop herself, the census reflex, the habit of nine years. Thirty visible. Chamber capacity, judging by the walkways, the garden terraces, the sleeping alcoves she could see opening off the upper galleries: two hundred. Two hundred souls. Forty-one at the station, and two hundred here, and the number arrived in her mind with a physical weight, like a stone set down on her chest.

Forty-two, she thought, absurdly, counting herself, and then corrected: two hundred and forty-two. The colony was three times the size she had spent nine years believing it was. The census she conducted every year, the tender dwindling ledger of Idris and Sefa and the thirty-nine others—that ledger had been wrong, incomplete, a count of the visible world only, and the invisible world had been underneath her feet the entire time, singing.

An older man descended the walkway. He moved carefully, favoring his left leg, and he stopped four meters from her and looked at her for a long time—at her face, at her station-issue coat, at her hands. Then he did something that undid her completely.

He spoke. Aloud. In accented, archaic, perfectly comprehensible Standard.

"You're the listener," he said. "From the metal house on the hill."

His voice was rough with disuse, a voice used mostly for singing, she guessed, or for the harmonic speech, a voice kept like a tool wrapped in oil cloth. But the words were clear. The words were *clear*, and that meant they had studied the surface transmissions, the old fleet bands, the loop—meant they had maintained Standard for two centuries the way you maintain a weapon you hope never to fire.

"Yes," Lena said. Her own voice sounded enormous and crude in the resonant chamber. "I'm Lena Nystrom. I'm—I listen. That's my work. That's all I've ever done here."

"We know." He glanced upward, and a woman on the third walkway made a small gesture with both hands, and the golden veins along the nearest pillar pulsed through a sequence Lena's ear caught and her mind translated almost before she willed it: *she heard the door-song. She answered it correctly. She came alone.*

"They've been arguing about you," the man said, and there was something almost like humor in it, dry and tired. "For three years. Since your instruments started hearing us properly. Some wanted to seal the upper passages. Some wanted—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. "My name is Tomas Ashfall. My family has lived under these flats for six generations. We came down when the ships burned."

"The ships didn't burn," Lena said quietly. "I've read the records. The fleet vanished. Earth declared it lost with all hands."

Tomas Ashfall looked at her with an expression she would spend a long time afterward trying to name—grief and fury and a terrible patience braided together, the expression of a man who has rehearsed a correction for two hundred years and finally has someone to say it to.

"That is what Earth declared," he said. "Yes. Earth is very good at declaring things." He turned his head and spoke then in the harmonic language, a short phrase, and the chamber answered him: the light-veins rippled outward from his position like a stone dropped in still water, and along the walkways, people sat down. All of them, everywhere, in a single motion, a congregation settling. Even the girl on the third tier folded herself cross-legged beside the railing, eyes never leaving Lena.

"Sit," Tomas said, gesturing to a bench of fused salt near the central pillar. "You walked far, and what I have to tell you will make your legs unhappy."

Lena sat. The bench was warm.

Above them, the layered inscriptions caught the pulse-light, and she read them now without meaning to, the way she read everything now, compulsively, helplessly—generations of names, she realized. Generations of names and dates and phrases of mourning and phrases of defiance, the whole archive of a hidden people written on the walls of their refuge in the language of the planet that had sheltered them. *We are still here,* the oldest layer said, in handwriting older than the station, older than the Corps, *we are still here, we are still here,* repeated like a heartbeat, like a prayer, like a fact asserted against two centuries of official erasure.

"Two hundred and six," Tomas said, following her gaze. "That's the count this season. We lose more than we gain, now. We have for a while." He lowered himself onto the bench beside her with a sound like rope creaking. "You conduct a census. Up there. Once a year—we can feel your transmitter pulses, the scheduling pings. Forty-one." He said the number gently. "You count your people because no one else will. We understand that better than you could know."

"Who counts you?" Lena asked.

"I do." He said it simply. "I am the counter. And I am going to tell you why we came down, and why we sent you the beacon, and when I am finished you are going to understand that you cannot carry this back up the hill. Not to your engineer. Not to your records. Not to anyone." His eyes were kind and absolutely unyielding. "Because Earth did not lose us, Listener. Earth *hunted* us. And the order to hunt us has never been rescinded. It is still executing. It has been executing, patiently, automatically, for two hundred years—and there is a machine in orbit right now, asleep, waiting for anyone to confirm we exist."

Far above, beyond the salt, beyond the rock, beyond the atmosphere, the cutter *Vantage* held its silent station, and neither of them said its name, because neither of them yet knew it.

But Lena felt the shape of it arrive anyway—the weight of the secret settling onto her shoulders like the flats' cold, total and inescapable—and somewhere in the golden light of the chamber, a child began, softly, to sing.
