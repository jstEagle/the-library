## 18. Grammar of Forgery

The salt flats at dusk were the color of an old scar, and Lena walked them the way she had once walked the corridors of the Linguistics Corps academy on Thessaly—slowly, as if the ground might be examined later and she wanted to leave nothing incriminating in her footprints.

Below her feet, two hundred meters down through basalt and brine aquifer, Yeva Ashfall was waiting for an answer. Above her head, in a decaying orbit that no longer deserved the word *orbit*, the Vantage ran its final sweep with the patience of something that had never once been impatient because it had never once been alive. And between those two pressures, in the thin habitable layer where forty-one people ate dinner and pretended tomorrow was ordinary, Lena Nystrom was trying to build a lie the way you build anything worth trusting: from the foundation up, out of true materials.

She stopped where the flats began their long shimmer toward the horizon and spoke aloud—not in English, not in the station's pidgin of supply manifests and weather complaints, but in the harmonic language, humming it low into her own chest so the sound traveled down through her boots and into the stone. *I need to see you. I think I know how to end this.*

The planet took eleven minutes to answer. It always did; the phrase had to travel through the deep conductive strata before the cave mouths repeated it, and Yeva had taught her to read the delay itself as punctuation. When the reply came it rose through the soles of her feet first, then her knees, then settled somewhere behind her sternum like a second heartbeat: *Come. Bring your grammar.*

Lena almost smiled. Almost. The Vantage's survey drones passed over the flats on a ninety-minute cycle now, and she had learned to fold herself into the shadow of the old brine-crystallizer ruins until they went by, their sensor masts sweeping the white ground with light that made no shadows at all. She crouched there while one passed, close enough to hear the faint ion whine of its lifters, and thought about how strange it was that she could feel nostalgia for a machine that wanted her dead. It moved the way the Corps' archive drones had moved, back when she was twenty-three and certain that language was a cathedral and she had been called to sweep its floors.

* * *

The caves received her the way they always did: by taking her name away.

There was no door anymore, in her mind. There was only the threshold where human sound stopped mattering—the point past which her voice became just another vibration among vibrations, no more privileged than water dripping or stone settling. She descended the switchback cut into the salt dome's flank, past the first and second airlock doors that Idris had spent three weeks teaching himself to service without manuals, and came at last into the long gallery the Ashfall called, in their speech-that-was-not-speech, the place where the mountain listens politely.

Two hundred people lived down here, and the first thing you noticed was the quiet—but it was the wrong kind of quiet, she'd learned. It was full. Every surface hummed at frequencies the human ear caught only as pressure, as mood, as the sense—so disorienting the first week—that someone was standing just behind you saying something important. The walls carried conversation the way still water carries light. Children ran past her trailing harmonics of laughter that echoed off the stone in colors. An old man sat repairing a water reclaimer by touch, his hands moving to a rhythm Lena recognized as a lullaby, because he was singing it to the machine.

Yeva was waiting in the map gallery, where the ceiling rose into a vault of flowstone and the community's whole history hung in grooves cut into the rock—a library you read with your palms. She was a small woman, silver-haired, with the deep stillness of someone who had spent seventy years being careful. When Lena entered, Yeva didn't turn. She raised one hand flat against the wall and let the stone speak for her.

*You walked badly today,* the wall said, translated through Lena's body as a sequence of pressures against her palm where she'd instinctively mirrored the gesture. *Your grief is loud. Sit.*

"I'm not grieving yet," Lena said aloud, and then, embarrassed by the crudeness of it, pressed her hand to the stone and gave it properly: *Not yet. But I've found the shape of what it will cost.*

Yeva turned then. Her eyes were pale, nearly colorless, adapted to a darkness that wasn't dark to her at all. "Say it in your tongue if you must," she said, in accented but fluent Meridian Standard—the Ashfall kept human speech alive the way station families kept heirlooms, polished and rarely used. "But say all of it. You have been carrying half-words down my stairs for six days."

So Lena laid it out, there in the listening vault, and heard for the first time how it sounded spoken plainly:

"The Vantage runs on protocol. Protocol is just language—frozen language, grammar hardened into law. It has one question: *has the unregistered colony surrendered?* If the answer comes in a registered language, in a form its linguistics module can validate, the sterilization order suspends. Pending verification. Indefinitely, if the verification holds." She paused. "Ashfall isn't registered. Hasn't been for two hundred years. Earth struck it from the catalog when it struck your fleet from the sky."

"Then we cannot surrender," Yeva said. "In any language it will hear."

"No," Lena said. "But I can register it."

The silence that followed was the kind the caves specialized in—total, attentive, heavy as water. Somewhere far above, muffled through two hundred meters of stone, a drone passed, and every person in the gallery felt it pass through their teeth.

"You carry Corps credentials," Yeva said at last. Not a question. They had never discussed it directly, but Yeva read people the way she read walls, and Lena had long ago stopped trying to hide anything structural.

"Linguist, third class, retired-in-absentia. My credentials are dormant, not dead. The registration protocol doesn't require active status—it requires a qualified cataloger, physically present with a living language community, submitting a complete linguistic profile." She swallowed. "Name, location, population, phonology, syntax, the whole architecture. Everything true. Filed under my signature."

"And when Earth reads it," Yeva said softly, "they will read your name beside ours. They will know exactly who opened the door."

"Yes."

"They will call it treason."

"The Corps calls it *unauthorized disclosure of restricted linguistic material*," Lena said, and heard her mentor's voice underneath her own so clearly she had to stop for a moment. "It carries the same sentence either way."

Yeva studied her for a long time. Then she did something Lena had never seen her do: she reached out and took Lena's hand, and pressed it flat against the flowstone wall, over a groove worn smooth by generations of palms.

"Feel this," she said. "This is my grandmother's mark. She was nine years old when the fleet went into the stone. She cut her first word here when she was twelve, and it says: *we are not hiding, we are waiting.* Two hundred years of waiting, Lena Nystrom. Do not offer me a plan that ends with my people traded for yours. Offer me one that ends with both of us still here."

"That's the only kind I'm offering," Lena said. "That's why it has to be me. That's why it has to be my voice."

* * *

Because that was the part she hadn't said yet, the part that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and she made herself say it now, walking Yeva slowly along the gallery while the walls listened:

"A forgery isn't a copy. Anyone can copy words. The Vantage's module won't parse words—it parses *signature*. Every speaker leaves a fingerprint in how they use a language: stress patterns, hesitation profiles, the way error clusters around emotional content. The module has been listening to this planet for decades. It has my transmissions. It knows how I speak—how I *mean*. A surrender broadcast in perfect Ashfall coming from an unknown source would flag as synthetic inside a minute. Coming from a source whose signature matches the station's resident linguist—" she exhaled, "—it becomes credible. Tragic, even. A colony breaking, its last educated voice pleading terms. Machines believe stories shaped like grief."

"So you will speak it."

"I have to speak it. Not perform it—speak it. If there's one seam, one place where I'm reciting instead of meaning, the module will find it. My mentor used to say the only unbreakable forgery is one the forger believes." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Which means I can't write a lie. I have to write something true that is also false. A surrender that means everything I actually feel—every word sincere—and adds up to a thing that never happened."

They had reached the gallery's end, where a natural window of clear calcite looked out—or rather *down*, since the cave curved—onto the underground lake, black and perfectly still, holding the ceiling's reflection like a second sky. Lena watched the inverted stalactites and felt the vertigo of it: a world that had learned to live upside down for two centuries.

"And after," Yeva said quietly. "After the broadcast. What remains of the woman who spoke it?"

Lena had asked herself that question every night for a week, lying in her bunk in the station's upper hab while the ventilation system breathed its patient mechanical breath. She had answered it differently each night. Tonight, down here, she found the honest version.

"My linguistic signature is the weapon and the wound. Once I fire it through an unauthorized channel, attached to a forged surrender, it's burned. Every Corps archive will carry the record: *Nystrom, L., traitor, method: language.* I'll never teach again. Never publish. Never stand in a hall of people who love grammar and be one of them." She touched the cold stone. "And there's a worse part. To make it believable, I have to mean the surrender. I have to grieve it honestly. And grief like that—you don't get to take it back afterward. Whatever I become on the other side of that broadcast, that's who I am now."

Yeva was silent for a long moment. The lake made no sound at all, which Lena had learned was itself a kind of statement—the water holding its breath.

"When I was young," Yeva said finally, "there was a man who wanted to give us up. Not to harm us—he believed surfacing was survival, that two hundred years of stone was enough. He composed a message to the sky in our own language, beautiful, flawless. We exiled him to the far galleries." Her pale eyes found Lena's. "He died alone. And the terrible thing, the thing I have never told my people, is that his message was *right*. It would have worked. The sky was empty for thirty years. He saw clearly and we punished him for it." She paused. "I am telling you this so you know I understand what I am asking. I am asking you to be right in a way that costs everything, and I am telling you that I know the price, because I once charged it to someone else."

Lena looked at the old woman—at the exile who had become a keeper of exiles—and felt something shift in her chest, some load redistributing onto deeper foundations.

"Then help me make it true," she said. "Teach me the grammar of surrender in your language. Not the words—the *shape*. Where grief sits in a sentence. How your people say 'enough.' If I'm going to burn my voice on this, it burns speaking you correctly."

* * *

They worked for six hours in the map gallery, and it was the hardest linguistic work of Lena's life, harder than the Corps exams, harder than nine years of decoding stone, because the material was herself.

Ashfall surrender, it turned out, was not a single utterance but a structure—a formal architecture with the elegance of everything the fleet had built during two centuries of enforced patience. It had movements, like music. First the *unmaking of the wall*: a declaration that the defenses of the hidden ones were lowered, phrased in the harmonic language as a physical gesture rendered in tone, the sound of a hand opening. Then the *naming of the children*: an accounting of the vulnerable, offered to the victor's mercy, which in Ashfall practice was less plea than dare—an implicit accusation that made mercy the only honorable path. Then the *transfer of the word*: the surrendering party yielded not land or weapons but the right to speak for them, placing their future in another's grammar. And last, always last, the *long vowel*: a sustained tone that had no lexical content at all, that simply meant *we remain*, held for as long as the speaker's breath allowed, because in a language built for people who survive by endurance, the length of the note was the length of the promise.

"It cannot be short," Yeva said. "A short surrender is a lie. Your machines will measure the breath."

"Then I'll train for it," Lena said. "I used to hold forty seconds as a girl. I'll hold sixty."

"You will hold eighty," Yeva said, with the mild tyranny of a teacher, "or you will not speak at all."

Later—much later, when the work had gone from agony to something like prayer—Lena sat alone at the gallery's edge with her slate, drafting the text she would speak, and discovered the trap Yeva had promised her.

Every line came out true.

*I lower the wall* — and she meant it; she was so tired of walls, of doors and airlocks and encrypted logs and the weight of a secret pressing on her ribs at every meal. *These are our children* — Sefa's face rose unbidden, the girl eating dinner three nights ago, glancing up at her with eyes too quick, and Lena meant that too, meant it with a ferocity that blurred the slate. *Take our word and keep it* — she had already given her word away, to Yeva, to Idris, to forty-one people who didn't know yet what they were protecting. And the long vowel — *we remain* — God, she had been saying nothing else for nine years, to a planet, to a dead loop, to a mother whose voice she could no longer remember.

She stared at the draft and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the forgery required no invention at all. It required only arrangement. The truth was all there, every piece of it sincere; the lie lived entirely in the frame—in the claim, implicit in the broadcast, that the surrender came from the Ashfall people as a whole, ratified, when in fact it came from one exhausted woman borrowing their grief because no one else could reach the microphone.

One lie. One seam. Everything else true.

She thought of her mentor then—Ovid Asare, third-class chair of applied xenolinguistics, erased from the Corps rolls for forging a ceasefire transcript on Meridian's sister colony, a forgery that had saved four hundred lives and cost him his name. She had been twenty-six, brilliant and loyal, and she had testified at his hearing. She had said the words *a translator's first duty is fidelity to the source*, and watched an old man's face accept them like a diagnosis.

*I'm sorry,* she wrote on the slate, in a private cipher, a message to a man who might be dead, addressed to an institution that had deleted him. *You were right about everything except the ending. The ending can be survived.*

She saved it to the encrypted log, next to anomaly 400, next to the truth-envelope she was building syllable by syllable, and closed the slate.

Above her, through two hundred meters of rock, the Vantage completed another circuit of its dying orbit, patient as grammar. Below her, two hundred people slept in the warm hum of the stone that had learned their names. And between them, in the narrow band of light and air where she had chosen to live, Lena Nystrom began, very quietly, to rehearse the opening of the wall—humming it into her cupped hands, feeling for the place where sincerity ended and strategy began, and finding, with a relief that frightened her more than the drones ever had, that she could no longer locate the border at all.

She practiced until her breath gave out. Then she breathed, and practiced again.

Eighty seconds. She would hold eighty.
