## 20. Telemetry War

Idris had spent thirty years learning to hear machines the way other people heard weather.

The station talked to him constantly—not in words, but in the grammar of vibration and heat. The water reclamation unit sang a half-tone sharp when its bearings were thinking about failing. The solar array's tracking motors stuttered in their sleep like dogs dreaming of running. Even the deep-cold silence of the reactor shielding had a texture to it, a pressure against the ear that meant *everything is fine, go back to bed*. He'd built his life inside that language because it never lied to him. Machines didn't have the imagination for it.

Which was why, at 0347 station time, lying flat on his back beneath the comms array with a soldering iron cooling in his hand, he knew immediately that something was wrong. Not with the array. With the sky.

The Vantage had changed its orbit.

He felt it before he confirmed it—a subtle shift in the interference pattern bleeding through the array's ground plane, the way the cutter's telemetry carrier now clipped the horizon at a different angle on each pass. He'd mapped that carrier the way he'd once mapped his mother's garden: by heart, by repetition, by love of a kind. For six days since the drones launched he had lived inside its rhythm, and rhythms did not lie either.

The survey pattern had been methodical, patient, a grid drawn over the salt flats and the lowlands and the shattered coast where the original colony domes had rusted into the dunes. North to south, east to west, the patience of a machine that had all the time in the world and no reason to hurry.

This pass was not on the grid.

Idris slid out from under the array, sat up in the dark, and let the cold of the maintenance bay sink through his coveralls while he thought. Above him, through three decks of plating and insulation, the stars were doing what they always did—burning indifferently over Kettle's Reach—and somewhere up there among them, a two-hundred-year-old machine was executing an order written by people who had been dead for generations, and that order said: *find them*.

He thought about Lena's face when she'd finally told him everything. Not the confession itself—he'd known something was coming, had been carrying the shape of it for weeks like a stone in his boot—but the moment after, when she'd finished and stood there braced as if for a blow, and he'd said, "Okay. What do you need me to build?"

She had looked at him then the way people look at a door they'd assumed was painted onto a wall.

What she needed him to build, it turned out, was a lie made of light. And now the lie was about to be tested.

* * *

The war room—if you could call it that—was the old hydroponics office, four meters square, smelling faintly of nutrient salts and the ghost of tomatoes nobody had grown in a decade. Lena had the long-range feed up on the wall display, the Vantage's telemetry stream rendered as Idris liked it: raw, unfiltered, a waterfall of numbers falling too fast to read but slow enough to feel.

"There." She tapped the screen. "Drone seven. It broke formation eleven minutes ago."

"Where's it going?"

Lena didn't answer, which was an answer. Yeva stood in the corner, half in shadow, her stillness so complete she might have been part of the wall. She had come up through the maintenance tunnels for this—the first time in her life, she'd said, that she had climbed toward the surface instead of away from it—and she watched the screen with an expression Idris couldn't parse, something between hunger and dread, the way you might watch weather that could be rain or could be fire.

"It's heading for the flats," Lena said finally. "Southeast quadrant."

The salt flats. The caves. Two hundred people asleep under forty meters of limestone, breathing through vents disguised as geothermal seeps, hidden so well and for so long that they had become, in every way that mattered to the universe above, geological features.

"How long?" Idris asked.

"At its current speed? Ninety minutes to overflight. Maybe less if it accelerates."

Idris looked at the waterfall of numbers. Somewhere in that stream was the drone's eye—its spectral imager, its ground-penetrating lidar, its little machine heart faithfully reporting everything it saw back to the cutter that had birthed it. And somewhere behind the cutter, folded into protocols older than anyone alive, was the sentence that would end everything if the eye saw what it was built to see: *unregistered habitation confirmed; initiate sterilization sequence*.

"Can we shoot it down?" Yeva asked. Her voice was low, resonant, the harmonic undertone that Idris was still learning to hear properly—speech with architecture in it.

"No," Lena said. "A lost drone triggers investigation. Investigation means more drones, better drones, drones that don't trust us."

"So we let it look."

"We let it look," Idris said, "at what we want it to see." He was already pulling his kit together, hands moving on their own knowledge. "Its telemetry comes home on a fixed carrier. If I can get between the drone and the Vantage—if I can put our transmitter in the path and hold phase lock—I can rewrite what arrives. The drone keeps flying. The Vantage keeps listening. Nobody up there ever has to know the picture changed in transit."

Yeva frowned. "You can do this?"

"I can spoof a telemetry feed," Idris said. "I've been doing it for six days. Every reading those drones have sent home, I've touched first. Salt flats read as salt flats. Caves read as—" he shrugged— "caves, if you squint. Limestone reads as limestone. It's been easy because there was nothing to hide. Tonight there's something to hide, and hiding things is harder than showing them."

"How much harder?"

He thought about how to explain it. "Imagine someone asks you what your house looks like, and the truth is fine, so you just... open the door. Now imagine they ask while standing in your kitchen, and there's a body on the floor. You don't just describe a different room. You have to build the room, live, while they watch you build it, and every beam and nail has to match the blueprints of a house that doesn't exist, and if one measurement is off by a centimeter they'll know you're lying, because machines are very good at noticing when the world doesn't add up."

Silence in the little room. On the screen, the waterfall fell.

"Ninety minutes," Lena said quietly. "Idris. Can you?"

He looked at her—this woman who had spent nine years teaching herself to speak for a planet, who was asking him now to spend ninety minutes speaking *as* the planet, to lie in a machine's voice better than the machine could—and he thought about the false all-clear, twenty-two years ago, the transmission he'd sent from the Meridian relay because the alternative was admitting the relay had failed and watching the mining crews sit out a storm on the surface with no shelter call. Three names. He still said them sometimes, in the dark, like a man touching scars to check they're real. Oyelaran. Vashti. Brandt.

He didn't know if tonight was penance or repetition. He suspected he'd never know. But he knew which direction his hands wanted to move.

"Yeah," he said. "Get me power and get me quiet. I'm going to need both."

* * *

The transmitter shed sat at the edge of the station's shadow, a corrugated box that had been patched so many times it was less a building than an argument between decades. Inside, Idris killed every light except the amber standby glow and woke the big dish's pointing motors, feeling them come alive under his palms like animals scenting wind.

Phase lock was the whole game. The drone broadcast on a tight-beam carrier aimed at the Vantage, and the Vantage listened with antennas tuned to expect a signal arriving from exactly that bearing, with exactly that Doppler drift, wearing exactly that timing signature. To intercept, Idris had to place his own transmission precisely in the drone's shadow—close enough in frequency and phase that the cutter's receiver would blend the two without complaint, strong enough that his version would dominate, clean enough that the seam would never show.

It was like threading a needle threaded through another needle, in the dark, while both needles moved.

He found the carrier at 0412. Locked. Held. The drone's voice poured through his equipment—telemetry frames, housekeeping data, and beneath it all, compressed and chunked, the image stream. He let it flow untouched for sixty seconds, learning its cadence, counting its frame intervals, mapping the checksums the way Lena mapped phonemes.

Then he opened the image channel and looked at what the drone was looking at.

Salt. White salt under starlight, rendered in the drone's spectral palette as a wash of thermal blues and reflective grays. Beautiful, actually. The flats at night had a lunar calm to them, and for one absurd moment Idris thought about how Sefa would love this view, how the girl collected images of the planet the way other children collected stones.

Then the drone's forward lidar pulsed, and the return came back, and there—under two meters of salt crust and forty meters of rock, faint as a heartbeat under a pillow—was a void. A cavity. A door-shaped absence in the world.

The drone hadn't flagged it yet. Its classification stack was still chewing: *subsurface anomaly, density discontinuity, probability natural cave formation 0.71.* Seventy-one percent. The number hung in Idris's vision like a held breath. Natural caves were common on Kettle's Reach; the planet was honeycombed with them. Seventy-one percent wasn't proof of anything.

But the drone was flying toward the anomaly, and with every pulse of that lidar it would gather more certainty, and certainty was a thing that only moved in one direction.

"Okay," Idris said aloud, to no one, to the machines, to himself. "Let's build a house."

* * *

Here is what lying to a machine requires, and why it is worse than lying to a person:

A person wants a story. Give them a story shaped right and they will pour themselves into it, grateful, because stories are easier than the truth. A machine wants arithmetic. Every photon the drone reports must balance against every photon physics says should be there. Every temperature gradient must obey thermodynamics. Every echo must arrive at the speed sound travels through the material you claim it traveled through. You cannot charm a checksum. You cannot improvise your way past conservation of energy.

But you can, if you are fast enough and know the terrain well enough, edit the world in transit.

Idris worked in layers. First the lidar returns: he caught each pulse-echo pair, ran them through a model of pure salt and solid limestone—no cavity, no door—and spliced the modeled returns into the stream in place of the real ones, timed to the millisecond, attenuated to match the drone's own calibration constants. Then the spectral imager: the cave vents breathed heat, a faint thermal bloom on the surface that a trained eye would read as *habitation*, so he sampled the surrounding salt's thermal profile and painted it over the blooms, frame by frame, keeping the diurnal cooling curve honest because the Vantage's analytics would check the curve against local time and he had learned, these past six days, that the cutter checked *everything*.

His hands hurt. He hadn't noticed when they'd started.

Frame. Model. Splice. Verify. Frame. Model. Splice. Verify. The loop consumed him. Time went strange the way it did in the last hour of a difficult repair—clockless, depthless, the self dissolving into the task until there was no Idris, only the work, only the waterfall of numbers and the small fierce joy of each second the forgery held. This was the part of engineering he had never told anyone he loved: the moments when the machine pushed back and he pushed back and the boundary between his mind and the circuitry went thin as gauze. It was the closest thing to prayer he had.

At 0531, the drone passed directly over the door.

He knew the moment it happened because the lidar pulse rate doubled—the drone's onboard logic had escalated to high-resolution scan mode, hungry, thorough—and the real returns underneath his forged ones lit up with the geometry of the cave mouth in loving detail: the machined edges, the counterweighted slab, the wear patterns of two centuries of feet. Someone had built that door to last. Someone had built it beautifully. And for eleven seconds it existed, naked, in the raw signal flowing through Idris's hands.

Eleven seconds. He ate them. Modeled them away. Replaced stone with stone, absence with presence, history with nothing at all. His soldering iron tip had gone cold in its stand sometime in the last hour and he didn't remember using it. Sweat ran into his eyes and he let it, because moving his hands to wipe it meant dropping the splice for half a second and half a second was a canyon.

The drone passed. The scan rate dropped. The classification stack, fed a steady diet of seamless salt, revised its estimate downward: *probability natural cave formation 0.83.*

Higher. Because now the model matched perfectly, and perfect salt was even more boring than imperfect salt. The drone was losing interest. The drone was deciding, in its small obedient way, that there was nothing here worth dying for, nothing here at all, just geology doing geology things on a forgotten world at the edge of everything.

Idris started laughing. It came out of him sideways, cracked, half sob, and he clamped it shut because laughing shook his shoulders and shaking shoulders shook the phase lock. But God, the relief—it went through him like current. They'd done it. He'd done it. The house was built and the machine had walked through every room and found nothing but furniture.

He held the lock another twenty minutes, feeding the drone's routine departure telemetry home clean, and then the drone turned back toward its grid and its carrier began to fade toward the horizon, and Idris Okonkwo sat alone in a dark shed on a dying colony and let himself believe, for almost a full minute, that they had won.

That was when the Vantage sent a message.

* * *

It arrived on the command band—the band Earth used, the band that had carried nothing but the forty-second loop for longer than Sefa had been alive. Idris stared at the incoming header for a long moment before he decrypted it, because headers told you things, and this header told him the message was addressed not to the colony, not to any registered station, but to *Earth Command Relay, Sector Nine, priority routing*.

The cutter wasn't talking to them. The cutter was talking home.

He decoded it with hands that had gone very steady, the way hands do when the adrenaline burns off and leaves only procedure.

*FLAGGED ANOMALY, SURVEY CYCLE 6, DRONE 7. TELEMETRY INTEGRITY NOMINAL. SOURCE DATA IRREGULARITY: LIDAR RETURN COHERENCE EXCEEDS MODEL EXPECTATION BY 0.4 PERCENT ACROSS SECTOR 12 OVERFLIGHT. PROBABILITY OF SENSOR DEGRADATION: LOW. PROBABILITY OF ENVIRONMENTAL EXPLANATION: MODERATE. REQUESTING INSTRUCTION. STANDING BY.*

He read it three times.

Coherence exceeding expectation. Of course. Of course, of course, of course. His forged returns had been too clean—modeled salt smoother than real salt, real salt full of grit and impurity and the honest mess of an actual world. He had built the house too well. No house was that well built. A 0.4 percent irregularity, invisible to anything but a machine comparing two centuries of baseline data against itself, and the machine had noticed, because machines notice, because noticing was the only thing some machines were for.

And now the question that had hung over Kettle's Reach for six days had left the atmosphere. It was crawling through the dark at lightspeed toward Earth, toward whatever remained of Earth's authority over this sector, toward offices that might be empty or might not, toward a decision that would come back someday as either *anomaly accepted, continue survey* or as a single word that Idris had read in historical files and never forgotten, because it was the blandest horror he had ever encountered, a word designed by bureaucrats to make genocide sound like maintenance:

*Proceed.*

He sat in the amber dark for a while. Outside, the sky was going gray at the eastern edge, dawn coming up over the salt flats the way it did every morning, indifferent and enormous, gilding the crust of a world that had no idea how close it had come tonight to being erased—or maybe it did know, maybe the planet knew everything, maybe that was what Lena heard in the seismic song, the long patient hum of a world waiting to see what its guests would do.

He thought about waking Lena. She was below, in the caves, sleeping among the Ashfall for the first time, learning their language with her skin. She deserved the sleep. She'd earned it.

He thought about the message, and the lightspeed gap, and what the distance meant. Earth was years away by transmission—no, months, the relay chain had shortened it, but still months, months of the Vantage sitting overhead with a flagged file and standing orders and a sterilization protocol that needed no one's permission to execute, only everyone's permission to stop.

Standing by. That was the phrase that mattered. The cutter was standing by. Which meant the countdown hadn't started yet.

Which meant the countdown was waiting for permission.

Idris saved the intercept, encrypted it twice, and keyed the station's internal line. Lena answered on the fourth chime, voice thick with sleep, and before he could decide how to say it gently, he heard himself say it plainly, the way you say things when the hour is too late for kindness:

"Lena. It worked. The drone saw nothing." A breath. "But the Vantage didn't believe the nothing. It's asking Earth what to do. We've got however long it takes a message to crawl home and an answer to crawl back—and after that, I think, we're out of road."

Silence on the line. Long enough that he checked the connection. Then Lena's voice came back, and the sleep was gone from it entirely, burned away like fog in wind, and what was underneath was something he'd never heard from her before: not fear, not grief, but a terrible clarity, the sound of a woman who had already walked past the point where fear was useful.

"Then we don't wait for the answer," she said. "Wake Sefa. Get Yeva on the line. Idris—we're done defending. It's time to build the surrender."

The line closed. In the transmitter shed, the amber standby light pulsed softly, patiently, like a slow heartbeat, and outside the dawn came up white over the salt, and somewhere far above, a machine waited in the dark for a word from a world that might be too dead to send one.
