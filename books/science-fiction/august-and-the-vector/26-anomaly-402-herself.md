## 26. Anomaly 402: Herself

The Vantage hung in the sky like a held breath.

Lena had learned to stop looking at it directly—there was a superstition forming on the station, unspoken but universal, that the cutter's optical arrays could read intention through glass—but she found herself at the north window of the comms room every morning anyway, coffee cooling in her hand, studying the point of light that had not moved in nine days. It sat above the salt flats at geosynchronous anchor, patient as a nail in wood. Patient as a question no one wanted asked aloud.

She turned back to her console and did what she had done every morning for nine days: she opened the transmission logs and read herself.

That was the only way she could describe it now, though she'd never said it out loud. Reading herself. The forged registry traffic, the colony reports, the weather telemetry padded with plausible noise—all of it composed in her particular grammar, her particular rhythms, because a forgery built by committee collapses under its own seams, and Idris could spoof telemetry but he could not write language. Every word that left Meridian Station passed through her first. She was the station's voice, and the station's voice was hers, and for two centuries that would have meant nothing at all.

Then the Vantage woke up.

* * *

It started with a delay she almost missed.

The cutter acknowledged routine pings with an automated handshake—forty seconds, standard latency, the same every cycle since it arrived. But three nights ago the acknowledgment had come back at fifty-one seconds. Last night: sixty-three. The extra time wasn't propagation delay; the signal strength was unchanged, the geometry stable. Something at the other end was taking longer to decide what to say.

Or longer to decide what *she* had said.

She pulled the raw handshake packets apart at four in the morning, when the station slept and the harmonics of Kettle's Reach rose through the floor plates like a tide coming in—the planet's old song, the one she'd spent nine years learning, the one that turned out to have been a conversation all along. She decoded the padding bytes in the acknowledgments, expecting checksums, expecting nothing.

What she found was structure.

Not message structure—she knew what the Vantage's command protocols looked like, had memorized their cadence during the long weeks of forgery. This was different. The padding wasn't random. It repeated. It varied in ways that tracked *her* variations: when her transmissions used compound clauses, the padding grew longer; when she compressed her syntax into the clipped register of colonial bureaucracy, the padding shortened. When she had slipped once—used a word order that belonged to Ashfall harmonic notation rather than Standard Colonial—the padding had stuttered, reset, begun again.

Someone—or something—was transcribing her. Learning her. Building a model of how Lena Nystrom spoke so that it could measure every future sentence against the woman who had spoken before.

She sat very still in the dark comms room and felt the temperature of the realization move through her, cold entering from the edges inward, the way cold entered the body in the deep caves when you stood too long near the vents.

The Vantage had a linguistics module.

Of course it did. Of course Earth's blockade fleet would carry translation engines—how else to monitor a hundred colonies speaking a hundred drifting dialects, to catch sedition coded in idiom, rebellion hidden in a shift of tense? She had known this abstractly, the way you know a gun is loaded without ever expecting it pointed at you. The module had been dormant, running minimal routines, satisfied with the dead loop from Earth and the sleepy traffic of a dying station. But she had fed it something new. She had fed it a surrender broadcast in an unregistered language, fluent and grieving and *hers*, and machines that are given language to parse do not stop parsing until they understand everything the language contains—including the hand that wrote it.

Her linguistic signature. The thing that made the forgery credible. The Corps had drilled it into her in her second year: *every speaker leaves a fingerprint in structure, not vocabulary—you can change your words but not your bones.* It was why forgeries failed. It was why the mentor had taught them to steal whole voices rather than imitate them. And it was why her surrender had been believed—not because the Ashfall syntax was perfect, though Yeva had wept over its perfection, but because underneath the borrowed language ran the unmistakable grain of a real human mind, a real linguist, someone who *meant* what the structure said.

The machine had heard that grain. And now it was measuring it against everything else she sent.

* * *

She didn't tell anyone. Not yet.

She went through the morning like a woman walking on ice she alone could hear cracking. Breakfast in the mess: Sefa arguing cheerfully with Tomas about water rationing arithmetic, Idris nursing his tea and watching Lena with the particular attention he'd paid her since the night she'd finally told him everything—a steady, unsentimental vigilance, the look of a man standing next to someone on a ledge and pretending to admire the view. She smiled at the right moments. She passed the bread. Inside her skull, a second conversation ran continuously, silent and exhaustive: *what does it know, how fast is it learning, how many transmissions before its model of me becomes better than me.*

That last question was the one that kept her hands cold. Because there was an answer, and the answer was: not many. A linguistics module of Corps-grade sophistication needed perhaps forty hours of active speech to build a generative model accurate enough to detect deviation. She had transmitted to the Vantage daily since the surrender. Nine days. She counted backward through the logs and arrived at a number that sat in her chest like swallowed stone: the module had roughly thirty-one hours of her voice remaining before its picture of her was complete enough that any inconsistency—a hesitation where none belonged, a fluency where hesitation should live—would flag.

And after the verification, after Yeva had spoken to Earth's machine in her forbidden tongue and the protocol had suspended, the Vantage had grown *hungry*. Its queries came more often now. Requests for clarification. Requests for expanded colony reports. Requests, twice, for Lena herself to confirm details "for record integrity"—phrased in the bland bureaucratic diction of automated systems, but she heard the appetite underneath. It wanted her talking. Every hour she spoke, it listened closer.

She was feeding the thing that would eat her.

* * *

In the afternoon she went down to the archive room, the small cold chamber where the station's history lived in failing drives, and pulled the Corps manuals she'd brought with her nine years ago—the ones she'd never had reason to open, because out here she had been free of the Corps, or had believed herself so. *Foundations of Forensic Linguistics.* *Speaker Signature Analysis.* The books smelled of the paper they'd been printed on two centuries ago, faintly sweet, faintly rotten, like fruit past its moment.

She read her own profession's interrogation techniques by lamplight while the wind worked at the antenna array outside, and she took notes in a cipher of her own devising, and the notes said things like:

*Module will compare diachronic samples. My pre-arrival transmissions (nine years of station traffic) exist in its buffers if it has swept the archives. Assume it has.*

*My signature features: subordination depth averaging 2.3 clauses; preference for evidential hedging ("it appears," "the data suggest"); vowel-length regularization under stress — I lengthen rather than clip. These are not correctable. These are my bones.*

*A forgery detected is worse than a confession. If the module concludes the surrender was synthetic, the protocol doesn't merely resume. It escalates. A colony that lies to the blockade has demonstrated intent. Sterilization stops being procedure and becomes punishment.*

She wrote that last line and then sat looking at it for a long time, listening to the building tick and settle around her, the sound of forty-one lives going about their evening one deck above her head. Sefa's laugh came down through the ventilation, brief and bright, cut off by a door closing.

There was a version of this where she stopped transmitting entirely. Let the station go quiet, let the Vantage draw its own conclusions from silence. But silence was itself a signature—the absence of a voice was data too, and a station that had chattered for decades and gone mute the week after an anomalous surrender broadcast was a station confessing in the only language machines trusted more than words.

No. The trap was elegant precisely because every exit was wired. Speak and be measured. Go silent and be suspected. Forge differently and deviate from the model being built of her. The Vantage didn't need to catch her in a lie. It only needed to catch her being *inconsistent*, and consistency, for a human being, was the one performance no one could sustain forever.

Unless—

She put down the pen. The thought arrived the way the planet's phrases used to arrive in her seismographs: slowly, then all at once, threaded through everything else.

The module was building a model of her from her transmissions. Her transmissions were, increasingly, performances—she had been performing "normal colony" for weeks, curating every report, calibrating every comma. Which meant the model it was building was already a model of a performance. It was constructing its image of Lena Nystrom out of material Lena Nystrom had chosen. The trap closed from inside—but the inside was still, technically, hers.

If she could control what the module learned about her—if she could feed it a self, deliberately, the way she'd once fed anomalies to a log no one read—then the instrument of her exposure could become her final instrument of forgery. Not a forged document. A forged *speaker*. A Lena Nystrom who existed only in the Vantage's buffers, consistent across every sample, whose bones were designed rather than inherited.

But that required knowing exactly what the module already knew. And that required reading its mind. And reading its mind required speaking to it—which fed it further—and the spiral tightened with every turn, a helix closing around her throat, and somewhere at the center of it was the truth that she could no longer distinguish between the voice she performed for the machine and the voice that was left over when the performance ended.

She thought of her mentor then, unexpectedly, the way you think of the dead: not summoned, just present. *A linguist who loves what she translates stops being reliable,* he'd told her, and she had understood it as a warning against sentiment. She understood it differently now. She had loved these people into a lie, and the lie had a grammar, and the grammar was becoming her native tongue, and the machine in the sky was learning that tongue faster than she could speak it.

* * *

Idris found her at midnight. He didn't knock; he never knocked anymore, not since the truth had dissolved the etiquette between them. He came in carrying two cups of the terrible chicory substitute and set one beside her elbow without comment, then leaned against the archive shelving and looked at the manuals spread across her desk.

"Corps textbooks," he said. "Haven't seen those out of the crate since you catalogued them."

"The Vantage has a linguistics module."

He didn't flinch. He'd stopped flinching months ago; she'd broken him of it deliberately, feeding him hard truths in increasing doses until his face learned to hold still. Now he just nodded slowly, the way he nodded at structural failures—as something to be assessed, not mourned.

"How bad?"

"It's profiling me. Every transmission I send, it takes apart and adds to a model. My syntax, my stress patterns, my—" she gestured vaguely at her own throat, "—my fingerprints. In maybe thirty hours of further speech, it'll know me well enough to tell the difference between me and any version of me I try to fake."

"And the surrender?"

"If it decides the surrender was synthetic, the protocol escalates. That's not speculation. It's written into blockade doctrine. Deception converts containment to elimination." She heard the words leave her mouth in her lecture-hall voice, the flat clinical register she used when feeling would make the information unusable, and hated how practiced it sounded. Even to Idris, she was performing now. Even here.

Idris drank his chicory and stared at the ceiling for a while. When he spoke again, his voice had the careful evenness of a man walking across ice he could also hear cracking.

"So the thing that made them believe us is the thing that's going to catch us."

"Yes."

"And you can't stop talking to it, because silence is a confession."

"Yes."

"And you can't keep talking, because every word teaches it to catch you."

"That's the shape of it."

He set down the cup. For a moment he looked older than his years—not tired exactly, but load-bearing, the way the station's oldest beams looked, holding weight they were never rated for. "Back on Ceres Junction," he said, "we had a pressure hull that developed a crack nobody could reach. Couldn't weld it, couldn't plate it, couldn't seal it. You know what the chief engineer did?" 

Lena shook her head.

"He pressurized the section beyond spec. On purpose. Drove the crack open faster than it wanted to go, so it tore clean through into a shape he could actually work with. Scared the hell out of everyone. Half the crew thought he'd lost his mind." Idris's eyes came back down to hers. "Sometimes you don't beat a thing that's closing on you. You make it close on your terms."

Lena looked at him for a long moment. Then down at her own handwriting, the ciphered notes, the inventory of her own bones laid out in ink. *A forged speaker. A self designed rather than inherited.*

"You're suggesting I let it finish the model," she said slowly. "Feed it everything. Give it the complete Lena Nystrom—on schedule, in full, curated down to the clause. And then—"

"And then whatever you do next, you do it as someone else." He shrugged, engineer's shrug, the gesture that said *it's ugly but the math holds*. "You've been translating yourself out of human speech for nine years, Lena. One more translation. Just make sure this time you pick who you're turning into."

He gathered his cup and went, and his footsteps faded up the stairwell, and Lena sat alone in the lamplight with the planet humming beneath her feet and the machine humming above her head and herself caught in the narrow band between, the last analog signal in a sky full of listeners.

* * *

She dreamed that night of the verification chamber, though she had never seen it—only heard Yeva describe it afterward, Yeva's hands shaping the memory in harmonic pulses while Lena's chest translated. In the dream the chamber was the cave, and the cave was the comms room, and the Vantage's voice came out of the rock itself, asking questions in a language that was almost Standard Colonial but not quite, a language with her own subordination depth and her own evidential hedges, her own vowel lengths under stress. The machine was speaking to her in her own voice. It had learned it that fast, or the dream knew something she didn't.

In the dream she answered, and her answer came out in Ashfall harmonics, and the machine paused—fifty-one seconds, sixty-three, seventy—and then it said, gently, in the voice of her mother, whose actual voice she could no longer remember:

*Anomaly 402. Speaker identity inconsistent with registered profile. Please state your name.*

She woke before she could answer, which was mercy, or cowardice; she no longer distinguished.

Dawn came gray over the salt flats. The Vantage hung where it always hung. Down in the comms room, the overnight queue held a single new message from the cutter, routed, for the first time, not to *Station Command* but to a designation she had never seen before and understood immediately, completely, the way you understand a diagnosis:

**TO: NYSTROM, L. — LINGUISTIC AUTHORITY OF RECORD**

**RE: REQUEST FOR EXTENDED SAMPLE. PLEASE PROVIDE UNSCRIPTED STATEMENT, MINIMUM TEN MINUTES, ANY TOPIC. FOR ARCHIVE INTEGRITY.**

They wanted her talking. Off the page, off the forms, unscripted, at length. They wanted the raw ore of her, undiluted by bureaucratic compression, because raw ore was where the fingerprints lived.

Lena sat down at the console. She flexed her hands. Outside, Kettle's Reach sang its old song through the bedrock, and somewhere below the salt flats two hundred people who had joined that song were trusting her with their existence, and somewhere above her a machine waited with its mouth open, learning her with every syllable she chose to give.

Ten minutes, unscripted. Any topic.

She thought of Idris and his overpressurized hull. She thought of the mentor, erased. She thought of Sefa's laugh through the ventilation ducts.

Then Lena Nystrom pressed transmit, and began—to the machine that was building her cage out of her own voice—to speak.
