## 28. Idris's Price

The storm came down off the salt flats at dusk, the way the autumn storms always did, dragging a tail of white dust across Meridian Station like a hem being pulled through the dark. Lena stood in the observation blister and watched it come, the wind carrying that faint mineral sweetness she had long ago stopped noticing and now, lately, noticed again—everything sharp-edged these days, everything arriving with its full weight.

Behind her, the station hummed its small domestic chord: recycler fans in the walls, the tick of thermal expansion in the hull, Sefa's laughter from the mess where she was teaching two of the younger kids a card game with a deck missing eleven cards. Forty-one souls. She counted them without meaning to anymore. It had become a reflex, like breathing, like the low harmonic she kept under her own voice even when she spoke plain English—the planet's grammar living in her throat now whether she wanted it there or not.

Idris found her at the blister an hour later, after the storm had settled into its long grinding patience against the hull. He carried two mugs of the chicory-substitute they'd been stretching for three years, and he handed her one without asking, which was his way of announcing that he intended to stay.

"You've got your thinking face on," he said.

"I don't have a thinking face."

"You have three faces. Thinking face, lying face, and the one you make when you're listening to the ground." He leaned his shoulder against the frame beside her. "This is the first one. Mostly."

Lena turned the mug in her hands. The chicory smelled burnt and familiar. Outside, the dust moved in sheets, erasing the flats, erasing the horizon, erasing everything except the small lit box they stood in.

"Idris," she said, "if you're here to ask again—"

"I'm not here to ask." He drank. Set the mug down on the sill. And then he did something she had almost never seen him do: he looked out at the storm instead of at her, and let a silence stretch until it had weight, until it became a thing he was choosing rather than failing to fill.

She waited. Nine years of tending a dying colony had taught her that men like Idris Okafor—men who fixed things, who kept their hands moving so their minds didn't have to speak—only talked when the silence got heavier than the words. So she waited, and the storm waited with her, and somewhere below them, two hundred people slept in the warm dark of the deep caves, breathing the planet's slow song, trusting her.

"It was the Corvid seam," Idris said finally. "Before your time. Before most anyone's time. Back when the station ran three shifts and Earth still answered our mail."

Lena went very still. She knew the Corvid seam the way you know a grave you've never visited—it was the collapsed gallery on the station's western limb, sealed since before Sefa was born, its door welded shut and painted over twice. The children called it the ghost wing. Nobody had told them why.

"There were miners down there when the tremor came," Idris said. His voice was level, almost conversational, which was how she knew how much it was costing him. "Three of them. Okonjo, Vashti, and a kid named Pell—nineteen years old, first contract, still wrote home every week. The seismic net gave us a warning window. Maybe ninety seconds, maybe less. Standard protocol says you sound the evacuation horn the moment the threshold trips. You don't wait. You don't confirm. You blow the horn and you scare forty people out of a tunnel for nothing if it's nothing, because the alternative is this."

He stopped. Lena said nothing. Outside, the dust scraped along the hull like something reading them by touch.

"The threshold tripped and I was shift lead," he said. "And I looked at the waveform and I thought—I *thought*—it looked like the harmonic signature of a settling event, not a shear. We'd had six false alarms that quarter. Six horns, six evacuations, and the company rep had already written me up once for 'excessive protocol invocation.' Productivity losses. There was a shipment due through the corridor the next morning, and if we evacuated the corridor, the shipment slipped, and the shipment slipping meant—" He made a small gesture with one hand, opening it, letting whatever it held fall. "It meant nothing. That's the joke. It meant nothing. A late shipment. A letter from Earth. But at the time it felt like a wall I had to hold up, and I held it, and I keyed the all-clear instead of the horn."

Lena's chest had gone tight. She understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what was coming, and she understood too that he needed to arrive there himself, at his own speed, in his own words.

"Ninety seconds," he said. "I gave them back eighty of them. I told myself I was buying them time to finish the cut cleanly, get out orderly, no panic, no one trampled in the dark. That's what I told myself. What I actually did was gamble three lives against my write-up file, and I lost." His jaw worked once. "The shear took the gallery in eleven seconds. Okonjo and Vashti never knew. Pell's suit beacon ran for four hours in the rubble before the air cell died. They recovered him on day nine. He was curled up facing the way home."

The chicory had gone cold in Lena's hands. She set it down on the sill because she couldn't feel it anymore.

"I transmitted the false all-clear," Idris said. "That's the part that matters. Not the judgment call, not the misread waveform—a transmission. My voice, my key, going out over the net saying *all clear, stand down,* when it wasn't. When three men were already dead because of it. And the inquiry bought it, because I was good, because I was reliable, because nobody who knew me could believe I'd lie about a thing like that." He laughed, short and airless. "Reliability. You know what they put on my commendation? 'Exceptional composure under ambiguity.'"

"Idris—"

"No. Let me finish, because I've been circling this for weeks and if I stop I'll never land." He turned from the storm at last and faced her, and in the dim light of the blister his face looked older than it had that morning, older than it had in the nine years she'd known him, as though he'd been carrying this the whole time and had only just now set it down between them where she could see its size. "You've been going out at night. Your speech has changed—you breathe in patterns now, did you know that? Four counts in, seven counts out. That's not human rhythm, Lena. That's geology. And you lie beautifully, which is the worst part, because I only recognize it because I do it too. Whatever's happening—whatever you found under the salt, whatever you're protecting—I'm not asking you to tell me. I'm telling you this so you understand what you're holding."

"And what am I holding?"

"A man who knows what a well-made lie costs." He picked up his mug again, needing his hands busy. "So here's my question, and it's the only one I'll ask. This thing you're building—the forgery, the broadcast, whatever shape it's taking in that head of yours. Is it penance or is it repetition? Because I have spent twenty-two years not knowing which one mine was. Every repair I make on this station, every system I keep alive, every night I spend keeping forty-one people breathing—is that me paying a debt or is it just me finding more elaborate ways to be trusted with other people's lives so I can fail them again at scale?" His voice cracked on the last word, barely, a hairline fracture in the concrete of him, and then it sealed itself over. "I can't tell. Twenty-two years, and I can't tell. And I look at you planning whatever you're planning, and I see the same arithmetic in your eyes, and I need to know if you've figured out something I haven't."

Lena looked at him for a long time.

She thought about telling him the truth—all of it, Yeva, the caves, the two hundred, the Vantage overhead running its patient countdown, the deadline that had stopped being abstract days ago. She had rehearsed this conversation a dozen times in the dark hours, and in every rehearsal she either lied completely or confessed completely, and both versions had felt like betrayal. But standing here now, with his confession still warm between them like a coal, she understood that neither script was right. There was a third thing. There was what you owed a man who had just handed you the ugliest fact of his life: honesty about what you could not give him.

"I can't tell you," she said quietly. "Not yet. And I won't insult you by pretending that's protection instead of distrust."

His mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "Appreciated."

"But I'll answer the real question," she said. "The penance one. As best I can." She turned to face the storm fully, because some things were easier said to weather than to faces. "I don't think it's either. Penance or repetition. I think that's a false binary, and binaries are how the Corps trained us to think, and I've spent nine years unlearning it." She breathed in—four counts—and out, seven, and heard him notice, and let him notice. "A lie like yours doesn't get paid off, Idris. There's no balance point where the ledger clears and you're absolved. Pell stays dead at any balance point. That's not a debt, it's a wound, and wounds don't get repaid, they get *carried.* The question isn't whether you've suffered enough. The question is what you do while you're carrying it. Whether the carrying makes you careful or makes you cruel. Whether you spend the rest of your life flinching from transmissions—or whether you learn to send true ones."

"That's not absolution," he said.

"No. I can't promise you absolution. Nobody can, and anyone who does is selling something." She finally looked back at him. "What I can promise is this: if you help me—if you choose to carry this next lie with me—I will never let you carry it alone, and I will never pretend it's clean. It isn't clean. What I'm doing isn't clean. I've stopped waiting for it to be."

Idris was quiet for a long moment. The storm thudded softly against the blister glass, patient as tide.

"Okonjo used to sing," he said. "In the tunnels. Badly. Off-key hymns from a colony church nobody attended anymore. Vashti complained about it every shift. Pell recorded it once on his suit log—we found the file in the wreckage recovery. Eleven seconds of the worst singing I've ever heard." He swallowed. "I keep it. On a chip, in my kit. I've listened to it maybe four times in twenty-two years. I keep thinking someday I'll listen to it and it'll mean penance finished. Like the song will tell me when the debt's paid." He shook his head slowly. "It never does. It just means I miss them. That's all it ever means."

"That's what it should mean," Lena said. "Missing them isn't the debt. Missing them is the truth. The debt was never yours to pay—it belonged to the people who built a system where a shift lead had to choose between a write-up and a horn." She heard her own bitterness surface, old and cold, the Corps's lessons rising in her like groundwater. "They always make it feel personal. That's how the machine protects itself. It teaches you that the failure is your character, so you spend decades auditing yourself instead of the design."

"Careful," Idris said dryly. "Keep talking like that and I'll start thinking you're wise instead of strange."

"I'm both. It's in my file."

He laughed then, really laughed, brief and rough, and some of the terrible age went out of his face, and Lena realized how much she had missed the sound—how long it had been since either of them had laughed at anything, with the Vantage hanging overhead like a closed eye.

Then he set down his mug, and straightened, and the humor drained out of him deliberately, the way a man drains a line before working on it.

"So," he said. "Here's what happens now. I'm not asking what you're hiding. But I'm telling you what I'm going to do, so you can plan around it or plan with it, your choice. Tomorrow I start hardening the telemetry relays. All of them. Redundant feeds, hand-built verification loops, the works. If anything up there—" he tipped his head toward the ceiling, toward the sky, toward the cutter that officially wasn't watching them, "—starts pulling data from this station, it's going to pull data I've touched. Data I trust. And if you need a signal shaped, or a feed spoofed, or a transmission made to look like it came from anywhere other than where it came from—" he met her eyes, steady, unflinching, "—you come to me. Not because I owe a debt. Because I'm good at it, and because I'd rather carry a live lie with you than a dead one alone."

Lena's throat tightened. She thought of Yeva's wary eyes in the lamplight of the deep cave. She thought of Sefa asleep down the corridor, dreaming whatever children dream on forgotten worlds. She thought of the forged surrender taking shape in her mind, syllable by syllable, and of how much of it would have to travel through wires and antennas and machines that Idris alone understood.

"You're conscripting yourself," she said. "Without knowing the mission."

"I'm volunteering," he corrected. "There's a difference. Conscripts get shot for desertion. Volunteers get to pick their post." He picked up both mugs, hers included, and paused at the blister door. "One more thing. The chip with Pell's singing. If this goes wrong—if whatever you're building comes apart and takes us with it—promise me someone finds that chip. Promise me it gets sent outward. Not upward. Outward. Somewhere his family's descendants might hear it someday and know he existed."

"I promise," Lena said, and meant it with her whole body, the way she had learned to mean things in the planet's language—every word a load-bearing structure.

He nodded once and went, his footsteps fading down the corridor toward the mess, toward the sound of Sefa's card game, toward the ordinary evening he was defending with twenty-two years of quiet and one fresh confession.

Lena stayed in the blister a while longer. The storm was thinning; through the ragged dust she could see stars coming back, and among them, if she knew where to look, the faint unhurried crawl of the Vantage on its patrol arc. She raised her hand halfway toward it—an old Corps gesture, half salute, half warding—and let it fall.

*Penance or repetition,* he'd asked. She turned the question over in herself and found it fit her as well as it fit him, and found, too, that her answer was the same one she'd given him: there was no clearing the ledger. There was only the carrying, and what the carrying made of you.

She was still standing there, counting stars, when the station's comm panel chimed behind her—one soft tone, then another, then a third, wrong somehow, spaced unevenly, like a heartbeat stumbling.

She turned. The panel's status light was amber. Earth's loop, the forty-second transmission that had repeated faithfully for decades, was stuttering.

She crossed the blister in four strides and pulled the decode, and the fragmentary text assembled itself on the screen in broken pieces, syllables dropping out mid-word:

**...VANTAGE... PROTOCOL COMPLETION REQUIRED PRIOR TO SIGNAL LOSS... AUTONOMOUS AUTHORITY GRANTED... ELEVEN DAYS...**

Eleven days.

Lena read it twice. Then she went to find Idris, because whatever she was going to build, she would not build it alone.
