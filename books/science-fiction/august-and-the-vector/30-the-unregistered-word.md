## 30. The Unregistered Word

The Corps terminal still remembered her.

That was the first cruelty of it. Lena sat in the relay room at three in the morning, station hum in her teeth, and watched the login screen bloom open at the touch of her old credentials—*Nystrom, L., Xenolinguistics Division, Field Classification: Isolated Post*—as if no time had passed at all, as if she were still twenty-six years old and signing on for a nine-year tour that was supposed to feel like exile and had instead become a marriage. The cursor blinked in a field labeled WELCOME BACK. Nobody had welcomed her back in a decade. The machine did it without meaning it, which was somehow worse.

She had expected the credentials to be dead. Expired, purged, folded into some archival tomb along with the rest of the Linguistics Corps' ambitions. But Earth's bureaucracy was a glacier: it moved slowly, it forgot nothing, and it crushed whatever it flowed over, but it kept its records intact beneath the ice. Her account lived. Her clearance lived—dormant, flagged *inactive*, but structurally sound, like a door sealed behind floodwater.

And behind that door, she had discovered over four sleepless nights of reading protocol documents older than Sefa, was Catalog Entry.

The Corps had maintained, since its founding, a registry of human languages—not only the ones spoken on Earth, but every language encountered, reconstructed, or documented in the long diaspora. Registration was the legal spine of first contact law. A registered language conferred standing. A registered people could petition, could negotiate, could surrender in terms that machines were obligated to parse. An unregistered language was noise. And under blockade protocol, noise from an unregistered source during an active sterilization window was not entitled to a reply.

The Vantage would not accept a surrender in Ashfall. Not because Ashfall was false—it was two centuries old, deep as bedrock, alive in two hundred throats—but because Ashfall did not exist. Not officially. Not anywhere in the great glacier's memory.

Unless someone made it exist.

Lena stared at the blank entry form and understood, finally, what her gambit actually cost. She had told Idris it was paperwork. She had told Yeva it was a door she could open. Both true, both lies by omission, because neither of them had read the submission requirements, and she had not yet made herself say them aloud.

She said them now, to the empty room, in no language anyone would have recognized—a habit nine years deep, a private grammar of pressure and breath—and then she translated herself into English, because this next part deserved to be said plainly even if only to herself.

The registration required a submitting linguist of record. It required field documentation: corpus, phonology, syntax, semantic mapping, native-speaker attestation. It required verification data sufficient for independent analysis. And it required, in Section One, in fields that could not be left blank:

Submitting linguist's full legal name.
Current location, precise to coordinates.
Certification of direct, sustained contact with the speech community.

Her real name. Her real location. Her real self, notarized into the permanent record of the institution she had fled, attached forever to a language that officially did not exist until the moment she signed it into being—which meant that the moment she signed it into being, she also signed the Ashfall people into existence in the eyes of Earth. Two hundred souls, coordinates included, delivered to the same bureaucracy whose blockade cutter was orbiting overhead with a sterilization order warming in its memory.

The truth was the payment. There was no way around it. A forged registration—an anonymous one, a laundered one—would fail authentication inside the Vantage's linguistics module before the first syllable finished parsing. The machine wanted a human being. It wanted a name it could check against its archives, a career it could weigh, a body it could hold responsible.

It wanted her.

* * *

She found Yeva below, in the chamber the Ashfall called the listening room, though the word they used for it translated more nearly to *the place where we agree to hear each other*. The harmonics moved through the stone there like weather. Lena felt them enter through the soles of her feet, up through her shins, settling somewhere behind her sternum where she had learned to keep the planet's song—the low C of the mantle, the slow arpeggios of the salt flats breathing, and threaded through all of it now, unmistakable once you knew, the human music of two hundred voices singing themselves into geology.

Yeva was teaching when Lena arrived. Six children sat in a ring on the warm stone, palms flat against the floor, learning the phrase that meant *the ground is speaking; be worthy of the hearing*. When they saw Lena they went quiet in the particular way children go quiet around something they have been told is important—reverent, a little afraid, curious anyway. One of them, a boy with ash-pale eyes, signed a greeting at her in the old hand-speech, and Lena answered, clumsy but correct, and the children made a sound that was almost laughter, almost wind.

Yeva dismissed them with a chord sung through cupped hands, and then it was the two of them and the planet's slow voice.

"You didn't sleep," Yeva said. In harmonic speech the observation carried more than the words: she sang the phrase for *a lamp burning past its oil*, and looked at Lena with the level patience of a woman who had spent two centuries' worth of inherited vigilance learning to wait.

"I need to tell you what the registration costs," Lena said. "All of it. Before I file."

She laid it out. The name. The coordinates. The attestation of contact—signed, dated, undeniable. The fact that the entry would sit encrypted in her transmission queue, keyed to release only after the Vantage accepted the surrender broadcast, but released *fully* when it released: everything true, everything documented, everything Earth would need to find them if finding them was what Earth chose to do afterward.

Yeva listened the way she did everything—with her whole body, weight settled, hands still, eyes doing the work that mouths did elsewhere. When Lena finished, the silence lasted long enough that the mantle's note changed twice beneath them.

"You are offering us to them," Yeva said at last. Not accusation. Measurement.

"I'm offering them you," Lena said. "There's a difference, and I'm betting all our lives that the difference holds. A registered language can't be sterilized quietly. It has to be *answered*. That's the law they wrote, back when they still pretended the law meant something. Once Ashfall exists in the catalog, the Vantage can't treat your surrender as noise. It has to process it, verify it, log it. And every log goes to the sector archive. Every archive gets read eventually. By someone. Someday." She heard her own voice thinning and steadied it. "I'm not giving you to Earth. I'm making you too visible to erase."

"And if they erase us anyway," Yeva said, "they will do it knowing our names."

"Yes."

Another silence. Then Yeva did something Lena had never seen her do: she reached out and took Lena's hand, turned it palm-up, and pressed her own palm against it—the gesture the Ashfall used for *witness*, the oldest word in their language, the one that meant *I see what you carry and I do not look away*.

"My grandmother's grandmother hid in a cargo hold," Yeva said, "and taught her daughter to sing quietly. Every generation since has been taught the same lesson: *be small, be stone, be nothing the sky can name.*" Her hand tightened. "You are asking me to let my people be named."

"I'm asking you to let them be named by me. First. On my record. With my life wrapped around yours in the filing." Lena swallowed. "If it goes wrong, they come for the linguist who certified contact. Protocol says the submitter of record is the accountable party. That's me. That's the whole design of it—I built myself into the load-bearing wall."

Yeva studied her for a long time. The stone sang. Somewhere above them, forty-one people slept in a tin can on the surface, and one of them was Idris, who was probably awake, because Idris was always awake lately, keeping his own kind of watch.

"The children asked about you today," Yeva said finally. "They asked if you belong to the surface or the deep." A pause, and then, in the harmonic phrase that had no honest English translation but meant approximately *the river does not choose which bank it waters*: "File your paper, Lena Nystrom. Name us. And when the sky comes to collect the name, make certain it collects yours with it."

* * *

Idris found her in the relay room at dawn, or what passed for dawn on Kettle's Reach—the hour when the dust storms turned amber and the light through the ports went the color of old honey. He brought two cups of the chicory substitute that passed for coffee and set one down beside her console without asking, which was its own kind of sentence in the language the two of them had been speaking for nine years.

"How bad is the paperwork?" he asked.

"Worse than lying," Lena said. "Lying only costs you the truth. This costs me—" She stopped. Started again. "Section One wants my name. My actual coordinates. Certification of sustained contact, signed in my hand. It's not a forgery, Idris. It's a confession with a delay timer on it."

He pulled a crate over and sat, knees creaking, and drank his chicory, and thought, because Idris thought the way rock formed: slowly, under pressure, without complaint.

"So after the broadcast," he said, "when the envelope opens—Earth gets a document that says *the last xenolinguist of Meridian Station, acting alone, contacted an unregistered colony of two hundred descendants of the Ashfall Fleet, certified their language, and brokered their surrender.*"

"They get exactly that."

"They get *you*." He set the cup down. "Lena. They'll take your credentials, your pension, your name in the histories—they'll probably want you on a transport back to stand in front of a review board composed of people who've never seen dirt. You'd be trading the rest of your life for—"

"For a language that exists," Lena said. "For two hundred people who get to be *people* instead of a protocol flag. For a surrender that a machine is legally required to believe." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "My mentor used to say the Corps' whole architecture was built on one principle: a lie won't survive notarization. So we don't notarize a lie. We notarize the truth and aim it like a weapon."

Idris was quiet for a while. Outside the port, the amber light crawled across the salt flats, and somewhere beneath those flats, two hundred people were going about the ordinary miracle of another day of survival.

"Sefa asked me yesterday why you've been going below," he said. "I told her you were mapping the deep aquifers. She looked at me the way she looks at broken fuses—like she already knows the thing is dead and is waiting to see if I'll admit it." He rubbed his jaw. "That girl's going to be trouble someday. Good trouble."

"She's going to be a linguist," Lena said, "if we're all still here in five years."

"That's the plan, then." Idris stood, joints protesting, and rested one broad hand briefly on her shoulder—the whole sermon, the whole absolution, delivered in four seconds of pressure. "Name yourself properly, then. If you're going to burn your name down, spell it right."

When he was gone, Lena opened Section One and began to type.

* * *

It took her six hours to write what should have taken twenty minutes, because she refused to rush any field of it, because every line was a stone she was setting into her own cairn.

**Submitting linguist:** Nystrom, Lena Aurore. Full legal name, unabbreviated, the middle name her mother had chosen and that Lena had not spoken aloud since the recruitment interview eleven years ago. Typing it felt like opening a door in a house she'd sold.

**Field post:** Meridian Station, Kettle's Reach, sector designation she still knew by heart. Coordinates to seven decimal places—the exact spot where forty-one people had loved each other badly and well through decades of silence.

Then the corpus. Here, at least, she was rich beyond any linguist in Corps history. Nine years of anomaly logs—four hundred and one entries, the last of them the night the beacon sang. She attached the seismic mappings, the harmonic transcriptions rendered into notation, the syntactic analyses she'd built alone at this very console, believing she was decoding a planet when she was really eavesdropping on a congregation. She attached the attestation recordings: Yeva's voice, the children's ring-song, the listening room's chord. She wrote the phonology section with her whole chest, because it was good work—it was the best work of her life, done in exile, unwitnessed, and it deserved to be written well even if it was also a fuse.

**Speech community:** Descendants of the Ashfall Fleet. Population approximately two hundred. Location: deep cave systems beneath the Halvard Salt Flats, coordinates enclosed. Cultural continuity: unbroken since fleet arrival, estimated 214 years. Language status: living, vital, transmitted intergenerationally.

Her cursor hovered over the final field.

**Linguist's certification:** *I certify that the foregoing documentation is complete and accurate to the best of my knowledge, that my contact with the speech community was direct and sustained, and that I submit this entry as the definitive catalog record of the Ashfall language.*

Direct and sustained. Accurate. Complete. Every word of it true—that was the trap's elegance, that was the payment's shape. The Corps had built a system that could smell a lie, so she was feeding it nothing but truth, arranged in an order that would save everyone, signed with a name that would ruin her. Her mentor had erased himself trying something smaller than this. She thought of him suddenly, vividly—his office smelling of tea and ozone, his hands moving as he talked, the last time she'd seen him saying *translation is betrayal, Lena, so betray carefully*—and she typed her certification, and beneath it, in the signature field, she entered her biometric key: the voiceprint sample the Corps had taken when she was twenty-six, her mother's language still in her accent then, her whole unbent future still in her throat.

The system accepted it. **Entry ASH-001 pending transmission. Status: queued — conditional release.**

One line remained, optional, unlabeled, a free-text field the old forms had kept for linguists' notes. Generations of field workers had used it for warnings, dedications, small immortal grievances. Lena looked at it for a long time.

Then she typed: *This language was not lost. It was hidden, and it survived, and the hiding was wise. To whoever reads this entry: the fault in the record is mine alone. The language was always true.*

She queued the transmission packet—encrypted, keyed to the Vantage's acceptance handshake, inert until the forged surrender was parsed and verified—and her finger rested on the send command for the length of four full breaths.

Outside, the amber light was failing into evening. Below the salt flats, two hundred people were singing the ground to sleep. Above them, a machine with a sterilization order ran its patient arithmetic, seventy-two hours and falling.

Lena Nystrom sent her life's final catalog entry into the dark, addressed to an empire that had stopped writing back, and watched the confirmation light blink green, once, like a held breath finally let go.

The console chimed. Incoming priority traffic, fleet band, automated header.

**VANTAGE // PROTOCOL NOTICE // LINGUISTICS MODULE QUERY // RESPONSE REQUIRED WITHIN 12 HOURS.**

The module had a question for her.
