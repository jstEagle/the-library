## 31. Everything True

The registration form had not changed in two hundred years.

That was the first thing Lena noticed, sitting in the dark of the transmitter room with Idris's salvaged terminal humming against her knees: the Corps intake template for a new language entry was the same one she'd memorized at twenty-two, the same fields, the same cold bureaucratic litany. *Catalog designation. Speaker population. Phonemic inventory. Syntactic typology. Submitter's credentials. Submitter's legal name. Submitter's current location.* Somewhere on Earth, or in the orbital archives that outlived whatever Earth had become, a form was still waiting to be filled, patient as a grave.

They had built a machine for remembering languages and forgotten to build one for remembering people.

She had been awake for thirty hours. The Vantage's deadline hung over the station like weather—seventy-two hours, now sixty-one, now a number that shrank every time she let herself look at it—and the plan they had assembled in the mess over two nights of argument and cold tea came down, in the end, to this: a surrender was only valid in a registered language, and Ashfall was not registered, and so Lena Nystrom was going to register it, using credentials that had been dormant for nine years, in a submission that would name her, locate her, and confess everything.

The truth and the lie, sealed in the same envelope.

She flexed her hands. They ached. Three nights of rehearsal with Yeva had left her knuckles swollen and her throat raw in ways that had nothing to do with speaking—the harmonic language was not made for a human body, and her body had begun, quietly, to file its complaints. She could feel the low registers fading already, a softness at the bottom of her range like ice going out of a pond in spring. She did not have time to grieve that. She put it on the shelf with everything else she was not allowing herself to feel, and the shelf groaned.

*Catalog designation,* she typed.

Her fingers hesitated over the keys. Not because she didn't know the answer. Because she knew it too well. She had spent nine years learning this language with her whole body—her sternum, her molars, the soles of her feet against the singing stone of the deep caves—and now she had to compress it into the Corps' taxonomy, to flatten a living thing into a specimen jar. *Designation: Ashfall Harmonic Complex (provisional).* The words looked wrong on the screen, small and dead, like a bird pinned through the chest.

"You're stalling," Idris said from the doorway.

He had a mug in each hand. He had been bringing her mugs all night, a rotation of terrible tea that had gone from comfort to ritual to something like a vigil. He set one down on the terminal housing, where the heat of the processors would keep it warm, and stood there with his sleeves rolled up and his face in shadow, and waited. He was very good at waiting. Nine years of fixing a station no one would resupply had taught him that most things could not be rushed, only tended.

"I'm translating," Lena said. "It's different."

"Is it."

"Translation is betrayal. Registration is—" She searched for the word and found it, and it frightened her. "Registration is testimony. It's swearing that what I say is real. It's putting my name under it."

Idris looked at the screen for a long moment. The cursor blinked in the designation field, patient as a pulse.

"Good," he said finally. "It should cost you."

* * *

She worked through the small hours, and the work was the strangest of her life, because every field demanded a different kind of truth.

*Speaker population: 204.* She had counted them herself, walking the lower galleries with Yeva, and the number had lodged in her like a splinter. Two hundred and four people. She had once conducted a census of forty-one and wept over how few they were; now she knew a hidden nation smaller than her own station's despair, and the arithmetic of it—two hundred and forty-five souls on an entire planet, all of them one bad decision away from sterilization—sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed.

*Phonemic inventory:* impossible. The Corps form wanted consonants and vowels, wanted IPA, wanted the architecture of a mouth. Ashfall had no mouth. It had stone and water and the resonant geometry of caverns, and its phonemes were pressures and durations, harmonics that lived below the threshold of hearing and were felt in the ribs before they were understood by the mind. She wrote it out anyway, forcing the language into notation it had never consented to, and as she worked she heard her mentor's voice, the one from the flashback she could no longer keep out of her nights: *Every rendering chooses a truth, Lena. Choose knowing what you're choosing.*

She was choosing the truth that would keep them alive. She wrote it all down.

*Syntactic typology:* she gave the Corps its categories—agglutinative, aspect-rich, evidentiality marked at every clause—and then, in the notes field, in language the form did not require and would not expect, she added the thing that mattered: *This language is spoken by the body entire. It cannot be learned by listening alone. It is also the language of the planet's seismic song, which the submitter has catalogued across nine years of observation as anomalies 1 through 401. The speakers did not hide in this world. They joined it.*

She stopped after that sentence. Read it again. Her heart was going hard, a bird against a window.

Because here was the trap she had walked into with her eyes open, the trap she had designed for herself, and she needed to look at it plainly at least once before she committed. The registration required her real name. Her real location. Her real self. If she filed it encrypted—release only upon acceptance of the surrender broadcast—then the truth would sit in Earth's archives like a bomb with a tripwire, and the Vantage would accept the surrender, and the protocol would suspend, and everyone would live. And then, some day, some clerk, some committee, some algorithm running its slow audit, would open the envelope and find her confession inside: *the Ashfall exist, here are the coordinates, here is what your blockade protocol was built to do, here is my name and here is my crime.* She would not be a traitor in the future tense. She was one already. She was simply choosing the hour of her own unmasking, and she had chosen the hour that bought two hundred and forty-five people their lives.

There was no version of this where she survived it clean. She had understood that in the mess hall, watching forty-one people decide one by one to lie with her. She understood it more completely now, alone, at 0400 station time, with the terminal's glow on her hands.

The terrible thing—the thing she would never be able to explain to the tribunal she could already imagine, the one in her head with its cold lights and its procedural voice—was that she felt calm. Not numb. Calm. The way the deep caves were calm, the way the planet's long song was calm, a frequency so low and so steady that everything frantic eventually came to rest inside it.

She had spent nine years learning to speak like the planet. Apparently she had also learned to feel like it.

* * *

Sefa found her at dawn.

The girl came into the transmitter room the way she came everywhere now—carefully, on the balls of her feet, with the changed look still on her face, the look of someone who had been through the cave door and come back carrying a weight that did not fit a twelve-year-old's shoulders. Since the mess hall, since the truth, Sefa had stopped asking questions. That was the worst part. Children are supposed to ask questions. Sefa had gone quiet the way the Ashfall were quiet, and Lena knew exactly which language had taught her that.

"You haven't slept," Sefa said.

"I'll sleep when it's done."

"Yeva says the low part of your voice is going." The girl said it flatly, factually, the way she reported airlock pressures to Idris. "She says you spent it too fast. She says humans bank their harmonics like water and you drank the reservoir."

"Yeva is usually right."

"She says you'll keep the high part. She says the high part is where the meaning lives anyway, and the low part is just—" Sefa paused, hunting for the word Yeva had used, her hands shaping it in the air before her voice found it. "—the floor. The floor goes and the room is still the room."

Lena looked at her for a long moment. The terminal screen glowed between them, half a confession, waiting.

"Did Yeva send you, or did you come on your own?"

"Both." Sefa came closer, stood at the edge of the terminal's light. She was growing so fast. Lena kept a mark on the doorframe from two years ago and the top of the girl's head had passed it in the spring, and no one had mentioned it because mentioning it meant admitting time was passing, and on Meridian Station they had a collective agreement not to notice that. "She wanted me to tell you something. She said: tell her that a name given truly is worth more than a name kept. She said you'd know what it means."

Lena closed her eyes. In the harmonic language, in the old formal registers Yeva had taught her in the deep galleries, *name* and *witness* were near-neighbors, separated by a single shift of pressure, the way *river* and *blood* were near-neighbors, the way *song* and *stone* were the same word inflected twice. A name given truly. A witness offered freely. Yeva was telling her that the confession was not a betrayal of the Ashfall but a gift to them—that to be named in Earth's records was, after two centuries of erasure, a kind of resurrection. *We were never here,* the hidden people had believed for generations. *Do not tell the sky.* And Yeva had turned it over, in the dark, in the way of speakers, and decided: tell the sky. Tell the sky everything. Let the sky choke on it.

"Tell her I know," Lena said. "Tell her I'm almost done."

Sefa didn't move. She was looking at the screen, at the fields of truth, and Lena watched her read—watched her lips move over *sterilization protocol* and *submitter's legal name*—and saw the girl understand, in one long slow moment, what the document was and what it would cost the woman who filed it.

"You're going to be in trouble," Sefa said. "Forever."

"Yes."

"Is it worth it?"

Lena thought of the lower galleries, the salt-light coming down through fissures, two hundred people who had never once seen an open horizon. She thought of Idris's hands on the telemetry rig, steady as a surgeon's. She thought of her mother's voice, which she had lost years before she lost her language, and of how some things, once given away, do not come back, and of how some things given away become larger than they were.

"Yes," she said.

Sefa nodded once, the way Yeva nodded, the way the planet nodded in its slow seismic assent, and left. At the door she paused. "I'm learning the high part," she said. "In case. In case someone has to keep the room."

Then she was gone, and Lena sat alone with the form, and finished it.

* * *

The last fields were the ones she had been circling all night.

*Submitter's legal name: Lena Okonkwo Nystrom. Corps credential 7-Alpha-114, Linguistics Corps, Cataloguing Division, status: dormant.* Her name looked strange in the terminal's plain font—too small for her, or she too large for it, the way a word repeated too many times goes strange in the mouth. She had not written her full legal name in nine years. On station she was Lena, or Dr. Nystrom, or the woman who talks to rocks, depending on who was joking. The name on the form belonged to a twenty-six-year-old who had volunteered for exile because she could not bear a profession that catalogued dying languages and did nothing while their last speakers were moved, relocated, administratively disappeared. She had come to Kettle's Reach to keep a record. She had become the record.

*Submitter's current location: Meridian Station, Kettle's Reach, coordinates as follows.* She entered the station's coordinates, and then, in the supplementary field, the cave system's—the salt flats, the door, the depth. All of it. Every number that mattered. She gave Earth the map to the people it had spent two hundred years trying not to find, and she did it in the same envelope as the plea that would keep the drones from burning them, and the two documents would travel together forever, the lie and the truth, the shield and the confession, inseparable as a word and its meaning.

*Enclosure conditions:* she set the release lock to the Vantage's own protocol handshake. The envelope would open only when the surrender broadcast was accepted and verified—only when the lie had done its work. Until then it would sit in the queue, encrypted, one packet among the station's routine telemetry, invisible, innocent.

Unless the Vantage's linguistics module flagged her first. Unless the verification failed. Unless the module had already begun pulling the thread of her linguistic signature and found, as she feared it had, the shape of a forger's hand.

She could not control that. She had spent her whole career believing that language could be controlled—that meaning was a machine with levers—and the last months had taught her otherwise. Meaning was weather. You could only stand in it and tell the truth about what you felt.

She moved the cursor to the certification field. The Corps form ended, as all Corps forms ended, with a declaration, and she read it now for the first time in nine years, and it read her back:

*I certify that the foregoing is a true and complete account, submitted under my own name, and that I accept full legal and professional responsibility for its contents.*

Under her own name. Full responsibility.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her most of all. She had rehearsed this moment in a hundred nightmares and in all of them her hands shook, and here they were, quiet on the keys, and the terrible calm had settled over her so completely that it felt less like courage than like altitude—like she had climbed so high the air had gone thin and still, and everything below, the station and the caves and the two fleets and the war two centuries cold, was small and silent and very far away.

She thought of her mentor, disgraced and erased, and wrote him a sentence in the notes field that he would never read, that no one would understand, that was not for the Corps at all: *You told me a linguist who loves what she translates stops being reliable. You were right. I have stopped being reliable. I have started being true.*

She thought of Yeva's message, carried by a child at dawn: *a name given truly is worth more than a name kept.*

She signed it.

The terminal took the packet, encrypted it, folded it into the outgoing queue between a routine atmospheric report and the station's water-reclamation log, and sent it upward into the dark, toward the Vantage, toward the archives, toward whatever Earth still was. The transmission light blinked three times and went out. The room was quiet. The tea in the mug on the terminal housing had gone cold without her ever drinking it.

Idris appeared in the doorway again. She hadn't heard him come. He looked at her face, and at the empty certification field, and he didn't ask. He just nodded, slow, and came and stood beside her, and put his hand on her shoulder, and they watched the transmitter's standby light breathe in the dark—on, off, on—like something sleeping, like something waiting to be woken.

"Sixty-one hours," he said. "Then the broadcast."

"Then the broadcast."

"And after that?"

Lena looked at the dead form on the screen, at her own name sitting in it like a stone at the bottom of a well. Somewhere above them, a machine was reading her life's final catalog entry. Somewhere inside that machine, a linguistics module was parsing every syllable she had ever sent it, hunting for the seam between the woman and the forgery.

"After that," she said, "we find out if the truth was heavy enough to hold the lie down."

The transmitter's queue light blinked once—an incoming handshake, a query, the Vantage reaching down its long cold hand—and the room went very still.
