## 33. Verification

The message came at 0400 station time, which was no time at all—Meridian Station kept its clocks out of habit, the way a widow keeps setting a second cup—and Lena read it three times before she let herself understand it.

*Protocol requires live-speaker verification. Transmit audio of a native speaker responding to generated prompts in the registered language. Failure to comply within one rotation will be construed as forgery and protocol will resume.*

The Vantage wanted a voice. Not a recording of a voice, not a constructed voice, not a voice stitched from harmonics and hope—a living throat, answering questions it had not been allowed to rehearse.

Lena sat in the comm room with the message glowing on her screen and felt the cold come up through the deck plates the way it always did before dawn, when the station's bones contracted in the dark. She had built the surrender broadcast the way her mentor had taught her: every syllable true in her body, every structure load-bearing, every pause placed like a stone in a wall. The Vantage's linguistics module had parsed it for eleven hours. Then it had asked its question, and the question was not about grammar.

It wanted proof that the language still lived in someone other than her.

She understood why. Any linguist worth her credentials could forge a surrender; the Corps had manuals on it, sealed annexes, case studies with names redacted. What a machine could not easily forge was a speaker—the particular imperfection of a mouth that had grown up inside a language, the hesitations that weren't errors but texture, the way a native speaker answered a question slightly wrong because the question itself was foreign. The module would generate prompts. It would listen for the grain beneath the grammar.

And there was only one throat on Kettle's Reach that carried that grain.

Yeva Ashfall had not spoken to the sky in two hundred years of inherited silence. Her people had taught their children the planet's song instead of their fleet's tongue precisely so that no transmission, no stray harmonic, no child's shout into a canyon would ever carry the signature Earth's instruments were built to find. Two centuries of folding a language small enough to hide inside geology. And now Lena had to ask Yeva to unfold it—to stand under an open antenna, in front of an Earth machine, and let the forbidden syllables leave her body as sound.

* * *

She went down through the salt flats at first light, when the crust glowed rose-gold and crunched under her boots like old porcelain. The cave door knew her now. It opened on Yeva's knock-pattern returned in greeting, and the warm mineral breath of the deep tunnels rose around her, and somewhere below, faint as memory, the planet's song was already moving through stone.

Yeva was waiting in the speaking chamber, the round room where the Ashfall conducted their hardest conversations—conducted them in pressure and tone, in vibrations passed hand to hand through the rail that ringed the walls. She was old in the way of people who had spent their lives listening: silver-braided hair, hands like root systems, eyes that took a moment too long to focus on the surface world because they had spent most of their years aimed inward, at vibration.

Lena laid the printout on the table between them. Paper, because paper could not leak. The Vantage's demand translated twice—once into standard script, once into the harmonic notation Yeva's people used when they needed to see what they usually only felt.

Yeva read it the way she read everything: slowly, with her fingertips resting on the page, as if the words might vibrate differently if you gave them time.

"One voice," she said at last, in the trade-speech they shared, the pidgin of gesture and borrowed words they had built over months. "Two hundred years of quiet. And your machine wants me to sing."

"It doesn't want singing. It wants answering. Prompts, responses. It will measure everything."

"Then it will hear everything." Yeva looked up, and there was no fear in her face, which frightened Lena more than fear would have. There was something older than fear—something like a woman standing at the edge of a field she had been told all her life was poisoned, deciding to walk into it barefoot. "Do you know what my grandmother said about this day? Not this day. This kind of day. She said: *the hiding is also a death, only slower.*" Her hand moved, and the chamber rail hummed a low agreement that Lena felt in her sternum. "We hid so well we almost disappeared even from ourselves. The children learn the song before the speech. Some of them can't speak Ashfall anymore—not really. Their mouths have forgotten the shapes."

"And yours haven't."

"No." Something moved behind Yeva's eyes, some grief with a long tail. "Mine haven't. That is why it has to be me. That is why I hate it." She pressed her palm flat against the table, and Lena felt the question arrive through the wood before she heard it in air: *Will your framework hold?*

"I don't know," Lena said, because Yeva deserved truth more than comfort, and because lying to this woman had become the one lie Lena refused to commit. "I built it as well as I know how to build anything. The registration makes the language legal. The surrender makes the protocol suspend. But verification—" She stopped. Started again. "Verification means trusting a machine that was built by the same government that wrote the purge order. I can't promise you what it will decide. I can only promise you that if it decides wrong, it will decide wrong with both of us standing in the way."

Yeva studied her for a long moment. Then she did something she had never done in all the months Lena had come below: she reached across the table and took Lena's hand, and spoke directly into her skin, in the old fleet-tongue, the forbidden language, the one that had lived in hiding since before either of them was born.

Lena didn't catch all of it. Her command of spoken Ashfall was scholarly where Yeva's was maternal—she got the shape of it, the weight. *You translated yourself out of your mother's tongue to find us,* the hand said. *Now I will speak my mother's tongue to save us both. We are the same kind of foolish.*

* * *

They had one rotation to prepare, and they spent it in the speaking chamber, and it was the strangest rehearsal in the history of two languages.

The Vantage's prompts would be generated live—that was the point, that was the trap. No script, no safety net. So Lena did what the Corps had trained her for and her conscience had fought for nine years: she became the adversary. She built a model of the module's likely queries from everything the cutter had transmitted since arrival, and she fired them at Yeva in rapid succession, harsh, procedural, deliberately strange.

*State your population. State your location. State your intent toward registered human settlements.*

Yeva answered each one in Ashfall, her voice low and level, and each answer was true, and each answer cost her something visible—a tightening at the jaw, a flicker of the eyelids, the small involuntary flinches of a body performing its own betrayal.

"You're shaking," Lena said, after the twentieth run.

"I am not afraid." Yeva flexed her fingers, watched them tremble, seemed almost curious about it. "This is not fear. This is what two hundred years feels like when it stands up." She looked at Lena. "Your machine will hear the trembling. Will it think the trembling is guilt?"

Lena considered. "No. It will think the trembling is age, or cold, or honesty. Machines that hunt forgery are hunting smoothness. You are not smooth, Yeva. Thank God."

Something like a laugh moved through the older woman—Lena felt it more than heard it, a warmth in the chamber's air. "Smooth," Yeva repeated, tasting the word. "For two hundred years we were smooth. We wore the mountain's voice over our own until our grandchildren thought the mountain was their grandmother." She stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Tomorrow I take the mountain's coat off. If the sky wants to kill us for it, then at least the sky will finally know our name."

That night Lena climbed back to the surface alone and stood outside the door in the salt flats while the twin moons made double shadows of her, and she did the thing she had promised herself she wouldn't do: she calculated odds. Registration validity: strong. Forgery detection: moderate risk. Module interpretation of a live speaker's hesitation: unknown. Unknown, unknown, unknown. She had spent her career turning unknowns into grammar, and here was the largest one of her life, and it would resolve in less than a day, and the resolution would be measured in two hundred lives or in none.

Her hands were shaking too. She noticed it with a strange detachment, the way she'd once noticed snow falling on a window in another life, on another world, in a childhood she had traded away syllable by syllable. *A linguist who loves what she translates stops being reliable.* Her mentor's voice, arriving on schedule, right on time to be useless. She loved them all now—forty-one above, two hundred below—and reliability had left her long ago, and all that remained was this: to aim her unreliability like a weapon and fire it once, straight, at the machine in the sky.

* * *

They ran the cable down the salt flats themselves—Idris splicing junctions with his big careful hands, swearing softly at the cold, refusing help he didn't need. Sefa carried the tool kit and asked no questions, which was its own kind of heartbreak; the girl had learned silence the way colonists learn weather, thoroughly and without complaint. By dusk the cave door had a line to the surface antenna, and the surface antenna had a line to the Vantage, and the whole fragile chain hummed with held breath.

At 1900 station time, Yeva Ashfall walked up out of the earth for the first time in her life.

She had seen the sky before—in childhood, briefly, on sanctioned surface work—but never like this, never standing in the open flats with the wind pulling at her braids and the whole hostile gorgeous dome of it overhead, stars coming out one by one like witnesses assembling. She stopped at the top of the ladder and simply breathed for a moment, and Lena, waiting beside the antenna housing, saw her close her eyes and turn her face upward, and understood that Yeva was doing what her people had done in secret for two hundred years: greeting the sky. Formally. As an equal. As an enemy who might become something else.

"The machine is listening," Lena said gently.

"Let it listen." Yeva opened her eyes. "It should hear how beautiful the world is. Maybe it will feel shame. Machines feel shame. I have decided to believe this."

Idris stood by the generator with his arms crossed, jaw set, radiating the fierce helpless competence of a man whose entire contribution to history was keeping a wire connected. Sefa hovered near him, and when Yeva passed the girl on her way to the microphone, Sefa did something unplanned: she reached out and touched the older woman's sleeve, just for a second, and Yeva covered the girl's hand with her own and squeezed once, and no language on the planet or off it could have said more.

Lena made the call. "Vantage, Meridian Station. Live speaker is present. Proceed with verification."

The reply came back in eleven seconds—eleven seconds across the gulf between a dying colony and a machine that had forgotten it was dying—and then the module began.

Its voice was flat, genderless, patient in the way of things that cannot hurry because they do not exist in time. It spoke first in standard register, then rendered each prompt in the Ashfall phonology Lena had submitted—rendered it competently, correctly, dead as a pressed flower. Hearing her language—Yeva's language—come out of the machine's mouth was like hearing a stranger recite your mother's lullaby with perfect diction and no blood in it.

*State the name of your people.*

Yeva leaned toward the microphone. The wind moved her braids. Somewhere below them, two hundred people were gathered in the speaking chamber, hands on the rails, feeling every vibration of this moment rise through the stone.

"We are the Ashfall," she said, in the fleet-tongue, in the open air, aloud, for the first time in two hundred years. Her voice cracked on the second word and she did not repair the crack. "Fleet Third of the Exodus. We did not die. We came down. We stayed."

The module processed. Eleven seconds. Twenty.

*State your reason for remaining concealed.*

And here it was—the question Lena had dreaded, the one with no safe answer, the one where any lie would ripple through the waveform like a stone in water. Yeva looked at Lena. Lena gave her nothing, because there was nothing to give except what they had agreed in the chamber, in the dark, holding hands across a table: *only truth. Truth is the one thing we cannot be caught forging.*

Yeva turned back to the microphone.

"Because your ancestors tried to kill us," she said. "Because we believed you still wanted to. Because hiding was the only mercy left to us, and we raised our children in it, and we are ashamed of nothing except how good we became at disappearing." A breath. "We are done disappearing."

The flats went silent except for wind. Lena realized she had stopped breathing and made herself start.

*State your intent toward the settlement designated Meridian Station.*

"They are our neighbors," Yeva said. "We listened to them for nine years through the stone—the woman who learned our song without knowing what it was. We could have taken their transmitter. We could have taken their lives. We chose to stay hidden rather than spend theirs to save ours." Her voice steadied, deepened, found the register Lena had heard in the speaking chamber, the one that moved through the chest before it reached the ear. "Whatever your order says we are, we were never that. Verify that. That is what verification is for."

Silence. The module's carrier light pulsed, thinking, weighing, measuring the grain beneath the grammar—the crack in *Ashfall*, the tremor, the terrible unsmooth truth of it.

Forty seconds. Sixty. Idris's knuckles whitened on the generator housing. Sefa had both hands over her mouth.

Ninety seconds. Then the machine spoke, and its flat voice had not changed, and would never change, and somehow that flatness was the most frightening sound Lena had ever heard:

*Speaker verified. Linguistic authenticity confirmed. Emotional signature consistent with declarant testimony. Protocol remains suspended pending final review. End of session.*

End of session. The channel closed. The carrier light died. The antenna stood against the dark, humming faintly, holding nothing.

Yeva stepped back from the microphone. For a moment she just stood there in the moonlight, looking at her own hands as though they belonged to someone new—as though the woman who had hidden for a lifetime had handed her body over, mid-sentence, to a woman who had spoken.

Then her knees buckled, and Lena caught her, and the two of them went down together onto the salt crust, laughing or crying or both, the sound torn and wordless, while above them the Vantage hung in orbit with its verdict suspended like a blade paused mid-fall.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, when Idris had carried Yeva's exhaustion down the ladder on his back and Sefa had brought tea that no one drank—Lena sat alone in the comm room running the session logs one more time, because joy made her suspicious and suspicion had kept everyone alive.

That was when she found it.

Buried in the module's closing telemetry, appended to the verification record like a footnote, a single line of metadata she had missed in the raw feed:

*Anomaly noted: declarant linguistic profile correlates with historical Fleet Third registry. Cross-reference flagged. Forwarded per standing directive.*

Forwarded. Per standing directive. To whom?

She pulled the routing header apart with hands that had gone very steady, the way they always did at the exact center of disaster. The packet had gone nowhere. Nowhere in the sector. Nowhere reachable. The Vantage had forwarded its flag along the last living thread of its connection home—

To Earth's loop. The dead line. The forty-second message that had repeated for decades, decaying, dropping syllables, until it failed entirely and left the cutter autonomous.

Except the loop wasn't dead. The loop had answered.

One packet had come back. One. Compressed, ancient, routed through relay ghosts that should have been dust, carrying a fragment of text that the Vantage had dutifully archived and that Lena now decoded twice, three times, her breath fogging the cold screen.

It was not an order. It was not a verdict.

It was a name, a date, and a single sentence in bureaucratic standard, sent from Earth decades ago by someone who had signed it with a clerk's humble rank—and it was addressed, impossibly, specifically, to whoever might someday read the Vantage's logs.

Lena sat very still in the blue light, listening to the station breathe around her, and read the sentence again, and felt the ground shift beneath two centuries of grief.
