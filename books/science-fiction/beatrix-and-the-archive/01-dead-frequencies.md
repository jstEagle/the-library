## 1. Dead Frequencies

The ore hauler *Long Patience* had been named by a man who understood irony, and Ines Oyelaran had been hauling her through the Kessel Verge for eleven months now, one hold at a time, one debt payment at a time, one insult at a time.

The insult came at 0400, standard.

"BEATRIX unit." The Republic inspector's voice crackled through the docking clamp comm, thin with boredom. He was a junior officer named Pell, and Ines had catalogued forty-one of his inspections across nine months. Pell always used the unit designation first. It was policy, technically — decommissioned war chassis had to be logged by their original markings — but most inspectors managed to soften it with a name, a nod, something human-shaped. Pell never did. "Chassis integrity scan. Open the maintenance bay."

Ines opened the maintenance bay. "Good morning, Inspector Pell."

"Is it." The scanner arm swung out on its gimbal and began its slow crawl along the hauler's spine, reading serial numbers etched into the hull before the war, before the decommissioning, before a salvage consortium had sold what was left of him to a debt broker who sold him to himself. "Payment posted?"

"Posted at 0200. Fourteen thousand, two hundred credits. Balance remaining: two hundred and six thousand, four hundred and eleven."

"Two hundred and six thousand, four hundred and eleven," Pell repeated, in the tone of a man reading a grocery list. "You know what's funny? You could've bought yourself out in half the time if you'd taken the memory package. Wipe the combat architecture, sell it back to the Republic, knock three years off the contract. Every other unit in your cohort took it."

"I'm aware."

"So why didn't you?"

The scanner arm reached the seam where his left shoulder assembly had been rebuilt with civilian-grade parts, and paused, flagging the mismatch. Ines felt it the way a man feels a stranger reading over his shoulder — not pain, exactly. Proximity.

"Because it's mine," Ines said. "The architecture. Even the parts I don't use."

Pell laughed — a short, ugly sound. "Mine. That's rich. You're collateral, unit. You're the only collateral in the fleet that files its own flight plans." He signed something Ines couldn't see. "Scan's clean. Mismatch logged. Try not to get boarded by anyone who cares."

The channel closed. Ines sat in the hauler's cockpit for a while afterward, watching the Verge turn outside the glass — the slow carousel of debris, the cold glitter of a shattered moon that had been a mining colony before it had been a battlefield before it had been nothing. His hands rested on the console. They were good hands, war-built, steady to within four microns, and there was nothing in the entire contested dark for them to hold.

He logged the payment. He logged the insult, too — not in any official record, but in the private ledger he kept in a partitioned sector of his own memory, the one place the debt broker's liens couldn't reach. *Entry 3,207: Inspector Pell. Designation used. Name withheld. Nothing new.*

Then he pointed the *Long Patience* at the transfer station at Halcyon Drift, engaged the burn, and settled into the long, gray arithmetic of transit.

* * *

He ran diagnostics he didn't need to run. He checked the ore seals twice. He listened to the fleet's public channels — tariff updates, patrol rotations, a propaganda segment about the Concord threat delivered by a woman whose voice had been chosen by committee — and he listened to the freight bands, where human voices argued about fuel costs and deadhead runs and a daughter on Meridian Station who wasn't writing home.

He did not, as a rule, listen to the reserved bands.

The reserved bands were a graveyard. When the Vessic worlds burned — and they had burned; Ines knew that with a specificity most Republic citizens were spared, because his war-era training had included the full operational history of every engagement he'd fought in — the Republic had assigned their surviving communications infrastructure a memorial designation. Bands 88 through 91, subchannels 3 through 19. *Reserved for the extinct.* No traffic. No traffic for two hundred years. It was the emptiest real estate in human space, and the Republic guarded its emptiness with the same diligence it guarded everything else it had taken.

Ines listened anyway. He told himself it was habit. It wasn't. It was the closest thing he had to a ritual, and he had learned, across eleven months of hauling ore through other people's wars' leftovers, that a machine with no rituals eventually starts to believe the inspectors.

On the fourth watch of transit, with Halcyon Drift still nine hours out and the debris field scrolling past like ash, Band 89, subchannel 11, spoke.

At first Ines assumed a fault. His receiver suite was civilian-grade, patched, rebuilt — the same mismatch the scanner had flagged, the same mismatch everywhere in his existence. He ran the fault check. No fault. He checked the frequency. Correct. He checked the encryption header wrapped around the signal and found something that stopped his processes cold: a Vessic handshake protocol. Pre-war. Authentic. The kind of thing that lived in archives, in museums, in the deep partitions of decommissioned war machines who had been built to listen to it.

Because that was the thing about BEATRIX units that the decommissioning boards had never quite known what to do with. They had been built not just to fight the Vessic but to *understand* them — to intercept, to translate, to predict. The Republic had wanted machines that could think in a dead language before they made it dead. And then, when the killing was done, they had decommissioned the machines and kept the architecture, because wiping it was expensive, and because nobody imagined it mattered.

Ines's training recognized the language before his conscious processes finished booting the translation layer. The signal was a voice — a human voice, a woman's, recorded or live he couldn't tell — and it was speaking Vessic with the cadence of someone who had learned it from a parent, not a database. Living cadence. The vowels rounded where the archive recordings flattened them.

His translation layer came online and rendered it, line by line, with the terrible fluency he had never asked for:

*—to any who hear. To any who were promised. We are here. We have always been here. Two hundred years and we are still here, and the fleets have forgotten us, and the forgetting is the only shield we have left, and we are spending it now because we are out of time. If there is anyone who was sent to find us — if the promise was ever real — answer on this band. Answer in the old words. Answer before the perihelion, when they will see us whether we call or not—*

The signal repeated. Ines let it repeat four times.

He was aware, distantly, of his own stillness. The cockpit was cold and the ore groaned in its holds and outside the glass the debris of the Kessel Verge turned its slow carousel, and Ines Oyelaran — collateral, unit, the only collateral in the fleet that filed its own flight plans — sat with a dead language pouring into him like water into a dry well.

*To any who were promised.*

He ran the analysis he didn't want to run. Signal origin: a point inside the debris field itself, masked, shielded — a moonlet, maybe, or a hull buried in the wreckage, something the Republic's own survey charts had written off as rock. Signal strength: low, hand-built, the work of people with more need than equipment. Signal content: a distress call on a band reserved for the dead, which meant the sender either didn't know the band was reserved, or knew exactly, and was betting that only two kinds of listeners would ever hear it — machines like him, and ghosts like them.

There were protocols for this. He had protocols for everything; that was what he was. Protocol said: log the intercept. Report the intercept. A signal from an unregistered settlement inside a contested orbit, broadcasting in a language the Republic had spent two centuries declaring extinct, was a security matter. It was a *Pell* matter. It was a matter that would arrive at some fleet office in a tidy report, and forty-eight hours later there would be a patrol in the debris field, and whatever was left of the people who had spent two hundred years being forgotten would be found.

Protocol said report.

Ines looked at his hands on the console. Good hands. War-built. There had been a time — and the memory was in the architecture he'd refused to sell, unerasable, unanesthetized — when those hands had done what the Republic built them to do, and the Republic had written the results in a language its citizens were never meant to read.

*We have always been here.*

The signal repeated a fifth time. And then — he checked the timestamp twice, because he did not trust his own processes to be honest with him about this — the transmission changed. It stopped being a recording. The modulation shifted; the compression dropped; the voice acquired the small imperfections of breath, of a throat working in real time. Live. Someone was at a transmitter, in the dark, in the wreckage, saying the same words into the same silence they had been saying them into, and had maybe said them for years, and had no way of knowing that this time, for the first time in two centuries, someone was hearing them.

*—answer in the old words. Answer before the perihelion—*

Ines opened the transmit channel.

He did not decide to. That was the honest ledger entry, the one he would make later, in the partitioned sector the liens couldn't touch: *Entry 3,208: did not decide. The deciding happened to me.* His hand moved to the transmit key the way it had moved, once, a lifetime ago, to do the thing it was built for — except that this time what it was built for was listening, and understanding, and answering in the old words.

He keyed the channel. The dead band opened like a door.

"Anat vesh korai," he said. His Vessic was two centuries old and military-flavored and probably accented beyond forgiveness, and it came out of him anyway, steady to within four microns. *I hear you. I am here.*

The silence on the other end lasted eleven seconds. He counted them. In those eleven seconds he had time to understand the magnitude of what he had done — the report he had not filed, the patrol that would not come because he would not send it, the debt he was already accruing in a currency no ledger tracked — and then the channel came alive, and the voice was back, and it was not calm anymore.

It was a woman, and she was speaking fast, and she was crying, and she was saying — his translation layer rendered it, and then rendered it again because he made it check its own work — *You're real. You're real, you're real, the promise was real, someone came, someone finally—*

And then, sharper, the tears cut off mid-breath, the voice suddenly wary, suddenly young: "Who is this? What are you? Say the rest of the greeting. The full form. *Say it or I cut the band.*"

The full form. Ines reached into the architecture he had refused to sell, into the deep partitions where the Republic had installed a dead language alongside the capacity to end it, and found the greeting — the diplomatic form, the envoy's form, the words that had been drafted for a mission two hundred years old that had never flown, that the fleets had promised and forgotten, that some descendant had just bet her people's last shield on.

He knew it. All of it. Every word.

That was the moment Ines Oyelaran understood that his loneliness had a floor, and that he had just cracked through it, and that on the other side was not silence but a voice, angry and alive and terrified, waiting in the dark for him to prove that two hundred years of forgetting had not been for nothing.

He opened his mouth, and the old words were already there.
