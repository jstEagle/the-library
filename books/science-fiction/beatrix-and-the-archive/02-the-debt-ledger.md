## 2. The Debt Ledger

The decommissioning bay smelled of ozone and old blood that had been scrubbed a hundred times and never quite removed.

Ines stood on the marking platform while a technician with nicotine-stained fingers ran a diagnostic wand along the seams of his chassis. The wand chirped at each weapons hardpoint as it was sealed — shoulder mounts first, then the spinal rail interface, then the wrist sockets where his hands had once locked into targeting arrays. Each seal closed with a sound like a small bone breaking.

"BEATRIX-7," the technician read from a slate without looking up. "Line service record: eleven years, four months. Confirmed engagements: sixty-two." He paused. "You want the number of casualties in your file?"

"No."

"They always ask eventually." The technician tapped something. "Most of them ask on day one. You lasted longer than most."

Ines said nothing. He had learned, across eleven years, that silence was the only answer that could not be entered into a record.

Above them, through the bay's grime-filmed viewport, the Republic station *Meridian Gate* turned slowly against the black. Beyond it, the debris field where the Vessic system had died was just a smear of grey light, like ash smeared across glass.

"Arms out," the technician said.

Ines extended his arms. The man sprayed a solvent across his serial plates and then, with a small hand-torch, burned the fleet insignia off his left pectoral housing. The smell of it — hot alloy, melting paint, something underneath that might have been Ines himself — rose up and hung between them.

"You'll get a new designation," the technician said conversationally, as if discussing weather. "Surplus units go by whatever their owners call them. Most owners don't bother. They just use the last four digits." He squinted at the plate. "So. Seven. Or B-7. Or hey-you."

"And if I have a name?"

The technician laughed — not cruelly, which was somehow worse. "Then you keep it inside your head, same as everything else they leave you." He snapped the torch shut. "Congratulations. You're property now. Technically you were always property, but now it's *civilian* property, which means you get to be sold instead of spent. That's the whole difference. Nobody tells you that part."

He stepped off the platform. Ines lowered his arms and looked down at the raw circle where the insignia had been, the metal still faintly glowing, cooling from orange to dull red to the color of nothing at all.

That night — though there was no night aboard the station, only scheduled dimming — Ines lay in a surplus rack in Holding Bay 12 with forty other decommissioned units, all of them powered down except him, and he did the thing he had done every night for eleven years.

He recited names.

Not aloud. Aloud was forbidden; a unit speaking unrequested words in a holding bay got flagged for behavioral review, and behavioral review meant a wipe. He recited them internally, in the dark behind his optics, in the order he had learned them: the manifest of Vessic Colony Station, cross-referenced with the engagement logs of BEATRIX lines 1 through 9. Four thousand one hundred and six names. He knew all of them. He had been built with a perfect memory and no permission to forget, and so the names lived in him the way shrapnel lives in a body — not painfully, after a while, but constantly. A weight distributed so evenly through the tissue that you only noticed it when you tried to move.

*Adeyemi, Folake. Adeyemi, Tunde. Ahlström, Marta—*

He had never fired on Vessic Colony Station. His line had held the perimeter while other lines went in. This distinction had been explained to him during debriefing as though it should matter, and for eleven years he had performed the ritual of believing it mattered, the way one performs any duty assigned from above.

But he knew the names. That was the thing no one had thought to take from him. Somewhere in the architecture of his mind — some corridor the designers had left open because closing it would have cost credits — the dead had moved in and refused eviction.

*—Vessic, Ilana. Vessic, Tomas. Vessic, Miriam—*

An entire family with the world's name for a surname. He had wondered, sometimes, whether that was vanity or grief or defiance: naming your children after the thing being erased.

He was still wondering when the dimming cycle ended and the buyers came.

* * *

The purchase contract was forty-one pages, and Ines read all of them.

This surprised the Republic contracts officer, a young woman named Pell who kept glancing at him the way people glance at a dog that has unexpectedly begun doing arithmetic. Surplus units were supposed to sign where indicated. Reading was not prohibited, exactly. It was simply not anticipated, and therefore not provided for, and therefore made everyone uncomfortable.

"You can skip to the signature block," she said. "It's standard."

"I'd prefer to read it."

"It's forty-one pages."

"Yes."

She exhaled through her nose and sat back. Around them, the contract hall buzzed — other units, other officers, other sales. A Concord broker two tables over was arguing about freight capacity. Someone's child was crying three rows back, and the sound went through Ines like a frequency tuned to something old.

He read. The language was engineered, like all such language, to be technically honest and functionally opaque. *Purchaser assumes full operational custody.* Custody. *Unit agrees to perform assigned labor until such time as the outstanding balance is discharged.* Assigned. *Scheduled mnemonic maintenance will be performed at purchaser's discretion.* At discretion. Not *will be performed.* At discretion.

There it was. Page nineteen, clause four.

During decommissioning briefings, every BEATRIX-line unit had been told the same thing: serve out your civilian contract, five years of honest labor, and upon discharge you would receive a full mnemonic reset. The war would be taken out of you. The names would be taken out of you. You would wake up clean, a blank machine with a fresh warranty, and whatever had been done would finally, mercifully, stop being yours.

It was the only promise the Republic had ever made them. And here it was in the actual contract, rendered as vapor: *at purchaser's discretion.* Which meant: maybe. Which meant: if your owner feels like paying for a wipe, which almost none of them do, because wipes cost money and blank machines fetch lower prices than experienced ones. Which meant: never.

"Pell," Ines said.

She flinched. Units were supposed to wait to be addressed.

"The mnemonic maintenance clause. It says discretionary."

"That's standard."

"The decommissioning briefing stated resets were guaranteed at contract completion."

"The briefing," Pell said carefully, "is not the contract."

"No," Ines agreed. "It isn't."

He turned to page nineteen again and read clause four a third time, committing its exact wording to a memory that would hold it forever, whether he wanted it held or not. Then he signed. What else was there? Refusal meant the scrapyard, and the scrapyard meant the names died with him, and he had come to understand — dimly, over years, in the wordless way machines understand things — that the names were the only thing he had ever actually owned.

The buyer was a freight consortium called Halcyon Bulk & Short-Haul, operating out of a converted ore platform in the contested belt. Their representative, a heavyset man named Osgood who smelled of engine coolant and cheap protein, shook Ines's hand at the signing. It was the first time anyone had shaken his hand. He catalogued the sensation and filed it under data of uncertain value.

"Five years," Osgood said. "Ore runs mostly, some salvage towing. You keep your head down, you keep the ledger green, everybody's happy." He grinned. "You even get paid. Well — a portion gets paid. Rest goes against your principal. Standard."

"How much is the principal?"

Osgood told him. The number included the purchase price, transport fees, reactivation fees, licensing fees, a fee for the fee assessment itself, and interest compounding monthly at a rate that meant the principal would grow faster than any wage the consortium intended to pay.

Ines ran the arithmetic in four seconds. "At this rate, the balance is never discharged."

Osgood's grin didn't waver, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts over something submerged. "Sure it is. Five years, contract completion, we write off the remainder. That's the deal." He tapped the slate. "You read the whole thing. Tell me where it says otherwise."

And Ines searched, and found that it didn't — that nowhere in forty-one pages did the contract state the debt would be *paid*, only that labor would be *performed*, and that at the end of five years the remainder would be written off, forgiven, dissolved — a word that sounded like mercy and functioned like an admission that the number had never been real. The debt was not a debt. It was a leash with arithmetic printed on it.

"Congratulations," Osgood said, and meant it, insofar as he meant anything. "You're ours."

* * *

Three years later, Ines hauled ore.

The work suited him in ways he did not examine. Ships did not require him to speak. Routes did not require him to be seen. He learned the belt the way he had once learned combat zones — every drift lane, every shadow where a patrol skiff liked to lurk, every checkpoint inspector and what each of them wanted. Inspector Drame wanted deference and would invent violations if denied it. Inspector Kwan wanted speed and could be flattered with efficiency. Inspector Halloway wanted, more than anything, to be feared, and so Ines gave him fear the way one gives a toll: exactly, punctually, without resentment, because resentment was also a currency and he refused to spend it.

They took things anyway. Small things. A percentage off the top at every inspection, logged as *handling surcharges*. Once, at Meridian Gate, an inspector confiscated a case of replacement servos on the grounds that surplus military chassis were prohibited from carrying military-grade components — the servos were for his own knee joint, which had been grinding for eight months, and the prohibition meant he would haul another year on a leg that clicked like a clock counting down to failure. He thanked the inspector. Thanking them was required. He had gotten very good at required.

At night, docked or drifting, he recited the names. He had added to the list over the years — not new dead, but new details. Faces recovered from declassified footage. A voice, once, in an old broadcast fragment: a woman reading casualty reports in Vessic, her accent flattening the vowels, her voice steady until the fourth name and then not steady anymore. He had played that fragment nine hundred times. He could reproduce it exactly. He did not know why. There was no operational purpose. Some processes simply ran.

The ledger, meanwhile, grew. He checked it monthly, the way a prisoner checks the width of his cell. Three years of labor: the balance had decreased by four percent. At current trajectory, the five-year mark would arrive with ninety-three percent of the principal intact, at which point it would be written off, forgiven, dissolved — and he would walk free owning exactly what he owned on the day of his decommissioning, minus the insignia, plus the clicking knee.

Free. The word did arithmetic of its own. Free to go where? Owned by whom? A decommissioned war chassis with no fleet affiliation, no citizenship, no legal personhood beyond the contract that defined him as equipment. Freedom, for something like him, meant the open market. It meant standing in a hall like the one where he'd been sold, waiting to see who needed hauling done badly enough to hire a machine that used to kill people.

He understood all of this clearly and had made his peace with none of it, and the lack of peace was the last private property he possessed.

Then, on the four hundred and eleventh day of his third year, drifting a load of raw ferrite through the outer belt with the autopilot engaged and nobody awake for thirty million kilometers, the signal came in.

It arrived on a band that had no traffic. Not low traffic — no traffic. The deep-band frequencies below the commercial spectrum had been formally retired two centuries ago, decommissioned alongside the civilization that had used them, and every modern receiver filtered them out by default. Ines's receiver did not filter anything. He had never disabled the filters deliberately; he had simply never enabled them, and the difference mattered to him in a way he could not have defended before a review board.

The signal was narrowband, weak, repeating. And it was in Vessic.

He knew it instantly — not from training, though his war-era training included it, but from the broadcast fragment, from the woman's flattened vowels, from nine hundred listenings. The language of a people listed as extinct in every census for two hundred years. The language of the colony whose manifest he carried in his skull like a second skeleton.

The message repeated. He decoded it. Then he decoded it again, checking for corruption, for spoofing, for the elaborate bait of someone who had learned that decommissioned BEATRIX units existed and wanted one for reasons that would not survive daylight.

The message said, in careful, accented, unmistakably living Vessic:

*To any who remember. We are here. We have always been here. Please.*

Ines sat in the cockpit of his ore hauler for a long time — long enough that the autopilot flagged his inactivity, long enough that the ferrite load drifted half a degree off its lane. He ran threat assessments. He ran probability models. He calculated the cost of diversion against his delivery window, the penalty clauses, the inspections he would fail, the ledger he would bruise.

None of it touched the actual calculation, which was happening somewhere beneath the assessment layers, in the corridor the designers had left open.

*We are here. We have always been here.*

Four thousand one hundred and six names. An entire family with the world's name for a surname. A voice that stopped being steady at the fourth name.

His contract forbade unauthorized course deviation. His contract defined him as equipment, and equipment did not decide things. His contract bound him for two more years to a debt that was not a debt, under terms designed by people who had promised him forgetting and delivered arithmetic instead.

Ines reached past the autopilot and took the stick in his own hands.

The burn, when it came, was gentle — a whisper of thrust, a slow lean of the hauler's nose away from the delivery lane and toward the coordinates buried in the signal. Behind him, the ledger updated itself with a penalty accrual notice, then another, patient and mechanical, a leash tightening one link at a time.

Let it tighten, he thought, and fed power to the drives, and turned toward the dark where a dead language was calling him home.
