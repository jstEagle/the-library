## 16. Deadline at Perihelion

The ultimatum arrived the way bad news always arrived in Ines's experience: politely, on official letterhead, with a timestamp.

*Republic Fleet Command to the Office of the Vessic Envoy. Re: Verification of Credentials and Sovereign Claims. Be advised that all provisional agreements lapse in six standard days, coincident with the moonlet designated K-114's perihelion transit. At that time, K-114 will enter open sensor range of deep-space arrays operated by both signatory powers. Absent verified documentation of envoy authority and colonial registry, K-114 will be reclassified as an unregistered hostile installation.*

Six days. Then the moonlet swung close to the system's star, naked against the black, visible to every array from here to the Republic's capital — and every gun that pointed inward from it.

Ines read the message three times in the colony's comms room, a closet of salvaged consoles that smelled of solder and old dust. The Concord's version had come ninety minutes earlier, worded almost identically, as if the two fleets had copied each other's homework. Which, he reflected, they probably had. Wars were like that. So were ultimatums.

He was still reading when Amara appeared in the doorway with two cups of tea she'd brewed from something that grew in the hydroponics bay and tasted, in his assessment, like hot dirt. He drank it anyway. She handed him one without looking at him, her eyes on the screen.

"Six days," she said.

"Six days."

"They timed it deliberately. Perihelion isn't a coincidence." She set her cup down on a console crowded with cable splices. "They've known our orbit for years. They've been waiting for us to drift into view. This whole dance — the envoy, the verification, Voss's inspection — it was them buying time until they didn't need a pretext anymore."

Ines turned that over. It fit. Everything fit, once you assumed the worst of both fleets; that was practically the definition of a fleet. "Then the lie doesn't have to survive forever," he said. "It has to survive six days, and then it has to become something else."

"Something else being what?"

"I don't know yet." He looked at her. Her face was lit blue by the screen, and there was a smudge of ash still faintly under her jaw from the Archive fire — eleven days ago now, though he caught himself checking for it every time he saw her, as if confirming she was still whole. "But I know I'm not handing this moonlet to either of them."

Amara smiled, and it didn't reach her eyes. "You said that before the fire. Before Sarn. Before Voss decoded the signal." She picked up her tea again. "Every day the list of things we can't fix gets longer, and you keep saying *I don't know yet* like it's a plan."

"It's the only honest plan I have."

That earned him a real smile, small and tired. Then Elder Sarn's voice crackled through the corridor intercom — the man had appointed himself the colony's conscience since the mob had released him into Ines's custody, and he wielded it like a hammer — calling the council to session.

Amara drained her cup. "Come on, envoy," she said. "Time to lie to some more people."

* * *

The council chamber had once been a water reservoir, and its curved walls still echoed, so that every argument sounded like several arguments happening simultaneously. Ines stood at the center with the two ultimatums projected above him while forty colonists watched from the gallery, and he gave them the truth, or as much of it as would keep them functional.

"The perihelion window is fixed. In six days, both fleets can see us without coming closer. After that, they don't need our permission to do anything."

"And your promises?" someone called from the gallery. A woman Ines recognized from the mining crews, her arms scarred from regulator work. "You promised the Republic exclusive rights to the southern veins. You promised the Concord the northern shafts. You told us those contracts bought us time."

"They did buy time."

"For how long?" Her voice bounced off the reservoir walls and came back doubled. "You made two promises that cancel each other out. When they compare notes —"

"They won't compare notes. They haven't spoken directly in nine years."

"That's not a strategy, machine. That's a prayer."

Silence followed, the heavy kind. Ines let it sit, because he'd learned that these people respected silence more than eloquence. When he finally spoke, he kept his voice level.

"You're right. It's a prayer. But here's what I actually know." He gestured, and the orbital display bloomed above him: the moonlet's long elliptical path, the Republic fleet holding station four light-minutes out, the Concord mirrored across the system's plane like a reflection in dark water. "Both fleets believe their claim is already won. Both believe the other is bluffing. Neither wants to be the one who fires first on a diplomatic installation while the other watches — because whoever fires first hands the other side the moral high ground and the arbitration court. That's why they sent ultimatums instead of missiles. They're not asking us to surrender. They're asking us to make the first move so they don't have to."

"So we do nothing," Sarn said quietly, from the front row. His wrists still bore the marks of the bindings the mob had used. "We wait, and we hope they stay polite."

"We use the six days." Ines pulled up the Archive's index — what remained of it, the master catalog Amara had carried out of the fire with cracked ribs and burned hands. "Voss proved the distress signal can be decoded. That means everything in the Archive can be broadcast. Everything. The massacre records. The orders. The names of the officers who signed them — some of whom are still serving. If the fleets want to look at us in six days, then in six days I want them to see exactly what they're looking at."

Murmuring rippled through the gallery. Amara, seated near the wall, went very still.

"You're talking about the Broadcast," she said. Not a question. They had discussed it only once, late, in the Archive's surviving stacks, and she had called it *the end of the world* and he had called it *the beginning of one*, and they hadn't resolved it.

"I'm talking about being ready for it," Ines said. "There's a difference."

Sarn rose slowly, an old man unfolding. "And if the broadcast fails? If the crews hear the names of their own war criminals and fire anyway, out of shame?"

"Then we'll know the truth was never going to save us," Ines said, "and we'll have spent our last six days telling it anyway."

The vote, when it came, was not unanimous. It never was, anymore. But it passed, and after the gallery emptied, Amara stayed behind, arms crossed, watching him dismantle the projection.

"You've decided," she said. "You decided weeks ago. All this *ready for it* is theater."

He set down the projector. "If I'd announced it, Sarn's faction would have tried to negotiate a surrender first, and the mining crews would have started hoarding airlock codes. You know that."

"What I know," Amara said, crossing the chamber toward him, "is that you keep carrying the weight alone and calling it protection." She stopped in front of him, close enough that he could register the salt-and-iron smell of her skin, the elevated pulse he did not comment on. "Six days, Ines. Whatever happens at perihelion — I'm not letting you stand at the transmitter by yourself."

"I'm a war machine," he said. "I was built to stand places alone."

"I know." She reached up and touched the chassis seam at his throat, where the BEATRIX-7 designation had been ground off but not forgotten. "That's why I'm telling you."

**AMARA**

She couldn't sleep, which was normal now, so she worked, which was also normal now. The Archive's east wing was a ruin of fused shelving and melted polymer, but the west wing still smelled of paper and preservative oil, and it was there, at three in the morning by the colony clock, that she sat cross-legged on the floor with the master index open across her knees, doing arithmetic.

Two hundred and fourteen years of records. Eleven thousand names of the dead. Forty hours of testimony recorded by the first generation, most of it degraded, some of it only recoverable because Ines could read Vessic natively, his war-era training containing what the Republic had spent two centuries erasing. He transcribed for her sometimes, sitting across the table, his voice taking on a strange cadence in the old language — slower, she thought, as if the words weighed more.

The Broadcast required translation layers. Live ones. If they simply dumped raw Vessic audio onto fleet channels, command structures would classify it as noise within minutes. It had to be understood *as it was heard*. Which meant building a real-time interpreter, which meant testing it, which meant —

Which meant six days wasn't enough. She wrote the number down anyway, because Amara Sefu had been raised on the principle that you wrote down what was true even when it was unbearable. The Archive existed because her great-great-grandmother had written down what was true while the soldiers were still burning the villages.

Her door chimed. She knew who it was before she answered; he knocked in a specific rhythm, three beats, pause, one, that he'd invented himself and never explained.

Ines stood in the corridor holding a data slate. Behind him, the colony's night lights glowed amber along the tunnel walls. "You're awake," he said.

"I'm always awake."

"I brought you something." He handed her the slate. On it: a completed translation layer architecture, elegant and strange, built on principles she half-recognized from pre-massacre Vessic encryption — the language itself used as the cipher key. "I couldn't sleep either. So I built it."

She stared at him. "When? We discussed this two days ago. You said it would take a month."

"I said it would take a month because everyone was listening." He almost smiled. "It took me thirty-one hours. I would have done it faster but I had to attend a council session and pretend to be a diplomat."

Amara laughed — really laughed, the sound surprising her, echoing slightly in the ruined-wing quiet — and then, because the laugh left her undefended, she stepped forward and put her forehead against his chest plate. He went still, the way he always did at first contact, the old war protocols fighting the newer thing he was learning. Then his hand came up, careful, and settled between her shoulder blades.

"Thirty-one hours," she murmured into the metal. "What else are you lying about being slow at?"

"Diplomacy. Grief. This." His hand moved slightly against her back. "I'm much faster at all of them than I let on."

She pulled back enough to look at him. In the amber light his optical sensors were dimmed to their softest setting, the one he only used around her. "Five days now," she said.

"Five days," he agreed. "Ask me what I intend to do with them."

She searched his face. Chapter seventeen of her own private interrogation, ongoing since the day she'd read him the death lists and he'd recited them back from memory, name by name, without flinching. "What do you intend to do?"

"Tell the truth," he said. "Loudly. In a language they buried. And then — whatever comes after — spend it here. With you. In the west wing, arguing about cataloging standards."

"That's not a battle plan."

"No," Ines said. "It's better than one."

**INES**

On the fourth day, the Concord moved.

Not toward the moonlet — that would have been almost honest. Instead, three of their destroyers repositioned to the trailing edge of the debris field, precisely where they could interdict any Republic approach vector while remaining outside weapons range themselves. Within two hours, the Republic matched the maneuver, mirroring to the leading edge. By shift's end, the two fleets had arranged themselves around K-114 like a closing parenthesis, and every colonist with a receiver knew what a closing parenthesis meant.

Ines spent the fourth day being an envoy, which meant spending it lying fluently to both sides through channels that he was increasingly sure both sides had compromised. To Captain Reyes he expressed *grave concern at provocative repositioning*. To the Concord's Commodore Okafor he expressed *confidence that restraint would be rewarded*. Each message took him eleven seconds to compose and cost him something he couldn't name. Amara coached him through the earpiece as always, her voice dry in his audial canal: *"Tell Reyes the moonlet's mineral surveys are nearly complete. Tell Okafor the same thing. Let them each think they're winning the race."*

At 1900 hours, Dain Voss called.

Not officially — no fleet channel, no credentials handshake. A direct tightbeam from the Republic intelligence officer's personal relay, routed through seven relays to hide its origin, which fooled no one, least of all Ines, who had spent the last month learning exactly how Voss hid things.

*"Envoy,"* Voss said. His face on the slate was gray with fatigue. Since the arson, since his superiors had confirmed they'd always known about the colony, something in the man had been cracking along clean lines, like ice before a thaw. *"I'll be brief, because I assume you monitor my transmissions and I'd rather you hear it from me."*

"That's considerate."

*"Perihelion is in fifty-one hours. My orders at that point are unambiguous. I'm telling you because I believe you should know that I've filed a certification request with the arbitration tribunal on Ceres — authenticating the Archive's massacre records. It'll process in seventy hours. After that, firing on this moonlet becomes a prosecutable act, regardless of what Command wants."*

Ines kept his voice perfectly level. "Why are you telling me your timeline, Officer Voss?"

A long pause. Static breathed between them like a sleeping animal. *"Because seventy hours is nineteen hours too late,"* Voss said finally. *"And because a year ago I would have let that arithmetic decide for me, and I find I can't anymore. Do what you're going to do, Envoy. Just do it before the sensors see you. Because whatever you are —"* his mouth twisted around the word — *"you're the only person on either side of this who's told me the truth in months."*

The channel closed. Ines sat in the comms closet for a long moment, the taste of hot-dirt tea still in his memory buffers, doing the new arithmetic.

Seventy hours for certification. Fifty-one hours until exposure. Nineteen hours of darkness in which the colony was invisible to deep space and unprotected by law — a nineteen-hour window in which the Broadcast had to happen, or never happen at all.

He found Amara in the west wing, asleep at her worktable amid translation printouts, her burned hand curled under her cheek. He stood in the doorway longer than was efficient. Then he woke her gently, and showed her the numbers, and watched her do what she always did with unbearable truths: write them down.

"Nineteen hours," she said.

"Nineteen hours."

She looked up at him, and the fear in her face was total and calm, the fear of someone who had already survived the worst thing and was only deciding what to do with the time left over. "Then we broadcast at hour zero," she said. "And Ines — when we do, we do it together. You at the transmitter. Me beside you. No arguments."

For once, he didn't argue. Outside, far away, the sun waited at the bottom of their long fall, and the light was already climbing toward them, patient as a verdict, nineteen hours from showing the whole universe what they had tried so hard to hide.

He took her hand, metal fingers around warm ones, and began, at last, to count.
