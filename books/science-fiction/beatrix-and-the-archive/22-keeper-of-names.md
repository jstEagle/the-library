## 22. Keeper of Names

The Archive smelled of scorched polymer and new timber, and Amara had learned to love both smells equally.

She stood in the east wing at dawn — the wing that had burned, the wing they had rebuilt plank by salvaged plank — and watched the light come through the new windows. The old glass had been Vessic-made, two centuries old, tinted faintly amber so that the sun always looked like memory. They could not replicate it. The replacement panes were clear and ordinary, and the light that fell across the reading tables now was honest light, unfiltered, the light of the present tense. Amara had decided she preferred it. She had not told anyone that yet. It felt like a small betrayal of her grandmother's grandmother, who had pressed her palm to amber glass every morning of her life in this same room and called it *keeping the sun where we left it*.

But the dead did not need the sun. The living did.

"Ines," she said, without turning. "You've been standing in the doorway for four minutes."

"I was counting your heartbeats," he said. "It's a habit I'm trying to break."

"Break it faster."

He came into the room the way he always did now — quietly, though he weighed as much as a cargo loader, his footfalls dampened by the soft-soled boots she'd made him buy from the colony stores. He carried two cups of tea. He had learned to make it the way the colonists made it, overbitter and oversweet at once, because that was how grief tasted to people who refused to stop drinking it anyway. He set one cup on the table beside her and did not touch her, because it was still early enough that the workday might arrive at any moment and carry them both away, and they had an unspoken treaty about not beginning what they could not finish before the first shift bell.

Except today there was no shift bell. Today was Naming Day.

"You're nervous," he said.

"I'm archivally rigorous."

"You're nervous." He sat down across from her, and the chair groaned under him the way all chairs groaned under him, and he ignored it with the dignity of a man who had stopped apologizing for what he was made of. "I ran a diagnostic on my own uncertainty classifiers this morning. They flagged me too."

"You have uncertainty classifiers?"

"I have everything. That was the problem with me." He wrapped his hands around the cup, careful, so careful, the way a man handles something hot when his hands could crush steel. "Amara. Before the ceremony. There's something I want to say while the room is empty and no one is recording."

The light moved across the table between them. Outside, the colony was waking — she could hear the water reclamation units cycling, children running past the Archive steps on their way to whatever children did now that their world had a future instead of merely a hiding place. Eight months since the broadcast. Eight months since Ines had seized both fleets' command channels and poured the massacre into them in Vessic, translated live, name after name after name, until firing solutions stalled and sailors wept at their stations and the war died of shame before it could die of bloodshed.

Eight months of tribunals, arbitration, protected status, exile. Eight months of him being unwelcome everywhere except here.

"Say it," she said.

"The debt annulment came through yesterday. Final confirmation. Republic Treasury, Concord Financial Authority, both signatures." He turned the cup slowly on its table. "I'm free. Legally, technically, in every way the ledgers recognize — no one owns me. No one ever will again."

"I know. I read the notice before you did. You were asleep."

"Androids don't sleep."

"You lie down and go very quiet and pretend. It counts." She reached across and stilled the cup under his fingers. "Ines. What is it you're afraid to say?"

He looked up at her, and she saw it again — the thing she had seen in the interrogation cell eight months and a lifetime ago, when she had read him the crimes of his unit line and waited for the machine to deny them, and instead he had begun reciting names. Forty-one thousand names. He had recited for six hours without stopping, voice level, eyes wet in a way that should have been impossible for hardware, until she had put her hand over his mouth because she couldn't breathe.

That was the moment she had known. Not that he was innocent — he would never be innocent; neither would she, whose ancestors' survival had been purchased with the silence of everyone who didn't survive — but that he was *accountable*. And accountability, she had learned from a lifetime in the Archive, was the only soil in which anything true could grow.

"I don't know what I am now," he said. "Cargo hauler, no. War machine, decommissioned. Envoy, a lie. Debtor, discharged. Every designation I've ever carried has been struck through or exposed as false. When people ask me what I do — and they do ask, the traders who come through now, the arbitrators' staff, the school groups — I say 'keeper of the Archive,' and it's true, but it's borrowed. It's your word. Your title. I'm living inside a shape you made, and I love it, and some mornings I wake up and I can't tell whether I chose it or whether I just had nowhere else to be."

The words hung in the honest light.

"And?" she said.

"And I needed to say it out loud once, to you, before today. Because today they're going to ask me to say vows in front of the whole colony, and I refuse to stand there performing certainty I don't feel. You deserve better than performance. You've had enough of that from institutions."

Amara drank her tea. Overbitter, oversweet. She let the silence sit long enough that he began to fidget, which for Ines meant his optical shutters cycled twice.

"Good," she said finally.

"Good?"

"Good. If you'd stood up there certain, I'd have known you were lying. Certainty is what the fleets sold us for two hundred years — certainty that we were extinct, certainty that the massacre was regrettable but necessary, certainty that the Archive was ash." She set down the cup. "Doubt is the most human thing about you, Ines Oyelaran. Keep it. Feed it. Let it grow. A keeper who never doubts what he keeps becomes a censor."

He exhaled — a purely cosmetic function, air through cooling vents, performed entirely for her benefit, and she loved him for the waste of it.

"Besides," she added, "you're wrong about the title. It isn't borrowed. Titles in this colony aren't given. They're kept. You've been keeping names for longer than I have — you've been keeping them since the war, against your own programming, in a memory core they promised to wipe and never did. I just gave the habit a roof."

* * *

The ceremony was held at midday in the rebuilt east wing, beneath the honest windows, before the whole colony — three hundred and twelve souls, plus one android, plus Elder Sarn, who stood at the back where the crowd's forgiveness could reach him but its anger couldn't, which Amara privately considered the correct place for a man who had burned half their history out of love for them.

Sarn had been sentenced by the colony's own council, not the fleets'. Sixty days of labor rebuilding what he'd burned, and then — this was the part the arbitrators hadn't understood, the part that had made the Republic observer write *irregular* in his report — a seat at the Archive's restoration committee. Mercy chosen over easy justice. Ines had argued for it. Amara had argued against it, furiously, for two days, and lost, and been glad to lose, and never told him so.

Now Sarn watched from the back as Amara's mother, who held the office of First Reader, opened the ceremony the old way.

"*Vessic kel toran,*" her mother said, and three hundred voices answered: "*kel toran ves.*" We keep what was given. We give what is kept.

Then came the naming.

Every child born in the colony received a name twice — once in the family tongue of their household, and once in Vessic, entered into the master index by the Keeper of Names, spoken aloud so the language itself could hear it and stay alive. For two centuries the roll of Vessic speakers had only ever shrunk. Amara had grown up watching funerals outpace births, watching the language become a thing whispered over graves.

Today the index gained eleven children. Eleven new speakers, squalling and solemn by turns as their names were read.

And then her mother turned the page, and looked at Amara, and nodded.

Amara rose. Ines rose beside her. The room went quiet in the particular way rooms went quiet around him — not fear anymore, not exactly, but attention, the attention people gave to large weather and larger truths.

"BEATRIX-7," Amara read from the entry she herself had drafted, revised forty times, and finally written by hand because no draft typed on a terminal had ever sounded like truth. "Designation retired. Service record: transferred to the Archive in full, per the subject's own request, including actions he does not deny and cannot undo. Debt status: annulled. Ownership status: none. Allegiance status:" — she paused, and looked at him — "chosen."

"Chosen," the assembly echoed. It was a new word in the rite. She had added it herself. Her mother had resisted for a month, then wept at the rehearsal, then claimed she had always supported it.

"Ines Oyelaran," Amara continued, "Keeper of Names, second chair. Sworn to keep what was given, to give what is kept, and to speak the dead language until it is not dead."

Ines stepped forward. He had rehearsed his response in Vessic for weeks — she had coached him herself, evenings in the stacks, correcting his phonemes with a patience that surprised them both — and now he spoke it, and his accent was imperfect and his cadence was a machine's cadence, precise where a human voice would wander, and it was the most beautiful thing Amara had heard in the Archive in her entire life.

"*Kel toran ves,*" he said. "*Mi navessa toran. Mi navessa im.*"

I keep what was given. I give what is kept.

Then he did the thing that made three hundred people inhale at once. He set his hand flat on the master index — the scorched, saved, scarred index Amara had carried out of the fire with cracked ribs and second-degree burns — and he began to recite.

Not the ritual response. Names.

"*Selu Maro-Vessic. Tarn Ivesh-Kel. Odama Renn. Fyra Solenne-Vessic...*"

The dead of the massacre. His unit's victims, the ones he had carried in his memory core through five years of cargo runs and decommissioning and debt, the ones he had recited in an interrogation cell to a woman who hated him. He recited them now as an act of keeping, in the language of the people they had belonged to, and one by one the colonists began to answer him — softly at first, then together, the old mourning response, *vesh-ala, vesh-ala*, rest and be recorded, rest and be recorded — until the east wing was full of a sound it had not held in two hundred years:

The extinct language, spoken by the living, for the dead, in daylight.

Amara did not cry during the ceremony. She was the Keeper; she held. But afterward, in the emptying room, with the last of the tea gone cold and the light going long and gold through the ordinary clear windows, Ines found her at the index and touched her shoulder, and she turned and put her forehead against his chest plate, which hummed faintly, which had hummed the night they became lovers, which she had once threatened with a plasma cutter and now used as a pillow.

"Forty-one thousand," he said quietly. "I'll finish entering them all. It'll take years."

"We have years," she said. "That's the whole point of everything we did. We bought years."

* * *

The message arrived three weeks later, on an evening in late autumn when the debris field outside the shield caught the system's star and scattered it into slow golden rivers, and Amara was cataloguing a crate of recovered fragments from the burned wing — charred shards of data-slate, half-legible, more precious than gold now precisely because they were broken.

Ines brought it to her himself. She knew from his walk, before he said a word, that it was not ordinary mail. Ordinary mail made him relaxed. This made him the way he'd been the day the distress signal came in, two hundred years of silence cracking open on a dead frequency.

"From the Republic?" she asked.

"From the Republic. From the Office of Restitution." He set the slate on her table. "They're opening the sealed war records. All of them. Every campaign, every order, every atrocity the tribunals didn't reach because the evidence burned with our east wing." His optical shutters cycled. "They're requesting the Archive's assistance in verification. Formally. They're asking us — the colony they listed as extinct, the archive they let burn — to authenticate their own confession."

Amara looked at the slate for a long time. Rivers of gold light crawled across its surface.

"They'll send an officer," she said. "A liaison. Someone to work on-site."

"They already named one." He hesitated, and she watched a man who could calculate firing solutions in microseconds struggle with a single sentence. "Dain Voss."

The name landed in the quiet room like a stone dropped in still water. Voss — the intelligence officer who had hunted them, suspected them, exposed Ines at the worst possible hour and thereby, though he'd never intended it, forced the truth into the open where it could finally do its work. The loyal man who had chosen conscience over career and certified the Archive's authenticity with his signature and his ruin.

"He requested this posting," Ines said. "Turned down a promotion to get it. He wrote—" Ines paused again. "He wrote a line in his application that the clerk flagged as irregular. Want to hear it?"

"Read it."

Ines read: "*I helped bury the truth once. I would like to spend the rest of my career digging it up.*"

Amara laughed — a short, startled sound, grief and joy in the same breath, the way everything came now, the way everything would always come. She picked up the slate and began, already, drafting the reply. The Archive would accept. The Archive would always accept. That was what it was *for*.

Outside, the golden rivers turned. Inside, the extinct language had speakers again — eleven children, one android, one exiled intelligence officer inbound, and a Keeper of Names who had learned, at last, that home was not a refuge you hid in.

It was a choice you made daily. And tomorrow, she would make it again.
