## 15. The Gray Rain, Again

Ilsa Reyes had spent forty years of her life—well, thirty-eight of them biologically, but who was counting anymore—learning to read the sound of a ship. The Halcyon had hummed in a particular key when its engines were happy. It had groaned in C minor when the gravity plating needed attention. And now, standing in the seed vault's third sublevel with her hand pressed against the cold wall, she heard the sound she'd been dreading for six days: the specific, arrhythmic thud of boots that did not belong.

"They're through the second bulkhead," she said into the comm. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which she supposed was the one useful thing medical training had ever given her. "Marguerite, how much longer on the archive seal?"

"Four minutes." Marguerite's voice crackled through, frayed at the edges. "The lock wants two biometric confirmations and it keeps rejecting my thumbprint like I'm a stranger in my own house."

"You are a stranger in your own house," said Tam's small voice from somewhere near the comm. "You said so yourself. You said this vault belongs to the seeds."

"Thank you, Tam. Deeply helpful."

Ilsa smiled despite everything. That was the thing about the children—the forty of them, aged backward into childhood by a treatment designed to erase them entirely—they kept being *children*. Even now, even here, huddled in the antechamber of the deep archive with their data cores clutched to their chests like stuffed animals. Priya had fallen asleep against a crate of drought-resistant sorghum. Marcus was teaching the twins a hand-clapping game in a whisper. They were terrified, and they were bored, and both things were true at once, because they were seven years old, and that is what seven-year-olds do.

That was why the boarding team frightened Ilsa more than any weapon could have. The Directorate didn't want these children dead. Death would be simpler. What the Directorate wanted was to make them *empty*—to reach inside those small heads and pull out Meridian like a splinter, leaving smooth unscarred skin behind. She knew, because she'd felt the edge of it once already, back aboard the Halcyon, when the fixer with the forgettable face had adjusted his little silver device and asked her questions she couldn't remember answering.

She remembered the answers now. That was the whole problem. She remembered everything.

* * *

It had started twenty minutes ago, when the vault's lights went amber and Marguerite's voice came over every speaker at once, calm in the way of someone reading a weather report about a hurricane: "We have a breach at surface level. Boarding team, twelve personnel, Directorate markings. Children to the deep archive. Ilsa—Ilsa, I need you on the antechamber door."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only adult down there besides me, and someone has to stay with me at the master console, and—" A pause. Static. Then, quieter: "Because you've already lost your adulthood once to these people. You shouldn't have to lose anything else."

Which was kind, and also completely wrong, as it turned out.

The children had moved fast, better drilled than anyone had a right to expect. Six days of siege will do that. Tam had organized them without being asked, counting heads twice the way Ilsa had taught her during the first lockdown drill, back when drills still felt like games. Forty children. Thirty-nine accounted for. The fortieth—little Dev, four years old, the youngest of them—had been found hiding in a supply alcove, chewing on an emergency ration bar he'd stolen from Ilsa's own kit, and had looked up at her with chocolate-brown eyes and said, "I saved you half," and Ilsa had laughed until she cried, briefly, privately, in the dark of the alcove where no one could see a doctor falling apart.

Now they were all in the antechamber, and the antechamber had one door, and the door had Ilsa.

* * *

The memory field came before the soldiers did.

She felt it first as a taste—copper and ozone, like biting a battery—and then as a pressure behind her eyes, gentle as a thumb pressing on a bruise. The Directorate's erasure tech. She recognized it the way you recognize a song from your childhood: not the details, just the shape of it, the way it wanted to slide under her thoughts and rearrange the furniture.

"It's starting," she called out. "They're deploying the field. Marguerite, whatever you're doing, do it faster."

"I'm doing it as fast as it can be done! This lock was built by my mother, and my mother built everything to be difficult, on principle!"

Behind Ilsa, the children were getting quiet in the wrong way—not calm quiet, but the held-breath quiet of animals sensing weather. Priya woke and immediately began to cry, silently, tears running down her face while she made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than wailing. Tam pressed close to Ilsa's side.

"What does it feel like?" Tam asked. "The gray rain. Does it feel like that?"

And there it was. The question Ilsa had been dreading since the boots started thudding.

Tam remembered the gray rain. All the children did, in fragments—that was the whole reason any of this was happening, the whole reason a neutral seed-vault caretaker was now barricaded underground with forty witnesses and a warship in orbit. The gray rain that fell on Meridian's agricultural terraces while the adults dissolved. The root cellar. The smell, which Tam had described once, in play therapy, with terrible precision: "like the air after lightning, but sad."

"No," Ilsa said, and crouched down to Tam's eye level, because some lies you don't tell even to save yourself grief. "No, sweetheart. The gray rain took memories away forever. This is more like... like when you fall asleep in the car and wake up somewhere else. Confusing. Scary. But what's real stays real underneath." She touched Tam's sternum, lightly. "What happened to you is in here. Not just in your head. In here. No machine reaches that."

"That's not scientifically accurate," Tam said, seven going on seventy, exactly as she'd been at thirty-seven. "But thank you."

Ilsa hugged her, quick and fierce, and stood up, and that was when the outer door began to glow at its seams.

* * *

Here is what Ilsa Reyes understood, in the last clear minutes she would ever have, with a clarity that felt almost like grace:

The erasure field was not targeted. It was area-denial. The Directorate's boarding team would flood the antechamber, saturate it, and sweep forward—and everyone in the room would lose the last several hours, then the last several days, then—depending on exposure—everything that made them themselves. The children's recovered memories, the testimony they'd rehearsed, the names and dates and the smell of the gray rain: gone, if the field reached them.

Unless something stood between the field's emitters and the core vault door.

Unless someone did.

She looked at the antechamber's layout the way she used to look at triage lines: fastest route, most survivable outcome, least cost. There was a manual override for the core vault's inner seal—a physical lever, old technology, vault technology, the kind of thing Adaeze Okonkwo had built precisely because she trusted gears more than people. The lever was on the far side of the chamber. Once thrown, the inner door would seal and stay sealed until the archive itself opened it.

Between the lever and the children stood approximately fifteen meters of open floor, filling slowly with a shimmer that wasn't quite visible and wasn't quite nothing, and behind the shimmer, twelve soldiers who had orders not to shoot her, probably, because dead witnesses were inconvenient witnesses, but who certainly wouldn't cry about it either way.

"Marguerite," she said on the comm, keeping her voice light, "how are those four minutes coming?"

"Two minutes. Why? What's happening?"

"Nothing. Small tactical decision. Nothing you need to worry about."

"Ilsa Reyes, I have known you for six weeks and lied to governments with you for five of them. Tell me."

"Tell you what?" said Ilsa, who was already walking toward the lever. "That I'm going to throw a big lever? Very dramatic. You'll love it."

* * *

The field hit her at the ten-meter mark.

It didn't hurt. That was the obscene part. It felt like warmth, like sinking into a bath, like her mother's voice saying her name on a summer morning forty years ago. Memories rose in her and began, gently, to blur at the edges. The launch of the Halcyon—blurring. The negotiation table on Meridian, the fruit they'd been served, the way the Meridian ambassador had laughed—blurring. The fire on the terraces, seen from orbit, too far away to stop—

No. She dug in. She'd spent six weeks rebuilding these memories from fragments, from the children's drawings, from data cores she'd learned to read with her hands shaking. She would not let them go soft now. She planted herself at the lever and gripped it and thought, deliberately, in order, like a surgeon running a checklist:

My name is Ilsa Reyes. I was the medical officer of the Halcyon. We came to make peace. There was no peace to make. Director-General Cass ordered the gray rain. Forty-one thousand people. I know this. I know this. I know—

The door at the far end of the antechamber blew inward.

Soldiers. Twelve of them, in matte-gray armor, moving with the awful competence of people doing a job they'd stopped thinking about years ago. Their team leader raised a hand and the shimmer of the field thickened, rolled forward like fog off cold water.

"Ma'am," the team leader said, and her voice was almost apologetic. "Step away from the mechanism."

"I'm afraid I can't do that," Ilsa said pleasantly. "Doctor's orders. Mine. I'm a doctor."

"Ma'am—"

"Also," Ilsa said, and here she found, rising up through the warmth and the blur, something that was not fear and was not anger but was somehow both and neither, something that felt like the vault itself speaking through her, "you should know what you're erasing today. These children watched forty-one thousand people die. They hid in cellars. They buried what they could bury. They carried the truth across forty years of dark space in bodies that were made small so no one would believe them." She wrapped both hands around the lever. "And I am the last adult witness they have, and I am telling you, soldier, with full medical authority: forgetting is a symptom. It is never a cure."

The team leader hesitated. One second. Two. Ilsa would remember that hesitation—or rather, she wouldn't, none of them would, that was the entire horror of it—but the hesitation existed, in that moment, and it mattered.

Then the field surged, and the world went soft at the edges, and Ilsa pulled the lever with everything she had left.

* * *

The inner door came down like a verdict.

She heard it through the thickening fog in her mind—a sound like a mountain deciding something—and heard, beyond it, muffled, Marguerite's voice on the comm saying her name, saying it again, saying *Ilsa, Ilsa, talk to me*, and Ilsa tried. She genuinely tried. She got as far as, "Sealed. They're sealed. Tell Tam—" and then the words went, gently, like birds leaving a branch.

The soldiers reached her. Hands on her arms, careful hands, strangely gentle, guiding her away from the door she had died—no, not died; worse—to defend. The field closed over her like water.

Somewhere very far away, a woman named Ilsa Reyes had once crossed forty years of space to broker a peace. Somewhere, she had held a child named Tam while Tam drew fire on terraces with a crayon gripped too hard. Somewhere, she had laughed until she cried in a supply alcove over half a ration bar.

The woman standing in the antechamber, blinking placidly at the gray-armored strangers, remembered none of it. She smiled at them, uncertain and polite.

"Is there a doctor aboard?" she asked. "I feel as though I've forgotten something important."

* * *

On the other side of the sealed door, in the deep archive, forty children sat in the dark around a single work lamp, holding each other, listening to the silence where Ilsa's voice had been.

Tam sat with her knees pulled up to her chest, the data core warm in her lap, and did not cry, because crying was for later and later was a country she refused to visit yet. Beside her, Dev chewed his ration bar and offered her the other half, and she took it, because refusing it would have meant explaining, and she couldn't explain, not yet.

The comm crackled. Marguerite's voice, raw as torn cloth:

"Children. Can you hear me?"

Forty small voices said yes.

"The door is sealed. They cannot reach you. I promise you, on my mother's grave and on every seed in this vault, they cannot reach you." A breath. "Ilsa held the line. Whatever happens next—whatever any of us forget—we will write her name down tonight, and tomorrow night, and every night after, so that the record remembers even if we can't."

Tam looked down at the data core. Inside it was everything: the massacre, the orderer, the isotopic proof, the confession of a dead woman who had finally, posthumously, grown a spine. Above them, boots on metal. Around them, kilometers of seeds sleeping in the dark, waiting for worlds that might never come.

"Marguerite," Tam said into the comm, in her flat, precise, seven-year-old voice. "How much longer on the broadcast?"

There was a pause. When Marguerite answered, she sounded almost like she was smiling, in the way people smile at funerals.

"Ten minutes, Tam. Give me ten minutes, and the whole galaxy hears everything."

Above them, something enormous began to cut through the inner door.
