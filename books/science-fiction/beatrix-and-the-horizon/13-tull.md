## 13. Tull

Warden Tull owned three shirts and needed none of them.

He stood in the retrieval bay of the *Charter's Mercy* in his underlayer, watching a technician calibrate the spectral analyzer, and thought about fuel. Most people thought about fuel the way they thought about air: it was there, it was expensive, you stopped noticing it. Tull thought about fuel the way a priest thought about sin. Every burn left a confession in the vacuum—a signature of isotopes, impurities, refinery quirks—as unique to a ship as a fingerprint was to a man. Or to something that only looked like one.

"Calibration's good," said the technician, whose name Tull had not bothered to learn because she would be reassigned before the week was out. "Though I'll say, Warden, this is an odd sample set. You want me to profile exhaust from a ship that's been dark for five years?"

"I want you to stop talking," Tull said pleasantly, "and start profiling."

She started profiling.

Tull pulled on his jacket—the second of the three shirts, the one without bloodstains that had fully set—and considered the file open on his wrist display. Subject: Pemberton, E., clone-line Halcyon designation 7-Theta-114. Property classification: escaped asset. Value at recovery: substantial. Value at termination: also substantial, though the paperwork for termination was still being drafted, which in Tull's experience meant the paperwork would arrive finished, backdated, and smelling faintly of Auditor Corvane's cologne.

He did not think about whether clones were people. That question had been settled for him long ago, in a classroom, by men who owned the classroom. Clones were property. Property ran. Property got retrieved. The universe was simpler that way, and Tull had built a career on keeping the universe simple.

What he thought about instead was the fuel.

* * *

The trick of tracking a man through contested orbits was not speed. Speed was for amateurs and people who died young with interesting hairstyles. The trick was patience, and arithmetic, and knowing what every station in a two-hundred-light-minute radius bought its propellant from and when.

Orsen Pemberton—he allowed himself the name; it cost nothing—had slipped Meridian Station ahead of a detention order, which meant he'd been warned, which meant either luck or a leak. Tull filed both possibilities. Luck expired. Leaks could be found and folded into the plan like extra flour into dough.

The subject had taken the Calliope Vane. That detail had made Tull smile, briefly, the way a chess player smiles when an opponent moves a piece exactly where you wanted it. A ghost ship, scrubbed of registration, running dark through contested space. Romantic. The kind of thing the original Pemberton might have done, back when he was alive and stupid in the ordinary way instead of dead and stupid in the permanent way.

But romantic ships still burned fuel. And the Calliope Vane, according to five-year-old maintenance records Tull had pulled from a Consortium archive that officially did not exist, had spent its last active years drinking a proprietary blend refined at Halcyon's own orbital foundry—a blend with a thallium isotope ratio so distinctive it was practically a signature scent. Expensive. Inefficient. The kind of thing a company used because it could, and the kind of thing a company's stolen property kept using because it knew nothing else.

You could change a transponder. You could scrape a hull clean and file off serial numbers with a patient hand. But you could not change what your engines had eaten for breakfast.

"Profile's done," the technician said. "It's... actually kind of elegant. Whoever compiled this cross-referenced refinery batch records against—" She caught his expression and course-corrected. "Three hits. Three stations within range sold compatible stock in the last six days."

"Names."

"Ceres Hollow, the toll rig at Vasko Drift, and a depot called Latch's Rest." She hesitated. "Vasko Drift is pirate-flagged. We'd need—"

"We won't be going to Vasko Drift." Tull studied the three points plotted against the likely corridors between Meridian and deep space. A man running scared bought fuel where buying was fast and anonymous. A man running smart bought where nobody asked questions. But a man running toward something—toward Kestrel-9, if Corvane's projections held—had to buy along the corridor, and the corridor narrowed the way all corridors eventually narrowed, funneling everything toward the relay like water toward a drain.

Latch's Rest sat at the mouth of the funnel.

"Prep the shuttle," Tull said. "And get me the depot owner's file. Everything. What he drinks, what he owes, what he's afraid of."

"Everyone's afraid of something," the technician said.

"Yes," Tull agreed. "That's what makes the work possible."

* * *

Latch's Rest turned out to be a fuel depot bolted onto a hollowed asteroid, run by a broad, sweating man named Ostrow who greeted Tull's shuttle with the particular dread of a small businessman watching a large institution dock.

"Warden." Ostrow wiped his hands on an apron that had given up its original color sometime during the previous administration. "We're licensed. Fully licensed. Whatever you're looking for, we keep records, we pay our tariffs—"

"I'm sure you do." Tull stepped past him into the depot's cramped office, where a kettle sat on a heating coil and a calendar featured a cartoon dog wearing sunglasses. He liked the calendar. It suggested a man who measured time in months rather than lies. "I'm looking for a ship. Freighter class, older hull, probably traveling with a woman. Bought fuel from you recently."

Ostrow's face performed a small, doomed theater of innocence. "We get a lot of freighters."

"You do." Tull picked up the kettle, weighed it, set it down. "Here's what I know, Ostrow. I know your daughter is enrolled in the medical program on Ceres Hollow. I know tuition there is paid quarterly. And I know that when the Consortium audits a depot—which happens when a warden files a form, and takes about four hours—unlicensed fuel sales surface very quickly, and medical tuition becomes a topic of conversation at the scholarship board."

The silence in the office had texture. Tull let it sit.

"I didn't sell to anyone suspicious," Ostrow said finally, quietly.

"No. You sold to a polite man who paid full price and didn't haggle, and a woman with a checkpoint clerk's posture who watched the door the whole time. Am I close?"

Ostrow closed his eyes as though praying to a god who had stopped taking his calls. "...Tuesday. Early. They took on enough for a long burn. Deep-space burn."

"Course heading?"

"He masked it. But—" Ostrow swallowed. "But the pump cycled twice on the starboard intake. Old habit on that line of hull. If you know the fill pattern, you can guess the tank configuration, and if you know the tanks..." He trailed off, hating himself with impressive thoroughness.

"If you know the tanks, you know the range," Tull finished. "Thank you, Ostrow. Your daughter has a fine future ahead of her. I'd hate for it to involve hearing about this."

He left the man a Consortium credit chit—not a bribe, merely a retainer against future conversations—and walked back to the shuttle feeling the pleasant warmth of a day's work going well. Behind him, through the depot's viewport, the cartoon dog on the calendar grinned its painted grin at a month that no longer mattered.

* * *

Back aboard the *Charter's Mercy*, Tull spread the data across his cabin wall like a man laying out a hand of cards he already knew how to play.

Tank configuration gave him range. Range gave him the corridor. The corridor gave him three plausible waypoints, and the fuel purchase at Latch's Rest gave him a departure window: Tuesday, early. From there it was pure arithmetic, the kind Tull found genuinely soothing, like counting beads. A ship burning that blend at that mass, conserving for a dark transit, would make X distance by Thursday, Y by Friday. Cross-reference with known traffic lanes, subtract the lanes with toll stations—Pemberton wouldn't risk a toll station, or would he? No. Not yet. Not until desperation outweighed caution, and desperation took longer than people thought to arrive. Caution was a deep well. Tull had watched men drink from it for weeks.

Which left the question he turned over each night before sleep, the way other men turned over worry stones: why was the property running *toward* something? Escaped assets ran away. Away was easy. Away was instinct. This one was burning expensive fuel on a vector that pointed, with almost insulting directness, at Relay Kestrel-9—a listening post so old and dead that half the charts marked it as debris.

Someone had called the property home. Tull didn't know who yet. He disliked not-knowing the way sailors disliked weather they couldn't read, but he respected it, the way you respect any loaded weapon lying on a table. He had sent a query up the chain requesting the origin trace on the ghost manifest, and the answer had come back within the hour, which itself was an answer: *the request has been noted. Proceed with recovery. Do not pursue ancillary lines of inquiry.*

Corvane didn't want him asking who had sent the manifest. Which meant Corvane knew, or suspected, or feared. Tull filed that too, in the quiet room at the back of his mind where he kept everything the Consortium believed he didn't notice. It was a crowded room. It was getting more crowded every year.

His comm chimed. Corvane himself, voice smooth as chilled glass: "Progress, Warden?"

"The subject refueled Tuesday at a depot on the corridor to Kestrel-9. I have his range, his probable waypoints, and a departure window accurate to six hours." Tull paused, savoring it. "I'll have him inside nine days."

A silence on the line, brief and satisfied. "Nine days. Good. And Warden—regarding the retrieval parameters. There may be an amendment coming through regarding disposition."

"Termination," Tull said. Not a question.

"The amendment hasn't been finalized."

"It never is, sir. It just arrives." Tull looked at the wall of data, at the thin bright line of a ship crawling toward the edge of dead space, carrying a dead man's face and a living man's secrets. "For what it's worth, I can bring him back breathing. Property retains more value intact, and intact property can answer questions."

"That," Corvane said, "is precisely what concerns the board."

The channel closed. Tull stood alone in his cabin with three shirts and no doubts, and set his chronometer.

Nine days. Somewhere ahead, a clock began to tick—loudly, patiently, in a voice only Tull could hear—and he smiled at it the way a man smiles at an old dog that still knows how to hunt.

He laid out the first shirt for morning. The one with the stains. Some work required a uniform.
