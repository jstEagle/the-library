## 19. Corvane's Confession Machine

Auditor Corvane had a theory about truth that he liked to share with new hires over brandy, in the same way other men shared fishing stories: *Truth is not a thing you find,* he would say, swirling the glass so the amber caught the light of the Halcyon boardroom's private suns. *Truth is a thing you build. And like anything built, it can be built well or built cheaply.*

He had never once built it cheaply.

The room he worked in now was nothing so grand as the boardroom. It was a sub-basement of Halcyon Tower, three floors below the legal archives and one floor above the server vaults, accessible by a single elevator that required his thumbprint, his retinal pattern, and — this was the detail he savored — a genetic swab confirming he was, in fact, himself. The Consortium ran on verification. It seemed only proper that its secrets should be verified too.

The room held a desk, a chair, a wall of displays, and a fabrication suite that the procurement department had innocently listed as a "forensic reconstruction workstation." Which, Corvane supposed, it was. It reconstructed forensics. It simply did so in reverse.

He settled into the chair with the sigh of a man beginning a long-anticipated meal, and woke the displays.

"Show me the Pemberton file," he said. "Consolidated view."

The screens bloomed with data: biometric scans, purchase records, decantation certificates, five years of cargo manifests filed under forged clearances so meticulous they were almost flattering. Somewhere out in the contested orbits, a copy of a dead man was hauling refrigerated protein and pretending very hard not to exist.

Corvane found this touching, in the way one finds a mouse touching when it builds a nest inside a piano. All that effort. All that careful silence. And none of it mattered, because the mouse had been purchased before it was born.

"Status of the murder case," he said.

The display refreshed. **CASE 4471-K: HOMICIDE, E. PEMBERTON (ORIGINAL). STATUS: OPEN — SUSPECT UNKNOWN.** Five years cold. Five years of investigators shrugging, of the file gathering digital dust, of the original's death being an unsolved footnote in a system that solved things only when solving them was profitable.

That had always been the beauty of it. A murder with no suspect is a wound that scabs over on its own. But a murder with a *suspect* — ah. A murder with a suspect becomes a story, and stories have endings, and endings close files, and closed files stay closed.

In three days, the case would have a suspect. In three days, Case 4471-K would end with the arrest, trial, and disposal of E. Pemberton, clone-line designation HLX-7-4419, escaped Consortium property.

And no one — not the courts, not the press, not the weeping ghost of public opinion — would ever again ask why the original had died.

Corvane cracked his knuckles, which was a habit he'd picked up from a forger he'd once had executed, and got to work.

* * *

The first piece was the simplest: the logs.

Every ship keeps a black box. Every port keeps a gate record. Every station keeps a thousand small mechanical witnesses that note who came and went and when, and these witnesses are trusted absolutely, because machines do not lie.

Machines do not lie. This was true. Machines also do not object.

"Reconstruction parameters," Corvane dictated, pulling up the template. "Subject: Pemberton, E., clone designation HLX-7-4419. Target event: homicide of Pemberton, E., original, occurring approximately 02:14 station-local, Meridian Station, docking arm seven."

He had chosen Meridian deliberately. The original had died there; the clone had recently docked there. Overlap was the soul of credibility. A lie that placed a man in two places at once collapsed under scrutiny, but a lie that placed him in the *right* place at the *wrong* time — that lie had legs. That lie could run.

The fabrication suite asked him, politely, how thorough he wished to be.

"Full spectrum," Corvane said. "Gate records, environmental sensor logs, transit-tube camera fragments — partial corruption, of course, we're not barbarians — and a payment trail." He paused, savoring. "Payment trail from the clone's account to a shell entity, which pays a second shell, which pays a third, which pays—" he smiled— "a man named Doss."

Doss. He'd get to Doss shortly. First, the money.

The suite generated the transaction chain in eleven seconds. Corvane reviewed it with the eye of a craftsman. Too clean and it looked manufactured; too messy and it looked like nothing. He adjusted the timestamps by fractions, introduced a rounding error in the third shell's ledger — the kind of error a nervous criminal makes and a smart one forgets to hide — and then, because he believed in artistry, he made the payment *slightly too large*. Not suspiciously large. Guiltily large. The size of a payment made by a man who wanted something done and wanted it done quietly and didn't trust himself to haggle.

"There," he murmured. "Now it's true."

He meant it without irony. In six months, when investigators pulled these records, they would find a coherent financial narrative: the clone, burdened by debts inherited from his original, paying to erase the debt at its source. Motive, means, opportunity — the holy trinity. Juries didn't convict on evidence. Juries convicted on *stories*, and Corvane was giving them a story with a moral: *the copy killed the man to become the man.* 

It was, he reflected, almost poetic. Pity no one would ever know he'd written it.

* * *

The second piece was harder, because it involved a human being, and human beings were the one material in Corvane's long career that never behaved exactly as specified.

His name was Juno Doss, formerly second mate of the freighter Calliope Vane, currently employed as a cargo inspector on Meridian Station, currently drinking himself toward an early grave on a salary that covered neither the drinking nor the grave. Corvane had kept an eye on him for five years the way a gardener keeps an eye on a seed he isn't sure will sprout.

Doss had been there, near docking arm seven, on the night the original died. Doss had seen something — Corvane had never bothered to establish precisely what, because what Doss saw was irrelevant. What mattered was what Doss could be persuaded to say he'd seen.

The witness interview room was on Meridian, but Corvane attended remotely, as a voice behind a privacy screen, identified only as "Counsel for the Consortium." Doss sat across from an actual Consortium counsel, a gray woman named Vetch who was very good at saying very little.

"You understand," Corvane said through the speaker, "that we're not accusing you of anything."

"Yeah," Doss said. His hands were wrapped around a cup of something hot that wasn't helping. "You keep saying that."

"Because it's true. You're here as a cooperative witness. The Consortium values cooperation." Corvane let the pause stretch. "It also remembers it. Your pension vestment, Mr. Doss. The medical coverage. Your daughter's tuition stipend — she's at the Calloway Academy, yes? Lovely school. Very expensive."

Vetch shot the screen a look that said *subtlety*, and Corvane ignored it, because subtlety was for people whose leverage was uncertain. His was not.

"What do you want me to say?" Doss asked, and there it was — the surrender, arriving right on schedule, wearing the costume of a question.

"I want you to tell the truth," Corvane said warmly. "Your truth. You were on the station that night. You saw someone near docking arm seven. And when the investigation stalled — when nobody listened to you — you kept the memory alive, all these years, waiting for someone to ask." He let the fiction settle over the man like a blanket. "You're not lying, Mr. Doss. You're finally being believed. There's a difference, and I think you can feel it."

Doss stared at the table. Then at the screen. Then at the table again.

"He came back, didn't he," Doss said quietly. "The dead man. I heard he docked last week. Same face. Same walk." He laughed, a wet, broken sound. "I figured I was drunk that night five years ago, and now I figure I'm drunk every night, so what do I know?"

"What do you remember seeing?" Corvane prompted, gentle as a lullaby.

And Doss — poor, tired, bought-and-paid-for-before-he-knew-it Doss — began to describe a figure near docking arm seven at 02:14, tall, heavyset, moving fast, face turned away. He described it hesitantly, then less hesitantly, then with the growing confidence of a man building a memory in real time, and Corvane sat back and listened to a witness be born.

When it was done, Vetch read the statement aloud. Doss signed it.

"Thank you," Corvane said. "Your daughter's tuition is secure. So is your pension. And Mr. Doss?" A beat. "So are you — as long as the statement stays exactly as written. Memory is a living thing. It grows. Don't let yours grow."

The feed cut. Corvane removed his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose, feeling the particular fatigue of craftsmanship. Coerced witnesses were the weak joint of any fabrication — flesh failed where silicon held — but a witness who believed his own testimony, who had *authored* it, who would defend it as truth because admitting otherwise meant admitting what he'd done?

That witness was stronger than any log.

He made a note: increase Doss's stipend, quietly, through the third shell. Gratitude hardened into conviction better than fear ever did.

* * *

By the fourth hour, the confession machine was humming.

On the displays, a complete evidentiary picture assembled itself: gate logs placing the clone on Meridian the night of the murder; corrupted camera fragments showing a figure matching his biometrics within forty meters of the crime scene; a financial chain linking his accounts to a known fixer; and now a living witness placing him at the arm itself. The original's death — blunt force, no weapon recovered, killer gone in under four minutes — recontextualized itself around the new data like water finding a channel.

Corvane reviewed each element twice, then a third time, hunting for the flaw that would unravel everything. He found three minor ones and corrected them. He invented one more flaw and left it in — a discrepancy in the camera timestamp, small enough to be dismissed as sensor drift, obvious enough that a diligent investigator would find it and feel clever, and an investigator who feels clever stops digging. He'd learned that trick from a prosecutor he'd ruined decades ago. She'd never understood why she lost the case. He'd sent flowers.

"Projected outcome," he said.

The suite rendered probabilities in cool blue text. **CONVICTION LIKELIHOOD: 94.2%. SENTENCE: PROPERTY FORFEITURE AND TERMINATION OF LINE. CASE 4471-K CLOSED.**

Ninety-four percent. In most endeavors, Corvane would have found that number insulting. But juries were weather, and weather was allowed its moods. What mattered was that the storm was aimed correctly.

He leaned back and regarded the whole construction with something close to affection. Five years ago, the original Pemberton had come to him with a folder full of lineage discrepancies — inheritance after inheritance flowing through clone-succession clauses, estates consolidating into Halcyon's founding families like rain running into a drain. The man had been so earnest about it. So *procedural*. He'd thought he was reporting an accounting error.

Corvane had tried, briefly, to buy him. When that failed, he had tried to frighten him. When that failed—

Well. Docking arm seven. 02:14. A wrench from the Vane's own tool locker, untraceable, and four minutes of darkness in the camera grid that Corvane had arranged personally, and which he was now, with exquisite irony, reconstructing as the clone's handiwork.

The son would hang for the father's murder, and the father's murder would bury the father's discovery, and the lineage fraud would flow on, silent and clean, for another generation.

There was only one variable left, and it was currently somewhere in the contested orbits pretending to be furniture.

"The clone's current position," Corvane said.

**LAST CONFIRMED DOCKING: MERIDIAN STATION. DEPARTED: [TIMESTAMP] — TRANSPONDER SIGNAL LOST AT STATION BOUNDARY.**

Corvane frowned. Lost? The Pemberton line was many things — obedient, durable, conveniently identical — but it was not inventive. Ships did not simply lose their transponders unless someone stripped them, and clones did not strip their transponders unless someone had frightened them.

Someone had talked. Someone on Meridian had shown him the registry entry, or the detention order, or both. Corvane made a mental note to have the port authority's staff audited down to the janitorial contracts, then set the thought aside. It didn't matter who. What mattered was direction.

A frightened animal runs for cover. A frightened *Pemberton* — and this was the flaw in the line, the flaw Corvane had catalogued years ago and never fixed, because fixing it would mean acknowledging it — a frightened Pemberton runs for *answers*. For the origin of its own haunting. For whatever ghost had put its name on a manifest that never flew.

Which meant Kestrel-9. Eventually. Inevitably. The relay at the edge of dead space, where the ghost signal had bounced, where whatever remained of the original's little conspiracy still smoldered.

Corvane stood, buttoned his jacket, and gave the displays one last look — the fabricated logs glowing, the witness statement sealed, the noose woven thread by patient thread.

"Warden Tull," he said to the comm. "Are you awake?"

A pause. Then a voice like gravel settling in a drum. "I'm always awake, Auditor."

"Good. I have a retrieval that may become a termination. Property designation HLX-7-4419. Last seen leaving Meridian dark. I want him found, and I want him found *quietly* — and Tull?"

"Yes."

"When you take him, take his hands intact. The courts like a signature."

He cut the connection before Tull could answer, because Corvane believed a good exit line should never be burdened with a reply.

Then he poured himself the last of the brandy, toasted the empty room — *to truth, built well* — and watched, on the final display, the little blue probability tick upward: 94.3%.

Somewhere out past the shipping lanes, a dead man's copy was flying toward a trap that had been waiting for him since before he was decanted.

Corvane switched off the lights and went home to sleep the sleep of the thoroughly justified.
