## 28. Anselm's Sacrifice

I have spent five years learning to be patient, and I am about to spend all of it in ninety seconds.

The core chamber of Kestrel-9 is a cathedral that was never meant to be entered by anything with a heartbeat. The archive sits at its center like a reliquary — a lattice of crystal storage older than the Consortium itself, humming with the accumulated whispers of a hundred years of dead-space traffic. Every intercepted transmission, every sealed manifest, every lie told across the dark and dutifully recorded by machines that had no one to tell. I came here to be alone with it. I built my life inside it the way other men build lives inside houses.

And now Tull's cutting charges are eating through the bulkhead forty meters aft, and Orsen Pemberton — my brother, my original's echo, my own face wearing a stranger's expression — is standing at the consent console with his hand hovering over it like a man afraid the stove is still hot.

"Say it again," he says. "The part about what happens when you open it."

"The broadcast needs a living Pemberton's biometric consent to authorize," I say. "That's the lock the original designed. He didn't trust passwords. Passwords can be tortured out of people. A heartbeat can't be faked." I tap the console's edge. "But the moment consent registers, it pings. Every Consortium receiver in range gets our coordinates down to the centimeter. Kestrel-9 becomes the most famous address in dead space."

"And you've known this for how long?"

"Since before you docked."

Orsen looks at me the way people look at a doctor who has just used the word *unfortunately* three times in one sentence. "You were going to let me find out after I pressed it."

"I was going to let you press it at all," I say. "That seemed like the more urgent courtesy."

Behind us, Reyes's voice crackles through the comm panel she's jury-rigged to the station's ancient power grid. She has spent the last hour rerouting juice from sections of this relay that haven't drawn power since before I decanted, and she talks to the machinery the way a woman talks to a mule she respects but does not entirely trust. "*Anselm. Bulkhead four is gone. They're through the antenna hall. You have maybe six minutes before they reach the core ring.*"

"Five," I correct her. "Tull doesn't dawdle."

"*Then stop correcting me and start moving.*"

There it is. The moment I have been rehearsing in the privacy of my own skull for five years, arriving exactly as such moments always do: too soon, badly lit, with no time to make it elegant.

Because here is what Orsen doesn't know yet, and what Reyes suspects, and what the station itself has been quietly telling me since we arrived: the consent lock requires a living Pemberton. It does not require the Pemberton to remain living. And more than that — the transmission array's focusing coils are fused. They need a manual override from inside the core housing, a physical switch thrown while the charge builds, and the charge build takes ninety seconds, and the core housing is not rated for occupancy during a charge build, and I checked the rating twice because I wanted very much to be wrong.

I have never once been wrong about the engineering on this station. It is the only thing about myself I have ever been able to rely on.

* * *

Let me tell you how I got here, quickly, because there isn't much time and because a man should get to narrate his own ending. It's the last vanity left.

I was decanted eleven years ago in a Halcyon facility on Ceres, in a tank labeled EXPERIMENTAL LINEAGE VERIFICATION — SUBJECT C. Not a name. A designation. The original Pemberton had been dead two years by then, though nobody told me that either. I learned it later, piecing it together from files I wasn't supposed to read, in a lab where the technicians called me "the asset" and fed me through a slot in the door.

They grew me to test something. That's all I was. A proof of concept for the fraud — evidence that a clone could inherit, could sign, could stand in a courtroom wearing a dead man's face and pass a dead man's estate into the hands of men who had never earned it. When the tests concluded, the plan was to file my serial off and lose me the way you lose a receipt. I escaped instead, at nineteen, through a coolant duct I still have scars from, and I made my way out here to the edge of everything, and I found Kestrel-9 half-dead and dreaming, and I moved in like a squatter who intended to pay rent in truth.

For five years I assembled the case. The original's ledger, recovered from the Calliope Vane's hull plating — Orsen found that; I'll grant him that, he found the ledger. But I found what the ledger pointed to. Fourteen estates stolen through clone-succession. Three witnesses murdered. Two clones decanted and disappeared. And the signature underneath all of it, cool and proprietary as a brand on cattle: Corvane.

I sent the ghost manifest because I needed a Pemberton. Not a copy of one — a *Pemberton*, with the legal inheritance, the registered lineage, the standing. I needed the man the law insisted existed, so that the law would have to listen when he spoke. I didn't know which of them would come. There were supposed to be three of us. I only ever accounted for two.

When Orsen walked through the airlock — wary, tired, carrying his invisibility around him like a coat he couldn't take off — I felt something I hadn't planned for. I had expected a tool. What stood in front of me was a brother. It was deeply inconvenient. I had scheduled my feelings for after the broadcast.

* * *

The cutting charges reach bulkhead five. The floor shivers. Dust sifts down through the cathedral light of the core chamber, catching in the beams like snow falling upward.

Reyes comes through the access hatch at a run, one arm strapped against her ribs where Tull's boarding party caught her at the fuel depot weeks ago, her face gray with pain and lit with the particular fury of a woman who has decided she is not done yet. "They're in the antenna hall. Anselm, whatever you're going to do—"

"I know."

"—whatever you're *thinking* about doing—"

"I know, Reyes."

She stops. She looks at me. She's a checkpoint clerk; she has spent her career reading faces under bad lighting, and mine, apparently, reads like an open book even though it's the same face she's been arguing with for months. Her mouth goes thin.

"No," she says.

"It's not a vote."

"Everything is a vote. That's the entire point of what we're doing here." She plants herself between me and the core housing like a barricade made of clerk. "You found another way. People like you always find another way. You've had five years alone with this station — tell me there's a maintenance crawlway, a remote actuator, a *something*."

"There's a remote actuator," I admit. "It burned out nine years ago. I could repair it in about fourteen hours with parts I don't have."

"How long do we have?"

"Four minutes."

She closes her eyes. When she opens them, she has rearranged herself into the version of Ilsa Reyes that signed off cargo manifests without asking questions for eleven years — the professional face, the one that holds. "Then we do the math out loud," she says. "Because if you're going to die in there, somebody has to check your work. That's not sentiment. That's procedure."

So we do the math out loud. The focusing coils: fused, confirmed twice. The override: interior only. The charge cycle: ninety seconds, non-interruptible once started. The shielding on the core housing: rated for vacuum exposure, not for occupancy, and the charge will flash the chamber to hard vacuum the instant the housing vents. Anyone inside dies. Anyone outside watches the broadcast fail, and then Tull arrives, and then everyone dies anyway, and Corvane's fraud sleeps on for another generation, digesting families.

"There's one variable," I say. "The vent sequence. If someone throws the override and then gets clear before full charge — no. No, the housing seals for the cycle. It's designed to. The original's engineers were thorough people." I almost smile. "We come from thorough people."

Orsen has been silent through all of it, standing at the consent console, and I realize he has understood before either of us said it aloud. He's my mirror; he was always going to arrive at the answer first. His face is doing something complicated — grief arriving ahead of schedule, arguing with arithmetic, losing.

"You said it yourself," he says quietly. "The lock needs a living Pemberton. One of us presses it. And the array needs a hand inside the housing. And there are two of us, and one housing, and—" He stops. Starts again. "Anselm. Why you? We don't even know if it has to be you. It could be me in there. I'm the one who inherited everything else. Let me inherit this."

"Because," I say, "you're the one the law believes in. You're the registered line. Your consent carries weight in a way mine never will — I'm a serial number someone filed off. If I survive and you don't, the broadcast goes out unsigned, and Corvane's lawyers will bury it in injunctions before the signal reaches the second relay. If you survive and I don't, the broadcast goes out under the name Pemberton, with a living, breathing, legally extant Pemberton attached to it, and every court in civilized space has to look at it." I shrug, and I'm surprised to find the shrug is honest. "Also, I know the station. I know the timing. Ninety seconds is a long time to be wrong in there, and I have never once been wrong about this station's engineering."

"That's not a reason," Orsen says. "That's a résumé."

"It's the best thing I have. I built it myself."

He laughs — a broken, awful sound, halfway to a sob, the laugh of a man whose brother has just made a joke at his own funeral. And I understand, in that moment, that this is the gift. Not the sacrifice itself; anyone can die, dying is common as rain out here. The gift is that I get to go first, and he gets to stay, and one of us finally gets to be somebody in the eyes of the universe that made two of us and named neither.

* * *

Bulkhead six goes. The sound is enormous in the cathedral — a whale's death knell sung through steel. Voices beyond it now, boot-heels, Tull's flat orders. Reyes takes up a position at the hatch with a salvaged arc-welder held like a sidearm, because it is the only weapon on this deck, and because Ilsa Reyes has apparently decided that today she is the last line of defense, and God help anyone who argues with a determined clerk.

I climb into the core housing.

It's snug — a technician's cradle, padded where my shoulders fit, switches within reach of both hands. From inside, looking out, I can see the whole chamber: the archive lattice glowing its slow patient blue, Orsen at the console, Reyes at the hatch, the dust still sifting down through the light. It's a good view. I have looked at worse views for longer.

"Charge sequence is armed," I call out. "Orsen. On my mark, you press consent. Don't wait for me. The ping goes out the instant you press — Tull will know exactly where we are, and he'll stop being careful. That's fine. Careful was always his luxury, not ours. The moment you press, the array starts drawing, and my cycle starts, and in ninety seconds everything Corvane buried goes to every receiver in range. Every court. Every newsfeed. Every clone sitting in a tank somewhere wondering what they are." I rest my hands on the override. "Every one of them gets to hear a Pemberton say it out loud."

Orsen's hand is on the consent plate. It's shaking. I remember shaking. I did it for the first year out of the duct.

"Hey," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I have any right to expect. "Brother."

He looks up.

"I spent five years being angry at you. Did you know that? Before you ever docked. Angry at the one who got the name, the papers, the life — soft and scared and invisible, but *free*. Free to be nobody. I used to think you'd wasted it." I shake my head. "You didn't waste anything. You carried it. All of it. The debts, the warrant, the murder case, the fear — you carried a dead man's whole life without once putting it down, and you never knew why, and you never once ran far enough to drop it. That's not soft. That's the strongest thing I've ever seen anybody do."

"Anselm—"

"Be the one who gets to be somebody." I say it clearly, so the archive hears it too, so it's in the record, because I have learned that the only immortality worth having is testimony. "Not him. Not the original. Not the asset. Somebody. You. Go."

I throw the override.

The housing seals around me with a sound like a held breath. Through the small viewport — a technician's convenience, ten centimeters of reinforced glass — I watch Orsen press his palm to the consent plate, and I watch the console light go from blue to white, and somewhere above us the great dead antennas of Kestrel-9 begin, for the first time in a decade, to sing.

The charge builds. Eighty seconds. The hum climbs through the cradle, through my spine, through my teeth. On the viewport, tiny and distorted, I see Reyes holding the hatch, and I see the first cutter-torch sparks bloom beyond her like hostile fireworks, and I see Orsen turn toward the housing and put his hand flat against the glass — against where my hand is, on the other side, the glass between us thinner than all the years between us.

Seventy seconds. The vacuum warning strobes. My ears are already aching. I find that I am not afraid, and I examine this fact with mild scientific interest, the way I examine everything, and I conclude: I was afraid for five years, continuously, and a man's ration of fear runs out. What's left is something better. Something like the moment before dawn on Ceres, the one morning I saw the sun rise through a grate, when the whole sky went from black to burning and I thought: *there is more light than the dark can hold.* I was right. I have spent my whole life since proving it.

Sixty seconds. Orsen's mouth is moving behind the glass. I can't hear him over the hum, but I don't need to. I know the words. They're mine. They're ours. They're the original's, wherever he is, watching his work finish itself through the hands of the sons he never met.

Fifty seconds. The light through the viewport is changing — the console's white bleeding into the chamber, into the archive lattice, which is opening like a flower made of testimony. Somewhere in the dark beyond this station, a signal is unspooling at lightspeed, carrying fourteen stolen estates and three murdered witnesses and one cold proprietary signature toward every ear in range. Corvane is finished and doesn't know it yet. The tanks on Ceres are listening. Whoever is in them, however many of us there are — they're listening.

Forty seconds. I put my forehead against the glass, across from my brother's hand, and I close my eyes, and I am surprised to find that my last thought is not grand at all. It is small, and warm, and it is this: *I was subject C, and I was the asset, and I was the ghost in the manifest — but for ninety seconds, I am the loudest voice in the sky.*

Thirty seconds. The hum becomes a roar. The light becomes everything.

Twenty—

* * *

The core housing blows outward in a bloom of white fire and silence, and the archive sings on without me, and the signal runs.

Through the ringing dark of the chamber, through the smoke and the snowfall of ash, Orsen Pemberton stands with his palm still pressed to the dead glass, and does not move, and does not speak, until the hatch behind him shears open and Warden Tull steps through with a kill order in one hand and murder in his face — and Orsen turns, tears cutting clean lines through the ash on his cheeks, and smiles at the warden with terrible calm.

"You're too late," he says. "Listen."

And overhead, from every speaker on Kestrel-9, the broadcast begins again — because Anselm programmed it to repeat — and the name *Corvane* fills the cathedral like a bell.
