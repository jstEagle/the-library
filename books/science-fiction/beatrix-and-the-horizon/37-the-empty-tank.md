## 37. The Empty Tank

The relay was quieter on the way out than it had been on the way in.

That shouldn't have surprised me. Stations don't grieve. They hum, they tick, they bleed heat into the dark until someone comes along and switches them off. Kestrel-9 had been doing it for two hundred years before any of us were decanted, and it would keep doing it long after the lawyers finished picking through what we'd left behind. But walking its cathedral halls again—past the scorch marks Tull's cutting charges had left, past the bulkhead where Reyes had rerouted power with her own scorched hands—I kept expecting the place to feel different. Lighter. Emptier.

It didn't. It felt like a house where somebody had just died, which was exactly what it was.

Reyes waited by the airlock with the salvage sled. She'd offered to come into the core with me. I'd told her no, and she'd accepted that in the particular way she accepted most of my decisions—by looking at me for three full seconds to make sure I knew she thought I was being an idiot, and then letting me be one anyway.

"Take your time," she said now, which was her way of saying *I know why you're really here, and I'm not going to say it out loud because you'd get defensive.*

"I'll take some of it," I said. "Not all of it. I'm not made of time."

"You're made of at least three people's worth," she said. "Spend it."

* * *

Anselm had vented the core section with himself inside it. That's the clinical version. The version I carried around in my chest was that my brother—the third copy, the one who'd been decanted as a lab experiment and spent five years alone in a dead relay assembling the evidence that saved us all—had looked at me across a console and decided that of the two of us, I was the one who should get to live. *Be the one who gets to be somebody,* he'd said, and then he'd sealed the door and opened the sky.

When the emergency systems finally repressurized the core—Kestrel-9's old automation, stubbornly faithful even after everything—we found him where he'd been standing. The vacuum had been quick and clean, and the cold had kept him. He looked like he was sleeping at his post, which in every way that mattered, he was.

Moving him was the hardest physical work I've ever done, and I once hand-loaded four tons of water ice through a gravity shear. Not because he was heavy. He wasn't. He weighed almost exactly what I weigh, because he was almost exactly what I am. Every time I took hold of an arm, my hands reported back numbers they'd learned from carrying their own body around for thirty years, and something in me flinched at the familiarity of it.

I wrapped him in thermal sheeting from the relay's stores—old survey blankets, printed with a logo for a company that hadn't existed in a century—and carried him out through the antenna halls, past the dishes that had caught his broadcast and thrown it across half the civilized dark.

At the airlock, Reyes took one end of the sled without asking. We went down the docking arm together, slow, like pallbearers, which is what we were.

Neither of us said anything until we were clear of the station. Then Reyes killed the sled's running lights so the only illumination was Kestrel-9 itself behind us, glowing faint and patient against nothing, and she said:

"He would've hated this."

"What, the blanket?"

"The ceremony. The solemnity." She shook her head. "Five years alone in a listening post. You don't come out of that sentimental. He'd want us to hurry up so he could get back to work."

"He *is* getting back to work," I said. "That's kind of the plan."

She looked at me.

I'd spent the transit back thinking about this—not just the burial, but the reading. The original's ledger. The cache we'd pulled from the Calliope Vane's hull plating, all those encrypted notes naming board members and stolen estates and forged successions, the whole rotten architecture of the Halcyon Consortium laid out in a dead man's careful handwriting. Anselm had spent five years reconstructing that same case from the other direction. Between the two of them, the original and the copy, they'd built the thing that brought Corvane down. Neither had lived to see it land.

It seemed wrong to bury either of them without saying the work out loud. Work you finish deserves witnesses. Even if the witness is a copy reading another copy's eulogy over the body of a man who shares both their faces.

"Fine," Reyes said finally. "But if you start crying, I'm going to pretend I'm checking the sled's telemetry."

"That's very generous."

"I'm a generous person. Ask anyone who's shot at me."

* * *

We took the Calliope Vane out to a stretch of deep space that doesn't have a name because nobody's ever needed to give it one—two days' burn past the last navigation buoy, far enough from any shipping lane that no salvager would stumble on the site, close enough to the old relay's coordinates that Anselm would, in whatever way mattered, still be within earshot of the antennas he'd lived inside for five years.

I'll admit I checked the star charts three times. There's a specific kind of care you owe a grave, and I wanted to get it right. Somewhere in the middle of the third check, I realized I was stalling, and Reyes realized it too, and neither of us said anything, because that's the arrangement we had.

The burial itself was simple. That part I'd decided early. No torpedo casket, no fancy trajectory, none of the corporate memorial packages that cost more than a dockworker makes in a year and deliver your loved ones into orbit as a slowly dispersing glitter of ash. Anselm wouldn't have wanted that. Anselm had spent his whole life as somebody's product; the last thing he needed was to spend his death as somebody's purchase.

So: thermal sheeting, a small transponder beacon keyed to his serial number—or rather, to the serial number that had been filed off his growth tank, recovered from the tank's own maintenance logs, because a man deserves to have his number back even if the number was never really his—a gentle push away from the ship, and the stars.

Before the push, though, I read the ledger.

I stood in the Calliope Vane's small cargo bay, holding the original's data slate in both hands, while Reyes stood by the outer hatch controls with her arms folded and her jaw set in the expression she used when she was determined not to feel things. And I read.

Not all of it. The ledger ran to nine hundred pages of forensic detail—dates, account numbers, shell-company registrations, the slow patient mapping of how eleven families had used clone-succession law to swallow forty-three estates that belonged to other people. Reading all of it aloud would have taken a week, and honestly, most of it was arithmetic, and arithmetic is a poor eulogy.

Instead I read the notes. The original's marginalia, the things he'd written to himself in the gaps between the evidence. Little observations. *Corvane drinks the same vintage his grandfather did—sentimental about theft.* *The Meridian port clerk falsified entry 4471 twice; find out who paid her.* *If anything happens to me, the pattern matters more than the proof. Patterns survive.*

And then, near the end, dated six days before his murder:

*Saw a girl today at the Meridian fountain. Dark hair, my mother's chin. Beatrix. She didn't know me. Why would she? I'm the man who let her inheritance get stolen and couldn't prove it yet. Soon. Whatever happens, the estate goes back to her. Write it down so somebody finishes it.*

I stopped there for a moment. Reyes noticed. She always notices.

"Keep going," she said quietly.

"There's not much left. Just inventory lists."

"Then read the inventory lists. He wrote them. They count."

So I read the inventory lists. Every line. The names of the stolen estates, the names of the families defrauded, the name of the auditor who'd signed off on each fraudulent succession—Corvane's signature appearing eleven times, in eleven slightly different hands, because the man had apparently practiced forging his own ancestors' authorizations and gotten bored halfway through. Reyes actually laughed at that one, a short wet sound she immediately disguised as a cough, and I pretended not to notice, because that was our arrangement too.

And when I got to the final line of the final page, I read it, and then I stood there holding the slate against my chest like an idiot, and Reyes hit the hatch cycle before I could make it worse.

The bay depressurized gently. The sheeting bundle drifted out through the open field, turned once—slow, unhurried, the way things move when there's nothing left to rush them—and caught the light of the nearest star.

I keyed the beacon. Somewhere out there, a small transmitter began repeating a serial number into the dark: *here. here. here.* Not a name. A number. But after everything, I figured Anselm would understand the difference between the two better than anyone.

We watched until the bundle was a dot, and then not even a dot. Reyes closed the hatch. The bay repressurized with a soft sigh, and the smell of recycled air came back, and we were two people standing in a cargo bay instead of mourners at the edge of forever.

"Well," she said.

"Yeah."

"You did good."

"He did good," I said. "All of him. All of us." I rubbed my face with both hands. "You know what the worst part is? I barely knew him. Two days. Two days of him being suspicious of me, and fair enough—he'd earned his suspicion, and I hadn't earned anything. And now I'm going to spend the rest of my life missing him."

"That's not the worst part."

"No?"

"No." She leaned against the hatch frame. "The worst part is you're going to catch yourself doing things he did. Little things. Turns of phrase. The way he'd tap a console twice before trusting it. You won't know if you learned them from him or if you always had them, because you're the same person, sort of, except you're not, and—" She stopped. "Sorry. Fever-brain. Ignore me."

Her wound was healing. Mostly. The surgeons at Meridian had done competent work, but competent work leaves scars, and scars leave moods, and lately Reyes's moods arrived with philosophical freight attached.

"I don't think that's fever-brain," I said. "I think that's just true."

"It's morbid, is what it is."

"It's inheritance," I said. "Turns out that's what it actually looks like. Not estates and signatures. It's catching yourself tapping a console twice and not knowing whose habit you're keeping alive."

Reyes looked at me for a long moment. Then she pushed off the hatch frame and headed for the galley. "I'm making tea," she announced. "You're coming. You're going to sit down for the first time in thirty-six hours, and you're going to drink something warm, and you're going to tell me one funny story about him. One. That's the toll."

"He wasn't funny."

"Everyone's funny. Especially the grim ones. Grim people are hilarious; they just don't know it."

* * *

Over tea—which was terrible, because Reyes made tea the way she argued legal points, with overwhelming force and no subtlety—I found the story.

It was from the logs Anselm had kept during his five years in the relay. Most of it was surveillance data and decrypted fragments, the grinding daily labor of building a case alone in the dark. But scattered through it were little entries, personal log fragments, the kind of thing a man writes when the silence gets too big and he needs to hear a voice, even his own.

Entry 1,204, year three of his exile: *Today the relay's coffee synthesizer produced something beige. I have named it Gerald. Gerald and I are not speaking.*

Entry 2,877: *A supply drone passed within sensor range today. First contact in eight months. I waved. It did not wave back. Typical.*

Entry 3,102: *Found a cockroach in the hydroponics module. Impossible—we're forty light-hours from anything living. Either it hitched on a drone or the universe is testing me. Named it Corvane. Fed it. Felt guilty about the comparison afterward. Apologized to the cockroach.*

By the third entry Reyes was laughing properly, the unguarded laugh she almost never let out, and I was laughing too, and somewhere in the middle of it I realized that this was the funeral. Not the vacuum and the sheeting and the beacon. This. Two people in a bad galley telling stories about a dead man until he became real enough to miss properly.

"He apologized to a cockroach," Reyes said, wiping her eyes. "And you're telling me he wasn't funny."

"He was funny. He was also five years alone, which does things." I turned my cup. "You know what he said to me? Right at the end. He said I'd lived soft. And he was right. I had. Forged clearances, quiet routes, no history—soft. Careful-soft. He survived a lab and a decade of running, and I survived by being invisible, and we both thought the other one had it easier." I shook my head. "Two days wasn't enough to fix that. It wasn't enough for anything."

"It was enough for him to trust you with the switch," Reyes said. "He built that archive lock for five years. He could have opened it himself. His biometrics were as good as yours." She tapped the table. "He chose you. Out of three Pembertons, he chose the one who'd show up late and do it scared. Make of that what you want."

What I made of it, privately, was that Anselm had understood something about me that I was still catching up to: that showing up scared counts. That the work matters more than the fear. That a copy who finishes a dead man's work has done something no original ever managed.

I didn't say any of that out loud. Instead I said, "Gerald," and pointed at the tea, and Reyes threw a sugar packet at me.

* * *

We burned back toward inhabited space the next morning, or what passed for morning aboard a freighter running on ship's clock and mutual agreement.

I spent the first watch in the Calliope Vane's crew quarters, which I'd been avoiding. Anselm had slept there, briefly, in the last days—there was a bedroll still rolled against the bulkhead, a mug in the warmer, a jacket on the hook that fit me perfectly for reasons that required no explanation. I'd told myself I'd deal with it later. Later had arrived, as it does.

I packed his things carefully. Bedroll, mug, jacket, a battered reader loaded with five years of logs, a hand-drawn star chart of the deep-space approaches to Kestrel-9 annotated in tiny furious handwriting: *good cover here*, *sensor shadow*, *if pursued, fake a coolant leak—they never check coolant*. At the bottom of his footlocker, wrapped in a spare pressure seal, I found the empty growth tank's serial plate—the one he'd pried off his own origin, carried across the dark, and kept like a wound he'd decided to stop hiding.

I sat with it in my hands for a while.

Then I put it in the box with the rest, sealed the box, and labeled it in the ship's manifest under a category the registry software didn't officially support but which I typed in anyway: *family effects.*

The system flagged it. Unrecognized classification. Do you mean *personal effects*?

No, I told it. I don't.

* * *

Reyes found me on the bridge at the end of watch, which surprised her, because I usually hid in the engineering section when I was sad—something about having tasks to do with my hands.

"We're coming up on the Meridian approach lane," she said, dropping into the copilot's seat. "Traffic control is going to hail us in about twenty minutes. Officially, we're a salvage vessel returning from an unregistered debris field. Unofficially, we're the two most famous people in the system, and every news feed from here to the Core wants an interview."

"Mm."

"'Mm,' he says. The man who broadcast the fall of a dynasty says 'mm.'" She studied me. "You okay?"

"I'm going to be." I watched the traffic display populate—dozens of ships in the lane, honest transponders, honest manifests, the ordinary machinery of ordinary lives. A month ago I'd have been sweating over my forged clearance right now, rehearsing my cover story, calculating whether the clerk would linger on my biometrics. Now my registration was real, my name was mine, and the biggest threat in the lane was a customs drone with a paperwork bug.

"Meridian first?" she asked. "Or straight to the colony ship? The old woman's convoy is two days off our heading."

I smiled despite myself. "You remembered."

"You told the story four times. Of course I remembered." She stretched. "Besides, you owe her a visit. Properly, this time. As yourself."

As myself. Three words, and they still landed strange, like a key cut for a lock I was only learning to use.

The comm chimed. Meridian Traffic Control, right on schedule, wanting our manifest and our intentions. Reyes raised an eyebrow at me—your ship, your call—and I keyed the channel, and gave the Calliope Vane's honest registration, and my honest name, and our honest intention, which was to come home, unload, refit, and then point ourselves at a drifting colony ship where an old woman was waiting to thank a dead man for something I intended to finish repaying.

"Acknowledged, Calliope Vane," the controller said, and there was something odd in the voice—young, nervous, a little awed. "Welcome back, sir. And, um. Welcome home."

The channel closed. Reyes and I looked at each other.

"Home," she repeated.

"Don't make it weird."

"Too late. Already weird. Deeply weird." She grinned and started plotting the approach. "Get used to it."

Out the forward ports, Meridian Station hung against the black, lit up along its docking arms, traffic crawling toward it like blood toward a heart. Behind us, two days' burn back the other way, a small beacon repeated a serial number into the dark—*here, here, here*—and ahead of us, somewhere past the station, past the colony convoy, past all the routes I'd once run scared, the horizon was doing what horizons do.

Waiting to be crossed.
