## 39. The Horizon Line

The message arrived at 0300 station time, which was the universe's way of telling me it had no respect for a man's sleep.

I knew before I opened it. You develop a feel for these things after a year of running honest routes through dishonest space. The Calliope Vane's comm array chirped once—a soft, polite chirp, the kind of chirp that says *I have something for you and I'm not sorry about the hour*—and I lay in my bunk staring at the ceiling of my cabin and thought: *there it is.*

"You're going to go look at it," Reyes said from the other bunk. She didn't open her eyes. She'd learned to sleep through anything except the sound of me making decisions.

"It could be nothing. Could be a routing update from Meridian."

"Orsen."

"Could be a coupon. Stations send coupons now. I got one for discounted hull sealant last month."

She rolled over and looked at me in the dim emergency lighting we kept on because full lights felt too much like optimism. Her hair was flattened on one side and there was a crease from her pillow running down her cheek, and she looked, as she always did at this hour, like the only real thing in a cargo bay full of possibilities.

"You have the face," she said.

"What face?"

"The face you make when a ghost is knocking. The one where you pretend it's indigestion."

"That *is* indigestion."

"Go look at your ghost, Pemberton." She closed her eyes again. "I'll make coffee. The bad kind. You've earned it."

* * *

The message sat in the queue with no sender ID, no routing header, no return address—which, in the old days, would have made my hands sweat and my mouth go dry. In the old days, an anonymous transmission meant someone wanted you dead or wanted you found, and both of those amounted to the same thing for a man built to be invisible.

But that was before Kestrel-9. Before Anselm. Before the broadcast that turned one clone's name into a legal precedent and one Consortium's dynasty into a smoking crater of indictments. These days anonymous transmissions mostly meant journalists, historians, or people who'd read about the Pemberton consent ruling and wanted to tell me their own stories—the brothers and sisters scattered across the shipping lanes who'd spent their whole lives filed under *property* and were only now learning they could sign things.

I opened it expecting another letter. What I got was a manifest.

I stood very still in front of the console for a long moment, reading it twice, three times, the way you reread a sentence that shouldn't exist.

It was formatted exactly like the ghost entry I'd found in Meridian Station's registry two years ago. Exactly. Same template, same obsolete port-code formatting, same little bureaucratic fingerprints—the work of someone who'd studied the original forgery down to its punctuation. A freighter listed as destroyed eleven years ago, the *Marigold Sen*, supposedly lost with all hands in a navigation accident near the Thessaly drifts. And on its new outbound manifest, dated three days from now, departing from a relay station that hadn't answered a hail in a decade:

**Passenger: V. Okonkwo — original.**

Not my name this time. Someone else's.

I sat down slowly in the pilot's chair, which creaked its familiar complaint, and I did the thing I'd learned to do instead of panicking, which was to make coffee first and panic second if there was time left over.

Reyes appeared in the doorway with two mugs. She'd read my posture correctly, because she didn't ask what it said. She just handed me mine—it was the bad kind, instant, slightly burnt, tasting like regret and caffeine—and leaned against the doorframe with her own.

"Well?" she said.

"Somebody's doing it again," I said. "The ghost manifest trick. Different ship, different name. V. Okonkwo." I turned the console toward her. "Original designation. Which means somewhere out there, there's a clone walking around carrying that name without knowing what's attached to it."

Reyes read it the way she read everything—fast, twice, lips moving slightly on the second pass, a habit she'd deny under oath. Then she was quiet for a while, which was how I knew it had landed.

"Same formatting?" she asked finally.

"Down to the commas. Whoever sent this studied the one that got sent to me. Studied it like scripture." I tapped the screen. "And they sent it to *me*. Not to the authorities, not to Okonkwo's clone, not to one of the forty thousand news outlets that still owe me favors. To me."

"Because you're the one who answered the last one."

"Because I'm the one who survived the last one," I said, and then heard how that sounded, and added, "Statistically speaking. Anselm would want that noted. He'd be insufferable about the math."

Her mouth did the small sideways thing it did when she was smiling but didn't want to commit to it yet. "You're going to go."

"I'm going to go."

"You know that's what they want. Whoever sent it. That's the whole design. It worked on you once."

"It did," I agreed. "And here's the difference between then and now." I pulled up the star chart, the one with our current route glowing along it like a thread of gold stitching through dark cloth. "Two years ago, a message like this was a trap with my name on it. Every unknown was a threat. Every silence was somebody hunting me. I ran those routes scared out of my mind, forged clearances, slept with a wrench by the bed." I took a sip of the terrible coffee. "Now? Now an unknown is just a question nobody's asked yet. And I've gotten pretty good at questions."

* * *

We spent the morning on it, because that's what we do now—we're a two-person investigative unit disguised as a freight crew, or possibly a freight crew disguised as an investigative unit; some mornings even I'm not sure which is the cover story.

Reyes dug into the Marigold Sen's loss records while I traced the message's routing. Old habits, both of us. Hers from six years as a checkpoint clerk who noticed everything, mine from a lifetime of being the thing other people tried to trace.

"Here's something," she said around midday, feet up on the console, which she only did when she'd forgotten I could see her do it. "The Marigold Sen wasn't hauling cargo when she went down. Officially she was a mineral survey vessel. Unofficially—" she scrolled, frowned, scrolled more, "—her insurance claim lists a passenger compartment refit. Sealed. Paid for by a holding company that dissolved itself nine days later."

"A holding company with a name?"

"Halcyon subsidiary. Fourth shell deep." She looked at me. "They never learn, do they? Four shells. Like that hides anything anymore."

"After the broadcast, four shells is basically a signed confession." I marked the routing on my end. The ghost manifest had bounced through six relays, each hop scrubbed clean, but scrubbing leaves residue if you know what residue looks like—I'd learned that from Anselm's notes, from the ledger we'd recovered from the hull plating, from a dead man's careful handwriting teaching a copy of himself how to pay attention. The trail ended at a relay called Sable Water Station, out past the Thessaly drifts.

Sable Water. I sat with that name for a moment.

That was the colony ship. The drifting settlement where the old woman had thanked me for a kindness the original Pemberton had done her—a debt repaid to the wrong man, a grief I'd carried ever since for a life I'd inherited but never lived. We'd gone back there eight months ago, properly this time, and she'd fed us stew and told me stories about E. Pemberton that I listened to like a son hearing about a father he never met. She still called me by his name. I let her, gently. Some inheritances you don't correct.

And now a ghost manifest routed through her sky.

"Oh, that's deliberate," Reyes said, watching my face. "Whoever sent this knows you went back there. Knows what that place means to you."

"Everybody knows what that place means to me. It was in the broadcast coverage. 'Clone hero returns kindness to elderly colonist'—they ran it for a week. There was a mural." I grimaced. "There's a *mural*, Reyes. Of my face. On a colony ship. Do you know what I spent thirty years trying to avoid? Murals."

"One mural."

"One is infinite. That's how murals work."

But underneath the grumbling, something was settling into place, quiet and steady, like ballast shifting right. Because here was the thing I'd figured out somewhere between Kestrel-9 and the courtroom, somewhere between burying Anselm in the deep black and registering the Calliope Vane honestly for the first time in her patched-together life:

Fear had been a full-time job. Purpose paid better.

* * *

We plotted the course that evening. The Thessaly drifts were nine days out at cruising burn, ten if we stopped, and we'd stop, because we always stopped now—at the toll stations where the pirates who'd once shaken me down for spare parts now waved us through and asked, shyly, whether we were really the ones from the broadcast; at the fuel depots where Tull's ambush had nearly ended us and where the depot manager, a round woman named Ferro, pressed a free filter cartridge on us every single visit and refused to hear otherwise; at the drifting hulk of the old colony ship where an old woman would feed us stew and call me by a name that was mine now too, in the way that matters.

"Ten days," Reyes said, leaning over the chart with me. Our shoulders touched. They usually did, at the chart. Neither of us had ever remarked on it. "You want to file the flight plan honestly? Declared cargo, declared purpose, transponder singing the whole way?"

I looked at the course line. Nine days ago—that is, two years and nine days ago—I'd have stripped the transponder and run dark, hugged the debris fields, forged a clearance so layered it needed its own table of contents. The muscle memory was still there. It would always be there. Vigilance doesn't leave you; it just changes employers.

"Yeah," I said. "File it honest. Let them see us coming." I smiled at the chart, at the long gold thread running out toward the dark edge of everything. "Besides, whoever's sending ghost manifests has been watching us for a while. Might as well give them something worth watching."

Reyes snorted. "You just want the pirates to wave at you again."

"I contain multitudes. Some of them are vain."

She laughed—the real laugh, the one I'd only started hearing after Kestrel-9, after she'd testified for six hours straight and come out the other side hoarse and triumphant and holding my hand in the corridor like it was the most ordinary thing in the world—and then she went forward to file the plan, and I stayed at the chart a while longer.

The message was still open on the secondary screen. **V. Okonkwo — original.** Somewhere out there, a person was walking around with that name, hauling whatever they hauled, sleeping however they slept, carrying debts and warrants and maybe an unsolved murder they'd never chosen and never questioned. Maybe they'd never even been told they were a copy. Maybe they thought the hollow feeling was just the hollow feeling, the way I used to.

Somebody wanted me to find them. Somebody who'd studied Anselm's work—or the original's work, the lines had blurred long ago about whose work it really was—and decided the next ghost should knock on my door specifically.

Trap or invitation. It had been trap or invitation once, and I'd gone anyway, and going anyway had turned out to be the only decision of my life I'd never once regretted.

I saved the manifest to the case file—the growing, ridiculous, wonderful case file, folder after folder of ghosts we'd laid to rest and names we'd given back—and then I plotted the final leg myself, by hand, the old way, because some things you don't trust to autopilot.

The course held. Nine days to the drifts. Ten with stops.

Behind me, Reyes was arguing with the filing system about our declared purpose—"*Investigative assistance, non-military*" apparently wasn't a category it liked—and outside the viewport, the traffic of Meridian Station wheeled past in its slow, lawful, unremarkable dance, none of it hiding from anything.

I looked at the horizon line where the station's light gave out and the true dark began, the line I'd spent a lifetime treating as a wall, and saw it for what it had always been: a road.

"Hey, Reyes," I called. "How do you feel about the Thessaly drifts?"

A pause. The sound of the filing system capitulating. "Cold, probably. Why, what's out there?"

"Another ghost," I said, and keyed the burn.
