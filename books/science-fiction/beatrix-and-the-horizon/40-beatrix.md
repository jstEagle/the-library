## 40. Beatrix

The letter had been sitting in my queue for eleven days before I worked up the nerve to open it.

That's not entirely honest. I'd opened it four times and closed it again, the way you might circle a door you're not sure you have the right to knock on. The sender line read only: *B. Vane-Halloway, Meridian Station, Civil Registry Annex.* No message preview. Just a name that made my hands go quiet in the way they do when something important is happening and my body knows it before I do.

Reyes found me on the third attempt, standing in the Calliope Vane's galley with a cup of tea gone cold in my fist and the unopened letter glowing on the console.

"You've been staring at that since Tuesday," she said.

"It's from her."

"I know."

"How do you—"

"Orsen." She leaned against the bulkhead and crossed her arms, and there was that look she got, the one that meant she was about to be patient at me whether I liked it or not. "You've said her name in your sleep twice this month. You reorganized the cargo manifests by date instead of destination, which I know for a fact you've never done, because you told me once that sorting by date was how cowards avoid thinking about where they're going."

"That's not—I never said coward."

"You implied it with great judgment." She nodded at the screen. "Open it. Whatever's in there, it can't be worse than what we've already survived. We fought a warden in an antenna cathedral. We watched your brother vent himself into vacuum so the truth could breathe. A letter from a lawyer-adjacent stranger is a Tuesday."

"It's Thursday."

"Then it's overdue."

I opened it.

*Mr. Pemberton,* it began, and then, as if the writer had caught herself being formal with someone who deserved better: *Orsen. Forgive me. I don't actually know what to call you, and I've rewritten this opening nine times.*

*I am Beatrix. My mother named me for the ship — did you know that? Everyone assumes it's the other way around, that he named his beloved freighter after his daughter, but no. He bought the hull first, when I was three weeks old and screaming in a rented berth on Ceres, and he used to tell me that a man should name his ship after the thing he loves most, because ships hear everything and deserve to carry good names. He said the ship would keep me safe when he couldn't. He was a sentimental man wearing a practical man's clothes.*

*I was six when he died. They told me it was pirates. I believed that for twenty-two years, because believing it was easier than believing that a consortium of respectable people had decided my father knew too much about their ledgers.*

*I have spent those twenty-two years becoming exactly the kind of person they never expected him to produce: a civil registrar. I process estate claims. Do you understand the joke of that? Every day I sit behind a desk and stamp documents, and every day some part of me is looking for the pattern he found — the clone-succession transfers, the estates flowing uphill into Halcyon board families like water running the wrong direction. I found fragments. Never enough. Then, eight months ago, the broadcast reached Meridian, and I sat in my office and listened to my father's own handwriting read aloud in a stranger's voice, and I wept into my coffee in front of two junior clerks who are now convinced I'm unstable.*

*The estate has been restored. All of it. The courts moved faster than anyone predicted once 'Pemberton consent' became precedent — there are judges now who want to be photographed next to anything with your name on it. The original holdings revert to legal next of kin. That is me. And I am writing to tell you that I have executed a transfer.*

*Half of everything is yours.*

*Please don't argue yet; let me finish. I know what you'll say. You'll say you didn't live his life, that you inherited his debts and his murder case and none of his joys, that the money isn't yours because the memories aren't. I've read your testimony. It's public record; I've read all of it, more times than I'll admit. You said something at the hearing that I copied out and keep in my desk drawer: 'An inheritance isn't what you're given. It's what you finish.'*

*So consider this not a gift but a completion. He started providing for me before I could walk. Someone finished the job. As far as I'm concerned, the accounting is simple.*

*Also — and this is the part I've rewritten most — I would like to meet you. Not the case file. Not the broadcast. You. The man my father's echo became when nobody was sculpting him into anything. I have questions about him that only you could answer, and questions about you that only you could answer, and I suspect the answers overlap in ways that will make us both laugh and cry at inconvenient moments.*

*The registry annex keeps terrible coffee. Come anyway.*

*Beatrix*

I read it three times. On the third pass, Reyes took the cold tea out of my hand and replaced it with a fresh one without comment, which is one of roughly four thousand reasons I keep her around.

"She signed off with a transfer," I said finally. "Half of everything. Reyes, I don't even know what 'everything' is."

"Does it matter?"

"It—" I stopped. Because the honest answer was no, and the dishonest answer was a long speech about principle that I'd been rehearsing in my head since the second paragraph, and she'd already heard the rehearsal through the bulkhead, probably.

"The old woman on the colony ship," Reyes said. "Sable Water. What was her name?"

"Maren."

"What did she thank you for?"

I looked at her. "You know this story."

"Tell it again anyway. I think it applies."

I sighed and sat down across from her, the letter still glowing between us like a small sun. "Maren thanked me for a debt forgiven. Twenty years ago, the original Pemberton hauled her family's seed stock out of a quarantine zone when nobody else would take the contract — no pay, illegal route, could have cost him his license. Her daughter got to grow up. She pressed my hand in that corridor and called me by his name and told me I'd changed her life, and I stood there taking credit for a kindness I had nothing to do with." I turned the cup in my hands. "For a year afterward I couldn't decide if that moment was the cruelest thing that ever happened to me or the truest."

"And now?"

"Now I think it was both, and that both was the point." I tapped the letter. "She's doing the same thing to me, Beatrix. Handing me credit for a life I didn't live. Except this time I can hand some of it back."

"So don't argue with the transfer," Reyes said. "Argue with the terms. Counter-offer. You're a cargo man — you know how contracts work. Take the inheritance, but make it mean something on your way through."

I stared at her for a long moment. "Have I mentioned recently that you're terrifying?"

"Daily. Usually with feeling."

* * *

We docked at Meridian nine days later, and I will confess that I dressed carefully, which is a thing I hadn't done since before any of this — since back when dressing carefully meant dressing forgettably, layers over biometrics, a silhouette designed to slide past checkpoints without catching light. This was different. This was a clean shirt. Reyes said I looked "almost like someone with a future," which from her is a sonnet.

The Civil Registry Annex occupied a wedge of Meridian's third ring, all pale wood and filtered daylight, the kind of place built by people who believed paperwork could be beautiful if you gave it enough windows. I checked in at the desk and gave my name — *Pemberton*, plainly, out loud, to a stranger, and felt the old flinch and let it pass through me like weather — and a junior clerk went very pink and said, "She's expecting you," as though I were a comet rather than a man.

Beatrix Vane-Halloway stood up from behind her desk when I came in, and the first thing I thought — absurdly, immediately — was: *she has his hands.* The original's hands, the ones I saw in every archived photograph, broad-knuckled and expressive, hands that talked while the mouth stayed careful. She had them folded in front of her the way people fold things when they're holding themselves together politely.

"You look like him," she said. Then she winced. "No. I'm sorry. That's — everyone says that to you, don't they? And it's not fair, because you don't. You look like yourself. I've seen the footage. You stand differently. He stood like a man braced against a door. You stand like a man who's decided which door he's walking through."

"That may be the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me," I managed, "and I've been publicly exonerated."

That broke whatever tension was holding us both upright. She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and then her eyes went bright, and mine weren't far behind, and we stood there in the pale daylight of the registry annex, two strangers sharing a face's architecture and a dead man's grief, laughing and crying at inconvenient moments exactly as predicted.

"Tea?" she said. "It's terrible. I warned you in the letter."

"I've had worse on six stations."

"Oh, I doubt that." She poured anyway, and we sat, and for a while we just talked — small things first, ballast before cargo. She told me about growing up on stories of the Calliope Vane, how she'd assumed the ship was a myth until the trial made it famous. I told her about finding the ghost manifest, about the empty growth tank, about Anselm. At his name she went still and put down her cup.

"He finished it too," she said quietly. "Whatever half is yours, a third of it should have been his."

"It was his choice," I said. "'Be the one who gets to be somebody.' His words. I've had a year to argue with them and I haven't found the argument yet."

"No," she agreed softly. "Neither would I."

Then she straightened, registrar-mode engaging like a ship coming about, and slid a document across the desk. "Now. Before you get sentimental and agree to something rash: the terms. Half the restored estate, liquid and fixed assets both, including — and here's where you'll object — the Calliope Vane's original registration charter."

I did object. "The ship's mine. I refit her. I registered her honestly, under my name, which was the whole—"

"—the whole point, yes, I've read the testimony, you're very consistent." She tapped the document. "This doesn't change your registration. It restores the *name*. Legally, historically, the vessel's founding charter lists her as *Beatrix*, commissioned by E. Pemberton, named for his daughter. When the estate was seized, the charter was struck and the hull re-registered under the generic designation you inherited. I'm offering you the original charter back. Rename her, if you want. Or don't. But I'd like to come aboard sometime and see her carrying it."

I looked down at the paper. *Vessel Charter: BEATRIX.* Five letters I'd been carrying around for a year without knowing it — the title of a book I didn't know I was living in, the name of a ship and a daughter braided together by a sentimental man in practical clothes.

"You knew," I said slowly. "When you wrote. You knew the whole time that the title of everything — the case, the broadcast, all of it — was your name."

"I hoped you'd figure it out eventually," she said. "It took you a year. I was starting to worry."

* * *

We flew out to the colony ship Sable Water three weeks later, Beatrix aboard the *Beatrix* — she'd insisted on the maiden voyage under the restored name, and Reyes had pretended to grumble about passengers and then spent two days showing her every system on the ship with barely concealed pride.

Maren met us at the airlock, older than ever and sharp as ever, and when she saw Beatrix she stopped dead.

"Well," Maren said. "There you are."

"You know her?" I asked.

"I knew her mother," Maren said. "Knew her before the quarantine run, even. Whole family, back on Ceres." She looked at Beatrix, then at me, and something passed across her face — the particular expression of a woman watching a debt travel full circle. "Your father hauled our seeds out of that zone because your mother asked him to. Did you know that? Everyone thinks it was charity. It wasn't. It was a favor between friends, and he never let me repay it, and then he died, and then—" she gestured at me, encompassing the face, the history, the whole improbable shape of things, "—and then the universe sent me his echo in a corridor, and I figured the account was finally settling itself."

Beatrix looked at me. I looked at Beatrix. Neither of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that the corridor wasn't already saying.

Maren pressed her hand into mine, the same gesture as before, and this time she said, "Thank you, Orsen Pemberton. By name. Both names. Whichever ones you're using these days."

"All of them," I said. "I'm keeping all of them."

* * *

The letter took me four drafts. Beatrix, when I showed her the final version before sending, said the first three had been "very noble and completely wrong," which was accurate.

*Dear Beatrix,* it read.

*Enclosed is the deed — countersigned, witnessed, and stamped by an actual registrar, which I understand is your love language. I accepted the inheritance. All of it. But I've amended the terms, and since you taught me that paperwork can be beautiful, I hope you'll approve of the drafting.*

*I'm keeping the name Pemberton. Not his — mine. It means something different now than it did when he carried it, and something different again than it did when they tried to use it against me. Names are cargo, I've learned. They're only worth what you haul them through. I've hauled this one through contested orbits, a pirate toll station, a siege in the static, and one truly terrible cup of registry coffee. It's earned its keep.*

*And I'm keeping the ship under her right name, with you listed on the charter as founding family, which means legally you're entitled to berth aboard whenever you like, which means practically that you'd better visit, because Reyes has already claimed the cabin across from yours and started arguing with the galley's temperature settings on your behalf.*

*Your father named his ship after the thing he loved most, because ships hear everything and deserve to carry good names. I've been thinking about that a lot. I think he was right, and I think he was also leaving instructions he didn't know he was writing. So here are mine: the Beatrix runs relief contracts now, out through the contested orbits, the routes I used to run scared and run openly. Every hold, every manifest, every honest ton of it carries your name into places that need reminding that somebody's daughter is watching. That's what I could make the inheritance mean. I hope it's enough. I suspect you'll tell me precisely how it isn't, at length, over terrible tea, and I find I'm looking forward to it.*

*One more thing. Anselm's marker rides in the forward hull now, under the plating where the ledger used to hide. Your father's work, his work, my work — finished together, as much as anything ever is. If you ever want to say hello to either of them, that's where they live. Ships hear everything. I figure brothers do too.*

*With respect, gratitude, and a standing dinner invitation,*

*Your father's echo, who finished the work.*

I sealed it, sent it, and stood in the galley of the Beatrix watching the confirmation light blink green, and Reyes came up beside me and handed me a fresh cup of tea without being asked.

"So," she said. "The great inheritance saga of E. Pemberton, closed."

"Not closed." I nodded at the nav console, where a new message pulsed in the queue — routed through Kestrel-9, of all the ancient, half-dead, stubbornly alive relays in the dark. An unknown sender. A ghost manifest, listing cargo that had never flown, bound for a port that barely existed, and a single line in the remarks field that made the hair on my arms stand up in the old familiar way:

*They say you find things other people lose. We lost something big. Name your price.*

Reyes read it over my shoulder and snorted. "Another one."

"Another one." I smiled, and plotted the course, and the horizon came up to meet us like it always does — not an ending, just the place where the next thing begins.

"Course laid in," I said. "Take us out, Ms. Reyes. Let's go see what somebody lost."

The Beatrix turned her bow toward the dark, carrying her good name home through the stars, and everything she heard was ours.
