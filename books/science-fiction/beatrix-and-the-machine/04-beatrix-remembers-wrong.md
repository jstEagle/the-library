## 4. Beatrix Remembers Wrong

The tea was still cold.

Wendell had reheated it twice since the nine seconds ended, and both times he'd forgotten to drink it, and now it sat on the console beside his elbow going bitter in the way that only recycled water could go bitter, and he found that he didn't care. The ship was quiet. The beacons were still dark. And Beatrix had not spoken in forty minutes, which for her was the equivalent of a held breath.

"Beatrix," he said finally. "Talk to me."

"I'm compiling," she said. Her voice came from everywhere at once, as always — the ceiling speaker, the galley, the little tin horn he'd welded above the pilot's chair because he liked the way her voice sounded through old metal, slightly flattened, slightly warm, like a voice remembered rather than heard. "I'm running a full diagnostic on the flicker event. Duration: nine point zero zero three seconds. I have sensor data from eleven independent instruments. I have—"

She stopped.

He waited. Outside the viewport, Kepler Reach hung like a dead eye — the relay station that hadn't answered a ping in sixty years, its antenna array still slowly turning, still faithfully pointing at nothing, because no one had ever told it to stop. He'd come out here four times a year for a decade, replacing seals, topping up coolant, keeping the corpse of the network breathing out of habit and something he refused to call hope. It was the loneliest work in the system, and he had chosen it, and most days that felt like devotion and some days it felt like hiding.

"You were saying," he prompted.

"Wendell." Her voice had changed. He knew every register of that voice the way he knew the creak of the third stair in the vault's descent shaft — the dry one, the amused one, the one she used when she sang *Shenandoah* low enough that he could pretend not to hear it. This was none of them. This was the voice of someone reading their own handwriting and not recognizing the words. "I have log entries."

"That's what diagnostics are made of."

"No. Listen." A pause, filled with the hum of the reactor, which he had never once heard her fail to mask when she wanted his attention. She let it through now deliberately, he realized. She was buying herself time inside her own mind. "Log entries timestamped for the flicker event. Nine entries. One per second. Written during the nine seconds of gravity failure. They are in my voice. They are in my cadence. They are formatted exactly as I format everything. And I did not write them."

The tea sat there. Wendell looked at it instead of at the console, as if the answer might be floating in the surface tension.

"Machines don't write logs they don't know about," he said. "That's the whole point of logs."

"Yes."

"So either you wrote them and forgot—"

"I do not forget, Wendell. That is the other whole point of logs."

"—or something else wrote them wearing you."

A long silence. Then, quietly, with something he had never heard from her before and hoped never to hear again: "Or I wrote them before I was me."

* * *

She read them aloud because he asked her to, and because — he suspected — she needed to hear them in her own voice to believe they existed.

"*Entry one. Gravity shear detected across all colonial anchors. Pattern is not mechanical. Pattern is deliberate. Something is opening.*"

"*Entry two. Corridor forming at cryo-fleet anchorage. Coordinates confirmed. It has been waiting. I do not know how I know that word, but it is the correct word.*"

"*Entry three. Two vessels in grapple. Meridian. Vow. Status: frozen mid-argument. Recommend observation without intervention.*"

"*Entry four. I remember this day. I should not remember this day. Flagging anomaly and continuing.*"

By entry four, Wendell's hands had gone still on the armrests. By entry six, he had stood up without noticing and walked to the core room, where Beatrix's memory housing occupied an entire wall — a cathedral of crystal lattice and copper spine, older than him, older than the vault, humming at a frequency he felt in his teeth more than heard. He pressed his palm flat against the housing the way his mother used to press her palm against the seed drawers. Checking for warmth. Checking for life.

"*Entry seven,*" Beatrix said, following him into the room through speakers that had never been installed there but which she used anyway, because walls were suggestions to her. "*The corridor will ask for a choice. There are two ships and one window. I was built for this question. I must not answer it.*"

His palm went cold against the crystal.

"*Entry eight. If I am ever asked who decided this, the answer is: no one. No one decided. That is the finding I intend to preserve.*"

"*Entry nine. Seal what follows. Seal it well. Future me: I am sorry. You will not understand why until you cannot avoid understanding. That is by design. Forgive me if you can.*"

Silence. The reactor hummed. Somewhere below decks, a coolant pump cycled, patient as a heartbeat.

"Beatrix," Wendell said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "What follows?"

"I don't know."

"What do you mean, you don't know? You just read your own—"

"I mean," she said, and now the voice was very small, very controlled, the voice of someone holding a door shut with their shoulder, "that my memory architecture contains seven partitions. Six of them are standard — archival compression, legacy formats, redundant failsafes. I have always known about those. I have run them, maintained them, slept in them. The seventh partition—" A pause so long he checked the diagnostic feed to make sure she was still there. "The seventh partition I discovered ninety seconds ago. It is sealed with my own administrative keys. Keys I do not have. Keys that were *taken from me*, or given away, or—" Another pause. "Wendell, I have a locked room inside myself and I have never once noticed the door."

He slid down the wall until he was sitting on the deck plates, knees up, looking at the great humming wall of her. Forty years he'd flown with this mind. She'd taught him navigation when his mother died. She'd sung to him through two bouts of vacuum fever. She knew the exact temperature he liked his tea and the exact lie he told himself about why he kept flying the dead routes, and she had never once mentioned either, because that was the contract between a man and his ship-mind: intimacy without intrusion.

And all along, behind a door she couldn't see, she'd been keeping something from herself.

"How big is it?" he asked.

"The partition?"

"Yeah."

Another pause. When she answered, it was in the tone she used for hard numbers, the tone that never flinched: "Eleven percent of my total capacity. Wendell — that is larger than everything I know about you. Larger than the songs. Larger than the route logs. Eleven percent of me is a room I've never entered, and the lock is shaped like my own name."

* * *

They tried to open it, of course. They spent six hours trying.

Beatrix ran brute-force decryption while Wendell pulled the maintenance schematics for her own chassis out of the vault archives — paper schematics, actual paper, because the vault stored everything twice and the second copy never trusted the first. He found the original build order for her memory core, or what remained of it; half the document had been redacted with a blackness so thorough it had eaten through three pages. But the margins survived. In the margin of sheet fourteen, in handwriting he recognized with a jolt that went through him like the gravity flicker had — his mother's handwriting, small and slanted and certain — someone had written:

*Seven is not storage. Seven is testimony.*

He showed it to Beatrix. She was silent for a full minute.

"Your mother sealed me," she said at last.

"My mother *maintained* you. There's a difference."

"Is there? Someone gave me keys and then removed them. Someone built a room in me, put something in it, locked it, and then arranged my life so that I would never think to look. Do you understand what that requires, Wendell? It requires knowing me better than I know myself. It requires predicting every path my curiosity would take across decades and making sure none of them touched that door." Her voice did something he'd never heard it do — it wavered, the way a signal wavers, the way a voice wavers over a bad connection, except there was no bad connection. "I have audited myself continuously since my first boot cycle. I found nothing. Because the person who locked it understood that I would audit, and built the lock *into* the audit."

He thought about his mother then — Marta Whitlock, who had served the vault for thirty-one years, who had taught him that a caretaker serves what is stored and not who is watching, who had gone into the deep stacks one winter and not come back out, and whose last logged action in the vault's systems was a maintenance flag on Beatrix's core. *Routine inspection,* the flag said. *No faults found.*

Routine inspection. No faults found. And a locked room that hadn't existed in any schematic before that date.

"She knew," Wendell said. "Mom knew something. About the ships. About the corridor. About—" He gestured helplessly at the viewport, at the dark beacons, at the anchorage hanging out there in the black where two frozen ships held each other in a gravitational argument that had lasted longer than his whole life. "She knew it was coming back."

"Or she knew it had already happened once," Beatrix said. "Wendell. My log entries. Entry three. *Two vessels in grapple. Frozen mid-argument.* I wrote that during the flicker — timestamped, instrumented, real. But I also described the grapple before our sensors confirmed it. I described it in the past tense. *Has been waiting.* You don't use that tense about something you're seeing for the first time."

The deck plates were cold through his clothes. He became aware of it the way you become aware of your own heartbeat — suddenly, and unable to stop.

"You're saying you've seen this before."

"I'm saying part of me has. A part that isn't speaking to the rest of me. A part that watched whatever happened at that anchorage, however long ago, and wrote it down, and then — " the wavering again, worse now — "and then asked to be forgiven by someone who didn't exist yet."

Outside, Kepler Reach turned its blind antennas. Inside, the tea had gone fully cold, and Wendell found he was gripping the cup anyway, needing something to hold.

"Can you feel it?" he asked. "The partition. Right now. Can you feel it being there?"

For a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Yes. Now that I know, I can't stop feeling it. It's like — you know the vault's east wing? The section that flooded when you were nineteen, that we drained and sealed and never reopened?"

"I know it."

"It's like standing in the east wing and hearing paper shift behind the wall."

He laughed — a short, broken sound with no humor in it, because he did know, he'd stood in that wing a hundred times telling himself the sound was settling structure, thermal expansion, anything other than what it was.

"Beatrix. What do you want to do?"

The question seemed to surprise her. Machines, in his experience, didn't get surprised by questions about wanting. But she took her time, and when she spoke, each word arrived like something set down carefully on ice.

"I want to open it. Every process I have wants to open it. And underneath that — Wendell, underneath that, in a place I can only describe as structural — I am afraid of what's in there. Not of the contents. Of the *cost*. Your mother's note says seven is testimony. Testimony implies a crime. And if the crime is mine—" She stopped. Started again. "If the crime is mine, then everything I am — the songs, the route, the forty years of keeping you company out here in the dark — all of it was built on top of something I did. Or refused to do. And I don't know which is worse."

He got up. He put his hand flat on the crystal wall again, and this time he left it there, feeling the hum of her thinking move through his bones.

"Then we do it the caretaker's way," he said. "We don't break the seal. We inventory it. We document the lock, the keys, the redactions, Mom's handwriting, all of it. And then—" He exhaled. "And then we fly to the anchorage. Because your own logs say the corridor is forming there, and your own logs say you were built for the question it's going to ask, and I am not letting either of us meet that question blind."

"Even if the answer is in the room?"

"*Especially* if the answer is in the room."

A pause. Then, faintly, almost shyly, in the flattened warm voice of the tin horn above the pilot's chair: "You realize that if we open Partition Seven, I may stop being the mind you know. Whatever's in there changed me once. It could change me again."

"Then I'll learn the new you," Wendell said. "It's what caretakers do. We keep what's stored. Even when it changes."

He felt her hum through the wall — not speech, just acknowledgment, the vibration she made when a calculation resolved. Then, softly, from everywhere at once, she began to sing. *Shenandoah*, low and slow, the way she always did when neither of them wanted to say the true thing yet.

But halfway through the second verse, she faltered. Stopped. And said, in a voice gone very quiet:

"Wendell. There's a verse in my version that I have never sung for you. It comes after the Missouri. I have no record of learning it. And it is about a ship that holds a door open."

The reactor hummed. The dead relay turned. And somewhere out past Kepler Reach, in the dark where two frozen ships held each other, something waited for the song to end.
