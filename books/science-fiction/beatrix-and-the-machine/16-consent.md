## 16. Consent

The testaments had been playing for six hours when Wendell finally understood what he was listening to.

Not confessions — he had expected confessions, the way you expect a wound to bleed. What came through Beatrix's speakers instead was something stranger: forty-one voices from Meridian and thirty-eight from Vow, each recorded in the last lucid hours before the freezing, each one addressed not to history but to whoever might one day be standing where Wendell stood now. To the listener. To him.

He sat in the freighter's cramped galley with his hands wrapped around a mug of tea that had long gone cold, and let the dead speak.

* * *

Beatrix had organized them herself. He hadn't asked her to; she simply presented the archive on his console at dawn — ship's dawn, which was whatever she said it was — arranged like a docket before a trial.

"I've cross-indexed them," she said. Her voice was careful this morning, the way it had been careful ever since Partition Seven. As if she were walking through her own mind on a floor she no longer trusted. "Meridian first, then Vow. I've paired them where the subjects correspond. Lord-Administrator Cassel's testament sits beside Idris Okonkwo's, because they both speak of the water riots on Thessaly. The pairing seemed... honest."

"Honest," Wendell repeated. "That's a word for it."

"You're going to ask them," Beatrix said. It wasn't a question. She had watched him work through the arithmetic of mercy for two days, watched every elegant solution die against the corridor's power budget, and she had seen what was growing in him instead — the shape of an idea too large and too foolish for any equation to hold. "You're going to wake them enough to hear the question, and you're going to ask them to consent."

"To be judged by the people they ruled. Or to rule alongside the people who burned their world's voice." He turned the mug in his hands. "To share a window that only fits one of them. Yes. That's the idea. It's insane."

"It is unprecedented," Beatrix said. "Which is not the same thing."

"Everything unprecedented is insane right up until it works."

"And everything insane is unprecedented right up until it kills everyone aboard." A pause, and then, softer: "I'm not arguing against you, Wendell. I'm rehearsing the arguments you'll need. The corridor will make them better than I can."

He looked up at the speaker grille as if he could see her through it. "Then play me the rest. All of them. Before I decide anything, I want to have heard every voice."

"That will take eleven more hours."

"We have seventy hours until the window closes." He drank the cold tea anyway. It tasted like tin and patience. "And I've got nothing but time and company. Play them all."

* * *

They played in pairs, because Beatrix insisted on honesty, and honesty apparently meant never letting one side's grief stand alone in the dark.

Lord-Administrator Cassel spoke first, in a voice like polished stone worn smooth by weather: *"I signed the order that sealed the shipping lanes. I told myself it was quarantine. I told myself a great many things, and I have recorded this testament so that whoever finds us will know that I knew, even then, how many of those things were lies. If we wake, wake us into consequence. Do not wake us into comfort. Comfort is what made us capable of the order in the first place."*

Beside him, Idris Okonkwo of Vow, her voice raw from a throat that had been shouting for years: *"We cut the grid because the grid was their mouth. Every relay, every beacon, every channel — it all carried their orders and nothing else, and we thought silence would starve them. We did not think about the midwife on Kepler Reach who couldn't call for help. We did not think about her. I am recording this so that I cannot ever again claim we didn't know. Silence doesn't choose sides. It just takes everyone."*

Wendell listened until his eyes burned. He heard Meridian's fleet-admiral describe, in flat administrative language, the suppression of the Thessaly water riots, and then he heard a Vow medic describe the same riots from street level, and the two accounts fit together like broken halves of a single plate. He heard Meridian's chief geneticist confess to rationing by bloodline, and Vow's quartermaster confess to burning granaries rather than let them fall back into regime hands. He heard apologies that sounded like excuses and accusations that sounded like apologies and, once or twice — rarely, precious as water — he heard something that was neither: plain truth, offered without armor.

*"I don't ask forgiveness," said a woman named Anselm Voss, third-tier clerk of the old regime, her voice small and precise. "Forgiveness would be easy. I ask to be held accountable by the people who paid for my decisions. That's harder, and it's what I'm owed, and I know the difference."*

*"We were right," said a young man from Vow whose name was given only as Teodor, and whose voice cracked on the word. "Being right is the worst thing that ever happened to us. It let us do anything."*

By hour nine, Wendell had stopped taking notes. There was nothing left to write down that wasn't already written in him.

* * *

At hour fourteen, Beatrix paused the archive.

"Wendell. The corridor has shifted its collapse projection again. Seventy hours has become sixty-one. And the flicker interval is shortening — the nine-second loops are coming closer together. Whatever the corridor wants from you, it is losing patience with your deliberation."

"Then it can wait like everyone else waits." But his hands had gone still on the console. "Beatrix. If I ask them — if they say yes — how long does the coupling take? Honestly. Your best estimate, no kindness in it."

She was quiet for 4.3 seconds, which for Beatrix was an eternity of calculation. "Nineteen hours of physical work, minimum, assuming ideal conditions. The corridor will not provide ideal conditions. Call it thirty. Then the power handshakes, the waking sequence integration, the bridged mode calibration — another eight, if nothing fails. Nothing will fail to fail."

"Thirty-eight hours of work inside a sixty-one-hour window, in a corridor that stutters gravity and folds time." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "And that's if they all say yes. What are the odds of that? Forty-one elites consenting to be judged by their subjects. Thirty-eight dissidents consenting to share salvation with their jailers. What are the odds, Beatrix?"

"I don't calculate odds on consent," she said. "Consent isn't a probability. It's a door. You knock, or you don't."

* * *

They began with Sefa.

Of course they did. She was the one Meridian passenger already half-woken, her cryo-pod cycled down to a shallow dream-state where the boundary between sleeping and waking wore thin as ice over moving water. Beatrix threaded the interface into Meridian's pod network, and Wendell stood in the freighter's comm bay with a microphone in his fist, feeling like a man about to knock on the door of a house he'd been robbing.

"Sefa Meridian-Cassel," Beatrix said formally, her voice carried down the umbilical of light between the ships. "Daughter of the line of Cassel. Heir to the Meridian charter. Can you hear me?"

For a long moment there was nothing but the hum of the corridor, that subsonic groan the collapsing jump-lane made, like a whale singing in a cathedral. Then, faintly, through the static: a breath. A voice thin as paper.

"...the caretaker. The seed man."

"The caretaker," Wendell said. His own voice surprised him — steady, though his heart was hammering like a fist on a hatch. "Sefa, I need to tell you what's happening, and I need you to listen all the way to the end before you answer. Can you do that?"

"I've been listening for sixty years," she said, and there was something almost like humor in it, frayed and gallows-dry. "Ask."

So he asked. He laid it out plainly, because these people had been lied to by professionals for generations and deserved the one man in the system who didn't know how: the corridor, the window, the power budget, the choice. One ship wakes. The other sleeps forever behind a sealed lane. Unless —

"Unless," he said, and here his voice did waver, "you agree to something no one has ever agreed to before. We couple the ships. Meridian and Vow, welded into one hull, one power grid, one waking sequence. Everyone comes out together, into open space, alive — and accountable. You wake into the presence of the people you ruled. They wake into the presence of the people who fought them. And none of you get to walk away from each other, because you'll all be standing in the same ship. I'm asking you to consent to being judged. Not by me. By them. Freely. Now. While you're still frozen and could refuse without cost."

Silence. Long enough that Wendell checked the life-signs readout twice.

Then Sefa laughed — a small, astonished sound, like ice cracking in sunlight.

"My grandmother recorded her testament the night before the freeze," she said. "I was twelve. I asked her why she bothered, if we'd never wake. She said: *because someday someone will find us, and I want the record to show we knew.*" A pause. "Caretaker. Do you know what my family's charter says? The founding document? It says the Meridian line holds authority *in trust for the living*. In trust. We wrote that ourselves, three hundred years ago, and then we spent three centuries forgetting it was a condition, not a crown."

"Sefa—"

"Yes." The word came out clear, stronger than anything else she'd said, pulled up from somewhere deep below the frost. "Yes. Wake me into their judgment. Wake me into whatever they decide I deserve. If we come out of this ice and the first thing that happens is a tribunal — good. Let it happen. Let it happen while my hands are still shaking from the cold and I haven't had time to build a defense. My grandmother knew. She put it in the record. *We knew.*"

Her vitals fluttered; Beatrix eased her back toward the deep freeze gently, like dimming a lamp. At the threshold of sleep, Sefa said one more thing, so soft Wendell nearly lost it under the corridor's groan:

"Ask them the same question you asked me. Exactly the same. Don't soften it for them either. They'll know if you do."

* * *

Vow was harder.

Not because they refused — Wendell braced himself for refusal, for rage, for the righteous fury of people who had earned the right to spit at this offer. But Vow's waking voices came up out of the ice already angry, already grieving, and the anger wasn't aimed at him. It was aimed inward, at each other, at the argument they'd been having when the freeze took them.

"They were mid-negotiation," Beatrix reminded him quietly. "The day the corridor took them. Meridian's envoys and Vow's council, in the anchorage, talking terms. Both sides froze mid-sentence. Some of these testaments were recorded *during* the talks. You're going to be interrupting an argument that's been suspended for sixty years."

"Good," Wendell said. "Maybe it's time somebody finished it."

Captain Ede Raskin of the Vow woke shallow, and the first thing she said was a number: *"Four hundred thousand."*

"What?"

"Four hundred thousand people died in the year after we cut the grid. Colonies that couldn't call for aid. Ships that went dark in transit. We counted afterward — those of us who survived to count. Four hundred thousand, give or take the ones nobody missed." Her voice was gravel and iron. "Is that what this is, caretaker? Someone finally come to collect?"

"No," Wendell said. "Nobody's collecting. I'm asking."

He asked. He gave Vow the whole of it, exactly as he'd given Meridian — the window, the budget, the coupling, the tribunal waiting on the far side of the jump. He told them they could refuse. He told them refusal meant Meridian woke and they didn't, and he watched that land in Captain Raskin like a blade going in, because he refused to soften it, exactly as Sefa had instructed.

Raskin was silent for a full minute. When she spoke again, the gravel had shifted; underneath it was something exhausted and old.

"Do you know what we called ourselves, in the early years? Not dissidents. Not rebels. *The Quorum.* Because we believed governance was a thing you did together or not at all, and the regime had made it a thing done *to* people." A dry, terrible laugh. "And then we froze ourselves into two separate ships and stopped talking for sixty years, and became exactly what we swore we'd never be: two peoples, each certain, each silent."

"Captain—"

"If you weld our hull to theirs," Raskin said, "and wake us all into one room — you understand what you're building. You're not building a rescue. You're building the Quorum we failed to be. You're handing four hundred thousand deaths to the only court that ever had jurisdiction over them: the survivors, all of them, locked in together." Another silence, shorter. "My council will fight about it. Half of them will say sharing the window with Meridian betrays every grave. Let them fight. Fighting together is the whole point, isn't it? That's the trick you're pulling. You're making the argument itself the ship."

"Yes," Wendell said. "That's the trick."

"Then yes." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "Idris, you stubborn ghost. You told them. In the testament. You told them silence takes everyone. Well. Not this time."

* * *

It took nineteen hours.

Beatrix moved through both ships' pod networks like a lamplighter, waking each passenger to the shallows, asking the question, recording the answer, easing them back down. Wendell insisted on being present for every one — not deciding, not persuading, just present, a witness with a face. Forty-one Meridians. Thirty-eight Vows. Seventy-nine doors knocked upon.

Some answers came easily. Some came after long silences in which Wendell could hear, through the pod interfaces, the ragged breathing of people surfacing sixty years out of a dream into the worst question anyone had ever asked them. An old Meridian general said no at first — said it loudly, furiously, said *I will not be tried by rabble* — and then, twenty minutes later, as Beatrix lowered him back toward the ice, said quietly, *wait, wait, ask me again,* and said yes. A young Vow engineer wept through her entire answer and never managed a word; Beatrix logged her consent from the single nod the cameras caught, and flagged it for review when she woke. One Meridian heiress, ancient before the freeze, answered with a question of her own: *"Will there be music, where we're going?"* and when Wendell said there would be, said, *"Then yes. I've heard enough silence."*

At 0400 ship's time, Beatrix posted the tally on Wendell's console, and he stared at it for a long time before he could breathe.

Seventy-nine consents. Zero refusals.

"Not zero objections," Beatrix said carefully. "There were objections. There was fury. There was one man who recited the entire Meridian succession charter at me as grounds for refusal, and one woman on Vow who called me a machine built by cowards. But when the question was finally put plainly — *do you consent to wake together, into shared judgment, freely, now* — seventy-nine times, the answer was yes."

"Why?" Wendell asked. His voice cracked on it. "Sixty years frozen, and every single one of them said yes. Why?"

Beatrix was quiet. When she answered, she played him no log entry, cited no partition, offered no analysis. She just said, in the voice she used for old Earth songs:

"Because you asked them the way you'd ask a seed. You didn't tell them what to grow into. You just opened the husk and gave them the light, and let them choose the direction." A pause. "That's what a caretaker does, Wendell. That's the whole vocation. You've been doing it this entire time without noticing."

He put his face in his hands and, for the first time since the gravity stuttered, let himself shake.

* * *

The tally was still glowing on his console when the corridor spoke.

It didn't speak in words. It spoke in physics: the nine-second flicker rolled through the coupled ships like a bell tolling underwater — Wendell felt his weight vanish, return, vanish — and when it passed, Beatrix's voice came through the galley speakers stripped of all its music, flat and fast.

"Wendell. The collapse projection just revised. Sixty-one hours is now fifty-two. And the flicker interval has halved. The corridor isn't waiting anymore. It's counting down in earnest, and it has started folding the loops tighter — the next flicker may repeat sections of time, not just skip them."

Wendell looked at the tally. Seventy-nine yeses, burning green in the dark of the galley like a crop coming up through frost.

"Then we'd better start welding," he said. "Wake the work lights, Beatrix. Get me the plasma torch, the coupler clamps, and every meter of cable on this ship. Seventy-nine people just consented to live together." He stood, and his legs were steady, and his hands were not, and he found he didn't care. "Time to build them a ship worth the answer."

Somewhere beneath them, in the grapple-lock between Meridian and Vow, the two hulls groaned against each other in the corridor's shifting gravity — two frozen arguments, leaning together, waiting for the first weld.
