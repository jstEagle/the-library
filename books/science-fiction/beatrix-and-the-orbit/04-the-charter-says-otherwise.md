## 4. The Charter Says Otherwise

The dream did not come to Sefu alone this time. That was the first thing he understood, even before he opened his eyes — that the fire had arrived in the settlement like weather, falling on every roof at once.

He woke with his mother's hands on his shoulders and his own voice already hoarse, as if he had been screaming for hours instead of minutes. The room was dark except for the glow-strip above the door, and the air smelled of the protein-yeast bread his mother baked on rest-days, a smell that should have meant safety and instead felt like something happening very far away from him, on the other side of a wall of heat.

"Mama," he said. "The river."

"I know." Her voice was strange — flat and careful, the way it got when she was frightened and didn't want him to see. "I know, baby. It's just a fever going around. Half the kids in the settlement have it."

But Sefu had never heard of a fever that put the same pictures in everyone's head. He lay still while she wiped his face with a cool cloth, and he counted what he knew the way he counted everything now, because counting made the fire smaller: the burning village built in rings around a well; the long street running down toward water; the bridge of woven cable; the ash that moved like a slow gray river through the lanes, swallowing doorways one by one. And the names. That was the part he hadn't told anyone, not even Tam. Names that came into his mouth whole, like words in a language he'd been speaking all his life without knowing it: *Meridian*. *Ayo*. *Kessu-of-the-third-house*. A woman's name that made his chest ache when he thought it, though he had never met her and never would.

*Ilane.*

He said it silently, testing it, and the ache bloomed again, grief with no address to send itself to.

His mother tucked the blanket under his arms the way she'd done when he was small. "No more talk of rivers," she said gently. "Doctor Amara says it's the new pollen from the east terraces. Your brain is making stories out of an itch."

Sefu didn't argue. Arguing made her grip the cloth tighter. But he lay awake after she left, watching the ceiling, and thought about how an itch doesn't know the names of dead people.

* * *

By second-shift the next day, the whole settlement knew. Not because anyone announced it — because the children told each other, in the feral efficient way children have, passing the dream between them like contraband at the cistern, in the hydroponics rows, behind the drying racks where the adults' eyes couldn't follow.

Sefu found Tam and the Adeyemi twins by the south fence, where the ground fell away toward the ash plain. They were doing the thing he had caught them doing for three days now: drawing. Every child in the settlement was drawing the same village, over and over, in dust and charcoal and scratched lines on ration tins. Tam's version had the bridge. Little Nia Adeyemi's had the well, ringed with houses like petals. None of them had shown each other their drawings first. When they laid them side by side, the pieces fit.

"Third house past the well has a blue door," Nia said. She was six and spoke with the terrible confidence of six-year-olds stating facts. "The paint is made of crushed shell. I can smell it."

"You can't smell a dream," said her twin, Obi, who was trying very hard to be the sensible one and failing, because his own drawing showed the same door.

"I can smell *that* one."

Sefu crouched and added a detail to Tam's sketch with one finger: the long street, and where it bent around the tanner's yard before reaching the water. "It bends here," he said. "There's a dog. Yellow, with a crooked ear. It runs ahead of you when you go down to the river."

Nobody asked how he knew. That was the strangest part — how quickly they had stopped asking. On the first night there had been crying and questions. By the third day there was only the quiet industry of children comparing notes on a place none of them had ever been, building it together out of memory that wasn't theirs, brick by brick, name by name.

"Meridian," Tam said. Just to say it. The word sat in the air between them.

"And Ilane," Sefu said, and his throat closed. Tam looked at him. Tam had a name too; everyone did. Sefu could see it sitting behind his friend's eyes, worn smooth from handling.

"Your sister says the navigator lady climbs the tower every week," Obi offered suddenly, changing the subject the way boys do when a feeling gets too big. "My brother saw her. She talks to it. To the beacon. Like it answers."

They all looked at each other. None of them had ever seen Jude Joubert up close — she kept to herself, up the hill, a tall gray figure against the sky — but the beacon was theirs too, in a way. It stood over the settlement like a lighthouse over a sea they'd never sailed. And last night, in the dream, standing in the burning lane while the ash-river rose past his knees, Sefu had looked up — the way you look up in dreams, knowing things you shouldn't — and seen, impossibly far above the smoke, a small steady light. Blinking. Patient. Like something keeping count.

Like something listening.

* * *

Administrator Voss held the meeting in the assembly hall on fourth-day, and the whole settlement came, because when Voss called a meeting, attendance was noted.

Sefu sat wedged between his mother and old Marisol, high enough on the benches to see the whole room. Voss stood at the front beneath the charter seal — the great embossed disc of the Meridian Colonial Charter, iron-gray, its motto picked out in faded gilt: *A NEW WORLD, PROPERLY KEPT.* Behind him, someone had hung the survey display, and it glowed with the maps Sefu had grown up under: the continent, the landing site, the green terraces, the ash plain marked in the pale hatching that meant *barren, uninhabited, no structures of interest.*

"People are frightened," Voss began, and his voice was warm, reasonable, the voice of a man handing out blankets. "Frightened people deserve facts. So here are the facts." He touched the display. "This world was surveyed three times before we ever left Earth. Three independent surveys. No settlements. No ruins. No signs of intelligent life anywhere on this continent. The land we farm was empty when our grandparents landed, and it is empty now."

He let that stand a moment. Then, softer: "What your children are experiencing is a shared fever response. It happens. Doctors call it mass psychogenic illness — one anxious child, then two, then a schoolroom. The mind is a social animal, friends. It catches stories the way it catches colds. Doctor Amara will tell you the same."

Sefu watched the adults. This was the part that frightened him more than the fire: watching them *want* to believe it. He saw relief move through the benches like wind through wheat. His mother's shoulders came down from around her ears. Old Marisol nodded along, murmuring. Fever. Pollen. An itch. Anything but the alternative, which was that the ground under their feet had a history, and the history had teeth.

A hand went up near the back — Devi Okoro, whose daughter drew the well every single day. "And the names?" she asked. "Children don't invent names in unison. My Ada woke up saying *Kessu* and so did the Okafor boy, and neither of them had ever—"

"The mind supplies plausible detail," Voss said smoothly. "Shared vocabulary, shared naming conventions — we all draw from the same linguistic pool. It feels uncanny. Uncanny is not evidence." He smiled, and it was a good smile, practiced in front of mirrors. "I understand how it looks. I promise you, if there were anything — *anything* — in the survey record suggesting otherwise, I would be the first person to sound the alarm. But the record is clean. Pristine. Three surveys, zero anomalies. We have nothing here but a hard week and some bad dreams, and I will not have this community scaring itself over ghosts."

*Ghosts,* Sefu thought. His stomach turned over. In the dream, the ash had risen past the windowsills of the third house, the one with the blue door, and something had been inside it, singing low, and the song had not been a ghost-song. Ghosts were finished with the world. Whatever sang in the blue-doored house was still here. Still waiting. Still *counting*, maybe, the way the beacon light counted.

He raised his hand. He was nine years old in a hall full of adults, and his arm shook, and he raised it anyway.

Voss saw him. Something flickered across the administrator's face — quick, quickly smoothed — and Sefu had the sudden animal certainty that Voss knew exactly which child he was looking at. "Yes, young man?"

"If the surveys were clean," Sefu said, and his voice cracked and he pushed through it, "how come the beacon's maintenance logs start before we landed?"

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere a bench creaked. Sefu heard his mother whisper his name like a warning, and old Marisol's dry hand close over his knee.

Voss's smile stayed exactly where it was, which was somehow worse than if it had fallen. "That," he said pleasantly, "is a clerical artifact. Timestamp conventions changed during transit. I'm surprised a child knows the beacon logs at all." A ripple of uneasy laughter, grateful for permission. "Which brings me to my final point. As a precaution against further — excitement — the beacon transmitter will be taken offline for scheduled review. Standard procedure. Nothing to worry about."

Behind him, the survey map glowed on, pale and pristine, the ash plain hatched in its neat lie of *no structures of interest*, and Sefu stared at it until his eyes burned, because he knew — the way he knew the bend of the street and the yellow dog and the smell of crushed-shell paint — that under that hatching, under the ash, the singer was still singing.

And somewhere up the hill, the woman who talked to the beacon was about to lose the only other ears on this world that were listening.
