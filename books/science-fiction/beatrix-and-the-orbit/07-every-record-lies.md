## 7. Every Record Lies

The evidence fit inside a ration tin.

Jude had carried it down from Meridian in her coat pocket, against her sternum, the way you carry a thing that is too heavy for its size. A shard of fused foundation-glass. A child's radius bone, small as a bird's, still wearing its pale rind of ash. And a strip of beacon foil — the old alloy, pre-landing manufacture — stamped with a serial number that matched no entry in the colonial registry. She had wrapped each piece in cloth like relics, and maybe that was what they were. The only sacraments an erased place could leave behind.

She walked into the administration hall at midmorning, when the light through the high windows fell in long amber bars and the smell of recycled air and hot laminate was at its thickest. Voss was at his desk with his sleeves rolled, signing supply manifests in his neat, bloodless hand. He looked up and did not seem surprised to see her. That frightened her more than anything else could have.

"Navigator," he said. "You've been out past the survey line."

"I've been to Meridian."

The pen stopped moving. For a moment the hall was so quiet she could hear the ventilation breathing, the slow mechanical tide of it, and beneath that — faintly, impossibly — the hum of the beacon carrying up through the bedrock, through the foundations, through everything they had built on top of what they had buried.

Voss set the pen down parallel to the desk's edge. He folded his hands. He had the stillness of a man who had rehearsed this moment the way Jude rehearsed beacon calibrations: thoroughly, and alone, and for years.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

She laid the tin's contents out on the polished surface between them, one piece at a time, the way you deal cards or lay wounds open. The glass first — dark, warped, bubbled where sand had melted and refrozen into something no storm makes. Then the bone. Then the foil.

"The fusion pattern is directional," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which felt like a betrayal of the bone. "It radiates from a single point above the village center. Orbital delivery. The charter says Beatrix never fired from orbit. The charter says the survey found no intelligent life. The charter says—" She stopped. Started again. "The charter lies, Administrator. Every page of it."

Voss looked at the bone for a long time. He did not flinch. That was its own kind of horror — the practiced calm of a man looking at something he had always known was there.

"Do you know what I did before this posting?" he asked.

"You were charter compliance. Earthside."

"I was records integrity." He said it the way other men say *undertaker*. "Eleven years ago, before the landing, I received the final version of the survey archive. My job was to verify it. To certify that what we brought down here was true." He turned the shard of glass over with one finger, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't trust. "It took me four days to find the seams. Four days, Navigator, and I was looking. Do you understand what that means? The rewrite wasn't done in haste. It was done by professionals. Every sensor log, every spectral analysis, every atmospheric reading — regenerated, not deleted. Deleted data leaves holes. This left none. It left a world with no one in it, rendered so completely that even the instruments would have sworn to it."

"Who ordered it?"

He almost smiled. There was no humor in it anywhere; it was just a muscle remembering an older shape. "You know who. You've been climbing to their tower every week for eleven years."

The words went through her like cold water through a crack in ice. "The crew."

"The crew." He nodded slowly. "Two hundred minds in the hold, voting on the fate of a world they'd never walk on. I have the minutes, if you want them. Session 44-C, held during the final approach, while the rest of us slept in our berths like good cargo." He pulled a slate from his drawer and set it down but did not activate it. "They found the settlement at Meridian on day six of orbital survey. First contact protocols were drafted. Osei herself wrote the opening address — I've read it. It's beautiful, Navigator. It's generous. It talks about shared sky and common inheritance." His voice flattened. "Then the dreamer reached up and touched the ship. Not attacked. *Touched.* Reached into the sleepers' dreams the way it reaches into your children's dreams now, showing them fire, showing them loss, showing them what had been done to it long ago by someone else — because the dreamer remembers other ships, other landings, older massacres. And two hundred brilliant, careful, loving people woke from that contact and voted, unanimously, that a species capable of reaching into human minds at planetary range could never be permitted to exist beneath a colony it might one day turn."

"They killed children because they were afraid of dreams."

"They killed children because they were afraid," Voss said, "and fear writes better records than truth does. The vote was unanimous. I checked it three times, hoping I'd misread. Unanimous. Two hundred to nothing. Even the ones who wept. Even Osei." He finally activated the slate and turned it toward her: rows of names, luminous and orderly, every signature a small act of erasure. "Your mentors. Your family. The people who raised you out of the crèche and taught you the stars. They looked at a living world and voted it empty, and then they went to sleep, and left us standing on the grave holding the deed."

Jude looked at the signatures. She knew the hands. She had seen Osei write hers ten thousand times on training reports, on birthday messages piped into Jude's bunk, on the flight plans that had taught her the difference between navigation and destiny. The same letters. The same confident loops. Signed here, under a resolution whose title she couldn't read without her vision swimming: *Disposition of Indigenous Anomaly.*

"Anomaly," she said. Her mouth had gone dry as ash. "A village full of children was an anomaly."

"A problem," Voss said quietly, "is only a problem until the paperwork is clean. After that it's history. And history, Navigator, is whatever survives the jump home." He leaned back. "Here is what you need to understand. If this comes out — if the bone and the glass and the dreamer's testimony reach Earth — then every claim this colony has is void. Our landing permit rests on an empty-world certification. Our water rights, our mineral charters, our children's citizenship. Everything. Two thousand lives built on a document those two hundred minds falsified while the rest of us slept. Expose them and you don't free anyone. You unmake everyone. Including the children who are dreaming right now, including Sefu Okonkwo, including you. You'd be burning the only home any of us has ever had to warm the ghost of a village none of us ever saw."

"And if we don't?"

"Then in nineteen days the window opens, the confession stays buried, the dreamer keeps dreaming, and your colonists' children inherit a covenant written in someone else's blood." He spread his hands. "I am not telling you the lie is right, Navigator. I'm telling you it's load-bearing. Pull it out and see what falls."

* * *

She walked back up through the settlement in the hour before dusk, and the colony looked different to her now, the way a face looks different once you've seen the skull underneath. The prefab homes with their solar skins drinking the last light. The hydroponics domes glowing green and innocent. Children in the lanes playing a game with chalk lines and thrown stones, shrieking, alive, oblivious — or not oblivious, she thought, watching one small girl pause mid-run and stare west toward the ash plain with an expression far too old for her face. Not oblivious. Inheriting.

Everything she belonged to was built on erasure. The tower she tended. The hold she guarded. The name *Beatrix*, which she had always thought meant hope, and which apparently meant *certification*. Eleven years of lonely devotion to a machine that sang lullabies over a massacre, and she had called it duty. She had called it love.

And yet.

The bone was still in her pocket. She hadn't given Voss all of it. The smallest fragment — a finger joint, hardly bigger than a grain of rice — sat against her palm now, and she closed her fist around it as she climbed the hill toward the beacon tower, where the transmitter pulsed its patient rhythm into the darkening sky, receiving, always receiving, the dreams rising off the plain like heat shimmer.

Nineteen days. Nineteen days until the window opened and the directive in the hold unlocked itself, and whatever the crew had left for her — apology or absolution or orders — came due.

But there was another clock she trusted more. The children weren't waking. She'd heard it in the lanes tonight, in the low frantic voices of parents: Sefu Okonkwo hadn't opened his eyes in thirty hours, and neither had eleven others, lying slack and breathing in their beds while somewhere beneath the ash the dreamer held them, showed them, refused to let go.

Voss thought the lie was load-bearing. He was wrong about what was bearing the weight. It wasn't the charter. It was the children — and they had stopped coming back.

Jude stood at the base of the tower with her fist closed around a dead child's finger bone and made her decision the way she made all of them: alone, in the dark, with the beacon humming overhead like a heart that wouldn't stop.

Tomorrow she would go down into the hold.

Tomorrow she would wake Captain Osei.
