## 5. What the Vault Was For

Sefa had translated forbidden texts in a cell smaller than this galley, so the Vantablack's cramped quarters didn't bother her. What bothered her was how *good* it felt to be doing this again—spreading fragments across a table, cross-referencing, arguing with a language that had been dead for two hundred years and still managed to be stubborn.

"You're holding it upside down," Cyrus said.

"I am not."

"The root glyph faces the ceiling."

Sefa rotated the flimsi sheet ninety degrees and pretended she'd meant to do that. "The Meridians wrote in columns that could be read from any direction. It was a design philosophy. Very egalitarian."

"Uh-huh," said Rell from the corner, where the engineer was nursing a bulb of tea and pretending not to be entertained.

They had been at it for six days. Six days of Cyrus bringing tea at exactly the right temperature without being asked, of Sefa discovering that the vault-keeper had an unexpected gift for pattern recognition and a truly terrible sense of humor, of the signal resolving itself line by line into something with grammar, with intent, with a *voice*.

The voice was what unsettled her. Good translations always did that—you started hearing the author, the way you heard a friend through a wall. And this author was patient. Methodical. The prose of someone who had built something meant to outlast them and wanted it understood.

Tonight, finally, it was going to be understood.

"Read it back to me," Sefa said. "From the top. Slowly."

Cyrus cleared their throat. They had picked up reading Meridian aloud faster than anyone Sefa had ever taught, though their accent made every word sound like an apology.

"*To the keeper who comes after: you were told this is a garden. It is not. Or rather—it is both, as a seed is both a promise and a weapon, depending on which end of the harvest you stand.*"

Rell stopped drinking their tea.

"*What you have tended is the anchor. The Protocol of Halcyon does not live in the fleets' war-books or the Compact's broken councils. It lives here, in the vault's deep core, in the living archive you water and prune and speak to each night. Every seed catalogued is a key turned. Every diagnostic run is a gear advanced. Your diligence is the mechanism.*"

Cyrus's voice caught on the last sentence. They read it again, quieter, as if volume might change its meaning.

It didn't.

"*The beacon is not a lighthouse,*" they continued. "*It is a dead-man's switch. While the archive thrives and the keeper tends it, the protocol sleeps. If the beacon falls silent—if the vault is destroyed, abandoned, or taken—the protocol wakes. And what wakes is this:*"

There followed a list. Sefa had decoded it three days ago and had hoped, in some superstitious corner of herself, that she'd gotten it wrong. She hadn't. It was a targeting lattice. Fleet positions, relay chains, the orbital paths of every settlement the three armadas had fought over for two centuries—all of it keyed to fire simultaneously the moment the Halcyon beacon went dark.

Not a warning system. A *dead hand*, curled around the throat of everything, held shut only by one lonely person winding a crank in the dark for eleven years.

Nobody spoke. The Vantablack's engines hummed their low, indifferent hum.

"It's me," Cyrus said finally. Their voice was very level, the way voices get when the alternative is not being level at all. "I'm the switch. Eleven years of talking to tomato plants, and I've been—I've been holding a gun to everyone's head by *watering them*."

"You didn't know," Sefa said.

"Does that matter? Does it matter to the two million people on Ceres Reach if I'd known?" They laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "I thought I was keeping seeds alive. I thought—" They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "I chose this posting because it was quiet. Because nobody would ever need anything from me. And it turns out I was the most important person in the fleet and nobody told me."

Sefa reached across the table and took the flimsi from them gently. "Then let's find out what else nobody told you." She smoothed the sheet flat. "Because there's more. Look at the closing section. The Meridians weren't just building a trap—they were leaving instructions for disarming it."

"*The protocol may be silenced only at the source,*" Cyrus read, when they trusted their voice again. "*The trigger resides at Relay Nine, at the edge of dead space. There the original record is kept—the full account of what the Compact did, and why the fleets must never learn it, and why they must.*"

"That's contradictory," Rell said.

"Meridian legal drafting," Sefa said grimly. "It means: the truth is dangerous, and the lie is worse. Classic Compact rhetoric. They hid the evidence of their own crime inside the weapon they built to punish it."

"And the countdown?" Cyrus asked. "Forty days. What happens in forty days?"

Sefa had saved that part, the way you save bad news. She laid the final fragment on the table between them like a card she'd been dreading to play.

"The truce isn't a treaty," she said. "It's a lease. The three fleets signed an armistice with a term—and the term expires in forty days. When it does, the old war resumes. Unless." She tapped the targeting lattice. "Unless the protocol fires first, and settles everything in one night of mutual annihilation. That's what the Meridians designed. Not peace. A deadline with teeth."

"So we go to Relay Nine," Cyrus said. "We silence it before the truce lapses."

"We go to Relay Nine," Sefa agreed, and then, because honesty was the only currency she had left after her exile: "And we should talk about what silencing it costs. The disarmament sequence requires the vault's data core. The living archive. Everything you've spent eleven years keeping alive gets consumed to kill the weapon it's been feeding."

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment. Then they nodded, slowly, like someone signing away a limb. "Seeds can be regrown," they said. "People can't. Let's go tell your captain."

* * *

Captain Oduya received the news the way she received most things: leaning back in her chair on the Vantablack's little bridge, boots crossed at the ankle, expression arranged into pleasant neutrality.

"A seed vault that's actually a bomb," she said. "A bomb that's actually a hostage. And the ransom note's written in a dead language. You two have been busy."

"You've read the translation," Sefa said. "You know what we're asking. Course to Relay Nine, edge of dead space. We silence the trigger before the truce expires."

"Mm." Oduya uncrossed her boots and sat forward, and Sefa watched something move behind her eyes—not alarm, not resolve. Something cooler. Something with a price tag attached. "Relay Nine. That's Meridian military infrastructure. Pre-Collapse. Do you know what's still intact on pre-Collapse stations?"

"Captain—" Rell began.

"Data cores," Oduya said, ignoring them. "Weapons schematics. The original protocol architecture, sitting there in the dark for two centuries, untouched. You're telling me the single most valuable piece of salvage in human space is sitting at the edge of dead space with a forty-day clock on it." She smiled, warm as a heat lamp. "Of course we'll take you. It's what the Vantablack is *for*."

Something in Sefa's chest went cold. She had spent five years among exiles and smugglers; she knew the difference between greed that jokes and greed that plans. This was the second kind.

"Captain," she said carefully, "the relay has to be destroyed. Or at minimum, its transmitter killed. Whatever's aboard—"

"Whatever's aboard belongs to whoever's brave enough to fetch it," Oduya said pleasantly. "That's just economics, archivist. Nothing personal." She stood, all brisk efficiency now, clapping her hands once. "Rell! Plot us a jump corridor to Relay Nine. Best speed. We burn tonight."

Rell glanced at Sefa—a long, unreadable look—then nodded and turned to the console.

Sefa found Cyrus waiting outside the bridge, arms folded, wearing the particular expression of someone who has spent eleven years learning to read weather in small signs.

"Well?" they asked.

"We have a ride," Sefa said.

"And?"

She hesitated. "And I think our captain just started pricing us."

Cyrus nodded once, unsurprised, unsurprising. "Eleven years alone taught me one thing," they said. "Everything that seems too good—free passage, free help, a friendly face at the airlock—has a bill attached. Question is whether we see it before it comes due."

Behind them, through the bridge door, they heard Oduya humming to herself as she pulled up star charts—old ones, pre-Collapse ones, the kind with Relay Nine marked in faded Meridian script.

The Vantablack shuddered, gathered itself, and jumped.
