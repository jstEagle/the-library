## 1. The Long Route

The morning bell was a lie Tamsin told herself, and she told it faithfully.

She had rigged it from a salvaged chime off Station Kestrel's old mess hall — a brass thing, pitted with two centuries of oxide, tuned to a note that no longer existed in any human scale. At 0600 by the garden clock it rang across the valley she had spent eleven years coaxing out of Meridian Reach's red silence, and she rose, because someone had to answer bells here, even if she rang them herself.

"Good morning," she said to the room. The room did not say it back. She said it anyway. That was the whole discipline of her life, distilled: say it anyway.

The garden came first, as it always did. Beatrix — she had named the project after her grandmother, who had died on the long approach without ever seeing soil that wasn't shipped — spread down the valley floor in terraced arcs, each one a different experiment in patience. Nitrogen-fixing lichen in violet crusts along the northern berm. Rice paddies shallow as spilled milk, their water warmed by geothermal taps she'd rebuilt three times. The windbreak poplars were only four meters tall, which was a failure by every projection and a miracle by every honest measure, and Tamsin loved them with the fierce, embarrassed love one feels for a child who is trying too hard.

She walked the rows with her sleeves rolled, checking moisture wicks, whispering to seedlings the way the old manuals warned against — *they don't need encouragement, they need phosphorus* — but the manuals had never been written for a gardener whose entire audience was asleep.

Because that was the truth of Meridian Reach, the truth she carried like a stone behind her sternum: the colonists this garden was grown for had never set foot on it. They hung in orbit, forty thousand souls divided between two cryo-ships, Halcyon and Vesper, frozen at the edge of dying and waiting for a world that wasn't ready yet. And Tamsin Joubert was the last active terraformer on the entire reach, which meant she was the only living person for whom any of this — the lichen, the rice, the poplars straining toward a sun that barely noticed them — had ever been meant.

She finished the morning rounds by 0900 and fired up the supply skiff by 0930, because the circuit waited for no one, least of all her.

* * *

The long route ran two hundred kilometers east to west, stitching together the derelict terraforming stations like beads on a wire that had mostly rusted through. Kestrel, Meridian Proper, Solace Deep, the Anchorages. Eleven stations built by a crew of sixty, back when there had been sixty; now tended by a crew of one, who slept in whichever bunk was closest to whatever needed fixing.

The stations had personalities now, the way abandoned houses do. Kestrel was the tidy one, its corridors still labeled in crisp stencil, its hydroponics racks empty but swept. Meridian Proper groaned in the wind like an old man complaining about his knees. Solace Deep had flooded its lower levels years ago and gone brackish and strange, bioluminescent algae blooming in the dark water so that walking its catwalks felt like crossing a lake of drowned stars. Tamsin talked to all of them.

"Morning, Kestrel. Your air scrubbers are lying to you again — I'll recalibrate."

"Meridian, you keep that up and I'm replacing your whole bearing assembly, don't test me."

"Solace, you're beautiful and you know it. Stop showing off."

Her voice echoed down corridors built for hundreds. She had learned not to flinch from the echo. The echo was company, of a kind.

At each station she collected what the ships could not make for themselves: mineral stock, catalyst cultures, replacement cells printed from patterns older than her grandmother. She loaded them into the skiff's hold alongside the sealed crates of garden produce she'd begun sending up the mass driver months ago — nutrient paste, protein algae, vitamin greens — stacking them in orbital transfer shells addressed to crews who would not taste them for years, maybe decades, maybe never.

Addressed to people who might already be dead, though no one would tell her if they were.

That was the part she didn't let herself think about during daylight hours. The comms blackout between the ships was official policy — *signal discipline during critical mission phases*, according to the charter, a phrase written by lawyers two centuries dead — and so Halcyon and Vesper spoke to her only through automated status pings, terse and identical, machine courtesies exchanged between strangers. *Systems nominal. Crew status: stable. End transmission.* Forty thousand heartbeats slowed to almost nothing, and the only word from either ship was *stable*, repeated like a prayer neither side believed in anymore.

Tamsin had decided, somewhere in her third year alone, that if the ships would not speak to her, she would speak to them. Loudly. On open channels. Whether or not anyone heard.

* * *

She reached the high relay station at dusk and pointed the dish skyward, where the two ships hung in their slow parking orbits like twin moons that refused to be full. Halcyon in the leading position, Vesper trailing, separated by eleven hundred kilometers of nothing — close enough that on clear nights she could see them both with the naked eye, two faint stars moving against the stars, keeping their distance from each other even in orbit. She had always found that odd. Nobody had ever explained it to her satisfaction.

"Halcyon, this is Meridian Ground, daily call." Her voice sounded very small under the enormous sky. "Garden report. The poplars hit four meters today — yes, I know, I promised you five by now, but the southern slope lost topsoil in the spring storms and I had to rebuild the windbreaks from scratch. The rice is coming in heavy. First real harvest since the blight year. I'm sending up thirty kilos of greens on tomorrow's launch window, so when you wake up, somebody's getting salad whether they want it or not."

Silence, vast and polite.

"I also wanted to tell you," she went on, because the ritual demanded honesty, "that I fixed the pump on terrace nine. Third time. If it breaks again I'm going to have words with whoever designed it, and since that person is probably frozen solid right now, consider this your warning."

She signed off, waited the customary minute, and swung the dish to Vesper.

"Vesper, this is Meridian Ground, daily call." Same words, same warmth, same careful cheer. "Garden report. Poplars at four meters. Rice coming in. Thirty kilos of greens headed your way tomorrow."

A pause. Then, softer, because the ritual had grown a second half over the years, one she'd never quite been able to explain:

"You're doing fine. Both of you. Whatever happened between you — and I know something happened, don't think I haven't noticed the way you keep your distance — whatever it was, it's not my business, and it doesn't change what you are. You're the reason. All of this—" she gestured at the darkening valley below, at the faint green shimmer of Beatrix under the floodlights, "—all of this is for you. Both ships. Equally. That's the job. That's the promise."

She held the channel open a moment longer than protocol required, listening to the carrier wave hum, that thin electronic breath that meant *someone is receiving, even if no one is answering.*

"Goodnight, Vesper. Goodnight, Halcyon. Sleep well."

She broke the connection and stood a while in the cold, watching the two faint stars crawl across the sky, eleven hundred kilometers apart, forever.

* * *

Nights were the hard part.

Back at Kestrel, in the bunk she kept made up with military neatness because disorder invited despair, Tamsin ate her ration of garden greens and read the old logs — the founders' logs, sixty voices reduced to text, arguing about drainage gradients and lichen strains with a passion that made her chest ache. They had expected to finish the terraforming in forty years. It had taken ninety-three, and eaten every one of them alive, and left her.

Sometimes she stood at the viewport and looked at the ships and let herself imagine the waking. The day the garden passed certification, the day the shuttles came down, the day forty thousand people stepped onto soil that smelled like rain and growth instead of sterilized steel. She imagined them asking her name. She imagined telling them about the poplars, the blight year, the pump on terrace nine. She imagined, just for a moment, being answered.

Then she checked the jump-window calculations again — the narrow gravitational corridor that opened once a decade, the only road home to the settled worlds, the reason the colony's timetable mattered at all — and saw that it was closer than anyone had planned for, and put the thought away in the drawer where she kept thoughts too large to hold.

She was drifting toward sleep when the console chimed.

Not the scheduled ping. Not the hourly telemetry handshake. An incoming signal, raw and unscheduled, flagged by the archive AI with a priority tag she had never once seen lit in eleven years of service.

Tamsin sat up in the dark. On the screen, a waveform scrolled, jagged and rhythmic, and beneath it the AI's tentative annotation:

*Signal source: Vesper. Modulation pattern consistent with Old Meridian Standard. Note: Old Meridian Standard has no known living speakers.*

She stared at it for a long time, her heart going strangely loud in the quiet station, and outside the viewport the two ships kept their slow, silent vigil overhead — one of them, it seemed, trying very hard to say something after all.
