## 5. Two Ships, One Window

Ninety days.

Ede Okonkwo stood at the viewport of Vesper's command ring and watched the window's coordinates scroll across the dead navigation board, numbers he had calculated so many times he could recite them the way other men recited prayers. The jump point would open at the edge of the system's heliopause, a throat of folded space that yawned once every eleven years and stayed open for six days. Six days to cross the gulf. Six days, and then the dark would close for another eleven, and the sleepers in his care would be eleven years deeper into decay, their cells fraying like old rope, their odds thinning with every cycle.

He had spent four decades keeping them alive on borrowed power, cannibalizing the ship's own bones — stripping the aft habitat ring for its conductors, gutting the hydroponics bays that had never been planted, pulling the copper from walls like marrow from bone. His hands knew every corridor of this ship the way a father knows the geography of a child's face. And in all that time, the ship had never once spoken back to him. Neither had the garden woman, until the signal. Until she.

He heard her skiff's thrusters before he saw her, a thin stutter of plasma against vacuum, and he watched the little craft crawl across the black toward Vesper's docking collar like a firefly approaching a cathedral.

Tamsin Joubert. The terraformer. The last one. She had answered his signal — his forty-year-old signal, a ghost's plea finally caught by living ears — and now she came to him every few days, her voice bright and strange over the comms, full of words like *germination* and *canopy* and *the children*, by which she meant the sleepers, all of them, both ships, as if they were seeds of one planting.

She was going to be the hardest part.

* * *

The docking collar clanged and cycled, and she came through into the command ring trailing the smell of green — actual green, chlorophyll and wet soil, a smell Ede hadn't breathed in thirty-eight years, since the last of Vesper's own gardens had died to feed its power grid. She was smaller than her voice suggested, wiry, sun-browned at the temples from the grow-lamps, with dirt worked so deep into the creases of her knuckles it looked like tattooing. She carried a satchel of seed packets against regulations and probably against reason, and she set it down on his console as carefully as if it were an infant.

"Ninety days," she said. No greeting. She'd learned that from him; he didn't waste words on weather that didn't exist.

"Ninety days," he agreed. "And only one of us can be ready."

She looked at him. He watched her do the arithmetic — he could see it move behind her eyes, the tonnage, the power draw, the man-hours, the cold equations of a woman who had spent her whole life making one garden out of nothing. "Prepping for jump takes a full crew," she said slowly. "Spin-up on the cores. Seal checks. Navigation calibration. I've read the manuals, Ede. I've read all of them, every night, for years. It's a hundred people's work."

"It's one person's work if that person has forty years of practice and doesn't sleep much." He let that sit. "I can have Vesper ready. I can't have both ready. The window won't hold."

"And Halcyon?"

The name came out of her mouth gently, the way she said everything, and he felt the old heat rise in his chest, the heat he had banked and tended for four decades the way she tended her garden — patiently, faithfully, feeding it on schedule.

"Halcyon made its choice," he said. "Forty years ago, in the dark, they chose themselves. They cut our power while we slept. They left two thousand people to freeze in the dark and took the last of the system's fuel for their own jump prep — and then the window closed on them too, and they've been sitting out there ever since, pretending to be asleep, pretending to be innocent." He turned to the viewport, where Halcyon hung in the distance, a pale sliver against the stars, beautiful and terrible. "They don't get to wake up. Not this time. Not at the cost of mine."

Tamsin said nothing for a long moment. He heard her breathing, the soft huff of a woman in a suit she'd worn too long. When she finally spoke, her voice was careful. "You told me they cut your power. You told me they came for you first."

"They did."

"You're certain."

"I was awake," Ede said, and this was true, and it was the only thing he had left that was true, and he wrapped his hands around it like a man warming himself at the last fire. "I was awake, Tamsin. I saw what they did."

* * *

He had been awake because he was always awake in those days — twenty-three years old, third-shift systems tech, unable to sleep in a ship that smelled of recycled fear. The colony's sun had been dying for a century; everyone knew the numbers; everyone knew there was one jump window left and two ships and only enough fuel infrastructure to prep one. The talks between Halcyon and Vesper had collapsed in a storm of accusations over resource audits. For six weeks the two ships had orbited each other at a cold, silent distance, like animals circling a waterhole in a drought.

On the night it happened, Ede was in the power distribution spine on Deck Nine, rerouting a failing conduit, when the first alarm sang — not the deep alarm of an external strike but the thin, urgent whistle of a power draw from outside the hull.

He remembered crawling up the spine toward the sensor node, his hands shaking, the ship's bones humming around him. He remembered the readout: Halcyon's tugs, clamped to their hull, siphoning from the main bus. He remembered the comm channel open, Halcyon's voice on it — *stand down, Vesper, stand down, this is a reclamation, you've been drawing over your allocation* — and he remembered the weapons board lighting up under his own hands, the defensive grid he was licensed to operate, the targeting reticle blooming green over the tug clamps.

He had fired.

He told himself, then, that it was defense. That they were stealing their survival. That the reticle was green and the decision was simple. The plasma lances took the first tug apart in a bloom of white fire, and the second tug tried to disengage and he took that one too, and then Halcyon answered — their own lances raking across Vesper's power spine, cutting the main bus, plunging the sleep decks into darkness while the cryo-systems screamed and died by decks.

Two thousand people, going dark in their pods, deck by deck.

Ede had spent the next eleven hours in the dark with a hand-crank and a jury-rigged conductor, alone, keeping the lowest cryo deck alive by hand, pumping the crank like a man pumping the heart of the world. By dawn he had one deck stable. One deck out of twelve. The other eleven decks had gone cold, and the cold had done what cold does.

He had told Tamsin about that night. He had told her about the tugs, about the siphoning, about the lances that answered. He had not told her that the first lances were his. He had not told her that the comm log from those first minutes — the log that would have shown Halcyon's voice pleading, explaining, *reclamation, allocation* — had been deleted by his own hand, three days later, in the long silence after, when the grief had hardened into something with edges. He had told himself the log was corrupted. He had told himself a great many things.

*Vesper fired first.* The sentence lived in him like a splinter working toward the heart. He had built his whole cathedral of patience around it, walling it off, and on top of it he had built the true things — the dark decks, the hand-crank, the dead — and the true things held up the cathedral, and the cathedral was named Justice, and he prayed in it every day for forty years.

* * *

"You're quiet," Tamsin said.

They were in Vesper's cryo bays now, Deck One, the deck he had saved. Two hundred pods in ranked rows, their indicator lights burning a soft amber, frost feathering the glass. Tamsin walked the aisle slowly, laying her palm flat against the pods one after another, a benediction, and Ede followed her and hated how much he loved watching her do it.

"They're beautiful," she said. "People forget that. Frozen people are beautiful. All that patience in one room."

"They've been patient long enough." He stepped beside her. "Ninety days, Tamsin. Wake them. Help me wake them. Vesper can be jump-ready — I've kept the spine alive, I've kept the cores warm, I've spent my whole life on it. Halcyon is a tomb with a hull. Their systems are dead, their crews are forty years deader, and even if we could prep two ships, the window won't take two. You know the math. You've always known the math."

"And the garden?"

The question landed like a blade. He watched her hands go still on the pod glass.

"The garden can grow again," he said. "Seeds keep. You told me that. Seeds keep for centuries. But people don't. People are the thing that doesn't keep." He turned her gently, by the shoulder, until she faced him, and he let her see his eyes, forty years deep. "I'm not asking you to hate them. I'm asking you to do the arithmetic that the universe already did. One ship. One window. Mine are the ones who were wronged. Wake mine."

Tamsin held his gaze, and he saw something move across her face — not agreement, not refusal. Doubt, arriving and settling in, taking its coat off, planning to stay.

"Justice," she said quietly. "You keep using that word like it's a place we can go. Like there's a door."

"There's a door," Ede said. "It's called the jump window, and it opens in ninety days, and it closes on whoever isn't standing in it."

* * *

That night — the ship's night, the artificial dimming he maintained by ritual — Ede sat alone in the power spine on Deck Nine, in the exact place where he had fired, and ran his hands along the conduit housing the way a penitent touches a rail.

He thought about the archive AI down on the garden station, the one Tamsin talked to, the old colonial intelligence with its half-corrupted memory of the crisis. He thought about the station logs, the fleet logs, the raw telemetry that Halcyon's tugs had broadcast in their final seconds — telemetry that still hung in the system's data relays like a sound hanging in a canyon after the shout. He had never been able to reach the relays from Vesper's gutted systems. He had never dared ask Tamsin to look. For forty years he had kept the splinter buried, and told himself that the truth and the justice pointed the same direction, and mostly, on most nights, he had believed it.

But she was going to look. He had watched her face. She was a woman who checked everything — he'd seen her recross-check a soil assay three times before planting a single seed. She would go to the logs. She would go to the relays. She would read the telemetry of two tugs dying in the dark, timestamped against the first lance discharge, and she would do the arithmetic that he had refused to do for four decades.

Ede put his forehead against the cold conduit and closed his eyes.

*Ninety days,* he thought. *Ninety days to be ready, and one of them to be honest.*

Down the spine, in the amber dark of Deck One, two hundred sleepers breathed their slow frozen breaths, waiting for a man to give them a future built on a story he was no longer sure he could keep telling.
