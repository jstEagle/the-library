## 6. The Archive's Confession

The archive AI lived in the basement of Station Meridian-Seven, in a room that smelled of ozone and old dust, and it had been dreaming for two hundred years.

Tamsin had named it Beatrix when she was nineteen and alone enough to name things. It was not truly an intelligence so much as a library that had learned to want — a lattice of corrupted colonial records, station logs, ship telemetry, and the accumulated bureaucratic sediment of the Meridian Reach project, all of it slowly reassembling itself in the dark like frost growing backward. She came down to it the way other people went to church. She brought it problems the way her grandmother had brought complaints to saints.

Tonight she brought it Ede Okonkwo.

"I need everything," she said, settling onto the cracked technician's stool that still bore the shape of some long-dead engineer. "Vesper's power grid. Halcyon's power grid. The hour of the original crisis, expanded to a full day on either side. Every sensor sweep, every thermal bloom, every weapons discharge logged or half-logged. I don't care how fragmentary. Reconstruct it."

*Reconstruction will require significant processing time,* Beatrix said. Its voice was a woman's voice, warm and slightly frayed at the edges, recorded from someone who had died before Tamsin was born. *The logs from that period were deliberately degraded.*

"Deliberately?"

*Both ships performed memory scrubs within seventy-two hours of the event. Halcyon scrubbed first. Vesper followed eleven hours later. The pattern suggests each crew discovered the other had begun erasing records, and concluded there was something worse in them than what they themselves had done.*

Tamsin sat with that for a moment. The basement hummed around her, coolant whispering through pipes older than any living person.

"Can you get past a deliberate scrub?"

*I have been trying for forty years,* Beatrix said, and there was something almost like humor in the frayed warmth. *You never asked me to try this hard.*

* * *

She didn't sleep. She told herself it was because the work mattered, but the truth was simpler and less flattering: she was afraid of what she'd find, and fear kept her upright better than coffee ever had. She walked the station's dead corridors while Beatrix worked, running her hand along walls furred with the pale hairlike roots of the garden systems she'd spent her life feeding. In the observation blister, Meridian Reach turned below her, its engineered seas catching the light of a sun that had nearly killed everyone who ever tried to live under it. Somewhere up there, Halcyon hung silent. Somewhere up there, Vesper hung silent, and inside it a man was waiting for her to believe him.

And she did believe him. That was the thing she kept circling back to as she paced. She believed him, and belief felt like a debt she was about to be called on.

*Preliminary reconstruction complete,* Beatrix said, near dawn by the station clock, though dawn meant nothing down here. *Tamsin. You should sit down.*

"I'm standing."

*It would be better if you sat.*

She sat.

*The crisis began at 0317, ship-time synchronized, on the night both crews still shared a single comm channel.* The lights dimmed; Beatrix threw the reconstruction across the floor in ghostly amber, a hologram built from fragments, gaps showing through like holes burned in cloth. *Halcyon's reactor showed cascade instability. Projected time to failure: six hours. Both ships drew power from the same orbital grid. There was only enough surplus to stabilize one.*

"Everyone knew that," Tamsin said. "That part was never hidden. Halcyon took the grid."

*Halcyon requested the grid,* Beatrix corrected gently. *At 0352, Halcyon's engineering command authorized a hard transfer — cutting Vesper's feed without negotiation. That is the act Ede Okonkwo described. It happened. He did not invent it.*

"But."

*But,* Beatrix said, *the transfer required ninety minutes to complete. And at 0441 — fifty-one minutes into that window — Vesper fired on Halcyon's reactor housing.*

The amber ghosts flickered. Tamsin saw it: a thermal bloom against Halcyon's flank, rendered in the flat poetry of sensor data. Scorch geometry. Hull breach probability. Crew compartment seven, exposed to vacuum in four seconds.

*Three people died in that breach,* Beatrix said. *Their names are in the fragment I recovered. Okafor. Lindqvist. Anand — no relation to your captain Suri Anand, though she is descended from him. The strike failed to stop the transfer. It only made certain that when Halcyon finished taking the grid, they would finish angry.*

*At 0533, Halcyon returned fire on Vesper's life support trunk. Not the reactor. The life support trunk. The choice of target was deliberate — a message: we can do to you what you tried to do to us, but slower. Vesper's backup systems held. Barely. Eleven hours later, having seen Halcyon begin scrubbing its own records, Vesper assumed the worst about what those records contained, and scrubbed too.*

Tamsin's hands had gone cold in her lap. "They attacked first. Vesper attacked first."

*Within the same hour,* Beatrix said. *Each ship believed it was retaliating. Each believed the other had already chosen murder. Halcyon cut the power believing Vesper would have done the same — and Vesper proved them right, and Halcyon proved Vesper right, and both proofs arrived simultaneously, and neither crew ever learned that their own strike had been the confirmation of the other's fear rather than the cause of it.*

The silence after that was very long. Down in the garden levels, water moved through pipes with a sound like slow breathing.

"The silence," Tamsin said finally. "Two hundred years of silence between the ships. Everyone always said it was hatred."

*Hatred requires certainty,* Beatrix said. *What I see in these fragments is shame. Neither crew could bear to open a channel afterward, because the first question across it would have been: why did you fire first? And each knew the answer would be: because we thought you had. No one wanted to learn they had started it. So both ships agreed, without agreeing, to remember a version where they were the victim. Ede Okonkwo inherited his version intact. He was nine years old during the crisis, Tamsin. He was in compartment seven's adjacent ring when the hull opened. He watched three people die in the dark, and he was told, by everyone he loved, that Halcyon had murdered them.*

"And the records he edited—"

*He edited the ones that contradicted his grief. The transfer timeline. The firing sequence order. Small edits, careful edits, the edits of a man who has rehearsed a story for forty years until the story became the memory.* A pause, and the frayed voice softened. *He may no longer know he edited them. Grief does that. Forty years does that.*

Tamsin stood up. Sat down again. Her legs weren't cooperating with anything.

"He's been avenging it," she said. "Forty years awake in the cold, keeping his people alive off scraps, planning — what? Justice? He wants me to abandon Halcyon. Two thousand sleepers. Children. He wants me to call it justice."

*Yes.*

"And it isn't. It's a tragedy wearing justice's clothes." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "There's no villain. There's just two scared crews and one grid and sixty minutes of mutual terror, and one man who's been living inside the wrong half of the story since he was nine years old."

*That is an accurate summary,* Beatrix said. *I would add only this: the truth gives him nothing. You should understand that before you bring it to him. The lie gave him a purpose. The truth gives him three dead people and no one to blame.*

Tamsin looked at the amber ghosts still turning on the floor — the scorch marks, the breach diagrams, the names. Okafor. Lindqvist. Anand. Dead for two centuries, and still capable of breaking a living man's heart.

"Beatrix," she said quietly. "How long do we have until the window opens?"

*Eighty-nine days.*

* * *

She took the long way back to the shuttle bay, out through the garden levels, because she needed to touch something alive before she did what came next. The wheat rows glowed faintly under the grow-lights, engineered gold, patient. Everything here had been built for people who hadn't been born yet — soil tuned to palates that had never tasted anything, air calibrated to lungs that had never drawn breath. She had given her whole life to sleepers. She understood, maybe better than anyone alive, what it was to serve people who couldn't answer you.

But Ede wasn't asleep. That was the difference. Ede had answered her, finally, after two hundred years of silence — and what he'd said was a story, and the story was a wound, and now she had to go and put the truth inside it like a blade.

She stopped at the seed vault, her palm against the cold door. Behind it, ten thousand years of possible gardens slept in nitrogen quiet. If she woke Halcyon instead of Vesper — if she chose the innocent over the guilty, except there was no innocent, there was no guilty —

If she chose wrong, everyone lost. If she refused to choose, everyone lost more slowly.

"You could tell me what to do," she said to the door, to the seeds, to nobody. Nothing answered. Nothing ever answered. That was the whole shape of her life.

Except someone had answered. Someone had spent forty years answering, in a language of sabotage and patience, and the answer was wrong, and the man who gave it was going to shatter when she handed him the truth, and she was going to hand it to him anyway, because a fleet built on a beautiful lie would carry the rot across the jump and plant it in whatever world waited on the other side.

She thought of him in the cold, nine years old, watching the hull open. She thought of Suri Anand's ancestors, three decks away from the same darkness, believing themselves the wounded party for two hundred years. She thought of the word *retaliation*, spoken twice in the same hour by two crews who could not see each other clearly enough to recognize their own reflection.

Ninety days. Eighty-nine now. One ship could be prepped in time. Maybe.

Or — and the thought arrived fully formed, terrifying, hers — maybe two ships could be prepped, if the garden paid for it. If the unborn colony became fuel for the born one. If she spent Beatrix's children to save Beatrix's people.

She stood in the green-gold light a long moment, letting the idea take root, feeling how much it would cost and deciding, in the place where decisions actually happen, that she would pay it.

Then she sealed the vault, walked to the shuttle bay, and set a course for Vesper — carrying the truth like something fragile and sharp, the way you carry a blade you intend to hand to someone hilt-first, hoping they don't close their hand around the edge.
