## 10. Beatrix Blooms

The ship had a name now, and the name was spoken in two accents.

Tamsin heard it everywhere she went aboard the Beatrix — in the mess hall where Halcyon's engineers argued torque tolerances with Vesper's, in the corridors where children born of frozen wombs were being carried for the first time in their lives, in the quiet hours when someone would say it like a prayer: *Beatrix. We are aboard the Beatrix.* Suri Anand had chosen it from a list Tamsin never made public — a name from Old Meridian Standard that meant *she who brings happiness*, and both crews had accepted it without knowing that Ede Okonkwo had once whispered it to his garden cameras across forty empty years, talking to the plants because no one else would listen.

She had not told them that. Some truths belonged to her alone, the way certain griefs do.

It was day sixty-one since the jump, and the ship still smelled of newness — solder and sealant and the green bite of nutrient solution cycling through pipes that had been garden veins only two months ago. The conversion was not finished. It might never be finished. But the Beatrix moved under her own power through the long dark between stars, and every bunk held a breathing person, and that was more than Tamsin had dared to believe possible on any of the mornings she had spent alone on Meridian Reach.

* * *

She transmitted the history on the seventh day, when the last of the sleepers had been woken and the dead had been counted: one hundred and nine souls lost to the cold across two centuries of silence, plus one man who had given his life holding a reactor core together with his bare hands and his stubborn, furious heart.

She did not editorialize. She read the logs as the archive AI had reconstructed them — the hour of mutual betrayal, Halcyon cutting Vesper's power believing they acted first in self-defense, Vesper firing back within the same sixty minutes believing the same. She read the forty years of Ede's transmissions, unanswered because no one was left awake to answer. She read the sabotage reports, the edited records, the scorch marks on hull plating that both ships carried like scars from the same wound.

Then she let it stand in the shared memory of the ship, available to every bunk, every cabin, every generation yet to be born in the dark between stars.

There were riots, at first. There were accusations, tears, long silences in mess halls where people refused to sit across from each other. There was an engineer named Vashti who spat at Tamsin's feet and called her a liar for three days before breaking down in the hydroponics bay and asking, voice raw, *why would anyone keep a lie alive for forty years?*

Tamsin had answered honestly: *Because the truth gave him nothing to live for, and he needed something.*

Vashti had not forgiven Ede. But she had stopped spitting.

* * *

On day thirty, they had buried him.

Not in soil — there was no soil yet, not truly, though the converted garden bays were beginning to produce something dark and crumbly at their roots that Suri insisted on calling earth. They buried him in vacuum instead, wrapped in the last of the Halcyon burial shrouds that had been intended for a crew that was never supposed to die, and both captains spoke over the open channel while the whole ship listened.

Suri Anand said: *He avenged a crime that never happened, and saved the people he meant to abandon. I do not know what to do with that. I only know we would not be alive without him.*

The Vesper first officer, a woman named Kess Oduya whose grandmother had been frozen beside Ede's own sister, said: *He kept us alive alone for forty years. Whatever else he was, he was faithful.*

Tamsin said nothing at all. She had already said everything, in the transmission, in the logs, in the truth she carried like a stone in her chest. What she felt for Ede Okonkwo was too large and too tangled for ceremony — gratitude and grief and fury and love all braided together into something that had no name in either of the ships' languages.

But she pressed her palm flat against the viewport as his shroud drifted away, and she thought: *You wanted someone to answer you. Look what your silence built.*

* * *

The hold was the warmest place on the ship, which everyone found strange until they understood why.

It was where Tamsin had stowed the salvaged seed vault fragments — the charred remains of her life's work, jettisoned into the black to buy Ede ninety seconds of reactor stability. Most of it was ash. Most of it would always be ash. But she had spent sixty days going through the wreckage pod by pod, cataloguing losses, mourning varieties that would never grow again: the silver-leafed cedars of Station Nine, the blue-flowering vetch she had talked to every morning for eleven years, the entire lineage of Meridian wheat bred for soils that would now never exist.

And then, on day fifty-eight, she had found it.

A single pod, heat-scarred but intact, its genetic core still reading viable when she ran the assay three times because she could not believe it the first two. A descendant of the original Beatrix strain — the terraforming rose that had given the project its name, the flower engineered to bloom in poisoned ground, to make oxygen where there was none, to be the first living thing in a dead world.

Ede had named the merged ship after it. Neither of them had known, until the end, that one had survived.

* * *

Now she knelt in the hold beside the germination tray, and the thing that should have been impossible was happening anyway.

A single green shoot, pale as candlelight, pushing up through sterile medium with the blind courage of all growing things. It had been doing this for six hours. Tamsin had not left it once. She had adjusted the light spectrum twice, recalibrated humidity, whispered to it in Old Meridian Standard because that was the language it had been bred to hear — nonsense, probably, but ritual mattered, and ritual was all she had left of the world she had given up.

"You're early," she told it softly. "I didn't expect you for another week."

The shoot did not answer, of course. But somewhere behind her, a voice did.

"It's real, then."

She turned. Suri Anand stood in the hold doorway, still wearing the captain's grey that both crews had adopted as neutral territory, her hands clasped behind her back in a gesture Tamsin had learned meant *I am trying to be humble and failing.*

"It's real," Tamsin said.

"Kess wants to plant it in the forward bay. Vashti says the light there is wrong. Half my crew thinks we should build it a shrine." Suri crossed the deck and knelt beside the tray, studying the shoot with the expression of a woman looking at a miracle she did not trust herself to touch. "Half of Vesper's thinks the same. Did you know that? First thing they've agreed on in two hundred years."

Tamsin almost laughed. "A shrine."

"A flower that survived the fire that nearly killed us all." Suri shrugged. "People need something to put their hands on. Something small enough to carry."

They sat in silence for a moment, two women watching a leaf the size of a fingernail tremble in recycled air.

"I read your transmission again last night," Suri said quietly. "All of it. The part where you talk about the garden — about what it was *for*. You wrote that you used to think it was for the colonists. For us." She looked up. "Was it?"

Tamsin considered the question honestly, the way she considered everything now, having learned at terrible cost what half-truths cost the people who carried them.

"At first," she said. "For years. And then it became for me, because you were all asleep and I needed a reason to keep going. And then—" she touched the edge of the germination tray, feeling the faint warmth of new life, "—and then I jettisoned it to save a man I'd known for ninety days, and I understood that it had never been mine at all. It was just passing through me. The way this one is passing through me now, into whatever comes next."

"Which is?"

"I don't know." She smiled, and it was the first smile in months that did not feel like a performance. "That's rather the point, isn't it? A garden isn't a destination. It's a promise you keep in the dark."

Suri was quiet for a long time. Then she reached out — slowly, asking permission with the gesture — and touched the tip of one finger to the rim of the tray, not quite touching the shoot itself.

"When it blooms," she said, "I want the whole ship to see it. Both crews. Together."

"They'll come," Tamsin said. "They've been coming all week. I pretend not to notice."

"And do you speak to it? When they're not here?"

"Every morning."

"What do you say?"

Tamsin looked at the small green thing that had cost her a planet and given her a people, and she told the truth:

"I say good morning. I tell it what happened yesterday. I tell it who died and who was born and who is angry and who has started to forgive." Her voice caught, just slightly. "I tell it things I wish someone had said to me, in all those years alone. And then—"

She stopped.

Behind her, from the corridor outside the hold, came footsteps. Not one set — many. Dozens. The soft shuffle of people who had learned, in sixty-one days, that the warmest place on the ship was also the place where a woman spoke to a seedling, and that if you arrived quietly enough you could hear her telling the story of humanity to a flower, and that hearing it was somehow better than sleeping.

Suri heard them too. She stood, offered Tamsin her hand, and pulled her gently upright.

"They're waiting," the captain said.

"For what?"

"For you to start." Suri smiled — a real smile, tired and hopeful and slightly embarrassed. "We've been listening for weeks, Tamsin. Through the door. All of us. Halcyon and Vesper both. You're the only one who talks to the future like it might answer back, and we—"

She paused, choosing her words with the care of a woman who had spent two centuries of inherited silence learning how to break it.

"We decided today that we'd like to answer."

Tamsin stood very still, her hand resting on the warm rim of the tray, and listened to the sound of dozens of people breathing just beyond the doorway — waiting, patient, present, *there* — and for the first time in eleven years, she was not speaking into the dark.

"All right," she said softly, to the captain, to the crowd, to the small green shoot unfurling its first true leaf in the light. "Good morning, everyone."

And the Beatrix — the ship, the flower, the name, the promise — answered.
