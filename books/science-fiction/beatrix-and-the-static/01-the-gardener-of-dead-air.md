## 1. The Gardener of Dead Air

Day 4,112 of the Meridian Caretaker Log. Or thereabouts. I stopped trusting the counter after it rolled over a leap cycle nobody had bothered to program, and honestly, after four thousand days alone, precision starts to feel like vanity.

This is Petra Oyelaran, sole registered resident of Meridian Colony, population: me, plus whatever's growing in Greenhouse Three, plus the mice I pretend not to have because admitting to vermin feels like failure and I have failed at quite enough this year.

If you're hearing this — and someone, someday, will hear this, because I refuse to believe otherwise — welcome. The tomatoes are doing well. That's the headline. Four thousand days of civilization's slow exhale, and the headline is tomatoes.

I talk to the plants too. I want that on the record, in case anyone ever audits my sanity. I talk to them the way you'd talk to a roommate who can't pay rent but does keep the air breathable, which, on Meridian, is a fair trade. The colony's atmosphere processors died eleven years ago, one by one, like old men in winter, and now the only oxygen worth having comes out of Greenhouse Three. So when I tell the basil it's doing important work, I am not being whimsical. I am being accurate.

* * *

Mornings start with the walk. You have to understand the geography of my life: Hab Block A, where I sleep, sits at the bottom of a shallow crater the founders called the Bowl, for reasons that were probably poetic in the planning documents. From my bunk to the greenhouse is six hundred meters of covered walkway, most of its glazing cracked, some of it missing entirely, so that on bad days the walk is less a corridor and more an obstacle course between myself and the thin, wrong, half-terraformed air outside.

The terraforming never finished. That's the short version of everything. The long version involves budgets, elections on a planet I've never seen, and a scandal I only know the shape of, the way you know the shape of a thing by the hole it leaves behind. Earth declared Meridian non-viable in year nine of the project. Declared us legally dead, which is a phrase I've turned over so many times it's worn smooth. Legally dead. As if the law had checked our pulses. As if the forty thousand people who were supposed to come had been weighed and found wanting before they ever boarded a ship.

They never came, of course. The first wave — the founders, the engineers, the botanists — they came, built the bones of the place, and then the money stopped and the ships stopped and eventually the people stopped too. Died out, shipped out, or simply lay down in their bunks one night and didn't get up with any particular drama about it. My grandmother was one of the last. She used to say the colony didn't fail, it was *failed*, active verb, and she'd tap the table twice when she said it, like punctuation, like testimony.

She also taught me the greenhouse. Everything I know about keeping things alive in impossible conditions, I learned from a woman who kept herself alive in impossible conditions right up until she couldn't, and then handed me the watering schedule like a relay baton.

So: mornings. Six hundred meters. I count the cracks in the glazing as I go — forty-one new ones since last season, which means either the structure is failing or the planet is settling, and I've decided not to investigate which, because both answers are depressing. At crack thirty-three there's a gap where a whole panel blew out years ago, and through it you can see the plain: gray-red regolith stretching to the horizon under a sky the color of a bruise healing badly. The terraformers got the sky partway done. It holds enough scatter to be blue-ish at noon, if you squint, if you're generous. My grandmother called it "the almost." *Go look at the almost,* she'd say. It's the most honest sky I know.

* * *

Greenhouse Three is the only one still pressurized, and keeping it that way is my full-time job, my hobby, my religion, and my prison, depending on the hour. It's a cathedral of old glass and older ambitions — three hundred meters long, arched like a whale's ribcage, filled with the green stubbornness of things that never got the memo about the colony being dead.

I do my rounds in order, always the same order, because ritual is what you build when the future stops showing up. Tomatoes first, because they're the divas. Then the leafy greens, then the root crops down in the trough beds, then the seed vault, which is the actual reason any of this matters, though I'll get to that.

The tomato row runs the length of the east wall, and this morning one of them has produced a fruit with a split in its skin, and I stand there holding it like a doctor delivering bad news. "You had *one job*," I tell it. It doesn't apologize. Tomatoes never do.

But here's the thing about the split fruit: it split because we got rain yesterday. Actual rain, condensation cycling off the patched cooling coils, dripping along the arch ribs exactly the way I engineered it to two seasons back, and the plants drank deep and swelled faster than their skins could handle. Which is to say: the damage is evidence of success. I've been thinking about that all morning. I'm not sure what to do with a lesson like that. *Growth hurts* seems too pat. *Everything alive will crack you open* seems too grim. Maybe just: keep watering.

The greens are fine. The roots are fine. The potatoes, as ever, are doing their work in the dark without complaint, and I respect them enormously for it. If I ever write a book about this place — and I have started writing a book about this place, seventeen times, all abandoned by page three — the potatoes are getting a chapter.

Then the seed vault, which is a room within the room, climate-sealed, backup-powered, and stocked with eleven thousand varieties of seed from Earth and from the first-generation hybrid programs. Eleven thousand futures in foil packets, catalogued by my grandmother's generation, waiting for settlers who were declared dead before they could be born.

That's the arithmetic I live inside. Every packet is addressed to someone who doesn't exist yet. And every day I keep them viable anyway. Not because I'm optimistic — optimism is a luxury crop and this soil won't take it — but because throwing them away would require more energy than keeping them, and spite, I've found, is a renewable resource.

* * *

Afternoons are maintenance, which is a polite word for losing ground slowly. Today's project is pump seven on the irrigation loop, which has developed a rhythm I can only describe as asthmatic. I spend two hours elbow-deep in its guts, replacing seals I fabricated myself on the workshop printer from a design I reverse-engineered from a part that went out of manufacture before I was born.

There's a whole civilization in that sentence, if you listen for it. A woman alone on a dead colony, printing spare parts for machines whose makers are dust, using designs she taught herself from manuals written for people who assumed there would always be someone else to ask. I am the entire supply chain. I am research and development, manufacturing, distribution, and customer complaints. Sometimes I file complaints against myself. They go unanswered.

While I work, the radio plays. It always plays. I keep it tuned across the emergency bands, the civilian bands, the dead frequencies, scanning on a loop, because that's another ritual: listening for a voice. In eleven years the scan has returned static, solar hiss, the ghost-carrier of a beacon on a relay station three hundred kilometers east that still transmits its automated ID into nothing, and once — memorably — what I swear was music, though it turned out to be a failing capacitor singing in the hab's own wiring, which was somehow worse than silence. Silence doesn't get your hopes up. Hope is the expensive emotion out here. I budget for it carefully.

My grandmother listened too, near the end. She'd sit by the radio for hours with her hands folded, and when I asked her what she was waiting for she said, "Company," and then, after a while, "Or proof. Either one." I didn't understand her then. I thought she meant proof of survivors elsewhere, other pockets of the scattered diaspora holding on. Now I think she might have meant something else. She said strange things near the end. She looked at the sealed archive building — the founder vault down in the old administrative quarter, the one structure on this world that never lost power, never lost pressure, never needed me — and she said, "That place is a letter, Petra. Somebody wrote it. Somebody's waiting for it to be read."

I told her the archive was just records. Deeds and soil assays and the boring paperwork of a project that died mid-sentence. She tapped the table twice and changed the subject, which is what she did when she knew something she'd decided I wasn't ready for.

I think about that a lot lately. I don't know why *lately*. Four thousand days of the same routine, and something in the last few weeks has made the archive feel less like furniture and more like a held breath.

* * *

Evening. The log again, because the log is the spine of the day, the ritual that makes all the other rituals cohere. I set the recorder on the potting bench, among the cuttings, and I give the day its accounting.

"Day 4,112. All systems holding. Pump seven repaired, efficiency maybe eighty percent, which in Meridian terms is a miracle and I'll take it. Harvested: one kilo two hundred of mixed greens, four hundred grams of tomatoes, one eggplant that I'm going to eat tonight with the good salt and consider it a holiday. Seed vault humidity nominal. Atmosphere nominal. Morale—" and here I always pause, because morale is the one reading I can't take with instruments. "Morale adequate."

Adequate. Four thousand days of adequate. There are worse words. I've lived through some of them.

"Message for the settlers," I say, because I always say it, because the logs were originally designed as orientation broadcasts for arriving colonists, and I never updated the format, because updating it would mean admitting no one is coming, and I have decided — as a matter of policy — not to decide that. "Welcome to Meridian. Your quarters are ready. Well. Ready-ish. The water reclamation unit in Block C is temperamental; kick it on the left side, not the right, it takes offense. The greenhouse is thriving. I've kept your seeds alive. I've kept your air alive. I know that sounds like a strange thing to promise strangers, but you're not strangers, really. You're just late. Eleven thousand packets' worth of late. When you arrive, the tomatoes will be overripe and I will complain about it, and it will be wonderful."

I stop the recording. The greenhouse hums around me, pumps and fans and the drip-feed tick of the irrigation loop, the sound of a small world insisting on itself.

And then, because the light is going and I always check it at dusk, I look out through the east glass at the administrative quarter below, at the squat dark shape of the founder archive, unlit, unchanging, patient as a stone.

Somebody wrote it, my grandmother said. Somebody's waiting for it to be read.

"Nobody's waiting," I tell her, out loud, the way I tell her things now, since telling her things is all I have left of her. "It's paperwork. It's a filing cabinet with delusions."

The archive says nothing, the way archives do.

I turn off the grow lights section by section, east to west, the way I have for four thousand days, and the dark comes down over the rows like a blanket tucked in by hand. Tomorrow: pump eight, glazing patch at crack forty-two, the potato chapter if I'm feeling literary.

Tonight, walking back to the hab under the almost-sky, I hear it.

Not music. Not a capacitor. Underneath the wind hiss on the open band, underneath the static that has been my only company for eleven years, there is a pattern. Rhythmic. Structured. Repeating.

I stop in the middle of the walkway, cold seeping through my boots, and I listen.

Static, my grandmother used to say, is never empty. Static is everything anyone ever said, still traveling, still looking for someone to land in.

The signal repeats again. And this time, I'm almost sure — almost impossibly sure — I catch a word in it.

A word in a language I have only ever heard in recordings. Old Terran Standard. Dead two centuries.

Someone is speaking.
