## 3. What the Founders Sealed

The archive door had a lock on it, which struck me as the funniest thing on the whole planet.

Two hundred years of silence. A colony declared legally dead by a government that couldn't be bothered to send a doctor, let alone a lawyer. And somebody had still gone to the trouble of locking the door — as if the dead might wander in and file a complaint. As if the only crime worth preventing on Meridian was trespass.

I stood in the maintenance shaft beneath the admin block with a cutting torch, a thermos of reconstituted coffee, and the signal still whispering in my earpiece like a hymn sung through a wall. Old Terran Standard. A language that had been dead longer than I'd been alive, broadcasting in clean, deliberate pulses from a vault nobody had opened since the Founding Generation walked out of it and, presumably, into their graves.

Somebody had wanted it buried here. Buried *here*, of all places — under the colony they were abandoning, on the world they were writing off, where no one would ever think to look because no one was ever coming back.

Except me. I was coming back. I came back every day. That was the whole problem with being the last caretaker: you become the only witness, and witnesses are inconvenient.

The torch bit through the last of the hinge bolts. The door swung inward on hydraulics that should have been dead and weren't, and cold air rolled out — cold, and *dry*, and faintly sweet, like the inside of a seed bank. Which, I supposed, is exactly what it was.

I went in.

* * *

The vault was smaller than I'd imagined. Vaults usually are. You spend two days tracing a mystery signal through bedrock and conduit, and you picture something vast and cathedral-dark, and instead you get a room the size of my greenhouse's potting shed, lined with server racks and a single terminal under a film of two centuries of dust.

The dust was the detail that got me, later. Not the servers, not the signal, not even the recording. The dust. Because dust means air, and air means somebody had kept the environmental seals maintained long after the last official breath was drawn on this world. Somebody had dusted in here. Somebody had *cared* in here, quietly, without putting it in any log I'd ever found.

I wiped the terminal clean with my sleeve and woke it up. It came on like it had been waiting, which machines always do, and which I find unforgivable.

One file. Not an archive — an archive implies plurality, implies choice. One file, flagged for playback on unauthorized access, and beneath it a line of metadata that made my hands go still on the console:

**RECORDED: MERIDIAN COLONY, YEAR 11. SPEAKER: DIRECTOR-FOUNDER AMARA OKONKWO-VELL. DISTRIBUTION: NONE.**

Year eleven. The colony had been funded for thirty. This was the year the money stopped.

I pressed play because there was no version of the story where I didn't.

* * *

The voice that came out of the terminal was warm and tired and precise, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this and then decided rehearsal was cowardice and started over from the heart.

*"My name is Amara Okonkwo-Vell. I was the founding director of the Meridian terraforming project. If you are hearing this, then either the colony survived, in which case I am dead and proud of you — or the colony did not survive, in which case I am dead and furious, and you should be too. Listen."*

I listened. I stood in that cold little room with my coffee going cold in my hand and I listened the way you listen at a funeral: completely, helplessly, unable to look away.

*"In year nine, our atmospheric processors achieved the viability threshold. Full nitrogen-oxygen conversion across the southern basin. Sustainable. Self-correcting. Meridian was going to live. I filed the certification myself, with my own hands, because I wanted the record to show that a human being verified it and not just a machine. Earth received that certification on the fourteenth of March, year nine."*

A pause in the recording. When she spoke again her voice had changed — flattened, the way voices flatten when someone stops trying to persuade and starts trying only to be accurate.

*"Eleven days later, Earth's Terraforming Authority filed a counter-assessment declaring the southern basin readings anomalous and non-replicable. They ordered the processor array shut down for 'recalibration.' We complied. We were colonists; compliance was our native language. The array was down for six months. When it came back up, the readings were worse — because they had sabotaged it. I have the maintenance records. I have the serial numbers of the components they swapped in, components manufactured to fail, shipped to us in crates stamped with the Authority's own seal. They broke our sky on purpose and then pointed at the broken sky and said: see, it was never going to work."*

My coffee was on the floor. I don't remember dropping it. I remember the sound it made — small, absurd, a tin little heartbeat in that silent room — and I remember thinking that I would hear that sound for the rest of my life.

*"Why?"* Amara's voice asked, and answered itself. *"Because Meridian was the proof case. The Authority had sold Earth's parliament on a generation of terraforming contracts — a trillion credits, four hundred worlds, all of it predicated on the Meridian model. If Meridian succeeded on schedule and under budget, then the next phase of contracts would have to be awarded by open bid. Open competition. The Authority's directors would have lost their monopoly, and with it the private licensing fees that made them rich. So Meridian could not succeed. Not on time. Not ever. We were not a colony to them. We were a scheduled failure, and the schedule mattered more than we did."*

*"They cut the funding in year eleven. The evacuation offer came with it — one transport, one window, take it or stay. Four hundred and six of us stayed. Not because we were brave. Because we had done the math ourselves, with our own instruments, and we knew what they knew: the world was viable. We stayed to finish the work they'd tried to abort, and we stayed because leaving would have made their lie true, and we could not bear to be the evidence for our own murder."*

*"We kept the processors running. We kept the gardens alive. We certified viability a second time, and a third, and sent the data on every channel Earth monitored. The data was received. I know it was received, because the Authority acknowledged receipt — and then classified it. You cannot say you were not told. You were told. Everyone was told. The lie was never ignorance. The lie was policy."*

I sat down on the floor. There didn't seem to be a reason to be standing. Standing implied you were going somewhere.

*"I am recording this in the last month of my life, and I am not being dramatic — the med systems say what they say, and I have made my peace with it. I am sealing this testimony in the founder archive, in Old Terran Standard, because by the time anyone might come looking, Standard will be a dead language, and dead languages are the only truly secure ones. The Authority's decryption mandates cover every living tongue. They will never spend the money to excavate a language no one speaks."*

*"If you are hearing this, someone learned a dead language to find the truth. That is a kind of devotion I don't have a word for. So I will simply say what I came here to say, and then I will stop, and the rest will be up to you."*

*"Meridian was murdered. Not failed. Not abandoned. Murdered — slowly, bureaucratically, with paperwork and sabotaged parts, by people who never once had to look at our faces. Four hundred and six of us stayed to tend a world that was already alive, because its being alive was the one thing we could still give it. Tend it. If you are hearing this — tend it. And tell them. Not for revenge. Revenge is a currency I have no use for. Tell them because a lie this large does not stay buried on its own. It has to be kept buried. Someone has to keep dusting the room. And someday, someone stops."*

*"I have stopped. Your turn."*

The recording ended. The terminal's standby light pulsed, patient as a held breath.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat on that floor. Long enough that the coffee stain dried. Long enough that my knees had opinions about the floor's hospitality.

Here is what I knew, and what I had always known, in the way you know a thing without letting yourself look at it directly, the way you don't look at the sun: the colony logs ended too neatly. The evacuation records had gaps you could drive a hauler through. The "atmospheric cascade failure" that supposedly killed the holdouts had never quite matched the weather data, and I had spent eleven years telling myself that was just bad record-keeping, because the alternative was unbearable.

The alternative was this. The alternative was Amara Okonkwo-Vell's tired, precise voice saying *murdered* in a dead language, in a locked room, under a world Earth had signed a death certificate for and never once checked the body.

I thought about my greenhouse. Every morning for eleven years I had recorded a log for settlers who were never coming, describing germination rates to an audience of zero, because that was the ritual, that was the devotion, that was how you loved people you'd never meet. I had thought I was tending a grave.

I hadn't been tending a grave. I'd been tending a *cover-up* — the living, breathing, photosynthesizing proof of a crime, kept alive by accident, by one stubborn woman with a watering can, while the people who committed it got statues.

I laughed. It came out wrong, more like a hiccup with teeth in it, but I laughed, there on the floor of the sealed vault of the murdered, because the absurdity was the only thing keeping the grief from crushing me flat. Eleven years. Eleven years of daily logs. Somewhere in that terminal's storage there were probably four hundred and six sets of daily logs just like mine, kept by people doing exactly what I was doing, for exactly the same reason, and Earth had looked at all of it and stamped it *anomalous, non-replicable*.

Non-replicable. I was replicating it right now. I was the replication.

* * *

I copied the file three times. Once to my wrist unit, once to a data core I'd carried for years without knowing why — caretakers are hoarders, we keep spare everything, it's a professional deformity — and once, after a moment's thought, to the oldest, dumbest, most obsolete storage format I could find in the racks, because Amara had taught me something in that recording: the secret of a dead language is that nobody thinks to kill it. Obsolescence is armor. I wrote the testimony to a crystal lattice drive, the kind they stopped making a century ago, and I put it in my breast pocket, over the heart, because I am sentimental and I have decided to stop apologizing for it.

Then I sat with the terminal a while longer and did the thing I do. I pulled up the colony's public log channel — the one I'd been broadcasting into for eleven years, the one I'd assumed was a void — and I checked its routing.

It had been received. Every entry. Eleven years of daily logs, acknowledged and classified, by the same authority, in the same office, by the same *desk*, if the metadata was honest. Somebody had been reading my garden reports. Somebody had been reading my germination rates and my soil pH numbers and the entry where I'd cried about the frost taking the citrus, and they had filed it all under a classification code, and the code, when I decoded it, translated to a phrase I will be repeating on my deathbed:

**CONTINGENCY: ASSET RECOVERY PENDING.**

Asset recovery. Not a person. Not a rescue. An *asset*. They had a file open on Meridian the whole time, and the file was waiting for something, and I did not need to be a genius to guess what the something was. Amara's testimony had a distribution flag of NONE — but the archive access log, oh, the access log had a different story to tell. The vault had been pinged remotely eleven times in the past two years. Monitoring. Someone at the far end of a very long pipe had been watching this room the way you watch a kettle.

And a kettle, eventually, whistles.

I got up. My knees filed a formal complaint. I took the crystal drive, and the data core, and one more thing — Amara's original maintenance records, the ones with the sabotaged serial numbers, because a testimony without evidence is just a story and I intended for this to be more than a story — and I sealed the vault behind me as best I could, which is to say badly, because I am a gardener and not a locksmith, and the torch had made sure of that.

Walking back through the maintenance shafts toward the greenhouse, I did the math on the signal. The one that had started all this. Old Terran Standard, broadcasting from beneath the archive, structured, deliberate — not a beacon, not an accident. A lure. Amara had said the testimony would play on unauthorized access, but the signal had come *first*. Something in that vault had been reaching out, patiently, for years, singing in a dead language to whoever might still be listening.

She'd built the whistle into the kettle. She'd trusted that someday someone would stop dusting the room.

I stepped out of the admin block into the gray afternoon light, and that was when my wrist unit buzzed with a notification I had never once received in eleven years of caretaking:

**INBOUND VESSEL. REGISTRY: EARTH TERRAFORMING AUTHORITY, RECLAMATIONS DIVISION. ETA: 40 HOURS. DESIGNATION: ASSET AUDIT.**

Forty hours. Eleven years of nothing, and now a ship, with a division name that sounded like a funeral home run by accountants.

I put my hand in my pocket, closed it around the crystal drive, and started walking back to my greenhouse — to the garden they were about to come and audit — moving as fast as my knees allowed, and faster than that.
