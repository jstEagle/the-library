## 5. Ash on the Glass

He gave me an hour to decide. That was the first thing I learned about Davor Ilic: he was generous with time he had no intention of honoring.

I spent forty minutes of that hour doing something that would have looked, from the outside, like nothing at all. I sat on my stool by the propagation bench with a cup of recaf going cold in my hands and watched the tomatoes. Not metaphorically. Actually watched them — the way the grow-lamps made their leaves translucent at the edges, green shot through with light like stained glass. Somewhere under the glass roof the wind was doing its usual work on Meridian's atmosphere, scouring the domes with dust fine as flour.

"You're not deciding," Ilic said from the doorway. He'd helped himself to my chair earlier and then abandoned it, which told me he was the kind of man who needed to move while he thought. "You're stalling."

"I'm grieving preemptively," I said. "It saves time later."

"Over plants."

"Over eleven years of work. Over a seed bank that took three generations of dead people to assemble. Over the only living thing on this continent that isn't trying to kill you." I turned the cup in my hands. "But sure. Plants."

He didn't smile. I'd been hoping he would, because a man who smiles is a man who wants to be liked, and a man who wants to be liked can be led around by his vanity like a goat on a rope. Ilic just watched me with those auditor's eyes, cataloguing. I got the distinct impression I was being written up for depreciation.

"The core," he said. "The archive message. Give it to me, and you keep your greenhouse. Reclamation has no interest in botanical assets. Frankly, we have no interest in this colony at all beyond what's on that drive."

"And if I don't?"

"Then asset recovery becomes asset disposal." He said it the way you'd read a shipping manifest. "Ms. Oyelaran. Petra. I've read your logs. All of them. Two thousand, four hundred and six daily entries addressed to settlers who are never coming." He paused, and for one unguarded half-second something moved behind his face — not pity, exactly. Recognition, maybe. "You address them like they're late. Like the ship is delayed, not canceled. Do you know how that reads to someone outside?"

"How does it read?"

"Like devotion," he said. "The expensive kind."

* * *

Here is what I did not tell him: the core wasn't in the greenhouse. It hadn't been since the night I first heard Old Terran Standard crackling out of the ground beneath my own feet like a voice from a grave that turned out to be occupied. I'd slept badly after that, and badly-slept people do sensible things. I'd moved the message into the last place anyone from Earth would ever look, because it required caring about something other than itself.

Seed canisters. The long-term vault stock — the ones sealed against two centuries of waiting, nitrogen-flushed, labeled in founder script. Canister 114 through 118, the heritage grains. Nobody audits seeds. Seeds are hope, and hope doesn't appear on balance sheets.

So when Ilic asked where the core was, I could tell him the truth with a straight face: *it's somewhere you'll never think to search.* He assumed I meant a hiding place. He did not understand that I meant the future.

What I told him instead was a lie with good bones. "It's in the habitat module. Under the floor plating in the comms room."

He considered that. I watched him consider it, and I watched him decide not to believe me, and I understood — with the particular clarity that comes when you realize you're already dead in someone's ledger — that the greenhouse had been condemned the moment he landed. The question was never whether. Only when, and whether I'd be standing inside it.

"An hour," he said again, and left.

* * *

I want to record what happened next accurately, because accuracy is all I have left of it now.

I did not run to the habitat module. Running would have confirmed everything. Instead I walked — slow, deliberate, a woman with nothing to hide — across the connecting corridor to the hab ring, down to the comms room, and made a great show of prying up floor plating that contained, beneath it, absolutely nothing but cable runs and forty years of dust. Let him watch. Let him conclude I was desperate and transparent and lying badly.

Then I went back for the garden.

Not the core. The garden. There were things in there that couldn't be replaced by any vault: the peach tree grown from a cutting of a cutting of a cutting, fourth-generation Meridian stock, its roots wrapped around a ceramic tag stamped with a name I'd never been able to read. The bees. Three hives of engineered pollinators, descendants of founders' bees, humming their low brown note in the corner bay. The wall of starts I'd been hardening off for a spring that kept not arriving outside.

If I'm honest — and the whole point of these logs is honesty, even the ugly kind — I'll admit I went back partly because I couldn't bear the thought of the bees dying confused. They'd never known anything but the greenhouse. Their entire universe was glass and lamplight and the smell of tomato flowers. You keep something alive for eleven years, you owe it a better death than smoke.

I was carrying the second hive toward the airlock when the first sprinkler head burst.

Not water. Foam suppressant, then nothing — the fire system had been dead for years, another line item Earth had declined to fund, and Ilic must have known that too, because the accelerant came through the ventilation intake the way a confession comes through a lawyer: technically legal, entirely intended.

The smell hit first. Solvent-sharp, chemical, wrong in a space that had only ever smelled of soil and green things. Then the sound — a soft whump along the north beds, and the dry mulch I'd laid down in autumn taking the flame like it had been waiting for permission.

I dropped the hive. I'm not proud of that. The case hit the deck plates and the bees came out angry and blinded, and I ran, because I am a coward about fire the way most living things are, and because some animal part of me understood before the rest of me did that Ilic hadn't come to negotiate anymore. The hour had been theater. The burn had started the moment my boots crossed back onto greenhouse soil.

Heat at my back like a hand shoving. Glass overhead beginning to sing — that high thin note tempered panes make before they let go, a sound I'd heard once in a storm and never forgotten. I got through the airlock and spun the manual seal and stood in the corridor with soot on my hands and watched, through the little reinforced port, my life catch.

* * *

People say watching something burn is fast. It isn't. Fire is patient. It walked through my greenhouse bed by bed, row by row, the way I used to walk it on inspection mornings, touching everything. It found the peach tree and climbed it. It found the drying racks and the paper envelopes of seed I'd labeled by hand, thousands of them, my handwriting going up in orange curls. It found the propagation benches and the misters and the little stool where I drank my cold recaf every morning and talked to plants because there was no one else to talk to.

The glass held for nine minutes. I counted. At minute nine the south dome let go all at once — not a shatter but a sigh, a whole curved sky of it folding inward and outward at once, and the wind came through and the fire stood up straight and roared like something finally allowed to speak.

And here is the thing I will carry until I die, the thing I want written down plainly: I felt rage, yes. Rage like a live wire, rage with teeth. But underneath the rage, worse than the rage, was a horrible flicker of *relief*. Eleven years I'd kept that garden alive for people who were never coming. Eleven years of logging moisture levels for ghosts. And some small, shameful part of me looked at the flames and thought: *now I don't have to anymore.*

That part of me lasted about four seconds before the rest of me wanted to tear it out with my hands. But it existed. I won't pretend otherwise. Grief does that — it hands you a knife and dares you to notice whose throat you're pointing it at.

The bees. I need to say it once and then not again: the bees died, or most of them. A handful made it out with me, riding the corridor draft, and I found three of them later on the inside of the hab window, walking slow figure-eights on the glass, looking for a flower that was ash by then. I put a dish of sugar water out. Two of them found it. I don't know why I'm telling you this except that somebody should know, somebody should carry it forward, because the official record will say *botanical assets destroyed during recovery operation* and there will be no line item for the bees.

* * *

Ilic found me an hour later sitting in the corridor with sugar water on my fingertips and soot in my hair. I heard his footsteps and didn't turn around.

"You weren't in the building," he said. Not a question. An audit finding.

"No."

"Where is the core, Petra?"

"Somewhere you'll never think to look." I turned then, and I made myself look at him, and I made my voice come out level. "You burned eleven years of work to make me give you a hard drive. Do you understand how that sounds? From out here? A man flew forty million kilometers to destroy a vegetable garden."

"It's not a vegetable garden and we both know it." He crouched down to my level, which I suspect was a trained de-escalation posture and which mostly succeeded in letting me see how tired he was. Interesting. Tired men make mistakes. "The message proves Earth abandoned a viable colony to bury a scandal. If that reaches the summit, the ceasefire dies in committee and the war goes hot again, and millions die on both sides. That's what your tomatoes are weighed against. That's the arithmetic."

"Then your arithmetic is broken," I said, "because the answer to a bad peace isn't a comfortable lie. It's a true thing handled by adults. And the first step of handling a true thing is not burning it."

Something crossed his face then — quick, quickly suppressed — and I recognized it because I'd seen it in the mirror that morning: the flicker of a man hearing an argument he'd been having with himself for longer than he'd admit. I filed it away. Auditors respect evidence. I intended to become evidence.

"You have until tomorrow's shuttle window," he said, standing. "Then I stop asking politely, and I promise you, Ms. Oyelaran, polite is the best version of me you will ever meet."

"Noted for my records," I said. "Which you've apparently read."

"All of them," he said, and walked away down the corridor, and I swear to whoever keeps the logs of enforcers that his shoulders were not the shoulders of a man who had won.

* * *

That night I packed.

Not much. Water, ration bars, the good thermal shell, the med kit, the repair kit, the shortwave handset. And the seed canisters — 114 through 118, five steel cylinders of heritage grain rattling faintly when I shook them, each one containing, nested in the center of its nitrogen-sealed heart, a data core holding the testimony of a woman two hundred years dead named Dr. Yusra Adeyemi, chief terraforming scientist of the Meridian project, who had looked into the cameras of her own archive and said, in a language nobody speaks anymore: *They knew. They signed the report. They cut the funding anyway, and they called us dead to avoid the inquiry.*

Five canisters. Five copies. Redundancy is the gardener's first law: never trust one seed to carry the whole harvest.

Before I sealed the pack I went to the window where the last bees were still walking their slow circuits, and I opened it to the corridor, because maybe some of them would find the hydroponics bay, and maybe they wouldn't, but I refused to be the second person in this story to seal something alive in with a lie.

Then I looked out at the burned greenhouse one more time. Under the stars the ruined dome looked like a ribcage, black struts against gray sky, and the ash on the remaining glass caught the starlight and glittered like frost. My garden. Kept for the unborn, killed by the born. I pressed my palm flat against the cold pane, over the ash, and I said the words I'd said at the start of every log for eleven years — *good morning, Meridian, this is your caretaker speaking* — except this time I said goodnight, and this time I added a postscript:

*I'm bringing help.*

Then I shouldered the pack, checked the compass bearing toward Cape Verity — three hundred kilometers of failed terraforming belt, salt flats, and dead air between me and the relay station — and walked out through the service lock into the wind.

Behind me, faint through the closing door, I heard the drones spin up.

He'd lied about the shuttle window too. Of course he had. Generous with time he never meant to honor — but this time, I was already gone.
