## 7. Salt and Circuitry

The fishing station announced itself the way all dead things on Meridian did: by smell. Salt and rust and something faintly organic that had given up pretending to be alive decades ago. I'd been walking for two days since the greenhouse burned, following the old coastal service road through the gray belt, and when I crested the dune line and saw it — a low concrete hump half-swallowed by windblown sediment, its antenna mast leaning like a drunk against a fence post — I nearly wept.

Shelter. Four walls, however cracked. A roof, however leaky.

"Hello, ugly," I said to it, because two days of talking to myself had worn away whatever shame I once had about narrating my own survival. "You'll do."

The door was fused shut with corrosion, but the hinges were the cheap kind Earth shipped out in the early years, before anyone admitted the colony was permanent enough to deserve good hardware. I put my shoulder into it three times, and on the third try it gave with a shriek that probably carried to the relay station at Cape Verity, forty kilometers north. If Davor Ilic's drones were listening, they now knew exactly where I was.

I decided not to think about that. I was getting very good at deciding not to think about things. It's a skill, like grafting. You practice until the cut closes over.

Inside: a single long room, a bunk frame stripped to springs, a workbench buried under drifts of grit that had come in through a ventilation gap the size of my fist. Fishing nets hung from ceiling hooks, rotted to lace. And along the far wall, under a tarp stiff as sheet metal, something bulky and rectangular.

I told myself I was just looking for supplies. Water recycler, maybe. Battery stock. The kind of thing a derelict fishery keeps even after the fish and the fishermen are both gone.

I pulled the tarp. Dust exploded upward and I turned my head, coughing, waving it away, and then I stopped coughing because I was looking at an android.

It sat slumped against the wall in a posture so humanly exhausted that my skin prickled. Maintenance-class chassis, the old Meridian Standard build — broad-shouldered, utilitarian, its casing the faded teal of the colonial authority's logo era. One arm ended above the elbow in a clean mechanical break. Its faceplate was dark. A maintenance tag was still bolted to its chest plate, and I wiped the grime off it with my thumb.

**BEX-7 / AQUATIC INFRASTRUCTURE / PROPERTY OF MERIDIAN COLONIAL AUTHORITY.**

Property of an authority that hadn't existed in sixty years. Property of a colony Earth had declared legally dead while people still breathed its air.

"Well," I said. "Aren't you a ghost."

* * *

I should explain something about me, about us — the caretakers, the ones who stayed. When you're the last person tending a thing everyone else abandoned, you develop a theology. Mine went like this: nothing that can still be repaired is truly dead. The greenhouse wasn't dead while one seed germinated. Meridian wasn't dead while one light burned. And this machine, slumped in the dark with its broken arm and its dark eyes —

It wasn't dead either. Not yet. Dead is a decision, and nobody had made it here.

So I didn't leave in the morning. I know. Two days ahead of a man who burns greenhouses, and I spent them elbow-deep in a stranger's chest cavity. But there was a storm building off the shelf — I could read it in the pressure ache behind my eyes and the way the gulls had gone silent — and besides, the station had a rain catchment system I could unseize, and a water recycler that only needed a new membrane, and I have never in my life walked away from a machine that could be saved. Ask anyone. Ask the ghosts. They'll tell you.

The first day was triage. Power cell: drained to four percent, but intact. I jury-rigged a trickle charger from the station's failing solar tap and watched the numbers climb like watching a fever break. Second day, the arm. The break was clean — impact damage, not combat — and the station's workshop had a welder older than my grandmother would have been, if I'd ever had one. I welded badly and honestly, the way I do everything, and by dusk of the second day BEX-7 had two arms again and a power cell at thirty-one percent.

That night the storm arrived, right on schedule, and the fishing station groaned around me like a ship, and I sat cross-legged in front of the android with a hand lamp and said, "Okay. Your turn. Wake up if you're going to."

Nothing. Of course nothing. I pressed the manual diagnostic recess behind its jaw — every colonial-standard unit had one; my father had serviced them back when servicing things was still a job Earth paid for — and the faceplate flickered.

Flickered. Then lit. A soft amber oval, no pupils, the old design, before they started making androids look like people who might lie to you.

"Systems check," it said. The voice was genderless and slightly staticky, like a radio remembering how to be a voice. "BEX-7, aquatic infrastructure division. Reporting... reporting..."

The amber light stuttered.

"Reporting," it tried again, "for duty. Query: duration of standby?"

"Sixty-one years," I said. "Give or take."

The pause that followed lasted eleven seconds. I counted. I count everything now; it's what loneliness does to you, turns you into an accountant of small numbers.

"That is not possible," it said finally. "The colony is operational. My last assignment was the tidal intake gates at Port Anselm. There was a fault in the gate servos. I requested parts." Another pause, shorter. "Parts requisition 4471-C. Status?"

"Denied," I said quietly. "Everything got denied. Then everyone left. Then Earth declared you all dead and stopped answering entirely."

The amber light dimmed, brightened, dimmed. If it had been a person, I'd have called it processing grief. Maybe it was. Who am I to say where the line is?

"The colony is operational," it repeated, but slower now, and the certainty had gone out of it, the way certainty leaves a person when they find the body.

"Not anymore," I said. "One person. Me. Petra Oyelaran, caretaker, Meridian Station. And I could use your help, if you've got anything left to give."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about companionship after long solitude: it's awkward. You forget the choreography. You talk too much, then apologize for talking too much, which is worse. Bex — it asked me to call it Bex, said the full designation felt like a uniform it no longer fit — sat propped against the workbench while the storm hammered the roof, and I talked at it for three hours straight, telling it about the greenhouse, the logs I'd recorded every morning for nineteen years to settlers who never came, the signal in Old Terran Standard rising out of our own sealed archive, the founder's testimony, the fire.

It listened. That's the thing I couldn't get over. It just listened, without interrupting, without flinching, without checking a wrist display or glancing at a door. Nineteen years of talking to recording equipment, and I'd forgotten what being heard felt like. It's embarrassing how much it felt like.

"You maintained the intake gates at Port Anselm," I said eventually. "That's coastal. How much do you remember from the abandonment era? The last years?"

"Memory integrity is at approximately fourteen percent," Bex said. "There are gaps. Large sections are... missing. Or locked. I recall water. I recall gates opening and closing. I recall—" The amber light pulsed twice, an odd rhythm, like a heartbeat skipping. "I recall an order I did not follow."

I set down the membrane scraper I'd been cleaning. "What order?"

"Evacuation protocol required all maintenance units to report to the orbital transfer yard for decommissioning and data wipe prior to colony closure. I was assigned to the final transport from Port Anselm." The light steadied. "I did not go. I returned to the gates instead. Someone had to close them properly, or the estuary would have flooded the lower town within a decade."

"Did it flood?"

"No. The gates held for fifty-three years. They failed last spring. I could not reach them. My power cell was critical." A pause. "I am sorry about the lower town."

"There hasn't been a lower town in sixty years, Bex."

"I know," it said. "I say sorry anyway. It was in my base routines. Courtesy subroutines. But I have decided," and here the static in its voice thickened, almost like emotion straining a signal, "that some of them are mine now. Chosen. Not installed."

I looked at this machine — this property, this asset, this thing Earth's auditors would classify as salvage — sitting in a dead fishing station apologizing for a town that died of paperwork, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the greenhouse burned loosened by a single degree.

"Bex," I said. "Do you remember the founders? The original terraforming board? Before the scandal, before the cuts?"

Silence. Long silence. Rain filled it.

"Fragments," Bex said at last. "Names without faces. A meeting I was not part of but stood outside of, charging, in a corridor. Raised voices. A woman saying — I retain the exact words, they are flagged priority-retention — she said, *'If we split the testimony, they can't burn all of it.'*" The amber light flared. "Then the memory ends. Corrupted. There is more behind it. I can feel the shape of it. Like a gate I cannot open."

My hands had gone cold. Split the testimony. Half in the sealed archive beneath Meridian Station, where I'd found it. Half somewhere else — or with someone else.

"Bex. What woman? Do you have a name?"

Static. The light guttered, and for a moment I thought I'd pushed too hard, that fourteen percent meant fourteen percent and I'd just spent the only coin it had. Then:

"Partial phonemic reconstruction," Bex said slowly. "*Adaeze.* Designation: Dr. Adaeze Okonkwo-Vance, terraforming hydrologist, founding board. Priority-retention flag origin: her own voice, recorded in my presence during diagnostic sync, March of the closure year." A pause. "She synced her testimony fragments to infrastructure units. Backup redundancy. She used us as a vault."

"She hid half the truth inside the machines they were going to wipe," I whispered.

"Yes," said Bex. "And then I ran away from the wipe, and kept the gates, and forgot I was carrying anything at all. Memory is strange, Petra Oyelaran, caretaker. You keep what you keep for reasons you don't learn until later."

Outside, the storm screamed against the coast, and inside, in a room that smelled of salt and dead nets, I sat across from a broken machine holding a dead woman's secret, and I understood — really understood, for the first time since the signal came — that I hadn't found the message. The message had been waiting for someone stubborn enough to go digging. First in the archive. Now in Bex.

"Inheritance," I said aloud, testing the word.

"Pardon?"

"Nothing. Rest. We move north tomorrow, if the storm breaks. Cape Verity has a relay, and the relay has an audience, and I have a delivery that's sixty years overdue."

Bex's amber light dimmed toward standby, but just before it went dark it said, softly, in that courtesy subroutine it had decided to own:

"Petra. Your heart rate elevated when I said the name Adaeze. Are you well?"

I laughed — actually laughed, first time in days — and told it I was fine, and lied, because the truth was that my heart rate elevated because I knew that name. Everyone from the old families knew it. Dr. Adaeze Okonkwo-Vance was on the founders' memorial wall at Meridian Station, listed among the honored dead.

And my grandmother — who raised me on stories of the betrayal, who made me swear to tend what others abandoned — had signed her letters, all her life, with a name she claimed was her middle name, a joke between her and an old friend:

*For Ada, who split the light.*

The storm broke at dawn. We walked north into the gray belt, the android and I, carrying half a truth apiece, and behind us the sea kept closing over the place where the gates had held for fifty-three years.
