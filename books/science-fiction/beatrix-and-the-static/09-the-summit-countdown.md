## 9. The Summit Countdown

The fishing station's radio had two settings: dead and lying. For three days it had done nothing but hiss at me like a cat I'd wronged in a previous life, and then on the fourth morning, mid-sip of recycled tea that tasted like regret and copper, it cleared its throat and started talking.

"—delegates from the Terran Assembly and the Colonial Concord have confirmed attendance at the peace summit convening in eleven days aboard Meridian Station—"

I stopped drinking. The tea went cold in my hand, which, to be fair, was an improvement.

Meridian Station. Not Geneva Orbital, not Concord Hub, not any of the dozen neutral venues where Earth and the breakaway colonies had spent four years throwing diplomatic furniture at each other. Meridian Station. The orbital platform above my colony. The one they'd decommissioned, stripped, and left hanging over our heads like a tombstone with a docking ring.

They were holding the peace summit in a graveyard. And I was standing in the grave itself, holding the eulogy nobody wanted read.

* * *

Bex found me staring at the radio the way you'd stare at a snake that had just recited poetry. The android's remaining eye whirred through its focus cycle — a sound I'd come to think of as thinking, though God knows what passed for thought in that dented skull.

"You've heard," I said.

"I have processed the broadcast," Bex said. Its voice still had that broken-harmonica quality, syllables arriving slightly out of order, as if the words had to queue up behind each other in a corridor too narrow for them. "Probability assessment: the summit location is not coincidence."

"Nothing about this has been coincidence since the day I was born, probably." I set down the tea. "Eleven days. Do you understand what eleven days means?"

"It means your deadline has changed."

"My deadline has *died*." I started pacing, which in the fishing station meant taking four steps, turning around, and taking four steps back, like a prisoner in a cell built by someone who hated prisoners. "I was walking to Cape Verity. Two hundred kilometers of gray nothing, relay station at the end, push the message out into the dark and hope somebody honest was listening. That plan took weeks. That plan assumed I had *weeks*. Now I have eleven days and the destination isn't a relay anymore — it's the summit itself. The message doesn't go out to strangers. It goes directly into the room where the people who buried it will be sitting."

"Or," Bex said, "into the hands of the people who sent a man to burn your garden."

Right. That. There was always that.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about hope: it hurts more than despair. Despair is flat. Despair is a bed you lie in because there's nowhere else to go. But hope — real hope, the kind with a date attached — hope is a knife you carry everywhere and keep forgetting you're holding until you grip something too hard.

For six years I had kept Meridian alive on ritual alone. Watering plants for settlers who would never come. Recording logs for descendants who would never exist. Tending a garden in a graveyard because the alternative was admitting the graveyard was all there was. That kind of devotion doesn't need hope. It runs on stubbornness, which is cheaper and burns longer.

But now there was a number. Eleven. A place. Meridian Station. A room full of delegates with cameras running, and in my hands — literally in my hands, in a seed canister wrapped in thermal cloth against my ribs — half of a testimony proving that Earth had known my colony was viable when it cut us loose. Had known, and had buried the proof under our own archive, in a language they'd let die so no one left alive could read it.

Except someone had read it. Except me.

"They'll have security," I said. "Summit security, Assembly marshals, Concord guards, and whatever corporate muscle Veyra Dynamics can dress up as contractors. And somewhere in that mix, Davor Ilic, reporting that he destroyed the core, hoping to God nobody checks his math."

"You believe he lied to his employers."

"I believe he looked at the founder recording for ninety seconds and then couldn't look at himself in a mirror for a week." I'd seen the crack in him, there in the canyon, when the storm tried to bury us and he chose digging me out over digging for the canister. Men like Ilic don't change. They just find the fault line that was always there and pretend it opened recently. "He bought us time. Maybe a little, maybe a lot. Depends how long it takes Veyra to audit their auditor."

"Then the calculation stands," Bex said. "You cannot reach Meridian Station by conventional transport. Your funds consist of—" it paused, eye cycling, possibly performing comedy, "—eleven credits and a jar of preserved algae paste."

"The algae paste is non-negotiable."

"There is a cargo shuttle depot forty kilometers south. Derelict since the withdrawal. If any craft remains flightworthy—"

"If any craft remains flightworthy, it'll be the one missing its guidance core, its fuel cells, and both seats."

"Yes," Bex agreed placidly. "That is the likely outcome. We should begin walking anyway."

And that — that absurd, rusted, half-blind machine agreeing with my suicide march without a flicker of hesitation — that was the moment I understood I wasn't alone anymore. Six years of talking to plants, and the first thing to answer me back was a maintenance android with a memory like Swiss cheese and the conversational warmth of a filing cabinet.

I wouldn't have traded it. I want that on the record.

* * *

We walked south along the old coastal road while the news feeds chased themselves in circles inside my earpiece. The summit announcement had detonated across every channel like a flare over black water.

Earth's position, per the Assembly spokesman: this summit represented a historic opportunity for reconciliation, a chance to heal the wounds of the Separation. He said *heal the wounds* with the smooth sincerity of a man reading a statement written by committee, and I found myself wondering if he knew. If any of them knew. If somewhere in the Assembly archives there was a folder matching mine, sealed and forgotten, or if the truth had died with the founders and lived on only in a seed canister pressed against a madwoman's ribs.

The Colonies' position, per Concord First Minister Adeyemi: the summit would proceed only if Earth acknowledged, in principle, the legitimacy of colonial claims arising from the abandonment era. She used the phrase *abandonment era* the way other people use *the war*, and I noticed she didn't say *the dead colonies*, because officially there were no dead colonies. Officially, Meridian had been a fiscal write-off. A rounding error with weather.

Officially, I didn't exist.

"Listen to them," I muttered, on hour six of the walk, gray plain on either side, the failed terraforming belt stretching toward the horizon like a scar that never learned to close. "Four years of war, thousands dead on both sides, and neither of them will say the actual word. Abandoned. We were abandoned. People were told help was coming and help was never coming and everyone in charge knew it."

"You are angry," Bex observed.

"I'm *hopeful*, Bex. It presents similarly."

"That is consistent with human biochemistry. Hope elevates cortisol alongside dopamine. Anger does the same."

"Thank you for medically validating my personality."

We crested a rise and I stopped, because below us lay one of the old atmospheric processors — a tower the size of a cathedral, dead these sixty years, its intake vanes furred with mineral frost. My grandfather's generation had built those. Had believed in them. Had been promised that in fifty years the sky would turn blue and the ships would come and Meridian would be a word that meant *home* instead of a word that meant *write-off*.

Fifty years came. The sky stayed the color of dishwater. The ships never came.

And now, in eleven days, ships would come. Hundreds of them. Delegates and aides and soldiers and journalists, descending on Meridian Station like locusts in formal wear, to negotiate peace over the corpse of the thing they'd killed.

Fine. Let them come. I'd be there too. And I'd bring the one guest of honor nobody had put on the seating chart: a dead woman's voice, recorded two centuries ago, saying *we knew, we knew, we chose the contract over the colonists* — in her own words, in Old Terran Standard, in a language Earth had murdered and I had resurrected alone in a greenhouse.

* * *

That night we sheltered in the processor's shadow, and I made the mistake of doing arithmetic.

"Cape Verity is still two hundred klicks north," I said, drawing lines in the dust with a stick. "Meridian Station is in orbit, which means I need a launch window, which means I need either the shuttle depot to contain a miracle or a port, and the nearest functioning port is New Caledonia, which is nine hundred klicks east, which is—"

"Not possible in eleven days on foot."

"Thank you, Bex, I was building to that."

"Then the shuttle depot is the only viable option."

"The shuttle depot is forty klicks of walking followed by almost certain disappointment."

"Yes," Bex said. "Hope presents similarly."

I threw the stick at it. It caught the stick. Of course it did.

But here's the thing I didn't say out loud, lying in my bedroll under a sky the color of old dishwater, listening to the wind moan through a machine built to give us air and never finished: I wasn't afraid of the walking. I'd been walking away from things my whole life — from Earth, from the evacuation lists, from the slow-motion funeral of a world. Walking was the one skill the abandonment era had given me for free.

What I was afraid of was smaller and sharper. It was the thought of standing in that summit hall, playing the founder's voice, watching the delegates' faces do the polite thing — the concerned nodding, the grave expressions, the procedural language — and then watching them file it. Bury it again, deeper this time, under treaties and trade schedules and the enormous gravity of *moving forward*. Because that's what institutions do with truth that costs too much. They don't deny it. They shelve it.

The founders hadn't been silenced by villains with guns. They'd been silenced by minutes and adjournments. By men who said *noted for the record* and meant *never*.

If I was going to spend my one life on this, it would not die in a subcommittee.

* * *

On the third morning of the walk, the radio did something new. It interrupted itself.

"—repeat, this is an official advisory from the Office of Summit Security. In preparation for the Meridian peace conference, a full communications blackout will be imposed across the Meridian system beginning in eight days. All unlicensed transmissions will be traced and jammed. All civilian traffic within the exclusion zone requires pre-clearance. Unauthorized vessels approaching Meridian Station will be—"

I turned it off.

"Bex."

"Yes, Petra."

"They're going to lock down the whole system. Eight days from now, nothing gets in or out without permission. Which means my eleven days just became eight, and my miracle shuttle just became a miracle shuttle flying through a military cordon."

Bex's eye cycled through a long series of focal lengths, which I'd learned was its version of a deep breath. "There is another implication," it said.

"Enlighten me."

"A communications blackout of that scale requires justification. Summits are normally *opened* to media. This one is being closed. Someone anticipates information that must not escape."

I stood very still on the gray road, wind pulling at my coat, and felt the knife of hope turn over in my chest.

Veyra Dynamics. Ilic's employers. They'd lost their auditor's report — or believed they hadn't — and now the most dangerous place on the planet for them was a hall full of delegates and cameras orbiting directly above the archive they'd spent two centuries keeping sealed. Of course they wanted a blackout. Blackouts are what you buy when you can't buy silence any other way.

"They know," I said quietly. "Somebody over there knows the message survived. And they're not sending another Ilic. They're sending whatever comes after Ilic."

"Then we should walk faster," Bex said.

So we walked faster. Forty kilometers to a dead depot and a maybe-shuttle, eight days to a locked sky, and somewhere behind us — or ahead of us, with men like that it hardly mattered — whatever Veyra sent next, already moving.

I put my hand against the canister at my ribs, felt the hard little cylinder of it, warm from my own body, and allowed myself exactly one sentence of hope before I folded it away and saved it for later:

*Hold on, Elena Vashti. You waited two hundred years to speak. Eleven days more, and I'll get you into the room.*

The road stretched gray ahead of us, and we walked.
