## 1. Contraband Heartbeats

There are three rules to running contraband through Meridian Orbital Customs, and I've broken all of them at least once, which is why I only have two left.

Rule one: never get attached. Rule two: never get noticed. The third rule used to be *never work for anyone who pays in unmarked scrip*, but that one died the winter my heat exchanger did, and I've regretted its passing every day since.

The scanner bay on Docking Ring Four smelled the way it always smelled — ozone and recycled coffee and the faint industrial melancholy of a station that had been scheduled for decommissioning eleven years ago and was still somehow paying its electric bills. I stood at the inspection counter with my manifest chip in hand and my heartbeat doing a very professional impression of boredom.

"Music files," said Inspector Okonkwo, who had been scanning my cargo for six years and had never once found anything, because Okonkwo was good at his job in the way that mattered: he'd stopped actually looking.

"Music files," I agreed. "Traditional Meridian folk recordings. Very niche. Very sad."

"Everything from Meridian is sad."

"That's the folk part."

He snorted and waved the scanner wand over the crate anyway, because procedure was procedure, and the wand hummed its little humming song, and somewhere inside forty sealed data-slates, two hundred and seventeen dead colonists' memories sat very still and pretended to be a symphony.

Here's how it works, in case you're new, or in case you're Core-side and reading this out of some anthropological interest in the people your government abandoned. A memory recording looks, to a dumb scanner, like dense audio-visual data with emotional metadata attached. Music files look like dense audio-visual data with emotional metadata attached. The difference is that one of them makes you cry because of a violin, and the other makes you cry because it's your mother's face, moving, laughing, alive, four months before the fever took her.

I carried both kinds. Only one kind paid.

"Clear," Okonkwo said, stamping the manifest without reading it. "You know, Eriksen, most smugglers rotate routes. Keeps them interesting."

"I'm not most smugglers."

"No," he said, with something that might have been pity if either of us could afford it. "You're punctual."

* * *

The run down to Meridian took forty minutes in my ship, the *Patient Answer*, which I'd named during an optimistic period of my life and now considered less a name than a diagnosis. She was a twenty-year-old short-haul freighter with a hull patch shaped like a question nobody wanted answered, and she handled like a shopping cart with opinions.

Meridian came up slow through the viewport, the way dying things do. From orbit it still looked almost pretty — a grey-green marble wrapped in cloud, the northern ice cap catching the system's dim sun like a coin at the bottom of a well. But I knew what the clouds were hiding. Shuttered hydroponics bays. A spaceport running at a tenth capacity. Whole districts where the streetlights had been sold for scrap and the residents had voted — formally, with minutes taken — to stop repairing anything they couldn't carry.

The Core wrote us off when I was nine years old. There was a ceremony. There was a speech about "fiscal realities" and "managed decline," which is what powerful people say when they mean *we're letting you die slowly so it doesn't make noise*. Then the subsidy ships stopped coming, and the colony did what colonies do, which is shrink.

Shrinking is a funny word for it. It sounds tidy. Like a sweater. What it actually means is this: the school in Halvard District has eleven students left, and their teacher is seventy-three. It means the hospital has one surgeon and a waiting list measured in seasons. It means that last year, old Marta Lindqvist sold her life-memories — sixty years of them, weddings and winters and the birth of two sons, one of whom made it offworld and one of whom didn't — to a Core memory broker, for enough scrip to buy passage to a world she'd never seen, carrying nothing but a body full of holes.

I flew her recording out myself, before she left. Part of the job. Somebody has to take the dead and the departed where they're going, and on Meridian those categories overlap more every year.

That's the trade, if you're wondering why anyone does it. Down here, a recorded memory is worth food. Worth medicine. Worth a month of heat. Grieving families sell pieces of their dead to brokers, and brokers ship them Core-side to rich people who like to *experience* authentic frontier tragedy — and somewhere in that pipeline, a cut comes back to the person willing to fly it past customs, because the Core taxes grief like everything else.

So I carry heartbeats in music crates, and I don't ask whose they are unless the paperwork requires it, and I have exactly two rules.

* * *

Delivery that afternoon was in the lowtown warrens under the old cannery, to a woman named Signe whose husband had died in a pressure accident back in the spring. She met me in a stairwell that smelled of rust and boiling cabbage, and she counted out the scrip twice, the way poor people do, and then she held the data-slate against her chest like a child.

"You're sure it's all there?" she asked. "All of him?"

"I'm sure it's what the broker sent me."

Which was true, and we both knew what it meant. Brokers trim. They cut the boring parts, the long silences, the arguments — the parts that make a person a person instead of a highlight reel. Somewhere in Core processing, her husband's worst days and dullest afternoons had been edited out for marketability, and what she'd get back was a husband optimized for consumption.

She knew. She bought it anyway. That's Meridian.

"Thank you," she said, and then, because everyone down here eventually says it, even to strangers, even to smugglers: "You'll be careful? Flying?"

"Always am."

"Liar," she said fondly, and went up the stairs hugging her dead man.

I stood in the stairwell a moment longer than the job required. This is the occupational hazard nobody warns you about. Not the scanners, not the inspectors, not the Core tax on human souls. The hazard is the stairwells. The two extra seconds. The moment where you think, *that could be someone holding me.*

Then I put the thought away in the box where I keep all such thoughts — the box is large, well-organized, and has never once been opened — and went back to work.

* * *

The *Patient Answer*'s cabin was three meters by four and contained everything I owned, which is the kind of sentence that sounds romantic until you count. I made tea from a ration pack that predated the abandonment, sat in the pilot's chair with my boots on the console, and ran the evening's ledger.

Profit margin on the Signe run: acceptable. Fuel reserves: insulting. Heat exchanger: holding, in the sense that a dam holds. And in the message queue, blinking with the patient insistence of a thing that knows it's interesting:

**INCOMING — ANONYMOUS — ENCRYPTION: COMMERCIAL-GRADE — OFFER ATTACHED**

Anonymous clients weren't unusual. Anonymous clients were half my business; grief doesn't like witnesses. But anonymous clients usually wanted memories moved from point A to point B, and this offer wasn't asking for a delivery route. It was asking for a destination.

An encrypted package, to be picked up from a dead drop in the orbital yards. To be carried to coordinates in the colony's abandoned northern sector. No recipient listed. No return contact. Triple rate, paid up front, in scrip so clean it had to be stolen.

I read it three times. On the third read, I caught myself doing the thing I never do — imagining. Imagining what kind of package needed triple rate and silence. Imagining the northern sector, which I hadn't flown over in two years because there was nothing there to fly over, just ice and the ruins of the old relay stations and wind that could strip paint.

Rule one: never get attached. Rule two: never get noticed. Rule three, deceased: never trust money that arrives too easily.

I looked at the fuel gauge. The fuel gauge looked back at me with the smug certainty of a machine that knows it has leverage.

"One job," I told the empty cabin, because talking to yourself is free therapy and the therapist on Meridian charges by the season. "One weird job, then back to heartbeats and music files."

The *Patient Answer*, being a ship and therefore incapable of judgment, said nothing. But somewhere in her ancient systems, I swear, the environmental controls cycled — a soft mechanical sigh, the sound of a vessel that had heard this speech before and knew exactly how it would end.

I queued the pickup for morning shift change, when the yard cameras rotated and the inspectors got sleepy. Then I lay in my bunk under a blanket rated for temperatures considerably warmer than my cabin, and stared at the ceiling panel I'd been meaning to replace since the abandonment, and did not think about Signe climbing the stairs with her husband pressed to her chest.

Did not think about it at all.

The box stayed shut. Mostly.

Outside the viewport, Meridian turned, grey and green and patient, wearing its clouds like a shroud it had grown tired of explaining. Two hundred thousand kilometers below, eleven children went to sleep in a school built for four hundred, and somewhere north, under meters of ice, in a sector I hadn't thought about in years, something waited at coordinates I wouldn't receive until tomorrow.

I fell asleep telling myself it was just another package.

I want it noted, for the record, that I almost believed me.
