## 2. The Client Who Paid in Silence

The message arrived at 0300 station time, which on Meridian meant nothing at all, because Meridian had stopped pretending to have a proper day-night cycle sometime before I was born. The colony ran on a kind of collective shrug. Lights dimmed when the power grid felt like it. People slept when the wind off the ice fields got too loud to argue with.

But the message came at 0300, and I was awake anyway, because that's what I did now. I slept in slices, like cheap bread.

**INCOMING: ANONYMOUS CLIENT — ENCRYPTION TIER 4**

I stared at it for a long moment with my feet propped on the console of my ship, the *Kindly Vulture*, a name I'd chosen at nineteen because I thought it was funny and had since grown into like an old coat. Tier 4 encryption wasn't just unusual. It was expensive. It was the kind of encryption that said *I have money and something to hide*, and in my experience those two things traveled together about as often as hunger and honesty didn't.

I opened it.

The job offer unfolded across my screen in clean, careful text. No voice. No face. No name. Just terms:

*Package pickup: Docking Bay 12, Meridian Orbital, 0600 local. Delivery: coordinates attached. Payment: triple standard rate, half upfront.*

Triple rate. I read it twice, the way you'd reread a letter from someone who owed you money, checking for the trick.

There was no trick visible. That was the problem. In the smuggling trade—and let's be honest about what I do; "courier" is a word for people with lawyers—triple rate meant one of three things. One: the cargo was dangerous. Two: the destination was worse. Three: the client wanted discretion so absolute they were willing to buy it by the gram.

Usually it was all three.

Attached were coordinates in Meridian's northern sector. I pulled up the map, such as it was—the colony's cartography hadn't been updated since the Core pulled funding, so half my maps were hand-annotated scans of hand-annotated scans—and found the marker sitting out past the last functioning settlement like a period at the end of a sentence nobody wanted to finish.

Northern sector. Abandoned sector. The part of Meridian where the ice had started winning.

*Delivery confirmation: none required,* the message finished. *No communication will be accepted or answered. Do not reply to this address. It will not exist after 0400.*

And sure enough, at 0359, the address dissolved like sugar in rain. Half payment hit my account at 0400 exactly, tripled and silent, and I sat there in the dark of my cockpit listening to the *Vulture*'s air recyclers wheeze their ancient song and thought about all the sensible things I could do instead.

Then I thought about my account balance, which had the approximate emotional weight of a held breath.

"Damn it," I told the empty cockpit, and the cockpit, being empty, agreed with me.

* * *

My handler is named Oskar Lindqvist, and he operates out of a tea shop in the Low Terraces that sells, as far as I've ever been able to determine, approximately four cups of actual tea per year. The rest is cover. Oskar has run contraband logistics on Meridian for thirty years, and he looks exactly like what he is: a soft, round man with reading glasses and flour on his apron, the sort of person your eyes slide off like rain off waxed canvas. It's a gift. He's told me it took him a decade to cultivate it, and another decade to stop being flattered when people underestimated him.

I found him at dawn, sweeping the step of his shop with the methodical patience of a man who has swept the same step through three administrations and one minor civil disturbance.

"Triple rate," I said, by way of good morning.

Oskar didn't look up. "Good morning to you too, Casper."

"Anonymous client. Tier 4 encryption. Northern sector delivery. No communication, no confirmation, no contact protocol at all." I leaned against the doorframe. "Tell me I'm crazy."

"You're crazy." He swept a little more. "Also, come inside, you're letting the cold in, and the cold and I have an arrangement."

Inside, the shop smelled of dust and dried mint and the faint ozone tang of a signal relay hidden behind the biscuit tins, because Oskar believed in tradition. He poured me a cup of something that was technically adjacent to tea and sat down across from me, folding his hands, which he only did when he was about to be serious or about to lie. With Oskar those were usually the same gesture.

"The northern sector," he said. "You know what's out there."

"Ice. Dead relay stations. The old weather array. A lot of very committed nothing."

"Mm." He sipped his own cup. "And you know what was out there, before the abandonment?"

I shrugged. "Before my time. Before almost everybody's time. Why, you going to tell me a story?"

"I'm going to tell you a warning dressed as a story, which is the only kind of story worth telling." He set down his cup. "When the Core wrote us off—what, forty years ago now?—they didn't just pull funding. They pulled *records*. Sealed whole sectors. There are facilities out in that ice that aren't on any current map, and the people who know where they are tend to be either dead, retired, or lying."

"And our anonymous friend knows where one is."

"Our anonymous friend," Oskar said, "is paying you triple rate to walk into a sealed sector carrying a package they won't describe, to a location they won't confirm, with no way to call for help and no expectation of ever hearing from them again." He smiled at me over his glasses, gently, the way you'd smile at a child about to touch a hot stove. "Casper. My dear. My favorite liability. This job smells like a trap the way a fish market smells like fish."

"It smells like money."

"That's what the trap wants you to think. Traps are very vain that way." He tapped the table twice. "Half payment's already landed, I assume."

"Non-refundable, I checked."

"Of course it is. They want you invested. First lesson of the trade, Casper: the cheapest thing anyone can buy from a smuggler is not silence. It's *commitment*. Get them paid, get them curious, get them halfway out onto the ice, and they'll finish the trip on their own momentum, because turning around means admitting you were fool enough to start." He sighed and looked out the frost-feathered window at the grey morning. "I've watched better smugglers than you die of momentum."

"Name one."

"I'd rather not, it'll ruin my breakfast." He stood, gathered my cup, and patted my shoulder on the way to the sink. "Take the job or don't. You're a grown criminal, you can make your own mistakes. But if you take it—"

"You'll want a cut for the warning."

"—I'll want thirty percent for the *logistics*, which you'll need, because whatever's in that bay at 0600, you're not flying it north without route clearance, and I'm the only person on this rock who still remembers which crevasse fields the old survey teams flagged before everyone stopped caring whether survey teams came back." He glanced back at me. "Twenty-five percent, and I'll throw in the thermos."

"Thirty percent, and I keep the thermos full."

We shook on it, because on Meridian a handshake still meant something, mostly that both parties were too poor to sue each other.

* * *

Docking Bay 12, at 0600, was the kind of place where light went to give up. The orbital ring above Meridian had been built for traffic that never came; most of its bays stood empty, doors open to vacuum, ribs of bare metal catching the sun in long cold glints. Bay 12 still had atmosphere, barely, and a single work lamp that buzzed like an insect with opinions.

The package was waiting on a cargo pallet, alone in the middle of the deck, which somehow made it worse. Packages should sit among other packages. A lone package is a statement.

It was a case, roughly the size of a coffin for a small person or a very large book, matte black, seamless, no markings except a single recessed panel glowing a patient amber. No manifest tag. No shipping seal. No manufacturer's stamp. When I ran my scanner over it—and I want it noted that I did run my scanner over it, because I am a professional—it returned the electronic equivalent of a polite cough. Shielded. Everything shielded. Mass, density, contents: unknown, unknown, and please stop asking.

"Well," I said to the bay, to the lamp, to the case. "Nothing suspicious here at all."

The case did not answer, which I chose to find reassuring.

I checked the pallet's docking log out of habit. It had arrived on a maintenance skiff four hours earlier, logged under a service code that traced back, when I chased it, to a shell company that traced back to another shell company that traced back, eventually, to a holding firm registered on a Core world I'd never heard of, which was itself probably a shell. Somewhere at the bottom of all that paper was a person who wanted something moved across the ice of a dying colony without a single word passing between us, and had paid enough to make me stop asking questions I couldn't afford to have answered anyway.

That's the thing about greed. Nobody tells you it doesn't feel like hunger. It feels like arithmetic. You stand there in a cold bay doing sums—half now, half on delivery, reactor parts for the *Vulture*, a new coolant recycler, maybe finally fixing the portside heater so winter stops living in my bunk—and every sum comes out the same way: *take the job*. Instinct stands off to the side making its little speech about traps and fish markets and momentum, and greed just keeps tapping the calculator, patiently, like Oskar sweeping his step.

Instinct is loud. Greed is patient. On Meridian, patient wins.

I wrestled the case onto my cargo sled, strapped it down, and spent a full ten minutes staring at it before I keyed the sled's lift.

One thing I noticed, and I noticed it because noticing things is the only skill I've never managed to drink away or sell: the amber panel pulsed. Slowly. Steadily. About once every four seconds.

Like a heartbeat.

I told myself it was a status indicator. Status indicators pulse. It's practically the law.

I told myself that the whole way back to the *Vulture*, and the whole way through pre-flight, and I almost believed it right up until the moment I laid in the northern heading and the nav computer chimed, cheerful and idiotic:

*"Route note: destination lies beyond last maintained beacon. Estimated distance to nearest emergency services: 340 kilometers. Have a pleasant flight!"*

"Yeah," I muttered, and lit the drives. "Pleasant."

* * *

Meridian from orbit looks like a coin left out in the rain—a disc of grey-white ice with a few brown smudges where people still bothered to generate heat. The northern sector was the smooth part, the featureless part, the part where the ice had smoothed over everything we'd built and then kept smoothing, patient as interest on a debt.

I flew low, following Oskar's route notes, which he'd scrawled on actual paper like a barbarian and which included helpful annotations like *"crevasse field, trust me"* and *"old survey marker, probably fallen over, don't land near it"* and, once, simply *"???"* next to a stretch of blank ice, which I chose to interpret as a test of character.

The *Vulture* flew well. She always does when she thinks I might finally fix her properly with the fee. Her hull ticked and settled around me, her ancient engines hummed their two-note complaint, and the case rode in the cargo hold behind me, pulsing its slow amber heartbeat into the dark.

Somewhere over the old weather array, I tried the client's channel one more time. Protocol said no communication. But protocol also said the address wouldn't exist anymore, and I figured confirming that was different from breaking the rule. There's a difference. Smugglers live on differences.

The channel was dead. Not static-dead, not busy-dead. *Gone*-dead, the way a room is gone after a fire. Whatever had spoken to me at 0300 had folded its tent and vanished so cleanly it left me wondering, briefly, whether I'd dreamed the whole thing—except for the half payment sitting in my account like a stone in a shoe.

"No communication will be accepted or answered," I recited to myself, banking north. "Fine. I can take a hint. I once dated a woman who communicated exclusively through sighs. You're not even the worst client I've had."

The *Vulture* said nothing. She's heard worse.

The coordinates put me down on a plain of blue-white ice under a sky the color of an old bruise, forty kilometers past anything with a name. And there—exactly where the marker said, which bothered me more than if it hadn't been—stood a structure.

A relay station. Old Core construction, pre-abandonment, the angular brutalist kind they built back when they expected to build a hundred more. Ice had climbed its flanks and frozen mid-ascent, so it looked less like a building than like a shipwreck trying to become a hill. Its antenna mast leaned, but not broken. And as I brought the *Vulture* down through the thin snow-haze, kicking up a rooster tail of crystals, I saw the thing that turned my spine to a column of cold water.

Lights. Faint, deep inside, behind frost-furred windows.

Nobody lived in the northern sector. Nobody was supposed to live in the northern sector. Oskar had said it himself: sealed sectors, erased records, facilities that weren't on any map.

The case on my sled pulsed its amber heartbeat, faster now—or I imagined faster, which amounted to the same thing—and somewhere beneath the ice, something that had been waiting a long time for a courier foolish enough to follow arithmetic instead of instinct began, very quietly, to wake up.

I cut the engines and sat in the sudden silence, hands still on the controls, doing the math one more time.

Turn around: lose the second half, keep the first, live to spend it.

Go in: triple rate, complete, plus whatever curiosity costs these days.

"Greed is patient," I reminded myself, and popped the harness, and stepped out onto the ice toward the lights.

Later—I would think about this later, much later, in ways I couldn't yet imagine—I would remember that the station's door was already unlocked. That it opened before I reached it. That whoever was inside had been watching my descent the entire way down, and had decided, somewhere in those long minutes, to let me land.

But standing there on the ice with the wind moaning through the antenna mast and my breath freezing in front of me, all I knew was that the job had stopped being a delivery.

The door slid open. Warm yellow light spilled out across the snow.

And a voice—dry, human, and entirely unafraid—said:

"Took you long enough. Get inside before the cold gets ideas."
