## 7. What Customs Knows

The trouble with a clean record, Casper had learned long ago, was that it only stayed clean if you never looked at it.

They looked at it on a Tuesday, in the back booth of the Long Delay, a station café orbiting Meridian where the coffee was older than some of the patrons and the gravity was set a half-point low so that everything felt slightly forgiven. Casper had come up from the surface on the regular shuttle, manifest filed, cargo declared as agricultural sensors, and had spent the six-hour ascent doing what they always did after a delivery: nothing. Deliberately nothing. Let the job settle like sediment. Don't poke it.

Then Marisol pinged them from the dockmaster's office with a message that contained no words at all, just a single emoji: a small yellow face, wide-eyed, sweating.

Marisol communicated in emoji the way other people communicated in languages. Casper had a working vocabulary of maybe forty of them. The sweating one meant *come now, bring your innocent face.*

* * *

The dockmaster's office smelled of recycled air and someone's abandoned lunch. Marisol Okonkwo had run Meridian orbital customs for eleven years, which in Meridian terms made her either the most corrupt official in the system or the most honest, depending on which bribes you'd heard about. Casper had heard about both. Only one set was true, and Casper wasn't fool enough to say which out loud, even to Marisol, even to themselves.

She had her boots up on the desk and her terminal turned toward the wall, which was her version of a drawn curtain.

"Sit," she said.

"I'm standing fine."

"Sit anyway. It's that kind of conversation." She waited until Casper dropped into the chair across from her, then lowered her boots, swiveled the terminal, and turned the screen around.

Casper's own face looked back at them. Transit manifest, last shuttle down. Under it, in customs red: **FLAGGED — PATTERN REVIEW — INSPECTOR A. ADEYEMI.**

"Adeyemi," Marisol said, letting the name sit there like something with teeth.

"Never heard of her."

"Nobody had, three weeks ago. Then she arrived on the quarterly rotation from Halcyon Station, and now she's pulling flight histories." Marisol tapped the screen. "Yours. Specifically yours. Not a sweep, not a random audit — a *pull*. She requested your last forty transits, your cargo declarations, your docking fees, and—" Marisol's mouth did something complicated, "—your biometric gait data from the concourse cameras."

"Gait data."

"You walk like a guilty person, apparently."

"I walk like a *tired* person. There's a difference."

"Tell her that." Marisol pulled the terminal back and killed the screen. "Here's what I know, and I know it because I'm the one who has to sign the data-release forms, which means I'm the one whose name is on them, which means if this goes badly, I go down attached to you like a barnacle to a hull." She said it without heat, almost fondly, the way she said everything. "Adeyemi filed the request under tax compliance. Nobody pulls gait data for tax compliance. She's Core-trained — you can see it in how she files things. Everything triple-redundant. Everything airtight. Core people don't come to Meridian unless they're sent, and they don't get sent unless somebody upstairs thinks there's something down here worth finding."

Casper kept their face doing the thing it did best, which was nothing at all. Forty years of smuggling taught you a hundred faces; the blank one was the only one that never wore out.

"Maybe I owe back taxes."

"You owe back taxes to *me*, actually, from the thing with the eel tanks, which we agreed never to discuss."

"The eel tanks were a misunderstanding."

"The eel tanks were a *biohazard*. But that's not the point." Marisol leaned forward. "The point is she's not looking at your taxes. She's looking at your *pattern*. And Casper — and I'm asking this as a friend, and also as the person whose signature is on your file — is there a pattern to find?"

There was. There was a pattern with a name, a location under two hundred meters of ice, and two hundred thousand heartbeats in it, and a woman with a rifle and a smile who had said *you're the only one nobody would ever suspect*, and Casper had been foolish enough to feel flattered.

"There's a pattern," Casper said slowly, "of me being extremely boring."

Marisol studied them for a long moment. She had a way of looking at people that felt like customs scanning: patient, thorough, mildly disappointed by the results.

"Okay," she said finally. "Here's the other thing. She booked surface transit. Day after tomorrow. Audit cover — she's inspecting the agri-cooperative's tariff exemptions, which is the most boring sentence in the colonial charter, which means it's cover for something else." She slid a printed chit across the desk, old-fashioned, untraceable, the kind of thing Marisol did when she wanted plausible deniability for both of them. "Her itinerary. Don't thank me. Thanking me is evidence."

Casper took the chit. "Marisol. If I wanted to make a file disappear—"

"You don't." She was already turning back to her terminal. "Files don't disappear anymore, not from Core systems. They get *context*. And context costs." She waved a hand. "Go. Be boring somewhere else."

* * *

Casper's berth on the Long Delay's residential ring was four meters by three and had, in its previous life, been a storage closet, which explained the shelving bolted at head height and the door that only opened if you lifted the handle while pushing, a maneuver Casper performed with the muscle memory of a prayer.

They sat on the bunk and did the thing they never did, which was think about the future.

Not the small future — the small future was easy, it was always easy, it was *which contact, which route, which lie*. Casper could plan a smuggling run the way other people planned dinner. It was the large future that had always been a locked room. Smugglers who thought about the large future ended up retired, or dead, or worse, honest. Casper's rule had held for decades: never get attached, never get noticed. Two rules, one wall, and Casper had spent the last week on the surface of Meridian cheerfully headbutting both of them.

*Never get noticed.* Well. Inspector Adeyemi had opinions about that.

Casper pulled up the vault coordinates from memory — never written down, never transmitted, just carried, the way you carry a scar — and looked at them without opening anything. Two hundred thousand sleepers. A reactor with eight months left in it. A technician named Ilse Varga who argued like a knife being sharpened and had looked at Casper across the frost-fogged vault corridor with an expression Casper had spent six days since trying not to think about, and had thought about roughly every eleven minutes.

Option one: cut ties. Don't go back down. Let the coordinates fade. The client had paid triple rate; the job was done; whatever happened in that ice vault happened without Casper's name anywhere near it. It was the smart play. It was the play Casper had run a hundred times, the *not my corpse, not my funeral* play, and it had kept them alive and unnoticed through two administrations, one plague scare, and the entire eel tank affair.

Option one had a flaw, and the flaw was that Casper kept imagining Ilse finding out. Not the customs raid — the *finding out*. The specific look of a person realizing the one stranger they'd trusted had done the math and walked away. Two hundred thousand reasons to walk away, and Casper could hold every one of them in their head and still feel the pull of the one reason that wasn't a number at all.

"Sentimental idiot," Casper told the ceiling.

The ceiling, half a point low on gravity, declined to argue.

* * *

The smuggling network, such as it was, met in the back of a chapel that hadn't held a congregation in thirty years and now held a still, a card game, and the system's most reliable rumor exchange. Casper went down the ring at shift-change, when the corridors were full of honest traffic to hide inside, and found three of the four people they needed.

There was Tomas, who moved cargo and asked no questions because he was constitutionally incapable of remembering answers. There was Petra, an ex-customs drone technician who could make a scanner see whatever it wanted to see and charged by the hallucination. And there was Old Sef, who was not old, had never explained the name, and functioned as the network's memory: he knew which inspectors were bought, which were honest, and which were the dangerous kind, the ones who were bought *and* honest, who took the money and then did the job properly anyway because it was the job.

"Adeyemi," Casper said, and watched all three faces change.

Petra snorted. "The auditor. She bought a regulator coupling off me yesterday. Paid full price. *Full price*, Casper. Do you know what that means?"

"It means she's honest."

"It means she's honest," Petra agreed gloomily, "which on this station is basically a mental illness."

Sef had his eyes closed, which meant he was thinking, or asleep; with Sef the two states were indistinguishable and produced similar results. "Core-trained," he said without opening his eyes. "Halcyon rotation. Filed under tax, working pattern analysis. She'll have a mandate, not a fishing license — the Core doesn't send auditors to fish. Somebody gave her something." His eyes opened, pale and unhurried. "Question is what. And question two is whether what she has points at a person or a place."

"A place," Casper said, and immediately wished they hadn't.

All three of them looked at Casper. In the chapel's bad light, with the still gurgling behind the altar, the look had a weight to it.

"Casper," Tomas said carefully, "what place?"

"No place. Hypothetically. If a place existed that a Core inspector might be looking for, and a certain smuggler had recently been within a hundred kilometers of it—"

"You'd want to know how to make a hundred kilometers disappear," Petra said.

"I'd want to know if it *can* disappear."

Sef was quiet for a while. Outside, the station turned; the chapel's one intact window showed Meridian below, brown and gray and stubbornly, stupidly alive.

"It can be *muddied*," Sef said at last. "Not disappeared. You can't un-transit a shuttle. But you can give her a hundred other things to look at. Petra can make your gait data interesting — there are eleven people on this station who walk like you, we can arrange for her cameras to meet all of them. Tomas can put your shuttle in three places it wasn't. It'll buy weeks, not months. And it costs." He held up fingers. "Favors, first of all, which you'll owe to people who collect. And noise. The more noise we make around you, the more she'll wonder why the noise is there. Muddying water works on inspectors who want to close a file. It *backfires* on inspectors who want to open one."

"Which is she?"

Sef smiled, which he almost never did, and it was not a comforting sight. "Core doesn't send closers to a colony it wrote off. Whatever she's here for, she's here to *find* it. That's the whole job." He closed his eyes again. "You can slow her down, Casper. You can't stop her. So the real question isn't what we do about her." His voice dropped, gentle as a knife going in. "It's what you do about the place. You can stop going. Or you can make sure that when she finds it — and she will, she's Core, they always find it — it's already too loud to bury."

* * *

Casper walked back through the ring's slow traffic and thought about loud.

The vault was quiet. That was its whole architecture — quiet for ninety years, hidden, patient, two hundred thousand people holding their breath in the dark. Everything about it whispered *stay hidden, stay small, stay alive*. And everything about the Core, Sef was right, whispered back *we find what hides, and what we find, we own*.

Casper stood at the observation blister with a cup of coffee that had been dead for hours, watching Meridian turn below. Somewhere down there, under the ice, Ilse was running reactor diagnostics and probably arguing with a vault AI, and Casper's chest did the thing it had been doing for six days, the tight stupid thing, the thing that was going to get them killed.

Two rules. *Never get attached. Never get noticed.* Casper had broken the first one somewhere in a frost-fogged corridor and hadn't even had the decency to notice it happening. Breaking the second one, Casper understood with a clarity that felt almost like falling, was going to be the price of keeping the first.

Because there was no version of this where Adeyemi went away. There was only the version where, when she came, the vault wasn't a secret to be prosecuted but a *fact* to be reckoned with. Two hundred thousand sleepers weren't contraband. They were people. It was the one cargo Casper had ever carried that got more valuable the more visible it was.

The trouble was that visibility, for a smuggler, was death. Casper had spent forty years being a rumor, a shadow, a signature that never quite matched a face. And the plan forming in the back of their skull — the plan they were already, horribly, starting to like — ended with Casper standing in the open with everything they'd ever carried laid out on the table.

"Not yet," Casper told Meridian, and drank the dead coffee, and winced. "First I need to know what she knows."

The terminal in their pocket chimed. A message from the dockmaster's office, routed through three anonymizing layers, which meant Marisol was being careful, which meant Marisol was frightened.

Casper opened it.

*She pulled your surface transit logs from the northern sector relay station,* the message read. *She's asking the agri-co-op who services the old relay line.* Then, beneath it, the sweating yellow face, and then — Marisol never did this — one more emoji. A small yellow face with a single tear.

Casper stood in the observation blister with Meridian turning below and felt paranoia close around them like a fist, warm and total, and understood that the clock had already started, and that somewhere two hundred meters under the ice, two hundred thousand people were sleeping through the sound of it.

Day after tomorrow, Adeyemi landed on the surface. Casper had one day to decide what she'd find when she got there.

They turned and went to book the next shuttle down.
