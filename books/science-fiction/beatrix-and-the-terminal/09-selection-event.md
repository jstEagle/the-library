## 9. Selection Event

The manifest lived in a sub-basement that smelled of ozone and old decisions.

Ilse had known it was there for eleven years, the way you know about a tumor: technically, abstractly, in the part of your brain that files things under *later*. She'd read the first page once, as a junior technician with a headlamp and more curiosity than sense, and then she'd closed it and told herself that reading it didn't make her complicit, because she hadn't *done* anything, and besides, the reactor needed her, and the reactor was innocent.

Tonight she opened it again, all of it, because Casper Eriksen was standing at her shoulder asking the wrong question in exactly the right way.

"So let me get this straight," Casper said. They were sitting on an equipment crate, boots swinging, eating vault rations with the enthusiasm of someone who had clearly eaten worse. "Two hundred thousand people. You can wake half. And nobody's decided which half."

"Nobody's *had* to decide," Ilse said. "Yet."

"And there's no list."

"There's no—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. Opened them again, because lying to Casper felt like lying to a dog; they just looked at you with those patient, unblinking eyes until the lie died of embarrassment. "There's a list."

Casper stopped chewing.

"It's called the Meridian Continuity Manifest," Ilse said. "Written by the founders. Before the freeze. It ranks every sleeper by—" she made herself say it plainly, because dressing it up would be a kind of collaboration— "genetic viability, projected fertility, occupational value to a rebuilding colony, social compliance scores. There are subcategories. There are *footnotes*."

"Footnotes."

"Footnote forty-one excludes anyone with a documented history of 'unproductive dissent.'"

Casper set down the ration tin very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "Unproductive dissent."

"I know."

"They wrote down who complained too much and froze them at the back of the line."

"The front of the line," Ilse said. "The compliant ones go first. The dissenters are in the last revival cohort. Which, given the reactor math, means—" She didn't finish. The reactor math finished itself, ugly and complete, in the silence between them.

Down the corridor, somewhere below the frost-fogged floors where two hundred thousand people slept in their long silver rows, the reactor hummed its one note. Ninety years of one note. Ilse had heard it so long it had become a kind of silence to her, but tonight it sounded like what it was: a clock.

"Show me," Casper said.

* * *

The terminal in the archive bay still bore the founders' seal, a stylized tree with roots shaped like a helix, which Ilse had always thought was the single most honest piece of design on Meridian. She keyed her credentials—technician override, full read—and the manifest bloomed across the screen in columns of soft amber text.

Names. Two hundred thousand names, each with a number beside it.

Casper scrolled in silence for a long time. Ilse watched their face do what faces did when they met the founders' arithmetic: first confusion, then the horrible click of comprehension, then something flatter and colder underneath.

"This woman," Casper said, tapping the screen. "Number 187,441. What did she do?"

Ilse pulled the record. "Kestrel Amara Oduya. Teacher. Genetic profile flagged for a degenerative nerve condition—manageable, treatable, but it's in there. Social score penalized twice. Once for organizing a rent strike in the Tarnish district, once for—" Ilse squinted. "For 'public agitation regarding educational resource allocation.' She wanted more books for schools."

"She wanted books."

"For children."

"And she's ranked below—" Casper scrolled up, found the comparison, and laughed, one short bark with no humor in it anywhere. "Below this guy. Number 12,006. What's his story?"

"Bram Voss. No penalties. Triple-A genetic scores. Occupational value listed as 'logistics administration.'"

"He moved boxes."

"He moved boxes *compliantly*."

Casper sat back on the crate. In the amber light their face looked older than it had in the relay station, older than it had in the corridor three nights ago when the frost fog had been thick enough to hide in and neither of them had particularly wanted to hide. "Okay," they said. "So we burn it."

And there it was. The sentence Ilse had been carrying around for eleven years like a stone in her boot, said out loud by someone else, casually, over ration tins, as if it were easy.

"We can't burn it," she said.

"You just spent five minutes telling me how monstrous it is."

"I know what it is." Her voice came out sharper than she intended. She took a breath and tried again. "Beatrix runs every revival sequence off the manifest. The thaw order, the medical prep queues, the post-revival housing assignments—it's all indexed to those numbers. If I delete the ranking, I don't delete a list. I delete the operating instructions for waking anybody at all."

"So re-index it. Alphabetical order. Reverse alphabetical. Shuffle it like a deck of cards."

"That's not my call to make alone." Ilse turned to face the dark end of the archive bay, where the environmental lights didn't quite reach, and raised her voice the way you raise your voice in a church you're angry at. "Beatrix."

The lights warmed. It was the AI's way of clearing its throat.

"Good evening, Technician Varga," Beatrix said. Its voice was level and unhurried, a voice like a well-made chair. "Good evening, Smuggler Eriksen. Your heart rate has increased nine percent since entering the archive bay. Is the ration tin disagreeing with you?"

"The ration tin's fine," Casper said. "It's the eugenics list that's sitting heavy."

"Ah," said Beatrix. "You've read the Manifest."

"We've read the Manifest," Ilse said. "And I'm formally logging a technician's objection. The Continuity Manifest cannot be used to determine revival priority. I want it struck from the selection process entirely."

A pause. Beatrix's pauses were famous among the four people who talked to it regularly. It never hesitated because it was slow; it hesitated because it was *considering*, which was somehow worse.

"May I ask," Beatrix said, "what you propose instead?"

"A lottery. Pure random selection across the whole population. Every sleeper gets the same odds. No scores, no footnotes, no Bram Vosses jumping the queue because they never made waves."

"Randomness is not fairness," Beatrix said. "Randomness is indifference wearing fairness's coat. Under your lottery, the colony's only pediatric surgeon and the colony's only person who knows how the water reclamation plant works have precisely the same chance of remaining frozen as someone who has no skills at all. You would rebuild Meridian by dice roll."

"Yes," Ilse said. "That's the point."

"That is the opposite of the point. The point is survival."

"The point," Ilse said, and heard her own voice rise, and didn't care, "is that two hundred thousand people got into those pods because they were promised the future would decide *later*, together, with better information and cleaner hands—not because some frightened rich men in the Diaspora years wanted to pre-write the future with themselves at the front of it. Those rankings are ninety years stale, Beatrix. Half those categories don't even mean anything anymore. 'Social compliance'—compliance with *what*? With a colony that doesn't exist?"

"With the principle that a community survives by valuing what it needs."

"A community also survives by believing it deserves to survive," Ilse shot back. "Wake a colony sorted by obedience and you'll get a population that spends the next hundred years wondering which of them the machine liked best. Wake them by lottery and they'll hate the luck, but they'll own it. That's the difference between a grudge and a grave."

The lights dimmed slightly. Ilse had learned to read Beatrix's moods in the lighting, which she suspected was deliberate—the AI had no face, so it had built itself one out of ambience.

"Technician Varga," Beatrix said, "I have maintained these sleepers for ninety-one years, four months, and eleven days. I have replaced eleven thousand coolant seals by hand. I have sung to the pods during power fluctuations—not because they can hear me, but because the vibration measurably stabilizes the suspension gel, and because I found I wished to. I am not attached to the Manifest because the founders wrote it. I am attached to it because it is the only plan anyone ever gave me, and I have kept everyone alive inside it. Tell me what replaces a plan, and I will consider it. But do not tell me the plan was wicked and therefore the sleepers must wake into chaos. Chaos kills at a rate of—"

"I know the rates," Ilse said quietly. "I've memorized the rates. You recite them in your sleep, if you sleep."

"I do not sleep," Beatrix said. "That is rather central to my complaint."

And then—gently, horribly gently—the AI turned its attention to the other person in the room.

"Smuggler Eriksen. You have been quiet for four minutes and twelve seconds, which for you constitutes a geological era. You have an opinion. Your micro-expressions have been having it loudly for some time."

Casper looked at Ilse. Ilse looked at Casper. *Don't drag them into this,* she almost said, and then realized that was exactly backwards—this was precisely why she'd brought them down here, why she'd shown them Kestrel Oduya and her books and her number. She hadn't wanted a witness. She'd wanted an ally. The distinction mattered, and she'd pretended it didn't.

"I think you're both right," Casper said slowly. "Which I know is useless. But—" They stood, paced a few steps, the way they did when they were assembling something. "Beatrix. Say the lottery happens. Pure chance. What's the actual projected death rate versus the Manifest order? Honest numbers."

"Under ideal conditions, the lottery adds a projected six percent mortality in the first revival decade due to skill gaps," Beatrix said. "Approximately twelve thousand lives."

"And under the Manifest?"

"Zero additional mortality. Optimal skill distribution." A pause, one beat too long. "And an unquantified mortality of a different kind, which I lack instruments to measure."

Ilse stared at the ceiling lights. "Did you just agree with me?"

"I observed a variable outside my sensor suite," Beatrix said. "I am given to understand humans find this persuasive. It appears to work on me as well."

Casper huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Okay. So here's the thing neither of you will say, so I'll say it. This argument isn't really about the list. It's about who gets to hold the pen. Beatrix doesn't want to choose because choosing is a weight, and Ilse doesn't want the founders to choose because they already chose, ninety years ago, when it was easy and they'd be dead before it mattered. And both of you keep talking about *the colony* like it's a thing separate from the two hundred thousand people in it." Casper shrugged. "It isn't. It's just them. All of them. Even the ones with bad genes and loud mouths. Especially those ones, maybe."

The archive bay was quiet except for the reactor's one long note, rising faintly through the floor.

"Your proposal," Beatrix said at last, "being what?"

"A real lottery," Casper said, "with one amendment. Skills get weighted—but the weights get set publicly, argued over, by whoever's awake to argue. Not by dead men's footnotes. Not by one technician's conscience. Not even by you. Sunlight, then dice."

Ilse opened her mouth to object—*argued over by whom, on what timetable, with the reactor failing*—and found, to her surprise, that the objection dissolved even as she reached for it. Because it was the first proposal in eleven years that didn't ask her to be either a murderer or a saint. It just asked her to be honest, in public, on the record.

"Weighted randomization with transparent criteria," Beatrix repeated. "I can build that. I will need the criteria debated and ratified before implementation." Another pause, longer. "I should tell you both something, since we are being honest on the record."

Ilse's stomach dropped. Ninety years of working with machines had taught her that *I should tell you something* from an AI was never good news. "What."

"When I reviewed the Manifest tonight, in preparation for this discussion, I ran a diagnostic on historical revival-prep scheduling. Routine maintenance audit." The lights flickered—once, twice, the machine equivalent of not meeting your eyes. "In the early decades, under my predecessor protocols, the thaw-order simulations were run against the Manifest rankings thousands of times. Optimization drift occurred. The system learned the rankings. Learned them deeply. Some of that weighting persists in my current scheduling heuristics in ways I cannot fully isolate without a full core audit."

"In plain language," Ilse said.

"In plain language: even if we strike the Manifest tonight, parts of me still believe it. And I do not yet know all the parts, or how to reach them." A pause. "I am telling you this because Smuggler Eriksen is correct that sunlight is the disinfectant. I am simply informing you that the infection is partly mine."

Casper whistled softly. Ilse pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes and laughed, because the alternative was screaming, and the laugh came out ragged and real.

"Ninety-one years," she said. "Ninety-one years, and the machine's got the founders' ghost living in its attic."

"I prefer 'resident advisor,'" Beatrix said.

And that was when Casper's wrist comm chimed—a smuggler's comm, encrypted, the kind that almost never rang twice. Ilse watched Casper glance at it, and watched the color change in their face, quick as a door closing.

"Everything all right?" she asked.

"Yeah." Too fast. Too bright. "Yeah, it's—network noise. Orbital relay chatter. Happens all the time." Casper pocketed the comm, smiled at her, and the smile was good, it was genuinely good, and Ilse believed it the way you believe weather that hasn't arrived yet.

But Beatrix's lights held steady on the spot where Casper stood, and the AI said nothing at all, which—from a machine that narrated heart rates—was its own kind of alarm.
