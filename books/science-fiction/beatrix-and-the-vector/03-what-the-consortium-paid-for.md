## 3. What the Consortium Paid For

The archive room of Meridian Colony occupied the basement of the administrative hab, a place the colonists called the Cold Office because the climate system had never been calibrated for human comfort — only for paper, and for the older storage media that preferred to be chilled like corpses. Quentin Whitlock had spent more nights down here than she cared to admit, hunched over terminals whose screens flickered in colors no one had chosen deliberately: amber, then a tired green, then amber again, as if the room itself could not decide what era it belonged to.

She came down at 0400, after filing her report on Tomas Reyes and the harvester that should never have moved. Her eyes were still burning from the screen upstairs, from typing *operator fatigue, maintenance deferral, sensor failure — all preventable* while his widow sat in the corridor outside with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a bus that would never come.

Four thousand and eleven reports. She knew the number the way other people knew their own birthdays.

Tonight she wasn't here for the dead. Tonight she was here for the nine seconds.

* * *

The burst transmission sat in her personal queue like a stone in a shoe. She had copied it three times, onto three different drives, and hidden them in places she would not write down even in her own notebook. The message was short. It was in her handwriting — not her typed hand, her actual hand, the slightly cramped script she used when signing reports, complete with the way her pen always stuttered on the W of her surname. And it was dated forty years from now.

*Quentin — stop counting alone. The vault is under you. Coordinates follow. Come before the clock runs out.*

Below it, coordinates. Below those, a string of characters that meant nothing to her yet but which her terminal insisted was a valid authentication signature, registered to an account created before Meridian Colony existed.

That was the sentence she kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. *An account created before Meridian Colony existed.* Because accounts required people, and people required somewhere to stand, and Meridian had been standing for sixty-one years. Whatever had opened that account had done so before there was ground beneath it.

So she started where every investigator starts: with the paperwork. If the colony's archives were honest, they would tell her who had paid for the ground. If they were dishonest, they would tell her something better.

* * *

The consortium's founding documents were stored on optical crystal, a medium chosen for its longevity and its hostility to casual reading. The reader was a slab of frosted glass the size of a coffin lid, and it took twenty minutes to warm up, humming a note so low Quentin felt it in her molars rather than heard it. Around her the Cold Office breathed its cold air, and somewhere above, through four decks of concrete and regolith shielding, the colony slept the shallow sleep of a place that had stopped believing tomorrow would be different.

Meridian had been founded, according to the charter, by the Consolidated Risk & Reclamation Consortium as an "agricultural demonstration settlement with actuarial oversight." That phrase — *actuarial oversight* — was how Quentin's job had been born. She was not a doctor, not an engineer, not a mayor. She was an insurance instrument with legs. The consortium had insured the colony against catastrophic loss, and someone, somewhere in a tower orbiting a warmer world, had decided that catastrophic loss was best prevented by one person whose entire function was to notice things.

She had always found it strange that the contract paid her so well and equipped her so poorly. Her office chair had a broken caster. Her lab equipment predated her birth. But her salary line had never once wavered in six years, through droughts and dock failures and the slow attrition of a population that had fallen from nine thousand to barely two thousand souls. Someone far away wanted her here, watching, and did not care whether she had a functioning centrifuge.

Now she pulled the charter's annexes, one by one, and read them the way she read accident scenes: slowly, looking for what was missing.

Annex One: land rights. Standard. Annex Two: water allocation, with schedules so optimistic they read like poetry. Annex Three: personnel. And there — Annex Three, subsection C, the staffing schedule — she found the first wrong thing.

The document listed the original caretaker staff. Forty-one names. Each name had a hire date, a salary code, and a memory-clearance notation that Quentin read three times before believing it: *MC-4, standard narrative package, arrival-sequence verified.*

Memory clearance. Narrative package. Arrival-sequence verified.

It was bureaucratic language, and bureaucracy was very good at saying terrible things in a boring voice. But there was no honest reading of *arrival-sequence verified* except this: someone had checked that the staff remembered arriving. Which meant someone had made sure they arrived — or made sure they believed they had.

Her own name was not on the list of forty-one. Of course it wasn't; she'd come six years ago, transferred from the Kestrel relay station, she remembered the shuttle descent, the dust like burnt cinnamon, Okafor meeting her at the airlock with his enormous warm handshake and his speech about how Meridian took care of its own —

She searched her own name anyway. It was habit. You searched for the missing person because sometimes the missing person was hiding in a field you hadn't thought to burn.

The terminal returned one result.

WHITLOCK, Q. — Employment Contract, Primary Instrument. Filed: Year Minus Four.

Year minus four. Four years *before* the founding date of Meridian Colony. Before the first hab slab was poured. Before the water reclamation towers stood. Before any of it.

Quentin sat very still in the cold and listened to the archive hum its subterranean note, and felt the floor of her life tilt by a degree so small that anyone else would have missed it, and she missed nothing, noticing was the whole of her, and so she felt it, the tilt, the hairline crack running through the word *six years ago.*

* * *

She pulled the contract itself. The crystal reader rendered it slowly, character by character, like a photograph developing in acid.

It was her signature. Not similar — identical. The same stutter on the W. The same slight downward drift at the end of each line, as if her hand grew tired of writing itself. The date fields were filled in a neat block print she recognized as her own because she still used it for evidence tags. The terms were generous. Absurdly generous. Hazard pay, longevity bonuses, a clause guaranteeing "continuity of person" — whatever that meant — and a termination provision that stated, in language so dry it was almost funny, that the employee's services could not be concluded while any investigation remained open.

Someone had written a contract designed to keep her investigating forever.

And at the bottom, in the guarantor field, instead of the consortium's seal, there was a string of characters. The same string from the burst transmission. The same authentication signature, dated forty years in her future, attached to a contract signed four years before her colony existed.

She copied everything. Then she sat back in the broken-castered chair and did the thing she did at accident scenes when the numbers refused to make sense: she said them out loud, quietly, to the empty room, because the ear catches lies the eye forgives.

"Signed year minus four," she said. "Colony founded year zero. I arrived year fifty-five." A pause. "I remember arriving."

The room said nothing. The room was very good at that.

There were only so many explanations, and she enumerated them the way she'd enumerate causes of death. One: clerical error, dates transposed, some clerk a century ago fat-fingered a calendar conversion. Possible. But the signature wasn't transposed; the signature was *hers*, in every particular, including the pen-stutter she'd developed at age nineteen on a world she supposedly hadn't seen until thirty-five. Two: forgery. But why forge a contract for a woman who wouldn't exist for sixty years, and forge it *well*, with clauses that fit her life like a glove fits a hand? Three —

Three was the door she didn't want to open, standing at the end of the corridor of the other two, patient as all locked doors are. Three was the possibility that the memory was the error. That the paperwork was true and the past was not. That she had not arrived six years ago at all, and everything she carried behind her eyes — the shuttle, the cinnamon dust, Okafor's handshake — was a furnished room, dressed by someone else, rented to her furnished.

You are what you remember. Everyone said so. But Quentin had spent eleven years learning what happened to people when the record disagreed with their story, and the record always won. Always. Juries believed ledgers over widows. Machines believed logs over operators. The universe, indifferent and exact, believed its measurements over everyone's grief.

If the record said she was older than her memories, then either the record was lying — or she was.

* * *

She went looking for Okafor at dawn, because dawn on Meridian came late and gray, the sun climbing over the reclamation towers like a man reluctant to get out of bed, and because Okafor was always at his desk by first light, drinking tea that tasted of iron and reading reports he never acted on.

Emmanuel Okafor was sixty-eight years old and built like the concept of patience given shoulders. He had administered Meridian for longer than Quentin had been alive — or so his file said, and she had believed his file this morning the way she had believed everything this morning, back when belief was free. He greeted her now the way he greeted every day: with a nod and a second cup already poured.

"You look like you slept in the Cold Office," he said.

"I did sleep in the Cold Office."

"Mm." He pushed the tea across the desk. "That's how it starts. First you sleep down there, then you start talking to the crystals, then one day you're filing a report about the ghosts in Annex Seven." His smile was warm and wide and, for the first time in six years, Quentin watched it the way she watched a harvester that shouldn't be moving.

"Okafor. I need the original staffing records. Pre-founding. The consortium's personnel annexes, unredacted."

Something crossed his face. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a half-beat delay before the smile resumed — but Quentin had built a career on small things. Small things were how harvesters killed farmers. Small things were how colonies died.

"Unredacted records are consortium property," he said pleasantly. "Access requires a tier-three authorization."

"I have tier-two."

"Yes."

"Who has tier-three?"

"The consortium." He sipped his tea. Above his shoulder, through the reinforced window, the gray light was strengthening, catching the frost on the tower glass, and the whole colony looked briefly beautiful, the way crime scenes sometimes did at certain angles.

"Okafor." She set the printed excerpt on his desk, face up. Her own signature stared at both of them. "My employment contract is dated four years before this colony was founded. My signature is on it. Explain that to me."

He looked at it for a long time. Long enough that she counted — she was always counting — and reached forty seconds, which is a very long time for two people to sit with a piece of paper between them.

Then he did the most frightening thing available to him. He was kind.

"Quentin," he said gently, "you've had a hard week. We buried Tomas yesterday. You've been sleeping in basements. Documents get corrupted. Crystals degrade. Dates slip." He slid the page back toward her with two fingers, carefully, the way you'd return something fragile to its owner. "Six years ago you stepped off that shuttle with two bags and a bad cough, and I shook your hand, and you asked me where the nearest coffee was, because you were that kind of person — the kind who arrives and immediately wants to know how things work. I remember it clearly." He smiled. "Some things don't need paperwork to be true."

"That's exactly backwards," she said quietly. "Everything needs paperwork to be true. That's the whole point of paperwork. That's the whole point of *me*."

For a moment — one heartbeat, maybe two — Emmanuel Okafor looked at her with an expression she had never seen on him before, and it took her a moment to identify it because it did not belong on a kindly old administrator's face. It was guilt. Specific, located, load-bearing guilt, the expression of a man who has helped carry something heavy for a long time and has just heard it shift.

"Drink your tea," he said. "Go home. Sleep in a bed. Some questions cost more than they pay out. Believe me — I've priced them."

*I've priced them.* An actuary's phrase. From a man who was supposed to be a caretaker.

Quentin rose, took the excerpt back, and walked to the door. In the doorway she stopped, because stopping in doorways was a trick she'd learned — people told you things to your back that they'd never say to your face.

"Okafor," she said, without turning. "What does MC-4 mean?"

Behind her, the silence lasted exactly nine seconds. She counted. She couldn't help it. She never could.

When he finally answered, his voice was warm and bureaucratic and utterly hollow, a shell of a sound, and it told her everything the words did not:

"I have no idea what you're talking about. Have a good morning, Quentin."

She walked out into the gray light, past the frost-covered towers, past the memorial wall where Tomas Reyes's name would go up next to four thousand others, and she understood, with the cold clarity of the Cold Office still in her bones, that the next place she needed to dig was not in the archives at all.

It was in the waterways at the colony's edge — where, three days ago, a feral twelve-year-old had looked up at her from a culvert with mud to her elbows and said, unprompted, in the flat tone children use for facts:

"You're the one who counts. Good. Somebody has to count what's under us."
