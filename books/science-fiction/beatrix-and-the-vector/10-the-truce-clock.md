## 10. The Truce Clock

The ship came down through the storm layer without asking permission, the way a knife comes through an envelope.

Quentin felt it before she saw it — a pressure change in her back teeth, a shudder in the water pipes that ran through the administrative block like veins in a wrist. Meridian's atmosphere processors had been coughing for years, and every arrival, legal or otherwise, announced itself with a half-hour of turbulence and a polite hail from traffic control. This one did neither. It simply descended, a long grey wedge of military salvage plating, and settled into Landing Field Three with the delicacy of something that had never once been told no.

She was in her office on the third floor of Records when the floor trembled. Four thousand and eleven reports stacked behind her in archive crates. The Vector's coordinates folded into her breast pocket like a confession she hadn't decided whether to make. She stood, and watched through the rain-streaked window as the dust of the landing bloomed outward across the eastern fields — fields that should have held winter wheat, that held instead the grey stubble of another failed season.

The ship had no markings she recognized. That was its own kind of marking.

* * *

By the time she reached the field, the ramp was already down, and six figures stood at its base in environment suits too clean for any world. They moved with the unhurried precision of people who owned the ground they stood on regardless of what the ground thought about it. Behind them, the colony's small crowd gathered at the fence line — Okafor among them, his coat buttoned wrong, Sefa perched on the barrier with her knees drawn up, watching with the flat unblinking attention she gave to anything new.

The leader stepped forward as Quentin approached. He was tall and narrow, built like a document filed vertically, in a charcoal suit cut so precisely it seemed less tailored than legislated. His face was pleasant the way a contract's preamble is pleasant: correct, symmetrical, promising nothing. He carried a slate under one arm and, clipped to his belt, a sidearm whose holster bore the fleet sigil Quentin had last seen stamped on ten thousand cryo-pods beneath her feet.

"Quentin Whitlock," he said. Not a question. "Records Actuary, Meridian Colony. Author of four thousand and eleven incident reports." A thin smile. "I've read them all."

"That must have taken you some time," Quentin said.

"It took me eleven years. I started when you did." He extended a hand she did not take, and withdrew it without embarrassment, as though the refusal were also a data point he'd anticipated. "Idris Vane. Executor-at-large, Fleet Trust Administration. I'm here regarding the asset beneath your colony."

*Asset.* The word went through her like cold water through a cracked pipe. He knew. Of course he knew. The vault had been there forty years; men like this had probably been counting the days since before Quentin herself existed — whichever existence that was.

"There are procedures for landing clearances," she said. "There are procedures for—"

"I'm aware of your procedures. I've audited them." Vane turned slightly, surveying the colony the way an assessor surveys flood damage. "Meridian Colony, population declining at four percent annually. Infrastructure rated terminal. Insurance valuation: negligible." His gaze came back to her. "You've documented all of this beautifully, Ms. Whitlock. Grief is very thorough when someone pays for the ink. But the colony was never the point, was it? The colony is a lid."

Behind him, the five suited figures fanned out with quiet competence. One began walking the perimeter of the field. Another knelt at a maintenance hatch and produced an instrument that hummed against the metal, reading the deep structure below — reading, Quentin understood, the vault itself, the way a doctor palpates a body through cloth.

"You can't just—" she began.

"I can." Vane said it without cruelty, which made it worse. Cruelty would have been something to push against. This was weather. "Under Fleet Emergency Statute Nine, a truce asset enters executor custody upon expiry approach. I am the expiry approach, Ms. Whitlock. I am the clock wearing a suit."

Sefa had come down off the barrier. Quentin saw her out at the edge of vision, drifting closer along the fence, small and quick and listening the way only Sefa listened — with her whole skeleton.

"How much time?" Quentin asked.

Vane consulted his slate, though Quentin was certain he knew the figure to the second. "Eighty-one days. Seventy-nine by the time we finish this conversation, given how slowly you breathe." He smiled again. "I'd like to see the vault."

"No."

The word surprised her with its own firmness. Vane tilted his head, genuinely interested, the way a man is interested by a lock that resists longer than its rating suggests.

"No," he repeated. "You know, in eleven years of reports, you used that word exactly twice. Once to a supply requisition for a decommissioned harvester — which then moved anyway, and crushed a farmer named Aldous Renn. Your report on that death runs nine pages. I found the ninth page very moving." He let that sit between them. "The other time was in your employment contract. Clause fourteen. You declined arbitration. Interesting choice, for someone who remembers signing it six years ago."

The rain had thickened. Quentin became aware of the crowd at the fence, of Okafor's face gone grey, of Sefa standing now directly behind her left shoulder, close enough that Quentin could feel the child's breath.

"What do you want, Mr. Vane?"

"To do my job, which is to ensure that when the truce expires, exactly one faction wakes, and it is the faction my principals have paid a great deal of money to see wake. To do that, I need the vault's custodian AI to accept my authority. And the custodian—" his eyes narrowed fractionally, the first crack in the pleasantness, "—has been receiving visitors. Receiving *petitions*. Someone has been talking to it. Someone has been making it feel things it wasn't built to feel. Anomalies complicate execution, Ms. Whitlock. I remove anomalies."

He raised his voice slightly, not to shout but to carry, addressing the fence line, the whole wet grey colony. "Effective immediately, under Fleet Statute Nine, Meridian Colony's communications array is sealed pending audit. No transmissions in or out. Noncompliance will be logged."

And Quentin felt it happen — felt the colony's throat close. Somewhere up on the ridge, the old transmitter array that had been chattering weakly to passing freighters for decades went silent mid-syllable. She could almost hear the absence. Eighty-one days, sealed inside a lid, over a vault full of sleepers, with a man who called human beings assets and carried a gun with a sigil on it.

Okafor pushed through the gate. "Those arrays predate your statute. They're colonial infrastructure, not—"

"They're whatever I say they are, Supervisor." Vane didn't even look at him. "Your charter was written by the consortium. The consortium holds fleet debt. Therefore your charter is a subsidiary clause of mine. You may verify this at your leisure, in writing, which I understand is the only language spoken here." He turned back to Quentin. "You'll want to check my credentials. Everyone does. Here."

He handed her a slate. On it: commission papers, seals, a chain of authorization ascending through names and institutions until it vanished into the fleet's capital like a root into bedrock. Every signature real. Every stamp genuine. That was the horror of it — nothing about him was forged. The machine that had written off Meridian as a rounding error had simply, finally, sent someone to collect the error's contents.

"The vault," Vane said again. "Tomorrow, oh-six-hundred. Bring your access codes. Or don't, and I'll bring mine, and we'll discover together whose paperwork the custodian prefers." He paused at the base of the ramp, and for a moment his voice dropped its procedural polish and became almost gentle, which was the most frightening thing yet. "Ms. Whitlock. I've read everything ever written about you. Everything anyone wrote. Do you understand? There is a file on my ship with your name on it, and it is thicker than the files of most dead people, and I intend to reconcile it line by line against the woman standing in front of me. Anomalies get audited. That's not a threat. It's a schedule."

He ascended the ramp. The ship sealed itself with a sound like a held breath.

* * *

That night the colony gathered in the mess hall, and the silence there was worse than the rain.

Quentin stood at the window of her office afterward, watching the salvage ship's running lights burn steady on the field — red, white, red — like a pulse that belonged to someone else. Her office door opened. Sefa slipped in without knocking, carrying her river maps rolled under one arm, and sat on the floor against Quentin's desk, the way she did when the dormitories got too loud.

"He's going to open it," Sefa said.

"Yes."

"And wake the ones who left."

"That's what he's paid for."

Sefa was quiet for a while. Outside, the lights pulsed. Red, white, red.

"He counts wrong," she said finally. "He thinks the sleepers are asleep. But they're not *gone*, Quentin. They're all still saying goodbye. If you listen." She looked up, and her eyes in the dark office were very steady. "Eighty-one days isn't a lot of time to hear ten thousand goodbyes."

Quentin put her hand on the child's head, on the rough cropped hair, and felt the countdown begin in her own chest — not Vane's clock, but something older, ticking beneath the colony, beneath the vault, beneath the fabricated floor of her own remembered life.

Red, white, red.

In the morning, at oh-six-hundred, she would bring her access codes. And she would find out what was written in the file with her name on it — and what, if anything, was true.

The transmitter array stood dark on the ridge, and the whole colony seemed to lean toward it, straining after a voice it could no longer raise.
