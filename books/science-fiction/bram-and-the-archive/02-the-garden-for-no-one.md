## 2. The Garden for No One

The morning after the world fell over, I did what I always do: I made tea and walked the rows.

It's a ritual older than my tenure here. The gardeners who came before me — the ones whose names are still stenciled on the seed cabinets, Halvorsen and Okonkwo and a third whose label has faded to just *—elle* — left behind maintenance logs full of small ceremonies. Check the moisture wicks at dawn. Sing to the tomatoes if you have nothing better to say. Log everything, because someone is coming, and they will want to know what happened while they were in transit.

Someone was coming. That had been the whole architecture of my life. Forty-one years of it, if you counted from the day I arrived as an apprentice with two duffel bags and a head full of terraforming doctrine. The colonists were delayed. The colonists would arrive when the Charter finished settling the inner stations. The colonists were *coming*, and the garden would be ready, and I would hand them a living world with both hands and step back into the margins of history.

I had said that sentence so many times, in so many variations, that it had worn smooth as river stone. I no longer noticed its shape.

The tea helped. It always helps. There's something about holding a warm cup while surveying catastrophe that keeps the catastrophe at arm's length, categorizable, survivable. I stood on the observation deck of the caretaker's station with the steam fogging the viewport and looked down at my garden, and I took inventory of what nine seconds of lawless gravity had done.

The trellises along Row Twelve had collapsed like tired dancers. The irrigation lines had jumped their couplings in three places, and the water they'd lost overnight had pooled into a marsh where the yams were now, presumably, delighted. The glass of the long greenhouse had held — bless whoever had spec'd it for meteor strikes — but the old windbreak pines on the north slope had not. Two of them lay on their sides, roots torn free of soil that had briefly forgotten how to hold them, pointing downhill like fallen soldiers indicating the direction of the enemy.

Nine seconds. Everything I'd built in four decades, tipped sideways by nine seconds of the universe clearing its throat.

And yet.

Here is the thing I could not stop noticing, the thing that kept pulling my eye away from the damage like a sore tooth pulls the tongue: the damage was not random.

I've spent forty-one years learning how plants fail. Windthrow has a signature. Flood has a signature. A gravity event — well, I'd never seen one, but I understood the physics well enough. Things fall down. Things fall in the direction of whatever the gravity was doing while it was misbehaving. You get chaos with a vector.

What I got instead was pattern.

Row Twelve hadn't collapsed outward or inward. It had collapsed in a spiral. Not a spiral like something twisting as it fell — a spiral like something *drawn*. The trellis poles lay in a curve so clean I initially assumed a cable had caught them and dragged them around, but there was no cable, no anchor point, nothing to pull from. They had simply fallen into an arc, each pole tangent to the next, describing a shape maybe thirty meters across, centered on —

Well. Centered on nothing, which was itself interesting, because "nothing" in my garden has a very specific address. The center of that spiral sat exactly under the beacon.

* * *

The beacon stands on the hill above the terraces, a needle of Charter-standard relay hardware bolted to a foundation poured before I was born. Its official designation is GB-77 "Brightwater," which tells you how much imagination went into colonial infrastructure, since Brightwater is also the name of the valley, the aquifer, and me. I grew up thinking of it less as equipment and more as a lighthouse: a thing that says *here*, a thing that says *someone is home*.

Except beacons transmit. Everyone knows that. And last night, during the nine seconds, every instrument in my station had screamed at once, and when the screaming stopped, the beacon's logs showed something that had kept me awake until the local dawn: a receive window. An inbound signal, locked and held, still ongoing.

Beacons don't receive. Or rather, this one wasn't supposed to, and yet here it was, drinking from some spigot in the sky, and the spiral of fallen trellises pointed at it like a compass rose drawn by drunk cartographers.

I drank my tea, told myself firmly that correlation is not causation, and went down to assess the yam marsh.

That was when I found the second pattern.

The yams hadn't drowned. Yams are patient; they'll wait out a flood with the serenity of creatures that store their own futures underground. But the new shoots — the ones that had broken soil only days before the flicker — had all bent. Every single one, across the entire flooded terrace, leaned in the same direction. Toward the beacon. Not toward the sun, mind you. The sun rises east over the ridge, and phototropism is not subtle; a plant leaning away from its light source is a plant making a statement.

These shoots leaned northwest. Uphill. Into worse light, thinner soil, against every instinct written into their cells — toward the beacon.

I knelt in the mud and measured the lean because measuring things is how I keep from panicking. Fourteen degrees off vertical, give or take, uniform across three hundred shoots. Then I walked the other rows. Beans: leaning, fourteen degrees. The young apple stock in the nursery beds: leaning, fourteen degrees. Even the algae tanks in the nutrient shed, which have no business tropism-ing toward anything, had developed a visible gradient, denser on the wall nearest the hill.

Everything in my garden was turning, slowly, patiently, toward the beacon, like a congregation finding its compass during a sermon I couldn't hear.

I want to tell you I responded to this with scientific detachment. What I actually did was stand in the middle of Row Nine with mud to my ankles and say, out loud, to no one, "What are you doing?"

The garden didn't answer. But the beacon's indicator light, visible above the tree line, pulsed once — slow, deliberate, nothing like its usual steady carrier blink — and I felt the bottom drop out of my stomach in a way that had nothing to do with gravity.

Because here's the thought that arrived, uninvited, wearing no clothes of comfort whatsoever: plants respond to signals. Hormones, light gradients, chemical whispers from root to root through fungal networks. Plants are not deaf. And if something was broadcasting on a channel that living things could hear — some modulation in the EM field, some vibration in the substrate, something underneath the physics I knew — then my garden wasn't damaged.

My garden was *listening*.

And it had been listening, apparently, to something that predates the question I didn't know how to ask yet.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what caretakers do: triage. I righted what could be righted, drained what needed draining, logged everything in the maintenance record with hands that only shook a little. I noted the spiral. I noted the lean. I wrote *cause unknown — recommend full spectrum survey* and then crossed out *recommend* and wrote *must*, because I am the only recommender left and I have decided to take myself seriously.

By late afternoon the light came slantwise through the pines and turned the floodwater to hammered copper, and I allowed myself the day's first honest emotion, which was grief. Small grief, controlled grief, the kind you can carry in a pocket. Trellises can be rebuilt. Pines can be replanted. But the flicker had cracked the cistern, and the cistern was original Halvorsen-era work, and there is no more Halvorsen-era anything. Every loss here is a loss of the last copy.

I was kneeling at the cistern, sealing the crack with resin and swearing at it with affection, when I heard the ship.

You know a ship's sound before you see it, if you've lived alone long enough. The garden makes a hundred noises — wind, water, birds we introduced and immediately regretted, the groan of thermal expansion — and a ship cuts through all of them like a wrong note. This one was a heavy descent burn, unpolished, the engine equivalent of someone clearing their throat too loudly in a library.

I came up the hill fast, resin still on my hands, and crested the ridge in time to watch a scavenger hauler drop through my sky.

She was ugly in the way that only ships patched by desperate people can be ugly: hull plates mismatched like a quilt, a drive cone that had clearly been replaced with whatever was available, and a name painted on the flank in letters that had been partially sanded off and repainted twice — I could make out *LONG ODDS* and before that, possibly, something Charter-regulation that someone had wanted very much to forget. She came down without any request for landing clearance, which was unsurprising, since there is no one to clear her. She flared hard over my north terraces — flattening another row of beans, thank you very much — and settled onto the flat ground below the beacon hill with the grace of a dropped suitcase.

Dust rolled up the slope. My dust. Dust I had spent decades keeping where it belonged.

I want to describe my emotional state accurately, because later events will make it seem quaint. I was furious. Not afraid — furious. This valley is not abandoned property. It is a garden planted for people who haven't arrived yet, tended by a woman who has given it everything, including, apparently, her sense of proportion. Strangers descending on it uninvited felt less like trespass and more like grave robbery, except the grave belongs to the future, which is somehow worse.

I did not, I should note, have a weapon. Caretakers don't. I had a resin gun, a soil auger, and a pruning knife that has seen better centuries. I picked up the pruning knife anyway, mostly for the feeling of holding something, and started down the hill at a pace I intended to look authoritative.

The ship's ramp dropped before I reached the flats.

Three figures descended. The first was tall and moved like he'd been built for smaller spaces — a pilot's rolling gait, jacket open over a flight suit whose Charter insignia had been burned off but not thoroughly enough to fool anyone. He saw me coming and raised both hands in a gesture that managed to be simultaneously conciliatory and deeply insincere.

"Caretaker," he called. "Don't shoot."

"With what?" I called back. "I'm armed with horticulture."

He grinned. Behind him, a broad-shouldered woman in engineer's coveralls scanned the horizon with the wariness of someone who expected the sky to owe her money, and a slight figure in a hooded coat stood perfectly still at the ramp's edge, face shadowed, watching not me and not the ship but the *beacon*, with an attention that prickled the hair on my arms.

"We're not here to cause trouble," the pilot said, closing the distance with his hands still up. "We're running from it, if that helps. Ship needs coolant and about six hours of nobody looking at us. We saw your beacon on the long scan and figured dead relay, dead valley, easy stop."

"It's not a dead relay," I said, sharper than I meant to.

"No," he agreed, and his grin faded into something more careful. "We noticed. That's sort of why we're standing here instead of orbiting. Your beacon's been singing for about twenty hours now, caretaker. On a band that doesn't officially exist. Our navigator heard it before our instruments did." He glanced back at the hooded figure. "Wren doesn't hear things twice."

I tightened my grip on the pruning knife and hated how it must look, this gray-haired woman defending a vegetable garden with secateurs. "This valley is seeded ground," I said. "There are contracts. There is a colony manifest. Whatever you think you can scavenge here, the answer is no. These plants are spoken for."

Something shifted in the pilot's face — not mockery, though I braced for it. Something almost gentle, the way people look at you when they know a thing you don't and are deciding whether today is the day you find out.

"Spoken for," he repeated softly. "Right." He stuck out a hand. "Ovid. That's Sefa, she fixes what I break. The quiet one is Wren, and Wren wants to see your beacon up close, and honestly, caretaker, after what it answered last night, so do I."

"Answered?" The word came out of me before I could decide whether to share vocabulary with strangers. "Answered *what*?"

Ovid looked at me for a long moment. Down the slope, the hooded figure — Wren — had begun, slowly, to walk uphill toward the beacon, uninvited, unhurried, as if drawn on a string.

"That," Ovid said, "is a longer conversation than six hours of coolant. But I'll give you the short version free, since you're pointing a knife at us." He nodded past me, at the garden, at the flattened rows and the copper floodwater and the fourteen-degree lean of every living thing in the valley. "Last night, for nine seconds, every clock in this system stuttered. Every ship, every station, every settlement. Gravity hiccuped, and when it came back—" he tapped his temple, "—something came back with it. And your little lighthouse here? It's not transmitting anymore, caretaker."

He smiled, and there was no humor in it at all.

"It's receiving. And whatever it's tuned to, it's been waiting a very long time for someone to pick up."

Behind him, on the hill, Wren reached the beacon's base and laid one bare hand against the metal, and every plant in my garden — every bean, every shoot, every algae cell in its tank — leaned a fraction of a degree further toward the sound.
