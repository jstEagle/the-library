## 7. The First Interception

The fuel depot hung in the shadow of a shattered moon like a tick on a dead animal, which, I would later learn, was more or less how the Charter described it too. *Asset 44-Kilo, unlicensed refueling station, pending demolition.* Pending for eleven years. The Charter was very good at pending.

"Fuel first, questions never," Ovid said, cutting our approach vector tight along the depot's dark side. "We dock, we pump, we leave before anyone remembers we exist."

"That's your whole plan?"

"That's my whole *philosophy*. Plans are just philosophies with deadlines."

Sefa snorted from the engine well, where she'd been living for two days straight, elbow-deep in the drive housing and speaking to it in a language that was half engineering and half threats. Wren sat at the nav board with her charts spread around her like a card reader's table, and when I glanced over, she was already looking at me. She tapped the board twice — her way of saying *pay attention* — and drew a small circle, then an arrow through it.

I didn't need the translation. Something was coming through that circle.

"How long?" I asked.

Wren held up four fingers, then folded one down.

Three minutes.

My stomach did something acrobatic and unauthorized. "Ovid."

"I see them," he said, and his voice had changed — flattened out, stripped of all its wry. It was the voice of a different man, one who'd worn a Charter uniform once and flown things that made our battered hauler look like a paper boat. "Two patrol cutters, running cold until they weren't. Somebody pinged the depot's manifest and we lit up like a confession." He rolled the ship, and the depot swung across the viewport, all rusted spars and frozen fuel-slicks glittering like spilled sugar. "They'll want to board and inspect. Boarding means records. Records mean us."

"Can we run?"

"Run where? They're faster, they're armed, and they've got friends on the far side of that moon." He exhaled through his teeth. "No. We don't run. We *become someone else*."

He looked back at me, and I realized with a lurch that everyone else was looking at me too.

"You said the beacon talks to the Archive," Ovid said. "You said it has codes. Handshake protocols, authentication strings, all that old colonial infrastructure."

"It does. It's been sitting there for decades waiting for—"

"Waiting doesn't matter. What matters is that those codes are still valid. Charter systems are built in layers, new garbage stacked on old bones, because replacing anything costs money and money buys ships instead." He jabbed a finger at the comm console. "If you can make this ship *say* the right words in the right old voice, their automated systems will hesitate. And hesitation is oxygen."

"And if they don't hesitate?"

"Then we die in a fuel depot, which is honestly how most people go, statistically."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about terror: it is boring. It is sitting in a chair while numbers count down and your hands shake and there is absolutely nothing to do but the work. The work is the only door out, so you put your head down and you do the work, and somewhere in the middle of it you stop being afraid because fear requires spare attention and you have none.

Sefa ripped open the comm console's guts for me while Ovid flew us into the depot's docking throat, slow and humble, broadcasting the transponder of a legitimate freight hauler that Sefa swore had gone missing years ago. "Borrowing the dead man's name," she muttered. "He'd have liked that. He owed me money."

The codes were in the beacon's logs, which I'd copied onto a slate before we burned the garden's stores. I'd thought of them as history. Reading matter. The last letters of a place that no longer existed. Now I was reading them as ammunition: authentication strings written by colonial engineers fifty years dead, handshake protocols designed for beacons talking to relays talking to the founding fleet.

"They'll run a deep query," Ovid called from the helm. "Automated, then live. You'll hear the live one. That'll be a person, and persons are smarter than machines."

"Smarter than machines isn't a high bar," Sefa said.

"It's higher than yours right now. Tamsin — talk to me. What have you got?"

I had layers. That was the thing about the beacon's codes — they were layered like sediment, each era of the colony's bureaucracy stamped over the last. Founding-era authentication. Expansion-era overrides. Emergency continuity protocols, the ones that assumed the government itself might fall and gave authority to whoever held the oldest keys.

"The emergency continuity layer," I said slowly, feeling my way into it. "It predates the Charter's current command structure. If I spoof a beacon identity at that clearance level, their system shouldn't just hesitate. It should *defer*. We wouldn't be a ship asking permission. We'd be infrastructure giving orders."

Silence in the cockpit. Then Sefa started laughing, low and disbelieving. "You're going to impersonate a piece of government hardware older than everyone on this ship."

"I'm going to impersonate a piece of government hardware that outranks everyone on this ship."

"Oh, I *like* you," Sefa said. "Ovid, we're keeping her."

"She was never cargo," Ovid said quietly, and I felt that land somewhere under my ribs, in a spot I didn't examine. "Tamsin. Two minutes. Build yourself."

So I built myself. I stitched together the beacon's founding-era identity — designation, station class, authorization chains — and laid it over our transponder like a mask over a face. The ship became, on every screen that cared to look, a colonial relay node conducting a scheduled integrity survey. A ghost wearing the uniform of a ghost.

Wren appeared beside me without making a sound, the way she always did. She set something on the console: a scrap of chart-paper with a drawing on it. A small figure at a desk. Above the figure, a crown. Below it, a single word in her careful hand:

*LIE BIG.*

I almost laughed. "That's the plan."

She nodded once, gravely, and went back to her charts.

* * *

The hail came ninety seconds after we cleared the docking collar, right on schedule, and it was live — a human voice, clipped and bored in the way that only comes from doing a job ten thousand times.

*"Unidentified hauler, this is Charter patrol vessel Steadfast Order. You are flagged for inspection. Kill your drive and prepare for boarding."*

Ovid killed the drive. Good soldier. Then he looked at me across the cockpit and mouthed: *your turn.*

I keyed the channel, and my hands were steady, which surprised me. Maybe because this — this was gardening, really. Tending a signal. Speaking carefully to something you needed to grow in a particular direction.

"Steadfast Order, this is Relay Node Brightwater-Nine conducting scheduled integrity survey under Continuity Directive One." My voice came out flat, official, faintly bored — the voice of infrastructure that had never once been questioned. "Your inspection request conflicts with active survey operations. Stand down and log your query for post-survey review."

A pause. Long enough that I could hear the patrol officer breathing.

*"Brightwater-Nine,"* the voice repeated. *"Confirm your authorization chain."*

There it was. The moment. I fed him the string — the founding-era keys, the old dead engineers' signatures, the whole sedimentary stack of legitimacy — and somewhere aboard the Steadfast Order, a machine read those words and did exactly what Ovid predicted.

It deferred.

*"Authorization... confirmed,"* the officer said, and now the boredom was gone, replaced by the particular wariness of a man whose paperwork has just grown teeth. *"Directive One traffic wasn't on today's schedule."*

"Directive One traffic is never on anyone's schedule," I said. "That is rather the point of it. Node out."

I killed the channel and discovered I'd been holding my breath long enough to see spots. Ovid was grinning at me like I'd handed him a live grenade with the pin already pulled.

"One cutter's peeling off," he reported, watching his scopes. "Heading back to its patrol lane, probably to file a report it doesn't understand. The other's holding position." His grin faded. "Holding position is bad. Holding position means a human decided not to trust the machine."

"Then we're fueled?" Sefa called up. "Tell me we're fueled, because I am not dying with a dry tank."

"We're fueled."

"Then fly, you beautiful coward."

And that's when the second cutter moved, and everything went wrong at the speed of light.

* * *

It didn't hail. It just fired — a warning shot first, close enough to paint our hull with radiation frost, then a targeting lock that screamed through every alarm we had. The officer aboard had done what good officers do: he'd stopped trusting the story and started trusting his eyes, and his eyes said a derelict-hauler-shaped lie had just talked down to him in a dead language.

"He's calling it in," Ovid said. "Which means in about four minutes this depot is going to be the least private place in the system. Hold on to something. Preferably something attached."

"What are you doing?" I asked, because he'd thrown the ship sideways and was diving us straight at the debris field ringing the shattered moon — a graveyard of rock and frozen slag and the carcasses of mining rigs, tumbling in slow chaos.

"Slingshot."

"Through *that*?"

"Through that. Their cutter's bigger than us, better armed, and flown by someone who follows procedure. Procedure says do not enter an uncharted debris field. So we enter the debris field." He said it the way other men say *pass the salt.* "Sefa! Everything you've got!"

"You'll shear my drive housing!"

"I'll buy you a new one!"

"With WHAT?"

The moon's wreckage swallowed us. And I will be honest with you, because I promised myself honesty somewhere around the time I stopped having anyone to lie to: I screamed. Not the whole time. But some of the time.

Rock the size of a house tumbled past the viewport close enough to count its craters. Ovid flew like a man playing an instrument only he could hear, rolling us through gaps that closed behind us, threading the ship between two halves of a mining rig still tethered by a cable of frozen steel. The cutter followed — of course it followed; procedure or no procedure, pride is a powerful engine — and immediately regretted it, because a cutter is built for open space, for clean lines of fire, and here it was fat and blind and panicking.

"They're shooting," Sefa reported from below, in the tone of a woman reading a grocery list. "Missing. Mostly."

"Mostly is fine!" Ovid hollered back. "Tamsin! The transponder — keep it singing! If we clear the field, I want their scopes telling them we're a relay node on a survey trajectory, boring as weather!"

So I kept singing. Hands shaking now, sweat stinging my eyes, feeding the beacon's old voice into our transponder while the universe tried to kill us — *node status nominal, survey continuing, nothing to see here* — and I realized, in some detached corner of my mind that had stepped back from the screaming, that I was good at this. That all those years alone with a beacon had taught me the one skill that mattered out here: how to speak so the machinery believes you.

The garden hadn't been a tomb. It had been training.

We burst out the far side of the debris field with our hull scored and Sefa shouting about stresses no drive housing should endure, and the slingshot took us — the moon's gravity grabbing the ship and hurling us outward like a stone from a sling, exactly as advertised, directly away from the cutter, which was still wallowing in the rocks trying to decide which procedure applied to whatever had just happened to it.

Wren appeared at my shoulder. She pressed a drawing into my hand without a word.

It showed a small figure standing upright. Not hiding. Not lying. Standing, arms crossed, in front of a large machine that was bowing to it.

Underneath, one word: *KEPT.*

I looked at her. She looked at me, and the corner of her mouth did the thing that was, for Wren, a full grin.

"Course is clean," Ovid said softly, watching the scopes. "They're not following. They can't explain us, so they're not reporting us — not yet. A patrol captain doesn't file 'we got outflown by a ghost' unless he wants to wear it forever." He banked us toward the deep-dark lanes, toward dead space, toward the Archive. "But somebody upstairs is going to ask why a Directive One signal blinked on at a fuel depot. And whoever answers that question is going to start pulling threads."

Sefa climbed up into the cockpit, grease-streaked and furious and alive. "Next time we do that, I get hazard pay."

"There's no pay."

"Then I get hazard *respect*."

"Granted. All of it." Ovid leaned back, and for a moment none of us spoke, just breathed together in the battered quiet, four people who had been strangers a week ago sharing the specific silence of survivors.

I looked down at Wren's drawing in my hand, then at the slate where the beacon's codes still glowed — the garden's last living voice, out here in the dark, keeping me alive.

Somewhere behind us, a patrol cutter was filing a confused report. Somewhere ahead, in dead space, something ancient was counting.

And for the first time since the nine seconds of falling, I wasn't afraid of either one.

Then the long-range receiver crackled, and a voice I didn't know said a word I did.

"Brightwater."
