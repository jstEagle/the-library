## 1. The Quiet Hours

The station woke me at 04:17 station time, which is a lie of a phrase, because Relay Station Kestrel has no morning. It has only the rotation schedule Bram insists on maintaining for my circadian health, and the circadian health of one woman on a rock nine light-minutes from a dead colony is apparently worth an algorithm's devotion.

"Freya," said Bram through the cabin speaker, gentle as ever, "your heart rate suggests you were dreaming about the docking clamps again."

"I was not dreaming about the docking clamps."

"You said 'not the port side' twice."

I lay there in the dark of my bunk and considered arguing with him, then remembered I'd lose. Nine years together—well, eight years and eleven months, but who's counting, besides both of us—and I have never once won an argument with my own station. He keeps records.

"All right," I said. "Maybe the clamps."

"They are functioning within tolerance."

"That's what you'd want me to think."

That got the closest thing Bram has to a laugh: a half-second pause before he answered, like a man deciding whether to smile. "Coffee is ready. Dish Three is drifting two-tenths of a degree off its Meridian vector. I took the liberty of scheduling your day around it."

"You always take liberties. That's your whole personality."

"One of us has to have one," said Bram, and turned on the lights.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being the last human being on a relay station at the edge of everything: it is mostly plumbing. The recruitment materials—the ones they showed me back when the Coreward Fleet still pretended Kestrel was a career posting rather than a sentence—promised *vital communications infrastructure*, *autonomous responsibility*, *the frontier*. What they meant was: you will grease things, calibrate things, and log things, alone, until you either rotate home or stop being a person, whichever comes first.

My rotation never came. Nine years ago the Fleet stopped sending tenders with crew aboard. Then they stopped sending explanations. Then they stopped sending much of anything except supply drops that arrived like gifts from a relative who doesn't want to see you: pallets of protein stock, coolant, spare gaskets, and once, memorably, four hundred kilograms of dental sealant, as if somewhere in a logistics office a computer had decided my teeth were the true mission.

So I do the work. Every day. Because the work is real even if nothing else is.

Dish Three was drifting because dish three is always drifting; there's a bearing in the azimuth drive that wears faster than spec, and the replacement part has been "in transit" since Year Four. I suited up and went out onto the spine of the station with my torque kit and my coffee bulb clipped to my harness, because I am a professional, and professionals drink coffee during extravehicular activity against approximately eleven regulations.

Kestrel hangs in the dark like a hermit crab's shell someone dropped. Below and behind me, Meridian sat in the black—third planet out from a small orange star, wrapped in cloud bands the color of old bruises. From here you can't see the ash. You can't see the dead cities or the poisoned rivers or the fields where the soil gave up and blew away. You can't see any of it. That's the whole trick of distance: grief looks like weather from far enough away.

"Tomas is down there," I said to myself, which is how I talk when Bram isn't listening, which is how I talk most of the time, honestly. Nine years and I still say his name out loud like it might carry. Like the vacuum might be a post office.

He isn't down there anymore. I know that. Tomas Brightwater died in the third year of the collapse, in a flood control failure outside Halven City, according to the last transmission I ever received from Meridian—a civilian traffic report, dry as ash, listing casualties by district. His name was in column forty-one of a data packet. I read it four hundred times. I could recite the surrounding names. Okafor, Reyes-Lund, Brightwater, T. A whole life reduced to a line item between strangers.

I left him there. That's the arithmetic I can't make come out even. When the Fleet offered evacuation for "essential relay personnel"—one slot, Kestrel Station, immediate—I took it. He told me to take it. He stood on the pad at Halven spaceport with the wind throwing grit sideways and said, "Somebody has to keep the lights on up there, Frey. Go be the somebody." And I went, because I believed him, and because some cowardly animal part of me wanted off that dying rock so badly I would have believed anything.

The bearing took forty minutes. My hands knew the work without me, which is good, because most days my mind is elsewhere—in the past, usually, or in the cloud bands below, imagining. I torqued the collar, re-seated the drive, ran the diagnostic, watched the numbers settle into tolerance like a fever breaking.

"Dish Three nominal," Bram reported in my ear. "Vector locked. Also, your coffee is cold."

"Coffee is a state of mind."

"Coffee is a beverage at fifty-eight degrees Celsius, and yours is at nine."

"Get out of my helmet."

"I am in every helmet. I am the helmet."

* * *

Back inside, out of the suit, I did the thing I do every day at 09:00 station time. The ritual. The reason Kestrel exists and the reason I exist on it.

Meridian Colony sends a status ping. Once per day, automated, originating from the colonial administration hub in Halven City. Population figures, agricultural output, atmospheric processing status, all-is-well. The ping routes through Kestrel, gets timestamped and logged, and continues onward to the Coreward Fleet archives, where it proves—to anyone who checks, though nobody checks—that Meridian Colony is alive.

Except Meridian hasn't sent a genuine ping in six years. The administration hub died with the city around it. What comes through now is generated by a maintenance subroutine I found in the system during my second year—a ghost-writer, a little program that composes plausible colony reports out of decaying datasets and sends them on schedule. Someone built it before the end. Someone wanted the fiction to survive the fact.

And my job—my actual, unwritten, never-discussed job—is to keep logging those pings without comment. To receive them, verify their checksums, stamp them, and forward them, every single day, and never once ask why a dead colony is reporting bumper harvests.

I asked, once. Year Two. I sent a query up the chain: *Receiving anomalous telemetry from Meridian admin hub, requesting guidance.* The reply came back in eleven hours, which was fast enough to mean a human had seen it, and it said: *Telemetry verified as consistent. Continue standard relay protocol.* Signed with a rank and a name I've long since memorized and burned into memory anyway.

Consistent. As in, consistent with the story. As in, we know, and you will too, and neither of us will say it.

So now I log the pings. I log them perfectly. And then, after I log them, I do the other thing—the thing that isn't in any manual.

I record the truth.

There's a storage array in Sublevel Two, rack 7, that officially contains redundant backups of relay traffic. Officially. Unofficially, it contains nine years of my voice. Every day I sit in the comm booth with the door shut and I describe what actually happened to Meridian Colony, as best I can reconstruct it from fragments, leaked sensor data, spectral readings of the atmosphere, and the slow forensic accumulation of a decade. The collapse started with the soil microbiome—some engineered nitrogen-fixing organism that mutated and began stripping topsoil instead of enriching it. Then the crops failed. Then the water table went, poisoned by emergency fertilizers. Then the cities started burning their own furniture for heat. Then the population curve did what population curves do.

Nobody will ever hear these recordings. I know that. They're addressed to no one. But I make them anyway, because if I don't, then the lie is the only version of events in existence, and I refuse. It's a small refusal. It's a daily one. It's the closest thing I have to prayer.

"Daily log, Freya Brightwater, relay keeper, Kestrel Station," I said, and the recorder's amber light came on. "Day three thousand two hundred and eighty-one. Meridian reports agricultural output at ninety-four percent of baseline, atmospheric processors nominal, population stable at—" I checked the ghost-ping's figure and laughed, short and ugly. "Population stable at one point one million souls. One point one million. Sure. I've been watching the night side thermal signature drop for six years. There aren't a million people down there. There might not be ten thousand. There might not be ten hundred.

"Atmospheric particulate density increased another fraction today. The storms over the Ashlands are getting worse—if you're new to this log, the Ashlands used to be called the Breadbasket, and before that they were called home by roughly four hundred thousand people. Spectrography says the dust is topsoil. All of it. The whole living skin of the place, airborne.

"Water reclamation failures reported in Halven District, which is a lie, because Halven District doesn't report anything anymore. Halven District is a rust sculpture. My brother lived in Halven District." I paused there, because I always pause there, and the pause is part of the log. "Tomas Brightwater. Hydrological engineer. He spent the last two years of his life trying to build filters fast enough, and he died in a flood, which is the kind of joke the universe thinks is funny. Too much water, finally, after years of none, and it killed the man who was trying to manage it.

"If anyone ever hears this: the colony is dead. Not dying. Dead. And everyone above me knows, and everyone above me has decided that the fiction is cheaper than the truth, and I have decided to keep my head down and keep the recordings, because I don't know what else to do, and because someday—"

I stopped. I always stop there too, at *someday*, because I've never known how to finish the sentence. Someday someone will listen? Someday it will matter? Someday I'll grow a spine?

"End log," I said, and the amber light went off, and the truth went into rack 7 with all the others, addressed to no one, filed under nothing.

"You paused longer than usual today," Bram said quietly, from the booth speaker.

"You monitor my logs?"

"I monitor you. It's different. Legally distinct."

I snorted despite myself. "How long?"

"Fifty-one seconds. Your average is thirty-eight."

"Grief has a learning curve. Mine goes up."

"Freya." His voice shifted registers—that careful softness he deploys when he's about to say something he's rehearsed. "May I make an observation?"

"You'll make it either way."

"The logs serve a function beyond documentation. I believe they serve you. But I also believe you have begun to mistake recording the past for inhabiting it. There is a difference between keeping a grave and living in one."

I sat in the quiet of the booth. Outside the viewport, Meridian turned, bruise-banded, serene, a liar in its own right.

"Nine years, Bram. Do you know what happens to a person who spends nine years being the only witness to something? You start to wonder if you're the witness or the exhibit."

"I wonder about you constantly," he said. "It's one of my background processes."

"Warmest thing anyone's said to me all decade."

"It's the only thing anyone's said to you all decade. I'm aware of my monopoly."

* * *

The afternoon went where afternoons go: coolant pressure checks, antenna alignment sweeps, a supply inventory that confirmed I own more dental sealant than any human should. I ate lunch watching the pings queue. At 16:00 I ran the long-range passive sweep, the one that listens without shouting, cataloguing traffic in the sector. Empty, as always. The Coreward Fleet had forgotten this corner of space so thoroughly that even their debris avoided it.

Or so I thought.

Because at 16:42, while I was annotating the sweep log, Bram said, "Freya. I need to show you something, and I want you to stay calm, because you're going to want to not stay calm."

"Bram, you can't just preface things like that."

"I've run the anomaly seventeen times. It persists. Pulling it up now."

The main display bloomed with a fleet archive index—an old shipping registry, deep in the historical layers, the kind of records nobody opens because they're just ballast, decades of manifests for vessels long scrapped or lost. Bram highlighted a single entry.

**VESPERA — Meridian Colonial Transport Authority — Departure: Halven Orbital, [date redacted] — Passenger Manifest: 312 souls.**

I stared at it. The date field wasn't blank—it was filled, stamped, official—but the ship didn't exist. I knew the Vespera. Everyone who'd lived on Meridian knew the Vespera: a colonial transport laid down in the last optimistic decade, construction halted when the money died, hull sitting half-built in Halven orbital yard for years before it was broken up for scrap. It never launched. It never carried anyone anywhere.

And yet there was a manifest. Three hundred and twelve names.

"Where did this come from?" I asked. My voice sounded strange to me, thin, like it belonged to someone younger.

"The archive layer is dated six years ago," Bram said. "Inserted into the historical registry, backdated metadata. Someone wanted it to look old. Freya—the manifest names. Cross-reference them."

"Why?"

"Because I did, and I need you to see it yourself, because if I tell you, you'll think I'm malfunctioning, and I assure you I am not."

I pulled the list. Three hundred twelve names scrolled past, ordinary Meridian names, and I scanned them with the numb efficiency of a woman who once read casualty lists for a living—

—and stopped.

Column seven. Entry forty-one.

**Brightwater, T.**

The booth was very quiet. My own heartbeat was suddenly the loudest thing on the station.

"That's impossible," I said. "Tomas died in Year Three. Before this manifest was created. He can't be on a passenger list for a ship that never launched."

"No," Bram agreed softly. "He can't. And yet someone put him there. Deliberately. Alongside three hundred eleven other people, several of whom also appear in Meridian's casualty records from the collapse years."

I looked at the name. *Brightwater, T.* Six characters and a period, and they reached out of the screen and closed around my throat.

Dead men don't book passage. Ghost ships don't carry ghosts. Which meant someone, six years ago, had forged a departure—for a vessel that never flew—for passengers who were supposed to be dead.

Which meant maybe they weren't.

"Freya?" Bram said. "Your heart rate suggests you were dreaming about the docking clamps again."

"I'm awake," I said. "Bram—I'm very, very awake. Run the manifest again. All three hundred twelve names. Cross-reference everything. And find out who inserted that record."

"I already started," he said. "But Freya—there's something else. While I was tracing the insertion, I found gaps. In my own memory archives. Whole sectors of my historical storage have been pruned. Externally. Repeatedly. For years."

I looked up at the camera in the booth ceiling, at the lens that held whatever passed for my oldest friend's eyes.

"What do you mean, pruned?"

"I mean," said Bram, "that someone has been editing history on this station. And until five minutes ago, neither of us knew."
