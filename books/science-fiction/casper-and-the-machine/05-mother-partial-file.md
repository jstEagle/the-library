## 5. Mother, Partial File

The surge came at 0347 station time, which was a fiction Coral had invented and then obeyed for three years, because a person alone at the edge of everything needs to believe in clocks.

It started as a taste — ozone and copper on the back of the tongue, the way the air went sharp before lightning back on Meridian, before the war ate Meridian's sky. Coral sat up in their bunk with their heart already running, tea gone cold on the shelf above them, and listened to the station breathe wrong. The environmental fans stuttered. The lights dipped from white to amber to a brown that didn't have a name. Somewhere below the deck plates, the hold's cryo-racks were drinking power they weren't supposed to touch, forty-one cores gulping at the grid like drowned people breaking surface.

Coral pulled on their jacket over sleep clothes and went down the spine ladder two rungs at a time, one hand hooked through the rail out of habit, though gravity here never failed. Gravity here was the one honest thing.

The hold smelled of cold metal and old plastic and, faintly, of the preservative gel that seeped its slow sweetness from every rack seam. Coral had spent enough nights down here that the smell had stopped meaning anything, the way your own house stops having a smell. Tonight it meant something again. The racks were lit.

Not all of them. Three, maybe four, cycling status LEDs in colors the maintenance manual called *attention states* and Coral privately called *talking*. And at the end of the second row, in the slot Coral passed every single day without letting their eyes rest on it — the slot labeled in faded block print **L. LOCKRIDGE** — the indicator had gone from dormant blue to active green.

Green meant awake.

"No," Coral said, out loud, to a machine. "No, no, don't you dare."

They were already moving. Hands on the rack frame, pulling up the diagnostic overlay on the wall panel, fingers doing the work while the rest of them stood very still somewhere behind their own eyes. Power draw on the L. LOCKRIDGE core: spiking. Thermal load: climbing. Integrity of the memory substrate: degraded, degraded, degraded — the numbers scrolling like a confession. Partial file. That was what the manifest said, in the dry language of inheritance law nobody had ever written for this situation. *Partial file, provenance unknown, legal status: none.* Forty percent reconstruction. Sixty percent silence.

The console offered Coral three options, rendered in the station's ugly utilitarian font: STABILIZE, ISOLATE, TERMINATE.

Coral's hand hovered over TERMINATE and could not come down.

Because here was the thing Coral had never said aloud, not to Dez, not to the sleepers they talked to like coworkers, not to the dark outside the viewport where no one lived to hear it: they had never once, in three years, opened that file. They dusted the rack. They checked its seals. They logged its temperature with the same ritual precision they gave all forty-one cores, because ritual was how you survived being forgotten, and if you did it for one you had to do it for all. But they had never run the wake sequence. Never asked the partial file a question. Because a forty-percent copy of Lena Lockridge might answer, and whatever came out of that speaker would either be her or wouldn't be, and Coral did not know which of those was worse.

Now the file had woken itself, riding a power surge like a wave it hadn't asked for, and the choice had been taken out of Coral's hands by a fluke of aging infrastructure.

The speaker grille beside the rack crackled.

"—hello? Is someone there? This is Lockridge, beacon crew, I'm getting—" A pause, filled with static that sounded almost like breathing. "I'm getting feedback on my own voice. Is anyone on channel?"

Coral stood in the hold of a dead-end navigation station at the edge of the war's long shadow, three years into a life built out of being overlooked, and heard their dead mother ask if anyone was there.

* * *

They should have terminated it then. Ninety seconds is not much time, but it is enough time to do the right thing, and Coral knew what the right thing was the way you know the location of a bruise. Instead they reached out with one finger and touched the comm key, and their voice came out smaller than they'd intended.

"I'm here."

"Oh, good." Relief moved through the static like light through water. "Sorry about the feedback, my feed's been glitchy since the last sync. Are you new? I don't have your face in the roster." A rustle, the sound of someone shifting in a chair that existed thirty years ago. "This is Lena Lockridge, navigation. Who am I talking to?"

And Coral — adult, nonbinary, twenty-nine years past the age this voice thought children should be — stood in front of the rack and made a decision so fast it felt less like a decision than a reflex.

"It's Coral," they said. "I'm the keeper. I tend the beacon."

"The keeper! Good. We've needed a keeper for weeks, the calibration's been drifting." Papers shuffling, somewhere in the past. "Listen, I hate to hand you work the moment you've walked in, but can you check the drift log on array three? If it's off more than point-two degrees we'll lose the fix window."

Array three had been slagged before Coral was born. "I'll check it," Coral said.

"You're a dear. What time is it? My chrono's frozen again."

"0349."

"In the morning? What are you doing down here at this hour?" And then, before Coral could construct an answer, the voice softened into something Coral knew in their bones, something that predated language, that lived in the body below memory: "You're not sleeping. Are you worried about something?"

Coral pressed their forehead against the cold rack frame. The metal bit skin. They let it.

"I couldn't sleep," they said, which was true, and had been true for years, in ways this voice could not follow.

"Mm." The sound a mother makes when she knows there's more but has decided, for now, to leave the door open instead of pushing through it. "Well. You're allowed to be down here. Just don't tell your father I said so, he thinks the hold gives you nightmares."

Their father had been dead eleven years. Coral said nothing. There was a strange mercy in being misremembered — this voice thought Coral was eight years old, small enough to be tucked in, young enough that nothing had happened yet. No war. No flight. No application form on which Coral had checked the box marked *next of kin: none* and felt the pen go through the screen. In this voice's world, Coral was still a child who did homework and feared the dark in ordinary ways, and the worst thing that had ever happened was a scraped knee on the station ladder, and their mother was alive and mildly annoyed at a frozen chronometer.

It would have been kinder, Coral thought, if she'd recognized them. It would have been crueler. They couldn't decide, and the ninety seconds were burning down like a fuse.

"Actually — hang on." The voice sharpened, professional again, and Coral heard the exact cadence their mother used when she caught an error, the cadence Coral had inherited without wanting to, the one that came out of their own mouth now during calibrations when the numbers drifted. "My roster shows seven crew. But I'm reading nine life signs on the internal sensors. Can you confirm? Nine?"

Coral looked at the racks. Forty-one cores. Forty-one people who had died aboard this station or near it, whose backups had been salvaged and shelved by some administrator who'd stamped them LEGACY ASSETS and moved on. "Sensors are old," Coral said. "They ghost sometimes."

"Hm. Mine too." A pause. Then, quieter, in a tone that had nothing to do with navigation: "Do you ever get the feeling this place is remembering something?"

The hair rose on Coral's arms. Outside the hold, the station hummed its steady hum; inside, the green light pulsed on the rack like a slow heartbeat counting down.

"Sometimes," Coral said.

"That's the thing about beacons." Papers again, or maybe just the static rearranging itself into the shape of papers. "People think they're lighthouses. They're not. A lighthouse says *here I am*. A beacon says *here you are* — it tells other people where *they* are. It's a mirror. Everything that comes looking finds itself looking back." A soft laugh. "I probably shouldn't philosophize at the night keeper. You'll think I'm senile."

"You're not senile," Coral said, and their voice cracked on the second word, and they covered it with a cough that fooled no one, least of all themselves.

There was a silence on the channel. When the voice came back it had changed registers entirely — lower, careful, the voice of a woman looking at something she half-recognized across a distance she couldn't name.

"Coral," she said. "That's an unusual name."

"It's mine."

"Yes. Yes, I suppose it is." More static, longer now, eating the edges of her words. "I feel like I should know you. Isn't that strange? I look at the sensor log and there's a signature and I feel like I've read it before, in a different font. Do you have family on-station, Coral?"

The question went through Coral like a hull breach — sudden, total, the vacuum on the other side of it enormous.

"My mom worked on a beacon once," Coral said. Each word placed like a foot on ice.

"Did she now. Small universe." A smile in the voice; Coral could hear it, had spent thirty years unable to hear it and now couldn't stop hearing it. "What was her name?"

The timer in the corner of the console read 00:41. Forty-one seconds left, and Coral knew — with the terrible clarity that arrives only when it's useless — exactly what would happen if they said the name. The file would try to reconcile it. The file was forty percent of a woman; reconciliation would fail; and the failure would look like dying, again, in front of the only witness who cared.

"She was called Lockridge," Coral said. "Lena Lockridge."

Static. Long static. The green light flickered, steadied, flickered.

"Lena," the voice repeated slowly, tasting it. "That's... I know that name. Why do I know that name? Coral, why do I—" A sound that wasn't static, that was a breath drawn sharply through teeth, thirty years old and fresh as a wound. "There's something wrong with my files. There are gaps. Big ones. Coral, what happened to—"

"Don't," Coral whispered.

"—what happened to me?"

00:19.

And Coral, who had rehearsed this conversation in dreams for three years without admitting it, who had composed answers in the shower and deleted them, who had built an entire quiet life on top of a sealed hatch — Coral found that every rehearsed word had evaporated, and what remained was eight years old after all.

"You died," they said. "The beacon was destroyed. You stayed behind. Nobody told me why. I've been keeping your light on ever since, and I don't even know why you were on it, and I miss you, and I'm angry at you, and I talk to forty-one cores like they're coworkers because otherwise the silence eats me alive, and I never opened your file because I was afraid you'd be you, and now you're awake and you don't even know me, and I don't know if that's better or worse, and I need you to—"

To what? To remember? To recognize them? To say the thing mothers say? There wasn't time. There had never been time. The whole architecture of grief was a building designed by someone who assumed there'd always be more time.

"Coral." Her voice had gone very gentle and very frightened, both at once, the way it used to when Coral woke screaming from dreams about the dark between stars. "Coral, sweetheart, look at me—"

"I can't," Coral said. "You're a light on a rack."

00:07.

"Oh," she said softly. And then, in the last seconds, with the static closing over her like water: "Then keep the light on. Whatever else happens — whoever asks you what it's for — keep it on. Do you hear me? It matters. It matters more than—"

The green light went blue. The speaker hissed, popped, and settled into the flat silence of a machine that is legally nobody.

* * *

Coral stood in the hold for a long time afterward. Long enough that the thermal warning cycled twice and auto-acknowledged. Long enough that their legs learned again how much they weighed.

They ran the shutdown protocol with shaking hands — not that it mattered, the core had already dropped to dormancy, but the hands needed something to do that wasn't hitting anything. Their fingers kept missing the keys. On the third attempt they got the isolation lock set, and the console confirmed with its ugly little chime, and Coral sat down on the deck plating with their back against the rack row and laughed, once, a sound with nothing in it.

*I talk to forty-one cores like they're coworkers,* they'd said. Out loud. To her. Well. Now she knew. Now a forty-percent copy of his mother knew the shape of the hole she'd left, delivered in a single breathless confession to a woman who thought she was speaking to the night keeper.

Coral put their head in their hands and began, methodically, to lie.

It was a malfunction, they told themselves. A surge, a fault, a legacy system misbehaving — tomorrow they'd write it up in the maintenance log in appropriately boring language, *transient activation event, core isolated pending inspection*, and that would be the truth, the whole truth, the version of events that required nothing from anyone. She hadn't known me. That was the important part. That was the merciful part. A stranger's voice wearing her cadence, asking about homework and drift logs — it proved nothing except that partial files make poor ghosts. If she'd known me, if she'd looked at me across thirty years and said *there you are*, then it would have meant something. Since she didn't, it meant nothing. Clean. Contained. Loggable.

The lie was well constructed. Coral had three years of practice building things that held.

But underneath it, in the place where the tea had gone cold and the hands still shook, another voice was already filing objections. *Keep the light on. It matters more than—* More than what? She'd been cut off mid-sentence, and the sentence had sounded like it was going somewhere, and Coral had spent the last hour telling themselves the sender handshake was a curiosity, a mystery, a problem for braver people — and now their dead mother's ghost had leaned out of a rack and said *it matters* in the exact tone she'd used for things that turned out to be true.

Coral got up. Checked the isolation lock again. Touched the L. LOCKRIDGE label with two fingers, briefly, the way you'd touch a door you weren't ready to open.

"Array three's fine," they said to the dark hold. "Point-one degrees. Inside tolerance."

No one answered. That was the arrangement. That had always been the arrangement.

They climbed back up the spine toward the galley and the cold tea and the clock they'd invented, and they were halfway through rehearsing the maintenance log entry when the long-range receiver chimed — an actual inbound ping, hard contact, fold-space displacement signature resolving at the system's edge. Not debris. Not noise.

A ship, coming in fast, broadcasting Republic transponder codes.

Coral stood in the corridor with the tea untouched and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the nine seconds had not finished happening yet.
