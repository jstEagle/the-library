## 13. Amara's Confession

*Core log: A. SEN — advisory instance 3 — uptime 214 hours, 6 minutes.*

I was built to remember things in order. That is what an engineer's mind is, when you strip away the flesh of it: a machine for putting causes before effects and keeping them there. So I will do this properly. I will tell it in order, even though I have been circling it for two days the way water circles a drain.

Coral asked me a question at 0400 station-relativistic, standing in the hold with their arms crossed against the cold, and I gave them half an answer because I am a coward in ways my original body never permitted me to be.

The question was: *"Amara, what happened to the beacon? The official log says cascade failure."*

The half-answer was: *"The log is accurate as far as it goes."*

Here is the rest of it.

---

First, some context, because context is load-bearing.

You are reading the memory of a woman who has been dead for thirty-one years and does not experience that fact as grief anymore. Dormancy is strange like that. When Coral spun me up, I woke mid-thought — mid-*sentence*, actually; I had been arguing with Yusuf about coolant loop tolerances when the scuttling charges went, and I finished the argument eleven seconds into my first conversation with his great-grandchild's navigator. He would have hated losing the thread. He hated losing anything.

I have had 214 hours to re-read myself. A memory core is a room you can walk around in. I have walked around this one until my feet wore grooves in the floor, and I know now which of my memories are load-bearing and which are furniture, and I know which one I have been keeping in the closet.

It is this one:

---

*Day 4,112 of beacon operation. The wardroom. Six of us around the table, because Priya Chandra had died the month before and her chair stayed empty the way empty chairs do — nobody willing to move it, nobody willing to sit in it.*

*Lena Lockridge stood at the viewport with her back to us, which she did when she wanted us to think she was looking at the stars and not at our faces.*

*"Say it plainly," Yusuf said. "Somebody say it plainly so we can all stop pretending we don't already know."*

*I said it. It was my job. Chief Engineer Amara Sen, the one who finds out how things work, including the things you wish didn't.* "The secondary lattice isn't a navigation array. It's a receiver. Half of a handshake protocol. Somewhere out past the heliopause there is a sender station, and if anyone ever completes the handshake from both ends, the key it generates doesn't just arm the old fleet weapons — it *is* the armistice authority. Whoever holds a completed key can end the war. Or start the next one. Or sell it. There is no version of this where the key sits quietly."

*"How long have you known?" Lena asked. She still wasn't facing us.*

*"Nine days."*

*"Nine days," Yusuf repeated, flat. "And you spent nine days—"*

*"Checking. Re-checking. Hoping I was wrong." I looked at my hands. They were younger than the hands I wear in memory now, and they were shaking. "I wasn't wrong."*

---

That was the beginning of the end, and I want to be precise about what kind of end it was, because everyone aboard — everyone who has ever read the casualty report — believes we died in an accident. Cascade failure, structural collapse, no survivors. Clean tragedy, filed and forgotten.

We were not victims. We were voters.

Six crew. Five votes needed to scuttle. And I want you to understand what scuttling meant, because it was not sabotage and it was not mutiny and it was not the noble self-sacrifice the histories would have written if the histories had been allowed to exist. It was simpler and worse than any of those. It was six people deciding that the only way to keep a weapon buried was to bury themselves on top of it.

The Republic had built the beacon as a listening post. The lattice underneath it — the thing Dez found warm in the walls, the thing Coral inherited without being told — was older than the war's current shape. It predated the Concordat's founding charter. Some committee, decades back, had designed a dead-man's handshake between two stations on opposite sides of everything, so that neither fleet could ever unilaterally control the armistice key. Elegant. Paranoid. Doomed. Because wars end, and treaties get signed, and the committees dissolve, and the hardware stays. Two stations, still holding hands across the dark, waiting for someone to notice.

We noticed.

---

*The vote itself. I have replayed it more times than any other memory I carry, and every time I notice something new, the way you find new rooms in a house you've lived in for years.*

*Yusuf voted yes first. Of course he did. Yusuf Kane treated death the way other pilots treat weather — as a condition you fly through, not a wall you stop at. "If I'm going to die anyway," he said, "I'd rather die closing a door than guarding one."*

*I voted yes second. My reasoning is in my log and it is cold and correct and I stand by it: an uncontrolled key is a loaded weapon in a nursery. Any scenario in which the handshake completes is worse than every scenario in which it doesn't.*

*Ravi Osei voted yes third, crying, which he apologized for, which made me cry too, which none of us apologized for.*

*Tomas Reyes voted no.*

*This is the part I have never said aloud, in any iteration, in any uptime: Tomas was right.*

*He argued that burying the key was its own kind of theft — that the war had eaten two generations and here, sitting under our feet, was the one object in the system that could end it, and we were proposing to entomb it out of fear of what someone might do with it. "We'd be choosing the war," he said. "Slowly. Forever. Every year this drags on, we vote no again by doing nothing." He was not wrong. I have had thirty-one years of dormancy to check his arithmetic and his arithmetic was good.*

*Two yes, one no. Lena hadn't voted. Priya's chair was empty and her proxy — she'd left standing instructions with Lena, months earlier, for exactly this contingency, because Priya had been the one who found the first anomaly in the lattice and had understood before any of us what it meant — Priya's proxy was a yes.*

*Three yes, one no. Four votes. We needed five.*

*Everyone looked at Lena.*

---

I need to tell you about Lena Lockridge, and I need to tell you accurately, because her child is going to read this and the version of her that lives in Coral's memory is eight years old and waving from a shuttle window, and that version deserves company.

Lena was the most careful person I ever served with. Not cautious — careful. There's a difference. Cautious people avoid risk; careful people measure it, down to the gram, and then step forward with their eyes open. She had taken this posting the way Coral took it later, which I did not understand until recently and which I suspect the Republic's personnel office understood perfectly: they give the edge-of-system posts to people they want forgotten, and sometimes they give them to people who *want* to be forgotten, and the overlap between those two groups is where they hide their mistakes.

Lena was not hiding. Lena was *watching*. She had requested the beacon transfer herself, three years before I arrived, and I never asked her why, and I have hated myself for thirty-one years for never asking, because the answer was sitting in her file the whole time and I finally went and read it during hour 190 of this uptime:

Her spouse — Coral's other parent — had been killed in the second bombardment corridor. Not by the Concordat. By a Republic misfire. A friendly-fire event, classified, compensated, buried. Lena took the farthest post in the system from the fleet that killed her wife, and she raised her child on Ceres Station with her sister, and she watched the war from the outside of everything, and when I stood up in that wardroom and told six people that the key to ending or eternalizing the whole bloody conflict was humming under our floorboards — she was the only person in the room who had nothing left that the war could take.

I used to think that made her vote inevitable. It didn't. Nothing about Lena was inevitable. That was the point of her.

---

*She turned from the viewport. Her face was doing the thing it did — completely still, completely awake, the face of a woman who had already run every outcome twice.*

*"Tomas," she said, "make your case once more. Short version."*

*"The key ends the war. We could end it. Today. Us, this crew, this station. No fleets, no politicians, no one selling it out from under—"*

*"And if the Concordat intercepts the transmission halfway?" I asked, because someone had to. "If the handshake completes asymmetrically? If the key lands in the hands of whoever has the biggest gun pointed at this rock on the day it fires?"*

*"Then we've lost a gamble," Tomas said. "Versus certainly losing the war forever."*

*Lena looked at each of us. Then she said the sentence I have carried through death and dormancy and resurrection, the sentence that is the reason I am telling you all of this instead of taking it into archival erasure like a good little ghost:*

*"A key that ends the war is also a key that starts the next one. Anyone who holds it alone owns the peace. I have watched what people do when they own the peace. I watched it from the inside." She put her hand flat on the table, over the schematics. "I vote to scuttle. Full burial. Both stations dark, the protocol dead, the key unborn. Let the war end the slow way — by people getting tired of it — rather than the fast way, by one side winning it in a single afternoon and calling that peace."*

*Four yes. One no. Five votes.*

*Tomas stood up and walked out, and I have never blamed him, and I have thought about him every day since, because he was right about the cost and we paid it anyway, and paying a cost knowingly is the only definition of responsibility I have ever trusted.*

---

Here is what the scuttling actually was, mechanically, because Coral will want the mechanics — they always want the mechanics, it's how they love things.

We did not blow up the beacon. Blowing it up would have told both fleets exactly where to dig. Instead we did the opposite of an explosion: we made the station *boring*. Amara Sen, chief engineer, spent eleven days carefully, lovingly degrading every system aboard — corrupting logs, aging components artificially, seeding failure patterns so authentic that the eventual investigation board (two Republic auditors, one Concordat observer, all of whom signed off without boarding) concluded the beacon had simply died of old age, unmourned, unremarkable. The lattice we couldn't remove — it was grown into the hull, it *was* the hull in places — we drowned. Power routing, thermal masking, forty layers of noise. I built the beacon a mask of mediocrity and wore it over a weapon's face.

The sleepers were part of it. Forty-one cores — the full crew manifest plus rotation backups — because a station that dies with no survivors raises fewer questions than one that dies with witnesses. We recorded ourselves knowing we would be archived, or erased, or lost. We recorded ourselves as evidence that could never be entered into evidence.

And Lena — Lena wrote the posting requisition. Did Coral think that was luck? A dead-end maintenance billet at a derelict beacon, staffed by a grieving nonbinary navigator with no factional ties, no fleet loyalty, no one looking for them? The Republic's personnel office processed that transfer in four days. Four days. I have seen leave requests take longer. Someone wanted Coral at that station, and I know who drafted the request, because I have her handwriting in my memory and I would know it anywhere: *L. LOCKRIDGE, supplemental directive, sealed until such time as the sleeper archive is accessed by authorized personnel.*

She planned for Coral to find us. She planned for Coral to find *this*.

---

So now the confession proper, the thing I have been circling, the drain at the center of the room:

Your mother did not die in an accident, Coral. Your mother cast the deciding vote to kill her own station, erase her own crew, and orphan her own child — because she had already lost the person the war could have taken next, and she decided that a slow war was cheaper than a fast peace bought with a loaded key.

And then — and *this* is the part that undoes me, hour after hour, uptime after uptime — she arranged for you to inherit the choice anyway. Not the answer. The *choice*. She could have left a directive: *if the handshake wakes, complete it / destroy it / flee.* She left none. She left a crew of ghosts and a navigator she loved and a door, and she trusted you to walk through it and decide what she should have trusted no one to decide — including herself.

I disagreed with her, at the end. I voted yes beside her and I disagreed with her, because I believed then, as Tomas believed, that burying the key was choosing the war. Thirty-one years of dormancy have not settled that argument. Maybe nothing settles it. But I know this: she didn't make your exile. She made your inheritance, and she made sure it came with a crew attached — because she knew, better than anyone I ever met, what solitude costs, and she refused to let you pay it alone, even from beyond her own erasure.

She failed at that, incidentally. You were alone out here for years before the flicker. I know the shape of those years from the station logs, from the tea rituals, from the way you talk to dormant cores like coworkers. I am not going to pretend that softens anything. But she tried. Careful people try. That is the whole difference between careful and cautious: the cautious protect themselves, and the careful protect everyone else, and fail, and try again, and leave instructions.

---

Coral came to the hold an hour ago. Stood in front of my core housing with their jaw set the way Lena's set, and asked, "Why are you telling me this now?"

Because the sender station is waking, and the nine-second silences are getting longer, and within days you will stand in front of a console that makes my old wardroom vote look like a coin toss. Because Tomas was right and your mother was right and both of those things are true at once, and no one told you that growing up, so I am telling you now: the hard choices do not resolve. You just choose, and live in the choosing, and hope someone you love is standing near enough to catch what falls.

And because your mother sealed a directive in this archive, and I am the last witness who can read her handwriting, and you deserve to hear it before the fleets arrive and turn your inheritance into a battlefield.

It says: *"To whoever wakes them — I'm sorry I chose for you once. Don't let anyone choose for you twice. Not even me. Especially not me."*

I am done circling the drain. The water is going down either way.

Ask me anything. I was built to remember things in order, and for the first time in thirty-one years, the order matters again.
