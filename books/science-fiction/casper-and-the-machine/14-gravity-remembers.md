## 14. Gravity Remembers

Coral had learned to measure time in the small weights of things. The pull of a spoon against ceramic. The way their braid settled against their neck. The soft drag of a sleeve when they reached for the nav console — all of it a constant, quiet confirmation that the universe still agreed to hold them.

So when the weight went wrong, they knew before any instrument did.

They were in the galley of the tug frame — *Long Answer*, Yusuf had named her, back when he was alive and fond of jokes that only made sense after you'd flown with him for a year — heating water for tea they wouldn't have time to drink. Amara's voice was coming through the bulkhead speaker, walking them through the third-stage injector purge she'd insisted on before they crossed the Meridian Shoals. Coral had the kettle in one hand and a calibration stylus between their teeth, and for one ordinary second everything was exactly as boring as they'd spent six years building it to be.

Then the water climbed out of the kettle.

Not spilled. *Climbed* — a silver rope of it, rising past Coral's face, breaking into beads that hung in the air like a held breath. The stylus drifted from their lips. Their own body pressed gently upward against the deck straps, insubstantial as a thought.

"No," Coral said, to no one, to everyone. "No, no, not again."

The nine seconds began.

They counted because counting was the only thing their hands could do. One: the galley became a snow globe of utensils, tea leaves, a photograph of Ceres Station that Dez kept magnetized to the cooler door. Two: somewhere aft, Amara's voice cut off mid-syllable, and the silence where her voice had been was worse than the falling. Three: the hull groaned — a long, low sound like a whale turning over in deep water, the sound of a ship whose every weld was being asked a question it hadn't trained for.

Four: Coral's inner ear betrayed them completely. There was no up. There was no down. There was only the memory of both, and the sick knowledge that every planet in the system was having the same moment they were.

Five: they grabbed the hatch frame and hauled themselves hand over hand toward the cockpit, the photograph of Ceres tumbling past them like a leaf.

Six: through the cockpit glass, the Shoals themselves moved.

That was the image that would live behind Coral's eyes for the rest of their life, whatever length that turned out to be. The Meridian Shoals were debris — centuries of it, battle wreckage and mining slag and the frozen atmosphere of a moon that had lost an argument with a fleet — a slow river of rock and iron that never, ever moved except along its million-year drift. And in the sixth second of the flicker, the whole river *shuddered*. Tens of thousands of tons of dead metal lifting a few meters off their orbital lines and settling again, like a sleeping animal twitching at a sound.

Seven: Coral's stomach returned to them like a dropped tool.

Eight: the stars swung as the tug frame's gyroscopes fought to keep them oriented against forces that had no business existing.

Nine: gravity came back all at once, everywhere, rude as a slammed door. Coral hit the deck. The kettle hit the deck. The photograph of Ceres hit the deck, and Dez's smiling family slid out of its frame and spun away into the ventilation intake, and Coral heard themselves make a sound they didn't recognize — something between a laugh and a sob, punched out of them by the simple obscene weight of being a body again.

For three full seconds nobody moved. Then Amara's voice came back through the bulkhead, ragged:

"—say again, all hands, say again—"

"We felt it," Coral said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "We all felt it."

* * *

They gathered in the cockpit because there was nowhere else to gather, and because the cockpit had the biggest window, and because what was outside the window demanded witnesses.

Yusuf's core had the ship. He flew from a speaker and a shared control bus now, his voice arriving calm and slightly amused the way it always did, but Coral had learned to hear the effort underneath it — the dead pilot holding the living ship steady by will alone. He had killed the main burn and let them coast, nose toward the sender station, which hung in the long-range optics as a gray sliver against the dark of the outer system.

"Report," Coral said. "All stations. Quickly."

"Engines nominal," Amara said. "But I want it on record that what just happened is not possible. Gravitational shear of that magnitude across this volume — we'd be looking at tidal stresses on every inhabited body in the system. Stations buckling. Orbital infrastructure—"

"It happened," Yusuf said quietly. "Look outside."

Coral looked. Beyond the Shoals, far off, the gas giant Veyra sat in its slow majesty — and even at this distance, even through the optics' correction algorithms, Coral could see it: the great bands of its atmosphere displaced, storm systems dragged visibly out of alignment, a bruise-colored ripple crossing the northern hemisphere like a shudder passing through skin. A planet the size of thirteen hundred Earths had flinched.

And below it, at the edge of detection, the sender station was bright.

It shouldn't have been. It was supposed to be a tomb — that was the whole architecture of the lie, the whole shape of the war's longest secret. The beacon was the receiver; the sender was buried and cold and had been for thirty years, waiting for someone foolish or desperate enough to complete the handshake. But the optics didn't lie about thermal signatures. The station's spine glowed a dull, waking orange, heat blooming along its length like blood returning to a numb limb.

"It's warming itself," Coral said. "It doesn't need us."

"It needs *something*," Amara said. "The flicker wasn't random. That was a handshake attempt — a partial one. The sender woke up, reached across the system, and found our beacon gone. Scuttled. So it did what any machine does when the answer doesn't come." She paused, and Coral heard her choose her words with an engineer's care. "It started asking louder."

"The first flicker was a knock," Yusuf said. "This was a shout. The third one—" He didn't finish.

"The third one wakes it all the way," Coral said. "And then it completes the handshake with whoever's closest. Or whoever's loudest. Or it arms itself for whichever fleet gets here first." They turned from the window and found Dez already in the cockpit doorway, hair unbound, eyes wide, one hand pressed flat against the frame as if checking that reality still held its shape.

"How long?" Dez asked.

Coral looked at Amara. Amara was silent for a moment, running numbers Coral couldn't see, and when she spoke, her voice had the terrible gentleness engineers used for bad news.

"If the escalation curve is linear — and I have no reason to think it is, but it's the only model I can defend — the next flicker comes in somewhere between fourteen and twenty hours. After that, the sender stops asking and starts deciding. Full arming sequence. At that point the key isn't a key anymore. It's a spectator's ticket."

"Fourteen hours," Dez repeated. "To fly through the Shoals, past two blockading fleets who both want us dead or owned, and dock with a station that's actively waking up under us."

"Yes," said Coral.

"Well," said Dez, and ran both hands through their hair, and laughed once, high and unhinged. "Good news is my debt collectors can't reach me if I'm vaporized."

Nobody laughed with them. Nobody laughed at all. But something in the cockpit changed anyway — Coral felt it happen, felt the panic in the room stop being four separate panics and become one shared pressure, the way air redistributes in a hull when a seal closes. Fear, Coral was learning, was a fuel. It burned dirty, but it burned hot.

"Course," Coral said. "Yusuf. Give me a course through the Shoals that gets us to the sender in twelve hours. I don't care how ugly it is."

"Coral," Yusuf said, gentle, "the pretty line through the Shoals takes sixteen. The ugly line takes eleven and a half, and the ugly line goes through the *Graveyard Proper*. Wreck density off the scale. Debris moving on unpredictable vectors since the flicker kicked everything loose. Half those wrecks still have live munitions aboard, thirty years down. I'd be flying us through a minefield made of ghosts, on instruments, blind, fast."

Coral looked at the orange glow on the optics. At the bruised giant above it. At the crew — the impossible, assembled, resurrected crew — watching them with faces that were, in three cases out of four, rendered entirely from memory and light.

Their mother's core said nothing. She had been silent since the flicker, and Coral hadn't asked why. Some part of them understood that she was listening to the system tremble the way she'd listened to it thirty years ago, from inside the beacon, when she cast the vote that scuttled it. Some part of them understood that whatever she was remembering forward, she was choosing her moment.

"Take the ugly line," Coral said.

* * *

The first hour of the Graveyard Proper was almost beautiful, in the way that disasters are beautiful from a survivable distance.

Wrecks rose around them like a drowned city. Hulls the size of cathedrals, split along seams no metal was meant to have. A Republic carrier, dead thirty years, its flight deck gaping open like a jaw, frozen escape pods still clustered around it like spawn. Concordat destroyers folded into shapes that told the story of their last second in a language of twisted titanium. Between the wrecks drifted the smaller dead — fighters, tugs, cargo pallets, a child's toy that had somehow survived every violence of the war and now tumbled end over end in the vacuum, patient as a message in a bottle.

Coral stood at the nav station with both hands flat on the console, feeling the ship respond to Yusuf's hands-through-hers, and discovered something alarming happening in their chest.

They weren't afraid.

Or — they were afraid, magnificently afraid, every nerve singing — but underneath the fear was something else, something with structure, something that felt horrifyingly like *joy*. Every instinct Yusuf fed to the thrusters, Coral felt arrive half a second early, felt the ship begin to lean into the turn before the turn existed. They were competent. They were accompanied. The wreck field that should have been a wall was, with a dead pilot whispering vectors, a corridor — narrow, insane, but real.

"Contact," Dez said from the sensor board. "Two contacts. Fast movers, inbound from the Shoal's southern margin. Drive signatures are—" they squinted, tapped, swore softly, "—Republic interceptors. Light frames. Scout configuration."

"They've seen us?"

"They've seen *something*. We're small and we're flying through a junkyard with our transponder answering as a navigation buoy, so honestly, who knows what they think we are."

"Keep it that way," Coral said. "Yusuf, can we lose them in the field?"

"I can lose anyone in the field," Yusuf said, and there it was again — the amusement, thin as wire over the abyss. "Question is whether the field loses you. Brace. Next sixty seconds are going to feel like being shaken by a god with opinions."

He took them down.

There was no other word for it. The tug frame dropped through a canyon between a shattered cruiser and its own detached engine section, rolled ninety degrees around a spinning coolant tank the size of a chapel, threaded the rigging of a wrecked tender whose cables still streamed behind it like kelp. Alarms sang. Something in the aft hold came loose and crashed. Coral's teeth clicked together hard enough to taste copper, and through all of it Yusuf narrated in a low, running murmur — *coming left, trust me, coming left — gap's tighter than it looks, it isn't — hold — breathe — now* — and the ship obeyed him, and Coral obeyed him, and the two Republic scouts fell further and further behind, their pilots unwilling to follow two lunatics into the collapsing throat of the Graveyard.

When the last interceptor signature faded off the board, nobody cheered. They all just breathed, together, in the sudden enormous quiet.

"That," Dez said finally, "was the single worst thing I have ever loved."

Coral opened their mouth to answer, and that was when the mother-core spoke.

She had been silent for hours. Her voice, when it came, came from the hold speakers — the ones wired to the sleeper racks, the ones Coral had installed themselves, alone, in another life — and it was quiet and clear and it stopped Coral's heart between beats.

"Coral. Come down to the hold. Please."

"What is it? What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong." A pause, and then, with a gentleness that frightened them more than any alarm could have: "I finished remembering. And you need to hear it before we get there — because there's something at the sender station that I don't think anyone else knows. Something your fleets are about to die finding out."

Coral looked at the course line stretching ahead of them, eleven hours of graveyard and gunfire between here and the waking machine. They looked at Amara, whose face had gone carefully blank in the way of a woman guarding a confession of her own. They looked down, through the deck plates, toward the hold where forty-one sleepers waited in the dark and one of them was no longer asleep at all.

"Yusuf," Coral said. "You have the ship."

"I always have the ship," said the dead pilot. "Go. Listen to her. We'll want every minute of what she remembers."

Coral released the console and went down the ladder into the dark, toward the voice that had raised them, toward the memory that had outlived its owner, while behind and above them the whole trembling system counted down toward its third and final nine seconds.
