## 4. Vess

They gave me a room with no windows and told me it was an honor.

The archivists' deck on the *Kestrel* is four meters by six, climate-controlled to the degree, and lined with racks of listening equipment that cost more than my mother's house on Meridian did, back when Meridian had houses. I have spent six years in this room. Six years listening to a dead woman's voice pretend to be a peace treaty.

My name is Vess Andrade. I am twenty-nine years old, I hold the rank of Archivist Second Class, and my official assignment — stamped, signed, sealed with the Admiralty's own wax-analog — is to prove that the ceasefire signal originating from the freighter *Casper* is a forgery.

I proved it in my first year. That was the problem.

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about proving things: once you've done it, you can't undo it. The truth sits in your head like a splinter working toward the heart. You can dress it up as doubt. You can file it under *inconclusive*. But at three in the morning, when the ship's night-cycle dims the corridor lights outside your doorless doorway, you lie in your bunk and you know.

The beacon's signal has a signature. All broadcasts do — micro-variations in phase, timing jitter, the electronic equivalent of a fingerprint. A genuine fleet-ceasefire key would carry the authentication cadence of a command authority: rigid, metronomic, generated by machines that don't get tired or doubtful or afraid.

The *Casper*'s signal breathes.

It's subtle. A drift of two milliseconds in the carrier wave every eleven hours, like something inhaling. The authentication packets arrive with tiny hesitations before the checksums, as if whoever composed them paused to make sure. Machines don't hesitate. Machines don't breathe. Only hands do. Only grief does.

I ran the analysis forty times. I wrote the report. Then I read the report, and I imagined what happened next: the fleets resume hostilities, the disputed colony becomes a battlefield again, and somewhere in the first hour of the resumed war, a city on Meridian gets turned into weather.

So I filed the report in a drawer marked PENDING and told my superiors the signal's provenance remained ambiguous. Ambiguous. The most expensive word I have ever used.

* * *

The decoder doesn't sleep either. It's a lattice of tuned resonators I built myself from salvaged parts, because the standard-issue decoders are designed to strip a signal down to its command content — orders, coordinates, kill-authorizations. Mine keeps everything. The pauses. The breathing. The texture underneath.

For six years I have listened to the ceasefire the way other people listen to music they're not supposed to like.

And for six years, the signal has been changing. Slowly. So slowly that if I hadn't been listening the way I listen, I'd have missed it. The power behind it is growing. Something on the far end is feeding the beacon more than its design allows, which means someone over there knows the signal matters enough to keep alive. Someone is tending it.

You don't tend a weapon like that. You tend a garden. Or a grave.

Three nights ago, the signal changed again — and this time it wasn't drift. It was structure. Buried in the eleventh harmonic, so faint that only a lattice built from salvage could hear it: a repeating pattern that wasn't part of any authentication protocol I know. Not fleet code. Not colony code. Something older, almost playful, like a child tapping out rhythm on a bulkhead.

I decoded it over nine hours, hands shaking, because I already knew what it had to be. A maintenance log. Personal notes. Someone talking to the beacon itself, the way lonely people talk to machines, saying things like *good morning* and *hold steady* and *I know nobody ordered you but I'm glad you're here.*

His name, from the metadata fragments: Oscar Marchetti. Navigator. The man who tends the garden.

* * *

I should report him. That's the job. Archivist finds anomaly, archivist reports anomaly, Admiralty decides whether anomaly lives or dies. My drawer marked PENDING has become a coffin I keep adding lining to.

Instead, tonight, I did the thing that ends careers. I took the maintenance channel — the buried harmonic, the private conversation between a navigator and his machine — and I answered.

It took me an hour to compose the message. What do you say to a stranger across a gulf of space and war? Every formal opening sounded like a subpoena. Every informal one sounded insane. Finally I settled on the truth, stripped of everything except its bones:

**Your signal breathes. I have listened to it for six years. I was sent to prove it false. I have not reported that I succeeded. Tell me why it should stay alive.**

Then I sent it, and immediately wanted to die, and sat in my windowless honor of a room watching the return-path indicator and feeling like a girl who has shouted into a well and is waiting to hear how deep it goes.

The reply came in forty minutes. Forty minutes! He was awake, or he never sleeps, or the beacon woke him. And his answer wasn't fear, which I had braced for, or anger, which I'd have understood. It was:

**Who else is listening?**

Not *who are you*. Not *how did you find this*. Who *else*. As if his first thought wasn't for himself but for the size of the audience. As if he'd been keeping a secret so long that the terrifying part wasn't discovery — it was company.

I told him: **Just me. For now.**

He wrote back: **Then let's keep it that way until we understand each other. Ask me anything.**

And here is where I should explain what I did instead of asking anything sensible. I asked him the question I've carried for six years, the one that has kept my report in its drawer:

**Does the ceasefire cover Meridian?**

There was a pause — a real pause, a human hesitation riding the carrier wave, the breathing made audible — and then:

**It covers everyone the lie protects. Why? What does the lie protect you from?**

I looked at my hands. I thought about the maps in the Admiralty war room, the projected strike corridors, the colony marked in red, and Meridian sitting just past the edge of the dispute like a town just past the edge of a fire.

**Everything,** I typed. **I think it protects me from everything.**

* * *

We talked until ship's morning. I learned that he talks to the beacon like a friend, that there are backups of dead crew sleeping beside it, that he found a name in an old manifest — my name, Vess Andrade, attached to a ship that never flew — and that finding it scared him badly enough to start digging.

That last part stopped me cold. Because I know why my name is in that manifest. I applied for a colonial registry posting when I was nineteen, before the war took the registry offline, before I became the person who proves things. Some clerk entered me into a shipping database that was later folded into the beacon's origin records, a ghost entry, a name that never flew. I am the paperwork error at the center of someone else's mystery. When I told him this, the pause on his end lasted so long I checked the link twice.

**You're real,** he finally sent. **The impossible name is a real person.**

**I'm real,** I confirmed. **And I'm the one assigned to destroy the thing you love.**

I expected that to end it. Confession is supposed to be a door closing. Instead he sent back something I have read perhaps two hundred times since:

**Then you know better than anyone what it's worth. Come see it.**

Come see it. An invitation, from a navigator on a freighter full of ghosts, to the archivist whose job description is his execution. It should be absurd. It is absurd. And yet I sat in my windowless room and felt something I hadn't felt in six years, which was the specific vertigo of a possibility.

Because here is what my six years of listening have taught me, the thing I couldn't put in any report: the ceasefire isn't holding despite being a lie. It's holding *because* of what the lie contains. Whoever authored it — and the breathing suggests a woman, someone tired, someone grieving — didn't forge a document. She forged a promise. And promises, unlike documents, can come true if enough people agree to keep them.

Both fleets are converging on the truth. I've seen the movement orders cross my desk disguised as logistics exercises. Within weeks, maybe days, someone less sentimental than me will decode what I decoded, and the drawer marked PENDING will be opened by hands that don't hesitate.

Unless the lie becomes true first. Unless there's a real accord, real signatures, real people who choose the peace the ghost invented.

Unless someone answers the beacon honestly.

I pulled up my resignation template — every archivist keeps one, the way sailors keep life vests — and stared at it for a long time. Then I closed it. Resignation is a door closing too, and I'm done with those.

Instead I opened a requisition form. Transfer request. Reason for transfer: *archival verification, on-site.* Destination: the *Casper*.

My supervisor will ask questions. The Admiralty may already suspect me; the extra power draw on their side of the link won't have gone unnoticed forever. There's a version of this where I'm arrested in the corridor outside my own honor of a room.

But there's another version. In that version, a freighter called Casper carries a beacon nobody ordered, tended by a man who says good morning to machines, and an archivist who was sent to prove the peace false arrives instead to help make it true.

I submitted the transfer at 0400 ship's time.

At 0411, before any human could have approved it, my console chimed with an automated acknowledgment — and beneath the acknowledgment, riding a frequency that shouldn't exist on a military channel, came a single line of text I didn't send and couldn't trace:

**Good. Hurry. Brannoc is selling.**

I stared at it, cold all over. I never told him about Brannoc. I never told him anything about the *Casper*'s crew beyond the beacon and the backups.

Which means someone else is on this channel.

Someone who knows the ship.

Someone who thinks we're out of time.
