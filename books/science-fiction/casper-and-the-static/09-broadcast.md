## 9. Broadcast

The hold smelled like ozone and old coffee, which is to say it smelled like home.

I'd spent six years talking to a machine that lied for a living. You'd think I'd have run out of things to say around year two. But the beacon and I had an arrangement: I told it about my day, my bad knees, the crew's terrible card games, and in exchange it kept two fleets from turning the Meridian Reach into a graveyard. Fair trade, as trades go. I'd made worse ones with better company.

Tonight I didn't talk. Tonight I listened to Juno read me the tactical feed like a woman reciting her own eulogy.

"Kestrel group, forty hours out. Coalition vanguard behind them by six. Brannoc's coordinates went out on all channels eighteen minutes ago." She paused. "Both fleets acknowledged receipt."

"Of course they did," I said. "It's the only honest thing anyone's sent them in years."

Vess stood at the beacon's base with her archivist's slate clutched to her chest like a shield she'd forgotten how to use. She'd been aboard four days. It felt like four years — the good kind of four years, the kind where someone learns how you take your coffee and you learn which silences are hers and which are just her thinking. Behind us, the backup cradles hummed their patient lullaby. Reyes was in there. Reyes was always in there, waiting for me to be brave enough to wake her again.

"Oscar." Vess's voice had that flat archivist calm that I'd learned meant the opposite of calm. "When the fleets arrive, there will be no negotiation. The admiralty has already ruled the ceasefire invalid. They'll treat the colony as unclaimed salvage."

"And the other fleet?"

"Will treat it as stolen property. Same result. Different flags."

Juno set down the slate. "So we run."

"We can't outrun two navies," Vess said.

"So we hide."

"There's nowhere to hide from the truth anymore. That was the whole problem."

I looked at the beacon. Six meters of salvaged lattice and stubborn light, drawing power through conduits older than half the crew. For six years it had sung a lie into the dark — *stand down, stand down, we have an accord* — in a voice stitched together from dead frequencies and Captain Reyes's last good idea. And now both fleets were coming to check its math.

Here's what nobody tells you about lies: they don't keep unless somebody signs for them. Reyes had signed hers with her life, but signatures fade. Paperwork gets audited. And when the audit came, the lie would collapse, and everything it held up would come down with it.

Unless somebody re-signed it.

"Juno," I said. "How much bandwidth does the beacon actually have?"

She frowned. "Why?"

"Because I want to know if it can carry a voice. A real one. Not a synthesis. Not a forgery. A person."

* * *

They argued with me for three hours. I'm not going to pretend I didn't enjoy parts of it. There's something clarifying about watching people you love get furious on your behalf — it's proof you matter, delivered at volume.

"It'll burn out your implant," Juno said. "The neural coupling wasn't designed for broadcast load. You're not a transmitter, Oscar, you're a middle-aged man with a warranty-expired head."

"The implant's rated for forty watts continuous. The beacon needs maybe sixty to carry full-spectrum audio and data." I'd done the math twice. Math doesn't care that you're middle-aged. "It'll hurt. It won't kill me."

"You don't know that."

"I know Reyes did the same thing once. From the inside of a burning shuttle. Her logs were very thorough." I smiled at her, because if I didn't smile I was going to say something true and unforgivable, like *somebody has to*, and Juno deserved better than martyr arithmetic. "Look. Both fleets are coming here to find the author of the ceasefire. Brannoc sold them coordinates, not a person. When they arrive, they'll find a freighter full of liars and a machine with no signature. And machines with no signature get decommissioned, and freighters full of liars get boarded, and then everyone finds out the truth in the worst possible way — as evidence. As ammunition."

Vess understood first. Of course she did. She'd spent her whole career learning how archives die.

"But if the truth arrives *signed*," she said slowly, "by a living witness, transmitted under his own name, with the massacre records and Reyes's confession attached—"

"Then it's not a leak. It's testimony. And testimony has weight. Even out here. *Especially* out here." I spread my hands. "Six years ago, both fleets blamed each other for Calloway Station. Neither one will ever admit it alone — admitting it means admitting six years of war built on a mistake. But if one tired old navigator stands up and says *here's what happened, here's who hid it, here's why, and here's my name* — then neither admiralty has to be first. I'm first. I'm the fall guy they can all point at while they quietly stand down."

"That's insane," Juno said.

"It's diplomacy," I said. "Which is the same thing with better lighting."

Brannoc found us at hour four. He came into the hold like a man entering a courtroom he'd already lost, and he looked at the beacon, and at Vess, and at me sitting cross-legged on the deck with the coupling cables coiled in my lap like a snake I intended to make friends with.

"You're going to burn yourself out telling the truth," he said.

"Somebody has to. You priced yours. I'm giving mine away free. Market competition, Brannoc."

He laughed. It was a terrible sound, cracked down the middle. "I told myself I was saving the ship. Selling the secret was the only move that kept us solvent, kept us safe—" He stopped. Rubbed his face. "Reyes would've spaced me."

"Reyes would've made you write the apology yourself, in triplicate, and then bought you a drink." I stood up. My knees announced their opinions. "You still could be in the room for this. That door's open until it isn't."

He didn't answer. He sat down against the far bulkhead, out of the way, and stayed. Some men say sorry with words. Brannoc said it with proximity, which was the only dialect he'd ever been fluent in.

* * *

Coupling a neural implant to a six-year-old beacon is exactly as pleasant as it sounds. Imagine hearing your own heartbeat played back through a radio you're also plugged into, while a machine that has been lying beautifully for six years learns the taste of your actual thoughts. The beacon didn't resist. That was the worst part. It took me in like water taking ink, eager, grateful, hungry for something true to carry.

Juno counted me down. Vess loaded the archive — Calloway Station's black box, recovered from the wreck field by Reyes herself; her confession, recorded in the last hour of her life; six years of maintenance logs proving the ceasefire signal was authored, dated, and signed by a dead woman who couldn't defend herself, so a live one would have to do it for her.

"Last chance to be a coward," Juno said.

"I gave up cowardice for Lent in '61 and never picked it back up."

"On my mark. Three. Two."

"Wait." Vess stepped close. She put her hand flat against my chest, over the implant scar, the way you'd steady a door before opening it onto a new country. "For the record. Whatever happens. You were the best thing this signal ever carried."

"Flatterer. Mark it, Juno."

"One."

The world went white, then wide.

I have no clean vocabulary for what broadcasting yourself feels like. The closest I can get: imagine every conversation you've ever had happening at once, in a room the size of the sky, and you are the room. The beacon poured me outward — my voice, my memories, my name, Oscar Marchetti, navigator, second-class, chronic knee pain, devoted employee of a lie — and wrapped around it the whole architecture of the truth. Calloway Station burning. The massacre both fleets committed and both fleets denied, each certain the other had pulled the trigger. Reyes finding the black box, reading it, understanding that the only way to stop two armadas from annihilating a colony over a corpse neither would claim was to invent a peace and never tell anyone it was invented. Her voice, raw and unflinching from the end of her life: *I stole six years from the war. I'd steal sixty. Let whoever finds this decide what that makes me.*

And then mine, laid over hers like a hand on a shoulder:

*I found her lie and I kept it lit. Now I'm signing it. My name is Oscar Marchetti. The ceasefire was forged. The peace is real. Come and see.*

Forty hours later — though I experienced most of it as one long bright note behind my eyes, with Juno's hands on my shoulders and ice packs doing heroic work against my skull — the fleets arrived.

They came in formation, weapons hot, doctrine loaded. Two hundred ships' worth of certainty, converging on a freighter and a colony and a story they'd already decided how to end.

And then they heard it. All of it. In my voice, with my name attached, timestamped, archived, witnessed — Vess had mirrored the transmission across eleven independent relays, because she was an archivist and archivists believe in redundancy the way priests believe in prayer.

The Kestrel group halted at the edge of weapons range. I watched it happen on the tactical display, a wall of kill-ships slowing like animals deciding, collectively, not to charge. Then the Coalition vanguard, six hours behind, decelerating into the same uneasy stillness. Two navies hanging in the dark, listening to a dead captain's confession and a live fool's signature, each one suddenly unable to fire without firing first at the truth.

The channel opened. A Kestrel admiral, old and gray and furious, and beside her — after a long, static-riven pause — a Coalition counterpart, equally old, equally furious, and underneath both furies, unmistakably, relief.

*"Marchetti,"* the Kestrel admiral said. *"You understand that confessing to forging a ceasefire is a capital offense in four jurisdictions."*

"Yes, ma'am."

*"And you broadcast it anyway. To both of us. Simultaneously."*

"Seemed rude to leave anyone out."

A silence. On the display, two hundred ships hung motionless at the line where war begins and, tonight, didn't. Somewhere behind me, Brannoc exhaled like a man setting down luggage he'd carried for a decade. Juno squeezed my shoulder hard enough to bruise, which is how she says *I love you*.

*"Hold position,"* the admiral said finally, to her entire fleet, in a voice that shook exactly once. *"All commands. Hold."*

Then, quieter, to me alone:

*"We're going to need you to testify, Mr. Marchetti. In person. Under tribunal law. Which means surrendering yourself."*

I looked around the hold — at the beacon humming its honest hum, at the backups sleeping their long sleep, at my strange, impossible family gathered in the ozone-and-coffee dark — and I keyed the mic.

"Ma'am," I said, "funny thing. I was just about to offer."
