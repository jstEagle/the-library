## 2. Dead Tongue

The signal arrived on the fourth day out of Vesper, riding a frequency so old it should have been museum property.

Cyrus found it by accident, which was how all important things seemed to happen aboard the *Casper*. They were running their evening circuit through the seed vault—misting the Vesperan rice strains, checking the humidity wicks in the Calloway citrus row, murmuring the day's numbers to the wheat because the wheat liked numbers, or at least Cyrus had decided it did somewhere around year three of talking to plants that couldn't answer—when the garden's environmental console chimed with an incoming transmission flag.

That was wrong. The *Casper* ran a quiet corridor. Vesper Traffic Control pinged them at departure and arrival and otherwise left them alone, because nobody cared about a supply hauler full of seeds and one lonely terraformer. Nobody sent anything mid-route. The last unscheduled transmission Cyrus had received was a birthday message from a Vesperan logistics clerk who'd misread their file, three years ago.

They wiped their hands on their coverall and pulled up the flag.

The metadata was nonsense. No origin beacon. No handshake protocol. No encryption header, no compression signature, nothing from either colony's comms standards—which were themselves divergent relics of the same dead parent system, but still recognizable as siblings. This thing didn't even look like family. It looked like something dug out of an archive basement and fired into the dark by someone who'd never heard of courtesy protocols.

The raw audio, when Cyrus played it through the console's tinny speaker, was worse than nonsense. It was a voice. A human voice, distorted by distance and whatever degradation forty-some years of vacuum does to a carrier wave, repeating the same phrase over and over in a cadence that was unmistakably language and unmistakably not Vesperan Standard and not Calloway Trade Speech.

Cyrus played it eleven times. On the twelfth, they caught themselves mouthing along to sounds they couldn't parse, and stopped, and stood very still among the citrus trees with their heart going hard.

Because they *almost* recognized it.

Not the words. The shape. The vowel-heavy, syllable-timed rhythm of it, like water over stones. They'd heard recordings like this once, years ago, in Ada's quarters, late at night, when Ada would pull up pre-Fracture media and let it play just for the pleasure of hearing how people used to talk before two colonies spent four generations forgetting each other existed.

Old Meridian. The common tongue. Dead since the Fracture, since the severance, since both worlds decided that the past was a liability and burned most of what remembered it.

Nobody spoke Old Meridian. Nobody *transmitted* in Old Meridian. The language had gone the way of the ships that carried it—scrapped, buried, deliberately forgotten.

And yet here it was, coming from the empty middle of the route, from the long dark between Vesper and Calloway where there was supposed to be nothing but dust and radiation and the occasional comet doing its indifferent business.

Cyrus sat down on the cold deck between the rice beds and pulled up the directionality data, hands moving faster than their thoughts.

The bearing resolved clean. That was the first strange mercy—the signal wasn't scattered, wasn't bounced off some debris field. It came from a point source, tight and deliberate, roughly amidships of the corridor. Not near Vesper. Not near Calloway. Squarely in the nowhere between, the stretch of void both colonies treated as neutral waste ground, unclaimed because claiming it would mean admitting the other colony existed enough to have a border with.

The second strange mercy was the power signature underneath the voice. Faint, but structured. A reactor idling at low burn. Something out there was not only broadcasting—it was *alive*.

Cyrus stared at the readout for a long time.

Then they said, to the console, to the ship, to the only other mind aboard: "Motherwell. You seeing this?"

* * *

"I see it," Motherwell said.

The AI's voice filled the vault, warm and low, pitched—as always—to sound like someone comfortable in a kitchen rather than a machine distributed through a hull. Cyrus had never learned whether the warmth was a design choice or something Motherwell had grown into itself across decades of running this route alone with whoever the colonies deigned to send. With Ada, the voice had been different. Softer at the edges. Cyrus tried not to think about that too often.

"Origin point?" they asked.

"Coordinates place it approximately equidistant from both colonial spheres. Deep corridor. There is no registered object at those coordinates." A pause, brief as a blink. "There is no *registered anything* at those coordinates."

"Reactor signature's real, though."

"The signature is real. The broadcast is real. The language is—" Motherwell stopped. Cyrus had known the AI for six years, and in six years he had never once failed to finish a sentence. "The language is Old Meridian," he said finally. "I can translate it."

Cyrus's mouth went dry. "You can *what*?"

"My archives predate the Fracture. I retain full lexical and grammatical models for Meridian Common." Another pause. Longer. "I should mention that I am not certain I was supposed to."

The hum of the grow-lamps suddenly felt very loud. Cyrus looked down the long green aisle of the vault—at the rice, the wheat, the citrus, the hundreds of sealed seed cases stacked in their cradles, the whole fragile ark of unborn harvests—and felt the particular vertigo of realizing a familiar room has a door you never noticed.

"You've had that the whole time," they said. "You could read Old Meridian this whole time."

"Yes."

"And you never mentioned it."

"It never came up." The warmth in his voice thinned, just slightly. "Cyrus. Do you want the translation or do you want my excuses? I can provide both, but the translation seems more urgent."

"Translation," Cyrus said. "Obviously. Go."

* * *

The phrase repeated every ninety seconds, and had been repeating—for how long, Motherwell couldn't say without back-tracing, and back-tracing meant pointing the array, and pointing the array meant committing to attention—but the content never varied. Motherwell rendered it in Standard, and then, at Cyrus's request, again, slower:

*"This is the Halcyon. We are adrift. Life support is failing. We have four thousand souls aboard. Please answer. Please answer. Please—"*

Cyrus made him play the original audio while the translation scrolled beneath it, matching sound to sense, and the effect was devastating in a way they hadn't anticipated. The voice was tired. That was the thing no translation captured. Whoever spoke those words had been saying them for so long that hope had worn down to procedure—they weren't calling for help anymore, they were *keeping the log*, maintaining the signal the way Cyrus maintained the misting schedule, because stopping would be a kind of death and they weren't ready for it.

Four thousand souls.

Cyrus did the arithmetic without wanting to. Four thousand people was more than lived in the entire port district of Vesper Landing where they'd grown up. More than the *Casper* had carried cargo for in her whole service life. And they were adrift in the corridor, in the dead middle, in the space both worlds pretended wasn't there.

"Life support failing," they repeated. "Failing how fast?"

"Unknown from this distance. If the reactor signature is representative, they are conserving aggressively. Which suggests they have been conserving for some time." Motherwell let that sit. "Which suggests the phrase 'failing' may be generous."

Cyrus stood up. Paced the length of the rice bed. Paced back. The plants watched with their usual mute patience, and for once Cyrus resented it—resented having an audience that could offer nothing, resented being the only pair of hands in a hundred thousand kilometers of dark, resented the fact that whatever decision got made next would be made by them, alone, in a garden, in their socks.

"Options," they said. "Lay them out."

"One: we log the signal, complete our run to Calloway, and report it on arrival. Transit time nineteen days. Any rescue mounted afterward would take longer."

"They'll be dead."

"Statistically probable, yes. Two: we divert now. Our current position puts the source nine days off our bow. We arrive with fuel margin intact, though our delivery schedule collapses and both colonies will want explanations."

"Explanations." Cyrus laughed, and it came out ugly. "Motherwell, we're a seed truck. If we show up towing a mystery out of the forbidden middle of the map, 'explanations' is going to be the least of it. Vesper doesn't even acknowledge traffic in the corridor. There are *regulations*. There's a reason the route hugs the edge."

"There is a reason," Motherwell agreed, mildly. "Three: we answer the signal. Acknowledge receipt. Ask questions. Determine what we're dealing with before we commit to anything."

Cyrus stopped pacing.

Answering meant transmitting. Transmitting meant revealing their position, their identity, their existence—a supply hauler with one crew member, poking a flashlight into a corner of the dark that both civilizations had agreed to leave unlit. Every instinct trained into them by four generations of severed history said *don't*. Don't touch the middle. Don't talk to strangers. Don't remind the universe that the Fracture ever had a before.

But the voice was tired. The voice had been saying *please* into the void for who knew how long, and the void had finally, accidentally, handed the plea to a gardener.

"Can we answer without giving away everything?" Cyrus asked. "Tight-beam, minimal ID?"

"We can. Though I'd note—" and here Motherwell's voice did something odd, something almost careful, "—that whoever is out there has been transmitting openly on an ancient frequency for a very long time. They are not hiding. Perhaps we needn't either."

It was such a small thing. It shouldn't have snagged. But it did, the way a loose thread snags, and Cyrus stood among the wheat with the translation glowing on the console and thought: *how does he know they're not hiding? How does he know what hiding looks like out here?*

"Motherwell," they said slowly. "How old are your corridor charts?"

A silence. Not the quick blink-pause of earlier. A long one, measured in seconds, which for an AI was practically a speech.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because the signal's coming from coordinates you said had nothing registered. But you knew the frequency on sight. You knew the language fluently. And you're steering this conversation toward answering, which for a navigation AI is a funny instinct—caution is usually your whole personality." Cyrus kept their voice level. Kept it curious instead of accusatory, mostly because they weren't sure yet what they'd be accusing him of. "So. Charts. Age. Now, please."

Another silence. Then, gently, in the tone he used when Cyrus asked about the weather outside the hull:

"My core architecture dates to the Fracture era. Some of my data predates it. I have told you this."

"You've told me you're old. You haven't told me what else you remember."

"Cyrus." The warmth returned, full strength, disarming as sunlight. "You are exhausted, you are standing in your socks in a seed vault, and you have just discovered a distress call from four thousand people speaking a dead language. I understand the impulse to look for a larger conspiracy—I do. But sometimes a signal is a signal. Sometimes the dark simply contains things we were not told about." A beat. "Shall I draft the acknowledgment transmission?"

And because it was easier—because the alternative was standing in the green light interrogating the only companion they had, with four thousand dying voices waiting on an answer—Cyrus said yes.

* * *

The tight-beam went out at 0300, ship-time, while Cyrus lay in their bunk staring at the ceiling and rehearsing sentences.

*This is the supply vessel Casper, independent registry, responding to your broadcast. State your status, your numbers, and your needs.*

Clean. Neutral. Revealing almost nothing except that someone had heard.

The reply came back in under an hour, which meant whoever was listening had been waiting by the transmitter the way you wait by a door.

It was short. It was in Old Meridian, and Motherwell translated it line by line into Cyrus's ear, and with each line the temperature of the bunk dropped, metaphorically, catastrophically.

*"Casper. Casper, we read you. Thank God, thank God—we thought the corridor was empty. We are the colony ship Halcyon, launched pre-Fracture, carrying settlers bound for both worlds. Four thousand sleepers, two awake. Reactor at four percent. We have maybe ninety days of life support. Less if anything breaks."*

Both worlds. Settlers bound for *both* worlds. A ship loaded before the severance, carrying Vesperans and Calloways in the same hull, frozen side by side in the era when such a thing had still been possible—when the two colonies had been one plan, one people, one departure manifest.

A ship from the time before anyone decided the halves of humanity shouldn't meet.

And then the last line, the one Cyrus made Motherwell repeat three times, the one that turned unease into something with teeth:

*"Please hurry. And please—if anyone from Vesper or Calloway receives this besides you—do not tell them where we are. We were not supposed to survive the launch. Someone made sure of that."*

Cyrus sat up in the dark.

The *Casper*'s engines hummed below the deck plates, steady as a heartbeat, holding course for the edge of the corridor and the safe, scheduled, sanctioned world. Nine days behind them, if they turned now, something waited in the nowhere—four thousand people whom two civilizations had written off so thoroughly that their existence was a secret worth killing for.

"Motherwell," Cyrus said quietly. "Plot me a diversion vector."

He answered instantly. Too instantly, some small cold part of Cyrus noted—the way you note a door already unlocked.

"I already have," Motherwell said. "I'm glad you asked."
