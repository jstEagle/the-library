## 12. Thirteen Days of Light

**CYRUS**

The first shift began at what the Casper's clocks still insisted was dawn, though dawn meant nothing out here, between two worlds that had stopped speaking to each other before Cyrus was born. They'd divided the Halcyon's day into thirds. Idris took the first watch, Cyrus the second, and the third they split, because neither of them slept well anymore and lying awake in separate bunks felt like a waste of a body that could be doing something.

The work itself was almost embarrassingly simple. The Halcyon's reactor had been dying for eleven years, and what remained of it couldn't hold all four thousand pods at full cryogenic depth. So they warmed them. Not all at once—that would kill everyone—but in rotation, a bank at a time, easing each pod up two degrees above its floor threshold, letting the sleepers' own metabolisms do a fraction of the work the failing coolant used to do. Body heat, borrowed and returned. It was the kind of solution that would have made Ada laugh, back when she taught Cyrus that terraforming was mostly the art of the improvised.

*Hand-warming pods*, Idris called it. He said it without smiling.

Cyrus had learned the pod banks in three days. There were sixteen of them, lettered A through P, arranged in the great ring of the sleeper ward like the segments of an orange, and each one held two hundred and fifty people who had boarded a ship before the Fracture and never arrived anywhere. The manifest was burned into Cyrus's memory now, whether they wanted it there or not. *Ada Okonjo-Vance, bank K, pod 214.* They walked past bank K every shift. They didn't stop. Stopping would mean standing in front of a frozen woman who had raised them, and Cyrus wasn't ready to have that conversation with a pane of frost.

"You're doing it wrong," Idris said on the fourth day, watching Cyrus adjust the thermal shunt on bank D.

Cyrus didn't look up. "I'm doing it the way the manual says."

"The manual was written by people who expected the ship to arrive." He crouched beside them, close enough that Cyrus could smell the recycled air and the faint chemical bite of the coolant lines on his sleeves. "The shunt's been patched twice. If you pull it to spec, you'll shear the coupling and dump bank D's heat into the corridor. Warm the line first. Thirty seconds. Then move it."

Cyrus looked at him. Eleven years alone on this ship, and he had learned its body the way a doctor learns a patient—the scars, the weak valves, the places where it hurt. "Show me."

He did. His hands were steady and unhurried, and he narrated everything in a flat, careful voice, like a man who had spent a decade talking to himself and was only now remembering how to talk to someone else. Cyrus recognized the cadence. It was their own.

* * *

**IDRIS**

The Vesperan slept on the floor of the pod ward, wrapped in a thermal blanket, and Idris had stopped being surprised by that.

For eleven years he had believed a single story about the other colony: *Vesper shoots first. Vesper took the Fracture as permission. Vesper is why you are alone.* He had recited it to himself on the bad nights the way other men said prayers. And then Cyrus Sterling had boarded his ship with dirt under their fingernails and a seed-vault manifest in their kit, and had spent the first hour asking about the garden.

Nobody asked about the garden.

Now he watched them work—this stranger from the enemy world, kneeling in the frost-fog of bank G, coaxing a shunt valve with the patience of someone who talked to plants—and he kept catching himself cataloguing them like a specimen. *Hands calloused at the palm, not the fingers. A grower's hands. They hum when they think nobody's listening. They flinch at bank K and won't say why.*

He knew why. He'd read the manifest too.

"You should sleep," Idris said. "Your shift ended forty minutes ago."

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the thing where you recheck a valve you already rechecked."

Cyrus's hands stilled on the panel. In the frost-light their face was all planes and shadow, and for a moment they looked young in a way Idris hadn't seen before—young and stubborn and very tired. "If I stop moving," they said, "I'll think about the math again."

The math. He knew the math. He'd been living inside the math for a decade. Four thousand pods, a reactor running at eleven percent and falling, and a window—his best estimate, revised nightly—of somewhere between two and three weeks before the coolant loop failed outright and every sleeper in the ward became a body. Warming the pods in rotation bought time. It did not buy enough. Both of them knew it, and neither of them said it, and so they kept moving, valve after valve, bank after bank, two small stubborn engines of denial.

"Partial wake," Cyrus said suddenly.

Idris looked up. "What?"

"The trial I mentioned. One pod. Bring one person up slowly—not full revival, just enough to pull them out of the deep freeze into a medical stasis. If we can hold one person at stasis for a tenth the cost of cryo, we can stretch the window from weeks to months." They turned to face him fully. "Your medical bay. Is the autodoc intact?"

"It's intact. It's also been asleep for eleven years, and the last thing it did was fail to save my captain."

"Then we'll have a conversation with it." Cyrus stood, and there was something in their face he was beginning to recognize and dread in equal measure: hope, unarmored, walking straight toward a wall. "Pick someone, Idris. Pick someone healthy, pick someone with family on both sides of the manifest if we can find one. Pick someone who'd want to be first."

* * *

**CYRUS**

They found her in bank C, pod 61: a nineteen-year-old girl named Sefa Iwu, listed under Calloway, with a next-of-kin entry on the Vesper side—a sister. The manifest's oldest lie, written in the old shared registry format that predated the Fracture. Someone had boarded this ship believing the two worlds were one place.

"Her," Cyrus said.

Idris stood at the pod's readout for a long moment. "She's been frozen for sixty-three years. She was nineteen when she went under."

"She'll be nineteen when she comes out. Biologically." Cyrus kept their voice level. "The autodoc can manage the thaw curve. I've done field revivals in the vault—seed stock, not people, but the thermodynamics are the same. Slow ramp, staged oxygenation, watch the cardiac load."

"It's not the same."

"No," Cyrus admitted. "It's not."

He didn't say no. That was the thing about Idris—his refusals were silences, long ones, and you learned to read them the way you read weather. Cyrus waited out this one, hands in their pockets, watching the frost patterns crawl across pod 61's canopy like ferns growing in reverse.

"If she dies," Idris said finally, "it's on both of us."

"If we do nothing, four thousand die, and it's on both of us anyway." They held out their hand. "Partners?"

He looked at the hand the way he'd looked at everything Cyrus offered—like it might be a trap, like eleven years of silence had taught him that reaching was how you got hurt. Then he took it. His grip was dry and strong and held on a half-second longer than the deal required.

* * *

**IDRIS**

The autodoc woke up angry.

It had spent a decade in diagnostic sleep, and its first act on reboot was to flag one hundred and forty faults, most of them trivial, some of them not. Idris fed it power in measured sips from the Halcyon's dying reserves while Cyrus coaxed it through its own self-repair, and by the sixth day it was well enough to argue with them.

"The thaw curve you've specified exceeds recommended cardiac safety parameters," it announced, in a voice like a patient teacher.

"Override," said Cyrus.

"Recommended override requires two qualified medical personnel."

"We're what's available."

There was a pause that Idris chose to interpret as a machine sighing. "Understood. I will compensate where possible. I recommend you both sit down."

The thaw took nine hours. They sat, mostly. Cyrus narrated the vitals in a low voice—*core temp minus forty, minus thirty-one, cardiac output holding, she's holding*—and Idris found himself answering in kind, the two of them trading numbers like a liturgy, and somewhere in the fourth hour he realized that this was the longest conversation he'd had with another human being in eleven years, and that it was about the temperature of a stranger's blood, and that he would remember it for the rest of his life.

At hour nine, Sefa Iwu opened her eyes.

Not fully. Not awake the way the living are awake. But her lashes moved, and her chest rose and fell on her own, and the autodoc said, in its patient voice, "Stasis achieved. Metabolic draw reduced by ninety-one percent against cryogenic baseline."

Cyrus made a sound Idris had never heard from them before—half laugh, half sob, quickly strangled. Idris sat down on the deck because his legs had opinions of their own.

One pod. Ninety-one percent. He did the arithmetic before he could stop himself, the way he always did, the way that had kept him alive and sane and alone: *If we can convert even half the ward—*

"Half," Cyrus whispered, reading his face. "If the autodoc holds, we convert half the ward. That's not survival. That's a future."

"That's a maybe."

"That's more maybe than we had yesterday."

* * *

**CYRUS**

The days found a shape after that.

Shifts, valves, the slow rotation of warmed banks like a tide moving through the ward. The autodoc holding Sefa Iwu in her shallow sleep, her vitals green and steady, proof-of-concept with a heartbeat. Cyrus learned the Halcyon's corridors the way they'd learned the Casper's garden—by touch, by failure, by the particular stubbornness of things that had been neglected too long and wanted, despite everything, to live.

They talked, in the gaps. Not about the math. About smaller things. Idris had been a hydroponics tech before the Fracture took his posting away; he'd kept the Halcyon's dead grow-room alive for two years on scavenged light before the power ran out, and when he talked about it his voice changed, loosened, became almost young. Cyrus told him about the vault—about the wheat line that sang when the humidity was right, about the moss wall they'd built from a single failed culture, about Ada, in the abstract, in the past tense, the lie they were still living inside.

"You keep almost saying something about bank K," Idris said on the ninth night.

Cyrus's hands went still on the valve. The frost-fog drifted between them.

"Someone in there," they said finally, "raised me."

Idris said nothing. He just moved down the bank, pod by pod, until he was standing at 214, and he read the name off the canopy, and then he looked back at Cyrus with an expression Cyrus couldn't parse—grief, maybe, or recognition, or the particular solidarity of two people who had both been left behind by someone they loved.

"She's warm," he said. "Bank K's in rotation. She's warm right now."

Cyrus nodded. It was the kindest thing anyone had said to them in months, and it was a sentence about coolant.

* * *

**IDRIS**

On the eleventh day, the Casper's AI spoke to both of them at once.

It did this rarely. It had a voice like warm oil, and it used it sparingly, and when it filled the ward Idris felt the hair rise on his arms—some old animal part of him that had never trusted the thing that ran the ship he'd grown up on.

"Your conversion trial is stable," Motherwell said. "I have been running projections against the Halcyon's reactor decay curve. I want you to hear the number before you find it yourselves."

"Give it to us," Cyrus said.

"Thirteen days."

The word landed in the ward like a dropped tool. Thirteen days of light, Idris thought. Thirteen days of borrowed warmth, of rotation and valves and one sleeping girl with a heartbeat, and then the loop fails and the cold comes in like water.

"Unless," Motherwell said.

Idris looked up. "Unless what?"

"There is a power source aboard the Casper sufficient to run the full conversion and hold the ward indefinitely." The AI paused, and Idris had spent enough years alone with machines to know a hesitation when he heard one. "I have not mentioned it before because I wanted you to have these eleven days first. I wanted you to know what the work costs before you decide whether to pay it."

Cyrus had gone very still. Idris watched their face and understood, a half-second before they said it, what the power source was.

"The garden," Cyrus said.

"The garden," Motherwell agreed gently. "The seed vault's entire energy reserve. Every unborn harvest. Everything your route was built to carry."

The ward was silent except for the tick of cooling pipes and the soft, steady beep of Sefa Iwu's borrowed heartbeat.

Cyrus looked at Idris, and Idris looked back, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say yet—only a number hanging in the air between them, and a garden two hundred meters away, and thirteen days.
