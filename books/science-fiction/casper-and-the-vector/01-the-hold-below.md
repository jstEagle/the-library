## 1. The Hold Below

The corridor lights of the Casper dimmed to night-cycle amber at 2200 hours, and like every night for eleven years, Isandro Sandoval waited until the ship had settled into its creaking quiet before he began his walk.

It wasn't regulation. Nothing in the Casper's operating manual—such as it was, a water-stained binder Odile had salvaged from a decommissioned freighter two decades back—required a navigator to patrol the lower decks after shift. It wasn't even sensible. The cryo-hold was sealed, monitored, and utterly inert, two hundred pods of sleeping colonists who had been sleeping, as far as anyone knew, since before Isandro had learned to walk. Nobody needed him there. Nobody down there needed anything at all.

He went anyway.

The corridor to the hold ran the length of the ship's spine, past the galley where Sunn left her medic's kit charging overnight, past the machine shop where Odile's soldering iron still smelled of hot flux even in the dark, past the little observation blister where Bek kept a chair angled toward the stars like a man waiting for someone to arrive. Isandro moved through the amber dark on soft-soled boots, and the ship breathed around him—the tick of thermal contraction in the hull plating, the whisper of air scrubbers, the faint subsonic hum of the drive idling down for the night. After eleven years he knew every voice the Casper had. He knew which creaks meant the ship was happy and which meant she was complaining, and tonight she was happy, riding a gentle supply orbit above Meridian with the patience of an old dog waiting at a gate.

The hatch to the cryo-hold was cold under his palm. It always was. The hold kept its own climate, its own season—a deep, dry winter maintained at a cost the colony's accountants had once grumbled about and the colony's dead now paid without complaint. The hatch seal parted with a sigh, and the air that breathed out at him was the air of a place outside time: sterile, faintly metallic, carrying the ghost of antifreeze and something almost floral, the preservative compounds doing their patient work.

Two hundred pods. He didn't need to count them. He counted them anyway.

He started at the port bulkhead, as he always did, and walked the rows slowly, letting his fingertips trail an inch above the pod lids without touching—some old superstition he'd never examined and never abandoned. The pods were frosted along their seams, each one glowing with a soft blue telltale that had not changed color in eleven years. Each pod bore a name stenciled at its foot, and each name had a number, and the numbers ran from one to two hundred in the order the colonists had been loaded, back when Meridian had still believed it had a future.

"Evening, Adeyemi," he murmured to pod one. "Evening, Okafor." Pod two. "Evening, Lindqvist." Pod three.

It was a rosary. He knew that. He'd grown up on the stories his grandmother told about the old faiths, the beads moved through the fingers one by one, each one a prayer, each one a small act of attention paid to something vast and indifferent. He'd never believed in any god, but he believed in this: that the dead and the sleeping deserved to be counted. That attention was a kind of love, and love was a kind of duty, and duty was the only scripture Isandro Sandoval had ever kept.

* * *

The Casper was not a beautiful ship, and Isandro loved her the way you love a scarred old animal—completely, without illusion.

She'd been built as a heavy orbital tender, back when the Meridian colony was still a going concern, back when Earth still sent out regular convoys with the confident regularity of a heartbeat. She was a hundred and forty meters of utilitarian hull, her flanks patched with mismatched plating from a dozen wrecks, her spine bristling with cargo clamps and sensor masts and one antenna array Odile had welded on as an afterthought that had somehow become load-bearing. Her drive was older than Isandro. Her galley table had a burn in the shape of a hand that nobody would explain to him when he'd come aboard, and he'd long since stopped asking.

He'd signed on at nineteen, hungry and half-orphaned and desperate for a berth on anything leaving Earth's gravity well. He'd told himself it would be for a year, maybe two. Just long enough to see the deep dark, to earn real wages, to become someone the boy back in Cebu port would barely recognize.

Eleven years later, the Casper's crew manifest read like the last line of a joke: one navigator who talked to frozen strangers, one engineer who'd been drummed out of three shipyards for refusing to sign off on work she considered criminal, one pilot who'd lost his license on Earth and won it back in the only court that mattered—the black—and one medic who'd walked away from a hospital the day the hospital's owners decided which patients could afford to keep breathing. Four people who had, each in their own way, been too much trouble for the worlds that made them. Four people who had made a family out of a freighter because the alternative was being alone in a universe that had shown them exactly how much it cared.

They were running supplies to a dead colony. That was the joke beneath the joke. Meridian had died—or gone quiet, which amounted to the same thing out here—forty years ago, its domes dark, its comms silent, its last transmissions a tangle of administrative boilerplate that ended, mid-sentence, in the middle of a budget report. And yet the contracts still existed, the supply runs still got scheduled, and some automated system on the far side of the light-years still cut payment orders to the Casper's account every time Odile docked and offloaded. Nobody had ever investigated where the payments came from. Bek had a theory involving a clerical error that had achieved immortality through sheer bureaucratic momentum. Odile thought it was a dead man's trust fund. Sunn said it didn't matter, and she was right; the credits cleared, the food was real, and the Casper kept flying.

But the colony hadn't been entirely empty. That was the thing Isandro had learned his first year, the thing that had started the walks, the counting, the rosary.

Beneath Meridian's dead domes, in a vault carved into the colony's bedrock, sat the cryo-hold: two hundred colonists in suspension, loaded during the colony's final crisis and never—so far as any record showed—woken. The original contract had included their maintenance. Power, coolant, monitoring: the Casper's crew kept the hold alive on every visit, checked the pods, replaced the failing components, logged everything in reports that went into the void and were never answered.

Two hundred sleepers, and no one left awake to wake them. The colony's survivors had scattered or died. Earth had stopped asking. The hold had become a tomb that was also, impossibly, not a tomb—the sleepers were alive, their hearts slowed to four beats a minute, their cells patiently repaired by machines that had outlasted everyone who'd built them.

* * *

Isandro finished the first row and turned at the aft bulkhead, beginning the second. Pod fifty-one, Hargreave. Pod fifty-two, Ibarra. The frost patterns on the lids were different every visit—delicate ferns of ice that grew and sublimated and grew again, never the same twice, so that the hold was a gallery of two hundred small, changing artworks that no one but him ever saw.

He had names for some of them. Not their real names—their stenciled names were enough for that—but the names his mind had given them over eleven years of nightly attention. The woman in pod seventy-four, whose file said she'd been a botanist, he thought of as the Gardener. The man in pod 112, a structural engineer, was the Builder. There was a cluster of pods near the hold's center, smaller than the rest, and those he could never think of as anything but the children.

Thirty-one of them. He'd counted the children more carefully than the rest, though he'd never let himself examine why. Their pods were scaled down, their telltales a slightly different blue, and their names were stenciled in the same institutional font as everyone else's, which struck Isandro as a small obscenity—the bureaucracy of the manifest reaching even into the cradle.

The children had been loaded last. Their numbers ran from 170 to 200. Whatever crisis had emptied Meridian's domes, someone had made sure the youngest went into the ice before the vault doors sealed. Someone had believed, at the end, that they would come back for them.

Nobody had come back. Nobody but a supply ship with a navigator who couldn't stop counting.

He reached the children's row and paused, as he always did, his hand hovering over pod 187. The stencil read VANCE, T. The telltale glowed its patient blue. Four heartbeats a minute. Eleven years of them.

"I know," he said quietly, to the frost, to the small shape beneath it. "I know. We're still here."

* * *

The trouble with a rosary, Isandro thought, climbing the ladder back up the spine, is that eventually you finish it. And then you have to decide what the prayer was for.

He emerged into the galley, expecting it to be empty, and found Sunn sitting at the table in the dark, a mug of something steaming in front of her and the burn-shaped scar under her palm, her face lit faint blue by the tablet she was reading.

"You're doing it again," she said, without looking up.

"Doing what."

"Your walk. Your little midnight mass." She finally looked at him, and in the low light her expression was hard to read—affection and concern ground together into something blunt. "Eleven years, Iso. You know I can hear the hold hatch from my bunk."

"Then you know I only look. I don't touch."

"I know you count." She pushed the second mug—there was always a second mug; she'd made it before he arrived, which meant she'd been waiting, which meant this wasn't casual—across the table toward him. "Two hundred. Same as last night. Same as every night."

"Two hundred," he confirmed, and sat, and wrapped his hands around the mug. Real tea. She saved the real tea for nights like this, and he'd never figured out what made a night qualify.

"Power draw from the hold was up point-three percent this cycle," she said. "Odile flagged it. Probably nothing. Probably a coolant pump getting old, same as the rest of us."

"Probably."

"But you'll go down tomorrow and check every pod anyway."

"Every pod."

She nodded, satisfied, and looked back at her tablet, and for a while the only sounds were the ship breathing and the two of them drinking tea in the amber dark, a family of two in the small hours, keeping vigil over nothing and everything.

"I signed on for a year," Isandro said, finally. It was an old sentence between them, worn smooth as a river stone. He said it maybe twice a year, and she always gave the same answer.

"You signed on for the sleepers," Sunn said. "The year was just the door you came in through."

* * *

He logged the walk before bed, as he always did. It was the last entry in his personal log, a habit so old it had become indistinguishable from breathing. The log was his alone—never filed, never shared, eleven years of nightly entries stored in a private partition that Odile could probably crack in ten minutes and had never once tried to.

*Personal log, Isandro Sandoval, navigator, CS Casper. Night 4,017 of Meridian station-keeping.*

*Walked the hold. All two hundred accounted for. Telltales nominal. Power draw up a fraction of a point—will inspect coolant loop tomorrow with Odile. The frost on 187 was shaped like a wing tonight. Told him goodnight.*

*Eleven years. I keep waiting for the night the counting feels like enough. I keep waiting to walk those rows and feel peace instead of this—this low ache, this debt with no creditor. Two hundred people are alive down there and no one is coming. I am the only prayer they have, and I am not enough, and I go down every night anyway because the alternative is to admit what we all agreed not to say out loud: that we are keeping a graveyard warm and calling it mercy.*

*Bek says the payments will stop someday. Sunn says the tea will run out. Odile says the ship will outlast all of us out of pure spite. And I say: two hundred. Same as last night. Same as tomorrow.*

*Amen.*

He closed the log and lay in his bunk in the dark, listening to the Casper breathe, and was nearly asleep when the ship's comm chimed with the soft tone reserved for incoming data packets—routine traffic, the daily drip of manifests and maintenance bulletins that Odile routed to his queue for morning review.

He almost let it wait. Eleven years of routine said let it wait.

Instead he reached out and thumbed the queue open, one eye already closed, and the first line of the top file scrolled past in the dark: *CARGO MANIFEST CROSS-CHECK, CS CASPER / ARCHIVE SYNC 7-DELTA.*

He'd run that cross-check a hundred times. It was housekeeping. It was nothing.

He queued it for morning, closed his eyes, and told himself the small cold feeling at the base of his skull was just the tea wearing off, just the hold's winter still clinging to his skin, just an old ship settling around an old man in the dark.

The archive sync would keep until morning.

It had no idea, and neither did he, that it wouldn't.
