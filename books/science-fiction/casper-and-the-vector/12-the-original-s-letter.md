## 12. The Original's Letter

The vault breathed cold.

Mara felt it before she saw it—that exhalation of preserved air rising through the flooded archive's lower levels, carrying the mineral smell of water that had not moved in decades and the fainter, stranger scent of paper kept dry against all odds. Meridian's archive vaults descended in concentric rings beneath the governor's residence, each ring sealed behind a door thick enough to survive a hull breach, and the deepest ring—the one the colony's own records barely admitted existed—had been drowned on purpose. Someone had opened the coolant lines into the sub-basement and let the black water rise to the ceiling of the corridor beyond, so that anyone who wanted what lay inside would have to go down into it, blind, breathing recycled air from a suit that smelled of Isandro's shoulder and old coffee.

She went down anyway.

Her helmet lamp carved a cone through water the color of steeped tea. Silt hung suspended around her like a slow snowfall, turning lazily when her boots disturbed it, refusing to settle. Somewhere above her, muffled by thirty meters of stone and floodwater, she could hear Isandro's voice on the comm—steady, unhurried, narrating his own progress along the upper gallery because he knew the dark made her nervous and he had decided, without ever saying so, that his voice would be the thing she held onto.

"—crossing the second catwalk now. There are filing cabinets down here, Mara. Actual paper. Somebody drowned an entire library rather than let it be read."

"Governor Achebe," Mara said. Her voice sounded small inside the helmet. "The seal order carries her countersignature. She didn't just permit it. She *wrote* it."

A pause on the line, long enough for the water to tick against her visor.

"You still want to open it?"

"I was copied without my consent," Mara said. "I'd like to know if the woman I came from at least left instructions."

* * *

The innermost door was a slab of composite alloy with the governor's seal cast into its face: a circle bisected by a plow-line, the emblem of a colony that had promised its settlers they would never again be tenants of anyone else's future. The irony of that emblem, given everything, sat in Mara's chest like swallowed ice.

Isandro joined her at the threshold, his lamp beam overlapping hers, their two cones of light making one bright wedge against the door. Up close she could see the corrosion where the waterline had licked at the seal for years, green-white blooms of it creeping across the plow-line like frost deciding whether to become a garden.

"The lock is mechanical," he said, running a gloved hand along the seam. "No power, no authentication handshake. Just—" He stopped. "Mara. Look at the keyway."

She looked. Set into the center of the seal, where a keyhole should have been, was a recess shaped like nothing mechanical at all: a shallow depression, palm-sized, ringed by a lattice of contact filaments fine as spider silk. She had seen that geometry before. Every sleeper in the cryo-hold wore it printed on the back of their neck—a biometric interface, a way for the pods to read the minds they were keeping.

"It doesn't take a key," Mara said slowly. "It takes a person."

"Or a copy of one." Isandro's voice had gone quiet in the particular way it did when he was trying not to frighten her, which always frightened her more. "Your implanted memories. The ones you said bled through when you woke. If the governor built this lock to answer only to herself—"

"Then it might answer to me." Mara raised her hand and stopped, fingers trembling in the lamplight. "Isandro. What if it reads me and decides I'm not her? What if it reads me and decides I *am*?"

He turned in the water to face her fully. Through two visors she could only see the shape of him, the broad dark outline of a man who ferried supplies past sleeping strangers for eleven years and counted them like a rosary every night, and who had never once, in all that time, asked anything of the universe except that someone notice what he was guarding.

"Then either way," he said, "you'll know."

* * *

The interface took her palm the way water takes a falling stone.

There was no pain. There was something worse: recognition. A sensation like being *read*—every filament in the lattice waking against her skin, and behind them, waking in answer, the dormant architecture of her borrowed mind unfolding layer by layer for inspection. Mara stood in the flooded dark and felt herself opened like a letter, and the hand doing the opening was gentle, and that gentleness was somehow the most terrible part.

Memories surfaced that were not hers.

*A garden on the residence roof, real soil hauled up at ruinous expense, and a girl of nine kneeling in it with dirt to her elbows while her mother explained why the tomatoes had failed.* — *A lecture hall on Earth, years later, where a young woman named Amara Voss-Achebe sat in the third row and listened to a man describe neural cartography: how a self could be mapped, compressed, stored.* — *A hospital corridor, white and endless, and a doctor saying* terminal *in the soft voice people use for words they hope you won't hear.*

And then a room she recognized with a lurch that had nothing to do with the water: the cryo-hold itself, seen new and gleaming, two hundred pods standing in their ranks like a choir waiting for a cue—and Amara Voss-Achebe walking down the aisle between them, dying by inches, touching each pod as she passed.

*They agreed,* the memory insisted. *They signed. They understood that a backup is not a resurrection, it's a seed. They understood—*

But underneath the insistence ran another current, older and quieter, and Mara sank into it the way she had sunk into the floodwater: willingly, holding her breath.

*Amara, age six, asking her mother what happens to the light when you turn off the lamp. Amara, age nineteen, reading the consent forms her mother drafted and finding clause fourteen and reading it eleven times. Clause fourteen: the backups will retain no claim upon the colony's resources, records, or future. Clause fourteen: the backups are archives, not heirs. Amara, age twenty-four, signing her own death warrant and her own afterlife in the same signature, and going to bed that night unable to remember which document she had been signing.*

The lock released with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

The door swung inward on darkness and dry air, and the first thing that touched Mara's face was warmth—warmth, after the cold of the vault, warm as a hand on a fevered forehead—and the second thing was dust, rising in the lamplight like a congregation standing to sing.

* * *

Inside was a single room, small, round, lined floor to ceiling with storage cells. Most were empty. Whatever the governor had sealed away, she had also, somewhere in the colony's last years, come back and taken most of it out again—or destroyed it; the scorch marks on three of the cells suggested destruction. But one cell remained intact, centered on the far wall at the height of a human heart, and it held a single object:

A letter. Paper. Handwritten.

Mara lifted it out with both hands, the way Isandro handled the fragile cargo manifests, the way he handled everything that could still be lost. The ink had faded to the brown of old tea but every word stood sharp, written in a hand she knew intimately, because it was her own—or nearly. The original's hand was surer, more slanted, worn smooth by decades of use where Mara's was still new-minted. Same bones, though. Same architecture. Looking at it was like looking at her reflection in water instead of glass.

Behind her, Isandro removed his helmet. She heard him breathe the vault's dead air without complaint, heard him settle onto the floor beside the door with his back against the wall—not leaving, not crowding. Making himself furniture. Making himself a wall she could lean against if she needed to.

She broke the seal. It was wax, actual wax, pressed with the plow-line emblem, and it cracked like a knuckle.

*To whoever wakes her—*

The handwriting wavered on the first line, then steadied.

*If you are reading this, then the seal failed, or you broke it, or you are her—in which case, hello. This is strange to write. I keep starting over. My mother would say I'm being sentimental. My mother sealed this room, so perhaps her opinion can wait.*

*I am dying. That isn't the confession; everyone on this colony is dying, we just do it at different speeds. The confession is this: I helped build the hold, and I do not know if what I built was mercy or theft.*

*You have to understand what we believed. Earth was going quiet—we could feel it even then, the transmissions getting shorter, the loops getting lazier, a whole civilization winding down like a toy left running in an empty room. Meridian was supposed to be the answer: a colony that would outlive its parent. And the hold was our insurance policy. Two hundred copies of two hundred colonists, frozen against the day the originals failed. We told ourselves it was hope. We told ourselves a lot of things.*

*Clause fourteen was mine. I wrote it. I wrote it because I was afraid—not of the copies, but of what we'd owe them. Because if a copy wakes and remembers being me, then she'll want my life. My records. My name on the residency rolls. And some part of me, some small and cowardly part, could not bear the thought of a second Amara Voss-Achebe standing in my kitchen, sleeping in my bed, loving the people I loved. So I legislated her out of existence. I called her an archive. I made her a thing that could be owned.*

*Then I got sick, and I had time—you get so much time at the end—and I thought about the girl in the pod with my face, and I understood what I had done. I had looked at my own possible future and locked it in a basement.*

*So here is what I can give you, whoever you are. It isn't much. It's the only thing I have left that no committee can countermand.*

*Permission.*

*You are not me. Please believe that, please build your whole life on it. You did not sign clause fourteen. You did not fail the tomatoes. You did not sit in the third row of Dr. Okafor's lectures pretending not to cry. Those were mine. They are finished. Whatever memories the instantiation gave you, they are seeds, not sentences—they are where you start, not where you end. You owe my mother nothing. You owe the colony nothing. You owe me nothing, least of all my griefs.*

*Be someone new. That's the whole letter. I've started it nine times and it keeps coming down to those three words, so I'll stop fighting them.*

*Be someone new. Choose your own name if mine sits wrong on you. Love whom you choose. Distrust committees. Plant real tomatoes.*

*And if there is a man—I don't know why I think there's a man, maybe I'm writing to a woman, maybe I'm writing to no one—but if there is someone who found you, who walked you out of the dark: thank him for me. Thanking him is the one selfish thing left in this letter, because I would have liked to be found too.*

*With more hope than I expected to have,*

*Amara Voss-Achebe*
*Governor's daughter. Formerly you.*

* * *

Mara read it twice. Then a third time, aloud, because Isandro deserved to hear it and because her voice needed something to do besides shake.

When she finished, the vault was very quiet. The water in the corridor outside lapped at the doorframe with the patience of geological time. Isandro hadn't moved from his place against the wall, but his helmet was in his lap and his eyes were wet, and he wasn't hiding it, and that undid her more than the letter had.

"She forgave me," Mara said. "Before I existed. She sat in this room, dying, and she forgave a person who hadn't been born yet."

"Not forgave." Isandro's voice was rough. "Released. There's a difference. Forgiveness means you owed her something. She's telling you that you never did."

Mara looked down at the letter in her hands—at the faded ink, the wavering first line, the sure final signature—and felt something shift in her chest that she had no vocabulary for yet. All her life since waking, she had carried her origin like a verdict: *copy, duplicate, echo, theft.* She had flinched from mirrors. She had signed ship's logs with a hesitation she pretended was modesty. And all that time, folded in a flooded basement beneath a drowned library, the woman she came from had been waiting to tell her the opposite of everything she feared.

*Seeds, not sentences.*

"Isandro." She wiped her face with the back of her glove, smearing tears across the visor seal, absurd and unbothered. "The distress record. The one the governor sealed. It's not just about the sleepers' consent, is it? If Amara changed her mind at the end—if she wrote *this*—then the record in the outer vault, the official version, contradicts her. Which means the official version is a lie somebody maintained after she died."

Isandro was already on his feet. She watched the thought land in him the way weather lands on open water—his whole posture changing, the navigator in him taking bearings.

"The hold's revival keys," he said slowly. "They authenticate against the colony's legal registry. Against clause fourteen. If the true record says the sleepers were backed up *against* their consent—if it says the copies are heirs, not archives—"

"Then the keys aren't just broken," Mara said. "They're pointed at the wrong law. And whatever Earth's last transmission loop sends down in eighty-one days—"

She stopped. The letter trembled in her hands.

In the lamplight, on the yellowed page, she saw it plainly for the first time: a date in the lower corner, written in the same fading ink as everything else. Not the date the letter was composed.

The date it was *sealed*—the date Governor Achebe had locked this room and drowned the stairs.

Three days from now, the manifest had said. Three days from now, the Calypso would fly. Mara Voss's name, dated three days ahead of itself, on a ship that never existed.

The letter was dated sixty-two years ago.

And beneath the date, in ink so fresh it still shone faintly in the lamplight, someone had added a single line that neither Amara's hand nor any pen on Meridian could have written:

*SHE IS AWAKE. THE LOOP KNOWS. HURRY.*
