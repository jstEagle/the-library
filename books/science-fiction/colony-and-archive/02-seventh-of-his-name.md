## 2. Seventh of His Name

The maglev to Corvane took eleven hours, and Silas spent every one of them rehearsing.

Not the interview — the interview was a formality, same as it had been six years ago and would be six years from now. Name, designation, occupation, biometric confirmation. The renewal of an identity license was the most boring ritual in colonial law, designed to be survived rather than experienced. What Silas rehearsed was smallness. How to sit. How to answer in flat sentences. How to be a beacon technician from nowhere, a man whose file was so thin it was practically a rumor.

Because Silas had done something stupid before boarding the train. Silas had run the manifest entry again, forty times on the ride south, and had copied it onto a data shard tucked against the sternum, under three layers of cold-weather weave. A ship that was never built, carrying passengers who were never born, logged by a beacon that no record said existed. You did not walk into the Office of Records carrying that. You walked in carrying nothing but your own tired face and a license chip, and you hoped the anomaly stayed where anomalies belonged: out here, at the edge of the map, where nobody looked.

Kestrel's Landing shrank behind the train like a white scar healing over. Silas watched it go and felt the strange double grief that had become familiar in recent days — grief for leaving, and grief for how little there was to leave. One man. One tower. One light blinking into a sky that had stopped answering thirty years ago.

*You are Silas Dravenmoor,* the rehearsal went. *Beacon keeper, second class. Stationed at Kestrel's Landing since your majority. No dependents. No travel history beyond this trip and one supply run to Halvard Station.* The facts were true. That was the trick of a good cover, if it could be called a cover when you were only hiding from yourself: the truth, carefully pruned, made the best lie.

The capital rose out of the tundra like a fist of glass and basalt. Corvane had been built fast and angry during the war years, all vertical lines and defensive geometry, and even now, with the ceasefire holding its fragile decade, the city still looked like it expected to be shot at. Directorate banners hung from the transit spires — the blue-and-grey of Earth's authority, legal on colonial soil by treaty, tolerated by everyone the way you tolerate weather.

Silas hated them. That hatred surprised him each time, surfacing clean and hot from somewhere below thought. He had no reason to hate the Directorate. He'd never been conscripted, never lost anyone to the war. But the banners made his jaw tighten and his hands curl, and he had learned, over eleven hours, not to examine why.

Some things you don't examine. Some things you just keep maintained, like a beacon.

* * *

The Office of Records occupied the bottom nine floors of the Archival Spire, and it smelled the way all government buildings smelled: ozone, recycled air, and old paper that nobody had the heart to burn. Silas surrendered his coat, his sidearm — ceremonial; he'd never fired it at anything but ice — and his dignity at three successive checkpoints, and was finally funneled into a waiting hall where two hundred colonists sat under grey light, renewing their right to exist.

His number came up in ninety minutes. Cubicle 44. A clerk with kind eyes and a scanner wand.

"Name?"

"Silas Dravenmoor."

The wand touched the license chip. The clerk glanced at the readout, then glanced again, and something in her posture changed — a small straightening, the way people sit up when they realize they're standing in deeper water than they thought.

"Dravenmoor," she repeated. "One moment."

"It's a common name in the colonies," Silas said, which was true, and also a lie, because it wasn't common, it was *founding*, and everyone knew what it meant the way everyone knows what a storm front means without being able to say precisely.

She didn't smile back. She pressed a stud under her console, and the wall panel beside the cubicle irised open onto a private lift.

"You'll need to come with me," she said. "Your renewal requires secondary review."

"I've renewed twice before. There's never been—"

"Secondary review," she said again, gently, and Silas understood that the conversation was already over, had perhaps been over since the moment the wand touched the chip. He stepped into the lift. It rose for a long time.

* * *

The Archivist-General's office was at the top of the Spire, above the clouds, in a room walled entirely in glass. Below, Corvane spread out in its grid of light and smoke. Beyond the city, the ice plain ran white to the horizon — the same plain, Silas realized with a lurch, that ran north to Kestrel's Landing. From up here the beacon's territory didn't look like a home. It looked like exile.

The Archivist-General stood at the window with his back turned. He was older than Silas expected — silver-haired, narrow as a blade, wearing the charcoal uniform of the Directorate civil corps with the collar unfastened, a small rebellion or a small exhaustion.

"Seventh," he said, without turning around.

"I'm sorry?"

He turned. His face was lined and patient, the face of a man who had spent decades reading bad news before breakfast. "Sit down, Mr. Dravenmoor."

Silas sat. The chair was real wood, which on this world was worth more than the chair's weight in fusion fuel, and its very existence told Silas that people who sat in it were meant to feel small and temporary.

"My name is Odile Vantrease," the Archivist-General said. "I have held this office for nineteen years. Before me, a man named Corliss held it for twenty-two. And in all that time, Mr. Dravenmoor, do you know what has been the single most classified file in the colonial archive? Not weapons deployments. Not troop movements." He lowered himself into the chair opposite. "It is a genealogy."

"I don't understand."

"No," Vantrease said. "You wouldn't. That was rather the point." He folded his hands. "Let me ask you something, and I want you to answer honestly, because I will know if you don't. Do you remember your childhood?"

The question landed strangely, like a key turning in a lock Silas hadn't known was there. "Of course."

"Describe it."

"A habitat module outside Halvard Station. My mother — Mara. She kept hydroponics. There was a dog. There was—" Silas stopped. The details were there, all of them, vivid and complete. And yet saying them aloud, in this room, to this man, they sounded like a description read off a card. "Why does that matter?"

"Because Mara Dravenmoor does not appear in any census," Vantrease said quietly. "Halvard Station had no residential modules outside its perimeter. And Elias Dravenmoor — the founder, the man whose face is on the ten-mark note — died childless." He let that sit. "You have memories, Mr. Dravenmoor. Beautiful ones. Complete ones. What you do not have is a past."

The room was very warm and very bright and very far from the floor. Silas heard his own pulse. "That's absurd. I'm sitting here. I have a license. I've renewed it twice."

"Yes. And both times, the review flag was suppressed by order of my office, because the alternative was bringing you here, and we agreed — my predecessors and I — that you were safer not knowing." Vantrease exhaled through his nose. "But then you filed a diagnostic query last week. Beacon 7-Kestrel, requesting registry cross-reference on a vessel designation. *Halcyon Vow.*"

The word *beacon* hit Silas like cold water. "How do you know what queries my station runs?"

"Because we built it," Vantrease said. "Or rather, we inherited it, and we have spent thirty years pretending we didn't. Every query it processes touches our systems, however briefly. For three decades the beacon has been quiet. Compliant. A light on a hill that asks no questions." His eyes, pale and unhurried, fixed on Silas. "And then its keeper asked about a ghost ship."

Silas's hand moved, involuntarily, toward his sternum — toward the shard under the weave — and stopped halfway, becoming instead a gesture of adjusting his collar. Vantrease watched the motion and, mercifully, said nothing about it.

"What is the beacon?" Silas asked.

"That," said Vantrease, "is the wrong question. The right question is: what are *you*?"

He opened a drawer. What he laid on the desk between them was a folder of actual paper, yellowed at the edges, sealed with a Directorate crest stamped in wax that had cracked years ago. He broke the rest of the seal without ceremony, as if he'd been waiting a long time for permission to stop keeping the secret.

"Elias Dravenmoor founded this colony. You know the history — the Landing, the first winter, the charter. What the history doesn't record is that Elias Dravenmoor was also the colony's first geneticist, and that in the last year of his life, working alone, he accomplished something the Directorate had been attempting for a generation: stable human cloning. Full memory architecture. Not a copy of a body. A copy of a *self*."

"He cloned himself," Silas said. His voice sounded far away.

"He made one. Designation Alpha-One. And then someone killed him for it — Elias, I mean, the original — six weeks after the first successful gestation. Shot on the ice plain, ten kilometers from his own laboratory. The killer was never found. The laboratory was burned. The research was seized." Vantrease turned a page. "But the work survived. Because the work was never only in the laboratory. It was in the bloodline. In the sequence. And over the following years, other copies surfaced. Or were made. The records disagree, and I have learned to distrust every record touching this file."

"How many?"

Vantrease looked at him for a long moment, and in that look Silas saw something he had never seen directed at himself: pity, worn smooth by repetition, the pity of a man who had delivered this news before and expected to deliver it again.

"Six," Vantrease said. "Counting you. Alpha-One through Alpha-Five are dead. One drowned under circumstances the coroner called impossible. Two burned in a habitat fire that investigators concluded was set from inside a sealed room. Three was found at the base of the Archival Spire itself — this building — with no record of ever having entered it. Four disappeared from a Directorate transport and was recovered eight months later, frozen, in a place the transport had never flown over. Five—" He paused. "Five was shot on the ice plain. Ten kilometers from a burned laboratory. The same distance. The same angle of fire, if the ballistics are to be believed, though the events were separated by nineteen years."

The room tilted. Silas gripped the armrests of the wooden chair and stared at the paper folder, at the photographs clipped inside — faces, five faces, and every one of them his own face, younger or older, thinner or heavier, but his. His jaw. His crooked left canine. The scar over his eyebrow that he'd been told came from a fall on the ice at age nine, except there had been no ice, no fall, no nine.

"And the sixth?" Silas asked. His voice came out steady, which frightened him more than screaming would have.

"The sixth filed a renewal fourteen years ago," Vantrease said. "In this office. Sat in that chair. Asked the same question you're about to ask. And then walked out of the Spire and was never seen again. No body. No travel record. No death." He closed the folder. "We assumed the Directorate's retrieval division finally got to them. We may have been wrong. People like you, Mr. Dravenmoor, are difficult to kill permanently. It seems to be the one thing you're consistently good at."

"Why are you telling me this?" Silas asked. "If the file is the most classified thing in the archive — if my predecessors suppressed my flags — why break the seal now?"

"Because of the beacon." Vantrease stood and walked back to the window, hands clasped behind him, a silhouette against the burning afternoon sky. "For thirty years it has transmitted nothing but its maintenance signal. Last week, for four seconds, it transmitted something else. An encrypted packet on a frequency we haven't monitored since the war. We couldn't decrypt it. But its routing header was addressed — addressed, Mr. Dravenmoor, personally — to a passenger aboard a ship called the *Halcyon Vow*. A woman named Ione Vashti."

The name went through Silas like a current, and with it came something worse than the name: a flash, half a second, of a woman laughing in a doorway full of dust and gold light, and the smell of engine coolant, and a voice — *his* voice, but younger, warmer — saying a sentence he couldn't hold on to. Gone before it finished arriving.

He realized his hands were shaking.

"I don't know that woman," Silas said.

"No," Vantrease agreed, watching him in the glass. "But you dreamed about her just now. Don't bother denying it; I've sat across from five of you. The memories surface when the pressure gets high enough. It's how the sequence protects itself." He turned back. "Here is what happens next, and I offer it because five of you are dead and I am tired of filing the deaths. The Directorate retrieval division will have flagged your renewal within the hour — my suppression authority ends at this desk, and there are people above me who have wanted this file open for thirty years. They will come for you, and they will not ask questions in a comfortable chair. So: take the southern line tonight, surrender yourself at the Directorate consulate, claim ignorance, and live out your life in a supervised residency. It's a small life. But it's a life."

"And the other option?"

"There is no other option," Vantrease said. "There is only the one the others chose, and you can see how it went." He picked up the folder, hesitated, and then — with the expression of a man stepping off a ledge — slid it across the desk instead. "Take it. Burn it, memorize it, I no longer care. Nineteen years I've kept this, and every year the beacon gets quieter and the war gets closer to starting again, and I begin to think the secret isn't protecting anyone. Maybe it never was."

Silas stood. The folder was lighter than it should have been, holding everything it held. At the door he paused, one hand on the frame.

"The sixth clone," he said. "Fourteen years ago. Did they say anything? Before they left?"

Vantrease was quiet for a moment.

"They asked what happened to the *Halcyon Vow*," he said. "Same as you. And when I told them the registry showed no such ship, they smiled — and I have never been able to forget that smile, Mr. Dravenmoor, because it wasn't a madman's smile. It was a man hearing exactly the answer he'd hoped for." He waved a hand, dismissal or benediction. "Go home. Or don't. But whatever you do — do it quickly. You have less time than you think."

The lift descended through the clouds, and Corvane rose to meet Silas, glittering and indifferent, and all he could think, all the way down, was:

*I'm the seventh.*

*And the sixth is still alive.*
