## 4. What the Sixth Left Behind

The beacon woke me at 03:12 with a perimeter alert, and for a long moment I stood in the dark of the maintenance bay trying to decide whether I was more afraid of an intruder or of being wrong about the intruder.

Thirty years on Kestrel's Landing and no one had ever walked up to my door. The Directorate's survey ships passed overhead twice a year and logged the beacon as derelict infrastructure — a clerical ghost, like the Halcyon Vow. The colonists in the capital had no reason to cross four hundred kilometers of ice plain. Nobody came here.

Nobody except whoever had built the beacon nobody ordered.

I pulled up the exterior feed. A single figure stood at the edge of the floodlight's reach, just outside it, as if they knew exactly where the light ended and had chosen the darkness deliberately. Thin. Hooded. Snow to their knees, which meant they'd walked the last stretch rather than take a skimmer — or couldn't afford one, or was avoiding every sensor that wasn't mine.

"Identify yourself," I said over the external speaker. My voice sounded strange in the empty bay, too loud.

The figure didn't move. Then, slowly, they raised both hands — palms out, empty — and stepped into the light.

I knew the face.

That was the impossible part. I knew it the way you know your own reflection, because it *was* my own reflection, twenty years weathered, hollowed at the cheeks, one eye clouded white from some old injury. The face looking up into my camera was my face, aged badly, and it was smiling like a man who has found something he'd stopped believing in.

"Hello, little brother," the figure said. "Or sister. I never could tell which one they'd make next."

* * *

Wren

You have to understand what thirty years alone does to a memory. It doesn't fade. It *ferments*. Every recollection of Elias Dravenmoor's last night went into the cask of my skull and turned, and turned, until I couldn't tell anymore which parts were his and which parts were mine, which parts happened and which parts I'd invented to fill the gaps where the archive had been cut out of me.

They took pieces when they made me. That's what cloning is, if you're Directorate property: you get the body, the muscle memory, the way his hands know a control panel before his mind does. But the memories themselves — the *record* — those get stripped, catalogued, archived. Except they missed some. Or left some. I've never decided which is worse.

So I carried fragments. The smell of ozone and blood. A woman's voice saying *run, Elias, run* — no, wait, that was wrong, she said *Elias, run*, and then a door, and then nothing, a hole in the sequence shaped exactly like the rest of my life.

I found the beacon because the fragments pointed here. Thirty years of following a compass needle buried in someone else's dying thoughts.

And now there was a seventh standing behind a door, watching me through a camera, and I could see my own young face staring back at me from the monitor mounted beside the airlock, and I swear to you I did not know whether to laugh or kneel.

"Let me in," I said. "Before something worse than me finds this place."

* * *

Silas

Every protocol I had said refuse entry. Unverified contact, remote location, sensitive installation. Every protocol I had also said *you are a licensed archivist-technician performing routine maintenance on a decommissioned relay*, which was a lie so old it had grown roots, and protocols written for liars are not much use.

I let them in because of the face. Because when Wren smiled, the left side of their mouth lifted a fraction higher than the right, and so does mine, and I had spent forty years believing that asymmetry was mine — a flaw, a signature, something no one else in the universe shared.

The airlock cycled. Cold rolled off them in waves; their coat was patched with material from three different decades. Up close the resemblance was worse than through the camera. It was like meeting a photograph of myself that had kept living without my permission.

"You're the sixth," I said.

"I'm whatever's left of the sixth." They looked around the maintenance bay with an expression I recognized from my own mirror — the inventorying glance of someone who measures rooms by how defensible they are. "Silas, yes? I read your renewal filing."

"My filing is sealed."

"It was flagged within six hours." They pulled back their hood. Underneath, their hair was gray-streaked and cropped short over old surgical scars behind the ear — interface ports, standard Directorate issue, the kind I'd been told were removed from all colonial records. "The Archivist-General opened a retrieval order the same day. You're lucky the paperwork moves slower than the hunters. Barely."

"The Archivist-General told me about the clones," I said carefully. "One through five. Dead. You — vanished."

"Vanished." Wren laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like a hinge that needed oil. "Is that what they call it? I ran. I took half a memory of a murder and I ran for thirty years, and every few years they'd find me and I'd run again, and somewhere in there I stopped being a person and became a *route* — a thing that moves between hiding places." Their good eye fixed on me. "And now you've filed a renewal. You've put your face, your biometrics, your *location assignment* into the Directorate's hands, and the moment anyone cross-references it against the Dravenmoor line—"

"They already know what I am. The Archivist-General himself—"

"The Archivist-General is the man who signed the order to kill the original."

The bay went very quiet. Somewhere above us, the beacon's antenna array ticked as the temperature dropped, the sound it made every night, the sound I'd built my whole life around, and for the first time in forty years it sounded like a clock.

"That's not possible," I said. "He was — he was courteous. He was almost kind."

"He would be." Wren sat down heavily on my workbench, uninvited, as if they'd sat on it a hundred times in some other life. Which, in a way, they had. "Kindness costs him nothing. He's not a monster, little sibling. He's an accountant. And we—" they gestured at their own face, at mine, at the space between us where the resemblance lived, "—we are outstanding debts."

* * *

Wren

Here is what I remember of the night Elias died, and I want it written down somewhere, even if the only place to write it is inside another copy of my own skull:

There was a ship called the Halcyon Vow. It existed. I know it existed because I remember welding my own hands to its hull plating — his hands, Elias's hands, but they are these hands, the ones in front of you now. There was a woman named Ione Vashti who laughed at his jokes before he finished them. There was a message — not a document, a *framework*, something that could have ended the war before it properly began — and Elias was carrying it to the launch, and there was a man waiting for him in the dry dock corridor with a Directorate sidearm and a signed authorization, and the authorization bore the seal of the Office of Archives and Records.

The office that licenses people like us. The office that decides which memories get copied forward and which get cut.

I don't remember dying. That's the mercy they left me, maybe the only one. I remember the door closing, and Ione screaming my name — his name — and then I remember waking up in a growth vat with a hole in my head where all of that used to be, except the hole leaked. The hole leaked for thirty years.

When I saw the seventh's filing come through the intercepts I still run — old habits, old scars — I thought: they'll send a retrieval team. They always send a retrieval team. And I thought: the beacon. Whatever the seventh is maintaining out there on the ice, the fragments say it matters. The fragments point at it like a wound points at the knife.

So I walked. Four hundred kilometers. My knees will tell you what I think of poetry.

But I got here first. That has to count for something. After thirty years of running, I finally arrived somewhere before the people chasing me.

* * *

Silas

We talked until the sky paled. Wren drank my coffee and ate half my protein stores and told me everything in no particular order, circling back, contradicting themselves, sometimes addressing me as if I were Elias, once addressing me as if I were the Archivist-General, apologizing to a man who wasn't in the room.

Half-mad, I thought. The word fit. But underneath the madness was structure, the way wreckage retains the shape of the ship.

"And the beacon?" I asked, near dawn. "Why does it matter? It's a relay. Decommissioned. Half the records say it shouldn't exist."

Wren set down the cup. For a moment the madness drained out of their face and what remained was so precisely my own expression — the one I wore during diagnostics, focused, afraid — that I had to look away.

"It's not a transmitter," they said. "It's a vault. The original hid the full archive inside it — every memory they cut out of us, all of them, including the night he died and the reason they killed him. I've been walking toward it for thirty years on fragments. You've been *maintaining* it for forty without knowing what you were keeping warm."

"That's not — the schematics—"

"Were falsified by the same office that falsified the manifest. Little sibling, look at me." They waited until I did. "Your renewal filing went out six days ago. Retrieval teams don't travel slow. We have days. Maybe less."

Outside, the ice plain caught the first light, and the beacon cast its long shadow across it, and I understood suddenly that the shadow had always been pointing at something, and I had simply never followed it to the end.

"What's in the vault," I said. Not a question. "What did he find?"

Wren smiled that lopsided smile, my smile, and said:

"Why do you think they killed him?"

Then the perimeter alarm sounded again — not the ground sensors this time, but the sky — and on my display, descending through the morning clouds toward Kestrel's Landing, came three ships flying Directorate colors.
