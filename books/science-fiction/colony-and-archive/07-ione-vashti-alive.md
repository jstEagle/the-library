## 7. Ione Vashti, Alive

The dry dock was buried under forty meters of glacier, and Ione Vashti had spent thirty years making sure it stayed that way.

She felt the tremor before she heard it — a deep, patient shudder in the ice, the kind the mountain made when something heavy moved across its surface. She set down her tea. It had gone cold hours ago; she drank it anyway, because ritual was all she had left of discipline, and discipline was all she had left at all.

The Halcyon Vow sat where Elias had left her, nose pointed at a wall of blue ice as if she still intended to fly through it someday. Thirty years of maintenance on a ship that would never launch. That was the joke of it. That was the whole shape of her life: keeping a dead woman's promise to a dead man's ghost.

*He's coming,* she thought, and did not let herself believe it.

The outer hatch groaned. Not the ice — the hatch. Someone was working the manual release, someone who knew the sequence, and only two people alive had ever known the sequence, and one of them had been shot through the chest on the steps of the Founders' Hall while she watched from a window she could never close.

Ione took the pistol from the drawer. It was older than she was. She checked the charge anyway, out of respect for the possibility that today might finally matter.

Footsteps in the corridor. Unhurried. Almost reverent.

Then he came through the inner bulkhead door, and Ione's hand — steady for three decades, steady through frostbite and famine and the slow erosion of every reason to keep going — shook so badly she nearly dropped the gun.

He was young. Of course he was young. They always were.

But he walked like Elias. He stood in the doorway like Elias, one shoulder lower than the other, head tilted as if listening to a frequency no one else could hear. And when his eyes found hers, they were not Elias's eyes at all. They were frightened. Elias had never once been frightened in her presence, not even at the end, especially not at the end.

"You're real," the young man said. His voice cracked on *real*. "The manifest listed you. The beacon listed you. I thought — I thought it was just another memory. Another thing he kept."

"Lower the weapon," Ione said, though he wasn't holding one. "And tell me who sent you."

"No one." He spread his hands. "Silas Dravenmoor. Seventh license-holder of the Kestrel's Landing beacon. I maintain it. I've maintained it my whole life, and until nine days ago I didn't know why."

"Nine days."

"The manifest came up in a diagnostic. Your name. A ship that doesn't exist." He looked past her, into the bay, and Ione watched him see the Halcyon Vow for the first time — watched him take in the hull, unscarred, gleaming under her work lights, a vessel built to carry four hundred souls to a peace conference that never happened. "Registry says she was never constructed."

"Registry says a great many things," Ione said. "Registry said Elias Dravenmoor died of a reactor accident. Registry says the colony war started over shipping lanes. Registry is a very diligent liar."

She lowered the pistol. Not because she trusted him — trust was a currency she'd stopped minting decades ago — but because her arm was tired, and because some traitor part of her had already decided, the moment he walked like that, with that shoulder, that this one she would not shoot.

"They cloned him," she said. "You know that now."

"I know I'm the seventh. I know five died violently. I know the sixth vanished." He paused. "Yesterday I learned the sixth didn't vanish. Wren. They're in Directorate hands. They bought me time on the ice plain, and I ran, because running was the mission, and I hate myself for how easily I ran."

"Wren." Ione closed her eyes. "So there was a sixth after all. I stopped counting at five. Counting them felt like counting murders I could have prevented."

"You couldn't have—"

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to cut. She softened it, barely. "Don't absolve me, boy. I've had thirty years of absolution from nobody, and I've earned every year of it. Sit down. You look half-frozen, and you're going to want to be sitting for what I have to show you."

* * *

She fed him first. Real protein, hoarded, the last of a stock she'd been rationing against a future she'd stopped believing in. He ate like the starving do, apologetically, and she pretended not to notice, and between them they constructed a small temporary fiction of ordinary hospitality, two strangers sharing a meal, which was the most human either of them had felt in years.

Then she took him to the cargo hold.

The Halcyon Vow's hold had been designed for terraforming equipment. Instead it held a single crate, two meters long, bolted to the deck, wrapped in enough shielding to hide it from every scan short of a physical inspection. Ione knelt beside it the way she knelt every morning, and entered the code — Elias's birthday, then hers, then the date they'd met, because he'd told her once that the only security worth having was a lock made of love, since no torturer could guess what mattered to you, and no algorithm could either.

The crate opened on a data core the size of a loaf of bread.

"This is what he was carrying," she said. "Not to Earth. Not to the colonies. To both. To everyone. He called it the Framework."

Silas went very still. "A peace framework."

"A complete one. Terms, verification regimes, disarmament schedules, an amnesty structure, a shared resource compact. Thirty years ago it would have ended the war before it became a war." She heard her own voice go flat and distant, the voice she used for this part, the recitation she performed for an audience of crates. "Elias wasn't a founder. That's another registry lie. He was a negotiator. He'd brokered the ceasefire on Meridian, the water accords on Thessaly. The Directorate hired him to draft a permanent settlement, and he did, and then he read the intelligence annexes they gave him, and he discovered the war itself was their product. Manufactured. Sustained. A consolidation engine. Every colony that lost autonomy, every charter revoked, every emergency power granted — all of it justified by a war the Directorate was feeding on purpose, from both ends."

"And they killed him for it."

"On the steps of the Founders' Hall, eleven days before the Framework's scheduled delivery. In front of me. In front of two hundred witnesses who all saw a stranger, a malfunctioning drone, a heart attack — everyone saw something different. That's the other thing you should know about that day." She finally looked up at him. "The murder was edited. Live. Everyone's memory of it was rewritten within minutes. Everyone's but mine. I don't know why mine held. Maybe a fault in the edit. Maybe they wanted one witness alive, somewhere, as insurance. Maybe cruelty is just efficient sometimes."

Silas's face had gone the color of the ice above them. "The beacon. The archive. I found his memories in it — digitized, whole. I thought it was a vault. A tomb."

"It's a delivery system. He built it before he died — he knew the odds, he used to joke about his own assassination the way other men joke about weather. The beacon holds everything: the Framework, the evidence of the manufactured war, the record of his death as he actually experienced it. But a broadcast can be jammed, denied, discredited. A dead founder's ghost story. So he built it to need a living carrier. Someone who could stand before the colonies and Earth and say *I remember*, and be believed, because the memories would be verifiably his." Her throat tightened. "Someone compatible with the archive interface."

"Compatible," Silas repeated. The word had gone strange in his mouth.

"He couldn't upload himself. The interface requires a living neural pattern in resonance with the stored one — close enough to carry it, distinct enough to survive the transfer. There was exactly one way to manufacture that compatibility." Ione reached out and, for the first time in thirty years, touched another person's hand. His. Cold. Trembling. "They didn't clone him to replace him, Silas. They cloned him to deliver him. Every copy they grew was a courier they hadn't briefed yet. You weren't born. You were addressed."

He pulled his hand back. Not unkindly. Like a man setting down something fragile before it breaks.

"I'm the envelope," he said.

"You're the message," Ione corrected. "The envelope is whatever they made your body for. What you do with it — that part was never theirs."

* * *

He slept six hours in the crew quarters, and Ione sat outside the door the whole time with the pistol in her lap, not guarding him, guarding the corridor, because if the Directorate had flagged his renewal filing then the clock was already running, and she had spent thirty years learning exactly how long it took hunters to cross an ice plain.

When he woke, he found her in the cockpit, and he sat in the pilot's seat — Elias's seat — without being told, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and the silence was the closest thing to a prayer either of them knew.

"The broadcast," he said finally. "From the beacon. If we transmit the archive directly, jamming or no jamming, every receiver in range—"

"Every receiver in two systems. Yes. But direct broadcast needs a manual interface. A mind in the loop, holding the channel open while terabytes pour through. The transfer is—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "The transfer is total. Whatever goes in doesn't come back out the same. The archive doesn't coexist with a person, Silas. It replaces them. Elias designed it that way deliberately. No one should carry that much truth and stay themselves. He called it the honest price."

"How long?"

"Minutes to transmit. Forever to be transmitted." She turned to look at him, and let him see all of it — the age, the grief, the thirty years of cold tea and buried ships. "I would go myself. I've been ready to die since the day he did. But the interface would burn through a neural pattern my age in the first seconds. I'd die screaming static, and the transmission would fail, and they'd bury us both in the same registry lie."

"So it has to be me."

"So it has to be a Dravenmoor." She hated the word as she said it. Hated that it was true. "Which means before you decide anything — anything — you should know what I know about the interface protocols. And you should know that Wren is still alive, because the Directorate doesn't waste assets, and a live clone is an asset, and if there's any chance of pulling them out before the fleet arrives—"

"Fleet?" Silas was on his feet.

Ione nodded toward the cockpit display, where a sensor contact she'd been pretending not to watch for the past hour crawled down the edge of the scope. Three signatures. Descending through the atmosphere. Broadcast-flagged, Directorate transponders, moving with the unhurried confidence of hunters who already knew where the prey was hiding.

"They followed your trail here," she said. "Or they followed mine. Thirty years, and they finally got impatient." She picked up the pistol and handed it to him, grip first, the way Elias had taught her to hand over a weapon: as a question. "So. Seventh of his name. We have perhaps two hours, a buried ship that has never flown, and a message worth ending a war. Tell me — are you ready to become it?"
