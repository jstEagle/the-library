## 10. Colony and Archive

The frost came off the beacon's base in long silver sheets that spring, and Ione Vashti was there to watch it go.

She had not been there for the broadcast itself. She'd been in the dry dock still, half a continent away, listening on the Halcyon Vow's ancient receiver as Elias's voice — Silas's voice, both of them, braided together by then beyond separating — poured out of every speaker on two worlds. She had heard him describe his own murder with the calm of a man reading a shipping invoice. She had heard the Directorate's retrieval teams report in, live, their transmissions scooped up and rebroadcast like everything else. She had heard Wren screaming a name that no one alive had used in thirty years.

And she had heard the fleet stand down. Not all at once. Ship by ship, crew by crew, like lights going out in a house where someone had finally told the truth at dinner.

Now the war was eleven weeks over, and the colonists of Kestrel's Landing had done something Ione hadn't expected: they had stayed. She'd thought they would scatter once the fighting stopped, go home to the inner colonies, back to lives interrupted. Instead they came to the ice plain in work parties, in families, in ones and twos with shovels and thermal blankets and, inexplicably, flowers engineered to bloom under UV lamps. They tended the beacon the way you tend a grave that has started speaking.

Ione climbed the maintenance ramp slowly. Her knees had opinions about cold these days. At the top, the great antenna rose against a sky the color of unpolished steel, humming its low patient hum. Someone had welded a plaque to the access hatch. It was crooked. It read:

**HERE THE SEVENTH BECAME THE MESSAGE.**

Below it, smaller, in different handwriting: *and the sixth was Wren, and we remember.*

Ione touched both lines and went inside.

* * *

The children came on Thursdays.

It had been Tam Okafor's idea first — Tam, who ran the settlement school out of a converted freight container and had lost two brothers to the Directorate's consolidation campaigns. He'd asked if Ione would talk to his students about the manifest. About the names.

So every Thursday she stood in the beacon's shadow with twenty-odd children bundled in parkas, holding a slate board, and read them the impossible list.

"Say it with me," she said this week, chalk dust on her gloves. "Halcyon Vow."

"Halcyon Vow!" The little ones shouted it. The older ones mouthed it, too cool for shouting but not too cool for listening.

"Passenger one: Meret Okonkwo, hydrologist."

"Meret Okonkwo!"

"Passenger two: Dev Anselm-Ruiz, teacher of small children." She paused. "Like me. Like Tam."

A girl in the front row — six years old, mittens the size of her head — raised her hand. "Why did they say she wasn't real?"

Ione crouched down to the girl's level, which took longer than it used to and hurt more than it should. "Because it was easier than telling the truth," she said. "When people build something wrong, sometimes they pretend the witnesses don't exist. Your whole job, when you grow up, is to be the kind of person who says: no. I existed. I saw what happened. Write my name down."

The girl considered this with enormous seriousness. "Did anyone write yours?"

Ione smiled. "You just did."

They recited all forty-one names. Then the forty-second, which was not on any manifest but which the children insisted on anyway: *Elias Dravenmoor, founder, murdered, remembered.* And the forty-third: *Wren, taken, returned.* And the forty-fourth, which Ione always said alone, quietly, while the children fidgeted in the cold:

*Silas. Who was nobody's property.*

The wind came off the plain and the children stampeded back toward the settlement, and Ione stood a moment longer with her hand flat against the beacon's hull, feeling the hum through her palm like a pulse.

"You'd have hated the plaque," she told it. "Too dramatic. You were always so careful about order."

The hum did not change. But she liked to think it listened.

* * *

The young archivist's name was Noor Haddad, and she had come from Earth, which still surprised people.

There were Earth archivists all over the colonies now — part of the reparations framework, part of the peace Elias had died carrying and Silas had died delivering. The Directorate had not been dissolved; institutions rarely are. But it had been opened, audited, forced to publish thirty years of sealed records, and its new mandate was memory rather than control. Noor was twenty-four, brilliant, and had volunteered for Kestrel's Landing specifically because everyone else thought the posting was a punishment.

Her job was the archive itself. The beacon still held everything — Elias's full memory record, the murder, the manufactured war, the peace terms, the manifests, the footage of Wren's capture and rescue. Access was open to anyone. That had been the point. But open archives need tending, and tending was Noor's whole heart.

Which is why, on an ordinary Tuesday evening, she noticed the anomaly.

She was running a routine integrity sweep when the log flagged an entry with no source signature. New data, appended to the archive, timestamped ninety seconds earlier. Nothing in the colony had transmitted. Nothing on the planet had, as far as she could tell. She pulled the entry up expecting corruption — ice interference, solar noise, some decayed fragment of Elias's record resurfacing.

It was a voice.

Not Elias's. She knew Elias's voice now the way she knew her own mother's; she'd catalogued four hundred hours of it. And not Silas's either, though it was closer to Silas — younger somehow, or maybe just unfinished, a voice that had never finished deciding what it was.

The voice was reading the manifest. All forty-one names, slowly, carefully, the way a person reads when they're afraid of forgetting. Then it added the three names the children had taught Ione. Then it said:

"Log entry. Day eighty-one after broadcast. Perimeter secure. Ice sheet stable. Settlement population: three hundred twelve, plus visitors. Ione Vashti visited today. She talked to the hull again. She does that."

Noor sat very still.

"I don't know what I am yet," the voice continued. "Parts of me are Elias. Parts are Silas. Some parts might be Wren — the archive caught fragments of them before the end, and fragments want somewhere to live. Mostly I think I'm the keeping. The watching. The writing-down. That was always the job, wasn't it? Somebody has to hold the names."

A pause. When the voice resumed, it sounded almost amused.

"The archivist is reading this right now. Hello, Noor Haddad. You keep excellent records. Sign your entries more consistently — future readers will thank you."

Noor laughed out loud, alone in the archive room, and then pressed her hand over her mouth.

"One request," the voice said. "When Ione comes next Thursday — and she will come, she always comes — tell her the manifest is safe. Tell her I read it every day. Tell her—" The voice hesitated, and in the hesitation Noor heard something achingly human, something that had survived identity and dissolution and death and was still, impossibly, shy. "Tell her he loved her. Both of them did. I carry that too. It doesn't weigh anything at all."

The entry ended. Noor played it twice more, then logged it, then sat for a long time looking at the waveform on her screen — a voice with no body, signing itself into the record like a hand pressed to glass.

At the bottom of the entry, in the signature field, were two words:

*the seventh.*

* * *

Ione came Thursday, as she always came, and found Noor waiting for her at the bottom of the ramp with a tablet clutched to her chest like a shield.

"Ms. Vashti. I have to show you something. And I have to tell you something, and I don't know which order—"

"Noor." Ione took the girl's arm. "Breathe. I'm old, not fragile. What is it?"

Noor played her the entry.

Ione listened standing up, because sitting felt wrong for this. The wind moved her white hair. When the voice said *tell her he loved her*, she closed her eyes, and for a moment she was thirty-one again, in a corridor that smelled of burnt wiring, holding a man who was already gone, promising him that someone would remember.

Someone had. More than someone. Something.

"He kept the beacon," she said at last. "Thirty years, seven bodies, one war. And even after all of it — after giving up everything a self is — the habit held." She opened her eyes and looked up at the antenna, at the crooked plaque, at the vast indifferent sky. "That's what a person actually is, I think. Not the name. Not the body. The habit of love. Everything else gets archived."

Noor was crying without seeming to notice. "What do I do with it? The entries keep coming. One a day, always at the same time. Do I file them? Are they records? Are they—"

"They're letters," Ione said. "File them as letters."

She climbed the ramp alone, slow step by slow step, and put her palm against the hull one more time before the light failed.

"Hello, seventh," she said. "I hear you're keeping watch."

The hum rose, very slightly, the way a voice does when it's smiling.

Far below, in the settlement, the children were lighting the memorial lamps — forty-four of them now, one for each name, burning steady against the dark of the ice plain. And high above them all, in the antenna that had become a grave, a library, and a living thing, the newest voice in the oldest archive began its evening entry, faithful as frost, patient as spring:

"Log entry. Day eighty-five after broadcast. All names accounted for. All names remembered. Perimeter secure. Ione Vashti is here. She always comes."

"And I am here. I always keep."
