## 6. The Third Signature

The relay station hung at the edge of the Meridian–Cael corridor like a tooth that had been pulled and never replaced. Officially it was called Relay Nine. Unofficially, on the courier charts my original had kept and I had inherited without understanding, it was called the Widow — because everyone who'd ever been stationed there had left someone behind, or been left by one.

I came in slow, engines cold for the last thousand kilometers, drifting on residual momentum like a man tiptoeing past a sleeping dog. The station's solar wings were shredded, one of them trailing a ribbon of dead photovoltaic cells that caught the light of Cael's sun and threw it back in long, shivering sheets. Beautiful, in the way that ruins are beautiful. The way things are beautiful when they've stopped being anyone's problem.

The coordinates from the cylinder put me at an airlock on the station's shadow side. Of course they did. My original had never met a door he liked walking through front-first.

I suited up, clipped the cylinder to my chest rig where it rode like a second heartbeat, and crossed the gap on a tether line. Through the airlock viewport I could see the interior: dark, frost-furred, a drift of frozen condensation hanging in the zero-g like a school of tiny silver fish. Six years of nobody. Six years of vacuum doing what vacuum does, which is keep secrets better than any archivist.

The lock equalized with a groan. I cycled through into air that tasted of metal and old ozone and something faintly organic — mold, maybe, growing in some warm pocket where a coolant line still leaked heat. A station that was supposed to be dead, breathing anyway. I noted that and decided not to think about what it meant.

* * *

Finding the cache took four hours.

My original had left instructions in the message fragment — a sequence of deck references disguised as shipping codes, which was cute of him, because I'd spent three years reading those exact kinds of codes without ever once suspecting they meant anything else. Deck nine, frame forty-one, junction box marked *CARGO LIGHTING — DEFUNCT*. It wasn't defunct. Inside, taped behind the breaker panel, was a data slate wrapped in anti-static film and a handwritten note on actual paper, which no one used anymore except people who wanted something to survive.

The note said: *If you're reading this, you're me. Don't be angry yet. Be angry after.*

So I sat down on a crate in a dead relay station, six hundred million kilometers from either world that claimed to want me, and I read.

* * *

The first thing on the slate was a treaty.

Not a draft. Not a proposal. A finished, verified accord — signed, sealed, cross-authenticated through three independent channels, dated eleven months before the Perihelion burned. The Meridian–Cael Armistice Accords, Final Text. Every clause resolved. Every border question answered. Every prisoner exchange scheduled. It ended the war. It had *ended* the war, eleven months before anyone knew there was anything left to end.

And then it had been buried.

I read the authentication chain twice because the first time I assumed I'd misread it. Three signatures. One from Meridian's deputy foreign minister — a man named Aldous Renholt, whose face was on public information broadcasts when I was growing up, explaining why peace was "a process, not an event." One from Cael's director of strategic continuity — Director Maren Voss, a name I knew only as a redaction in customs bulletins Ines Okonkwo complained about. And a third signature that wasn't a government at all.

The third signature belonged to a broker. An entity called Corvane Interstitial Holdings — a logistics firm, nominally. The kind of firm that moved cargo between two worlds officially at war, because wars don't stop trade, they just make it expensive, and expensive is where brokers live. The signatory was its principal: one Emeric Corvane. No photograph. No biography. Just a name and a hash-verified key, which in this business is more identity than most people ever get.

Three signatures. And under them, in my original's own hand — his handwriting, which is to say mine, which is to say a hand I recognized the way you recognize your own reflection in a window you didn't know you were looking into — a summary:

*Renholt killed ratification because ratification ends the emergency powers that fund his ministry. Voss killed it because the war's frozen state justifies her entire apparatus — her budget, her authority, her surveillance grid. Corvane killed it because a cold war is the perfect market: both sides buying, neither side winning, prices forever high. They didn't disagree with the treaty. They agreed with it completely. That was the problem. Peace would have cost each of them everything they'd built, so they buried it together, across enemy lines, while their governments went on shooting.*

*I carried the verification copies. All three parties trusted me because I was nobody — a clone courier, invisible by design. They thought invisibility meant loyalty. It meant witness.*

*They found out. The Perihelion didn't burn by accident.*

I stopped reading. I floated there in the dark of the junction bay, holding a dead man's slate in a dead man's hands, and I understood that the anger he'd told me to save was already here, arrived early, filling up all the space inside me like floodwater.

Six years. Six years of two worlds glaring at each other across a silent corridor, rationing, grieving, teaching their children the other side was monsters — while three men held a finished peace in a drawer and let the war run because the war paid them. People I'd delivered sealed messages to. Ceremonies I'd flown past. Names read aloud on memorial days. All of it furniture arranged around an empty chair where a treaty should have been sitting.

*Don't be angry yet,* he'd written. *Be angry after.* He'd known me. He'd known exactly how I'd take it.

* * *

There was more. There was always more; my original had never once in his life done anything halfway.

Beneath the treaty text were the operational details — the part that turned grief into work. Verification keys, split three ways, enough to authenticate the accord against any government's records. A list of dead-drops and sympathetic contacts, most of them years stale but a few marked *STILL GOOD AS OF LAST CONTACT*, including a courier network running the same corridor I ran, people who owed him favors the way I owed him a face. And a final section, flagged in red, titled simply: *THE THIRD SIGNATURE*.

I thought at first it referred to Corvane. It didn't. It referred to a problem.

The accord required all three signatures to be presented simultaneously for authentication — that was the design, corruption-proofing, so no single party could forge the others' consent. Renholt's key was recoverable; my original had copied it before the Perihelion burned. Voss's key was here, embedded in the slate. But Corvane's key — the broker's third — existed only in Corvane's private vault, and my original had died trying to get close to it. The last entry in the section read:

*Without the third signature, the accord can be dismissed as forgery. With it, it's undeniable. Corvane keeps it close. Find the man, find the key. I'm sorry I couldn't finish. You were always going to be faster than me. You're younger. Use that.*

He'd apologized to me. To a copy. As if I were the continuation and he the rough draft, instead of the other way around. I don't know why that undid me more than the murder did. Maybe because it was the first time anyone had ever addressed me as if I mattered — as if being his echo was a job and not a defect.

I laughed, alone in the dark, and it came out wrong, and I let it.

* * *

I was still floating there, slate in hand, cataloguing the specific shapes of my fury — there was a cold one for Renholt, a bureaucratic one for Voss, and something hotter and less articulate reserved for Corvane — when the station spoke to me.

Not literally. Worse. A light came on.

One amber indicator, down the corridor toward the comms core, blinking in a rhythm I recognized because every courier recognizes it: a proximity ping. Someone had queried the station's dormant transponder from outside. Standard traffic check. Except Relay Nine had no scheduled traffic, hadn't for six years, and whoever was pinging it wasn't broadcasting an identification code.

I killed my suit lights and drifted to a viewport.

Out in the black, against the starfield, a ship was matching my orbit. Small, dark-hulled, running without running lights — a watcher's profile, the kind of hull intelligence services use when they want plausible deniability about existing. It wasn't Meridian registry styling. It wasn't Caeli either. It was the third thing, the neutral thing, the thing that belongs to men like Emeric Corvane.

The ping repeated. Closer. Patient.

I understood then what my original had understood too late: that invisibility was never protection. It was just permission for other people to decide what happened to you. I had spent three years being no one, and no one had finally caught up with me, and now there was a treaty in my hands worth more than both worlds' militaries and a ship out there deciding whether I was a ghost or a witness.

The amber light blinked again. Third time. Final warning before approach.

I tucked the slate against my chest, next to the cylinder, next to the note that said *be angry after* — and I was, God help me, I was furious enough to burn a hole through both worlds — and I did the one thing no invisible man should ever do.

I keyed the station's ancient transmitter and broadcast, on open channel, to the ship in the dark:

*"Relay Nine to unidentified vessel. This is Oscar Villalobos. I know who you work for. And I know what he buried."*

Silence. Then the ship's drives flared.

It started moving toward me.
