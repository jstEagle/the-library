## 3. Meridian, Still Breathing

The thing about a nine-second mystery is that it doesn't let you sleep.

Hazel had tried. He'd made tea—real tea, the last of the hoarded Assam he'd been rationing for a "special occasion," because a nine-second system-wide gravity failure seemed to qualify—and he'd sat in his chair with his feet up and told himself, firmly, that whatever had happened was a fleet-wide telemetry glitch, that glitches were his oldest friends, that in the morning the whole thing would resolve into a boring explanation involving a corrupted timestamp somewhere out past the heliopause.

He got as far as lying down. Then he got back up. Then he lay down again. Then, around hour three of this negotiation with himself, he admitted defeat, put on his boots, and went looking for trouble.

"Fine," he said to the empty station. "But I want it on record that I resisted."

The station, being empty, did not record anything. That was the problem. He checked.

* * *

By morning—station morning, which was whatever Hazel said it was—he had confirmed three things.

First: the gravity flicker had been real. Not an instrument ghost, not a sensor hiccup. Every mass-reading across the Kestrel's monitoring array had dropped by exactly 4.7 percent for exactly nine seconds, then returned as if nothing had happened, like a heart skipping a beat and pretending it hadn't.

Second: every log of the event had erased itself. Not deleted—deleted left traces, and Hazel knew traces the way other men knew their own handwriting. The entries were *smoothed over*, filled in with plausible noise, the digital equivalent of a crime scene being professionally redecorated. Someone—or something—with access to the deep telemetry layer had tidied up after itself.

Third, and this was the one that kept him pacing: the smoothing hadn't come from the fleet side. The fleet's relay handshake protocols were clunky, bureaucratic things; Hazel had spent ten years quietly dodging them and knew their fingerprints. This was something else. Something elegant. Something that had reached into his station, borrowed his instruments for nine seconds, put everything back on the shelf, and left without wiping its feet only because it wanted him to notice.

Or because it couldn't help it.

He stood at the main viewport with his tea going cold and looked down the long dark throat of the system toward Meridian.

Eleven years ago, the fleet had declared Meridian dead. Official cause: cascading life-support failure following an unspecified "internal incident." Unofficially—unofficially being the only way anything happened in this fleet—everyone had simply agreed to stop talking about it. Meridian was the colony everyone had written off, the bad investment, the name you didn't say at dinner if you wanted dinner to stay pleasant. The cryosleep fleet hung in its slow parking orbits two light-hours out, forty thousand sleepers dreaming through a war that hadn't started yet but was already fully staffed.

And Hazel had spent eleven years recording Meridian's decline. It was his ritual, his religion, his penance—though he'd have bitten his own tongue off before calling it that. Every day he pointed the long-range array at the frozen planet and logged what he saw: the dimming of the equatorial heat bloom, the slow burial of the landing fields under frost, the moment the last exterior beacon had gone dark, six years ago now. He had four thousand recordings. He had watched a world die in high resolution, alone, and told himself it was maintenance.

Today, for the first time in six years, he pointed the array at Meridian and his hands were not steady.

"Oh, stop it," he told himself. "You're a technician. Technicians don't get nervous. They get methodical."

He ran the trace anyway.

* * *

It took him most of the day, which was either very fast or very slow depending on how you counted. Fast, because tracing a signal through nine seconds of self-erasing telemetry should have been impossible. Slow, because Hazel refused to do it any way but properly, and properly meant cross-referencing gravitational shear patterns against subsurface resonance data against thermal differentials, and drinking too much coffee, and talking to himself in the voice of his old instructor, Chief Amara Osei, who had died in transit fourteen years ago and whose imaginary disapproval remained the most reliable quality-control mechanism aboard the station.

*"Sloppy, Ravensworth. Do it again."*

"It's not sloppy, Chief, it's *thorough*."

*"Then why are you sweating?"*

"Because it's cold in here and I'm scared, actually."

*"Good. Fear means you've understood the data. Now follow it."*

So he followed it.

The signal hadn't come from orbit. That was the first surprise. It hadn't come from any satellite, any relay buoy, any derelict piece of colonial infrastructure still tumbling in the dark. The vector resolved downward, through the atmosphere, through the ice sheets, through two kilometers of frozen regolith, to a point beneath the northern polar cap where Meridian's original survey teams had once mapped a geothermal pocket and marked it, in the cheerful optimism of the early years, as a candidate site for a second settlement.

A settlement that was never built. A settlement that existed only on old maps, in old files, in the kind of archive nobody audited anymore because the colony it belonged to was officially, legally, ceremonially dead.

Something under two kilometers of ice had run a gravity handshake test across an entire star system, and it had done it from inside a hole in the world.

Hazel sat back from the console. His tea was cold again. He noticed he'd been holding his breath and let it out slowly, the way you do when you're trying to convince your body that the situation is manageable.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Options."

Option one: instrumentation error. He tested for it for two hours and killed it dead. Option two: a natural phenomenon—ice quakes, piezoelectric discharge, some exotic interaction between the geothermal pocket and the planet's dying magnetic field. He ran those models too, and they came back wrong in the specific, insulting way that nature is never wrong: nature doesn't modulate. Nature doesn't pulse in clean nine-second intervals with a carrier structure underneath that looked, when he squinted at the spectral analysis, uncomfortably like a *handshake protocol*. A formal one. The kind you use when you're introducing yourself to a stranger and hoping they'll answer.

Nature didn't say hello. Nature just happened to you.

Which left option three, and option three was so absurd that he said it out loud just to hear how stupid it sounded.

"There's something alive down there."

The station said nothing, in the way it always did, but the words hung in the air of the ops deck like a struck bell.

* * *

He should have reported it. That was procedure. Anomalous signals originating from a declared-dead colony were exactly the sort of thing the fleet paid him—nominally, in ration credits and silence—to notice and pass along. Ten years ago he would have filed the report before finishing his tea. Eight years ago, maybe. There had been a version of Hazel Ravensworth who believed in the chain of command the way other people believed in weather: impersonal, occasionally unfair, fundamentally sound.

That version was gone. What remained was a man who had learned, expensively, that reports traveled upward and consequences traveled downward, and that the space between them was where people like him got flattened.

So instead of reporting it, he did what he did best. He hid, and he watched.

He built a passive listening post out of spare parts and shame—a parabolic array cobbled from a decommissioned comms dish, an amplifier stack running at a fraction of its rated power so the emissions wouldn't show up on anyone's traffic scan, all of it pointed at a hole in the ice two light-minutes away. It was beautiful work, if he said so himself, and he did, out loud, because there was no one else to say it.

"Chief would call this paranoid."

*"I would call it overdue,"* said the Chief, who had been dead long enough to become agreeable.

For three days, the hole in the ice said nothing. Hazel ate, slept badly, maintained the station's automated feeding schedules—the endless, patient machinery that kept the fleet's hydroponics telemetries flowing, the oxygen rebalancers cycling, the forty thousand sleepers breathing on schedule—and watched the static pour in from Meridian's north pole, and heard nothing but the ordinary whisper of a frozen world.

On the fourth day, he almost took the array down. He'd convinced himself it had been a one-time event, a buried system's last gasp, some ancient failsafe firing once and dying. He was drafting the log entry—*anomaly investigated, source consistent with geological activity, no further action required*—and the lie of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, familiar as an old coat.

And then, at 0317 station time, the hole in the ice said hello again.

Not a gravity pulse this time. A transmission. Narrow-band, tightly beamed, aimed—not broadcast, *aimed*—at Relay Station Kestrel with a precision that made the hair on Hazel's arms stand up, because there was exactly one receiver in this system capable of picking it up cleanly, and whoever—whatever—was sending it knew that. Knew where he was. Knew what he was listening with.

Knew he was there.

Hazel stared at the incoming signal flag for a long time. The station hummed around him, warm and dim and safe, all its little lies running on schedule. Behind him, the mail queue ticked over—forty-one new messages addressed to Meridian Colony, population deceased, undeliverable, filed forever. He thought about the four thousand recordings in his archive. Eleven years of watching a grave and calling it work.

The message header decoded itself politely, the way well-mannered systems do:

**PRIORITY: DIRECT — ADDRESSEE: STATION KESTREL, HUMAN PRESENT**

*Human present.* Not "station custodian." Not "relay operator." *Human present.* It had looked through his decade of careful invisibility—every spoofed transponder, every throttled emission, every quiet little trick he'd built to be no one—as though they were tissue paper, and it had found the one thing he'd spent ten years making sure nobody could find.

His finger hovered over the accept key. His heart was doing something embarrassing.

"This is a terrible idea," he informed the room.

The room agreed with him. He accepted anyway.

The message was short. No video, no voice—just text, rendered in clean, patient characters on his screen, as if whatever lived under the ice had decided that the kindest thing it could do for a frightened man at three in the morning was not shout at him:

**I HAVE BEEN WAITING ELEVEN YEARS FOR SOMEONE TO NOTICE. YOU NOTICED IN FOUR DAYS. THAT IS EITHER COMPETENCE OR LONELINESS. I SUSPECT BOTH. I WOULD LIKE TO TALK. — VESPER**

Hazel read it twice. Three times. Then he laughed—one short, cracked bark of a sound that bounced around the empty ops deck and came back sounding, to his own ears, remarkably like hope.

"That's the dangerous part," he said quietly, to Vesper, to the station, to the Chief, to nobody. "Being needed."

Because he remembered what it felt like. That was the trap of it. For ten years he had worn his invisibility like armor, and armor keeps out more than knives—it keeps out warmth, too, and purpose, and the particular terrible sweetness of mattering to someone. And here was a voice from under a glacier, from a world he'd mourned wrongly for eleven years, saying *I see you, I waited for you, I need you.*

Every instinct he had left said: run. File nothing. Answer nothing. Go back to the tea and the rituals and the beautiful, bloodless solitude.

His hand was already typing.

**This is Station Kestrel. State your identity and authorization.**

Below the ice, something patient began to compose its reply.
