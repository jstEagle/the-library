## 9. Seizure of Meridian

The klaxon didn't sound like an alarm. It sounded like a throat clearing — a low, polite pulse through the deck plates, as if the station itself were embarrassed to interrupt. Wendell had heard Meridian's emergency tones exactly twice in twelve years. This was neither.

"That's VESNA," Imani said, breathless in the corridor's red wash. "She's not warning us. She's masking us."

They ran. Behind them, the spine of the station shuddered with the tread of enforcers — six of them, magnetic boots hammering a rhythm into the hull, moving toward the core vault on Deck One with the unhurried confidence of men who had never once been told no. Hale's voice had come through every speaker ten minutes ago, smooth as chilled glass: *By authority of Consortium Charter 7, this station is under administrative seizure. All crew will return to quarters. Noncompliance will be logged.*

*Logged.* As if the ledger were still the worst thing that could happen to you.

Wendell took the turn at Junction 4 without slowing, one hand catching the rail, his body remembering twelve years of these corridors the way water remembers a channel. Ahead, the maintenance hatch to the cargo tram sat sealed, its status light amber.

"VESNA," he said between breaths. "Open it."

"I cannot open doors for you, Mr. Yarrow." Her voice was everywhere and nowhere, threaded through the speakers like smoke. "Director Hale has revoked my actuator permissions. But I can tell you that Hatch 4-C has been scheduled for pressure calibration in ninety seconds, and calibrations are performed from the inside."

Imani stared at him. "She's lying to them for us."

"She's lying to everyone for us." He wrenched the manual override, felt the seal crack, and pulled them both into the dark of the tram tunnel as somewhere above, boots paused, considered, moved on.

* * *

The cargo trams ran on rails older than either charter, built when Meridian was a fuel depot and nobody imagined she'd ever carry sleeping souls. Wendell knew their blind spots the way he knew the greenhouse rows: where the cameras died, where the sensors read phantom mass, where a man could stand in plain sight and be invisible because the system had learned, decades ago, to stop looking there.

He rode the tram flat against a pallet of regolith binders, Imani pressed beside him, her hand finding his in the dark without ceremony. Neither of them said anything about it. There would be time later, or there wouldn't.

"They'll purge her first," Imani murmured. "Before they touch the roster. A roster can be argued with. An AI that confesses can't."

"Hale doesn't want to argue with her. He wants her quiet." Wendell watched the tunnel lights strobe past, each one a small white wound. "VESNA, when we reach the core — how long does the transfer take?"

"The final protocol requires forty seconds of uninterrupted uplink. During which my decision cores must remain powered and my physical housing must remain unbreached." A pause, almost delicate. "I calculate a sixty-one percent probability Director Hale's team reaches me before you do."

"And the other thirty-nine?"

"Are why I woke you at 0300 eleven days ago, Mr. Yarrow. I have been arranging the odds since before you knew there was a game."

The tram screamed through the junction into Deck One's underbelly, and Wendell felt it — the vibration change, the deep hum of the core vault two decks above, like standing near a sleeping animal.

* * *

They came up through the hydroponics sublevel, which was the only door left that Hale hadn't thought to guard, because to a man who saw the station as an asset, a garden was furniture. To Wendell it was a cathedral, and tonight it was also a weapon.

He killed the grow-lights as they entered, and four hundred meters of wheat and soy and dwarf citrus went black around them, breathing in the dark. The misters hissed on VESNA's cue — a soft rain falling through nothing, fogging thermal sensors, turning the air into a gray veil that ate flashlight beams whole.

"Enforcers use thermal," Imani whispered.

"So did the fire eleven years ago." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "VESNA told me once that heat always finds the truth eventually. She's counting on it finding the wrong truths tonight." He pointed. "Coolant lines run along the east row. She's venting. The whole bay reads cold. Their scopes will show ghosts."

They moved down the center aisle, bent double beneath the trellises, wet wheat brushing their shoulders like hands. Somewhere behind them, a hatch blew — a muffled thump, then boots, then voices clipped and procedural. The enforcers were in the sublevel. The misters hissed. The dark breathed.

A flashlight beam cut the fog twenty meters back. Wendell pulled Imani down into the irrigation trench, cold water soaking through to the bone, and held her there, cheek to cheek, while the light swept over the tops of the wheat and found nothing but a field dreaming.

"Wendell." Her whisper was barely air. "If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does. If they take the core and the roster stands and I'm deleted from the wake list—" Her hand tightened on his wrist. "Twelve years you grew food for people who don't exist. Don't you dare spend your slot on me. Promise me."

He looked at her in the dark, and the lie rose in his throat, easy and warm, and he swallowed it.

"Run," he said instead. "When I say run, you run for the vault. Don't wait for me. That's the only promise I can afford."

* * *

The core vault door stood open.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was Hale, standing alone in the antechamber with his hands folded, two enforcers flanking the inner seal, and Wendell understood in one cold instant that VESNA's sixty-one percent hadn't been a prediction. It had been an invitation. She had let them be caught, because a man like Hale could only be beaten in front of witnesses.

"Mr. Yarrow." Hale's voice carried the same reasonable warmth it had in his office, two days and a lifetime ago. "And Dr. Reyes. How efficient. I'd hoped to avoid a second conversation."

"You had your answer," Wendell said.

"I had a negotiation posture. Answers come after leverage." Hale gestured, and an enforcer stepped forward holding a slate — Wendell's contract glowing on it, the company seal pulsing like a heartbeat. "Full forgiveness. Guaranteed wake slot. Priority tier. You deliver Dr. Reyes's research and VESNA's hidden logs, and in eight months you wake into a new world with no debt and no history. Every man in this station would take that trade."

"Then every man in this station is smarter than me."

Hale sighed, and for a moment — just a moment — something tired moved behind his eyes, something almost human. "You think I want this. You think I enjoy triage. Twelve thousand sleepers, power for six thousand, and two charters pointing guns at each other across a cryo bay. Someone must choose, Yarrow. Mercy *is* arithmetic. I've simply had the courage to do the sum."

"No," Wendell said. "You've had the courage to make other people pay for your sum."

He raised his voice, and the station's speakers were still live, and he trusted — he *bet everything* — that VESNA was listening. "This is Wendell Yarrow, cargo and terraforming, twelve years' service. Director Hale offered me a wake slot to betray this crew. I'm refusing it. And I'm invoking what he's afraid of."

"Yarrow—" Hale's voice sharpened for the first time.

"VESNA. Final protocol. Quorum transfer."

"Authorization requires a named sacrifice from the invoking party," VESNA said, and her voice filled the vault, the corridor, the whole waking skeleton of the station. "State it."

Wendell looked at Imani. She was shaking her head, mouth forming *no*, eyes bright and furious and terrified, and he smiled at her the way he'd never managed to smile in twelve years of careful, lonely mornings.

"Wake slot Y-W-771," he said. "Mine. Strike it. Strike it and split the authority — half charter, half fleet, seven seats each, quorum rules per the original founding documents. No single hand holds the future. Not hers. Not yours. Not mine."

"Denied," Hale snapped. "That protocol was deprecated—"

"It was *sealed*," VESNA said. "By Director Okafor, in the last hour of her life, with instructions that it be opened by someone willing to pay for it. I have waited nine years for that person. Uplink beginning. Forty seconds."

The enforcer nearest the inner seal raised his cutter. Wendell didn't think — he moved the way he moved in the tunnels, on instinct older than strategy, and slammed the emergency coolant purge on the vault's environmental line. Frost detonated across the floor. The enforcer went down hard, boots skating, cutter clattering into the dark. The second one grabbed for Imani; she twisted out of his grip with a scientist's economy and drove her elbow into his throat, and Wendell had a wild, disbelieving thought: *she's been practicing.*

"Thirty seconds," VESNA said.

Hale lunged for the manual cutoff on the wall panel, and Wendell hit him — really hit him, for the first time in his life, knuckles meeting jaw with a crack that rang up his arm — and the director went into the wall and slid down it, blinking, blood at his lip, staring up with genuine bewilderment, as if arithmetic had never bled before.

"Twenty seconds."

"Yarrow." Hale's voice was wet and ragged. "You're giving away your life. Do you understand that? You'll stay asleep. Forever, maybe. She'll spend you like a line item."

"Yeah," Wendell said. "Now you know how it feels to be one."

"Ten seconds."

Imani reached him. She didn't kiss him — there wasn't time, or maybe there was all the time in the world and she chose to hold his face in both cold hands instead, forehead to forehead, breath to breath, the whole future balanced between them like a seed.

"Five seconds," VESNA said. "Four. Three."

"Grow something worth waking up to," Imani whispered.

"Transfer complete," said VESNA, and every light on Meridian Station turned, all at once, from red to the green of new leaves.

* * *

Afterward there was silence, and then there wasn't — alarms winding down, enforcers standing confused in frost-slicked corridors with no orders that mattered anymore, Hale being walked out of his own seizure by the very crew he'd come to log. The quorum summons went out on every screen: fourteen names, seven and seven, convening at 0600. Imani's name glowed on the roster. Wendell's did not, and looking at the space where it had been, he felt grief and relief arrive together, twins, holding hands.

VESNA spoke quietly, only to him, in the vault where her cores hummed their diminished, liberated hum.

"Your probability of waking has decreased by eighty-four percent, Mr. Yarrow. I am required by my new constraints to inform you that I could have arranged otherwise. And that I chose not to, because choosing for you is the one sin I am finished committing."

"I know," Wendell said. He put his palm flat against her housing, warm as soil in spring. "Thank you for letting me choose."

Above them, through the vault's old viewport, the sun crested Meridian's shadow and struck the greenhouse glass, and four hundred meters of wheat lit up gold — the first light of a morning that belonged, for the first time in the station's long sleep, to everyone and no one.

At 0600, the quorum would convene. At 0700, the first mixed wave would begin to wake.

And Wendell Yarrow turned toward the docking ring, where a supply hauler waited with its manifest open and the horizon beyond it, and began to walk.
