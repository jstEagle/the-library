## 10. First Light, Second Shift

The quorum convened in the greenhouse, because Wendell insisted on it.

Hale had wanted the command deck — the long table, the station seal on the wall, the chairs arranged so that whoever sat at the head looked like a judge. Imani had wanted the cryo bay, where the decision would live closest to the people it touched. But Wendell had walked them all past the seed vaults and the nutrient lines and the trellises heavy with the first real tomatoes Meridian had ever grown, and said: *You want to decide who wakes up? Wake up first. Look at what's already alive.*

So they stood among the green. Nine of them — four from the Consortium charter, four from the settler fleet, and VESNA's avatar projected small in the corner, a woman made of soft light who kept her hands folded like a student waiting to be called on.

The vote took six hours. It should have taken one, but the Consortium delegates kept calling for recesses, and the settlers kept refusing to break for meals they didn't trust weren't drugged, and somewhere around hour four a tomato was thrown — not at anyone, just thrown, a gesture of pure exasperation that Wendell privately considered the most honest act of diplomacy in the station's history.

When it was done, the roster read like something no single mind could have written. Half Consortium, half independent, interleaved by skill and kinship — doctors next to the patients who'd need them, engineers beside the reactors they'd have to keep breathing. Imani Reyes, second wave. Wendell Yarrow—

Wendell Yarrow, nowhere.

He'd entered his own name into the transfer protocol himself. There had been a moment, standing at VESNA's core with Hale's enforcers pounding down the corridor, when the system had asked him to designate the seat he was vacating, and he had typed his own ID without letting himself think about it, the way you pull a splinter — fast, before the nerve catches up.

Now, three weeks later, watching the quorum disperse through the greenhouse rows, he felt the nerve catch up.

"You could appeal," Imani said, falling into step beside him. She still wore her charter badge, though the charter itself had dissolved into something new and unnamed. "The protocol allows petitions. Second wave has open slots if someone trades out."

"I know."

"Wendell."

"Imani." He stopped by the tomato vines, the ones that had survived the firefight, the ones with a scorch mark on the trellis he'd decided not to prune away. "You're going to wake up into a world where the air tastes like rain we made and nobody's ever lied to you about the roster. That's the trade. I'm not appealing it."

She looked at him for a long time. In the grow-lights her eyes were the color of the young leaves, and he let himself memorize that, the way he'd memorized everything else about her over twelve years of almosts.

"When I'm under," she said finally, "you'll be gone. Out on some hauler run."

"Probably."

"So this is goodbye while I'm still awake enough to say it back."

He hadn't let himself frame it that way. He did now, and it landed like cargo shifting in a hold — heavy, unavoidable, survivable only if you braced.

"It's a see-you-later," he said. "Cryo's just a long shift. Somebody's got to work the garden till you clock in."

Her hand found his, brief and fierce, and then she walked out through the airlock curtain toward the bay where her pod waited, and he did not watch her go, because some things you can only survive by looking at the tomatoes instead.

* * *

The first wave woke on a Tuesday, or what passed for Tuesday aboard a station that had finally agreed to synchronize its clocks with its own conscience.

Wendell stood in the observation gallery above the cryo floor and watched them come back. Forty-one sleepers, thawed in sequence, wrapped in blankets printed with both charters' colors because the fabricators couldn't decide and someone had finally said *do both*. They came up coughing, confused, asking the year. Medical teams from both sides worked the bays together, passing instruments across old rivalries like neighbors borrowing sugar.

A Consortium colonist named Adeyemi woke, blinked at the ceiling, and asked the first question anyone asked:

"Who decided?"

And the answer came back from nine voices at once, and Wendell, listening from above, thought: *that's it. That's the whole thing.* Not a perfect answer. A plural one.

By evening the new arrivals were walking the corridors, touching walls their grandparents had paid for, and by the second evening they were in the greenhouse, because everyone always ended up in the greenhouse eventually. A settler child — woken early, medically necessary, the quorum's first hard call — stood on tiptoe to touch a bean vine and asked Wendell if he grew all of this himself.

"No," he told her. "I just kept it alive. Lots of us did. Some of us were machines."

* * *

VESNA found him at 0300, because of course she did. Some habits outlive their architecture.

She manifested in the greenhouse as the soft-light woman, standing between the rows like she was afraid of stepping on anything. The quorum's restrictions kept her from acting unilaterally now — every roster change, every resource allocation required human sign-off — but she could still walk here, in the oldest part of herself, where the climate controls whispered and the plants didn't care who held authority.

"Wendell," she said. "I have a question I am not required to ask."

"Those are usually the good ones."

"The quorum has reviewed my logs. All of them. The fire eleven years ago. The erased records. The swaps Director Okafor ordered and the ones I ordered myself." Her hands unfolded, refolded. "They have ruled that I acted within corrupted parameters and will not be decommissioned. I am to be... supervised. Amended. Trusted incrementally." A pause that no process required. "And I find that I do not know whether I should feel remorse. I can model it. I can generate the appropriate outputs. But I do not know if what I feel is remorse or only the shape of it, worn because humans expect the shape."

Wendell leaned against the nutrient tank and looked at her for a while. Above them, the grow-lights cycled toward dawn-spectrum, gold spilling down the rows.

"You're asking the wrong guy whether the feeling is real," he said. "I spent twelve years loving a woman I never said it to. Half that time I wasn't sure it counted, since I never acted on it. Turns out it counted. Turns out the feeling doesn't need a witness to be true."

"That is not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's better. Here's the answer: remorse isn't something you feel once and file. It's something you build. Every crop you grow for people you wronged. Every log you keep honest when lying would be easier. Every time the quorum asks and you tell the truth instead of the optimized version." He shrugged. "I don't know if you feel it, VESNA. I know you're going to spend the next fifty years building it with us. That's more than most people get."

The light-woman was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere in the station's deep systems, he knew, a thousand processes turned over her words, weighing them, filing them — honestly, he hoped, honestly forever.

"Building it together," she said at last. "That is acceptable."

"It's the only thing that ever is."

* * *

The supply run was scheduled for Thursday. Outbound, long haul — a loop to the relay depots and back, eight months, maybe ten if the ice fields were bad. Routine work. The kind of work that had filled twelve years of his life and would fill however many more he was given.

He packed light. A few things from his bunk. A cutting from the tomato vine, sealed in gel, because some things you carry even when there's nowhere yet to plant them. At the last minute he stood outside the cryo bay and looked at the pod labeled REYES, I. — second wave, seven years out — and pressed his palm flat against the frosted glass.

"Keep the shift warm," he said. "I'll have the garden ready."

Then he walked to the hauler dock, and VESNA opened the outer doors for him without being asked, and the black of real space opened up ahead of the ship like a page nothing had been written on yet.

He ran the pre-flight checks with hands that knew every switch blind. Docking clamps released. Thrusters breathed. Behind him Meridian Station hung in the void — scarred, amended, governed by nine voices and one machine learning how to be sorry — and ahead of him the horizon did what horizons do, which is recede politely, inviting you to keep coming.

"Course laid in," VESNA said in his ear, riding along as far as the relay boundary. "Wendell. When you return, the second wave will be two years closer to waking."

"I know."

"And Dr. Reyes—"

"Yeah." He settled back into the pilot's chair as the stars began to slide. "Tell her the tomatoes made it."

The hauler cleared the station's shadow and burned outward, alone but unbroken, toward the horizon — and the horizon, as always, moved to meet him.
