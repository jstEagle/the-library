## 1. The Garden of Unborn Seasons

The morning light on Meridian came late and reluctant, filtered through a sky the color of old pewter, and Jude Whitlock had learned to greet it the way one greets a stranger who keeps promising to visit and never does. He rose at 0530 by the station clock — a habit inherited from no one, taught to him by no one, simply the shape his days had worn into themselves over eleven years — and he stood for a moment in the doorway of his quarters, listening to the station breathe.

It breathed. That was the strange mercy of it. The atmospheric processors cycled with a low tidal hum, the water reclamation units murmured in the walls like sleeping animals, and somewhere beneath the floor, the geothermal taps ticked as they had ticked since before Jude was born. Meridian Station was built to hold four hundred souls. It held one. And still it breathed around him, patient and mechanical, a body waiting for its blood.

He made tea from dried nettle he grew himself in the hydroponic annex, because real tea had run out in year six and the supply ships had stopped coming in year nine. He drank it standing at the observation window, watching the frost burn off the terraced fields below — fields plowed and seeded and plowed again, waiting for farmers who existed only as names on a manifest.

At 0600, he began his rounds.

The seed vault occupied the eastern wing of the station, three levels deep, climate-sealed and redundant-backed, the most protected space on the entire continent. It was also, Jude thought some mornings, the loneliest room ever built by human hands. Row upon row of cryo-drawers, each labeled in careful print: *wheat, Durum strain 7. Soybean, drought-adapted. Apple, heirloom varietals, batch 12.* Forty thousand accessions. Every crop a generation of planners had believed Meridian would need. Every one of them asleep, waiting for a spring that kept not coming.

He walked the aisles slowly, checking readouts he could have checked from his terminal, because walking them was the point. The drawers glowed their soft amber standby-light, thousands of small suns in miniature, and Jude touched each one as he passed — not all of them, that would take hours, but enough. A fingertip against cold polymer. A nod, almost. *Still here. Still here.*

"You're early," he told drawer 4-117, which held barley. "You're always early."

His voice sounded absurd in the vault's hush, and he let it sound absurd. That was another thing he'd learned: to be ridiculous on purpose was a kind of armor. If you spoke to seeds, you were eccentric. If you stopped speaking altogether, you were something else, something the silence could get into.

At 0700, he recorded the morning log.

This was the ritual that mattered most, though he would never have admitted it. He settled into the comms alcove, squared his shoulders out of habit even though there was no camera discipline left to maintain, and pressed record.

"Meridian Station daily log, cycle four thousand two hundred and nineteen. Exterior temperature minus eleven Celsius, trending seasonal. Atmospheric processors nominal at ninety-six percent efficiency. Soil remediation complete on terraces five through eight; nitrogen fixation holding without supplement, which is better than projected." He paused, sipped his nettle tea, watched steam curl in the alcove's cold light. "Seed vault status: all forty thousand accessions viable. Germination tests scheduled for next week, sample batch thirty-one. No anomalies."

Then the part he never skipped, the part that took longest, the part that had once been mandated and was now entirely his own:

"To the settlers of the second wave, when you arrive: good morning. Today I finished clearing the irrigation channels on the east terraces. The water runs clear now, cold and fast, and if you stand at the headgate at dawn the light comes down the channel like something poured. I've left the southern slope unplanted. I know the plan said root vegetables, but I think it wants orchard trees, and I'd rather argue with you about it in person than decide alone." Another pause. His voice did something complicated on the word *alone* that he pretended not to hear. "The wildflowers on the ridge are blooming early this year. Purple and white, small as fingernails. You'll want to see them. That's all for today. Jude Whitlock, terraformer second class, signing off."

He had made four thousand two hundred and nineteen of these logs. In the first years, they had gone out on the colony band, relayed toward the shipping lanes, answered sometimes by an automated acknowledgment that felt almost like company. Then the war had thickened above them like weather, the relays had gone quiet, and now the logs went nowhere at all. He recorded them anyway. A letter written is not the same as a letter sent, but neither is it nothing.

He spent the middle of the day outside, because the body needed work or it began to believe the mind's worst suggestions. He repaired a section of windbreak fencing, chipped ice from the cistern intake, and stood for a long time on the ridge among the tiny flowers, letting the wind push at him the way a large animal might nudge something it hadn't decided whether to eat.

The grief, when it came, came sideways, the way it always did. Not as a memory but as a fact of physics: the weight of four hundred empty bunks, forty thousand sleeping seeds, four thousand logs addressed to no one. He had been twenty-three when he was assigned here, a low-caste terraformer with a genetic-memory implant and a contract nobody else wanted, told the settlers were eighteen months behind him. Eighteen months had become eleven years. Somewhere up there, Earth and the colonies were burning each other's skies, and Meridian had fallen through every crack in every ledger until only Jude remembered it was supposed to have people.

He ate dinner alone at the long table in the mess hall, at the seat nearest the window, because sitting at the far end where four hundred would someday gather felt too much like a lie he couldn't afford.

That night, the glitch came.

* * *

He was in the seed vault after hours, running the germination viability scan on batch thirty-one, when his implant stuttered.

Jude felt it first as a wrongness in his teeth. Then the world tilted — not physically, nothing so honest — but inside, behind his eyes, where the implant lived in its lattice of engineered neurons. The readout display hovering at the edge of his vision shredded into static, and through the static came a fragment: heat, and the smell of turned earth after rain, and a woman's voice saying words he could not quite resolve, urgent and close, as if her mouth were beside his ear.

He gripped the edge of the scanner console. The amber lights of the cryo-drawers swam. For three seconds — he counted them later, obsessively, the way he counted everything now — he was somewhere else, or someone else, standing in this same vault but younger, newer, the labels on the drawers printed in a different hand, and someone was laughing in the corridor beyond the door, more than one someone, a whole station full of someones—

Then it stopped. The static cleared. The vault was silent, and cold, and held exactly one heartbeat.

Jude stood very still, breathing carefully, the way you breathe when you're trying not to startle a dangerous animal. The genetic-memory implants were standard issue for terraformers — compressed instructional archives, techniques and taxonomies, the accumulated how-to of a hundred worlds. They did not contain laughter. They did not contain rain-smell. They did not contain women's voices saying urgent things directly into your skull.

He ran a diagnostic. The implant reported itself clean, nominal, uncorrupted. Diagnostics always did; he knew that the way he knew the taste of nettle tea. Whatever had moved through him had moved through the parts of the machine that didn't report to machines.

He should have logged it. Protocol said log everything, report anomalies to the colonial authority on Relay Nine, wait for instructions that would take a decade to arrive if they arrived at all. Instead he sat down on the floor between the wheat drawers and the soybean drawers, knees drawn up like a child, and replayed the fragment in memory — his memory, whatever that meant now.

Heat. Earth after rain. Her voice. And underneath the words he couldn't resolve, one thing had come through whole, one thing the static hadn't touched, and it was this: she had been afraid, and she had been certain, and she had been speaking to someone specific. Not broadcasting. Addressing. The way you speak into a letter you know will outlive you.

The way he spoke into his logs, every morning, to settlers who would never come.

Jude sat in the amber dark for a long time. Above him, through the vault's shielding, the war moved in its slow invisible orbits, and the frost rebuilt itself on the terraces, and the station breathed its patient mechanical breath around its single occupant.

When he finally stood, his legs were stiff and his tea, forgotten on the console, had gone cold. He looked down the long aisle of sleeping seeds, thousands of small suns, and found that his loneliness had changed shape overnight. It was no longer an emptiness. It was a suspicion.

Someone had been here before him. Someone had hidden something in the only place on Meridian that was built to keep things safe.

And whatever she had buried in the dark with the seeds, it had just begun to wake up — and it knew his name.
