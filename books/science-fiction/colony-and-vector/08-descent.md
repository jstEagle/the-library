## 8. Descent

The *Kestrel's Wake* had been Sera Okonkwo's home for eleven years, and she was going to burn it in four minutes.

She stood on the bridge — a cramped wedge of salvaged consoles and hand-painted bulkheads that the colonial fleet would have called a corvette and she called a coffin with good lighting — and watched Meridian's surface come apart beneath her. Earth's bombardment ships hung in high orbit like a row of patient surgeons, and every few seconds one of them exhaled fire. The strikes walked across the southern plains in slow, deliberate lines: not trying to kill anyone, not yet. Trying to erase something. Craters bloomed where the old survey stations used to be. The terraforming grid flickered and died sector by sector, a map of lights going out like a city at curfew.

"Jude." She keyed the comm, kept her voice level the way you keep your hands level when the deck is shaking. "They're hitting the vault perimeter in ninety seconds. Tell me you're sealed."

His voice came back thin through static, threaded with the strange doubled cadence he'd carried since the archive below — Ilsa Reyes living somewhere behind his eyes now, two griefs wearing one face. "Sealed. The cradle's holding my biometrics steady. Sera—" A pause. On the open channel she could hear the garden around him: water cycling, the soft mechanical breathing of the seed vault, ten thousand unborn seasons waiting in their drawers of glass and frost. "The shaft entrance is under the north terrace. Once I'm in, it seals behind me."

"I know."

"You'll be alone up here."

"I've been alone before," she said, and cut the channel before he could argue, because arguing cost time and time was the only currency either of them had left.

On her tactical display, the colonial fleet was coming in low and hot from the western limb — her fleet, technically, though the order that had arrived an hour ago had made the word *technically* do a great deal of work. *Seize the carrier. Detain Whitlock. Signal integrity is secondary to signal possession.* Command wanted Ilsa Reyes's testimony the way a drowning man wants air, and they did not care what got crushed getting it. Earth wanted the same thing, just with different flags on the missiles.

And between them, descending into the planet's throat in a maintenance lift older than both fleets, was a terraformer who smelled like soil and carried the memories of everyone his government had abandoned.

Sera settled into the pilot's couch and began, methodically, to commit treason.

* * *

The plan had taken shape over three days of shared meals neither of them trusted and one long night in the flooded archive, when Jude had surfaced from the memory bleed sobbing names he'd never learned and she had held his shoulders and felt, through her gloves, the tremor of a dead woman's grief moving through him. They hadn't spoken about it afterward. Some things you don't narrate; you just build on top of them.

Now she ran the numbers again because running them again was easier than thinking about what came after.

The relay sat at Meridian's core — or near enough; the old colonists had drilled their transmission spine down through forty kilometers of mantle rock, a needle of engineered lattice threading the whole planet, designed to broadcast to a generation of settlers who were never sent. To reach it, Jude needed the lift, the lift needed power, and power meant the geothermal tap at Station Nine, which meant the surface, which meant the bombardment corridor.

Which meant someone had to make Earth's gunners look elsewhere.

Her display showed the *Wake's* systems green across the board. Reactor at full burn. Jamming arrays spun up, drawing so much power the cabin lights dimmed and the air recyclers dropped to a wheeze. She had stripped the ship for this — pulled the med bay's reserves, the water reclamation core, everything portable, and loaded it into the supply skiff docked beneath the bridge. Enough to keep the station alive for a year after she was gone. She'd told herself it was logistics. She knew it was a will.

"Sera." Jude again, quieter now. The lift was moving; she could hear the deep subsonic groan of it through the comm, stone swallowing sound. "I'm reading your reactor signature. You're running it hot enough to be seen from orbit."

"That's the idea."

"That's suicide."

"It's a diversion," she corrected. "Suicide is when there's nothing worth the dying. There's a whole planet down there full of testimony somebody thought was worth forty years of hiding. I'm just the courier's escort." She watched the first Earth strike bracket Station Nine's coordinates — close, too close, the shockwave rippling through her hull sensors like a fist on a drum. "Jude. When you get to the relay—"

"Don't," he said.

"—you say what she couldn't. All of it. Names, dates, the orders, the vote. Everything Earth buried and everyone who starved while they buried it. You put it into every receiver in this system until nobody can pretend they didn't hear."

A long silence. Through the channel she heard him breathing, or maybe it was Ilsa breathing, or maybe it was both of them, some merged rhythm that belonged to neither and both. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough. "Come with me. The lift takes two. We jam nothing, we hide the *Wake* in the ash shadow, we go dark, and we let the relay do its work without anyone burning for it."

"And when the loop closes and both fleets come down here looking for whoever opened it? You think they stop at the surface?" She shook her head at a man who couldn't see her. "Someone has to be the loud thing, Jude. That's always been someone's job. Today it's mine."

"You said you'd never go back to being invisible," he said. "You said that's what they did to people like us. People from colonies that don't make the news feeds."

"I did say that." She smiled, and it hurt her face. "Turns out there's more than one way to be seen."

* * *

She brought the *Kestrel's Wake* up out of the atmosphere at 0340 station time, engines screaming against gravity she no longer had any reason to respect, and turned her nose toward the Earth formation like a challenge thrown down in a bar.

They took the bait the way she'd known they would. You don't ignore a warship burning straight at your line with active jamming blanketing half a hemisphere — especially not when your intelligence officers have spent three days telling you the colonial signal is *right there*, on that ship, in that hull. Two Earth destroyers peeled off the bombardment line to intercept. On the far side of the planet, her own fleet adjusted course, hungry, certain, closing on the position she was broadcasting with the shameless clarity of a lighthouse.

Everyone looking at her. Nobody looking at the north terrace, where a shaft of ancient engineering had just swallowed a man carrying the truth of a war.

"Attention unidentified vessel," crackled the Earth channel, all frost and procedure. "You are entering a declared exclusion zone. Kill your jamming and stand down or you will be fired upon."

Sera leaned back in the couch, put her boots up on a console that had survived three campaigns and wouldn't survive a fourth, and opened the fleet-wide channel — all of it, Earth and colonial alike, every ear in the system.

"This is Captain Sera Okonkwo, Colonial Defense Fleet, registry 77-Kestrel. And I have a story to tell you."

She talked. That was the whole weapon: talk. She fed them fragments — the flicker pattern in Meridian's mass, the nine seconds of falling, the name Ilsa Reyes repeated until it became a drumbeat — enough to hook them, not enough to satisfy, keeping every ship in the system fixed on her voice while below them the lift sank through basalt and heat. She told them about a seed vault tended by one man for settlers who never came. She told them about logs recorded year after year into silence. She watched the Earth destroyers' weapons charge and kept talking, because as long as she was talking, they were listening, and as long as they were listening, they weren't firing at the ground.

The first salvo took the *Wake's* port array and threw her sideways in her harness. Warning tones layered over each other, a choir of alarms. Fire suppression gas hissed from the ceiling. Somewhere aft, something structural gave up with a sound like a whale dying.

She kept talking.

The second salvo she rode out behind the wreck of her own jamming drone, hull scorched, thrusters coughing plasma, and by then the colonial fleet was in engagement range of the Earth picket and nobody — nobody — was watching Meridian anymore. The two formations slid together like tectonic plates, missiles crossing in the dark, and Sera Okonkwo sat in the middle of the collision she had arranged and felt, absurdly, calm. Eleven years of following orders written by people who had voted, she now knew, to let a colony die in the dark. Eleven years of saluting the signature on those orders.

This was better. This was finally honest work.

"Captain Okonkwo." Her own fleet's channel now, her commodore's voice, shaking with fury. "You are ordered to cease broadcast and surrender your vessel immediately. You are relieved of command. You are—"

"With respect, sir," she said, cutting him off for the first time in her career, "check your priority traffic in about twenty minutes. Then decide whether you want that sentence on the record."

She killed the channel. She checked the lift telemetry one last time — forty kilometers down and falling, Jude's lifesign steady inside the cradle-glow of borrowed memory — and then she looked at the tactical plot, at the beautiful stupid chaos she'd made of two fleets' attention, and allowed herself exactly five seconds to feel afraid.

Five seconds. Then she rolled the dying *Wake* toward the Earth flagship, jamming at maximum, transponder screaming her name and her registry and her crime, and bought the next ten minutes with everything she had left.

Below her, deeper than bombs could reach, a door sealed over a shaft, and a man began to run toward the center of the world.

* * *

The bridge burned around her in slow motion — smoke the color of dusk, emergency lights pulsing red like a heartbeat refusing to quit. Sera braced in the couch, hands still dancing across consoles that answered less and less, and watched the last telemetry feed from the planetary grid: the lift arriving, the relay chamber opening, a single figure stepping into a cathedral of light forty kilometers beneath the bombardment and the battle and the noise.

Earth's final transmission loop was already spinning up above her, its countdown bleeding through every intercepted channel. Minutes now. Maybe less.

"Hold them off," she whispered to the wreck of her ship, to the fires, to the eleven years. "Just hold them."

On the plot, the loop's clock ticked toward lock, and far below, Jude Whitlock raised his hands to the oldest machine on Meridian — and began to speak.
