## 12. The Missing Week

The ledger didn't lie. That was the whole point of it.

Rosalind had built the thing himself, ten years ago, in the first month of his caretaker contract: a physical logbook, paper and ink, kept in a drawer that had no network connection and no reason to exist except that he trusted it more than he trusted himself. Every entry in his own handwriting. Dates, hours, tasks performed, anomalies noted. The vault's spine, written down by the only person who walked it daily.

He sat in the caretaker's office with the book open under a work lamp, and he read the same page for the fourth time.

**Cycle 3,412 — 3,419: [seven days unaccounted]**

That wasn't what he'd written. He knew what he'd written. He'd written *Cycle 3,412–3,419: coolant loop replacement, stack nine through fourteen, solo.* He could almost see the ink. But the ink on the page said something else now—faded, water-stained, ordinary—and where his memory insisted there had been eight lines of maintenance detail, there was instead a gap so smooth it looked intentional. Not torn out. Rewritten. The paper itself was different in that stretch, a slightly warmer white, as if someone had replaced pages and aged them to match.

Someone had gotten into his drawer. Into his paper. Into the one record he'd kept because he thought no one could touch it.

He turned back ten years of entries with hands that wouldn't stay steady. The gap was the only one. Everywhere else the ledger matched his memory like a mirror—dull, exact, boring. But that week, that single week a decade ago, was a hole shaped like a person.

His person.

*You were somewhere,* he told himself. *People lose weeks. Grief does it. Isolation does it. You live alone in a dead vault; your brain is not a reliable witness.*

But his brain had never done this before. And the paper had never lied before either.

He copied the cycle numbers onto his forearm in grease pencil, an old hauler habit, then closed the ledger and went looking for what the vault remembered better than he did.

* * *

The vault's environmental systems were older than the corporation that now claimed to own them, and that was the only reason they still worked. Founders' hardware. Analog sensors, hardline telemetry, a building that kept its own diary in pressure logs and thermal traces whether anyone asked or not.

Rosalind spent two days in the sub-basement reading those diaries.

The air-handling records were the key. A sealed facility breathes, and every breath is logged: which rooms drew air, when, how much. He pulled the decade-old atmospheric data and graphed it against the missing week, and there it was—the vault had breathed differently for seven days. Medbay-level filtration active. Cryo-support power draw. A signature he recognized from hauling medical cargo: stasis pod, occupied, cycling.

Someone had been in cryostasis in his vault during the week that didn't exist.

And the pod bay listed in the draw was one he'd never been to. Sub-level four. His access card opened it without hesitation—which meant his card was always supposed to open it, which meant whoever rewrote his ledger hadn't bothered rewriting his permissions, because why would they? He wasn't supposed to remember the room existed. You don't lock a door you've erased from the map.

The lift down took ninety seconds. He counted all of them.

* * *

Sub-level four was cold in the way places are cold when the cold has been running uninterrupted for years. Frost furred the doorframe. His breath came out in ghosts. Emergency strips lit along the floor at his approach, dim amber, guiding him down a corridor that had no dust on its center line—because something had been wheeled along it, recently enough, more than once.

The medbay opened up around him like a held breath.

Six cryo-pods in a row, five of them empty, lids raised, interior lights long dead. The sixth stood at the end, lid closed, frost thick as fur on the glass, and beneath the frost a soft blue pulse. Still powered. Still drawing. Ten years of quiet electricity keeping something at four degrees above nothing.

He already knew what he'd find inside. Some part of him had known since the ledger. But knowing a thing and standing in front of it are different animals, and his body understood this before his mind did: his legs stopped working three meters out. He stood on the frozen floor of a buried room and looked at a coffin-shaped machine and could not make himself walk the last few steps.

The pod's status display glowed through the frost, patient as a headstone:

**SUBJECT: COLDHARBOUR, R.**
**SESSION: POST-TRAUMATIC MEMORY SUPPRESSION — FULL SPECTRUM**
**DURATION: 168 HRS**
**STATUS: COMPLETE**

Full spectrum. Not a trim, not a softening around the edges. They hadn't removed the week. They'd removed everything the week was attached to, pruned the branch and cauterized the stump, and left him a man named Rosalind Coldharbour who hauled cargo and kept seeds and never once wondered why the name felt like a coat borrowed from someone bigger.

Kestrel's Landing. The docking codes in his hands. The red door. His mother's voice saying a word he still couldn't hear.

They'd wiped him here. In his own vault. In the week his own handwriting couldn't account for, because during that week his handwriting had belonged to someone else—someone who signed consent forms, or didn't sign anything at all, someone lying in this pod while technicians adjusted the dose.

He walked the last three meters.

The frost on the glass had thinned where breath had fogged it, over and over, across years. His own breath. He'd been down here before. He'd stood exactly here, probably more than once, pressing his face toward the glass, feeling grief with no object, the way you feel rain falling somewhere you can't see. And then he'd gone back upstairs and logged his cycles and hauled his cargo and called the ache insomnia.

Rosalind put his palm flat on the lid. The metal was so cold it burned.

"Show me," he said. His voice cracked the silence and immediately sounded small in it. "Whatever's left. Show me."

The pod's interface woke at his touch—caretaker credentials, full access, because of course they'd given him the keys to the room they'd erased. Session logs scrolled. Most of the pre-wipe data was flagged purged, overwritten, gone the way Kestrel's Landing was gone, the way everything Halcyon touched went. But suppression sessions leave residue. The machine had recorded what it took, even if it couldn't give it back: a manifest of amputations.

**REMOVED: EPISODE CLUSTER — FAMILY (MATERNAL)**
**REMOVED: EPISODE CLUSTER — RESIDENCE (KESTREL'S LANDING, SURFACE DISTRICT 2)**
**REMOVED: EPISODE CLUSTER — EVENT: ORBITAL DEBRIS / CASUALTY NOTIFICATION**
**RETAINED: PROCEDURAL MEMORY — DOCKING PROTOCOLS, KESTREL STANDARD**

Casualty notification. He read it four times. Somewhere in the missing week, someone had told him his family was dead, and he had grieved so completely that grieving had become the crime scene, and Halcyon had come in after and cleaned it. Left the wound, removed the knife, removed the hand that held it, removed the war.

He laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry note with nothing behind it, echoing off six pods.

Procedural memory retained. Docking protocols. That was why his hands knew Kestrel's Landing—because grief could be cut out but muscle couldn't. They'd taken his mother and left him her harbor codes. Taken the red door and left him the motion of opening it. He was walking archive residue, a man made of other people's careful deletions, and he'd spent ten years being grateful for how smoothly his life ran.

He slid down the pod's flank until he was sitting on the frozen floor, back against the machine that had eaten him, and for a long time he did nothing at all. The amber strips hummed. The frost crept. Somewhere overhead, centuries of seeds waited in their stacks, patient as they'd been for generations, and he envied them—dormant things, waiting things, things that got to keep everything they were and just pause.

Grief, when it finally arrived, arrived without images. That was the cruelest architecture of it. He mourned a mother whose voice he'd lost twice—once to death, once to surgery. He mourned a home he could describe only in fragments: a red door, a gravity slightly lighter than standard, the smell of something cooking that he would spend the rest of his life failing to identify. He mourned the version of himself who had wept in this room, who at least had known what he was crying about. That man had been robbed of even his own funeral.

"I'm sorry," he said—to the pod, to the boy with the red door, to whoever he'd been before the week. "I don't remember you. I'm sorry I don't remember you."

The pod pulsed its slow blue answer, which was no answer, which was just electricity doing its job.

* * *

It took him most of a day to stop shaking, and when he finally climbed back into the lift, he was no longer the man who'd ridden it down. That man had suspected. This one knew. And knowledge rearranged the furniture of fear: Halcyon hadn't rewritten his ledger last month or last year. They'd done it a decade ago and maintained it ever since, quietly, the way you maintain anything important. Which meant someone still checked. Which meant someone still cared whether Rosalind Coldharbour stayed a fiction.

Which meant the moment he stopped being one, they'd know instantly.

He stood in the caretaker's office and looked at the false ledger, at the smooth white pages where his week used to be, and understood the choice sitting in front of him with terrible clarity. He could close the drawer. Go back to hauling cargo under a name that fit like a borrowed coat. Stay invisible, stay safe, stay hollow—a curated man in a curated life, surviving beautifully inside someone else's edit.

Or he could take the residue and build something true out of it.

His forearm still bore the grease-pencil cycle numbers. He looked at them a long moment, then added one line beneath, in the same old hauler shorthand, the language of men who write things down because the universe keeps trying to unsay them:

*Sub-4. Pod six. They took the week. Find who sold it.*

Because a wipe has two ends. Someone had programmed it. Someone had signed off. And machines that delete people keep records of the deletion—Halcyon taught him that much tonight, in a frozen room, with a dead woman's son pressed against the glass.

He was reaching for the comm to call Ilsa Vann when the vault's perimeter alert chimed, soft and courteous, the sound it made for scheduled deliveries.

Except nothing was scheduled. And the transponder pinging at his dock was broadcasting a registry code he'd only ever seen in Meridian's corporate traffic lanes.

Halcyon had come to check on their edit.
