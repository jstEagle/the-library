## 13. Betrayal at Meridian

Ilsa Vann had spent thirty years learning how information died, and she knew the smell of it before she saw anything wrong.

Meridian Station smelled like that now. Not literally—Meridian smelled the way it always had, of recycled air and fry-oil and the sweet chemical bite of coolant leaking somewhere in the lower decks, a leak nobody would ever fix because Meridian was a waystation, not a home, and waystations were allowed to rot in places no one looked. But underneath all of that, under the noise of the market concourse and the argument between two dockhands over a pallet of water reclamation filters, there was a wrongness she could feel in her teeth. The same feeling she'd had at Halcyon, the day before the Kestrel protocol went live, when everyone in the memory-integrity division had been asked to sign a supplemental nondisclosure and nobody asked why.

She'd signed it. That was the thing about herself she could never scrub out, no matter how many paper records she hoarded like contraband relics: she always signed.

"You're doing the face," Rosalind said beside her.

They were walking the concourse with the unhurried gait of people who belonged here, which was its own kind of forgery. Ilsa had spent a decade perfecting the walk of someone unremarkable; Rosalind had spent one perfecting the walk of someone else entirely. The archive core rode in a cargo case between them, disguised as seed-stock samples—technically true, which was the best kind of disguise—and every meter they moved it closer to the Ferrous Daughters' rendezvous berth felt like carrying an open flame through a paper forest.

"I'm doing threat assessment," she said.

"You're doing the face you did on Ceres Row when you told me the paper archives were real."

"They *are* real."

"That's not what I said."

He was right, which was annoying. She adjusted her grip on the case handle. "The contact is late."

"Contacts are always late. It's how you know they're contacts."

"Dezirae Okafor is never late." The name came out of her mouth and immediately tasted wrong. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. Names were weight. "She was never late," Ilsa corrected. "Ten years ago. When I knew her."

Rosalind glanced at her. Under the dockworker's cap and the cheap jacket he was still the most watchful person she'd ever met, a man built entirely out of checking exits. "You vouched for her."

"I said she *was* reliable. Past tense. I also said we should find another route, and you said—" 

"—every other route goes through Halcyon-monitored space, yes, I remember my own bad decisions, thank you." He exhaled through his nose. "We're committed. Berth 44, ninety minutes. We do the handoff, we're on the Daughters' ship, and this thing stops being our problem."

"It doesn't stop being our problem. It becomes everyone's problem. That's the point."

"Poetic. Keep walking."

But Ilsa kept cataloguing. It was the archivist's curse: she couldn't stop indexing. Dezirae Okafor, junior records clerk, Meridian civil registry, whom Ilsa had known in another life—the life before the disgrace, before she'd walked out of Halcyon with a head full of protocols and a conscience full of holes. Dezirae who had once smuggled Ilsa a printout of her own personnel file because Ilsa suspected it had been edited and wanted to know by how much. Answer: substantially. Dezirae who had cried about it, quietly, in a stairwell, and then helped anyway.

That was ten years ago. People changed. People got bought. People got *approached*, which was worse than bought, because approached people still believed they were free.

And there was the other thing. The thing she'd been chewing since they'd docked.

"Her file's gone," Ilsa said quietly.

"What?"

"In the public registry terminal back at customs. I checked. Dezirae Okafor, Meridian civil registry, employment record—it returned a null result. Not 'no record found.' Null. A redacted-format null." She watched his jaw tighten. "I wrote those null formats, Rosalind. I helped design them. A missing person gets a gap. An erased person gets a *shape* of absence, formatted to look like clerical error. Someone reached into Meridian's registry and pulled her out with a corporate tool."

"Maybe she left. Maybe she's dead."

"Then her file would show a gap, or a death flag. This shows erasure. Which means either Dezirae is already gone the way Kestrel's Landing is gone—" she heard her own voice flatten, the way it did when she was most frightened, "—or she's working for them now, and they cleaned her trail as a courtesy."

They walked twenty meters in silence. Somewhere above them a cargo lift groaned under load, and the station's ancient bones ticked as it rotated through its slow false dawn.

"So we abort," Rosalind said finally.

"We can't abort. There's no second contact. There's no second window. The Daughters' ship clears Meridian in three hours and after that we're two fugitives with a priceless analog archive and no ride, standing in the one place Sable's ships are already watching." She'd run the alternatives all night on the transit in, and every branch ended the same way: Halcyon custody, or a very long cold wait in a place with no exits. "We go to the berth. We just don't trust anyone past arm's length. You take the core through the maintenance spine and come at the berth from inside. I make the approach from the concourse, visible, normal. If it's clean, we hand off in the open like honest smugglers. If it's not—"

"If it's not, you're the one standing in the open."

"Yes."

"Ilsa."

"It's arithmetic," she said. "You're the one who can open the vault stacks. You're the one whose muscle memory knows things neither of us understands yet. If they take me, they get a disgraced archivist with a grudge. If they take you, they get everything." She smiled, and it felt like wearing someone else's face. "Besides. I've been captured before. Metaphorically. By a corporation. I survived it."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be honest, which is different."

* * *

Berth 44 sat at the end of a service spur where the lighting failed in intervals, and Dezirae Okafor was waiting under the last working lamp, exactly on time, which was somehow worse than being late.

She looked older. Everyone did. Her hair was cut short now, practical, and she wore a Meridian port-authority jacket that fit her too well—tailored, not issued. When she saw Ilsa she didn't smile. She looked past her first, scanning the concourse behind, and Ilsa watched her do it and felt the arithmetic turn over in her chest like a stone.

"You came," Dezirae said.

"You sound surprised."

"I'm surprised you're alive. The last thing I heard, you'd gone to ground after—" She stopped herself. After the disgrace. After Ilsa had stood up in a review hearing and said the words *memory-integrity division* out loud and been professionally disassembled for it. "After everything."

"People say that to me a lot lately." Ilsa set the case down between them, keeping her hand on the handle. "You got my message?"

"Fragmented, routed through four relays, paid for in untraceable scrip. Very you." Dezirae's eyes flicked to the case. "This is it? What he found in the vault?"

"It's what we agreed to move. That's all you need to move."

"I need to know what I'm moving, Ilsa. My ship, my risk."

"Your ship?" Ilsa kept her voice level. "Last I heard you were a registry clerk."

"A lot of things have changed in ten years." And there it was—a flicker, quick as a stutter in gravity, something moving behind Dezirae's eyes that wasn't guilt exactly but was adjacent to it, some cousin of guilt, guilt's poorer relation. "The Daughters' berth is 47. They're expecting the case in forty minutes. You should be aboard before then."

"You're not coming with us?"

Something crossed Dezirae's face then, fast, and Ilsa would spend a long time afterward replaying it frame by frame, trying to decide whether it had been pity. "No," Dezirae said. "I'm not."

* * *

Ilsa waited until Dezirae turned away to check the spur's camera blind spots—professional, thorough, a woman who knew surveillance the way Ilsa once had—and keyed her comm on the private channel.

"She's lying about something," she murmured. "Maybe everything."

Rosalind's voice came back low, from somewhere inside the maintenance spine, breathing filtered through the case's comm relay. "About what specifically?"

"The jacket. Port authority doesn't tailor. And she said *my ship*. Registry clerks don't have ships." Ilsa watched Dezirae finish her sweep and turn back, composed again. "Also she never asked me how I found her. Ten years dark, and she never once asked how I got a message to her. Because she already knew I could. Because she's been watching the same channels I used to use, from the other side."

"Say the word and we walk away. We lose the Daughters, we lose the window, but we keep the core."

"And then what? We sit on it until Sable finds us? No." Ilsa looked at the case. Inside it, wrapped in seed-stock padding, lay several centuries of uncorrupted truth and the only proof that eleven thousand people had ever existed. "We're past the point where caution buys anything. Caution is for people with futures to protect. We do the handoff. But I want you ready to run the moment anything smells wrong, and I mean *wrong*, not inconvenient. Promise me."

A pause on the channel. "I promise I'll run if you tell me to run. Same as you'd better."

"Deal."

She killed the channel and walked toward Dezirae with the case, and the distance between them—fifteen meters, ten, five—felt like the longest index entry she'd ever written.

* * *

It went wrong at the moment it should have gone right.

Dezirae took the case handle, and their hands overlapped on it for one second—one ordinary, transactional second—and in that second Dezirae's other hand, the one Ilsa hadn't been watching, pressed something flat against the case's underside. A tag. A beacon chip. Ilsa felt the click of it through the handle, a tiny mechanical kiss, and thirty years of training fired all at once.

She didn't let go of the case.

"Hello, Dezirae," she said loudly, pleasantly, conversationally, while her thumb found the case's emergency seal and blew it—not opening it, just breaking the pressure lock so the lid sat loose, unlatched, deniable. "Tell me who's coming."

Dezirae's composure cracked exactly one millimeter. "Ilsa—"

"Because someone is coming. You tagged the case, which means you're not the buyer, you're the bait, and bait always has a hook behind it. So who's behind you? Halcyon security? Sable's own detail? Or did they give you a choice, back when they cleaned your file—cooperate or be erased—and you signed?" She heard her own voice rise and hated it, hated the tremor in it, hated that after everything she still sounded like a woman pleading at a review hearing. "Did you sign, Dezirae? Did you sign the supplemental NDA?"

Down the spur, at the far end, the lights changed. Not flickered—*changed*, shifting from failing amber to clean institutional white, and Ilsa's stomach dropped through the station's rotating floors, because she knew that transition. She'd specified it. Emergency corridor illumination, Halcyon standard, deployed during security actions to disorient and mark. Somebody had just switched Meridian's service spur onto a Halcyon power feed.

They were already inside the station's systems.

"Run," she said into the comm, not a whisper, a command, the voice she'd never once used on Rosalind Coldharbour until this moment. "Maintenance spine, aft, now. Don't come for me. Don't be noble. *Run.*"

"Ilsa—"

"*Run.*"

And then she turned back to Dezirae, and she did the only thing left in her inventory, the thing she'd been rehearsing in her head since the vault, since the flicker, since the first night Rosalind had shown her the altered ledger and she'd understood what she was actually atoning for.

She made herself loud.

She grabbed the case with both hands and shouted—about the massacre, about Kestrel's Landing, about eleven thousand names, about gravity weapons and edited registries and a null-shaped hole in the world where a colony used to be—loud enough that heads turned on the concourse, loud enough that any listening device within fifty meters captured every word, loud enough that whatever happened next, the words were already loose, already spreading, already impossible to fully recall. Erasure worked on quiet things. She'd designed it that way. So she refused, with her whole body, to be quiet.

Boots on metal, far off, closing. Dezirae reaching for her, saying something—Ilsa caught *sorry*, or maybe *run*, maybe both—and Ilsa shoved the case hard into Dezirae's chest, empty now except for padding and seed samples, worthless, a decoy of nothing, and spun away toward the opposite end of the spur, away from Rosalind, drawing the white-lit corridor after her like a lure.

They took her at the junction of the service spur and the freight concourse. Four of them, in plain jackets that fooled no one, moving with the economy of professionals. She didn't fight, mostly. She fought just enough—long enough to see, over a shoulder, the maintenance spine hatch swing shut, long enough to hear, faintly, the receding rhythm of running feet that were not hers.

Long enough to know he'd listened.

Then a hood, and the smell of Halcyon transport polymer, and the particular silence of a place where records were kept.

* * *

The holding cell was clean, white, and temperature-neutral, which told her more than an interrogation room would have. Interrogation rooms were theater. This was storage. They weren't going to question her yet; they were going to *file* her.

She sat on the bench and did what she always did with unbearable things: she indexed them.

Item: Dezirae Okafor, compromised, timeline unknown, method unknown, probably the oldest method—fear. Item: the beacon tag, standard Halcyon pattern, third generation, meaning Sable's people had prepared for this specific handoff, meaning they'd known about the Daughters' route, meaning the leak wasn't Dezirae alone, meaning somewhere in the fragile chain between the vault and Meridian there was another shape of absence she hadn't noticed. Item: Rosalind Coldharbour, status unknown, presumably clear, hopefully running, carrying nothing but his own dangerous head.

Item: the case. Empty. She'd handed them an empty case in front of witnesses while shouting the word *massacre* into a public concourse, and somewhere in Halcyon's chain of command a report was already being written that would say *subject detained, materials recovered,* and that report would be wrong, and wrong reports were the only kind of report Ilsa Vann had ever been able to weaponize.

They'd taken her personal effects. All except one, because the intake technician had missed it, because it had looked like nothing: a scrap of paper, actual paper, folded eight times in her breast pocket. On it, in pencil, in a cipher of her own design—the kind of thing a disgraced archivist doodled in the margins of her disgrace—was the Ferrous Daughters' fallback rendezvous, the one she'd never told Rosalind about, because she'd never told anyone about it, because a backup you share isn't a backup.

She unfolded it, read it, folded it again.

Then she sat in the white silence and made herself face the other item. The one she'd been avoiding.

Item: Ilsa Vann helped design the protocol that erased Kestrel's Landing. Item: she had signed. Item: a man had spent ten years not knowing he was the sole survivor of the thing she'd helped bury, and she had known him for weeks before telling him, and she had told him only when it became useful, and she would carry that the rest of her life, however long the rest of her life turned out to be.

She had bought his escape with the only currency she had left: herself. And the worst part, the part that sat in her chest like ballast, was that it hadn't even felt like sacrifice. It had felt like *payment*. Like a debt finally coming due, almost a relief.

The cell door, when it opened, was going to open on questions she wasn't ready to answer. But she had been signing things her whole life. It was time, at last, to refuse to.

Footsteps in the corridor outside. Measured. Unhurried. Two sets, and the cadence of someone important walking behind someone protective.

The door opened, and Director Osei Sable stepped in, flanked by an aide, and looked at Ilsa Vann the way an auditor looks at a discrepancy.

"Archivist," Sable said. "Let's talk about what you think you know."

Somewhere beyond the walls, beyond the station, beyond the reach of any beacon or registry, a man was running dark with the truth in his hands—and Ilsa smiled at Osei Sable with everything she had left, and said:

"Let's."
