## 18. What Rosalind Chooses

They gave him a room with a window.

That was the first thing Rosalind noticed, and the thing that frightened him most. Ten years of hauling cargo through contested orbits had taught him that captivity came in two flavors: the kind with no windows, and the kind with one. The first meant they didn't care what you saw. The second meant they wanted you to see exactly this—a sweeping view of Halcyon's holding station at Vantage-9, all clean white spars and slow-turning solar sails, beautiful as a knife laid out on silk. *Look how orderly we are,* the window said. *Look how permanent. Nothing you do will change any of it.*

He sat on the edge of a bed that was too soft and counted his assets.

One: he was alive. Two: the analog archive was not on this station—it was sealed in the Ferrous Daughters' smuggler hold aboard *Copper Lark*, running dark somewhere in the Kestrel debris field, and only three people alive knew that. Three: Sable had offered him a bargain instead of a bullet, which meant she needed something from him, which meant he still had leverage, however thin.

Four—and this was the one he kept circling back to—she had offered to give him back his memories.

*Full restoration of your pre-wipe neural pattern. We kept it, Mr. Coldharbour. Or should I say—whatever your name was before Kestrel's Landing.* Her voice, replaying in his head for the hundredth time, calm as a customs officer reading a manifest. *You could be whole again. All of it. Your mother's voice. The red door. Everything the decade took from you. In exchange, you hand over the archive core and sign a statement confirming the flicker was a routine gravimetric survey.*

A lie in exchange for a self.

Rosalind pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw constellations. He thought about the fragments that had been surfacing for weeks now, unbidden, like bodies rising in flooded water: a woman's voice singing off-key in a language he couldn't name. The smell of hot metal and green things growing under grow-lamps. A red door with a child's handprints on it, paint worn to pink at knee height. He had spent ten years being nobody, and some traitorous part of him—the part that woke up at night reaching for a name that wasn't there—wanted it back so badly his teeth ached.

And that was exactly why he couldn't take it.

Because Sable would choose what to restore. She'd already shown him how casually Halcyon edited people—shifted dates, a missing week, an entire colony deleted from every registry in human space. If she rebuilt his memory, she would rebuild it *curated*. His mother's voice would come back, but maybe without the part where she argued with the mining consortium. The red door would come back, but maybe without whatever he'd seen behind it when the gravity lance fired. He wouldn't be whole. He'd be whole *as designed by the people who killed everyone he loved*.

A restored memory from Halcyon Dynamics was just a longer leash.

The door chimed. Soft, courteous. Everything about this place was soft and courteous, like a predator wearing cologne.

"Mr. Coldharbour," said the guard outside—or the escort, or whatever they called themselves here. "Director Sable is ready to receive your answer."

Rosalind stood. He'd spent the last six hours pretending to deliberate, and the deliberation hadn't been fake—he'd genuinely turned the offer over, tested its weight, let himself want it. But underneath the wanting, while he sat there looking out the beautiful window, his hands had been busy. The transfer collar they'd fitted around his neck for the "memory restoration procedure"—a sleek white band, medical-grade, supposedly to monitor his vitals during neural mapping—had a service latch. Medical equipment always did; medics needed to remove them from unconscious patients. And the latch's release tab was on the left side, because the technician who'd fitted it was right-handed, and right-handed people always mounted the mechanism where their own fingers fell.

Ten years of hauling cargo taught you more than docking codes. It taught you that every system was built by someone lazy, rushed, or overconfident, and usually all three.

"I'm ready," he said.

* * *

Sable received him in a conference room with a real wooden table, which on a station was either a statement of obscene wealth or obscene confidence. Probably both. She stood at the far end, hands folded, flanked by two security officers who managed to look bored and lethal simultaneously.

"You've considered the terms," she said. Not a question.

"I have." Rosalind walked toward her, slow, letting his boots sound against the deck. "I have one counteroffer."

Something flickered in her expression—not surprise, exactly. Recalculation. "I'm listening."

"You tell me my real name." He stopped three meters away, inside conversational distance, just outside lunging distance. "Not the file designation. Not 'Kestrel survivor, subject seven.' My actual name, the one my mother used. You give me that for free, no strings, and I'll consider everything else."

For a long moment Sable said nothing. Then she did something he hadn't expected: she looked almost sad.

"That's the tragedy of you," she said quietly. "You think there's a record we're withholding. There isn't. We erased it *thoroughly*, because thoroughness is the entire product. I don't know your name, Coldharbour. Nobody does. That's not cruelty—that's quality assurance." She unfolded her hands. "So. Do we have an agreement, or do I proceed with the alternative disposition?"

"The alternative being?"

"A shallow grave in the debris field, and the archive recovered from your friends by less patient means. I'd rather avoid it. Bodies create questions. Agreements don't."

Rosalind nodded slowly, as if weighing it. As if he were the kind of man who could be bought with wholeness. "Then here's my answer," he said. "No."

He saw it hit her—not the refusal itself; she'd probably gamed that at sixty percent probability—but the fact that he'd walked into her room, said no, and expected to walk out. Her chin lifted a degree. "Guards."

They moved. And Rosalind moved faster, because he'd already started moving before he said no—had started moving, in fact, the moment the door closed behind him, drifting a half-step leftward across the floor under the cover of his own pacing, until he stood directly beneath the environmental access panel he'd clocked on his way in. His left hand went to the transfer collar. The latch gave with a click so small it was swallowed by the guards' first footfalls.

The collar wasn't a weapon. It was better. It was a *signal*.

Every monitoring system in the station read the collar's biometric feed, and when the feed flatlined—which it did the instant he thumbed the emergency release—the station's medical AI concluded, with flawless institutional logic, that subject seven was experiencing cardiac arrest during pre-procedure intake. Alarms bloomed. Doors began cycling to admit emergency response teams. The lights shifted to triage amber. Somewhere below, a medbay scrambled.

Chaos, Rosalind had learned, was just order with its schedule disrupted. You didn't need to break a system. You just needed to make it help you.

He wrenched the access panel open—it wasn't even locked; maintenance corridors never were, because locking them meant logging them—and dropped into the dark before the guards reached the empty space where he'd been standing. Behind him he heard Sable's voice, still calm, still terrible: "Seal the deck. And get me a line to the vault task force. Now."

That last sentence followed him down the ladder like a stone tied to his ankle.

*The vault task force.*

She knew. Of course she knew. He'd escaped her net twice, and Sable didn't chase people—she narrowed possibilities. There was only one place the archive could matter enough to die for. Only one place worth burning.

The vault.

* * *

The maintenance crawlways of Vantage-9 smelled like lubricant and old coffee, and Rosalind loved them with his whole heart. Every station had arteries like these, forgotten by management, staffed by one overworked tech per shift, and every artery led eventually to a dock.

His was a supply skiff, prepped and fueled by a Ferrous Daughter named Okonkwo who'd taken a bribe and a promise in equal measure. It sat at berth fourteen with a cargo manifest claiming six tons of recycled polymer. The manifest was fiction. The skiff was real. He dropped through the ceiling hatch into the berth's service gantry, dropped again into the skiff's airlock, and had the engines spinning before the station even finished its alarm cycle.

"Vantage traffic control to unidentified skiff at berth fourteen," crackled the comm. "Hold position. Hold position or we will—"

He cut the channel, killed his transponder, and burned.

The station's defense grid woke up grudgingly, the way institutional systems always did—first a warning laser paint that tickled his hull, then a pair of interceptors launching from the dorsal bay with the unhurried menace of ships whose pilots had never once been shot at. Rosalind flew the way he'd flown for ten years: ugly, efficient, and completely without pride. He didn't outrun the interceptors. He dove into the debris ring that haloed Vantage-9's gas giant, killed his drive, and let momentum carry him through a graveyard of shattered mining platforms where any pursuer's targeting solutions dissolved into noise. Twice a hull fragment the size of a house tumbled past close enough to read the stenciled logos of companies that no longer existed.

In the silence of the drift, with his life support whispering and the interceptors' engine flares flickering somewhere above the ring plane like distant lightning, Rosalind finally let himself feel it.

Grief, first. For the version of him who might have taken the deal—who might have woken up tomorrow remembering a mother's voice, a red door, a childhood, and believed it. That man would have been happy. That man would also have been Halcyon's finest work: a massacre's sole survivor, lovingly reprogrammed into a witness for the defense.

Then fury, which was cleaner. Sable had offered him his soul back as a bribe, and when he refused, she'd reached for fire. Not for him—for the seeds. For centuries of genetic inheritance, the founders' testament, the last uncorrupted truth in human space. Because that was the final lesson of the nine seconds, the one he'd been circling since the day gravity stuttered and the universe pretended nothing happened: power doesn't fear violence. Power fears *records*. A man can be edited. An air-gapped analog archive, duplicated and scattered, cannot.

Which meant he had one move left, and it wasn't escape. Escape had been his only skill for ten years, and he was done practicing it.

He waited four hours in the debris, long enough for the search pattern to loosen, then burned hard for the coordinates Okonkwo had given him—a dead comm relay at the ring's edge where *Copper Lark* was supposed to surface if the word came through. He sent the word on a tight-beam, three characters, an old Ferrous Daughters code meaning *come armed, come fast, come deniable*.

The reply came back in ninety seconds. One character. An old Daughters code meaning *already moving*.

* * *

They met in the shadow of the relay, hulls kissing at magnetic clamps, and transferred the archive core across a two-meter gap in vacuum suits. The core itself was disappointingly small—a titanium cylinder the length of a forearm, packed with microetched nickel plates, the founders' testimony, the Kestrel records, the gravity-lance telemetry, ten thousand years of intent in a package you could kick across a room. Ilsa had spent three sleepless days duplicating the plates; two full copies rode in separate compartments of *Copper Lark*'s hold, and a third was welded inside the skiff's water tank, because the Daughters believed in redundancy the way other people believed in gods.

Ilsa herself floated through the airlock last, helmet under her arm, face gray with exhaustion and something worse.

"They took Meridian's traffic logs," she said. "After I was captured. Sable's people reconstructed every approach vector we used. Rosalind—" She stopped, made herself finish. "They know the vault. They've known since yesterday. I'm sorry. I held out as long as—"

"I know you did." He pulled off his own helmet. The skiff's cabin was cramped and smelled like recycled air and panic sweat, and it was the safest place he'd been in a week. "It doesn't matter how they knew. I told Sable myself, standing in her pretty room. Refusing her was always going to point her at the vault. The only question was timing."

"Then we run," said Brannigan, the Daughters' captain, from the cockpit doorway. Big woman, gray buzzcut, a face like a closed fist. "Deep space. Six months dark. They'll burn the vault, they'll tear apart the debris field, but they can't search forever. We've done it before."

"And then?" Rosalind asked. "We sit on the truth for six months while Halcyon finishes rewriting every registry in the system? While the inquiry Ilsa wants to trigger gets buried under fresh fabrications? No." He set the archive core on the table between them, and all three of them looked at it like it might bite. "There's one transmitter in this system powerful enough to punch through every corporate filter, every jamming lattice, every polite little firewall Halcyon owns. The vault's original array. Founders' hardware. Broadcast-grade, omnidirectional, built to reach the outer colonies before the relay network existed. Sable thinks the vault is a liability she needs to destroy."

He felt something settle in his chest—not calm, exactly. More like the moment after you commit to a burn and cut the engines. No more steering. Just trajectory.

"She's right. It is a liability. So we use it before she puts torpedoes in it."

Brannigan stared at him. "The vault's got a Halcyon strike wing inbound. You want to fly *toward* it."

"I want to beat it there." He was already pulling up the vault's schematics on the cabin display, his fingers moving with the muscle memory of a decade's caretaking—every corridor, every stack, every ancient cable run. "The array takes time to spin up. Hours. Which means I need to be in the transmitter room before their first torpedo fires, and I need someone drawing their eyes while I do it."

Silence. Then Ilsa laughed—short, cracked, disbelieving. "You're going to broadcast yourself along with the data. Unencrypted. Your face, your voice, your flight history, all of it. You know that, don't you? The moment you key that array, anonymity is over. Forever. Every bounty board, every boardroom, every database. You'll never be invisible again."

Rosalind looked at the display, at the schematic of the vault he'd tended for ten years like a secret, like a grave, like a church. He thought about the red door, and the voice singing off-key, and the name nobody in the universe possessed anymore except whoever he decided to be next.

"Invisible was the plan," he said. "The plan got nine million people deleted. Time for a new plan."

Brannigan grinned like a knife coming out of a sheath. "*Copper Lark* draws the strike wing. You get ninety minutes, maybe less. After that, Coldharbour, we are gone, and you're alone in a burning building with the only truth in the system."

"Ninety minutes is generous," Rosalind said, and keyed the burn.

Behind them, far off across the dark, unseen but no longer unimaginable, Halcyon's strike wing turned toward the seed vault with fire in its bays—and Rosalind flew straight down its throat, toward the last home he had ever known, carrying the truth in a water tank and nothing left to lose.
