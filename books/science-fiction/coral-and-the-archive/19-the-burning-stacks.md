## 19. The Burning Stacks

The first torpedo hit at 03:14 vault time, and Rosalind felt it in his teeth before he heard it.

He was two hundred meters below the surface stacks, in the transit gallery between the cryo-bays and the analog core's antechamber, when the whole structure rang like a struck bell. Dust sifted down through the emergency lighting in long golden threads. His boots registered a shudder that had nothing to do with machinery—something above had *broken*, and the vault was telling him about it through its bones.

Then the second impact, closer, and the lights went from amber to red.

"Vault integrity breach," said the caretaker system, in the calm voice it had used for a hundred years of quiet. "Upper stacks four through nine compromised. Atmospheric loss in progress. Fire suppression engaged."

Fire suppression. Rosalind stood very still in the red light and did the math he didn't want to do. The suppression system was designed for electrical fires, for a smoldering coolant line, for one bad rack in one bad bay. It was not designed for what Halcyon had just done, because no one had ever been insane enough to burn the seed vault. The vault was neutral ground. The vault was the one thing every faction, every government, every corporation had agreed to leave alone for three centuries, because even monsters understood you didn't torch the last backup of the living world.

Sable had understood it too. That was the thing Rosalind kept snagging on as he ran. She'd sat across from him in that gray room on the Halcyon station and offered him his own memories back like a gift wrapped in barbed wire, and when he'd refused she hadn't raged. She'd made a note. And now her note was falling out of the sky in the form of kinetic penetrators, and the vault was burning because he had chosen to be a person instead of a ghost.

*You did this,* said a small cold voice in the back of his skull.

*They did this,* said another voice, older and steadier, the one that sounded like Ilsa. *You're just the excuse.*

He ran.

The transit gallery sloped downward toward the core, and ahead of him the air was already wrong—a brown haze creeping along the ceiling, tasting of scorched polymer and something sweeter underneath, organic, ancient. Seed oil. Centuries of seed oil, impregnated into every gasket and sealant and wooden crate the founders had ever touched. The vault wasn't just burning; the vault was *fuel*.

His comm crackled. Static, then the Ferrous Daughters' channel, then static again. He'd left the *Kestrel's Lament*—no. Not that name anymore. He'd left the ship—the *Coral*—docked at the old surveyor's cradle on the far side, with Maren Voss of the Daughters holding the board and swearing she'd keep the engines warm until he came back or didn't. He couldn't raise anyone. The vault's external array was already gone; he could feel the absence of it, the way you feel a missing tooth.

The analog core was four levels down and eight hundred meters along the deep galleries. The founders had built it that way on purpose—buried under rock and permafrost and the mass of the seed stacks themselves, so that no weapon short of a planet-cracker could reach it without going through everything else first. Sable's torpedoes were doing exactly that. Going through everything else first.

Rosalind took the maintenance ladder down instead of waiting for the lift, because the lift shafts ran up into the burning levels and lifts in fires were coffins with buttons. The rungs were cold. His hands knew them. Ten years of hauling cargo through contested orbits, and before that a decade of careful anonymity, and before *that*—a blank, a white space, a missing week—but his hands still knew these rungs, knew the third one from the top that flexed, knew to shift his grip left where the weld was proud. Some part of him had been climbing this ladder since before the erasure. The body remembered what the record had been edited to forget.

At the bottom of the shaft, the heat found him.

It came down the gallery in a slow tide, not flame yet but pressure, the breath of the fire above being pulled through the ventilation arteries toward the lower levels, because fire eats air and the vault's lungs were built to circulate, not to starve. He pulled his mask up over his face—an old cargo-hauler's rebreather, rated for forty minutes of bad atmosphere, and he'd been inside for twenty-five already—and kept moving.

The first burning stack opened on his left like a wound.

Bay seven of sublevel three had lost its door entirely. Through the frame he could see racks of seed canisters glowing dull orange at their edges, the chromium plating blistering, labels curling: *Zea mays, landrace, Andean highlands, 2087.* A century-old maize that existed nowhere else on Earth or off it. Next rack: *Amaranthus, collection of the Yucatán cooperative.* Next: rice varieties from terraces that were probably underwater by now, or paved, or simply forgotten. The canisters were designed to survive almost anything—vacuum, radiation, a hundred years of neglect. Almost anything except sustained heat in an oxygen-rich environment with nothing above them but more burning canisters.

He could hear them. That was the worst part. As the seals failed, one by one, each canister exhaled a small pressurized sigh—a tiny death-rattle of nitrogen escaping—and the gallery was full of them, dozens a minute, a chorus of small soft failures, like the vault breathing out.

Rosalind stood in the doorway of bay seven for exactly three seconds. Long enough to understand he could not save any of it. Not one rack, not one canister, not one landrace of maize. There were eleven thousand species in the upper stacks and they were all dying at once and he was one man with a rebreather rated for forty minutes.

Then he turned his back on it and ran, because there was one thing in this mountain that could not be allowed to die, and it was not a seed.

* *

The deep gallery narrowed past sublevel five, the architecture changing under his feet—from the vault's public bones to its private ones. Rougher stone. Older concrete. Hand-painted stencils on the walls that predated the corporate era: *DEEP ARCHIVE — AUTHORIZED CUSTODIANS ONLY,* and beneath it, in smaller letters someone had added a century ago and someone else had carefully never painted over: *THE RECORD OUTLIVES THE REGIME.*

Ilsa had translated the founder's marginalia for him once, late at night on the *Coral*, her fingers tracing the scan of a handwritten page. *They knew,* she'd said. *Halcyon's memory-integrity division didn't invent reality-editing. They inherited it. The founders built this place because they assumed, correctly, that someday someone would decide the past was negotiable.* She'd laughed, a short bitter sound. *We used to call people like me archivists. Then we started calling us a threat surface.*

The heat behind him was growing teeth now. He could hear the fire itself—not the canisters' little sighs anymore but a low continuous roar, the sound of the ventilation system giving up and becoming a chimney. The temperature gauge on his wrist unit read 61 degrees and climbing. His rebreather ticked over to thirty minutes remaining.

Ahead, the corridor bent around the last bulkhead, and there it was: the antechamber door to the analog core. Two meters of layered ceramic and leaded glass, unmarked except for a single engraved line above the handle:

*WHAT IS WRITTEN HERE WAS SACRIFICED FOR.*

He pressed his palm to the reader. The lock thought about it—he could feel the delay, the system straining through smoke-damaged relays—and then the bolts drew back with a sound like a held breath released, and the door swung inward onto cool, dead-still air.

Inside, the analog core was a cathedral of paper.

Not metaphorically. The founders had built a chamber forty meters long, lined floor to ceiling with sealed niches of fired clay and wax-impregnated cedar, and into those niches they had placed the true archive: original manifests, founding charters, custody ledgers, personal testimony—handwritten, signed, witnessed, duplicated in triplicate, some of it on acid-free stock, some of it on vellum, some of it, in the oldest cases, pressed into clay tablets because the founders had reasoned that the only format guaranteed to outlast every future technology was one of the oldest ones. Air-gapped. Analog. Uncorruptible by anything that didn't physically walk in here and burn it.

Which was, Rosalind realized, standing in the cool dark with fire eating the world above him, exactly what was happening.

He had come here to take the core out. That had been the plan since Meridian, since Ilsa, since the ledger with its missing week: extract the physical archive, move it off-world with the Ferrous Daughters, broadcast from safety. But plans were things that happened to other timelines. Sable had moved first. The Daughters had lost a ship and a friend getting Ilsa out, and Ilsa was now somewhere safe and half-broken and furious, and the extraction window had closed while he was sitting in a Halcyon cell deciding whether to buy back his own soul.

So the plan was dead, and the fire was coming down the mountain, and the archive weighed—his eyes tracked the niches, did the terrible arithmetic—eleven tons. Eleven tons of truth, and one man with a hand truck and thirty minutes of air.

He couldn't carry it out.

But he didn't have to carry it out. He had to make it *unerasable*. And there was one transmitter left in this whole burning structure that Sable's people hadn't killed, because they didn't know it existed, because it wasn't in any registry, because the founders had hidden it the same way they'd hidden everything—in plain sight, buried under something boring.

The seed inventory. Sublevel six. The chamber that had survived everything so far.

Rosalind hauled the first niche-crate off its shelf—fifteen kilos of charter and testimony, the founders' own voices in their own ink—and got it onto the cart, and his shoulders screamed, and he went back for the next one.

* *

By the twentieth trip his rebreather read fourteen minutes and the antechamber door was hot to the touch.

He worked in a rhythm that was almost prayer: pull the crate, log its niche number on the manifest slate—Ilsa would want the order preserved, Ilsa would *kill* him if he scrambled the provenance—stack, return, pull. The gallery behind him had crossed from haze into open flame somewhere around trip twelve; he'd seen it through the bulkhead viewport, a wall of orange rolling slow and total down the corridor he'd run through minutes before, consuming bay after bay, and the canister-sighs had become a constant hiss like rain on a hot road, hundreds of seals failing at once, the collections of a century venting their last nitrogen into the fire.

He let himself think about them only in lists, because lists were survivable. *Zea mays, Andean highlands. Amaranthus, Yucatán. Oryza, terrace strains, unknown provenance.* Each one a name spoken over a grave. Grief later. Grief was a luxury with a fourteen-minute fuse.

Trip twenty-three was the testimony section, and he slowed despite himself.

These were the thinnest crates, the oldest. Founder-era. He'd read fragments with Ilsa by lamplight—a woman named Ade Okafor-Ceylan who had signed herself *first custodian and last optimist*; a man whose name had been scrubbed even here, his pages surviving but his signature burned away deliberately, some private shame or some protection. Their handwriting filled page after page: why they'd built the vault, what they expected to be forgotten, instructions for custodians not yet born.

One line he'd memorized without meaning to, because it had felt less like reading and more like being addressed:

*If you are carrying this, the world has already tried to lose it once. Carry it louder.*

He loaded the crate onto the cart. His arms were shaking. Twelve minutes of air. The heat at his back had a weight now, a hand pressing between his shoulder blades, urging him deeper and faster all at once.

And then, on trip twenty-four, the ceiling came down.

Not the whole ceiling—the antechamber's outer corridor, the last stretch between him and the stairwell to sublevel six. He heard it before he saw it: a groan of structural steel, a crack like the vault's spine giving way, and then the far end of the gallery folded inward in a bloom of dust and ember-light, and the route he'd mapped in his head, the route back down and around to the inventory chamber, stopped existing.

Rosalind stood in the hot dark with a cart full of the truth and no road out of the mountain.

Ten seconds. He gave himself ten seconds to feel it—the animal surge of panic, the taste of copper, the absurd clarity of thinking *I survived Kestrel's Landing, I survived the wipe, I survived Sable's cell, and I'm going to die in a basement carrying boxes*—and then the ten seconds ended, because survival had always been his only skill, and skills don't wait for permission.

There was another way. There was always another way; the founders had been paranoid engineers, and paranoid engineers built redundancy. The deep archive had a secondary access—a freight lift, manual-crank, installed for moving the clay tablets, documented in a founder's marginal note Ilsa had flagged months ago with a dry comment: *they really did think of everything.* It let out into the inventory level. If it still functioned. If the shaft wasn't already full of fire.

Nine minutes of air.

He wheeled the cart to the far wall, found the recessed hatch behind a false panel of cedar, and threw the crank. The mechanism fought him—seized bearings, a century of disuse, heat-warped guides—and then caught, and the platform began to descend into the dark with him and eleven tons of sacrificed-for standing on it.

Above him, through the closing hatch, he watched the antechamber door begin to glow.

* *

Sublevel six was cold.

The inventory chamber sat below the frost line, insulated by design, and the air hit his face like water when he cracked the rebreather to check his remaining time—six minutes, the display said, though displays lied and he chose to believe seven. Around him stretched the seed inventory proper: the master catalog, the duplicate index, row upon row of climate-sealed drawers holding the *records of* the seeds rather than the seeds themselves—paper trails, accession numbers, provenance chains. Boring. Perfectly boring. Which was why, somewhere in this room, behind the boringest fixture in it, the founders had hidden the transmitter.

He found it where the marginal note said it would be: behind the drawer bank marked *MISCELLANEOUS DUPLICATES — NON-CIRCULATING.* A panel, a cavity, and inside it a machine that looked like it had been built by people who loved radio the way monks loved manuscripts—copper coils, hand-wound transformers, a crystal set the size of a coffin, and stenciled across the housing in flaking paint: *OPEN CHANNEL ARRAY — NO KEY REQUIRED — NO KEY POSSIBLE.*

No key required. No key possible. Unencrypted, untraceable, unerasable. The founders had built a mouth for this mountain and then taught it to shout on every frequency at once, and then they'd hidden it behind a drawer of duplicates, trusting that whoever finally needed it would be desperate enough to look.

Four minutes.

Rosalind connected the last crate of testimony to the array's document scanner—a brass-lensed contraption that photographed pages onto microfilm and fed them to the transmitter's memory drum—and threw the main switch.

Nothing.

He threw it again. Checked the coupling. Found the fault: a break in the antenna feedline, running up through the mountain to the surface array, severed by the torpedoes or the collapse or simple heat. The mouth was intact. The throat was cut.

Three minutes.

He stared at the broken feedline, and the fire above him roared down through the structure like a tide turning, and somewhere in his chest the cold voice said *it's over, get to the lift, save yourself, you're good at saving yourself, it's the only thing you've ever been good at*—

—and then, faint, through the chamber's emergency hardline, a voice.

"—Rosalind, do you copy, *anyone* down there copy—" Maren Voss. The *Coral*. Still holding position at the surveyor's cradle, still warm, still waiting. "—upper stacks are gone, whole north face is slag, if you're alive you need to tell me *now*—"

He grabbed the handset. "Maren. I'm in sublevel six. I have the archive. I need your ship's comm array pointed at this mountain and I need it *now*."

Static. Then, ragged: "The vault's coming down, Rosalind. I can't dock."

"You don't dock." He was already pulling the severed feedline free, measuring it against the length of spare cable in the chamber's maintenance locker, doing the math with shaking hands. "You park in high orbit and you point every dish you have at the north face. There's a hardline conduit running from this level to the old surveyor's mast—it's how the founders tested the array. If I can bridge the feedline to the conduit, your ship becomes the antenna."

Silence on the channel. Then Maren, quietly, in the tone of a woman adding another name to a list she was keeping for later: "Daughters don't leave people in burning buildings. Bridge your cable. We'll be your throat."

Two minutes of air. Maybe three. He spliced the feedline to the conduit with bare hands and a welding tool and no gloves, because the gloves were in the cart and the cart was across the room and some costs you pay in skin. The splice held. The diagnostic light on the array turned from red to green.

The transmitter woke with a hum he felt in his sternum, and the memory drum began to turn, and the first page of the founders' testimony—Ade Okafor-Ceylan's handwriting, *first custodian and last optimist*—flashed under the brass lens and became signal, became light, became a shout aimed at every open channel in the system.

Carry it louder.

Rosalind Coldharbour knelt in the cold heart of a burning mountain, bleeding from both palms, feeding the last uncorrupted truth in existence into a machine that could not be silenced—and above him, through a hundred meters of stone and fire, the vault that had raised him sighed its centuries out one canister at a time.

On the diagnostic screen, a new line appeared, unprompted, in the array's century-old firmware:

**UPLINK ESTABLISHED. EXTERNAL NODE DETECTED: VESSEL *CORAL*. BEGINNING FULL ARCHIVE TRANSMIT. ESTIMATED TIME TO COMPLETE: 41 MINUTES.**

Forty-one minutes. He had maybe three of air, a mountain collapsing on his head, and a man named Sable in orbit with more torpedoes.

Rosalind pulled the rebreather back over his face, looked up at the humming array, and started laughing—low, cracked, unstoppable—because of course. Of course it would take longer than he had. It always did.

Then the hardline crackled again, and Maren's voice came through, tight and strange:

"Rosalind—you need to see this. Something's launching from the far side of the moon. It's not Halcyon transponder code. It's not *any* transponder code. And it's headed straight for you."
