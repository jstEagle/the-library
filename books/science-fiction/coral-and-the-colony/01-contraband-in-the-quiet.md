## 1. Contraband in the Quiet

The trick to smuggling memories, Felix Winterbourne had learned over eleven years and four hundred runs, was that customs officers never looked at what they were afraid of.

Inspector Duvall of Meridian Station Customs was afraid of her. Not in the way he'd be afraid of a gunrunner or a plague carrier — nothing so honest. He was afraid of her the way small men are afraid of women who smile too easily, who know their names, who once mentioned, casually, over an inspection that should have taken twenty minutes and took forty because he kept finding reasons to talk, that his mother had died when he was nine and he still dreamed about her kitchen. He'd never told her that. He'd sold it, actually — or rather, someone had pried it out of him at a memory clinic on Ceres during a bad year, and it had passed through six brokers before landing in Felix's hold between a chef's perfect evening in Paris-Under-Dome and a soldier's last good day before everything after went wrong.

She hadn't sold that one. Some things you keep off the market. She'd just... remembered it for him, quietly, whenever he waved her through with slightly more suspicion than necessary, which was his way of being kind.

"Freighter *Magpie's Rest*, you're cleared for approach," Duvall's voice crackled through the comm, stiff as starched linen. "Standard inspection protocols apply."

"Of course they do, Inspector," Felix said warmly. "I'd expect nothing less from the finest customs service this side of the Belt."

A pause. The particular pause of a man deciding whether he's been insulted. "Docking bay seven. Don't make it weird, Winterbourne."

"I never make it weird. Weirdness finds me. It's a gift."

* * *

Her crew was waiting in the galley when she came down the ramp, because her crew was always waiting in the galley, because the galley had the coffee and the coffee was the only law aboard the *Magpie's Rest* anyone respected.

Okafor had claimed the corner bench, as usual, her enormous frame folded around a mug that looked like a thimble in her hands. She'd been a terraforming engineer before the corporations burned her name off every registry she'd ever touched, and now she kept the *Magpie*'s engines running on prayer and salvaged parts and the occasional act of minor blasphemy. She nodded at Felix, which from Okafor was a speech.

Pip was not nodding. Pip was vibrating. Pip was seventeen, all elbows and enthusiasm, and currently holding up a data slate like it was a religious text.

"You got them," Pip said. "You actually got them. The Vesta choir memories. All twelve singers?"

"Fourteen." Felix hung her jacket on the hook by the galley door, a jacket with more hidden compartments than a magician's coat. "Two cellists. Apparently choirs have hierarchy disputes even at the end of the world."

"The end of the world wasn't the end of the world," Okafor rumbled. It was her most frequent contribution to conversation and her least negotiable position. Vesta had cracked; it had not ended.

"No," Felix agreed, because arguing with Okafor about Vesta was like arguing with gravity. "It wasn't."

Twelve — fourteen — recordings pulled from the last concert hall on a dying colony, bought from a grieving archivist at a price Felix would never admit aloud, destined for buyers on three different stations who wanted to remember beauty they'd never lived. That was the trade, when it was clean. People sold their best days so someone else could rent them, and Felix moved the merchandise and told herself the money went somewhere useful, which mostly it did, which mostly was the point.

The rules were simple, and she recited them to Pip every time the kid got starry-eyed:

One: never take a memory from someone who doesn't understand what they're selling. Two: never sell a memory back to its owner at a profit — grief has no margin. Three: never record your own. Not ever. Not once.

"Why not?" Pip had asked, the first week, bright-eyed and stupid with youth. "Wouldn't that be safer? Backup?"

"Because," Felix had said, "a person who can buy back their own past will sell it twice. And I've met people who've sold themselves twice. There's nothing left in there but the receipt."

What she hadn't said, because Pip was seventeen and didn't need it yet, was simpler: everyone else's memories fit inside her head like they belonged there. Twelve years of other people's sunsets, first kisses, last words, kitchens where mothers sang while bread rose. She carried more human life than anyone she knew. And every night, when she closed her eyes, there was nothing of hers behind them. Just the freighter, just the routes, just the quiet.

Restless competence, Okafor had once called it, watching Felix reorganize the cargo manifest for the third time in an hour. Loneliness wearing a work uniform.

Felix had thrown a wrench at her. Okafor had caught it without looking up.

* * *

Customs inspection went exactly as inspections go: Duvall scanned crates, Felix made tea, Duvall drank the tea and pretended it wasn't excellent, and neither of them mentioned kitchens or mothers or the way his hands shook slightly when he passed the choir crates, because one of those fourteen voices had been singing the song his mother used to hum, and Felix had checked that specifically, because kindness in her business meant doing your homework.

He left. The *Magpie's Rest* undocked. Meridian Station fell away behind them like a coin dropped down a well.

And then, forty minutes into the burn toward the jump gate, with Pip asleep in the copilot seat and Okafor humming in the engine room two decks down, the long-range array picked up something impossible.

At first Felix thought it was interference. A signal shouldn't sound like that — shouldn't have that texture, that old-fashioned modulation, that careful hand-crafted structure from before compression algorithms did the thinking for everyone. It repeated on a loop, patient as a lighthouse.

She ran it through translation. Nothing. Ran it through historical comms archives. Nothing. Ran it through the deep-pattern matcher that cost her half a year's profits and only spoke when spoken to.

The matcher returned one result. One match, confidence 61%, source flagged: *personal memory file, provenance unverified, acquired Meridian Station, cycle 4471, sold to private buyer, contents: colonial-era linguist, final transmission training simulation.*

Felix sat very still in the pilot's chair.

She remembered the chip. Of course she remembered it — she remembered everything, that was the curse of the trade. A linguist named Amara Okonkwo-Vance, who had spent thirty years teaching herself a language no living person spoke, reconstructed from fragments, from children's rhymes, from a single surviving transmission. The memory chip held her sitting at her console, translating a distress call that had come two hundred years ago from a colony ship lost with all hands, and weeping as she worked, because the distress call was beautiful, and no one had ever answered it.

The buyer had paid well and vanished. Standard.

But the signal coming through her array right now — looping, patient, desperate across two centuries of silence — was that same call. Word for word. Cadence for cadence.

Except the ship it came from had been declared lost before Felix's grandmother was born. Except the coordinates attached to it pointed at empty space, a graveyard orbit nobody bothered to chart. Except distress calls don't repeat for two hundred years unless something is still alive to send them.

"Pip," Felix said.

The kid surfaced from sleep like a fish rising. "Wha—? Are we there?"

"We're turning around."

"That's not the direction of 'there.'"

Felix fed the signal to the main display, let it fill the cockpit — that old, careful, hand-made pattern, a voice from a dead tongue saying *please* in a language nobody had spoken since before the colonies fell.

Somewhere in her head, in a memory she'd sold years ago but never quite stopped carrying, a linguist wept over the same words and wished someone would answer.

"New heading," Felix said, and set it. "*There.*"
