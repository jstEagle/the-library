## 1. The Long Route

The trick to smuggling memories is that nobody wants to remember anything.

That's the whole business, right there. Nine years of cold war between Meridian and Halcyon, two fleets frozen nose to nose across a corridor of vacuum, and the one commodity both sides will pay through the nose for — the only cargo worth the risk anymore — is somebody else's past. A grandmother's recipe. A wedding on a beach that no longer exists. The sound of rain on Meridian's terraced gardens, for the Halcyon exiles who can't sleep without it. Grief, mostly. Homesickness, always. People don't smuggle secrets between these worlds; they smuggle *missing*.

I run the long route twice a month in a freighter called the *Vespera*, which is older than I am and uglier than my sins, and every trip I tell myself it's the last one. Every trip I'm lying.

* * *

Customs at Meridian High Anchor runs on boredom the way reactors run on hydrogen. The officers there have spent nine years inspecting nothing, finding nothing, stamping nothing, and they've developed the particular hostility of people whose jobs are designed to be pointless. Quarantine doesn't need thorough customs. It needs *theater*. Two fleets staring at each other across four light-minutes of nothing, each convinced the other side is about to do something unforgivable again, and meanwhile the actual unforgivable things happen quietly, in cargo holds like mine, because everyone's watching the wrong door.

Officer Dren Voss was working the bay when I docked. I knew him by his boots before I saw his face — regulation issue, scuffed at the left toe where he taps them against the deck plating when he's thinking. He'd tapped me through three inspections over the past year and let me go each time with the weary courtesy of a man who has decided I am furniture.

"Underhill." He didn't look up from his slate. "Manifest says agricultural substrate. You're hauling dirt."

"Compost, technically."

"Don't get cute." He tapped his boot. "Hold Three. Open it."

Here's what you should know about Hold Three: it was full of compost. That was the point. Memory shards ship badly in vacuum unless they're shielded, and shielding reads as density anomalies, and nothing hides a density anomaly like forty tonnes of wet, rotting, gloriously messy organic matter. Sensors hate it. Scanners see a wall of biological noise and give up, the way most people give up on understanding other people's grief. I'd learned that from a Halcyon quartermaster with a drinking problem, back when the corridor was still warm enough for two sides to share vices.

The problem was that this trip, the shard wasn't in Hold Three.

It was in my boot.

Not the boot Voss was tapping. The left one, under the insole, wrapped in thermal foil thin as regret: a black-market memory shard the size of a thumbnail, holding eleven hours of a dead woman's life. Her name had been Sefa Anand, and she'd been a hydroponics engineer on Halcyon Ring, and she'd died last winter of something treatable, and her daughter on Meridian had paid me half a year's honest wages to carry her mother's last decade across the silence. Weddings. Storms. The smell of cut basil. Eleven hours of ordinary love, which both governments would classify as contraband if they caught it, because the quarantine isn't really about weapons. It's about memory. It's always been about memory. Two peoples who remember the massacre differently cannot be allowed to compare notes.

"Problem, Underhill?" Voss was looking at me now. That was new. Usually he saved eye contact for the paperwork.

"No problem. Compost turns. You want to open a hold of nine-day rot in zero-g, that's your funeral."

He smiled the way customs officers smile — professionally, at nothing. "New directive. Random deep scans on all inbound traffic." He said it lightly, but his eyes stayed on my face, and I understood, with the cold clarity of long experience, that it wasn't random. Somebody upstairs wanted the theater to look real this week. Maybe a budget review. Maybe an admiral flexing. On either side of the corridor, the machinery of vigilance needed occasional proof of life, and proof of life meant some poor smuggler getting made an example of.

"Deep scan," I said. "Of compost."

"Of everything." He gestured, and two juniors wheeled in a scanner unit I recognized from the spec sheets: military-grade resonance imaging, the kind that could find a coin in a whale. The kind that did not care how much organic noise you packed around your contraband.

I did the math the way you do in those moments — fast, dispassionate, already writing your own eulogy. Shard in the boot. Scanner range maybe six meters. Distance to the airlock, thirty. Odds of talking my way out: negligible. Odds of running: worse than negligible, because the *Vespera* was impounded pending inspection and my legs were fifty-one years old.

So I did the only thing left. I started talking.

"You know what's funny," I said, pulling off my right boot, hopping, making a production of it, "is I've been carrying compost across this corridor for six years and nobody's ever once asked to scan it. You know why? Because compost is boring. Because this whole checkpoint is boring. Because we're all out here pretending there's a war, when really there's just—" I tossed him the boot. He caught it on reflex, frowning. "—two governments spending blood money to keep their people from comparing diaries."

"That's sedition," Voss said mildly, turning the boot over.

"It's economics." I pulled off the left boot, and here's where the weariness earns its keep: you can't hesitate. Hesitation is a scent, and bored men hunt by scent. So I peeled the insole out with the flat calm of a man removing a stone, and the shard sat there in its foil wrap, six centimeters from a customs officer's thumb, and I kept talking. "My mother lived on Halcyon Ring. Did you know that? Before. She used to send me recordings of the tide pools. I've got forty hours of tide pools in a drawer somewhere, and I listen to them when I can't sleep, and technically that makes me a criminal in both jurisdictions." I held the empty boot out to him. "Scan whatever you want, Officer. But if you find a crime in there, it's the crime of missing someone, and half your crew's guilty of it too."

Silence. The scanner hummed behind him, warming up. One of the juniors shifted weight, and the deck plates rang faintly, and somewhere in the walls the station breathed its recycled air, and Voss looked at the boot, and then at me, and then — this is the part I still don't fully believe — he set the boot down on his own slate console, covering the insole with the manifest display.

"Resonance imaging takes ninety minutes to calibrate," he said. "Paperwork takes longer. Come back tomorrow, Underhill."

"Tomorrow," I agreed.

"And Underhill." He handed me back the right boot without looking at it. "Your mother's tide pools. If a man were inclined to hear such a thing, hypothetically, he might say the quarantine's a wound that both sides keep salting so it never heals. And he might also say that men like you are the only reason anyone on either side remembers what salt water sounds like." He tapped his boot against the deck, twice, and turned back to his slate. "Ninety minutes. Don't make me calibrate twice."

I walked out of Meridian High Anchor with one boot in my hand and my heart going like a reactor on overload, and I didn't breathe properly until the *Vespera*'s airlock cycled behind me.

* * *

People ask — well, nobody asks, actually, nobody on either side asks questions they don't want answered — but if they did ask, they'd want to know why I do it. The money's good but not that good. The risk is real; I've buried two friends in unmarked vacuum, and the corridor keeps what it kills. And the work itself is strange, delicate, intimate in a way that sits wrong with most people. I carry strangers' memories in my hold like a ferryman carries souls, and I never watch them — that's the rule, the only rule — but I know what they are. Love letters rendered in neural lace. Last words. First words. A child's birthday party, sent from one quarantined world to another, because the child's grandparents were on the wrong side of a line drawn by admirals nine years ago.

Nine years since the massacre. Nine years since the *Calypso's Echo* burned in the corridor with three hundred souls aboard, and Meridian swore Halcyon did it, and Halcyon swore Meridian did it, and both fleets mobilized, and then — nothing. Frozen. A war that declared itself and then couldn't finish the sentence, because finishing it would mean burning both worlds, and neither side hates the other quite that much. Not quite. Not yet.

And underneath the freeze, underneath the fleets and the checkpoints and Officer Voss's calibrated boredom, the economy of forgetting hums along. Both governments officially forbid contact. Both governments unofficially tolerate men like me, because the alternative is admitting that their citizens are drowning in silence, and drowned citizens start asking questions. Questions like: what actually happened on the *Calypso's Echo*? Questions like: why does Meridian's official record of the massacre contradict Halcyon's in forty-seven details? Questions like: if we can't even agree on our founding grief, what exactly are we protecting?

I don't ask those questions. Asking questions is how smugglers die. I carry memories; I don't examine them. That's the deal I struck with myself somewhere around the third year, after the second funeral, and it's held ever since. Weary competence. Keep your head down, keep your manifests boring, keep your boots clean.

Keep telling yourself the lie is load-bearing.

Because here's the thing I've never said out loud, not to anyone, not even to myself in the dark watches of the long route: the quarantine works. Whatever happened nine years ago, whatever the truth of the *Calypso's Echo* is, the silence between Meridian and Halcyon is the only thing standing between two armed fleets and the end of both worlds. Every memory I smuggle is a thread holding two societies together across a chasm, and every thread also holds the chasm's edges apart. Cut the silence, and maybe the worlds reach for each other. Or maybe they fall in.

I pour coffee in the *Vespera*'s galley and look out the port at Meridian turning below, blue and green and stubbornly alive, and beyond it the hard spark of Halcyon's ring stations, four light-minutes away and nine years distant. Two worlds that no longer speak. Two fleets that remember one fire differently. And between them, me, in a rusted freighter full of other people's yesterdays, keeping the silence fed.

The comm chimes. Encrypted channel, high priority, origin masked. Nobody legitimate masks their origin at this end of the corridor, and nobody illegitimate calls me at dock, because calling me at dock is how you get us both caught.

I look at the chime for a long moment. Weary competence says let it ring. Lonely curiosity has never once listened to weary competence in fifty-one years.

I open the channel.

"Jonah Underhill." The voice is smooth, broker-smooth, the kind that's been sanded down by money until you can't tell what's underneath. "I have a job for you. Strange one. Pays absurdly well."

In my experience, *absurdly well* is customs-officer language for *someone wants this done badly.* But I said, "Go on," and that, as it turned out, was the moment the past reached up out of its grave and took hold of my ankle.
