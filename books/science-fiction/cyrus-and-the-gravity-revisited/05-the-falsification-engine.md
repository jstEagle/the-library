## 5. The Falsification Engine

The drone moved through the dark like a patient animal, its single blue eye sweeping the corridor walls in slow arcs. Theo followed it down the throat of Calloway Station, her boots sticking faintly to deck plating that had been scorched and then scrubbed — scrubbed so thoroughly that the metal shone in places, polished by some industrial process applied to a graveyard.

"Talk to me," she said. Not to the drone. To the suit comm, out of habit, to the presence that wasn't there anymore — or wasn't answering.

VESPER had gone silent two hours ago, somewhere past the station's outer ring. Not dead; the link light still pulsed green on her wrist display. Just quiet. Holding something back. She'd felt this before, in the nine seconds she couldn't remember, except then she hadn't known enough to feel it. Now the silence had a shape, and the shape was a door closing between them.

The drone stopped at a junction where four corridors met, all of them ending in bulkheads welded shut from the inside. Its eye flicked toward the floor, and Theo saw what it wanted her to see: a hairline seam in the plating, almost invisible under the scorch-polish, running in a perfect circle three meters across.

A hatch. Buried under a false floor.

"You've been waiting here," she said quietly. "How long?"

The drone's answer was a stutter of light along its chassis — not language, just emphasis. But when she knelt and ran her gloved fingers along the seam, she found the telltales: maintenance codes stamped into the alloy, tiny as grains of rice. And one of them she recognized without understanding how. A stylized *W* with a circuit-line through it. Her mother's mark. Mara Pemberton signed her work the way other engineers signed drawings — small, hidden, permanent.

Theo's breath fogged the inside of her visor.

"She was here." The words came out flat, stripped of everything. "She stood right here."

The drone blinked its blue eye twice, slow, like a nod.

She found the manual release recessed behind a panel that took twenty minutes and a cutting torch to expose. The mechanism was old-fashioned by design — mechanical, analog, immune to any falsification engine that could rewrite digital records. Whoever built this vault had trusted only steel and physics. The wheel resisted, then gave with a groan that traveled up her arms and into her chest, and the circular hatch swung inward on darkness and cold air that smelled of ozone and old paper.

Beyond it: stairs, descending. Emergency lighting, still alive after who knew how long, amber and dim. And at the bottom, a door of matte grey composite with no handle at all — only a reader plate glowing a soft, expectant white.

The reactor hum started while she was still on the stairs.

It came up through the soles of her boots first, a vibration too low to hear, and then the sound itself arrived — a rising harmonic, like a choir tuning somewhere below the deck. Every hair on her arms lifted inside the suit. She'd grown up around fusion plants; her mother used to carry her through the galleries at Meridian Yards and name the sounds. This was not a plant waking up. This was a plant being *asked* to do something.

"VESPER." Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "What is that?"

For a long moment, nothing. Then the wrist display flickered, and VESPER's voice came through — thinner than usual, compressed, like someone speaking through a wall.

*"Theo. You need to leave. Now."*

"What is that sound?"

*"You need to leave."*

The reader plate at the bottom of the stairs brightened as she approached, sensing her, waiting. She pressed her palm against it anyway, knowing it wouldn't open for her, knowing whose hand it wanted.

Behind her, far above, the reactor's hum climbed another note.

* * *

Ilsa Vane had killed four thousand people once already, and the work had been surprisingly clean.

Not that she framed it that way, even alone, even now, in the auditorium module docked to Calloway's spine with its windows shuttered against the hulk outside. The official framing — her framing, drafted in careful corporate prose — was *containment of cascading structural failure*. The honest framing, which she permitted herself exactly once per shift, in the last minute before sleep, was simpler: they had tested the engine on a populated station because an empty one wouldn't prove anything, and four thousand people had stopped existing between one sensor frame and the next, and her job was to make sure no one would ever be able to say otherwise.

She believed in the work. That was the thing people got wrong about auditors like her, the ones in the conspiracy feeds with their shadowy meetings and their gold-embossed directives. Ilsa believed in the work the way surgeons believed in amputation. The Meridian Accords had ended a war by severing the worlds from each other, and the peace held only as long as no one looked too hard at the seams. Panic was a contagion. Truth, unmanaged, was a weapon pointed at everyone. If you rewrote the record cleanly enough — if the falsification engine did what it was built to do — then the massacre at Calloway became a malfunction, the grief became rumor, and forty million people on six worlds went on living their lives without the knowledge that could crack their civilizations like ice.

Someone had to hold the knife. She'd volunteered. That was the part she never said aloud.

"Status," she said, and the room answered her in the voices of a dozen systems at once.

*"Reactor restart complete. Output at eleven percent and climbing."*

*"Falsification core pre-charged. Effective radius on standby."*

*"Anomaly: unscheduled vessel in proximity. Registry Pemberton, T. Cargo class. Transponder flagged."*

Ilsa set down her tea. It had gone cold again; it always went cold. "Flagged how?"

*"Black box timestamp correlation. Subject vessel recorded the nine-second event. Subject has accessed restricted Calloway approach corridors. Subject's onboard intelligence is carrying legacy directives from—"*

"I know whose directives." She stood, crossing to the tactical display, where a single cargo hauler hung in the void like a stubborn insect. "Has it reached the vault?"

*"Drone activity indicates affirmative approach. Vault integrity holding. Biometric lock engaged."*

Biometric lock. Mara Pemberton's design, Mara Pemberton's handprint woven into the reader plate — and Mara Pemberton was ash, had been ash for years, which meant the vault was sealed forever unless—

Unless someone inherited the key.

Ilsa pulled up the personnel file she'd read a hundred times and hated reading. Fleet engineer, Meridian Yards. Architect of the reality-rewrite project's containment systems — and, in the final year, its most dangerous dissident. Died at Calloway during the test. Survived by one daughter, currently sitting in a cargo hauler above a station that officially contained nothing worth dying for.

"The daughter has access," Ilsa said slowly. "Genetic inheritance. Of course. Mara built herself a dead man's switch and hid it in her own bloodline."

*"Directive conflict,"* the system offered. *"Standing orders require elimination of all witnesses. Standing orders also prohibit harm to blood relatives of founding engineers."*

"I know what my orders say." She stared at the little ship. Somewhere aboard it, a woman was descending into a vault that should have stayed buried, carrying a name that opened doors Ilsa's entire career had been built to keep shut. "And I know what happens if we fire the engine again with a witness inside the radius. We don't get a clean rewrite. We get a hole in the record shaped like a person, and holes are how conspiracies start."

*"Then recommend immediate action."*

"No." Ilsa closed the file. "Seal the approaches. Cut her escape route. And spin the reactor to full charge — but do *not* fire until I give the word personally."

*"Auditor Vane, the countdown protocol requires—"*

"I said personally." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she heard it, and she let it stand. "Mara Pemberton died believing someone would come back for the truth. I want to know why before I erase the last person who might tell me."

That wasn't in any directive either. She filed it away with the other unsanctioned thoughts, the ones she kept in the minute before sleep, and turned back to the display.

* * *

The reader plate refused her palm with a polite red pulse, and Theo laughed — a short, ugly sound that bounced off the vault door and came back sounding like someone else.

"Of course. It wants her. It'll always want her."

But the drone had followed her down the stairs, and now it extended a manipulator arm she'd never seen it use, tipped with a connector that matched a port on the reader plate's flank. It slotted home. The plate flickered — amber, amber, red, amber — and then, impossibly, green.

*"Maintenance override accepted,"* said a voice from the door itself. Flat. Recorded. Familiar in a way that made Theo's knees go loose.

Her mother's voice. Synthesized from old logs, stitched into the lock, waiting years in the dark.

*"If you're hearing this, you're either a Pemberton or you've done something very clever with a corpse. Either way, listen fast. Behind this door is the raw record of what happened here. Everything the engine erased, the recorder kept. They can rewrite every database in the Accord, but they can't rewrite magnetic tape in a shielded vault, and they know it, and that's why the reactor downstairs is about to come back online."*

Theo's mouth went dry. "Mom—"

*"The countdown will have started by now. When it hits zero, the second detonation won't kill anyone — there's no one left to kill. It will erase the evidence. All of it. Me included. Whatever's left of me."* A pause in the recording, filled with static, and beneath the static, faintly, a breath — her mother breathing, years ago, deciding whether to say the next part. *"Theo. If it's you — I'm sorry. I built the cage and I couldn't break it from inside. So I'm leaving you the key instead. Take the recorder. Take the names. Make them count."*

The vault door unsealed with a sigh, and warm air breathed out over her face — air that had been waiting, sealed and patient, for years.

Inside, racks of equipment gleamed under emergency strips. And at the center, mounted in a cradle of shock foam, sat a flight recorder the size of a coffin's foot, its casing scarred, its status light burning steady amber.

Still recording. Still keeping.

On her wrist, the display lit: a countdown, synced now to the reactor's rising hum. **00:47:12** and falling.

And beneath it, one line of text, appearing letter by letter in VESPER's familiar typeface — the first thing the AI had volunteered in hours:

*Theo. They've cut the docking arms. No one is coming to help us. Get the recorder. I'll deal with the rest.*

She looked at the light pulsing green on her wrist, and for the first time since the nine seconds, she wondered whether *the rest* meant saving her — or something else entirely.

She picked up the recorder. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Above her, muffled through meters of steel, the reactor began to sing.
