## 7. Border of Dead Space

The last beacon was called Vantage-9, and it had been dying for a decade.

Astrid watched it fall behind him on the nav display—a stuttering point of amber light that pulsed once every eleven seconds, like a heart deciding whether to keep going. Officially it was maintained by the Outer Lane Authority. Unofficially, nobody had serviced it since before he'd been decanted, and its signal had degraded into something less like a lighthouse and more like a man calling out in his sleep.

*Eleven seconds. Pulse. Eleven seconds. Pulse.*

Then nothing at all.

He sat with it for a long moment after the display went dark, hands loose on the yoke of the *Marguerite's Folly*, a name he'd never learned the origin of and had stopped wondering about somewhere around year three. The hauler was forty meters of patched alloy and secondhand ambition, and she carried nine hundred tons of refined chromium she didn't actually contain—manifest fraud being one of the smaller sins he'd committed since inheriting a dead man's debts—and beyond this line, none of it mattered, because none of it would ever be checked.

That was the thing about the border. It wasn't marked by buoys or fences or gunships holding position in a glittering picket line. It was marked by silence. The beacon network ended, and past it there was only the raw noise of the universe: pulsars ticking like faulty clocks, the hydrogen hiss of the galactic plane, the occasional shriek of a magnetar two thousand light-years away whose light had left home before human beings had invented writing. No traffic control chatter. No customs pings. No automated tolls deducting fees from an account that was already bleeding.

No law. No rescue. No witnesses.

"Okay," Astrid said aloud, because the ship had felt too quiet lately and talking to himself was cheaper than talking to the computer, which answered in a voice calibrated by some corporate psychologist to be maximally inoffensive and which he hated with a quiet, professional passion. "Okay, girl. Just you and me."

The *Folly* did not answer. She creaked instead—a long, groaning flex of her spine as her thermal envelope equalized against the cold—and Astrid chose to take it as agreement.

* * *

He spent the first six hours doing what he always did when the silence closed in: working.

There was always work. A cargo hauler was less a ship than a slow-motion argument between entropy and maintenance, and entropy had deeper pockets. He crawled into the number-two drive housing and re-seated a coolant coupling that had been weeping vapor for two days, scalding his knuckles through the glove in the process. He flushed the water reclaimer, which coughed up a sludge of mineral scale and one unidentifiable biological horror he elected not to examine closely. He recalibrated the long-range array, because if he was going to fly toward a distress signal that shouldn't exist, transmitted in a language that hadn't been spoken in two hundred years, he wanted the ears on this ship to be as sharp as he could make them.

It was while he was up in the comm blister, elbow-deep in the array's guts, that the silence really got its teeth into him.

Out here, the background chatter of civilized space—the constant low murmur of a million ships talking to a million other ships, couriers and tugs and liners and patrol cutters all overlapping into a white noise you stopped hearing until it was gone—that was gone. What remained was the signal itself, still repeating on the frequency where he'd found it, faint as breath on glass.

*Old Meridian.* His translation suite had chewed on it for three days and produced nothing but a confidence score so low the software had essentially apologized. But the memories in his head—the ones that came with the body, the ones that weren't his, the ones he'd woken up with in a vat in a Halcyon licensing facility ten years ago—those remembered the language. Not fluently. Fragments. The original Astrid Brightwater had studied it, apparently, in the last months of his life, and whatever he'd learned had been burned into the clone template along with everything else: his gait, his allergies, his taste in coffee, and the shape of words like *selu-vane* — *help us* — and *kireth* — *remember*.

Astrid had spent ten years trying not to think about the original. It was a discipline, like not scratching a healing burn. You woke up in someone else's body with someone else's face looking back at you from the med-bay mirror, someone else's murder case sitting in your inherited files like an unpaid bill, someone else's creditors sending polite, increasingly less polite inquiries to an address you couldn't legally use—and you learned to fold all of it down small and put it in a box and sit on the box. That was how you survived. That was how he'd survived.

But the signal had opened the box. And now, alone past the border of everything, with no customs officer to perform for and no dockhands to nod at and no one alive within four light-years who could hear him if he screamed, Astrid found himself saying the words out loud just to hear them in the dark.

"*Selu-vane,*" he murmured, testing the solder joint on the array's primary feed. "*Kireth.*"

Help us. Remember.

His translation software thought the transmission was noise. His own skull disagreed, and the disagreement felt like standing too close to an open airlock.

* * *

He ate dinner strapped into the pilot's chair, watching the fuel numbers and not liking them.

The contract had paid half up front—which was itself suspicious; honest clients paid on delivery, because honest clients expected you to actually deliver—and the routing instructions had put him on a course that no sane freight captain would have flown for any cargo listed anywhere in the manifest. Chromium didn't need to go through dead space. Nothing needed to go through dead space. Dead space was where cargo went to stop existing, along with the ships carrying it and the insurance claims filed afterward.

Which meant the client wasn't paying for cargo. The client was paying for proximity. Someone else had heard the signal—or heard about the signal—and they wanted a ship out here, and either they'd picked his ship at random, which the money said they hadn't, or they knew exactly whose ship they were hiring, which was a much more interesting and much worse possibility.

Astrid turned his ration pouch over in his hands. Chicken-adjacent protein, the label said, in four languages and a legal disclaimer. He'd been eating chicken-adjacent protein for ten years. When the warranty expired—fourteen months from now, give or take, assuming the cellular degradation curves stayed on schedule and didn't do what clone bodies sometimes did, which was fall off a cliff without warning—he wouldn't need to eat anything ever again, and part of him, the tired part, the part that had been tired since the vat, thought that sounded almost restful.

Almost.

He crushed the thought the way you crush a spider: fast, and without looking at what was left. There'd be time for rest later. Right now there was a signal, and a client who wanted him out here, and a dead man's memories whispering a dead language in his ear, and those three things together made a shape he recognized. The original had followed a shape like this once. It had gotten him killed in a docking bay on Ceres Station, bludgeoned and dumped between two cargo containers, his death ruled accidental by an investigator who'd spent eleven minutes on the scene.

Astrid knew because he'd read the file. He'd read it a hundred times. It was the only inheritance that had ever felt like it belonged to him.

"You got sloppy," he told the original, conversationally, around a mouthful of chicken-adjacent protein. "You got curious, and you got sloppy, and somebody noticed."

The ship creaked. Somewhere aft, the water reclaimer gurgled like a swamp.

"Yeah," Astrid agreed. "I know. I'm getting curious too. Difference is, I'm cheap and hard to kill. Warranty says otherwise, but warranties lie."

* * *

Sleep came in shallow dives.

He dreamed, as he often did, of the loading dock on Ceres. In the dream he stood where the security camera had stood, watching the original walk the row of containers with his collar up against the dock chill, checking seals, humming something—Old Meridian, Astrid realized, the original had been humming it even then, some fragment of melody from his research—and then the dream did what the dream always did, which was show him the shadow detach itself from behind container 44-Kilo and follow, patient as tide, and Astrid would try to shout a warning and produce no sound, because he was watching himself fail to save himself, and the dream understood that irony better than he did.

He woke at 0340 ship-time with his heart slamming and his hands already fisted, and lay in the bunk listening to the *Folly*'s bones tick as she cooled.

That was when he noticed how small the ship had gotten.

It happened gradually, out here. Every light-year past the beacon line, the hull seemed to draw in a little tighter around him. The corridor from the cockpit to the galley was eleven steps; he counted them now, compulsively, and they felt shorter than they used to. The bunk was a coffin with better padding. The cockpit was a fishbowl pointed at an ocean of nothing. Out in the lanes, the *Marguerite's Folly* had been a working vessel among thousands, anonymous but accompanied, one ant in a column of ants stretching to the horizon. Out here she was the last room in the universe, and the walls were closing by millimeters a day.

Isolation did things to people. He'd hauled enough rescue-salvage contracts to have seen the logs of ships that had gone quiet—not crashed, not breached, just *quiet*, crews lost to the particular madness of the deep lanes, found weeks later sitting in neat rows in the mess hall, or gone entirely, airlocks cycled from the inside. The deep had a way of finding the crack in everyone and leaning on it.

Astrid lay in the dark and took inventory of his cracks, because knowing them seemed like a kind of armor.

One: the anger. Ten years old and banked like coal, fed by every customs shakedown that reminded him he was property on a payment plan, every official who looked at his registration chip and saw not a man but a licensed reproduction with a declining warranty. Two: the grief, which he refused to call grief because it was for a man he'd never met and yet was, in every cell, and grief for yourself was a luxury the schedule didn't allow. Three: the fear, which was new, or newly loud—the fear that the original's curiosity was genetic, baked into the template, and that he was walking the same road at the same pace toward the same container bay, and that this time there would be no clone waiting in a vat to inherit the mistake.

Three cracks. He leaned on none of them. Instead he got up, made coffee that tasted like hot cardboard, and went back to the cockpit to watch the numbers.

Because here was the thing about isolation, the thing the deep-lane madmen had missed, or maybe the thing they'd found and drowned in: solitude didn't have to hollow you. It could also *concentrate* you. Out here there was no one to perform for, no identity to maintain, no name to defend—his name wasn't even legally his, and past the beacon line it wasn't anything at all. Stripped of audience, stripped of law, stripped of every structure that had spent ten years telling him what he was allowed to want, Astrid Brightwater sat alone in a fishbowl at the edge of everything and discovered that underneath the weariness, underneath the resignation he wore like a coat, there was still a core of something harder.

Purpose. Or its raw ore, anyway. Something with weight to it.

The original had found something out here—or near here—and it had killed him. The signal was still singing. Somebody, or something, at the far end of this silence had been calling *selu-vane, kireth* into the dark for who knew how long, and the whole apparatus of civilization—the beacons, the patrols, the courts, the registries, the entire shining machinery that had priced Astrid's life in monthly installments—had heard nothing, or heard and not cared. Out here, past the border, the question changed. It wasn't *what is the law going to allow*. The law was four light-years behind him and falling further every hour. The question was *what are you going to do*.

Nobody was coming. Nobody was watching. Whatever he decided out here would be decided by the only authority remaining: himself.

It should have terrified him. For the first hour it did. And then, somewhere in the small hours, watching the fuel gauge and the nav plot and the faint persistent thread of the signal on the scope, the terror transmuted—not into comfort, he wasn't a fool, but into resolve. Cold, specific, load-bearing resolve. He would find the source. He would find out what the original had found, and why it was worth killing over, and whether the murder that had defined both their lives had been the random tragedy the file claimed or something else entirely. And if it was something else—if there was a name at the bottom of it, a hand, an order given in a clean office by a clean person—then Astrid was going to carry that name back across the border and nail it to a door, warranty expired or not.

The dead man's memories stirred at that, restless, like something turning over in sleep. Astrid let them. For once he didn't close the box.

* * *

On the fourth day past Vantage-9, the stars ahead did something wrong.

He saw it first as a smudge, a region of the forward view where the starfield seemed slightly too sparse, slightly too dim, as though someone had breathed on the glass of the universe. He ran the optics twice, checked for ice on the outer lens, checked for a fault in the display. Then he plotted the region against his charts and found nothing—no nebula, no dust shoal, no registered anomaly. Just a patch of sky, roughly a degree wide, where the light arrived thinner than it should.

And threaded through it, at the very threshold of detection, the signal had changed.

For four days it had repeated on its cycle, patient as erosion. Now the intervals were shortening. The pattern was denser, more urgent, the Old Meridian syllables stacking on each other in his inherited memory like people pressing toward a door. *Selu-vane. Selu-vane. Kireth. Kireth-selu.*

It knows I'm here, Astrid thought, and immediately rejected the thought as superstition, and then rejected the rejection, because he was past the border now, in a place with no rules except the ones the universe enforced, and the universe had just shown him a hole in the sky shaped like an absence.

He brought the throttle up a notch. The *Folly* groaned, settled, and drove on into the thinning starlight, eleven steps of corridor and nine hundred phantom tons of chromium and one borrowed life, all of it aimed at the dark like a thrown knife.

Behind him, the last echo of the beacon network faded below the noise floor forever.

Ahead, the signal called again—and this time, unmistakably, a second voice had joined it.
