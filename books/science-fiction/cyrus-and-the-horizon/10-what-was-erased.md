## 10. What Was Erased

The tea was still warm in his hand when Sefa led him down.

That was the detail his mind kept snagging on, the way a boot heel catches on a deck seam. Warm tea. Fresh air scrubbed with the faint green smell of growing things. A corridor lit at humane intervals, painted in colors that had no business surviving two centuries of vacuum and neglect. Everything aboard the relay argued against everything he knew about derelicts, and Astrid Brightwater had spent ten years crawling through the guts of dead stations, cataloguing their rot for insurance adjusters who never left their offices on Ceres. Derelicts did not smell like thyme. Derelicts did not have handrails worn smooth by use.

"Careful on the stair," Sefa said, and her voice did that thing it always did—arrived in Old Meridian first, a low river-sound language full of rounded vowels, then resolved into meaning a half-second later as the translator implant in his cochlea caught up. The implant was standard fleet surplus, forty years old, designed for trade tongues. That it could parse Old Meridian at all was a small miracle he'd stopped questioning somewhere around the second landing.

"I'm careful everywhere," he said. "It's why I'm still walking."

She glanced back at him, and in the amber light her face was a topography he couldn't read—not amusement, not rebuke, something older than either. She was seventy if she was a day, maybe older; the relay's recycled air had dried her into something like driftwood, all grain and hardness, but her eyes were quick and dark and missed nothing. They had missed nothing since the moment she'd opened the inner hatch and found him standing in her corridor with a cargo hauler's worth of suspicion on his shoulders.

"You walk like a man counting exits," she said. "That is not the same as careful."

He had no answer for that, so he said nothing, and followed her down.

* * *

The archive was below the water reclamation level, which told him how seriously its builders had taken concealment. Nobody hid anything above the plumbing. Below it, past three pressure doors whose seals were newer than the doors themselves, the corridor narrowed and the lighting shifted from amber to a cool, patient blue, and then they were standing in front of a wall.

Not a wall of data stacks or server racks—he'd expected racks, the humming cathedral of storage he knew from every corporate vault he'd ever broken into or hauled freight out of. This was a wall of crystal. Rows upon rows of optical lattice, each wafer the size of a dinner plate, mounted in brass cradles that someone had polished so recently the metal still held fingerprints. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The blue light came up through them from beneath, and the whole chamber glowed like shallow water over white sand.

Astrid stood very still.

"How much?" he asked, because his mouth was dry and the question was easier than the ones crowding behind it.

"All of it," Sefa said simply. "Every census record. Every land grant, every birth registry, every song we bothered to write down, every contract, every debt, every grave marker. Meridian fit in a room, in the end." She moved along the wall and touched one of the wafers with two fingers, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head. "My grandmother's grandmother helped carry these here. She was nine years old. She remembered the weight."

"The colony was destroyed," Astrid said. It wasn't a question. It was the history every schoolchild got, delivered in the flat voice reserved for tragedies too old to be anyone's fault: *Meridian Colony, lost in the Colony Wars, population presumed deceased, records lost with it.* Two centuries of presumption. He'd learned it in a state school on Titan from a teacher who'd already turned back to the board before finishing the sentence.

"Destroyed," Sefa repeated. She let the word sit between them like a stone on a table. Then she crossed to a console—a real console, physical keys under a dust cover, connected to the lattice by cabling braided in patterns he didn't recognize—and lifted the cover. "Sit," she said. "I will show you what *destroyed* means, in the language of the people who did it. You will find it more comfortable than mine. Theirs has fewer words for grief."

* * *

The first file was a census fragment. He recognized the format instantly—Halcyon Combine standard archival schema, the same schema Halcyon used today, the same schema that governed half the shipping manifests Astrid falsified on a monthly basis. Seeing it attached to Old Meridian names was like seeing a familiar coat on a corpse.

The fragment listed households. Occupations. Water allotments. And in a column that made his jaw tighten, a field labeled **STATUS**, populated with a word his implant translated as *reassigned*.

"What does reassigned mean?" he asked, though he already knew. He'd seen the word before. He saw it every time he loaded indenture-contract labor onto outbound ore barges—people who'd sold twenty years of their lives for passage, whose contracts were administered, coincidentally, by a Halcyon subsidiary.

"It means," Sefa said, "that the war did not kill us. The war was the opportunity. Read the next file."

The next file was an order. Not a battle order—an administrative one. Letterhead: **HALCYON COMBINE, OFFICE OF ASSET RESTRUCTURING, MERIDIAN THEATER**. Date: eleven days after the last recorded engagement in the Meridian system. It was addressed to regional directors, and it used the phrase *liquidation of unproductive holdings*, and Astrid read it twice because the first time his eyes kept sliding off it, refusing to accept that the words meant what they meant.

Meridian's population—forty thousand people, farmers and orbital welders and teachers and children—had been transferred. En masse. Under emergency wartime provisions that were supposed to expire with the war and instead became the legal foundation of the Combine's labor system. Their colony's charter was voided. Their citizenship lapsed—the paperwork called this *administrative simplification*. Their property was absorbed. And their records—

"Here," Sefa said quietly.

The third file was a destruction order. Burn authorization. Every physical archive on Meridian and its moons, every data vault, every personal device registered to colonial infrastructure, to be purged and the purge certified. Because a population without records was a population without claims. A people who could not prove they had existed could not prove they were owed anything.

"They weren't lost," Astrid said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like audio compressed past its tolerance. "They were deleted."

"Now you understand why I send my signal in the old tongue," Sefa said. "Anyone who could answer it would already know enough to care. Anyone else—" she shrugged, a small dry motion, "—anyone else hears noise. Static. A ghost. Halcyon's own filters classify Old Meridian as extinct-language chatter and route it to a spam queue. I checked, once, through a broker. My forty years of distress calls are sitting in a server on Vesta marked *non-actionable*. Forty years of shouting into a machine that files your voice under garbage."

The fury arrived in him slowly, which surprised him. He'd expected it to hit like decompression. Instead it rose like floodwater, cold and steady, filling spaces he hadn't known were empty. Ten years of hauling other people's cargo under a dead man's name. Ten years of customs officers looking through him like glass. Ten years of being legally adjacent to existence himself—and all that time, the machinery that had done it to him had done it, first and worst, to forty thousand people, and then had burned the evidence so thoroughly that two centuries later the erasure was taught to children as weather. As tragedy. As nobody's fault.

* * *

He asked to see more. He doesn't remember deciding to ask; the words were simply out of his mouth, and Sefa nodded as if she'd been waiting decades for exactly that sentence.

She showed him the labor rosters. Meridian names assigned to Combine asset numbers, shipped across four systems, generation after generation, the indentures renewing themselves through fine print and inherited debt until the name Meridian appeared nowhere except in the private registers of families who refused to stop speaking the old language in secret. She showed him photographs—actual photographs, printed and scanned and rescanned until they were mostly grain—of streets, of harvest festivals, of a schoolhouse with children in the doorway making faces at the camera. She showed him the final census, taken the week before the deletion order, and read aloud some of the names while Astrid sat with his hands flat on his knees like a man bracing against turbulence.

And then, without transition, she opened a different directory—one organized not by the archive's own logic but by date of addition, recent entries first—and Astrid felt the temperature of the room change.

"These are not ours," Sefa said. "These came to us. Over the years. People who found us, or whom we found. Descendants, mostly, who traced their family lines back far enough to ask questions, and asked the wrong person, and had to run." Her finger hovered over the directory. "And one entry that is neither descendant nor refugee. One entry that arrived thirty-one years ago, encrypted in a professional cipher, with a message attached."

"Play it," Astrid said, and heard in his own voice something he hadn't heard in ten years: the original's cadence. The borrowed throat tightening around borrowed grief.

It was audio. A man's voice, mid-forties by the sound of it, clipped and controlled in the way of someone who'd rehearsed being calm. The cipher key decoded a header as he listened: **CASE FILE 7-A. BRIGHTWATER, J. INVESTIGATION CONTINUES.**

"My name," Astrid said stupidly. "That's—that's my name."

"It is the name you wear," Sefa said. "It was his name first. Listen."

The recording was short. Three minutes. The man identified himself only as an investigator retained by "descendant interests"—the same phrase Sefa used for her scattered network of runners and kin. He said he had spent fourteen months tracing the disposition of Meridian labor assets and had found what he called "the spine of it": a chain of authorizations running from the liquidation order upward, through four signatures, to an office that still existed inside Halcyon's current directorate. He said the chain proved the deletion was not wartime chaos but deliberate policy, planned before the war ended, executed after, and concealed ever since by periodic purges of any archive that surfaced. He said there had been others who looked. He listed three names and said, in a voice that did not quite stay level, that all three were dead, and that the deaths had been ruled accidental, and that he did not believe in that many accidents.

Then he said: "If you're hearing this, I've been slowed down. Maybe stopped. The proof chain is split—I won't keep it in one place, I'm not a fool. Part of it travels with me. The rest is cached where they'll never think to look, somewhere old, somewhere theirs, somewhere that predates their maps. If the descendants' network receives this, tell them I'm close. Tell them Meridian gets its name back."

The recording ended.

The blue light hummed through ten thousand crystal wafers, holding forty thousand lives in a room the size of a chapel, and Astrid Brightwater sat in it and understood, with a clarity that felt less like thought than like gravity, several things at once.

That the original had been here. In this room, or one like it, speaking to these people or their predecessors. That the original had been hired by the same network Sefa belonged to, had found the same spine of proof, had hidden part of it "somewhere old, somewhere theirs"—and that there was exactly one place in the settled systems that matched those words, and he was sitting in it.

That the original had been murdered for it. Not randomly, not in some dockside robbery, not the senseless bad luck Astrid had spent ten years telling himself it was whenever the anger got loud enough to interfere with sleep. Murdered deliberately, professionally, by the same machinery that had erased a world, and the debts and the unfinished case and the failing body had all been handed to Astrid like an inheritance of shrapnel.

And that the killer—or killers—were named in a chain of signatures that might be sitting in the walls around him, waiting.

"Astrid." Sefa's voice, gentle, in the old tongue first and then translated. "You have gone somewhere far away."

"No," he said. He looked at his hands. They were shaking, and he let them. "No. For the first time in ten years, I'm exactly where I am."

He stood. The fury in him had finished rising now, and it was cold and clean and it had edges, and he tested each edge as he spoke.

"He cached part of the proof here. In your foundation, in your lattice, somewhere I'd bet my ship it's still sealed. Which means the case isn't unsolved. It's just been waiting for someone to come collect it." He turned to her. "Sefa. How many people know this room exists?"

"Fewer than twelve," she said. "Now fewer, perhaps. Some of the twelve have gone quiet over the years in ways I did not like."

"And does Halcyon know? Today, right now—do they know this relay is live?"

Something moved behind her eyes, and for the first time since he'd met her, the old woman looked away from him.

"That," she said, "is a longer story. And I think you should hear it before you decide what kind of fool you're going to be. Because you have the look of a man about to do something brave and irreversible, and I have buried enough of those."

Outside the archive, somewhere above them through layers of steel and silence, the relay's ancient transmitter cycled through its forty-year vigil, singing its dead language into the dark—and Astrid, following her up the stairs, did not yet know that the dark had begun, at last, to sing back.
