## 14. Enforcers

The warning came as a whisper, which was somehow worse than a shout.

Astrid had been in the relay's lower gallery, elbow-deep in a conduit junction that predated the Colony Wars, when the ship's proximity suite pinged his wristband with the gentle insistence of a housekeeper announcing guests. He almost ignored it. The last three alerts had been debris—a shattered hull plate tumbling through dead space, a comet fragment shedding ice, nothing. Dead space earned its name honestly. That was the whole point of it.

But this alert carried a vector and a burn signature.

He climbed out of the conduit well, wiping coolant residue on his thigh, and pulled up the tactical overlay on the nearest wall panel. The relay's ancient systems rendered it in amber wireframe, all soft edges and patient geometry, like a diagram drawn by someone who had never feared anything.

There, at the edge of detection range: a drive plume. Military-clean. Decelerating.

"Karrow," he said into his comm. "Get up here."

Static. Then her voice, rough with interrupted sleep. "If this is another rock—"

"It's not a rock. It's burning fuel to slow down. Rocks don't do that."

Silence while she did the math he'd already done. A ship decelerating toward their position had either found them by accident—which happened to no one, ever, in dead space—or it had been given their coordinates. And there were only two parties who could have those coordinates. One of them was standing in this room.

"Time to intercept?" Karrow asked.

"At current deceleration, eleven hours. Maybe twelve if they're being cautious." He watched the plume flicker, recalculating. "They're not being cautious."

* * *

They gathered in the relay's central chamber—the room Sefa called the heart, where the archive's access terminals glowed behind their museum-glass casings and the air still carried the faint mineral smell of the tea she brewed from desalinated condensate. The survivors came too, drawn up from the deep compartments by some instinct that ran older than language. Eleven of them plus Sefa, twelve people who had never existed on any registry, standing in a circle around a man whose legal identity would expire before his body did.

Sefa arrived last, moving slowly, her hands folded. She looked at the tactical display for a long moment without touching it. Then she said something in Old Meridian—short, clipped—and Ilo translated, the boy's voice thin.

"She says she has seen this before. Forty years ago. When she first started sending."

"Seen what before?" Astrid asked.

Ilo listened, frowned, tried again. "Ships. Ships that come when she calls. She thought—" He paused, working through the grammar of two centuries of separation. "She thought no one could hear her. But sometimes things came anyway. Things that weren't answers."

The chamber felt colder than its climate controls explained. Astrid turned to Sefa directly, and though they shared no common tongue beyond fragments and gesture, he pointed at the display, at the decelerating plume, then made the shape of a question with his hands.

Sefa answered with a stream of Old Meridian, and it was Tam—not looking at anyone—who spoke. The young enforcer hadn't gone back to Karrow's ship after the standoff; he'd stayed, orbiting the group like a moon uncertain of its planet. "She says," Tam began, and his voice cracked once before steadying, "she says the relay can hide them. All of you. It was built to do it."

"Hide how?"

Tam translated, slowly, Sefa watching him with an expression Astrid couldn't parse—pride or grief or both braided together. "The relay isn't just a transmitter. It's a mirror. It can wrap this whole structure in noise—make every sensor read empty vacuum and background radiation. Halcyon ships passed within a thousand kilometers during the erasure and logged nothing. She says the colony itself survived as long as it did because the same technology hid Meridian from the survey fleets."

"And the cost?" Astrid asked, because there was always a cost, and because he'd learned to ask before hoping.

Tam hesitated. Sefa watched him struggle, then said something gentler, longer, and he exhaled before translating. "While the mask is active, the transmitter is blind. Deaf. It can't send and it can't receive—it pours everything into hiding. And if we broadcast..." He looked down. "If we broadcast, the mask dies. Permanently. The array burns out its masking lattice to power the transmission. After that, the relay is just... a lit candle in a dark room. Visible from anywhere. Forever."

"So we hide now, and we're hidden until we choose to speak," Astrid said slowly, working it through. "And the moment we speak, we're naked. No second choice. No taking it back."

"Yes," Tam said. "That's what she's saying."

Karrow had been quiet through all of it, arms crossed, studying the amber wireframe with the flat attention she gave cargo manifests. Now she spoke. "Eleven hours. How long does it take to spin up this mask?"

Sefa answered before anyone could translate, holding up one hand, fingers spread, then closing them slowly into a fist. Tam didn't need to interpret that.

"Minutes," Astrid said. "Good. So we have a real choice, and we have time to make it badly." He looked around the circle—at Ilo, at the others whose names he was only beginning to learn, at Sefa with her forty years of unanswered calls. "Nobody decides anything yet. First we find out who's coming. Karrow—I want your ship cold and dark, tucked against the relay's shadow side. If they scan, they see the relay and whatever story it wants to tell them. Your transponder history stays your business."

"And my crew?" Karrow asked, and there was something underneath the question, a blade wrapped in it. Drenn was still in custody aboard her vessel, locked in the aft hold under watch since the sabotage. Since the fight. Since Astrid had pulled him off the transmitter array with his hands around the man's throat and no clear memory afterward of whether he'd meant to stop.

"Your crew is your problem," Astrid said. "Mine is making sure whoever's inbound doesn't get close enough to count our heartbeats."

* * *

The next hours passed in the particular agony of waiting that Astrid knew better than sleep. He'd spent half his life in transit lounges and customs queues, in the purgatory between departure and arrival, and he recognized the texture of it: time stretching like taffy, every minute doing the work of ten. He walked the relay's corridors. He checked the masking system's status displays three times, though Sefa's systems reported themselves with the serene confidence of machinery that had never been asked to hurry.

In the observation dome, he found Ilo sitting alone against the curved glass, knees drawn up, watching the stars wheel with the slow patience of a structure that rotated to keep its inhabitants sane.

"You should be below," Astrid said, sitting beside him. "With the others."

"I wanted to see it." The boy's trade-tongue was better than most of the survivors'—he'd taught himself from salvaged entertainment files, decades of old broadcasts Sefa kept in the archive. "The ship. Before it gets here. Sefa says when the mask goes up, we won't be able to see out anymore either. Like closing your eyes so no one sees you." He traced a circle on the glass. "It feels like losing."

Astrid considered that. "It feels like losing because you've been hiding your whole life. Hiding was survival. This—" he nodded at the approaching point of light, still too far to resolve, "—this is different. This is choosing when to stop."

"Ilo looked at him with the unguarded directness of the young. "Are you going to choose? To broadcast?"

"I don't know yet."

"You do," Ilo said. "You've known since you heard Sefa's signal. You just keep checking if it's safe to know."

Astrid had no answer for that, mostly because the boy was right, and the knowing sat in his chest like swallowed stone. He put a hand briefly on the kid's shoulder and left him to his stars.

* * *

At four hours to intercept, the hail came.

Not a broad-sweep challenge—an actual hail, tight-beam, addressed with a precision that made Astrid's skin crawl. The relay's communications suite parsed it and offered translation in crisp corporate standard:

**UNREGISTERED STRUCTURE, DESIGNATE DS-4417-K. THIS IS HALCYON COMBINE ENFORCEMENT VESSEL *VIGILANT ORDER*. YOU ARE IN VIOLATION OF SURVEY EXCLUSION STATUTE 9-C. TRANSMIT IDENTIFICATION AND PURPOSE WITHIN ONE HOUR OR BE SUBJECT TO BOARDING AND ASSET FORFEITURE.**

Asset forfeiture. As if the relay were salvage. As if the twelve lives inside it were inventory.

"They named the statute," Karrow said over the link. She'd come up from her ship to stand in the central chamber, and she read the message twice. "That's not a pirate shakedown. That's paperwork. They came expecting to file a report."

"They came expecting an empty derelict," Astrid said. "Something they could log, strip, and erase from the record. Statute 9-C is the exclusion code for sites flagged as culturally void—no heritage value, no survivor claims. Someone filed that designation." He felt the cold spreading through him as he said it. "Someone at Halcyon has been maintaining the fiction that this relay is legally nothing for two hundred years. They didn't stumble on us. They came to check on a lie."

Sefa stood at the edge of the group, listening to Tam's rapid translation, and when he finished she spoke a single word. Tam went pale.

"What did she say?" Astrid asked.

"She said 'the sweepers.'" His throat worked. "That's what the elders called them. The ships that came after the erasure. Looking for anyone who was missed."

The silence that followed had weight, mass, gravity. Twelve people who owed their existence to a machine's ability to lie about their presence, hearing that the lie was about to be tested by the descendants of the same machine's original purpose.

"We answer?" Karrow asked. "Or we go dark?"

"We go dark," Astrid said. "We spin up the mask, we kill every emission including ours, and we let them find exactly what their own paperwork says should be here: nothing worth remembering." He turned to Sefa. "How long can the mask hold?"

Tam translated. Sefa's answer was long, and her voice took on the cadence of recitation, of something learned and repeated across generations like a prayer. When she finished, Tam was quiet for a moment.

"She says the mask held for six days once, when she was young. A survey ship sat in orbit for six days and saw nothing. But she says—" he faltered, "—she says each time it wakes, it wakes weaker. Parts of the lattice are dead. She doesn't know how long it holds now. No one has needed it in forty years."

"So we might get six days or six minutes," Astrid said.

"Yes."

"Wonderful," Karrow muttered.

* * *

They gathered at the mask console—an alcove off the central chamber where the wall opened like a flower onto controls of dark glass and inlaid metal, interfaces designed for hands two centuries dead. Sefa worked them with the ease of lifelong habit, murmuring to the system in Old Meridian, and the relay answered her in tones below hearing, a hum Astrid felt in his molars more than his ears.

Around them, the survivors arranged themselves without instruction—hands on walls, on each other, the way families brace for turbulence. Ilo had come down from the dome. He stood next to Astrid, and without thinking about it, Astrid put a hand on the boy's shoulder again.

The hum deepened. On the tactical display, the amber wireframe bloomed outward, a sphere of interference expanding to swallow the relay, Karrow's docked ship, everything. Outside, sensors would begin reading cold rock and radiation noise. Inside, the stars in the observation dome would blur and smear like paint in rain.

On the external feed, the last clear image showed the *Vigilant Order* as a hard white spark, still decelerating, still coming.

Then the mask closed over them like water, and the spark was gone.

* * *

For two hours, nothing. The relay breathed its low note. People ate, slept in shifts, tended the small domestic fires that kept twelve lives going in a tin can at the end of nowhere. Astrid sat with the original's case file open on a private terminal, rereading entries he'd memorized, looking for anything about enforcement protocols, boarding procedures, the habits of Halcyon hunters. The original had known them well. The original had died of them.

At hour three, the hum stuttered.

It was small—a hiccup in the molar-deep tone, a flicker on the lattice diagnostics. Sefa was at the console instantly, hands flying, voice sharp with command. The stutter smoothed. The hum resumed.

But Astrid had seen the diagnostic flag before it cleared, and he caught Sefa's eye, and he saw in her face that she'd seen it too. A section of the masking lattice, deep in the array's structure, had failed to respond. Not degraded. Dead. The mask was holding, but it was holding with a hole in it, and whether that hole faced toward the *Vigilant Order* or away was a question answered only by luck.

"How long?" he asked Tam quietly.

Tam asked Sefa. Her answer was brief this time. Bitter.

"She says: 'As long as it must. It has always lasted as long as it must.' And then she said—" Tam stopped, and restarted. "She said, 'But I am tired of lasting.'"

Astrid looked at the old woman, bent over her ancestors' console, keeping a two-hundred-year vigil with failing machinery and sheer inherited will, and something in his chest turned over. Ten years he'd been running on borrowed time, borrowed name, borrowed body. He knew what it was to be maintained past your design life by stubbornness alone.

"We're not asking it to last forever," he said. "One day. Maybe less. We need it to hold until we decide."

Sefa looked up at him, and though the words between them needed Tam to cross the distance, the look needed no translation. It said: *Decide faster.*

* * *

At hour five, the *Vigilant Order* entered visual range of the relay's position—and found, according to every instrument aboard it, nothing at all.

They knew that because the ship broadcast its confusion on open channels, tight-beamed queries bouncing off empty coordinates. **DS-4417-K, RESPOND.** **SURVEY RECORDS CONFIRM STRUCTURE AT THESE COORDINATES.** **RESPOND OR BE LOGGED AS DERELICT-CONFIRMED VOID.**

Derelict-confirmed void. Astrid tasted the phrase. Somewhere in a Halcyon records office, a clerk would enter those words, and the relay would pass officially out of existence, and the sweepers would burn away satisfied, and Sefa could spend another forty years calling into a silence that had learned to ignore her.

It would be so easy. Hide, wait, survive. Keep the candle covered. Keep the twelve alive and invisible and erased, the way they'd always been.

He looked at Sefa. At Ilo. At the others, huddled in the hum of the mask that was their whole world's walls.

At hour six, the *Vigilant Order* did something new. It stopped querying the coordinates and began a systematic sensor sweep—a slow, methodical spiral, painting the region with active pulses, the way you search for something you believe is hidden rather than something you believe is absent.

"They're not leaving," Karrow said flatly. "They know the records say something's here, and they're going to keep looking until they find it or run out of patience."

"How much patience does a Halcyon enforcer have?"

"More than a masking lattice with a hole in it."

The hum stuttered again. Longer this time. In the observation dome overhead, Astrid could hear the stars smearing—could almost hear it, the sound of light bending wrong—and he understood with sudden, total clarity what Ilo had meant. Hiding felt like losing because it was losing. Every hour behind the mask was an hour the erasure won by default, an hour the dead stayed unnamed, an hour the living practiced being nothing.

And somewhere beneath the fear, cold and certain as arithmetic, the other truth waited: the moment they dropped the mask to broadcast, every sensor in the system would see them. The *Vigilant Order*. Whatever followed it. Everyone.

One choice. Permanent. No taking it back.

Sefa's hand rested on the console, on the controls that could seal them in silence or open them to the universe, and she looked at Astrid across the humming chamber, and waited—as she had waited forty years—for an answer.

"The vote," Astrid said quietly. "Tomorrow. Everyone. Survivors first." He turned to the tactical display, where the white spark of the sweeper ship crawled through its patient spiral, searching the dark for people it had been sent to make sure didn't exist. "Before they find us, we decide who we are."

Behind them, unheard beneath the mask, the *Vigilant Order* adjusted its spiral inward—and its next active pulse swept across a patch of dead lattice, and for 1.4 seconds, the relay flickered into existence on their scopes like a heartbeat.

Somewhere on that bridge, an officer marked the anomaly.

The clock, already ticking, ticked louder.
