## 18. Repair Under Deadline

The transmitter array looked like a cathedral that had been shot through the heart.

Astrid stood in the relay's maintenance cradle—a skeletal gantry of Old Meridian alloy, two centuries old and still holding true—and stared up at the wound Drenn had left in it. The main transmission spine ran the length of the array like a vertebrae column, and where the saboteur's charge had gone off, it was simply *gone*: a meter and a half of conduit vaporized, coolant lines shredded, the focusing lattice around it sagging like melted candlewax. Beyond the gantry, through the open work-bay doors, dead space stretched to every horizon, black and absolute, the kind of dark that made you understand why ancient peoples had feared the sea.

Behind him, boots clanged on the gantry grating.

"You're standing there like it's a funeral," said Brannic Voss, Karrow's engineer. She was a broad-shouldered woman with a welder's burn scarred across her left cheekbone in a shape like a comet's tail, and she carried a toolbag the size of a coffin with one hand. "It's not a funeral. It's a patient. Patients can be saved. Corpses can't. Which is this?"

"Ask me again in six hours."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I've got." Astrid turned and pulled up the diagnostic overlay on his wrist deck. The relay's own systems had helpfully rendered the damage in cold amber glyphs: TRANSMISSION SPINE SEVERED — FOCUS LATTICE DEGRADED 61% — COOLANT LOOP B — CATASTROPHIC LOSS. "We need to bridge the severed spine, rebuild the focusing lattice from whatever we can cannibalize, and restore at least one coolant loop before their ship gets inside weapons range. Otherwise we're broadcasting a eulogy instead of testimony."

Brannic spat over the railing—an old spacer habit, meaningless in a pressurized bay but somehow still satisfying—and squinted at the damage the way other people squinted at a bar tab they intended to dispute.

"Six hours," she said. "You want a miracle in six hours."

"I want a transmitter in six hours. The miracle's optional."

That got the ghost of a grin out of her. Then she dropped the toolbag on the grating with a clang that rang down the whole gantry, unrolled it, and started pulling out equipment with the brisk violence of a surgeon who'd already decided the patient would live because the alternative was unacceptable.

"All right," she said. "Show me your junk pile. Every ship carries one. Yours just happens to be two hundred years old, which means either it's garbage or it's better than anything made in the last century, and I'm betting on the second one, because nobody builds things like this anymore." She patted the gantry rail almost affectionately. "They built it to outlive them. Look at it. It has."

*So did Sefa,* Astrid thought, and didn't say.

* * *

They worked.

There was no other word for it, no grander word, though later—much later, when people asked him about those hours, and some always did—Astrid would find himself reaching for grander words and discarding all of them. They worked. That was the whole of it. Two people in a broken cathedral hanging in the dark, with a warship burning toward them out of the silence, rebuilding a voice.

The first problem was the spine. The severed section needed replacement, and nothing aboard the hauler matched Old Meridian spec—nothing matched anything, really; his cargo hauler was a mongrel of three decades' worth of improvised repairs, held together by prayer and nonstandard couplings. But the relay itself was a treasure house. Its builders had stocked it for a siege: spare lattice struts in sealed vaults, coils of superconductive filament wrapped in wax paper like something out of a museum, coolant line stock coiled in drums that Brannic opened with the reverence of an archaeologist and the speed of a looter.

"They *planned* for this," she kept saying, delighted and unsettled by turns. "Who plans for this? Who stocks a listening post with enough spares to survive a sabotage attempt?"

"People who knew someone would come to shut them up," Astrid said.

Brannic went quiet at that. She didn't ask what he meant. Word traveled fast on a small crew; Karrow would have told her people everything by now—the archive, the survivors in the deep compartments, the eleven children of a colony that officially had never existed. And if she hadn't told them everything, she'd told them enough.

They cut the filament to length using a laser guide borrowed from the relay's own fabricator, which hummed to life after two hundred years of standby with a sound like a struck bell. Astrid held the replacement spine section in a zero-g clamp while Brannic fused the joints, her torch hissing in short controlled breaths, molten metal blooming and cooling in silver beads that drifted upward in the low gravity like slow snow falling the wrong way. The fumes rose around them—flux and ozone and hot alloy, a smell that got into the sinuses and stayed—and somewhere below them, Sefa's voice could be heard through the comm, reading coolant pressure numbers aloud in Old Meridian, translating herself into trade cant for their benefit with the patience of a woman who had spent forty years talking to machines and had gotten very good at waiting.

"Joint four," Brannic said. "Tell me it reads clean."

Astrid checked the scanner. "Clean. Better than clean. Your beadwork's prettier than the original."

"The original wasn't done by me." She flipped her visor up. Sweat had carved tracks through the grime on her face. "Everything I do is prettier than the original. That's the tragedy of being good at your job on ships nobody loves."

* * *

The second problem was time, and time was the one thing in the universe that refused to be salvaged.

Every forty minutes, the relay's long-range passive array updated its picture of the approaching vessel, and every forty minutes the picture got worse. The Halcyon enforcement ship—it had a registry number, HCV *Assurance*, which Astrid found obscenely funny in the way that only things that aren't funny at all are funny—was not hurrying. It didn't need to hurry. It was coming at the pace of an institution that had erased a colony and expected to erase a relay with the same paperwork. Forty minutes became three hours became five, and the *Assurance* grew from a smudge of drive flare to a distinct point of light, patient as a debt collector.

Between updates, they rebuilt the focusing lattice.

This was the delicate work, and it fell mostly to Astrid, because the lattice segments were old and brittle and required a touch he'd learned hauling fragile cargo through contested orbits for ten years—the touch of a man who handled other people's valuables because none of the valuables were ever his. He moved along the array's ribs in slow magnetic-booted steps, seating each segment, aligning each emitter petal by hand, feeling for the faint magnetic click that meant the lattice remembered what it was for.

Brannic worked below him on the coolant loop, swearing with creativity and variety. At hour three she surfaced from the drum housing holding a fitting that had corroded through and announced, to no one and everyone, "I'm going to invent time travel purely so I can go back and fight whoever skimped on this gasket," and then disappeared back down into the machinery.

It was during hour four, both of them wedged in the gantry's shadow sharing a water bulb and a ration bar that tasted like sweetened cardboard, that the talking started—not the working talk, the numbers talk, but the other kind. The kind that comes out when hands are busy and eyes are on the work and the dark outside is too big to look at directly.

"Karrow says you inherited yourself," Brannic said. No preamble. Engineers never used preambles; they just applied current and watched what sparked.

Astrid chewed the ration bar. "She say how?"

"She said you woke up in a vat with a dead man's memories and his creditors, and you've been running his debts ever since." Brannic shrugged. "I didn't believe her. Then I watched you handle those lattice segments. Nobody learns that touch in one lifetime. You've got somebody else's ten thousand hours in your hands."

"My own, technically. Just not my own *first* time through them." He turned the water bulb over in his fingers. "The original Brightwater hauled cargo too. Same routes, same ship, mostly. When I woke up, my hands already knew the docking clamps. My feet already knew which deck plate rattles. Ten years of muscle memory handed to me like luggage." He paused. "Along with the murder."

"Yeah. That part." Brannic was quiet a moment. Outside the work-bay doors, the stars did their ancient indifferent glittering. "I don't know what to say to that. On my ship we'd buy you a drink and tell you it gets easier, and both of those things would be lies."

"A drink would still be nice."

"When this is done, I'll buy the drink. If we live, the drink's real. If we don't—" she gestured at the dark with the ration wrapper, "—then the drink's theoretical, and theoretical drinks are calorie-free, so honestly it's a win either way."

Astrid laughed. It surprised him, the laugh—rough and rusty, like a mechanism that hadn't been engaged in years. He realized, distantly, that he couldn't remember the last time he'd laughed with another person. Alone, maybe. Alone, at the absurdity of customs forms. Not with.

"What about you?" he asked. "How does a woman like you end up welding for Vess Karrow?"

"Woman like me." Brannic snorted. "Say what you mean. Ex-fleet, ex-convict, ex-wife. In reverse order, roughly." She said it lightly, the way people say heavy things when they've practiced the lightness for years. "Fleet taught me engineering. Prison taught me patience. The marriage taught me—" she considered, "—that some people will sign anything, including a person, and call it love. After that, salvage felt honest. You take what's genuinely abandoned and you make it fly again. Nobody gets hurt. Nothing gets erased." Her eyes flicked toward the deep compartments, where eleven stateless souls were sleeping or pretending to sleep. "Until somebody decides a whole world counts as abandoned property."

"Is that why you're here? Why any of you are here? Karrow took a bounty on this signal without asking what it was."

Brannic was silent long enough that Astrid thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "Vess doesn't ask questions because questions are liabilities. That's the surface of it. Underneath—" she rubbed the comet-tail scar on her cheekbone, an unconscious gesture, "—underneath, her whole family came off a station the corporations wrote off. Not erased like yours. Just... written off. Decommissioned with people still on it. She was eight. She got out in a lifepod and the pod was picked up by a scrap hauler, and the scrap hauler's captain charged her parents' insurance company for the rescue, and the insurance company paid it out of the settlement owed to the survivors, so effectively her family paid for their own lives and then spent thirty years paying interest on it." She crushed the ration wrapper into a ball. "Vess doesn't hate corporations out of principle. Principles are for people who can afford them. She hates them out of arithmetic."

"And you?"

"I hate them out of craftsmanship." She nodded up at the lattice, at the two-century-old joints they were re-fusing. "Look at this. Somebody built this to *last*. To carry a message across generations if it had to. And Halcyon sent one man with a charge pack to end it in half a second. That's the whole difference between us and them, right there. We build. They delete." She pushed off the grating, back toward the coolant drum. "Break's over. Joint seven won't fuse itself."

"No," Astrid agreed, and followed her down. "But it's going to wish it had."

* * *

Hour five. The spine was bridged. The lattice stood at eighty percent integrity, petals aligned, drinking power from the relay's ancient core with a rising harmonic hum that Astrid felt in his teeth. One coolant loop was restored and a second was at sixty percent, held together by a fitting Brannic had machined from a cargo tie-down bracket and described, lovingly, as "an abomination that will outlive us all."

Sefa brought tea.

She appeared on the gantry without sound, the way she seemed to do everything, carrying a battered thermos and two cups that had to be older than the relay itself—hand-thrown ceramic, glazed the blue-green of shallow water, impossibly fragile objects to have survived two centuries of vacuum-adjacent living. She poured without asking. Steam curled up into the work lights.

"Drink," she said, in trade cant thickened by an accent that belonged to no living nation. "Old Meridian saying. *Tea before battle is for the hands. Tea after battle is for the heart.* Your hands are shaking. Drink."

Astrid looked down. His hands were shaking. He'd assumed it was fatigue.

He drank. The tea was bitter and smoky and perfect, and the ceramic was warm against his palms, and for ninety seconds nobody said anything, and the three of them stood in the skeleton of the murdered colony's last voice, drinking tea while a warship crawled toward them across the dark.

Then Sefa tilted her head, listening to something on her internal feed, and her face changed.

"Passive contact update," she said. "*Assurance* has altered thrust. They are no longer patient." A pause. "They are also transmitting. On a channel only this relay would bother to monitor." She looked at Astrid, and her eyes were very old and very steady. "They are addressing you. By name."

The cup in Astrid's hands had stopped warming them. Or perhaps his hands had simply stopped being warm at all.

"*Brightwater*?" he said.

"*Brightwater*," Sefa confirmed. "And they know you are awake, because they are asking for an answer."

Below them, faintly, the new spine hummed—whole, healed, ready to speak. Above them, through the dome, the *Assurance*'s drive flare burned like a hole being burned in the night. And somewhere behind Astrid's eyes, in the wet architecture of a clone body running ten years past its service estimate, something flickered—a stutter, a gap, half a second where the taste of the tea vanished and the gantry under his boots was suddenly unfamiliar, and then it was back, and he was himself again, more or less.

More or less was going to have to be enough.

"Patch it through," he said. "Let's hear what they think my name is worth."
