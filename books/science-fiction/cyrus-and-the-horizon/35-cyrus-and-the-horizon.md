## 35. Cyrus and the Horizon

The progress bar had been climbing for eleven minutes, and Astrid had stopped trusting it twice already.

It sat in the corner of his vision like a slow sunrise—forty-one percent, forty-two—each tick bought with heat the array couldn't afford and power the relay was bleeding from reserves that hadn't been touched since before his original was born. The transmitter housing glowed a dull cherry through the maintenance lattice, and every few seconds the coolant system exhaled a long, rattling sigh that Sefa had told him, through Tam's careful translation, was the same sound it had made during the Colony Wars.

*It has sung like this before,* she'd said. *It knows how to die loudly.*

Astrid sat at the console with his hand on the send key, not because the key needed holding—the transmission was committed, running on its own momentum now, a stone rolled to the top of the hill—but because letting go felt like abandoning something mid-labor. His other hand rested on the armrest, and beneath the skin of that forearm the warranty tremors had gone from occasional to constant, a fine electric buzzing, as if his nerves were a badly tuned receiver picking up a station that didn't exist.

Forty-three percent.

Through the observation dome above him, the dark was no longer empty.

The Halcyon fleet had come in like weather. Three hulls, maybe four—he'd lost count when the second wave of point-defense flares bloomed against the relay's outer skin, silent white chrysanthemums that died as fast as they opened. Beyond them, farther out where the dead space began its long true silence, other lights had appeared: the press ship *Verity's Ledger*, running no weapons and no armor, its transmission array blazing with a greed that had nothing to do with money. And beyond *that*, ragged and unannounced, the militia—colonial ships with patched hulls and names painted over, descendants of other erasures, arriving without a single coordinated signal because they had never needed one. They had all heard the fragment broadcast from Chapter Twenty-Two's desperate, overheated burst, and they had all come the way water comes to a crack.

Forty-four percent.

The dome shuddered. Somewhere below, in the deep compartments where the survivors waited, Astrid heard—Imsomething, a sound transmitted through the relay's old bones—children moving, being gathered, being counted. Sefa's voice, steady and low, speaking Old Meridian in the cadence of a lullaby or a muster roll. He had learned three words of the language in the past weeks. *Cyrus* was one of them.

She had told him about it on his second night aboard, when the tea was still strange and the relay still impossible. They had been standing in the archive vault, surrounded by two centuries of a colony that officially had never existed, and he'd asked her why the caretakers had named the relay the way they had. She'd thought for a long time, and then she'd said a word that his translation software—useless with Old Meridian, useless with everything that mattered—couldn't touch, and Tam, who had been Meridian-descended and Halcyon-trained and was now neither, had translated it with visible care:

*Cyrus. The horizon you walk toward and never reach.*

"It's not a sad word," Tam had added, quickly, as if the distinction were a matter of survival. "In Old Meridian it's not sad. It's—the word for what keeps you moving. The thing ahead of you. You're not supposed to arrive. Arriving is a different word. *Cyrus* is for walking."

Astrid had thought about that for a long time afterward, in the way he thought about everything now—slowly, with gaps, his borrowed memory stuttering like a drive with a cracked coupling. He had spent ten years walking toward a horizon that was never his: a name that wasn't legally his, a murder case that wasn't legally his, a life with an expiration date stamped into its warranty by the same corporation that had erased an entire world. He had never once expected to arrive anywhere. He had only ever expected the walking to stop.

And now the walking had become this: forty-five percent, a hand on a send key, a fleet breaching the silence to snuff out a word that had waited two hundred years to be spoken aloud.

* * *

The first breach alarm was not a sound but a change in the light.

The relay's interior had run for two centuries on the warm amber of its original fittings—light designed by people who had expected to live under it, tuned to the color of a sun none of them would see again. When the boarding klaxon engaged, the amber didn't turn red so much as it *deepened*, darkening toward the color of the tea Sefa drank, and every shadow in the corridor grew a second shadow behind it.

Astrid didn't move from the console. That was the whole of his assignment, the one he'd given himself and the one Karrow had cursed him for accepting: *stay at the send key until it fills. Let everyone else do the dying in the corridors.* Karrow had said it as a joke. It had not been a joke.

Forty-six percent.

His comm crackled with her voice, stripped down to its hardest edges by the relay's ancient internal system. "Boarding torpedoes on the aft ring. Four, maybe five breaching teams. My ship's drawing their point-defense—Ilo's flying, and Ilo flies like the warranty expired on *her* nerves too, so we're buying you minutes, not hours." A pause, filled with the sound of her ship taking a hit somewhere far away and her deciding not to mention it. "How much longer, Brightwater?"

"Fifty-four percent."

"That's not a number I can fly against."

"It's the number there is."

Another pause. When she came back, her voice had changed registers—the one she used for things that were true. "My sponsor's offering triple to anyone who pulls you off that console and puts you in a box. I want you to know I quoted him a counteroffer involving the box and a very long airlock."

"Did he take it?"

"He's still thinking. Keep walking toward your horizon, hauler." The channel died.

Forty-seven percent. The dome above him flickered as the relay's power grid rerouted around a severed section, and for one heartbeat the dark outside was fully visible—unfiltered, unshielded, the whole theater of it. The Halcyon fleet had formed a loose crescent around the relay, weapons flashing in disciplined silence. Against them, Karrow's salvage ship *Magpie's Debt* wove a drunken, brilliant line, its salvaged thrusters flaring in patterns no military academy would teach and no insurance actuary would approve. And beyond the crescent, the militia ships had begun to close—not firing yet, just *arriving*, the way a verdict arrives.

Forty-eight percent.

Astrid felt the warranty tremor climb from his forearm into his shoulder, and then, with a lurch of genuine fear, into his jaw. His vision doubled at the edges. He knew what this was. He'd read the failure cascade in the relay's medical bay, in the old diagnostic that had told him the truth about Halcyon's flag on his license: *neural lattice degradation, accelerating, terminal.* The clone body was a leased machine, and the lease was up, and somewhere in the fine print Halcyon had written themselves an early-termination clause.

He thought, with a strange calm, about the original. The man whose face he wore, whose debts he'd inherited, whose murder case had pulled him across dead space like a hook through the cheek. The original had died for this archive before Astrid had even woken in the vat. Everything Astrid had done since—the signal, the relay, the survivors, the broadcast—had been, in some sense, a dead man's errand. And yet it had never once felt like someone else's errand. That was the thing he could never explain to Karrow, to Sefa, to anyone. The memories were inherited, but the *walking* was his.

Forty-nine percent.

The console chimed: external fire had found the transmitter array's primary cooling loop. The cherry glow through the lattice brightened toward orange. The progress bar did not slow. The array had been built by people who expected to be shot at, and it was dying exactly the way it had been designed to die—loudly, and on schedule, and without stopping.

* * *

Sefa came up the last flight of stairs slowly, one hand on the rail, the way she climbed everything now. Behind her, at the edges of the amber light, the survivors had gathered—not hiding in the deep compartments anymore, but standing in the corridor that led to the observation dome, the eleven of them and the wounded and the children, stateless people watching a fleet come to finish a job two centuries old.

Tam stood at Sefa's shoulder, translating before she finished speaking, his voice low and even.

"She says: sit straight. You're slouching."

"I'm dying," Astrid said. "I'm allowed to slouch."

Tam translated. Sefa's answer came back sharp. "She says her grandmother died transmitting, and she sat straight, and the signal was clean. She says posture is the last thing they can't take from you."

Astrid sat straight.

Sefa lowered herself into the seat beside him—the keeper's seat, worn smooth by two centuries of hands—and did something he had not seen her do before. She reached out and rested her hand flat on the console, over the old Meridian glyphs inlaid there, glyphs that his software had never been able to parse and that he now understood he had never needed to parse. She began to speak. Not loudly. Not a prayer, exactly, and not a report. It had the rhythm of a manifest being read aloud, or a census.

Tam listened, and his face did something complicated, and when he translated, his voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second.

"She's reading the names. All of them. The colony registry—every person Halcyon deleted. She's reading them into the transmission's metadata, so they'll arrive with the archive. Embedded. Unremovable." He swallowed. "She says a record without names is just a file. She says Halcyon erased the people but kept the *files*, and that was their mistake, because files can be copied but a name spoken by a living voice is—" he searched, found nothing in Standard, and gave up and said the Old Meridian word instead, "*cyrus*. She says the names are the horizon. They're what everyone who hears this will walk toward. Justice, testimony, whatever comes next—it's all walking toward them."

Fifty-one percent.

The dome shuddered again, harder. Dust sifted from fittings that had not been disturbed since before the Colony Wars. Through the amber light, Astrid saw the survivors in the corridor—saw Ilo's younger brother holding a stranger's hand, saw a woman Astrid had never learned the name of pressing her palm to the wall as if steadying the whole relay by touch. None of them had run for the shelters. He hadn't asked them not to. They simply hadn't.

Fifty-two percent.

His vision whited at the edges and came back. In the gap, he heard his own voice say, clearly, in a tone he did not choose: *Hold the line. Hold the line.* The original's cadence. The original's stubbornness, outliving its owner, living now in a body the owner had never worn. Astrid gripped the send key and held.

* * *

The fleet breached the dome at fifty-eight percent.

It was not dramatic, from inside. A section of the observation dome's outer lattice vaporized, and the vacuum came in with a sound like the world exhaling through its teeth, and the emergency shutters slammed down across three-quarters of the sky, and the amber light went red at the seams. What remained visible through the last unshuttered panel was a wedge of black, and in it, the lights.

That was the thing Astrid would carry past the end, past the console, past the warranty, past whatever came after: the lights.

Because the transmission was reaching them. He could see it happening. Every listening station, every relay spur, every independent receiver within range was lighting up on his display like coals catching one by one—*Verity's Ledger* confirming receipt, a colonial relay spur confirming, an old mining beacon confirming, a station he didn't recognize confirming, then two, then six, then a constellation of them, each one a small dawn igniting in the dark of dead space, each one now carrying the names, the testimony, the orders, the signatures, the whole two-hundred-year crime spoken aloud in a dead woman's living voice.

They had come to kill a signal. And the signal was already becoming everywhere.

Sixty-one percent.

"Array temperature critical," the relay said, in its two-century-old voice. "Estimate to thermal shutdown: four minutes."

"Progress estimate?" Astrid asked.

"Transmission completes in three minutes and fifty seconds."

"Then we're fine," Astrid said, and heard Karrow laugh over the comm, and heard Sefa keep reading names.

The Halcyon fire intensified. The dome's remaining panel crazed, a spiderweb of fracture lines blooming across the wedge of sky, and through the cracks Astrid watched a militia ship take a killing hit and split, silent, its debris spreading like seeds. He watched *Magpie's Debt* put herself between the relay and a Halcyon cruiser's firing arc, hull burning along one flank, refusing to move. He watched the press ship hold position, transmitting everything, transmitting *him*, transmitting the red-lit dome and the old woman reading names and the fleet firing on all of it—transmitting the proof of what Halcyon was willing to do to keep two hundred years of silence.

Let them watch. Let everyone watch. That was the whole plan, and the whole plan was working, and it was working *because* they had come to stop it, and there was a symmetry in that so clean it felt almost like mercy.

Sixty-eight percent.

Sefa's hand was still on the console. Her voice had not wavered. Tam had stopped translating—not because he'd given up, but because he'd begun to understand, and understanding had made translation unnecessary. He stood with his hand over his mouth, listening to the language Halcyon had tried to kill, spoken in the chamber Halcyon had come to kill, being broadcast on every open channel Halcyon could not close.

Seventy-one percent.

Astrid's hand spasmed on the send key. The warranty tremors had reached his chest. He could feel his heartbeat doing something irregular and unlicensed, and he found, examining the feeling with the detached curiosity of a man reading someone else's diagnostic, that he was not afraid. He had been afraid on the hauler, alone, the night the signal first came. He had been afraid crossing into dead space, and afraid in the dark of the keeper's relay, and afraid when the enforcer's transmission had spoken his name. But fear, it turned out, had a horizon too, and he had walked past it somewhere in the last three minutes, and on the far side of it there was just this: a console, a key, a number climbing, an old woman's voice, a sky full of small dawns.

Seventy-nine percent.

The dome panel failed entirely. The vacuum howled through the observation level, and the emergency bulkheads came down between the dome and the corridor, and Astrid felt the pressure change in his ears and the cold begin to reach for him through the console's mounting, and none of it mattered, because the array was hardened, because the transmission ran on internal power, because the relay had been built by people who had always known that the last thing they said would be said under fire.

Sefa did not stop reading. The cold took the observation level in stages, and she read through all of them, her breath fogging, her hand flat on the glyphs, her voice going thinner and never going quiet. Astrid pulled his jacket off and put it around her shoulders without a word, and she did not stop reading to thank him, and that was the most fluent conversation they ever had.

Eighty-four percent.

Through the last crazed wedge of dome, the lights were everywhere now. The whole dark was seeded with them—stations waking, relays forwarding, ships heaving to, the fragment and the flood of it spreading outward at the speed of light into a galaxy that had been told, for two hundred years, that there was nothing here to hear. Distant stations lighting up like dawn. Not one dawn. Hundreds. A sunrise with no sun, breaking all at once along the edge of the silence.

*Cyrus,* Sefa had said. *The horizon you walk toward and never reach.*

But look at them, Astrid thought, watching the lights catch. Look at them all arriving.

Ninety-one percent.

The Halcyon fire was walking up the relay's spine, deck by deck, methodical, killing the structure around the signal the way you'd burn a forest around a fire you couldn't put out. The console beneath his hands was hot. The cold at his back was colder. His body sat exactly between them, a leased machine at the end of its lease, holding the line between the heat of what they'd done and the cold of what they'd tried to erase.

Ninety-six percent.

He heard the original's voice one last time—not a memory, exactly, and not his own. Just the cadence, the stubbornness, the thing that had walked ten years of contested orbits and two centuries of silence to be here, in this chair, at this key. *Hold the line.* His hand did not move.

Ninety-nine percent.

Sefa read the last name.

The progress bar filled.

One hundred percent. *Transmission complete,* said the relay, in its patient two-hundred-year-old voice, to no one and to everyone, and the array died with a sound like a held breath finally released, and the dome went dark, and the cold came in fully, and Astrid Brightwater sat straight in the keeper's chair with his hand on the send key, watching the last wedge of sky fill with lights—dawns without number, spreading out into the dark faster than any fleet, faster than any fire, faster than dying—

—and the last thing he saw was the horizon, everywhere, arriving.
