## 36. Aftermath

The transmission ended at 0347 station time, and the universe changed its mind about Meridian.

It did not happen all at once. That was the strange part—the thing Vess Karrow would spend the rest of her life trying to explain to people who hadn't been there. She had expected collapse to look like an explosion: a single detonation, a stock ticker bleeding red, directors vanishing into private shuttles while their towers went dark behind them. Instead it looked like weather. A pressure change rolling across a hundred systems, slow and total, bending everything before it arrived.

She watched it from the *Magpie's* bridge, still docked hard against the relay's scarred hull, with the array cooling behind her and the smell of burnt insulation working its way through the recyclers. On the main display, open channels that had been static for two centuries filled with voices.

Not news anchors first—she'd expected those. Voices first. An archivist on Ceres reading the names of the erased aloud, stumbling over Old Meridian pronunciation, correcting herself, starting again. A teacher in the Jovian periphery who had kept her grandmother's language alive in secret for forty years, weeping on an open band because she had thought she was the last one and now there were twelve living speakers aboard a salvage relay at the edge of dead space. A colonial militia officer from Vesta transmitting his unit's founding charter, which turned out to contain a clause written by a Meridian refugee no one had ever asked about. Fragment after fragment, testimony answering testimony, the archive Astrid had fired into the dark coming back multiplied, annotated, corroborated, *believed*.

"They're not even arguing," said Okafor, her engineer, standing at Karrow's shoulder with a soldering iron still warm in his pocket. His voice had a raw edge to it; he'd been crying somewhere below decks and thought he'd hidden it well. "You'd think they'd argue. Deny it. Say it was fabricated."

"Nobody fabricates two hundred years of paperwork," Karrow said. "That was always Halcyon's problem. They were thorough."

The Combine's own thoroughness was what killed it. The archive contained the original orders—signed, countersigned, filed in triplicate according to Halcyon's own retention protocols, because the men who ordered an entire colony deleted had been too proud of their efficiency to destroy the receipts. Testimony followed testimony. Payroll records showed the indenture contracts. Shipping manifests showed where eleven thousand people had been scattered across company holdings under numbers instead of names. And threaded through all of it, like wire through cable, ran the final testimony of a cargo hauler named Cyrus Brightwater, murdered ten years ago for knowing too much, naming his killer with a dying man's precision: Director Edmun Hale, Office of Records Integrity.

By 0600, three independent press ships had arrived at the relay, riding the coordinates that the fragment broadcast had seeded across half the arm a week earlier. By 0900, a colonial militia squadron out of Vesta took up station-keeping at a respectful distance, their commander requesting permission to approach in careful, formal Old Meridian that made Sefa laugh for the first time in days—a short, cracked sound, like ice giving way.

By noon, the warrants began.

* * *

Karrow watched the fall of Halcyon Combine the way she watched everything: from the outside, with her hands in her pockets and her weight on her back foot, waiting for the trick.

There was no trick. That unsettled her more than any trap would have.

The markets moved first. Halcyon's charter was suspended by the Colonial Trade Authority within thirty-six hours—unprecedented, unconstitutional, done anyway, because the public reckoning left the Authority's board with a choice between lawlessness and complicity, and complicity had suddenly gotten expensive. Company assets froze. Company bonds became wallpaper. The great orbital headquarters above Titan went dark section by section as subsidiaries severed themselves like lizards dropping tails, each one issuing statements of horrified ignorance, each one attaching the same internal memos the archive had already published, each one lying badly enough to be funny if you had the stomach for it.

Directors resigned. Directors were arrested. One threw himself from a habitat balcony on Ganymede, and the press, to its lasting shame, led with the jump rather than the names of eleven thousand people he'd helped disappear.

And Hale fled.

Karrow learned it from a militia broadcast relayed through the press ships: Edmun Hale, Director, Office of Records Integrity, had not been aboard his Titan office when the warrants came down. He had not been at his residence, or his country estate on Mars, or aboard either of his two registered yachts. He had gone to ground somewhere in the wide gray economy of forged transponders and cash fuel depots that men like him had spent decades building for other purposes and now needed desperately for themselves. The warrant described him as armed, dangerous, and in possession of assets belonging to the Meridian estate—which was lawyer's language, but underneath it Karrow heard something else. It meant they thought he'd taken money. It meant they thought he might run forever on what he'd stolen from the dead.

"He'll surface," Sefa said.

They were in the relay's observation dome, the two of them, because Karrow had found herself drifting there without quite deciding to. It was becoming a habit she didn't examine. Below them, the survivors' compartments hummed with a life they hadn't had two weeks ago—doors open, children running in corridors that had been silent for generations, Ilo teaching militia officers Meridian grammar with the merciless patience of the young. Above them, the dome's ancient glass held the slow wheel of stars, and beyond the glass, dead space stretched out black and patient in every direction.

"You can't know that," Karrow said.

"I know men like him." Sefa's hands were folded in her lap, knuckles like river stones. She wore the same plain garments she always wore, but someone—Ilo, Karrow suspected—had embroidered the collar with a line of Meridian script, and she touched it now and then the way other people touched charms. "He spent forty years erasing people and calling it recordkeeping. Men like that believe the ledger is the world. When the ledger turns against him, he will come to amend it. He will want the original documents. He will want us." She said *us* without hesitation now. Two weeks ago she had said *me*. "He will surface, Captain Karrow. And when he does, every eye in settled space will be looking. Let them have him. We have work here."

"What work? The fighting's done."

Sefa turned her head, and in the starlight her face was unreadable and very old. "The fighting is never what takes longest," she said. "The counting is. Eleven thousand names, Captain. Someone has to read them all."

* * *

The salvage claim was Okafor's idea, technically, though Karrow liked to pretend she'd been thinking along the same lines.

"It's derelict property in unclaimed space," he argued, on the third day, while they rebuilt the array's secondary heat sinks together in the relay's engineering spine. "No registered owner. No successor entity—Halcyon's dissolution wiped the original transfer chain, and the colony itself has no legal existence to inherit anything. Under maritime convention, which every court in the arm still honors because nobody's bothered to replace it, first vessel to effect salvage and maintain possession holds title pending adjudication." He tapped the bulkhead. "We effected. We're maintaining."

"You want to own the relay."

"I want the relay to *exist*," Okafor said, and for once there was no irony anywhere in him. "Look around, Captain. Press ships are leaving. Militia rotates out next week—they've got their own borders to worry about. The cameras go away, and what's left? Twelve stateless people on a structure held together by prayer and two-hundred-year-old gaskets, sitting in dead space where nobody can hear them scream. Somebody has to hold title. Somebody has to be *responsible* for this place. Otherwise the next Halcyon comes along and it's just a derelict again."

Karrow looked at him for a long moment. Then she pulled up the claim forms on the *Magpie's* console and filed them before she could think better of it.

*Vessel Magpie, registry KS-4417, claims salvage of deep-space structure designated Relay Meridian-Prime, position as transmitted, intent: preservation and custodianship.*

The adjudication bots processed it in nine minutes. There was no one to contest it. Halcyon's legal apparatus, which would once have buried such a claim under forty years of motions, had ceased to exist as anything but a cautionary tale taught in law schools. The claim came back stamped and sealed, and Karrow stared at it for a long time—the first thing she had ever owned that wasn't a ship or a debt—and felt something turn over in her chest that she refused, for several days, to name.

The press called her the Warden of Dead Space within the month. She hated it. She kept the clipping anyway.

* * *

The survivors held a census.

It was Sefa's insistence, and it was the act that Karrow understood last of everything that happened that season, until one night she didn't understand it anymore and simply knew it, the way you know your own hands.

Twelve names. Read aloud in the dome, entered into a ledger Sefa had sewn herself from salvaged fabric because paper felt more real than a file. Sefa Meridian-Prime, keeper. Ilo, born aboard, first generation never to know the compartments as hiding places. Nine others, ranging from a woman of sixty who remembered the last caretaker before Sefa to a boy of six who had never seen another human being outside the relay until the press ships came. Twelve names, read aloud, witnessed, recorded.

"The Combine deleted a census," Sefa explained, when Karrow asked why it mattered. "So we make one. Every time. As long as there is anyone to count. That is how you refuse. Not with guns—with ink."

Karrow thought of her own name, Vess Karrow, which she had spent forty years treating as a registration number attached to a hull, something to be traded, mortgaged, hidden behind false transponders when the customs boats got curious. She thought of Astrid Brightwater, who had carried a dead man's name like a debt collection notice. She thought of the way Ilo had stood up in the vote and said *I want to be seen*, and how the word *seen* had landed in that room like something physical, something with weight and temperature.

"Ink," she said.

"Ink," Sefa agreed.

* * *

The wreckage cleanup took eleven days, and Karrow supervised all of it, because custodianship, she was learning, was mostly logistics wearing a ceremonial coat.

Halcyon's enforcer vessel—the one that had opened fire on the relay during the full broadcast, the one Karrow's *Magpie* and the defecting militia wings had held off for nineteen minutes that everyone aboard would remember differently for the rest of their lives—drifted a thousand kilometers off, broken into four major pieces and a halo of debris. Its crew had scuttled rather than surrender to militia custody; two lifeboats had made it out before the reactor died, and the militia had picked up fourteen survivors who would spend years in tribunals explaining themselves. The rest stayed with the ship.

Salvage rights on the wreck fell to Karrow by the same convention that had given her the relay, and she claimed it, stripped it of everything useful—armor plate, fusion bottles, a medical bay in better condition than anything the relay possessed—and then did something that made Okafor look at her strangely for a week.

She towed the largest piece of the hull into a decaying orbit around the system's lone airless rock and let it burn.

"No marker?" he asked. "No memorial?"

"It's a Halcyon gunship," Karrow said. "The memorial's already built. It's the dome upstairs, and the ledger, and the twelve names. I'm not going to mark the grave of a weapon." She paused, watching the first fragments kiss the rock's horizon in brief orange lines. "But log the crew numbers. Fourteen hundred people on that ship, most of them conscripts and kids chasing a pension. Log them. Somebody should know they existed, even if all they ever did was point the wrong gun."

Okafor logged them. It took six hours. He played Meridian funeral songs while he worked, music that had survived two centuries in the memory of one old woman, and neither of them spoke again until it was done.

* * *

Justice, Karrow decided somewhere in the second month, arrives the way light from dead stars arrives: late, diminished, and undeniable. You cannot speed it up. You can only make sure that when it finally gets where it's going, someone is standing there to receive it.

The tribunals ground forward on Ceres and Vesta and Titan. Former directors testified against former directors in an endless cascade of plea bargains. The Meridian Estate Board—twelve survivors, one keeper, and a growing staff of volunteer archivists who arrived monthly on the militia supply runs—filed claims that would take decades to resolve, and filed them anyway, because ink. Restitution funds were established, mismanaged, restructured, and established again. Three planets formally apologized. Apologies, Sefa observed, cost nothing and weighed less, but she accepted them on behalf of the dead, because refusing them would have cost the living more.

Hale remained unfound. Every few weeks a sighting surfaced—a fuel depot in the Tartarus Reach, a pleasure habitat off Alpha Lyr, a face in the background of a smuggler's port recording—and every few weeks the sighting dissolved on inspection. But the warrant stayed live. The reward stayed posted. And in the relay's dome, every evening, Sefa read names aloud into the dark, and among the eleven thousand were the ones Hale had signed personally, and Karrow came to understand that the old woman was not mourning so much as *waiting*, with the patience of someone who had already waited forty years and could wait forty more.

"We'll get him," Karrow said once. "Somebody always talks. Somebody always needs the money."

"Yes," Sefa said placidly. "But that is your hunt, Captain. Mine is longer. I am hunting every name back into the light. He is only one name." She smiled, thin and terrible. "Though I confess I look forward to reading his."

* * *

On the fifty-third day, with the relay's transmitter rebuilt better than it had ever been, with the census ledger on its third volume, with Ilo teaching Meridian to anyone who'd sit still and several who wouldn't, Karrow finally went below to the console deck where it had all ended.

She told herself it was an inspection. Custodial responsibility, structural assessment, all of it true. But her boots carried her past the transmitter housing and the scorched corridor where Tam had made his choice and the compartment where Drenn had nearly killed them all, and they carried her, without consulting her, to the operations station at the center of it all.

The chair was still there. The console bore the marks of use—fingerprint smudges in the dust of a place that had been dustless for two centuries, a water ring where someone had set down a cup, a hairline crack in the display housing from the day the fleet breached the dome. Nobody had cleaned it. Sefa had forbidden it, quietly, without explanation, and everyone had obeyed.

Astrid Brightwater sat in her memory the way he'd sat in that chair: slumped, frost on his eyelashes, hand resting on the send key as if he'd only stepped away for a moment, expression calm in a way that had frightened her more than blood would have. Warranty expired, the medics said later. Neural cascade, systemic failure, nothing anyone could have done—not out here, not with what he'd spent of himself. He had known the price going in. He had paid it with his hand on the key until the progress bar filled.

The survivors had wrapped him themselves, in fabric Sefa had woven, and the militia had offered a military transport home, and the survivors had declined. He was theirs, they said. He would stay with the relay. There was a niche in the foundation level, beside the sealed vault where the original's proof had slept for ten years, and a plaque that Ilo had cut by hand, and Karrow had stood at the funeral and discovered, to her considerable irritation, that she was capable of grief for a man she'd met as a rival and a mark.

Now she stood at the console and put her hand where his had been. Cold metal. Warm memory.

"Custodian's report," she said aloud, to no one, to him. "Array's stable. Census is current. Your survivors are insufferable and magnificent, and the boy's going to be a scholar if somebody doesn't kill him first. Karrow out."

Behind her, footsteps. She turned, and it was Okafor, and his face was wrong—not grief, not fear, something between them and stranger than both.

"Captain," he said. "You need to see this. The medical bay—we've been cataloging the relay's systems, and there's a partitioned archive in the diagnostic core, encrypted, running hot. It's been drawing power continuously since the day we scanned him." He swallowed. "Vess. It's a neural pattern. It's complete. It's *him*."

Karrow looked back at the cold console, at the frost-line ghost of where a man had sat and refused to leave, and felt the whole shape of the season tilt beneath her.

"Sefa," she said. "Get me Sefa. And wake the others."
