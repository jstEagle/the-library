## 2. Verdance, Half-Grown

The hub smelled the way it always did when Lior came down out of the hills: recycled air and hot metal and, underneath it all, the faint green ghost of the algae vats that kept forty thousand people breathing. He'd been gone eleven weeks on the long route. Eleven weeks of his own company and the hauler's autopilot and the gardens in their cradles, and now the hub hit him like a wall of noise.

He docked at Cargo Gate Four and stood a moment in the hauler's doorway, letting the crowd wash past him. Verdance had grown since his last run. That was the official story, anyway — growth, expansion, the colony coming into its own. But Lior had spent twenty years reading landscapes, and what he saw was not growth. It was crowding. The residential spires had added three new levels each, stacked like teeth in an overcrowded jaw. The market concourse had swallowed the old community garden — his mother's first project, the one she'd planted the year he was born — and replaced it with a row of oxygen kiosks where citizens queued with ration chits held out like communion wafers.

*Personal allocation reduced to 412 liters per day,* said the sign above the kiosks, in cheerful amber letters. *Thank you for breathing responsibly.*

Breathing responsibly. He almost laughed. Somewhere in the archives there was footage of the landing generation, the ones who'd come down in the first shuttles with seed vaults and terraform rigs and a charter that promised open sky within a century. They'd breathed free atmosphere for the first time on this world and wept. Now their grandchildren queued to buy it back by the liter.

"Whitlock." A cargo clerk waved him over, not looking up from her console. "You're late. Route schedule says you should've been in nine days ago."

"Storm season in the Meridian passes." He handed over his manifest. "Lost four days waiting out a dust front at Station Kettle."

She scanned the manifest, frowned. "You're carrying seed stock?"

"Always."

"Priority's changed. Unload the foodstuffs first. Seeds go to secondary storage."

Lior felt something tighten behind his ribs. "Secondary storage is unpressurized overflow."

"It's pressurized enough."

"For how long?"

The clerk finally looked up at him. She was maybe thirty, tired in the specific way of people who'd stopped expecting things to get better. "Look," she said, quietly, "I just process manifests. Take it up with Supply Council if you've got a complaint."

"I will."

"Don't bother." She slid the manifest back across the counter. "Councilor Vane announced the reallocation last week. Seed programs are being scaled back. Food security comes first, he said." Her mouth twisted around the phrase like it tasted bad. "*We can't feed trees to children.*"

"That's not what the seeds are for."

"I know what they're for." She glanced past him, at the queue at the oxygen kiosks, at the tired faces in it. "Everybody who thinks about it knows what they're for. Go home, Whitlock. You look like you need sleep."

He didn't argue. There was no one to argue with; the argument had already been won by whoever shouted loudest, and he knew exactly who that was.

* * *

His uncle's face was everywhere.

It had been everywhere for years, but on this visit it seemed to have multiplied. Vane Whitlock looked out from the public screens in the transit corridors, from the posters pasted over the old mural of the First Landing, from the wrist terminals of strangers in the market. The portrait was always the same: silver-haired, square-jawed, eyes fixed slightly above the camera as though gazing into Verdance's glorious future. The caption beneath varied only in emphasis.

*STRENGTH THROUGH SOVEREIGNTY.*

*VERDANCE FIRST. VERDANCE FOREVER.*

*THEY TOOK YOUR SISTER. DON'T LET THEM TAKE YOUR CHILDREN'S AIR.*

That last one was new. Lior stopped in front of it for a long moment. It hung in the corridor outside the council chambers, three meters tall, and it showed a stylized image of Ashfall — the sister colony, the estranged one, the enemy — as a black shape crouched against the stars, reaching down toward a small green world with grasping metal fingers.

They took your sister.

Not *we lost Councilor Mara Whitlock forty years ago under circumstances never fully explained.* Not *the investigation was closed prematurely.* *They took her.* As if Ashfall had reached across the dark and plucked her out of the sky. As if grief were a weapon to be issued to every citizen along with their ration chit.

Lior had been six years old when his mother died. He remembered almost nothing: a white room, someone crying, the smell of antiseptic. His uncle had raised him afterward, and Vane had been — Lior searched for the honest word — *attentive*. Attentive about school, about meals, about the anniversary memorial every year, where Vane would stand before the crowd with his hand on young Lior's shoulder and speak about sacrifice and enemies and the debt Verdance owed.

He had never once spoken about what Mara had actually been doing in the last week of her life. Lior had assumed, for forty years, that Vane didn't know either.

"You're staring at my poster."

Lior turned. Vane stood at the end of the corridor, flanked by two aides, holding a cup of tea he wasn't drinking. Up close, the portraits flattered him. The real face was heavier, older, with deep lines around the mouth that the artists smoothed away. Only the eyes were the same — fixed slightly above you, always, as if you were standing a little to the left of where history needed you to be.

"Councilor." Lior nodded. "Uncle."

"Lior." Vane crossed the corridor and gripped his shoulder, the same shoulder, the same grip, the same pressure he'd applied at every memorial since Lior was six. "Good route? The southern settlements fed?"

"Fed. For now. The Kiln Basin aquifer's dropping faster than the models predicted. Another three years and—"

"Three years is a lifetime." Vane released his shoulder. "Verdance has survived worse than a dry aquifer. We survived *them*." A tilt of his head toward the poster, toward the black shape among the stars. "You're back just in time, actually. Council session tomorrow. I want family in the gallery when we vote on the grid defense appropriation."

"The what?"

"Orbital defense modernization." Vane's voice took on the cadence Lior recognized from a thousand broadcasts — the measured, reasonable tone that made aggression sound like housekeeping. "Ashfall has been reactivating platforms on their side of the Lagrange points. Our early warning grid is forty years old. I intend to fix that."

"Reactivating platforms for what purpose?"

Vane smiled. It was a good smile. It had been on currency, once. "For the same purpose we'd reactivate ours. Deterrence. Peace through strength, nephew. It's the only peace that lasts." He raised his untouched tea in a small salute. "Come tomorrow. Sit with the families. It matters, having the martyr's son visible."

Martyr's son. Lior carried the phrase out of the corridor like a stone in his shoe.

* * *

His apartment hadn't changed in the eleven weeks he'd been gone, which was itself a kind of change; the building's maintenance drones were falling further behind every cycle, and half the units on his floor had standing repair tickets older than some of the children in the settlement schools. He dropped his pack, opened the window onto the atrium — real window, pre-fab era, a luxury nobody built anymore — and let the hub's noise rise around him.

That was when he noticed the quiet.

The atrium below should have been full of children. Evening hours were theirs — the one unstructured block in a colony day scheduled down to the minute. Usually the space rang with them: ball games against the fountain wall, races around the planter beds, the shrieking politics of kids negotiating territories that shifted hourly.

Tonight there were perhaps a dozen down there, and they weren't playing. They sat in loose clusters on the fountain's rim, talking in low voices, or lay sprawled on the warm pavement staring up through the atrium skylight at the early stars. One little boy, maybe seven, was asleep sitting up, head lolling, while an older girl watched over him with the weary competence of a parent.

Lior leaned on the windowsill and watched them, and the unease that had been sitting in his chest since the cargo gate grew a degree colder.

He went down.

"Evening," he said to the girl keeping watch. "Slow night?"

She looked up at him. Ten, maybe eleven, dark circles under her eyes that belonged on a night-shift worker. "Everyone's tired," she said.

"Tired? It's seven o'clock."

"I know what time it is." She said it without rudeness, which somehow made it worse. "Nobody sleeps good lately. My brother was up three times last night. He wouldn't say why." She glanced at the sleeping boy. "He talks in his sleep now. Counting."

"Counting?"

"Numbers. Backwards." She shrugged, a gesture too old for her shoulders. "All the kids do it. Ask anybody."

He did ask anybody. He walked the rim of the fountain and asked, gently, the way you ask children when you don't want to scare them, and got the same answer in a dozen variations. Tired. Bad dreams. Talking in their sleep. And underneath the variations, repeated so often it stopped sounding strange and started sounding liturgical:

*A voice. A lady's voice. Counting.*

A boy of eight told him, "She counts down from a big number but I always wake up before she gets low. I don't wanna hear her get low." He said it matter-of-factly, the way you report weather.

"What does she sound like?" Lior asked.

The boy considered. "Kind," he said finally. "Like somebody who knows you. Like a mom, maybe. I don't got a mom so I'm guessing."

Lior's mouth had gone dry somewhere in the middle of the conversation. He crouched down to the boy's level. "Where are you when she counts? In the dream."

"There's a door." The boy pointed at the far wall of the atrium, at blank prefab paneling. "Green door. She's behind it. She counts and the number gets smaller and the door gets—" He paused, frowning at his own memory. "Closer. Every night it's closer."

Around the fountain, Lior realized, other children had gone still. They were listening. Not surprised — listening the way you listen to someone describe your own house. A girl with braids nodded slowly. "Ours is green too," she said. "Same door."

"Same dream," said another.

"Every night," said the first boy, and yawned, and looked suddenly, terribly young. "When's she gonna finish counting, mister? Do you know?"

Lior did not answer. He stood there in the evening noise of a colony that was running out of air, patience, and time, surrounded by children who shared a single dream, and heard — absurdly, impossibly, across forty years of silence — a voice he knew better than any voice on this world or any other.

Kind. Like somebody who knows you.

Like a mom.

He walked back to his apartment without feeling his feet touch the ground. On the shelf beside his bed sat the box he'd carried through twenty years of long routes: his mother's letters, unsent, unread, sealed in her own handwriting. He touched the lid once, lightly, the way you touch a door you're not ready to open.

Behind him, out in the atrium, faint through the glass, he could hear a child begin to count in his sleep.

*"...nine... eight..."*

Lior did not sleep that night either.
