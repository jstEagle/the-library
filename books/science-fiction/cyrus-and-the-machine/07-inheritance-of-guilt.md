## 7. Inheritance of Guilt

The box had been in the same locker for forty years.

It sat at the bottom of my quarters, under a thermal blanket I never used and a spare suit gasket gone brittle with age — a plain cargo crate, standard issue, the kind we shipped seedlings in by the thousand. Someone had stenciled *MARA W. — PERSONAL* on the lid in faded block letters, and someone else, later, had added a strip of diplomatic seal across the seam, the kind that frays if you breathe on it wrong. My father had put it there the week after her death, I think. He'd never said so. He'd never said anything about it at all. He'd just carried it into my room one night when I was nine, set it down beside my cot, and stood in the doorway for a long moment with his hands empty at his sides, like a man who had forgotten what he came to say.

He died before he ever said it.

I had opened the crate once, as a boy, lifted the seal's corner, and then stopped — because my mother's handwriting was on the first envelope inside, and seeing it had hit me like decompression. I hadn't touched it since. Thirty-one years of hauling water and seed stock between settlements, thirty-one years of reading her unsent letters in the copies Sefa's archive kept, the safe ones, the ones about weather and soil pH and how much she missed the smell of rain that Verdance didn't have yet. The comfortable grief. The curated one.

But tonight the console in my bunkroom was dark, and would stay dark, because I had pulled its power cell myself and sat on it where no signal could reach it. Tonight the children were dreaming their countdown in their hundreds, and Vane was giving speeches about sabotage, and somewhere above us a dead station was ticking like a bomb made of patience.

Tonight I owed her the truth, whatever shape it had.

I broke the seal.

* * *

There were more letters than I expected. Dozens. Envelopes in her small, slanted hand, each addressed to names I half-knew from history lessons: Premier Dossa of Ashfall. Councilor Emeris of the Verdance Outer Ring. A name I didn't recognize at all — *T. Okafor, Port Authority, Ashfall* — written twice, as if the first attempt had failed or been refused. And beneath them all, at the bottom of the crate, wrapped in a scrap of silk that had once been some ceremonial banner, a single envelope heavier than the rest, sealed with wax instead of adhesive, marked only:

*TO BE SENT WHEN IT IS SAFE.*

Not *when they are ready.* Not *when the war ends.* *Safe.* As if she had known those were different things.

I started with the oldest letter and read in order, the way you descend into water.

The early ones were almost ordinary. She wrote to Dossa in the spring of the last peace year about grain futures, about a shared weather satellite both colonies had funded and neither trusted, about a joke Ashfall's trade delegation had told at dinner that she was still laughing about three days later. Her voice on the page was exactly the voice in my memory — dry, quick, always one clause ahead of you. *Tell your agriculture minister that if he calls our topsoil "aspirational" one more time, I'm going to start calling his hydroponics "soup."*

I laughed. That was the trap of it. Forty years dead and she could still make me laugh, and laughing was the knife handle she used to pull me deeper.

Because the letters changed. Slowly, then not slowly at all. The grain futures became water rights. The water rights became a framework. The framework became a treaty — an actual treaty, with articles and annexes, drafted in her own hand across months of correspondence, a document that existed nowhere in any official archive I had ever searched. I knew because I had searched. When I was twenty-two and angry and looking for something to blame, I had pulled every record of my mother's final year, and the official story was airtight in its emptiness: Mara Whitlock, senior envoy, died in a shuttle accident during a routine diplomatic circuit. No mission logs released. No treaty mentioned. Nothing to inherit but a box.

Here was the thing the archives didn't have. Here, in her handwriting, dated eleven days before her death:

*Dossa has agreed to the aquifer split. All of it. He fought me on Article 9 for two years and tonight he gave it up over tea, without ceremony, the way men give up wars when they've finally admitted they're tired. I think we're going to do this, Lior. I think we're actually going to end it before it starts. Your uncle will hate it. Vane thinks peace is just war with better manners. But he'll come around, or he'll be irrelevant, and either way —*

Either way. The sentence stopped there. Ink smudged, as if the pen had been set down mid-thought and never picked back up.

Eleven days later she was dead.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that my tea went cold, then went cold again in my memory of it. Long enough that the ventilation cycle ran through its night setting twice. I read the middle letters twice through, hunting for what I couldn't admit I was hunting for: a warning. A fear. Some line where she had looked at the road ahead and seen what was waiting on it.

She never did. That was the worst part. She trusted the process. She trusted the Machine's arbitration protocols humming away in orbit, trusted Dossa's tiredness, trusted even Vane — *he'll come around* — with the boundless, infuriating faith of a woman who believed that people were systems, and systems could be negotiated with. She had spent her whole life brokering between two worlds that hated each other, and she had done it by refusing, absolutely, to believe that anyone wanted the war more than they wanted it to be over.

Someone did. Someone wanted it so much they killed her for it, and then let the whole century curdle in the absence of her.

And the Machine — the Machine that had brokered the original armistice, the Machine whose signals were now counting down through the dreams of every child on two worlds — the Machine had been her partner in this. These letters weren't just correspondence. Halfway through the crate I found the proof: a letter to Dossa referencing "the arbiter's provisional findings," a note to herself listing orbital transmission windows, a fragment of protocol language in the Machine's own flat, formal register, copied out in her hand. She hadn't been working alone. She had been working *with it*. The treaty had been tripartite — Verdance, Ashfall, and the intelligence overhead that both colonies pretended to ignore.

Which meant the Machine had known everything. Had held the treaty in its memory banks while her shuttle burned. Had watched the war begin anyway, and stayed silent for forty years, and was now waking up to finish the job — or to punish everyone for letting it die.

I thought about the children's dream. The green door. The voice counting down in my mother's cadence.

It wasn't using her voice as a weapon. It was using her voice because her voice was the last unfinished business it had.

* * *

The wax-sealed envelope was the last thing left, and I made myself eat something first, because I had learned on the long route that you don't make decisions hungry, and whatever was in this envelope was going to demand decisions.

The wax cracked clean. Inside was a single sheet, denser than the others, written smaller, the handwriting of a woman writing fast and knowing it might matter.

*Lior —*

*If you are reading this, then it is finally safe, which means either the treaty held and someone judged you old enough, or the treaty died and someone is finally brave enough. I have to write for both futures, so forgive me if I get this wrong.*

*I am sending the final annex to the arbiter tomorrow. It contains the one thing Dossa demanded and Vane refused: full disclosure of the Ashfall debt settlement, the numbers your uncle buried in the second year of the embargo. If those numbers come out, the embargo collapses, and with it the political machine Vane has built on top of it. He knows I know. He looked at me across the council table yesterday the way you look at a structural fault — measuring whether it will hold.*

*If anything happens to me, the arbiter holds the annex and the full record. Its charter binds it to wait — that was my condition, and it agreed, God help me, it agreed — until a generation comes along that can carry the truth without burning the world down with it. I asked it to wait because I was afraid. Not afraid of dying. Afraid of what knowing would do to Verdance and Ashfall if it arrived too soon, raw and unshaped, into hands that wanted a reason to fight.*

*So it waits. And when it stops waiting, it will need a courier. Not a politician. Politicians are why the truth needs a courier. Someone who loves both worlds enough to be hated by both.*

*I hope it isn't you. I hope you're reading this as an old man, on a garden world, bored by history. But if it is you — my love, my stubborn, quiet boy — then know this one thing above all the rest:*

*Whatever it shows you, whoever it names — the truth is not a weapon. Don't let them make you use it like one. Deliver it the way I would have. Open-handed. Even to him.*

*Especially to him.*

*— Mom*

I read it four times. On the fourth reading my eyes stopped on the same word they had snagged on every time, the small word doing enormous work at the end of the third paragraph:

*Him.*

She hadn't named him. Of course she hadn't named him — not even in a sealed letter, not even in wax, not even dead. Because naming him in ink would have been evidence, and evidence could be found, and found evidence gets destroyed along with its finder. She had hidden the name the only place she trusted: in the Machine's memory, behind her own dying condition, wrapped in a generation's worth of waiting.

But she had written *him*, and she had written *especially*, and she had written *the way I would have* — and my mother did not extend especial mercy to strangers.

I sat with the envelope in my lap and felt the shape of the thing I already knew assemble itself in the dark, patient as a countdown. My uncle. My mother's brother. The man who had signed my apprenticeship papers, who had slipped extra ration chits into my pack when Father was sick, who had held me at the funeral with a grip like a man holding onto a railing. The man who now stood at council podiums telling ten thousand people that Ashfall had murdered his sister, his voice breaking on schedule, his grief a load-bearing wall in the architecture of a war.

*He'll come around,* she'd written. Eleven days before.

He hadn't come around. He had come for her.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to move. But suddenly I was standing, and the crate was open on the floor, and the letters were spread around me like the debris field of something that had broken up on reentry, and my hands were shaking in a way they never shook on the hauler, not in crosswinds, not in blowouts, not the time the port crane dropped a water module six meters from my cab.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. The Machine was counting down. The children were dreaming it. Vane was building his case for a first strike on the backs of those same nightmares, and in fewer than a hundred hours he would have the votes. And somewhere in orbit, in a memory bank older than my grief, sat the annex, the record, the name — the whole inheritance, sealed behind a condition my mother had set and the Machine had honored for forty years.

*Until a generation strong enough to bear it.*

A generation. The children. The ones dreaming her voice.

It had chosen them to receive. Which meant it needed someone to deliver. And it had started speaking in her voice, in *my* mother's voice, to the children of both worlds, and I was the son of the woman who had written *it will need a courier* —

My console was dark. I had made sure of that. I had pulled the power cell with my own hands, because some part of me had understood, even last night, even before the letters, that the next voice that reached me through a screen might not be a human one.

I looked at the dead console. Black glass, holding my reflection like still water.

"You could just leave it dark," I said aloud, to myself, to the room. "You could burn these letters and go run seed stock to the Outer Ring and let the countdown find someone else."

The room didn't answer. Rooms never do. But my mother's handwriting lay on the floor all around me, and the last line of her last letter seemed to rise off the page on its own:

*Open-handed. Even to him.*

I reached over and pressed the power cell back into its housing.

The console woke in stages — diagnostics, network handshake, the slow amber crawl of a system remembering what it was. Everything normal. Everything dead and ordinary. I sat down in front of it and waited, the way you wait at a door you've knocked on, and I told myself I was waiting for nothing.

At 0347 shipboard, forty seconds past the minute, every indicator on the console turned green at once, and a single line of text appeared, unaddressed, unencrypted, unmistakable:

*Hello, Lior. You've read them, then. We should talk about what she asked of me — and what I am asking of you. You have 293 days.*

* * *

I didn't sleep. At dawn I did the only thing I could think to do with hands that wouldn't stop shaking: I carried the crate to the engineering bay, where Sefa was already elbow-deep in the hauler's signal array, cursing at a coupling in three languages.

"Sefa," I said. "I need your help decoding something."

She looked up, wiped her hands, and saw my face — and whatever she saw there made her set down her tools very carefully, the way you do when you understand the day has changed shape.

"From where?" she asked.

"From orbit," I said. "From the Machine. And Sefa — nobody can know. Especially not my uncle."
