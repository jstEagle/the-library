## 9. The Children's Testimony

Iyo dreamed the door again.

She was getting good at it now — at arriving, at standing where the dream wanted her to stand, on the wet black soil with the green door floating a hand's width above the ground like it had been set there by someone who didn't understand doors. Behind her, the other children of Settlement Kestrel stood in their sleep-clothes, barefoot, silent. Forty-one of them. She'd counted once and the counting had felt important, though she couldn't have said why.

The voice was already speaking when she arrived. It always was.

*Two hundred and sixty-one.*

Her mother's voice would have been warm. This voice was warm too, but wrong-warm, like soup reheated too many times, like something that had learned warmth from a recording. Iyo had stopped being afraid of it around day ten. Now she just listened, the way you listen to rain on a roof you can't fix.

*Two hundred and sixty-one.*

Then the door opened — and this was new, this had never happened before — and instead of the green light that usually poured out and washed everything to the color of young leaves, there was a face.

A man's face. Younger than any father she knew. Dark hair combed back neat, a jaw still soft with youth, eyes that were doing something Iyo didn't have a word for yet. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking past all the children, at something behind them, and his expression was the expression of a person watching a thing happen that he had decided to let happen.

Iyo woke up with her heart going hard and her hand already reaching for the stylus on her cot shelf, because some part of her, deep below the dreaming, knew what she was supposed to do.

She drew him before the dream could fade. She drew him three times, fast, each one better than the last, until the third one looked like a person and not a rumor of a person. Then she sat in the dark of the children's dormitory at Kestrel Station, surrounded by forty-one sleeping kids who would all wake up having seen the same face, and she looked at what she'd made.

She thought: *I know him.*

She thought: *No I don't.*

Both thoughts were true, and neither helped.

* * *

In Settlement Marrow, six hundred kilometers east across the ash plains, Tam Oduya woke at 0340 with his brother's elbow in his ribs and his own name half-spoken in his mouth, because in the dream someone had called it — not the warm-wrong voice, but a woman's voice, low and urgent, saying *Tam, Tam, remember this part* — and then the door had opened and the young man's face had been there, and the woman's voice had said, very quietly:

*Look at him. Look as long as you need.*

Tam was nine. He lay in the dark doing what he'd been taught to do with big feelings, which was shrink them down until they fit in a box, and label the box, and put the box on a high shelf. But this feeling wouldn't shrink. It kept unfolding, bigger every time he folded it, until finally he gave up and named it honestly: he was afraid of a face he'd seen in a dream, and worse than the fear was the certainty underneath it, the bone-deep knowing that the face belonged to somebody real, somebody whose bread he might have eaten, whose shadow he might have walked behind in the market corridors without ever turning around.

He got up. He went to the dormitory wall where the younger kids' drawings were pinned — because the drawing thing had started two nights ago, spreading between settlements like a fever nobody caught in waking life — and he added his own version of the face among the others.

When the morning shift came through, an aide named Pell stopped in front of the wall and stood there for a long time. There were eleven drawings of the same man. Different hands, different ages, different skill levels — a toddler's smudge, Iyo Kestrel's startlingly precise lines, Tam's careful middle effort — and all of them, unmistakably, the same person.

Pell took a picture with his slate. He told himself he'd show it to his supervisor. He told himself it was probably nothing, kids sharing dreams was a known thing, sleep-studies said so, collective suggestion, mass hysteria, all the words for explaining away a thing you don't want to be true.

But Pell had grown up on stories of the Machine, the way everyone had — the great orbital intelligence that brokered the peace and then went silent, forty years ago, almost to the day. And the countdown number in the children's reports had dropped again overnight, steady as a heartbeat, and no mass hysteria in the literature counted down in perfect synchrony across six hundred kilometers.

He sent the picture anyway. To the only address he trusted more than his supervisor's: the hauler pilot who fixed the Kestrel gardens, the quiet one, Whitlock.

* * *

Dessa Vane did not draw the face.

Dessa was seven, and she was the councilor's daughter, and she had learned early — the way children of important men learn things, through the skin rather than the ears — that some knowledge is dangerous to hold visibly. So when the door opened in last night's dream and the young man's face appeared, and the warm-wrong voice said *look at him*, Dessa looked, and understood, and woke, and said nothing.

Because the face was familiar in a way that lived under familiarity. Not like recognizing a neighbor. Like recognizing your own hands.

She lay in her bed in the council residence, in the good oxygen, in the wing of the hub where the air always smelled faintly of real citrus because her father liked it, and she turned the face over in her mind the way she turned stones over in the creek beds on school trips, looking for what was underneath.

Uncle Lior had that same line at the corner of the eye. The way his mouth went when he was trying not to smile — she'd seen it on the face in the door, or almost seen it, a ghost of it, the way a photograph of a river resembles the river.

*Daddy's family,* she thought, and then, because she was seven and honest even when she was careful: *the face is Daddy, but young. Or it's somebody who looks how Daddy used to look.*

She got up, took one of her own drawing sheets — good stock, heavy fiber, the kind her father brought back from council supply — and drew the face once, carefully, better than she let anyone know she could. Then she folded it into eighths and put it inside the lining of her boot, where she kept the other secret things, and went to breakfast, and ate her protein porridge, and said nothing.

Across the colony, in eleven settlements, other children were making other choices about the same face. Most drew. A few told. One boy in Solace tried to burn his drawing and couldn't get the flame to catch in the thin air and ended up burying it in the compost trench instead, which somehow felt more permanent.

By 0900 hub time, the drawings existed in enough quantity and enough agreement that they had stopped being a curiosity and become a fact. Facts, on Verdance, eventually reached the council. But first, if you knew whom to send them to, they reached a hauler pilot somewhere on the long route between gardens.

* * *

Lior was knee-deep in nutrient slurry at the Halcyon terraces when his slate chimed with Pell's message, and he nearly lost his grip on the transfer hose.

He rinsed his hands, which was absurd — the slate was sealed against worse than slurry — but he needed the three seconds of ritual before he looked. He'd been avoiding the dream reports for days, telling himself the children's nightmares were stress, were rationing fatigue, were the ordinary psychic weather of a colony running low on everything except fear. Sefa had traced the pulses to the derelict station; Lior had heard the Machine speak through his own dead console; he had 261 days ticking somewhere behind his sternum. He did not need drawings.

He opened the file anyway.

Eleven faces rendered by eleven children. He swiped through them slowly, and the world did the thing it does when a floor you've been standing on turns out to be a painting of a floor.

The first few he resisted. Kids distort. Kids exaggerate. The jaw was too soft, the hair too neat —

The fourth drawing was Iyo Kestrel's, and it was good, unnervingly good, the kind of likeness that comes from a child who draws what is actually there instead of what she knows should be there. And Lior looked at it and felt his mother's name rise in his throat like sickness.

Because he knew the face. Of course he knew the face. It hung in the council rotunda at twice life size, rendered in official bronze: Councilor Edmond Vane, architect of Verdance's resilience, father of the security state, uncle to Lior Whitlock. But the bronze was sixty years old and stern, and this face was young — thirty, maybe less — with the sternness not yet grown into it, only promised in the set of the mouth.

And there was something else. Something the children had drawn without knowing they were drawing it, something that appeared in eight of the eleven pictures in small variations: the angle of the head. Turned slightly away. Looking at something off-frame, to the left, downward.

Lior had spent his whole childhood studying the one holorecording that survived of his mother — four seconds of Mara Whitlock laughing at some function, before the war, before Ashfall, before everything. He knew every frame of it the way other people knew prayers. And in the last frame, just before the recording cut, her gaze had gone off-camera, to the left, downward, toward someone standing at the edge of the shot.

Toward whoever she had been about to speak to next.

The children had drawn the killer's point of view. They had drawn the face as it leaned over her — no. No, they had drawn the face as it watched her, from beside her, from the left and slightly below, the way you look at a sister.

Lior sat down on the terrace edge, slurry soaking through his suit, and made himself say it inside his own skull, plainly, the way the Machine had spoken to him: *They are showing the children the murder. Piece by piece. Night by night. And they are showing it to me.*

Not vengeance. He understood that now, with a clarity that felt like falling. The Machine had held this for forty years — Sefa's decodes said as much, a name redacted even from its own memory — and it had chosen to deliver the truth through the only channel no government controlled, no council censored, no security apparatus thought to monitor: the shared country of sleeping children. It was making the whole colony witness. It was making the truth too big to bury, carried in forty small skulls that no one could arrest.

And it had saved the face for the moment the countdown passed two hundred and sixty, because a verdict needs a defendant before it needs a date.

His slate chimed again. Sefa.

*"Tell me you got Pell's message,"* she said, no greeting, her voice tight in the way it got when she'd been awake too long staring at waveforms. *"Because I've got something, and you need to be sitting down, and also you need to be somewhere that isn't an open terrace."*

"I'm sitting."

*"The pulse pattern changed last night. Same carrier, same source, but the payload structure shifted — it's not telemetry anymore, it's structured data, image data, broadcast on a band only the old medical sleep-monitors even have receivers for."* A breath. *"Lior, it's been transmitting the face. On repeat. All night. To every sleeping child on both colonies."*

"Both," Lior said.

*"Both."* Silence on the line, the specific silence of an engineer who has just watched her own sentence detonate. *"Ashfall's kids are dreaming him too."*

Lior looked out over the Halcyon terraces — the young wheat, the nitrogen-fixers, the slow patient work of making a dead world breathe — and thought about his uncle in the rotunda, giving his speech about enemy sabotage, about Ashfall poisoning their children's sleep. About how right Vane had been, and how catastrophically wrong, and how the truth was so much stranger and so much worse: the enemy hadn't poisoned the dream.

The dream was the antidote. And it had been sitting in orbit, waiting forty years, holding its breath.

*"There's one more thing,"* Sefa said, quieter now. *"The image data has a signature embedded in it. Old encryption, diplomatic-grade, pre-war. I ran it against the public archives because I'm thorough and apparently self-destructive."* Pause. *"It's your mother's cipher, Lior. The face comes wrapped in your mother's encryption key."*

Below the terraces, far off across the ash plain, a dust storm was building along the horizon, a brown wall rising with the patience of something that has all the time in the world and intends to use it. Lior watched it come and understood, with the horror of total clarity, that the storm was not the weather.

It was the schedule. His uncle would have seen the drawings by nightfall. And a man who has spent forty years being the only witness to his own crime does not hesitate when the witnesses multiply.

"Sefa," he said. "How fast can we move the seed vault?"
