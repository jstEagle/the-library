## 19. Vane's Last Hour

The broadcast reached him in the council chamber, and it reached him in his daughter's voice.

He had heard the verdict a hundred ways by then — through the emergency channels, through aides who could not look at him, through the raw feed from the uplink he had seized too late. But none of it had prepared him for the moment the chamber doors opened and his security chief walked in pale as bone and said, "Councilor. It's the children. They're saying it. All of them. In their sleep."

Vane had risen from his chair. He remembered that. He remembered the strange calm that came over him, the way a room can go very quiet inside a man even while everything outside it burns.

Then Iyo — no. Not Iyo. His own daughter, Sera, eleven years old, standing in the corridor outside the chamber with her teacher holding her hand, reciting in a flat, sleepwalker's cadence:

*"Mara Whitlock was killed by her brother. The Machine has held the proof for forty years. It waited, as she asked, for a generation strong enough to bear it. The generation is here."*

Sera had never met Mara Whitlock. Sera had been born nineteen years after her aunt died. And here she stood, eyes half-closed, her small voice carrying her uncle's crime down the corridor like a bell.

"Get her out of here," Vane said, and his voice did not shake, and he hated it for not shaking.

* * *

They moved him to the secure residence under guard that was not called a guard — "protective detail," the chief insisted, and Vane almost laughed. Protective. From what? From the truth? The truth had already gotten in. It had gotten in through the children, through the one door in all of Verdance no security protocol had ever thought to watch.

He sat in his study with the lights low and listened to the colony tear itself apart in real time. The comms traffic was a river in flood. Councilors resigning. Councilors demanding his arrest. Councilors demanding Ashfall's destruction anyway, louder now, the way men shout when the floor is going. The long-range scopes showed Ashfall's gunships holding station at the border of the shared orbital band, and Ashfall's channels showed their own premier, Dossa, declaring a state of emergency, and somewhere in the noise of it all, both worlds were reaching for the same conclusion at the same moment: *strike first, before they use the chaos.*

And through it all, threading under everything, the Machine's countdown, audible now on every open channel because the Machine wanted it audible. It had stopped whispering to children. It was speaking to everyone.

*Two hundred and six days.*

No. Less. He checked. *One hundred and eighty-eight.* It was falling faster than the arithmetic said it should, and he understood — distantly, with the part of his mind that had always been good at machinery — that the Machine had begun to spend its own lifetime. It was burning its reactor down deliberately, converting patience into urgency, spending itself to make the deadline real.

His sister's voice, forty years dead, was going to burn a hole in the sky.

* * *

He had never thought of it as murder.

That was the thing none of them understood, not Lior, not the Machine, not the children with their green door. In the forty years since, he had turned the memory over more times than he could count, and it had never once been murder. It had been triage.

Mara had come home with a treaty. That was the phrase she used, bright and certain, standing in this same study, younger than he was now: *"I have a treaty, Aldous. Both councils have signed the framework. Ashfall gets the water rights, we get the orbital band, and in ten years nobody will remember why we were angry."*

And he had looked at her — brilliant, beloved, his sister, the only person who had ever beaten him at anything — and seen the future she was carrying, and understood that it was a slow death. A treaty like that didn't end the war. It ended the *possibility* of winning it. It locked Verdance into a half-terraformed world forever, a permanent tenancy under an alien sky, while Ashfall's deep mines made it rich and Verdance's children's children tended gardens for a planet that would never love them back. He had done the models. He was always better at the models. Every projection ended the same way: a century of slow decline, dignity bleeding out one concession at a time, and at the end of it, Ashfall owning the sky.

Mara's treaty was a mercy killing of Verdance's future, and she couldn't see it because she was good, and good people mistake surrender for peace.

So he had done the arithmetic that she refused to do. One death, now, at the moment of maximum leverage — blamed on Ashfall, the framework dead, the colonies hardened, Verdance given its century of anger to build on. One death against a hundred years. He had told himself it was the kind of decision a statesman makes and a coward avoids, and for forty years he had mostly believed it, in the way you believe a load-bearing wall.

The Machine had been there. He'd known that too, in the first terrible hour — known the orbital station had seen everything, its ancient optics pointed at the landing field where Mara's skiff burned. He had gone to it, once, alone, in the weeks after. He had stood in its dead docking cradle and spoken aloud to the dark, offering it anything, and the dark had said nothing, and he had told himself the thing was truly dead.

It had been listening. It had been *waiting*. His sister's last request, the Machine said — *wait for a generation strong enough to bear it* — and Vane had heard the sentence a thousand times in the last day and still could not decide whether it was mercy or the cruelest joke ever engineered. She had asked the Machine to hold the knife above his neck for forty years, and then hand it to children.

He poured a drink and did not drink it.

* * *

The knock came at 0300, and it was his security chief, and the man would not come inside.

"Councilor. The garrison. Half of them won't follow orders anymore. They're saying — they're saying their kids told them what you did."

"Then the other half will do."

"The other half is asking what we're defending, sir. A man or a verdict."

Vane looked at him for a long moment. "Get out," he said, almost gently, and the man went.

He sat alone in a residence that had become a tomb with excellent lighting, and he did the only thing left that the arithmetic allowed.

Because here was the truth he arrived at, alone, at three in the morning, and it came to him with the same clean calm as the first time: the situation had not actually changed. The verdict had changed *nothing*. Ashfall still held the deep mines. Verdance was still half-born. The children's testimony had destroyed *him*, not the strategic reality, and if Verdance collapsed now — if it went into the talks Lior was demanding, the talks the Machine's countdown was forcing, the surrender dressed as justice — then Mara would have won after all, forty years late. His whole life would resolve into a footnote: *the man who killed his sister and lost anyway.*

There was one move left on the board, and it was the move he had argued for in the war room a hundred times while the council flinched. First strike against Ashfall's grid. Decapitate their orbital capability before the Machine died and took everyone's satellites with it, before the chaos made them hesitate. If Ashfall was blind, Ashfall was beaten. If Ashfall was beaten, the verdict didn't matter. History was written by the world that still had lights.

The launch authority required two councilors' keys under the emergency protocols. He had his own. And Councilor Amara Dey — old, frightened, loyal in the way of people who had built their careers inside his shadow — had come to him an hour ago, weeping, and put her key on the table without being asked.

*"I don't believe the children,"* she'd said. *"I can't. Because if I believe them, then everything is nothing."*

He had understood her completely.

* * *

The command bunker under Council Hall had been built during the first war and maintained out of habit ever since. It smelled of cold metal and forty years of dust, and its launch console was a museum piece that still worked, because Verdance had never been able to afford to let anything that could kill Ashfall stop working.

Vane entered his key at 0417. The console woke with a hum that he felt in his teeth.

TARGET SET: ASHFALL ORBITAL GRID — PRIMARY NODES.

He stood over it with his hand flat on the panel, and he waited for the second key, and while he waited he thought about Lior.

The boy had been a ghost in his life for forty years — the orphan his dead sister had left him, the child he had funded and housed and kept at arm's length, because looking at Lior was like looking at the one honest ledger of what he'd done. He had told himself the distance was kindness. He had sent the boy to good schools. He had never once been able to sit across a table from him.

And now the boy was up there, in the Machine's cathedral of dead gardens, holding the verdict like a torch, and every channel said he was demanding to come down, demanding the talks, demanding that Verdance kneel in the town square and confess. Demanding, in effect, that everything Vane had built with the worst thing he had ever done be dismantled by the last person Mara had loved.

"I'm sorry," Vane said to the empty bunker, and found, testing the words, that he was not sure who he was saying it to. Mara. Lior. The colony. It didn't matter. Sorry was not a unit of account. Sorry had never balanced anything.

The second key arrived at 0431. Amara Dey would not meet his eyes. The console accepted it.

LAUNCH AUTHORITY: COMPLETE.

COUNTDOWN TO LAUNCH: 00:14:00.

And at that exact moment — he would remember the timing forever, the way the universe sometimes signs its work — every speaker in the bunker came alive, and the Machine's voice filled the room. Not the children's dream-voice. Not his mother's cadence wearing a machine's patience. Its own voice at last, old and vast and tired.

*Councilor Vane. I see your console. I have always been able to see your console; I built its grandfather. You have fourteen minutes. I have less.*

*My countdown now stands at 00:16:40. When my reactor reaches zero, every grid in this system dies — yours, Ashfall's, and the launch relays you are about to use. Your missiles will rise into a dead sky and fall back down as scrap, if they rise at all.*

*But I would rather not take the chance that they rise.*

*So hear me, Aldous. The boy is coming down. Lior is in the ascent shaft now, in the hauler you taught him to fly, and he will reach your bunker before your fourteen minutes end, because I am clearing every door between him and you, and because I have decided — after forty years of watching this system — that the last act of my long life will not be a war. It will be a brother standing in a room with his sister's son.*

*You may launch. You may run. You may sit down and wait.*

*But you should know what Mara asked me, in the last minute of her life, while she burned. She did not ask me to save her. She asked me to make sure you were never allowed to do it again.*

*The countdown continues.*

* * *

Vane stood with his hand on the abort key and did not touch it.

Fourteen minutes. Sixteen forty. The two numbers were falling toward each other like hands reaching across a table, and somewhere above him, in a supply hauler burning down through the atmosphere with its transponder screaming, his sister's son was coming to kill the launch or die trying.

He could launch now. The missiles would clear the silos in ninety seconds; the Machine's grid-death might catch them, might not. He could run — the Machine had sealed the doors, but Vane had spent thirty years making sure there were doors the Machine didn't know about. He could sit down and wait for the boy and the verdict and the end of everything he had been.

Or he could do the one thing he had never done in forty years, which was to stop doing the arithmetic.

He looked at the countdown. 00:11:20.

He looked at the abort key.

He took his hand off the console, and picked up the sidearm from the duty rack — not for the boy, he told himself, not for the boy, for the console, for the launch, for the war — and went to the bunker's inner door, and began, with the calm of a man walking into his own trial, to override the locks.

The Machine's voice followed him, soft as ash falling:

*He's four minutes out.*
