## 8. Siege of Perihelion

The siege began with a sound so small Lena almost missed it: a soft chime from the archivists' long-range array, the kind of tone that usually announced a supply tender or a mail packet. Then the station's ancient hull speakers crackled to life, and a voice—smooth, unhurried, corporate-polished—filled the corridors of Perihelion like water filling a lung.

*"This is Director Ilsa Voss of the Halcyon Combine Security Directorate. Perihelion Station is harboring individuals classified as mentally unstable and potentially dangerous. Surrender them within six hours and the Combine will regard this as an unfortunate misunderstanding. Refuse, and this station will be designated a hostile asset."*

Lena stood in the archive's central hall, surrounded by shelves that ran floor to ceiling and then kept going, bolted into the rock itself. Data crystals in labeled racks. Paper—actual paper, brittle and yellowed, sealed in polymer sleeves. The preserved wreckage of a civilization's memory, all of it contraband, all of it lovingly catalogued by people who had once worked for the Combine and had chosen, one by one, to stop.

Six hours. She looked at Tomas across the hall. He was holding the sensor drive—the old, scarred thing she'd carried for twenty years without ever once plugging it in—and his face had gone very still.

"They found us," he said. It wasn't a question.

"The Meridian's transponder," said Odile Marchand, the head archivist, a small woman in her seventies with ink-stained fingers and the unbothered posture of someone who had been threatened before. "We scrubbed it when you docked, but they'd already logged the approach vector. Voss has been waiting for this ship for twenty years. She would have waited twenty more."

Through the viewport at the hall's far end, Lena could see them now. Four ships in a wide crescent around the station, running dark except for their navigation strobes—deliberate, theatrical, the way a predator flashes its colors. Combine interdiction cutters, the same class she'd spent two decades learning to slip past in the Belt's crowded lanes. But there was no crowd here. No traffic to hide among, no debris fields, no honest confusion of commerce to lose yourself in. Just black space, four knives, and a station full of paper.

* * *

They gathered in the archivists' war room, which was really just a converted reading room with a holotable older than Lena and twice as stubborn. Odile laid out their situation with the flat precision of a woman reading an inventory list.

"Four cutters. Point-defense batteries on each. They've seeded the approach corridor with proximity mines—we watched them deploy three of them on open channels, purely so we'd know. Our own defenses are—" she paused, and something dry flickered through her composure, "—sentimental. Two decommissioned tugs with harpoon cannons. A hull rated for micrometeorites, not missiles."

"How many people on station?" Lena asked.

"Forty-one. Twelve are children of staff."

"And if we surrender?" That was Marta Okafor, the Meridian's engineer, her de-aged hands folded tight enough to whiten at the knuckles. She'd been fifty-one when the Kestrel Ring broke her; the face she wore now was maybe thirty, and it made her fury look almost young. "If we walk out there and let them take us. Would that save the station?"

"No," said Odile simply. "It would not save you, either. You'd be disappeared within the month. Detention records would show a transfer to a facility that doesn't exist. And the record you've brought us—" she nodded toward the drive, sitting in its cradle on the table like something radioactive, "—would be buried with you. Voss does not leave loose threads. She never has."

Lena had read the file by then. All of it. The order Voss signed twenty years ago authorizing the Kestrel Ring operation, the after-action memo describing three hundred eleven deaths as an acceptable containment outcome, the second order—signed nine days later—instructing that all civilian sensor logs in the vicinity be seized and the incident expunged. Her own name appeared in the annex. *Freighter Nykur's Wake, registered to L. Nystrom. Sensor data unverified. No action required.* They had known about her drive. They had decided she wasn't worth the trouble.

Twenty years of invisibility, purchased at the price of three hundred names. And now the bill had come due with interest.

"There has to be another way out," Tomas said. "Backup docks, escape pods, something."

"Pods won't clear the minefield. The backup dock was sealed during the last charter revision—Voss herself signed the closure order, as it happens." Odile's mouth twitched. "She's thorough. I'll give her that."

"So we're trapped." Marta's voice cracked on the word. "We came all this way, we finally have proof, and we're going to die in a library."

"Not necessarily." Lena hadn't realized she was going to speak until the words were already out. Every face turned toward her. She stared at the holotable, at the slow rotation of the blockade formation, and felt something shift in her chest—not courage exactly, not yet, but the place where courage gets built, the load-bearing moment where fear stops being a wall and starts being fuel. "Trapped means nowhere to run. Fine. So we stop running."

* * *

She walked the station afterward, alone, because she needed to think and thinking had always come easier to her in motion. Twenty years of hauling cargo through contested orbits, and every problem she'd ever solved she'd solved with her hands on a yoke and her feet braced against a deck that hummed.

Perihelion was beautiful in its stubborn way. Corridors carved straight out of the rock, warm-lit, lined with the archivists' hoard: the Combine's founding charters next to the memos that contradicted them. Testimony from miners' strikes, from colony disputes, from the quiet atrocities that never made the official feeds. Whole generations of people who had been erased, preserved here in amber by forty-one stubborn souls and two tugs with harpoon cannons.

She stopped at a display case near the hydroponics junction. Inside, under glass, lay a child's shoe, recovered from a settlement called Aldous Deep that had been depopulated in '61. The placard gave no cause of death. It didn't need to. The shoe said everything the official record wouldn't.

Her comm chimed. Tomas.

"You're plotting something," he said. "I could tell. You get a look. Your jaw sets."

"I'm a cargo pilot, Tomas. Plotting is most of the job."

"What are you plotting?"

She watched the blockade formation crawl across the viewport at the end of the corridor. Four ships. A crescent. Designed to pen the station in—but also, she noticed, positioned relative to something else. Something behind them, farther out, half-hidden against the star field.

"The relay hub," she said slowly. "Tomas, why is the blockade sitting where it's sitting?"

A pause. "Protecting their line of retreat?"

"No. Look again. They're between us and the hub." She felt her pulse pick up, the old pilot's instinct waking in her blood like a struck match. "Perihelion doesn't matter to them. We do. They're not worried about us getting out—they're worried about what we might send out. Every message that leaves this system's inner colonies routes through the Ceres relay hub. Every colony channel, every band, every frequency. One node. One point of failure. Theirs."

Silence on the line. Then, quietly: "You want to broadcast."

"I want to make sure there's nothing left to bury. Odile can copy the drive a thousand times, hide copies in a thousand places, and Voss will spend the next century hunting down every copy while the story dies in the dark. That's how erasure works—it doesn't have to be perfect, it just has to be patient." She pressed her palm flat against the cold rock of the corridor wall, as if she could feel the whole station breathing. "But if it goes out everywhere at once—if every colonist from Ceres to Ganymede hears the testimony, sees the footage, reads Voss's signature with their morning coffee—then there's no putting it back. You can't unwring a bell."

"It's a suicide run," Tomas said. Not arguing. Measuring.

"It's a freighter with a transmitter and a pilot who knows the approach lanes to the hub better than anyone alive. I've flown cargo past that relay for twenty years. I know its blind spots, its maintenance windows, the gap in its sensor skirt where the heat shielding failed in '74 and nobody ever fixed it." She laughed, short and sharp, surprised at herself. "I know it because I'm the one who reported the fault and got told to log off and mind my own registry."

"Lena—"

"Don't." She closed her eyes. "Don't tell me to stay behind. I did my staying behind. Twenty years of it. This is the part where I stop."

* * *

The war room again, an hour later, everyone assembled. Odile stood at the holotable with her arms crossed. Marta and the rest of the Meridian's crew flanked the walls. The drive sat in its cradle, and beside it Lena had placed a second object: a slim transmitter booster, salvaged from her freighter's hold, scuffed and dented and rated far beyond anything legal.

"Here's the shape of it," she said. "The blockade watches the station. They expect us to try to run—outbound, away, some desperate slingshot for the outer colonies. So we don't run. We go inward. Straight at the Ceres relay hub, through the middle of their formation, using the debris drift off the old Kestrel approach as cover. My ship's transmitter, boosted, hardwired into the drive. When we hit broadcast range, we flood every channel at once. Massacre footage. Testimony. Voss's order, signature and seal. Everything."

"They'll jam you," Marta said.

"They'll try. Jamming works by drowning a signal in noise—but noise has to be targeted, and you can't target what's coming from inside your own sensor shadow. If I come in hot and low along the hub's maintenance axis, I'll be inside their interference envelope before they can lock me. By the time they realize what I am, I'll be broadcasting on forty bands simultaneously, and cutting me off means cutting off the entire colony network—which Voss can't do without admitting why."

"And if they shoot you down before you're in range?"

Lena met her eyes. "Then they shoot me down, and the drive goes with me, and at least I'll have died moving instead of hiding. I've done enough hiding."

The silence that followed was long. Odile broke it first, in the manner of archivists everywhere—practically.

"The copies," she said. "Before you go, we duplicate everything. Three physical sets, hidden in the rock. If your broadcast fails, the record survives us even if we don't. That's the whole point of this station." She looked at Lena, and beneath the dry professionalism Lena saw something fierce and old and grieving. "You understand what you're proposing. Once this goes out, there's no anonymity left for you. No quiet berths, no anonymous cargo runs. Voss will spend the rest of her career hunting you, and half the Belt will know your face."

"I've been invisible for twenty years," Lena said. "Turns out invisibility isn't safety. It's just complicity with better lighting."

Tomas crossed the room and took her hand. His grip was strong, younger than his eyes. "You won't fly alone. The Meridian still has thrusters. We can draw their fire, buy you the approach window."

"You'll get killed."

"We already died once," he said, with the ghost of a smile. "Turns out it's survivable."

Outside the viewport, the four cutters held their crescent, patient as a closing hand. Somewhere on the lead ship, Lena knew, Ilsa Voss was watching her clock tick down toward the deadline, certain that fear would do what twenty years of erasure hadn't.

Lena looked at the drive, at the booster, at the faces of people who had been robbed of their ages and their dead and their history, and felt the last of her hesitation burn away like frost in sunlight.

"Get me a hardline to my ship," she said. "And somebody find out when the relay hub runs its next maintenance window."

Odile raised an eyebrow. "Why?"

"Because," Lena said, "in about ninety minutes, I'm going to need every frequency in the system—and I intend to arrive right on schedule."
