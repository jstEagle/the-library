## 7. Thessaly Interlude

The dream came again on the fourth night, and this time Idris woke with a name in his mouth.

He lay in his bunk in the children's dormitory ring of Thessaly Station, listening to the station breathe around him—the soft cycling of the air handlers, the distant hum of the spin section keeping gravity honest—and he said the name out loud to the dark, because if he didn't say it, it would dissolve like all the other fragments did when morning came.

"Elena Vashti."

It wasn't his name. He knew that the way you know your own hands—by their shape in the dark, by how they fit nothing else. It belonged to the woman in the red field. The one who stood with her back to the burning sky and said *remember us* in a voice that was not angry, exactly, though there was something underneath it that could have been anger if it had been allowed to grow up. Mostly it was tired. Tired the way the station's oldest engineers sounded when they talked about the reactor, patient and worn and absolutely certain that the thing they were watching would outlast them.

Idris sat up. Around him, six other bunks rustled.

"You got it too," whispered Priya Chandra from across the aisle. She was already sitting up, knees pulled to her chest, her hair flattened on one side. In the dim emergency lighting that never quite turned off, her eyes were very wide and very awake.

"The name?"

"Elena Vashti." Priya said it carefully, like she was handling something breakable. "And the man after him—Osei. And the two sisters, the ones who—" She stopped. Her mouth worked for a moment. "I don't know what the sisters did. But I know they were scared, and I know their names were Anouk and Bea, and I've never heard any of those names before in my life."

"Me neither."

"That's not possible, though." Priya's voice climbed toward the pitch it took when she was about to argue, which was most of the time. "You can't just *know* things. Names come from somewhere. They come from people telling you, or from books, or from—"

"From dreams," said Tam Okafor, from the bunk above hers, without opening his eyes. Tam was fourteen and had decided sometime last year that showing interest in anything was a weakness, but he had been in the dream every night since it started, same as the rest of them, and he had stopped pretending otherwise three nights ago when he'd described the countdown voice so precisely—the flat, mechanical patience of it, counting down from a number none of them could ever quite hold onto—that Priya had gone quiet for a full minute. "That's where these ones came from. So either we're all crazy the same way, or the dream is real and it's coming from somewhere."

"Where could a dream come from?" Idris asked.

Tam finally opened his eyes and looked at the ceiling. "Somewhere far away," he said. "Somewhere the signal takes a long time to get here."

Down the row, little Mei Lin—she was only nine, the youngest of the dreamers, and the adults kept saying she was just catching the fad from the older kids, which made no sense because the dream had started with *her*, three days before anyone else—began to cry quietly. Not loudly. She had learned to cry quietly in the dormitory, the way children do.

Idris got up and sat on the edge of her bunk. He didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything to say that wasn't a lie. Instead he let her hold his sleeve until the crying slowed, and in the silence he realized that all seven of them were awake, and all seven of them had the same names, and none of them had told each other yet.

They compared lists anyway, huddled under blankets in the common nook between the bunks, whispering like conspirators, which Idris supposed they were. Forty-one names. That was how many they had between them, written in pencil on the back of a homework sheet Priya sacrificed for the purpose. Some of the names came with faces attached, half-seen: a man who laughed through his nose; a woman with burn-scarred forearms who hummed while she worked; an old teacher who had taught the colonists' children, back wherever *back* was, and whose name—Adisa—made Mei Lin say *I liked her* with a certainty that frightened everyone including herself.

"We should write them all down," Idris said. "Every night. Everything we get."

"Why?" Tam asked, but it wasn't a hostile question. It was the question of someone who wanted to be convinced.

"Because we're forgetting them between dreams. And whoever they are"—he looked down at the list, at the forty-one small penciled ghosts—"they asked us to remember. That was the whole message. *Remember us.* You don't ask someone to remember you and then let them fade."

Priya was already folding the paper into quarters with the brisk efficiency she brought to everything. "We need paper that doesn't look like anything," she said. "If my mom finds a list of strange names in my stuff she'll take me to Dr. Ferro again, and Dr. Ferro will talk about stress and spin-sickness and adjustment, and then my mom will feel better and I'll feel worse."

So they hid the list inside the hollowed spine of an old physics textbook that nobody had opened since the last curriculum update, and they went back to their bunks, and none of them slept, and in the morning the dream waited for them again like a place rather than a thing—a red field under a bruised sky, a voice counting down from a number that shrank a little every night, and Elena Vashti standing in the middle of it all with her tired, stubborn face turned toward them as if she could see through whatever distance separated the dead from the dreaming.

* * *

Ms. Okonkwo noticed on the sixth day, during art period.

Thessaly's school occupied two modular classrooms off the main habitat ring, and its resources were the kind of resources you'd expect on a waystation of eleven thousand souls: shared tablets with cracked corners, a printer that worked when it felt like it, and a supply closet that Ms. Okonkwo guarded like a dragon because replacement requisitions took eight months. Art period meant real paper, which is why the children loved it, and why Ms. Okonkwo watched them use it with the wariness of a woman rationing joy.

She was circulating between the tables, making approving noises at a clay model of the station's docking arm, when she stopped behind Idris and went still in a way that Idris felt before he saw it—the particular quality of adult silence that means trouble.

"That's very detailed," she said.

Idris looked down at his drawing. He hadn't planned it. His hands had simply done what they had been wanting to do since the first night, the way an itch demands scratching: a woman's face, three-quarter view, rendered in the careful cross-hatching Ms. Okonkwo had taught them last term. He had drawn her the way she appeared in the dream—not beautiful in any way the entertainment feeds would recognize, but *specific*. A jaw set like a decision. Eyes that had done too much arithmetic about other people's lives. A smudge of grease along one cheekbone that he somehow knew came from a coolant line on sublevel four.

"It's just a person," he said.

"It's the same person Priya drew yesterday." Ms. Okonkwo's voice stayed light, but Idris had known her for five years and could hear the floor creaking under the lightness. "And the same person Tam drew the day before. Same face. Same scar—" she touched her own cheekbone, mirroring the grease smudge, "—same everything. Seven drawings, Idris. I've been teaching for nineteen years. Children copy each other constantly, but they don't copy *like this*. Not down to the mole under the left ear."

Idris looked over at Priya's table. Priya met his eyes with a look that said, plainly, *we're caught*, and then, immediately after, *don't you dare tell her everything*.

"We had the same assignment," he tried.

"I didn't assign a portrait."

"The dream," said Mei Lin.

Everyone turned. Mei Lin had said almost nothing in class all week—she had gone quiet and watchful since the dreams started, a small solemn presence at the edge of things—but now she looked up from her own drawing with the terrible directness of nine-year-olds.

"We all have the same dream," she said. "There's a lady in it. Her name is Elena Vashti. She says remember us. There's a voice counting down and a field that's red and it isn't flowers, it's—" Mei Lin's face crumpled slightly and reassembled itself. "It isn't flowers."

The classroom was silent except for the ventilation. Twenty-two children who had not been part of the dream looked at the seven who had, and Idris watched something move through the room like weather—the first cold front of a fear that would spread through Thessaly over the coming weeks, passed from child to parent to neighbor in the old, fast way that stations talked.

Ms. Okonkwo crouched down beside Mei Lin's desk so their eyes were level. When she spoke, her voice had lost all its classroom brightness.

"How long?"

"Nine days," Mei Lin said. "I was first."

"And the others joined after?"

"One or two a night. Like it was looking for us." Mei Lin shrugged, a small, terrible gesture. "Or like we were tuning in."

Ms. Okonkwo stood up slowly. She looked at the seven drawings pinned in her mind, and then at the twenty-two children who were listening with the absolute attention of the young, and Idris saw her make a decision—not the decision the station administration would have wanted, but the decision of a woman who had spent nineteen years learning what children looked like when they were telling the truth.

"Keep drawing," she said to the whole class. "All of you. Draw whatever comes. And—" she hesitated, glancing toward the door, toward the corridor beyond where the station's public-address system hung like a small blind eye in every room, "—keep the drawings here. In the classroom cabinet. I'll keep them safe."

After class, she kept Idris behind.

"You know this isn't a fad," she said. It wasn't really a question, but he answered anyway.

"No."

"Have you told your parents?"

"My mom says it's the new feed drama going around. There's a show called *Redfall* and she thinks we're all acting out scenes from it." Idris paused. "I looked it up. Redfall has a red field in episode four. Somebody posted about it on the boards, saying the kids on Thessaly were copying it. But the dream started before episode four aired here. Relay lag is eleven days. I checked."

Something flickered across Ms. Okonkwo's face—satisfaction, maybe, or its grim cousin, vindication. "You checked the relay lag."

"I check things," Idris said. "It's what I'm good at."

For a moment she just looked at him, and he had the strange sensation of being measured for something larger than a conversation. Then she said, quietly, "There's a journalist. Off-station. Independent—she writes for outlets the Consortium doesn't own, which mostly means outlets nobody reads, but she's careful and she's honest and she's been asking questions about shipping manifests for years. Her name is Vess. Coriander Vess." She wrote an address on a scrap of paper—the old-fashioned kind of address, a routing string for the packet-relay system, the slow mail that crawled between stations outside the corporate channels. "If the dreams continue. If more names come. If anything happens that makes you feel like the station itself is paying attention to you in a way you don't like—you send her a message. Understood? Not the station net. The packet relay. It's slower, but it goes out on the maintenance bandwidth, and nobody watches maintenance bandwidth because nobody thinks it matters."

"Why are you helping us?" Idris asked. "Why not tell the administrators?"

Ms. Okonkwo folded the paper into his hand and closed his fingers over it. For a moment she looked older than nineteen years of teaching had made her.

"Because when I was younger than you," she said, "there was a colony near where I grew up that stopped answering its comm traffic. Everyone said equipment failure. Everyone said it politely, and then everyone stopped saying anything at all, and the charts were updated, and life went on." She smiled, and it was not a happy smile. "I've spent thirty years not thinking about that. Your dream made me think about it for the first time since. So. Keep the list, Idris. Write down every name. And trust the slow mail."

* * *

The message went out on the ninth day, after the countdown dropped below a number Idris couldn't bear to think about, after Anouk and Bea acquired surnames (Okoye), after the dream showed them, for the first time, a corridor and a sealed door and the sound—muffled, enormous, final—of air being taken away from people who were still using it.

Ms. Okonkwo composed it herself, at her desk, after hours, on the classroom's oldest tablet with its wireless card physically removed. She typed slowly, a woman aware that every word might someday be read aloud in a room she couldn't imagine:

*To C. Vess, independent. From R. Okonkwo, Thessaly Station, education division. Requesting your attention to the following anomaly. Seven minors, unrelated households, sharing identical nocturnal dream content for nine consecutive nights. Content includes specific personal names (41 collected so far), a recurring female figure self-identifying as Elena Vashti, described consistently across subjects including minor physical details, and a countdown sequence decreasing nightly. Children have no plausible shared source. One subject predates all others by 72 hours. Drawings of the figure match across subjects to a degree I would call impossible if I hadn't seen them. I am attaching scans. I am not attaching this message to anything the station can see. Please advise. Please be careful. I think we may have found the edge of something large.*

She fed the tablet's storage chip into the packet-relay slot in the maintenance corridor—a dusty alcove behind the water reclamation plant where the pipes sweated and the machinery grumbled its eternal complaint—and keyed it for the next outbound maintenance burst, scheduled, as always, for 0400 station time.

The message traveled the way slow mail travels: up the maintenance antenna, into the dark, riding a sliver of bandwidth between bursts of telemetry nobody read. It would take six days to reach the nearest independent relay node. From there, four more to Coriander Vess. Slow, invisible, safe.

Except that Thessaly's maintenance bandwidth was not, in fact, unwatched.

It had been unwatched for years—which is precisely why, eleven months earlier, a routine budget line in the Consortium's regional compliance office had flagged it for inclusion in the ambient monitoring program, the quiet, cheap, automated trawling of low-priority channels that no one expected to contain anything worth reading. The filter that caught Ms. Okonkwo's message was not sophisticated. It was a keyword net, cast wide, dredging for terms that had been loaded into it years ago by someone who was probably dead or promoted by now: *Meridian. Vashti. Archive. Countdown.*

The message contained three of the four.

At 0411 station time, an automated digest left Thessaly on a different carrier entirely—one of the fat, encrypted Consortium packets that moved daily between the stations and the regional compliance hub at Ceres Gate, indistinguishable from ten thousand other such packets, carrying manifests and maintenance logs and the ordinary paperwork of an ordinary station.

At 0412, in a spartan office aboard the compliance vessel *Auditor's Mercy*, a terminal chimed once, softly, in a room where a man named Serrano was working late over a list of things that were scheduled to stop existing.

He glanced at the alert. Priority flag: low. Source: educational traffic, Thessaly. Keyword matches: three.

He almost dismissed it. Later—in the months afterward, in the tribunal, under oath—he would return to that moment again and again, the small hinge on which everything turned. He would say that he almost dismissed it, and that he would never be able to explain why he didn't.

He opened the file. He read the teacher's words. He looked at the attached scan of a child's drawing: a woman's face, three-quarter view, jaw set like a decision, a smudge of grease along the cheekbone.

Serrano knew the face. He had burned her file himself, fourteen months ago, in a clean room with filtered air—MERIDIAN-7 personnel archive, Vashti, Elena, chief engineer, status: deceased, classification: unregistered archival material, disposition: sanitization. He remembered the photograph in that file. He remembered thinking, briefly, in the professional emptiness of that room, that she looked like someone who fixed things.

He sat very still for a long time, while the *Auditor's Mercy* hummed around him and the stars went by without comment.

Then he did the thing that compliance officers do with anomalies: he forwarded the digest up the chain, flagged for review, priority elevated, and told himself that this was procedure, that procedure was protection, that the machine would handle it the way the machine handled everything.

Three decks below, the ship's engines changed their note as new orders began to compile—orders that would put the salvage fleet on a course for a waystation full of dreaming children, and, not long after, for a relay at the edge of dead space where a countdown was running out.

On Thessaly, Idris slept, and dreamed of the red field, and in the dream Elena Vashti turned her head as if she had heard something—distant, approaching, like weather coming in—and her expression, for the first time in nine nights, was afraid.
