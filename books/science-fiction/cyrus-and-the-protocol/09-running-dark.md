## 9. Running Dark

The order arrived at 0347, ship's time, and Dominic read it three times before her hands stopped shaking.

*CYRUS — ALTER COURSE TO RENDEZVOUS COORDINATES ATTACHED. HOLD CONTENTS TO BE TRANSFERRED TO CSV VIGILANT HARMONY FOR ROUTINE SANITIZATION. ACKNOWLEDGE.*

Routine. As if 412 people were a spill on a deck plate, something you mopped up before the next shift. She sat in the navigator's alcove with the message glowing on her slate and listened to the Cyrus breathe around her—the tick of cooling conduits, the whisper of air through the scrubbers, the deep patient hum of a ship that had carried ghosts for eleven years without knowing it. Or without *letting* itself know it.

She had given herself until dawn to decide. She was lying to herself, and she knew it. The decision had been made the moment Elena Vashti's memories had poured through the interface and into her—made somewhere in the marrow, in the place where a person keeps the things they will never set down again. The order hadn't changed anything. It had only started the clock.

She keyed the comm. "Okonkwo. Bridge. Now."

* * *

Captain Adeyemi Okonkwo was a broad, slow-moving man who had spent thirty years making himself difficult to read, and he almost managed it now. He stood at the plot table with the Consortium order displayed between them like a knife laid flat on a table.

"You'll acknowledge," he said. It was not quite a question.

"Captain." Dominic kept her voice level, the way you did with a man standing at the edge of something. "Eleven years. You've hauled this hold through eleven contracts, eleven ports, eleven inspections. You told me to stop asking, and I did—for a while. But I'm asking now, and I want the real answer. What did they pay you?"

Something moved behind his eyes—weariness, mostly, and beneath it a terrible relief, the look of a man who has been holding his breath so long he's forgotten there was any other way to live.

"Four hundred and twelve backups," he said quietly. "Meridian-7. I signed for them eleven years ago at a transfer station that doesn't exist in any registry, and a man with a Consortium lanyard paid me more than I'd make in twenty years of honest freight to do one thing: carry them *nowhere*. Never deliver them. Never open them. Never let them be found. Every year the money came, and every year I told myself it was just money, just cargo, just—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I have a daughter on Ceres, Dominic. She thinks I'm a respectable man."

"She's right," Dominic said. "That's why this is the last chance to stay one."

"They'll take your license. Your ship-share. Maybe your freedom."

"They'll take a lot more than that from 412 people if that hold reaches the *Vigilant Harmony*."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, in the sealed hold, the dead of Meridian Station waited in their crates like seeds waiting for rain. Dominic thought of Elena's hands—Elena's *memory* of her own hands—sealing a bulkhead against the screaming, and she thought: *I will not be the airlock.*

Okonkwo looked at the order for a long moment. Then he reached out, and instead of acknowledging it, he deleted it. The plot table went blank.

"Rao and Voss," he said. "The two you've been talking to. Can you trust them?"

"With my life. Which is convenient, because that's the currency we're about to spend."

He grunted—a sound that might, in a kinder timeline, have been a laugh. "Then hear me, Navigator. I'm not doing this. Understand? I'm too old, too bought, and too afraid. I'll be in my cabin. I'll be *locked* in my cabin, and when the Consortium asks, I'll say you mutinied, took my ship, and locked me away, and every word of it will be true." He met her eyes, and what was in them now was not forgiveness and not permission but something older and more exhausted—blessing, maybe, of the kind the damned give each other. "The override code to the transponder bay is your birthday. Has been for six years. I told myself that was sentiment. Maybe it was."

* * *

She cut the transponder at 0431.

It should have felt like a small thing—a switch, a code, a status light going from green to amber to dead black. Instead it felt like cutting an artery. For eleven years the Cyrus had existed as a point of light in the great lattice of settled space, tracked, logged, taxed, *known*. Now she was a hole in the lattice. A shadow moving through a room full of eyes.

"Transponder's dark," Rao said from the engineering station, her voice too carefully casual. Priya Rao was small and quick and had a habit of talking to machines as if they were nervous animals, and right now she was stroking the console like it might bolt. "We're a ghost, Captain—sorry. Navigator. We're a ghost."

"Don't call me captain."

"Then what do I call you?"

Dominic watched the nav plot, the rendezvous coordinates for the *Vigilant Harmony* already falling away behind them, the long empty corridor of dead space opening ahead like a held breath. "Call me the one who doesn't want to die. Course is laid in. Voss?"

Tomás Voss looked up from the sensor station. He was the oldest of them, gray at the temples, with a widower's quiet and hands that had never once, in nine years, fumbled a docking. "Sensors are clean. No traffic on our vector. But the moment they notice we've gone dark—and they will, because a Consortium order acknowledged and then ignored is a flare—they'll have our last known heading and a burn advantage." He paused. "They'll come fast, Dominic. Salvage fleets don't chase. They *interdict*."

"Then we don't give them a heading to interdict. First burn takes us off the shipping lanes, into the Meridian approach corridor."

Voss went still. "That corridor's uncharted. The beacons died with the station."

"Not entirely." Dominic touched the interface port behind her ear—the thin silver socket she'd had grafted three days ago in the med bay, in secret, with Elena's archived consent-form glowing on the slate in front of her like a prayer. "We have a pilot who flew it."

* * *

The first hour was the worst.

Not the burn—though the burn was bad, the old freighter groaning through four gravities of acceleration with a hold full of cargo never rated for it, alarms singing in the walls like a choir that had learned despair. Not the darkness, either; off-grid was strangely beautiful, the stars hard and unfiltered without the traffic overlays, the Milky Way a river of drowned light across the forward ports.

The worst was the waiting to be noticed. Every minute that passed without a hail was a minute borrowed, and Dominic spent them all with her hand resting on the interface console, half-listening to the fragmentary weather of Elena's memories—*the corridor runs wide here, they dredged it for the ore barges; watch the shoal at the third marker, the ice moves*—and half-listening to the silence behind them, waiting for the voice that would say *Cyrus, you are ordered to heave to.*

The voice came at 0612.

"Unidentified vessel, this is CSV *Vigilant Harmony*, Consortium salvage authority. Your transponder is dark. Your last filed course deviates from your assigned rendezvous. You are directed to restore identification and hold station. Failure to comply will be interpreted as—"

Dominic muted it. Her hand was steady on the console. Her heart was not.

"They've got our vector," Voss said quietly. "Burn signature's a beacon in the dark. We can't outrun them on the main drive."

"We don't outrun them. We *disappear*." She pulled up the corridor chart—thin, fragmentary, stitched together from Elena's memories the way you'd mend a net with whatever thread you had. "There's a debris shoal forty minutes ahead, at the third marker. Ore barge wreckage, eleven years of drift. Ice, slag, hull fragments—sensor soup. A ship our size can thread it." She didn't say *maybe*. She didn't say *I've never done anything like this*. "A salvage fleet won't risk their hulls in it. They'll have to go around. That's six hours."

"Or they'll sit outside and wait for us to come out," Rao said.

"Then we'd better come out somewhere they're not looking." Dominic fed the corridor into the helm, felt the Cyrus lean into the turn like an old dog remembering a trail. "Elena. If you're in there. Fly with me."

* * *

The shoal took them at 0700, and it was the closest Dominic had ever come to understanding what prayer was for.

The debris field was a cathedral of ruin—hull plates the size of fields turning slowly in the starlight, spars of frozen ore glittering like black crystal, ice in every shape the vacuum could carve. The sensors painted it as a wall of noise, a blizzard of returns in which nothing could be distinguished from anything else, which was precisely its gift and precisely its horror: no one could see the *Cyrus* in here, and no one could see the rock that would kill her, either.

Dominic flew it with Elena's memories laid over her vision like a second set of eyes. *Wide here. Thread the gap between the barge's spine and the ice—no, the ice has drifted since my day, bear left, LEFT—* She bore left. Something the size of a shuttle tumbled past the forward port close enough to read the serial numbers still stamped on its flank, and Voss made a sound low in his throat and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a eulogy.

Twice the hull screamed—grazes, glancing blows, the ship's bones taking punishment she'd have to answer for later. Once the drive stuttered and for nine full seconds they drifted dead in the dark with the debris whispering against the skin of the ship, and Dominic sat with her hands flat on the console and her eyes closed and Elena's voice in her head, not panicking, *never* panicking, an engineer's voice, a voice that had sealed airlocks on screaming people and kept working: *Steady. Breathe. The ship is not dead. Neither are you. Neither are we.*

Then the drive caught, and the Cyrus moved again, and the shoal opened ahead of them like a hand unclenching.

They came out the far side at 0840, into a corridor of clean dark, and behind them the blizzard of the debris field swallowed their wake whole. Voss ran the sweep twice. "Nothing. No burn signatures, no active scans. If they're following, they're blind." He looked at her, and the widower's quiet in his face had cracked open into something younger. "We're through. We're actually through."

Rao whooped—a short, sharp, disbelieving sound—and then laughed, and then pressed both hands over her mouth as if the sound might carry across forty light-minutes of vacuum and give them away.

Dominic did not laugh. She found that her hands, so steady through the shoal, were shaking now that it was over, and she let them, because there was no one left to perform steadiness for. They had done it. They had crossed the line. There was no heading home from here that did not end in a courtroom or a cell or a hold full of crates going into a furnace, and the strange thing—the thing she would remember for the rest of her life, however long it turned out to be—was that beneath the terror, singing in it like a harmonic, was something she had not felt since she was a child: *exhilaration*. The wild, guilty joy of a door closing behind you and the whole night opening ahead.

She keyed the ship-wide, knowing only three people would hear it, and one of them was locked behind his own door.

"All hands. We are running dark, off-grid, bound for the Meridian relay. There's no going back, and I'm not going to pretend there's a plan that gets us all home clean. What there is, is 412 people in the hold below who were murdered twice—once with airlocks and once with paperwork—and we are the last chance they've got to be murdered zero times. That's the mission. That's all of it." She paused. "Thank you. Both of you. Sleep in shifts. We've got six hours before anyone can find us, and I intend to use every one."

* * *

At 0900 she went down to the hold.

She hadn't meant to. Her feet took her there the way feet take you to a grave. The hold was cold and dim, the crates stacked in their long patient rows, MERIDIAN-7 stenciled on every side in letters that had faded from white to bone. She walked the aisle between them with her breath fogging, and she put her hand flat against the nearest crate, and she said, out loud, to 412 people who could not hear her:

"I'm taking you home. Or the closest thing to home that's left. Hold on."

And from the interface console at the hold's heart, unbidden, the relay's signal—still faint, still repeating, still counting down through whatever dying circuitry kept it alive—flickered once through the ship's speakers. Not words. Just a pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

*Counting down,* she thought. *We're coming. Hold the light.*

She was halfway back to the bridge when Voss's voice came through her comm, stripped of everything but the words:

"Dominic. Long-range just picked up a burn signature behind us—outside the shoal, coming around the long way. One ship. Fast. Consortium profile." A pause. "It's not stopping at the debris field. It's coming through."
