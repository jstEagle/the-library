## 18. The Dream Fulfilled

Idris woke without the dream.

That was the first thing she understood, lying in her bunk on Thessaly with the station's night-cycle lights dimmed to their lowest amber, that the silence inside her own skull was not emptiness but absence—the way a room feels after a guest who has lived there for months finally leaves. For one hundred and nineteen nights, the dream had been there when she closed her eyes: the red field stretching to a horizon that curved the wrong way, the voice counting down from numbers too large to hold, and the woman—Elena, though no one had told them her name until they'd drawn her face a hundred times—saying *remember us* in a voice like a hand laid gently on the back of the neck.

Tonight there was nothing. Just the ordinary dark of her cabin, the hum of Thessaly's air scrubbers, the creak of her father's footsteps in the corridor outside as he made his insomniac rounds of the hab ring.

She lay still for a long time, listening to the nothing.

Then she was out of bed and at her terminal before she could talk herself out of it, fingers moving through the children's channel they had built in secret, thread by thread, while the Consortium's tracking array still watched—or while it had watched, until the night it hadn't. The channel was called the Red Field, because everything they named came back to that field eventually. Forty-one children across four stations. All of them awake. All of them posting the same three words over and over, in different fonts, different languages, some of them misspelled by hands too small or too shaking to get it right:

*It stopped.*

*It stopped.*

*It stopped—but listen—*

And then, buried in the flood, a message from a girl named Wren on Ceres Gate, twelve years old like Idris, whose older brother had been detained eleven days ago for what the Consortium's notice had called *participation in unauthorized signal activity*: **It didn't stop. It ended. There's a difference. Turn on any open channel. Any of them.**

Idris turned on every channel she had.

* * *

The first thing she found was not news. It was noise—a wall of it, all of settled space talking at once, every frequency she could reach crowded with voices overlapping voices, anchors shouting over each other, raw audio from ships, from stations, from somewhere that sounded like wind, though there was no wind in space. She scrolled through it with her heart going hard against her ribs, catching fragments:

*—testimony from four hundred and twelve archived persons, ruled admissible this hour by the independent tribunal at—*

*—Concorde Mining directors named in warrants, three already in custody at—*

*—the freighter Cyrus, heavily damaged, currently adrift near the relay designated Meridian Memorial, crew status—*

Her breath caught on that last fragment. The Cyrus. The ship from the dream—not the dream itself, but from the messages Elena had begun sending them in the final weeks, when the countdown had accelerated and the dream had stopped being a haunting and become an instruction. *There is a ship coming,* Elena had said, in the voice that was not quite a voice but something older and stranger, memory given broadcast shape. *Children, you are not the audience. You are the relay's second tower. When we speak, carry it. When we ask, act.* And they had acted—they had blinded the array on Thessaly, forty-one children coordinating across four stations through a shared dream, passing codes in drawings and rhymes, and it had worked, and it had cost Tam his freedom for eleven days.

Tam. Wren's brother. Detained in the Consortium administrative block on Ceres Gate, in a room with no window, while his sister organized from the outside and Idris carried the network's spine from Thessaly.

She found the news feed from Ceres Gate next, and there it was, third item down beneath the tribunal headlines: *Detained minor released; Consortium spokesperson cites "procedural review of enforcement protocols."*

The footage showed a boy walking out of a gray building into gray light, blinking, thinner than the last public image Idris had seen of him, with Wren breaking past the barrier line to reach him, and the two of them holding each other in front of the cameras while reporters shouted questions neither of them answered.

Idris sat back from the terminal. Her eyes were burning. She realized she was smiling so widely it hurt her face.

* * *

They gathered in the Red Field within the hour—all forty-one of them, or as close to all as the lag between stations allowed, their messages stacking up in the shared space like people crowding into a room. It wasn't a real place. It had never been a real place. But after a hundred and nineteen nights of dreaming together, none of them needed it to be; they knew each other the way you know people you've stood beside in a fire.

**Did everyone see?** Wren sent. **He's out. They let him go.**

**We saw,** said Milo from Halcyon Deep. **Everyone saw. Everyone saw everything, my mum's been crying since breakfast and she won't say why but it's the names, isn't it. It's the list.**

It was the list. That was the thing Idris kept coming back to, the thing she couldn't stop touching with her mind the way you touch a sore tooth. Four hundred and twelve names, read aloud on every channel, over and over, because the backups had insisted—not demanded, never demanded, but asked, politely, terribly—that each name be spoken in full before any of the evidence was discussed. Elena Vashti, chief engineer. Okafor, Anselm, hydroponics. Vashti, Noor, age six. On and on, four hundred and twelve times, until the anchors' voices went hoarse and the tribunals' clerks ran out of ink and the whole of settled space had heard the population of Meridian Station pronounced syllable by syllable, resurrected by grammar if nothing else.

People who had officially never existed now existed in every mouth.

**My dad says they're going to convict them,** sent a small voice Idris didn't recognize at first—a new child, joining from Juno Waystation, one of the ones who had come late, after the dream widened. **The Consortium people. He says he's never seen anything like it. He says the whole station is watching the feeds instead of working.**

**Mine too,** said someone else. **Ours too.**

**Is it over?** asked the new child. **Is that why the dream stopped? Because it's over?**

Idris typed slowly, because she wanted to get this right, because she had somehow become the one who explained things, which still astonished her—she, who had spent twelve years being the quiet kid in the back row, the one teachers described as *watchful* in report comments that meant *worrying*. She thought about how to answer, and what came out was not what she had planned:

**I don't think it's over. I think it's finished. Like a story is finished. The bad part is done being told. Now comes the part where everyone has to live with having heard it.**

She stared at her own words on the screen, surprised by them. Then, below, Wren sent a single line:

**Elena would have liked that.**

And the channel went quiet—not empty, but full of a silence that felt communal, forty-one children across four stations all thinking of the same woman at the same moment, a woman none of them had ever met except in a dream, except as a voice counting down, except as the shape of a face they had drawn until their pencils wore flat.

* * *

Her mother found her there an hour later, still cross-legged at the terminal, the open channels murmuring in the background like a tide. Idris expected the usual questions—*why aren't you asleep, do you know what time it is*—but her mother only stood in the doorway for a moment, arms folded, looking at her daughter with an expression Idris had never quite seen on her before. Not worry. Something adjacent to awe, worn carefully, like a coat borrowed from someone bigger.

"The counselor came by today," her mother said. "From the relocation office."

Idris's stomach dropped. Relocation. The word the Consortium used before, when families got moved for reasons no one explained. "Are we—"

"No." Her mother crossed the room and sat on the edge of the bunk, her hand finding Idris's hair the way she'd done when Idris was small. "No, love. It's the other kind of visit. They're asking about Meridian. About heirs." She said the word strangely, tasting it. "Apparently—" and here her mother's voice did something wobbly, something Idris had never heard it do, "—apparently your grandmother's name is on a list. Your father's mother. Sefa Halloran-adjacent line, the notice said, I don't understand half of it, but there's a registry now, a legal one, and they're tracing descendants. Of the people who died. Of the people who were *erased*, Idris. They're un-erasing them, one family at a time, and ours is—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"Grandma Ada," Idris said quietly. "She used to sing a song about a red field. I thought she made it up."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. "You never said."

"I didn't know it mattered." She almost laughed. "I didn't know *anything* mattered, not really, not until the dream. Mum—when Grandma sang it, was there a woman in it? A woman saying remember?"

"I don't—" Her mother frowned, reaching back through decades. "There was a counting part. She always counted backward. I thought it was just a game." Her frown deepened into something like vertigo. "Idris, are you telling me your grandmother remembered Meridian? That she *knew*?"

"I think everybody knew," Idris said, "a little bit. In pieces. And the dream put the pieces together." She shrugged, feeling suddenly very young and very old at once. "That's what Elena said we were. Pieces. She said the backups were the whole record but people were the echo, and echoes can travel where records can't. Through songs. Through kids."

Her mother pulled her into a hug then, fierce and wordless, and Idris let herself be held, her face pressed into her mother's shoulder, smelling the familiar soap and engine grease of her, crying a little—not the ragged crying of fear, which she had done plenty of during the hundred and nineteen nights, but something slower, heavier, the crying that comes after, when the body finally believes it's safe enough to grieve.

Because that was the thing nobody warned you about, Idris was learning. That relief and mourning came braided together, strand over strand, impossible to pull apart. Four hundred and twelve people were dead and had been dead for years, and knowing their names did not bring them back, and winning did not undo the airlocks sealing, and Tam was free but he would never be entirely the boy who'd walked into that gray building, and she herself—Idris Okonkwo, twelve years old, watchful—would never again be a person who believed that dreams were only dreams, or that adults in charge knew what they were doing, or that the truth took care of itself.

But the names were spoken. The warrants were issued. The red field was green now, in the dream's last image, which all forty-one of them had seen on the final night before it ended: the field turning green under a sky that curved the right way, and the voice—four hundred and twelve voices, layered, harmonized, unmistakably alive-saying—

*Thank you.*

Not remember us. Thank you. As if remembering had been a gift they received rather than a debt they collected. Idris thought she would carry that inversion for the rest of her life, the way you carry a stone in your pocket that has become, without your deciding it, a talisman.

* * *

Near midnight, station time, a message arrived in the Red Field with routing flags none of them had ever seen—official-looking, encrypted, addressed not to the channel but to its members individually, which meant someone outside had learned who they were. Several of the younger kids panicked. Wren posted a screenshot of the header and asked if anyone recognized the origin code.

Idris did. She'd seen it in the final transmissions, in the last week of the dream, when Elena had shown them the faces of the people coming to help: a battered salvage tug, a defected compliance officer in handcuffs of his own arranging, and a navigator with a burned arm and a spine like a cable, who had looked directly out of the dream at Idris—at *her*, specifically, though Elena swore afterward that the choice of focus had been the navigator's own—and said, *When this is done, someone should teach you children how to fly properly. What you did with that array was either genius or a war crime, and the difference is training.*

The message was short. Idris read it twice, then read it aloud to her mother, who had refused to go to bed and was sitting wrapped in a blanket at the foot of the bunk like a co-conspirator, which, Idris supposed, she now was.

*To the children of the Red Field, from D. Halloran, aboard the Cyrus (what's left of her):*

*The tribunal has asked me to tell you formally what the news is saying informally: your testimony—the drawings, the recordings you made of your dreams, the array logs from Thessaly—has been entered into the record. You are listed as witnesses, not victims. Counselor Amara insists I use the word "witnesses." She says it matters legally. I think it matters more than legally.*

*Captain Osei did not survive the broadcast. I want you to know that he chose it. He was paid for eleven years to carry those backups nowhere, and in the end he carried them everywhere, and he died with the transmit light on. When they name the memorial wing at the rebuilt relay, his name will be second, after Elena's. He would have hated that and secretly loved it.*

*One more thing. The Cyrus flies again in six weeks—new hull plates, old conscience. We're going to places that have forgotten things too. There are grown children of Meridian aboard already, and room for more, and I'm told by several of them that I don't get a vote on whether a certain twelve-year-old from Thessaly signs on when she's of age, only on whether I've taught her navigation well enough beforehand.*

*So. Lessons start when you're ready. Bring the others who want to learn. The dead taught you to listen; someone should teach you to steer.*

*Hold empty. Conscience full.*

*—D.H.*

Idris read it a third time silently. Then she looked at her mother, who was crying again, and laughing, and doing both helplessly at once.

"Well," her mother managed. "I suppose I'd better meet this navigator before I agree to anything."

"You already agreed," Idris said. "You cried at the names. Everybody who cried at the names is in it now. That's how it works. That's how it's worked since the beginning." She turned back to the terminal, to the Red Field, where forty-one children across four stations were waiting for her to speak, because apparently that was her job now, and maybe forever. She thought about the green field. She thought about the countdown that was no longer counting down. She thought about a ship with an empty hold, sailing toward whatever else in the dark had been buried and left for quiet.

She began to type.

**Everyone stay on the channel. There's something we need to talk about. It's about what we do next.**
