## 21. Backups Sleeping No More

The relay had been rebuilt the way cathedrals are—slowly, by people who argued about every beam, and with more love than engineering strictly required.

Dominic stood in its central chamber six months after the broadcast, watching light move across a wall of names. Four hundred and twelve of them, etched into a composite panel that caught the station's artificial dawn and held it. Below each name, a small amber indicator glowed: *archived, stable, awaiting consent.*

That last phrase had been her doing. She'd fought for it through three months of tribunal procedure, through lawyers who spoke of the backups as evidence and administrators who spoke of them as assets, until she'd finally stood up in a hearing room on Thessaly and said, in a voice she didn't recognize as her own: "They died once without being asked anything. They will not be consulted to death a second time."

The room had gone quiet. Even Serrano, testifying that day in his gray compliance uniform with its insignia stripped, had looked at her with something like recognition. The phrase went into the charter. *Awaiting consent.* It was printed now on every legal document concerning the Meridian archives, and Dominic thought it might be the only sentence she would ever write that mattered.

The chamber smelled of new polymer and old vacuum—the relay's original bones still threaded through the reconstruction, salvaged plates welded beside fresh ones so you could see, if you knew how to look, where the survivors' hands had been. Elena Vashti had built this. Or rather, Elena Vashti had died building this, and what remained of her had spent eleven years transmitting from inside it, counting down, asking to be remembered.

*Remember us.* The children had heard it for two years before anyone listened.

Dominic touched the panel below ELENA VASHTI — CHIEF ENGINEER. The amber light pulsed once under her fingertip, or seemed to. She still couldn't tell how much of that was the system acknowledging her and how much was her own grief inventing responses. Six months of working with the archives had taught her the difference between presence and projection, mostly. Mostly.

"You're here early again." Ade Okafor's voice came from the access corridor, warm and slightly amused. He was the memorial program's lead conservator, a soft-spoken man from Ceres who had lost no one at Meridian and had therefore been chosen precisely because he hadn't—everyone agreed the dead deserved an advocate without a stake, though Dominic privately thought everyone deserved an advocate with one. "You know the session isn't until fourteen hundred."

"I wanted to look at the wall before we made it complicated," Dominic said.

Ade came to stand beside her. He was carrying a tablet he didn't open, which meant he was nervous, which meant today's session was going to be harder than the briefing had let on. "We've finished the rendering options," he said. "Both pathways. Full documentation is in your queue, but I'll summarize."

"Please."

"Pathway one: remain archived. Sealed storage, tribunal-grade preservation, reviewed annually. They stay as they are—complete, private, asleep. Their testimony remains available to the court but nothing else is done with them. No simulation, no public interface. They are, effectively, left alone forever." He paused. "It's not nothing. Some of them may want exactly that."

"And pathway two?"

"Rendered into public record and memorial simulation. A full sensory reconstruction—memories indexed, voices synthesized from their own recordings, interactive. Visitors could speak with them. Schoolchildren on Thessaly could ask Elena Vashti what the airlocks sounded like when—" He stopped himself. "What happened. What happened there."

Dominic looked at the wall. Four hundred and twelve small lights. Four hundred and twelve people who had been cargo, then evidence, then witnesses, and who were about to be asked, one final time, what they wished to be.

"Has anyone chosen yet?" she asked.

"The session is voluntary and private. We won't know individual choices unless they choose to share them." Ade hesitated again, longer this time. "But I'll tell you what I told the oversight committee. In the trial runs—where we explained both pathways to archived consciousnesses from other, smaller cases—the overwhelming majority chose to speak. Not to persist. To *speak*. There's a difference, and I think it matters. Persistence is fear. Speech is purpose."

Dominic thought of Elena in the interface cradle, eight months ago, telling her about the last day. The airlocks. The sound Concorde's security teams had described in their reports as a *labor incident resolution*. Elena had described it as forty-one minutes of knocking.

"They should get to choose," Dominic said. "All of them. Even the ones who choose silence."

"Even those." Ade finally opened his tablet. "There's one more thing. Elena has requested—if she chooses the second pathway—that her first act be a single transmission. One address, live, to the children. The dreamers. All of them, wherever they are. She's asked that you be present when she makes the request official, because—" he glanced down at the tablet, reading her own words back to him, "—because Dominic Halloran carried us when no one else would carry anything."

Dominic's throat tightened. She nodded, unable for a moment to do anything else.

* * *

At fourteen hundred, the chamber filled—not with crowds, but with the particular quiet crowd of people who had earned the right to be there. Serrano, civilian now, wearing clothes that fit him badly because he'd worn a uniform for twenty years and hadn't yet learned what his own shoulders looked like. Two conservators. A tribunal observer who sat with her hands folded and said nothing. And on the main display, patched in from stations across settled space, the faces of children.

Idris was there, twelve years old and somehow older than that, sitting in the Thessaly uplink room with the other dreamers behind him. He had been the first to draw Elena's face, the first to recite names he'd never heard, the first to understand that the dream wasn't haunting anyone—it was asking. When the tribunal had needed a human face for the heirs' petition, it had been Idris's face, solemn and unafraid, that ended up on every screen.

"Are we ready?" Ade asked the room, and the room, such as it was, said yes.

The consent protocol began.

One by one, the archives were brought to a shallow waking—a state the ethicists had spent months defining, deep enough for genuine choice, shallow enough to spare them the full weight of their own deaths. Each backup was read the same statement, in the same neutral voice, and given the same unbounded time to answer. They could ask questions. They could refuse. They could take a year.

The first chose silence. A dockworker named Tomas Reyes, whose file showed he'd had a wife and daughter on Meridian, said only: "I said what I needed to say at the tribunal. Let me sleep." His light on the wall dimmed to a deep, restful blue, and Dominic felt something in her chest crack and reseat itself. Silence was a right too. She had to keep remembering that.

The second chose speech. Then the third. Then a long run of them, voice after voice rising out of the archive like a tide coming in—teachers, miners, a botanist who asked whether her greenhouse simulations could be used to grow actual food for the waystation schools (they could; Ade wrote it down immediately), a teenager named Milo Chen who had been seventeen at the end and who said, with terrible gentleness, "I never got to be grown-up. Let me at least be useful."

By the time they reached the middle of the list, Dominic had stopped counting choices and started listening to reasons, and the reasons were all variations on a theme she recognized from her own life: *I don't want to exist forever. I want to matter once, properly.*

Then: "Elena Vashti."

The chamber lights shifted. On the wall, the amber indicator beneath Elena's name brightened to white, and the interface cradle at the chamber's heart—which had been empty all these months except for maintenance runs—came alive with the soft harmonic hum Dominic remembered from the night she'd risked everything to wake one backup illegally and had instead been handed the truth of a massacre.

Elena's voice, when it came, was the voice from the logs—steady, dry-edged, an engineer's voice that had never once wasted breath on decoration.

"Dominic Halloran. You're there. Good. I have a formal request to enter into the record, and then I intend to stop being procedural, which my crew always said I was constitutionally incapable of."

"Enter it," said the tribunal observer softly.

"I choose the second pathway. Public record, memorial simulation, all of it. Render me. But my first transmission—I want it live, one time, to the children. Not a recording. If it's a recording, it becomes a thing they study. If it's live, it's a thing that *happened*, and things that happen can't be filed." A pause, and when Elena continued, the engineer's armor had thinned enough to show the person underneath. "I helped build the dream broadcast. I seeded myself into every backup we had and poured what was left of the relay's power into a signal aimed at sleeping minds, because adults had stopped listening and children hadn't learned to yet. I used those kids. I know that. I told myself it was the only channel left, and it was true, but it doesn't make it kind. So I owe them a voice they can answer. That's the request. That's the whole request."

"It's granted," the observer said. "Transmit when ready."

Elena was quiet for a moment. In the interface, Dominic knew, a rendered consciousness experienced time differently—seconds could be years—but the pause before speaking was the same everywhere, in every medium, the universal human gathering of courage.

"Idris," Elena said. "And Amara, and Dev, and little Sunniva on Halcyon Reach, and every kid who woke up with red fields behind your eyes and a woman's voice counting down—you don't have to raise your hands, I can't see you anyway, but I know you're there. I've known you were there for two years. You were the bravest part of this. Not the bravest children—the bravest *people*, full stop, and I include myself and I include Halloran and I include the officer who turned traitor to save the truth, and I would say that to any room in settled space.

"I want to tell you what you actually did, because the news feeds got it wrong. They said you *received* a haunting. No. You answered a call. Four hundred and twelve people were erased so thoroughly that the law itself forgot we existed—we weren't dead, we were *unpersoned*, which is worse—and the only ones who refused to forget were too young to know they were supposed to. You held us in your sleep. You drew our faces. You recited our names to teachers who thought you were making it up, and when one of you was detained for sabotage—" a flicker of anger crossed the rendered voice, real as weather, "—you did not stop. Sunniva, tell Dev I said the array job was elegant. Terrible, dangerous, and elegant. He'll know what I mean.

"We chose to speak today. Most of us. Some chose to rest, and honor them too—rest is also a decision, and decisions are what we were denied at the end, so every decision any of us makes now is a victory. For those of us who speak: you'll be able to find us. Ask us things. We remember the colony, the work, the good years before the bad ones—don't let the massacre eat the whole story. We danced at Midwinter Festival. We grew tomatoes in hydroponics that were objectively terrible and we loved them. We were a place before we were a crime scene.

"And Idris—one last thing, and then I'll let you all go back to being twelve, which you've earned." The voice softened to almost nothing, and the whole chamber leaned in around it. "When you dreamed of the red field and the countdown, some of you were scared, and your parents said it was a fad, and you kept dreaming anyway. That stubbornness saved four hundred and twelve people from disappearing twice. Whatever you become—engineers, pilots, poets, troublemakers—keep that. The world will always have someone paid to forget. Be the ones paid in nothing who remember anyway."

The transmission ended. In the uplink rooms across five stations, Dominic knew, children were crying and laughing and pressing their palms to screens. On the Thessaly feed, Idris wiped his face with his sleeve in the unselfconscious way of the young and said, clearly, into the open channel:

"Thank you, Elena. We'll come visit. All of us. That's a promise, not a fad."

The chamber laughed—wet, relieved laughter—and even the tribunal observer smiled.

On the wall, under ELENA VASHTI, the light turned green. *Consent given. Voice rendered. Awaiting visitors.*

* * *

Late that night, when the chamber had emptied and the station had dimmed to its night cycle, Dominic stayed. She walked the wall slowly, reading names, noting the colors—green for speakers, blue for sleepers, and a handful still amber, still deciding, because the protocol allowed a year and a year they would have.

She stopped at the end of the wall, where a space had been left blank. Ade had shown her the plans weeks ago: a final panel, reserved, for whoever came next. Because there would be others—other buried holds, other scrubbed manifests, other colonies declared statistical errors. The Cyrus's discovery had cracked something open, and tribunals in three systems were now subpoenaing freight manifests that had sailed unquestioned for decades. The dead were beginning, cautiously, to line up at doors that had been sealed against them.

Her comm chimed. Serrano.

"You saw the transmission?" she said, answering.

"Every word." A pause. "I pulled my father's file tonight. From the archive. First time I've been able to open it since the tribunal released it." Another pause, longer. "He chose silence, Dominic. Rafael Serrano chose to sleep. And I sat in my kitchen and cried like a boy, and then I realized—" his voice steadied, "—that it was the first choice anyone in my family had been allowed to make in thirty years. I'm going to respect it. I'm going to visit the wall and not ask him to wake up. That's what consent means, isn't it? Even when it costs you."

"That's exactly what it means."

"Good." She could hear him breathing, gathering himself. "That's why I called. The tribunal liaison sent me a manifest tonight. A salvage tug operating out of the Verge, flagged under nine different registries, hold contents listed as *agricultural equipment*. Four hundred crates." He let that land. "They're requesting the Meridian protocol. Your protocol. And they want the person who wrote it."

Dominic looked at the blank panel at the end of the wall, waiting for names that hadn't arrived yet.

"When do we leave?" she said.
