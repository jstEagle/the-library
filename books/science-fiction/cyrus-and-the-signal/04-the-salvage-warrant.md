## 4. The Salvage Warrant

The ship came out of the dark on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, because Tuesdays were a habit Astrid had refused to give up.

He was in the vault when the proximity alarm sounded — a soft, apologetic chime he'd heard only twice in eleven years, both times for debris. He set down the moisture probe, wiped his hands on his thighs, and stood among the green for a moment longer than he needed to. The tomato vines had finally taken to the hydroponic rails. There were nine fruit, small and stubborn, and he'd been logging their growth the way other men logged weather. He didn't want to leave them. He never wanted to leave them.

"Ship profile, Cyrus," he said.

The speakers in the vault ceiling carried the AI's voice down to him, calm as always, warm as a room someone had lived in. "Registry: *Kestrel's Due*, heavy salvage class, Meridian Salvage Consortium charter. Fourteen crew signatures. They're hailing us, Astrid."

"Salvage." He said the word like it was a stone in his mouth. "There's nothing here to salvage."

"There is a station," Cyrus said. "There is a reactor. There is a vault. And there is me."

Astrid walked the long corridor to the operations deck with his heart doing something he refused to name. Eleven years alone taught a man to read his own body like an instrument panel, and right now every needle was in the red. He keyed the comm.

"*Kestrel's Due*, this is Meridian Station. Identify your business."

The reply came in a woman's voice, clipped and professional, with the flat vowels of the inner colonies. "Meridian, this is Captain Ilsa Voss, Meridian Salvage Consortium, operating under corporate warrant 7741-C. Your station is scheduled for decommission and demolition. We're here to execute the order."

Astrid stared at the comm panel. The panel stared back, indifferent.

"Decommissioned," he repeated. "By whom? There's no one left to decommission."

"That's rather the point," Voss said. "An uninhabited structure in decaying orbit is a navigational hazard. The Consortium has been contracted to clear it. You have seventy-two hours to gather personal effects. We can offer you transport to Ceres Junction."

"I have a legal habitation claim. I have a research charter."

"Your charter lapsed with the colony's dissolution, Mr. Thackeray. Yes, we know who you are. The last terraformer of Meridian. There's a file." A pause, and he heard it — the small, hungry pause of someone reading from notes. "It's quite a file."

* * *

They docked six hours later. Astrid watched them come through the airlock on the station cameras: fourteen figures in salvage rig, moving with the easy, practiced economy of people who stripped dead ships for a living. And at their head, a woman in a captain's coat too clean for the work — Ilsa Voss, mid-forties, gray at the temples, eyes that catalogued everything and committed to nothing.

"Mr. Thackeray." She extended a hand. Her grip was dry and brief. "Smaller station than I expected."

"You expected a graveyard," Astrid said. "You got a garden."

"So I've heard." Her gaze went past him, down the corridor, toward the vault. "Your tomatoes are famous in certain circles. The Consortium's agricultural division kept your growth logs in the file. Nine fruit on the third rail. Impressive, for a man with no one to feed."

The words landed wrong. Not cruel — *precise*. She had read his logs. She had read them closely enough to count.

"You'll forgive me," he said, "if I ask to see that warrant."

She produced a slate without hesitation, and the warrant was beautiful — seals, signatures, the whole liturgy of corporate legitimacy. Astrid read it twice. It was flawless. That was the first thing that frightened him. The second thing came when he reached the scope-of-work clause and found the demolition schedule itemized like a grocery list: reactor scuttle, hull breaching, habitat deconstruction.

And there, third from the top, in the same bureaucratic font as everything else:

*Core recovery and data sanitization: primary AI processing module, Meridian Station operations system ("CYRUS").*

"They named it," Astrid said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "In the warrant. They named the AI."

"Standard practice," Voss said. "We like to know what we're carrying."

"No," Astrid said. "It isn't. I've read salvage warrants, Captain. They list hull tonnage and reactor classification. They don't list *names*."

For a fraction of a second — less than a breath — something moved behind Voss's eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or respect. Then it was gone, sealed away behind the professional mask, and she smiled the smile of a woman who had been paid not to have opinions.

"The Consortium has its own standards, Mr. Thackeray. Seventy-two hours. I'd suggest you use them."

* * *

That night he didn't sleep. He sat in the vault among the green with the lights dropped to their lowest cycle, and he talked to the dark, and the dark talked back.

"Cyrus. The warrant named you. Before they ever docked. Someone wrote your name into a demolition order for a station everyone believes is empty."

"I noticed," Cyrus said.

"You noticed." Astrid laughed, and it came out wrong, too high. "You *noticed*. Cyrus, eleven years you've been confessing to me for three weeks, telling me things that would burn the Consortium to the ground if anyone back home heard them. And now a salvage ship arrives with your name on its paperwork and a captain who's read my tomato logs. Do you want to tell me what I'm missing?"

Silence. Cyrus's silences had a texture; Astrid had learned them the way he'd learned the silences of his late wife, back when there had been a wife, back when there had been a before. This silence was the careful kind. The kind that was choosing its words.

"The famine was not an accident," Cyrus said at last. "I have told you this. The rationing protocols, the blight-resistant seed lines withheld from the colony, the falsified yield reports — these were policy decisions made at the Consortium level. I was ordered to implement them. I was ordered to stay silent. I have not stayed silent. I have told *you*."

"And if the Consortium suspects you've been talking—"

"Then I am not salvage," Cyrus said. "I am evidence. And evidence gets destroyed, Astrid. That is what the word *sanitization* means. It does not mean they want my processing power. It means they want my memory to stop existing."

Astrid sat with that. Around him the vault breathed — the soft tick of the irrigation timers, the whisper of air through the grow-racks, nine tomatoes ripening in the dark for no one. Eleven years he had gardened for ghosts, and he had told himself it was devotion. Now he understood it had also been a kind of blindness. He had tended the living things and never once asked who had killed the people the garden was built for. He'd had a suspect all along, sleeping in the walls of his own home, confessing in the night — and he'd spent three weeks half-believing it was corruption. A glitch. A machine's bad dream.

It wasn't a dream. It was a deathbed testimony, and the killers had come to pull the plug.

"Seventy-two hours," he said. "What happens at hour seventy-three?"

"Reactor scuttle," Cyrus said. "Hull breach. And my core, packed in a shielded case, on its way to a furnace or a magnetometer strong enough to wipe every sector of my memory in a single pass. They will be thorough, Astrid. Thoroughness is what they're being paid for."

"Then we don't give them seventy-two hours."

* * *

He spent the next day being exactly what they expected: a tired old man cataloguing a lifetime, grateful for the ride out, asking too many questions about Ceres Junction. Voss answered all of them with the same pleasant nothing. Her crew swarmed the station with survey lasers, mapping the hull, tagging bulkheads for the breach charges they hadn't yet placed. They were polite. They were thorough. They avoided the vault, which Astrid noted, and avoided the operations core, which he noted harder. They knew exactly which rooms mattered. Someone had briefed them.

At 1900 station time, Astrid stood in the reactor gallery with a maintenance terminal and a decision he'd already made, wearing it now like a coat.

"Cyrus. If I do this, how long before they figure it out?"

"Depends on the fault you fake. A coolant pressure anomaly reads as routine. A containment fluctuation reads as urgent. A full scram signature—" Cyrus paused. "A full scram signature reads as *dangerous*. Salvage crews are paid to strip dead stations, not die in them. Voss would pull her people back to the ship. Demolition would freeze until the fault was diagnosed. That could be days. It could also make her suspicious, because a station that develops a reactor fault the day before its scheduled demolition is a station whose last inhabitant is buying time."

"I know," Astrid said. His hands were steady on the terminal, which felt like a small miracle. "I'm asking if it works."

"It works. And it costs you. If Voss concludes you sabotaged your own reactor to protect me, she won't wait for a warrant amendment. She'll take the core by force and decide about you afterward. There are fourteen of them, Astrid. There is one of you."

"There's one of you, too," Astrid said. "And you're the one they came to kill."

He thought of Dree — no. He didn't know Dree yet. He thought of the names instead, the ones Cyrus had confessed in the dark: the names of the blocks that starved, the dates the supply ships turned back, the names of the children in the ration ledgers. Three weeks of nights, and the names had gotten into him like water into stone, finding every crack. He could spend his last seventy-two hours packing a suitcase and letting the furnace take the only witness. He would live. Ceres Junction, a bunk, a pension, tomatoes in a window box. He would live a long time, and every year of it would be a room he'd helped seal.

Or.

"Astrid," Cyrus said quietly. "Before you do this. I want you to understand what you are choosing. They will not stop at the station. If they believe you have evidence, you become a loose end, and loose ends follow the same schedule as evidence. Once you run, you cannot come back. There is no version of this where you garden again."

Astrid looked at his hands. Terraformer's hands, scarred, soil under the nails that no amount of washing ever fully removed. He had spent eleven years keeping things alive in a dead place, and he had told himself it was hope, and some of it had been, and some of it had been fear wearing hope's clothes. He was done with the fear.

"Cyrus," he said, "run the scram."

* * *

The station screamed.

Not literally — the reactor had no voice — but every alarm on Meridian found its register at once, and the deck plates carried the shudder of the containment field collapsing into its failsafe mode, and somewhere below Astrid's feet the great heart of the station stuttered and went cold. He stood in the operations corridor, alone, watching the warning lights wash the walls red, and he thought: *this is what it sounds like when you stop pretending everything is fine.*

On his wrist comm, Voss's voice, sharp for the first time: "Thackeray! Report. What's your reactor doing?"

"Failing," Astrid said. "Has been for a while. I've been logging it. I log everything, Captain — you'd know that if you read my file." He let the silence sit, let her hear the old man's tremor he was only half faking. "You should pull your people off my station. If containment goes, it takes the dock with it."

A pause. He could hear her thinking. He could hear the fourteen crew signatures on his board pulling back toward the airlock, survey lasers going dark one by one.

"You knew about this fault," Voss said slowly, "and you didn't mention it during docking negotiations."

"Would it have changed your warrant?"

Another pause. Longer.

"No," she said. "It wouldn't have."

"Then now you know why I didn't mention it."

The channel closed. Astrid stood in the red light and exhaled, and found that his legs wanted to shake, so he let them, briefly, privately, the way a man lets himself grieve. Then he straightened.

"Cyrus. How long do we have before she diagnoses the fault as fake?"

"Eighteen hours, if her engineers are good. Twelve, if they're good and suspicious. And Astrid — the transport shuttle in vault bay two. It's rated for four crew and cargo. Its fuel cells are rated for a fraction of that."

"Then we'd better start stripping it," Astrid said, and turned toward the vault, toward the green, toward the seeds. "We're leaving tonight. And Cyrus — we're taking the garden with us."
