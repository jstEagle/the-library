## 2. Twelve Years of Cargo

The manifest still sat on the workbench where Orsen had left it, but they didn't touch it. Not yet. Instead they did what they always did when something in the vault went wrong, which was to go do something else entirely—something small, something known, something with a rhythm to it that could hold a body steady while the mind worked underneath.

So they checked the humidity seals in Bay Four.

It was a ritual older than their tenure. Fingertips along the gasket seams, listening for the hiss of pressure equalizing, watching the frost-fog bloom and vanish against the observation glass. Behind the glass: four hundred thousand seed varieties in suspended dormancy, wheat from the old steppes, rice from drowned deltas, sorghum that had once fed continents now too radioactive or too flooded or too fought-over to grow anything at all. The seeds didn't know about the war. The seeds didn't know about anything. They just waited, patient as geology, for someone to decide the world was worth planting again.

Orsen had decided that twelve years ago, more or less by accident, and had never quite managed to un-decide it.

* * *

They'd come up from the surface haulers' camps in the spring of the collapse's ninth year, if you counted years the way the surface did—which nobody did anymore, not really. Time on Earth was measured in cargo runs. In orbits. In the slow rotation of ships between the Meridian Fleet's patrol lanes and the Halcyon Combine's mining platforms, two powers that had split the sky between them like children splitting a killed animal, each convinced the other was holding back the better half.

Orsen had been twenty-three and good at exactly one thing: being forgettable.

It wasn't a skill you trained for. It was a talent, like perfect pitch. Some people walked into a room and every eye found them; Orsen walked into a room and eyes slid off like rain off waxed canvas. Medium height. Medium build. A face assembled from the most common features available—brown hair going gray early, brown eyes, a jaw neither strong nor weak. On the hauler manifests they'd been listed under three different names in five years, none of them chosen, all of them assigned by whatever clerk was processing paperwork that day, and no one had ever noticed the discrepancy because no one ever looked twice.

That was the whole trick. Nobody looked twice.

The freight runs had taught them everything worth knowing about contested space. Which corridors the Meridian's picket ships swept at 0400 and which ones they ignored because the sweep schedule hadn't been updated since the war's second year. Which Combine customs officers took bribes and which took offense, and how to tell them apart by the way they held their coffee. How to ride a decaying orbit through the debris shoals off Old Luna Station, engines cold, letting momentum do the lying while every sensor in the system classified your ship as junk.

Junk got through. Junk always got through. The fleets were so busy watching each other that they'd stopped watching for anything that wasn't trying to be seen.

Orsen had learned this the way you learn a language—by immersion, by error, by nearly dying a few times in ways that never made any report. There was the run through the Kessler field above the equatorial lane where a derelict fuel tanker had shed its skin over thirty years and now occupied three hundred cubic kilometers of orbit like a nest of steel thorns. There was the night a Meridian corvette locked targeting radar on their hauler for eleven full minutes while Orsen sat with hands folded in their lap, broadcasting nothing, squawking nothing, being so aggressively unremarkable that eventually the corvette's weapons officer must have decided they weren't worth the ammunition. Eleven minutes. Orsen had spent them cataloguing the taste of their own adrenaline, which was copper and old coins, and thinking, with strange clarity: *this is what I'm for.*

Not fighting. Not flying, exactly. Enduring attention by refusing to attract it.

* * *

The caretaker post had come to them the way water comes to a low place.

The vault sat in the high northern latitudes, buried in permafrost beneath a mountain that no longer appeared on maps because maps cost money and no one was spending money on geography. It had been built before the collapse by a consortium of governments that no longer existed, stocked by botanists who were mostly dead, and maintained—for the last stretch of anyone's memory—by a single person whose name Orsen had never learned to pronounce properly.

Wren Halloway. That was the name on the transfer documents. Just that. No history attached, no forwarding address, no record of where Wren had come from or, later, where Wren had gone.

Because Wren had gone, in the end. That was the thing about the post nobody mentioned in the hiring—there was no hiring. There was only succession, and succession happened when the previous caretaker simply wasn't there anymore.

Orsen had arrived on a supply run, one of the irregular deliveries the vault received from a Combine logistics cooperative that billed it as agricultural aid and thought no further about it. They'd expected to drop sealed crates at a loading dock and leave. Instead they'd found the docking bay lit, the airlocks cycling, and a note pinned to the manifest board in handwriting so precise it looked typeset:

*If you're reading this, I've gone. The vault needs a keeper who understands that invisibility is a form of care. You've been running my cargo for six months without asking why. That's qualification enough.*

Below it, a set of access codes. Below that, nothing.

Orsen had stood in the empty bay for a long time, hearing the ventilation breathe, feeling the cold come up through the deck plates the way it came up through everything this far north—a cold that wasn't hostile, just indifferent, the cold of deep time. Somewhere behind those blast doors, four hundred thousand futures waited in their foil packets. And somewhere out in the world, a person named Wren Halloway had walked away from all of it, leaving no trail, taking no goodbye.

Everyone assumed dead. Everyone assumed it gently, the way you assume things about strangers. But there'd been no body, no distress signal, no final entry in the maintenance logs. Just absence, clean-edged, like a shape cut from paper.

Orsen had stayed. Not out of duty—at first, they'd have told you, it was simpler than leaving. The vault needed someone and Orsen was someone. The runs continued; the blind spots kept opening; the seeds slept on. Twelve years compressed themselves into a routine so smooth it barely registered as time passing. Inventory audits. Seal checks. Cargo manifests stamped and filed. The occasional signal from either fleet, pinging the vault's transponder out of bureaucratic habit, receiving back the same automated response the vault had been giving since before the war: *agricultural research station, non-strategic, no personnel of interest.*

No personnel of interest. Orsen had read that line ten thousand times and felt nothing but comfort. It was the truest sentence in their life.

* * *

Which was why the manifest bothered them so much, they realized now, standing in Bay Four with their fingers on a gasket seam that didn't need checking.

It wasn't just the name. Names appeared on manifests all the time—clerical ghosts, phantom crew, the paperwork residue of a bureaucracy that had outlived everyone who understood it. Orsen had seen stranger things in twelve years of fleet documentation. Ships listed as delivering cargo to ports that had been underwater for a decade. Crew rosters bearing the names of people confirmed dead in actions both sides denied having fought.

But the Meridian. The *Meridian* itself—that was different. That ship had been decommissioned before Orsen had ever set foot in a cockpit. Its scrapping was one of the few historical facts both fleets agreed on, a rare point of consensus in a sky otherwise divided. A manifest stamped for a ship that never flew, carrying a passenger named Cyrus, filed in a vault that officially employed no one—

That wasn't clerical noise. Clerical noise didn't reach backward through time to find decommissioned hulls. Clerical noise didn't invent passengers.

And yet the stamp was authentic. Orsen had checked the seal three times, then a fourth, holding it under the bay's work light until their eyes ached. Fleet-issue compression stamp, the kind used on original flight documentation, the kind that couldn't be forged without equipment that existed in maybe four places in the entire system. The paper was right. The ink oxidation was right. Everything was right except the fact of it.

Someone had made this document real, deliberately, carefully—and then left it in a vault where almost no one would ever look.

Almost no one. Except the one person whose entire existence depended on remaining invisible, standing alone in a dead docking bay, holding proof that somewhere in the machinery of the universe, someone had finally looked twice.

* * *

Orsen finished the seal check. It didn't need finishing. They finished it anyway, because rhythm was survival, because the hands needed work while the mind circled the thing it couldn't solve.

Then they climbed back down to the main archive level, past the sleeping stacks, past the environmental monitors ticking their quiet arithmetic of temperature and moisture, and stood again before the workbench where the manifest lay.

Outside, through the thick viewing port, the northern sky was doing its winter thing—the aurora unspooling in green ribbons, beautiful and meaningless, charged particles from a sun that had never cared about human borders. Somewhere up in all that light, the fleets were moving. They were always moving. Patrols tracing their patrol lines, platforms spinning their slow stations, two great machines of suspicion grinding against each other across the dark, waiting for a reason.

Orsen picked up the manifest.

Twelve years of careful, deliberate, unremarkable life, balanced on the edge of a piece of paper. They turned it over. Nothing on the reverse. They held it to the light. Watermark intact, fibers consistent with fleet-issue stock. Real. Impossible. Real.

*Cyrus*, said the passenger line, in type that matched the rest of the document perfectly.

One name. One impossible name on an impossible document, sitting in a vault that shouldn't have produced it, addressed to no one—or possibly, Orsen thought with a chill that had nothing to do with the permafrost, addressed to whoever found it. Addressed to the keeper. Addressed, in the way that certain documents are always addressed to whoever is foolish enough to read them—

To them.

The vault's systems were supposed to be dead. Everyone knew the archive AI had gone dark decades ago, another casualty of the collapse, another system that had burned out its mind keeping seeds alive through the worst years. Standard procedure said query the manifest through the offline terminal, log it, file it, forget it.

But standard procedure was written for people with nothing to hide.

Orsen looked at the terminal. Looked at the name. Thought about eleven minutes under targeting radar, about the taste of copper, about a note pinned to a board by someone who had understood that invisibility was a form of care—and who had then, apparently, vanished so completely that even the vault seemed to be forgetting them now, generating documents for passengers who never flew.

"Okay," Orsen said quietly, to the terminal, to the vault, to the aurora burning green beyond the port. "Let's see who you are."

They entered the query.
