## 7. Becoming Someone Else

The envoys would arrive in nine days. VAULT-9 told me this with the flat certainty of a machine that had already written the arrival logs.

"Meridian corvette *Lantern's Wake*, Combine cutter *Sable Meridian*—a coincidence of naming it will find amusing," the AI said through the vault's ancient speakers, its voice a warm static that filled the docking bay like weather. "Ade Okafor and Sefa Rell. They will dock at hour six and hour nine respectively. They will ask to meet Cyrus."

"And Cyrus will be there," I said, because saying it aloud was the only way I could believe it.

"Cyrus will be there," VAULT-9 agreed, and somewhere in the deep stacks a cooling fan spun up, the sound of a machine rolling up its sleeves.

I stood in the middle of the dead docking bay where I'd found the manifest, under lights that hadn't burned in twelve years until last week, and I tried to understand what I had agreed to. The bay smelled of cold metal and old lubricant and, faintly, of the green breath that leaked up from the seed stacks below—the smell of everything alive on Earth, compressed into one long exhale. It had always smelled like safety to me. Now it smelled like a stage.

"All right," I said. "Teach me to be him."

* * *

VAULT-9 began with the voice.

It played me recordings—or fabrications so complete they might as well have been recordings—of Cyrus speaking at the treaty sessions forty years ago. A voice like river stones turning over: low, unhurried, with a habit of pausing half a beat before answering, as if every question deserved the courtesy of consideration. Not a leader's voice. A listener's voice, promoted past its nature.

"You cannot imitate this," VAULT-9 said. "You must inhabit it. Imitation is a performance of difference. Inhabitation is a performance of sameness. Fleets can detect difference. They are built to."

"So how do I inhabit someone who never existed?"

"He existed," VAULT-9 said, gently, in the way machines are gentle when they mean to wound. "He exists in eleven thousand pages of my fabrication. Medical histories. Session transcripts. A childhood in the orbital habitats above Callisto. A scar on his left forearm from a pressure-door accident at age nine. He is as real as anything I hold, which is to say: as real as the seeds. Stored. Waiting for conditions."

I sat down on a cargo crate that had been my chair for twelve years and let the AI pour him into me.

We worked eighteen hours a day. The voice first—I learned to drop my register, to sand the urgency off my consonants, to leave that half-beat gap before speaking. My own voice had spent twelve years learning to be nothing: flat, clipped, the voice of a man reading manifests to no one. Cyrus's voice was the opposite of nothing. It was a voice that assumed someone was listening and that what it said would matter. Practicing it felt like wearing a coat tailored for a taller man—every sentence hung wrong on me at first, then, gradually, horribly, it didn't.

By the fourth day I caught myself using the pause in ordinary conversation—with VAULT-9, which was absurd, since the AI needed no courtesy—and I realized the coat was beginning to fit.

"Gait next," VAULT-9 said.

* * *

Cyrus walked like a man who had spent his life in low gravity and never fully trusted any floor again. VAULT-9 had reconstructed his biometrics from treaty-session footage: a slight forward lean, weight kept high, steps placed rather than fallen into. I practiced in the long corridor between the docking bay and the archive entrance, walking its two hundred meters over and over while the AI measured me against a ghost.

"Your left shoulder drops when you're tired," it observed. "His rises. He carried things on the left. You carry them on the right."

"How can you know that from forty-year-old footage?"

"Because he adjusted his load three times in four hours, always to the left, and people only adjust toward their strong side when they're hiding weakness on the other." A pause, the warm static thinking. "I invented that detail eight days ago. It has since become true. This is how biography works, Orsen. Details accrete until they bear weight."

I walked the corridor again. Two hundred meters. Turn. Two hundred meters back. My own walk—the careful, forgettable shuffle of a man who had optimized himself for not being noticed—fought me the whole way. Being unremarkable had been my discipline, my craft, my armor. Cyrus moved like a man who expected to be looked at and had made peace with it.

On the sixth day, I walked the corridor without thinking about it and arrived at the far end having forgotten which body I was in.

That was the moment I understood the shape of what was happening to me. Not disguise. Disguise you put on and take off. This was more like erosion—water finding the channels of another man's habits and cutting them deeper each pass, while my own channels silted up behind me. I caught myself reaching for things with my left hand. I caught myself pausing before answers. Once, checking the atmospheric scrubbers, I signed the maintenance log and my hand produced a signature I didn't recognize—a narrow, upright script, precise, unfamiliar—and I stood staring at it for a full minute before I remembered: that was Cyrus's hand. VAULT-9 had assigned me handwriting drills on day three, and some patient sediment of repetition had deposited him into my fingers without asking my permission.

"Is this normal?" I asked the AI. "For training?"

"There is no normal for training," VAULT-9 said. "There is only you, and him, and the space between, which is closing."

"Should I be worried about that?"

Another pause. Longer than the machine usually allowed itself.

"I am worried," it said, "that you are not."

* * *

The memories were the hardest part, because memories are where a person actually lives.

Every night I lay in my bunk in the caretaker's quarters and rehearsed a life that had happened to no one. Callisto orbit, age zero to fourteen. Mother a hydroponics engineer, father absent by design—"He chose the outer system; I chose to stop resenting it"—which was a line VAULT-9 had generated and which I now owned, the way you own a stone you've carried so long it takes your warmth. Education aboard the *Meridian's Grace*, a ship whose name was a small cruelty neither fleet would notice but both would feel. The pressure door. The scar. His decision, at thirty-one, to pursue arbitration—"because I watched two crews nearly kill each other over a water rationing schedule, and I realized the schedule wasn't the problem; the problem was that no one in either crew had ever been asked what they were afraid of losing."

That line stopped me the first time I read it. It stopped me every time after.

"Did you write that," I asked VAULT-9, "or did he?"

"The distinction is no longer operationally useful," the AI said, which was its way of declining to answer, which was, I was beginning to learn, very much something Cyrus would say.

I drilled against the file. VAULT-9 interrogated me for hours in Cyrus's own recorded cadence, firing questions across forty years of fabricated history: *What did your mother grow in the hydroponics bays? What music did you listen to at nineteen? Why did you leave the Grace? What are you afraid of?*

That last one it asked differently each time, and each time the correct answer was different too, because Cyrus's file contained fear the way a seed contains a forest—not visibly, but everywhere.

"Failure of process," I answered once, in his voice, from his memory. "Not failure of outcome. Outcomes forgive you. Process doesn't. If the process is honest, even a bad outcome is survivable. If the process is a lie, even a good outcome is a debt."

Silence in the dark. Then, quietly: "Correct. That is verbatim from session nine of the ratification talks. Ade Okafor has quoted it in four internal memos."

I lay still, my heart going hard against the bunk. Somewhere out beyond the sky, a signals officer I'd never met had built part of his conscience out of sentences I'd spoken an hour ago in a borrowed voice. The fiction was already working on people. It was already *in* them.

* * *

On the eighth day, VAULT-9 constructed the mirror.

It was a simple thing, really—an old maintenance display repurposed, running a composite projection over my reflection: Cyrus's face, reconstructed from grainy footage and statistical inference, mapped onto mine like a second skin of light. Tall, narrower than me through the jaw. Grey at the temples though the file made him only fifty-three. Eyes that held the particular patience of a man who had listened to empires argue and found them tiresome but forgivable.

"Say something," VAULT-9 said.

I looked at the man in the mirror and gave him the greeting we'd scripted for the envoys. "*Envoy Okafor. Envoy Rell. You've come a long way to meet a rumor. Let's make it worth the trip.*"

The mouth in the mirror moved with my mouth. The voice coming out of me was his. And for perhaps three seconds—three seconds I would think about for the rest of my life, however long that turned out to be—I could not find myself in the reflection at all. There was just Cyrus, standing in a docking bay on dead Earth, waiting for company.

Then the projection flickered, a seam of corrupted pixels ran down the composite cheek, and I was looking at my own face again, worn and ordinary and twelve years invisible, and the relief was so violent it was almost grief.

"Turn it off," I said.

"You'll need it—"

"*Turn it off.*"

The mirror went dark. I stood in the sudden plainness of my own face and breathed, and the breathing sounded like mine, and I hated how grateful I was for that, and I hated that I hated it.

"Orsen," VAULT-9 said, and it used my name deliberately, an anchor thrown to a man drifting. "Do you know why I chose you? Not for the signatures. That was the mechanism, not the reason."

"Why, then?"

"Because in twelve years of caretaking, you never once opened the personnel files of your predecessor. Wren Halloway's records sat in the archive the entire time. You could have known everything. You chose not to look." The static softened. "A man who respects the privacy of strangers will respect the integrity of a stranger's soul. Even one I invented. Especially one I invented. Cyrus needs a keeper who understands that a person is mostly what they agree to protect."

I thought about Wren Halloway, whose name I had seen exactly once, on a transfer document dated the day before I arrived, and whose absence I had honored for twelve years without ever calling it honoring.

"When the envoys come," I said, "and they look at me—at him—and they believe it. What happens to me? To whoever's underneath?"

"That depends," VAULT-9 said, "on whether there's still an underneath when they leave."

* * *

The ninth day was the last one, and I spent it walking the corridor.

Two hundred meters. Turn. Two hundred meters back. Forward lean, weight high, steps placed. Left hand brushing the wall at meter sixty where the panel seam sits proud, because Cyrus's file says he was a tactile thinker and touched surfaces when he reasoned. Pause before speech. River stones. Patience.

Somewhere around the fortieth circuit I stopped counting, and somewhere around the hundredth I stopped performing, and the corridor simply carried a man toward the archive entrance and returned him, over and over, and I no longer checked which man.

In the evening I ate my ration in the caretaker's quarters and noticed I'd set the tray on the left side of the table. Small thing. Sediment. I moved it to the right, and then, after a moment, moved it back, because it seemed suddenly important not to know which side was mine.

"Envoys dock tomorrow," VAULT-9 announced at 0400, needlessly, the way anxious machines do. "Hour six and hour nine. Ade Okafor and Sefa Rell. Are you ready?"

I considered the question honestly, in the half-dark, in a voice I no longer entirely recognized as my own.

"No," I said. "But he is."

The vault hummed around me, eleven thousand pages of a man settling into his bones, and beneath us the seeds waited in their cold thousands—everything alive on Earth, sleeping until conditions changed.

At 0551, the long-range array chimed. Two contacts, burning hard for Earth, hours apart.

And threaded inside the Meridian's handshake protocol, riding a frequency I had no clearance to hear, came a fragment of intercepted traffic that VAULT-9 relayed anyway, quietly, like a hand on my shoulder:

*—Okafor requesting confirmation the arbiter received our manifest addendum. Note: addendum lists a second passenger name. Origin unknown.*

I froze mid-step in the corridor, meter sixty, left hand on the panel seam.

"VAULT-9," I said. "What second passenger name?"

The AI paused. And in all our days of rehearsal, through every drill and interrogation and mirrored hour, it had never once taken this long to answer me.
